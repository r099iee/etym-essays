Heathen (noun and adjective) is an ancient Germanic term carrying deep religious, cultural, anthropological, and historical weight. As a noun, it historically denotes an individual who does not adhere to a widely recognized monotheistic world religion (specifically Christianity, Judaism, or Islam); in traditional Christian ecclesiastical writing, it served as the Germanic equivalent to the Latinate pagan or biblical gentile. In contemporary secular parlance, it is frequently used as a colorful, informal, or jocular pejorative for someone perceived as lacking manners, cultural refinement, moral discipline, or civilized habits. In modern religious terminology, it has been proudly reclaimed by adherents of Heathenry (contemporary Germanic Neopaganism).

Primary Religious, Cultural, & Pejorative Senses:

  • Historical & Christian Ecclesiastical Meaning (Non-Abrahamic Believer): In Christian theology and historical chronicles, a heathen is any person, tribe, or nation that worships polytheistic, animistic, or ancestral deities rather than the God of Abraham. During the Christianization of Northern Europe, the Anglo-Saxons, Scandinavians, and Frisians applied hǣðen to those who continued to venerate Odin, Thor, Freyr, and the traditional Germanic pantheon. Later, during the Crusades and the Age of Discovery, European writers indiscriminately applied heathen to indigenous peoples worldwide.
  • Colloquial Pejorative & Jocular Reproach (Uncivilized / Ill-Mannered): In everyday modern English, heathen is widely employed as a mildly derogatory, humorous, or exasperated insult describing an individual who exhibits barbarous table manners, unrefined social etiquette, a lack of intellectual culture, or reckless disregard for polite conventions (e.g., "wipe your boots before entering, don't act like a heathen", "those little heathens left a complete mess in the playroom").
  • Modern Neopagan Self-Identification (Adherent of Heathenry): In contemporary religious studies, Heathen (frequently capitalized) is the chosen self-designation for modern revivalists and reconstructionists of pre-Christian Germanic and Norse polytheism (often synonymous with or related to Ásatrú or Forn Siðr). These modern practitioners actively embrace the historical label, investing it with pride, ecological reverence, and ancestral honor.
  • Adjectival Application (Pagan / Barbarous / Wild): Used attributively to describe practices, rituals, idols, tribes, or belief systems associated with heathenism (e.g., "heathen rites", "heathen temples", "heathen lands"). In literature, it evokes wildness, ancient polytheistic mystery, or fierce resistance to monotheistic orthodoxy.

Polysemic Shifts, Socio-Cultural Bridges, & Semantic Mechanics:

  • The Heath-Dweller Hypothesis & Biblical Loan-Translation: The historical bridge linking the word to the landscape lies in Proto-Germanic *haiþiz (an open, uncultivated, wild heath overgrown with heather). Early Christian translators (most notably Bishop Ulfilas in the fourth century translating the Bible into Gothic) adapted *haiþinaz ("dweller on the uncultivated heath") to render Greek ἐθνικός (ethnikos, "heathen, gentile") and Latin paganus (which itself originally meant "rural villager / rustic peasant"). Because rural populations living on remote heaths clung stubbornly to traditional pagan customs long after urban centers had converted to Christianity, the "heath-dweller" became the ultimate symbol of the unbaptized, resistant pagan.
  • Colonial Civilizing Rhetoric: In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, European imperial expansion paired the word heathen with concepts of racial and cultural inferiority, creating a moral imperative (the "civilizing mission") that justified colonial subjugation under the banner of converting heathen souls.
