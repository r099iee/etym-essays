stokes (verb, third person singular present of to stoke): The verb to stoke means to poke, stir, and add fuel to a fire so that it burns steadily — to tend a fire, furnace, or stove by feeding it fuel and arranging the coals. Its essential meaning is maintenance: where kindle and ignite name the beginning of a fire, stoke names the keeping-alive of it, the repeated, humble, almost liturgical act of prodding the fuel and pushing in more so that the flame is sustained. In the source passage — a winter scene in which a boy tends the fire while "dark turned fields with rags of snow" lie outside and "darker woods beyond that harbor yet a few last wolves" — stokes carries the whole moral drama of the setting: the fire must be tended every day, without spectacle, or it goes out, and that daily repetition is what stands between the travelers and the cold. The word's tone is deliberately tactile and unheroic: a rhythmic jabbing and jiggling of the wood, a small chore that keeps the fragile margin of warmth alive in a dead world.

Polysemy and extensions: (i) the primary sense — to poke and stir a fire, furnace, or stove, and to add fuel to it ("he stokes the fire"); (ii) the industrial sense — to tend the firebox of a steam engine, ship's boiler, or furnace; the "stoker" is the man who feeds the furnace of a steamship or locomotive, and "stokehold" / "stokehole" is the compartment where he works, an age-of-steam vocabulary inherited in the 18th–19th centuries; (iii) the modern figurative sense, extremely productive — to feed or intensify an emotion, conflict, or controversy as one feeds a flame: "to stoke anger," "to stoke anxiety," "to stoke fears," "to stoke a controversy" — in journalism and politics, to deliberately keep a feeling burning, to add fuel to a fire of public sentiment; (iv) related figurative uses — to stoke demand, stoke inflation, stoke a rivalry, to increase pressure or intensity; (v) of engines and machines, to stoke an engine to keep it running (as in the example sentence "He stokes the engine to keep it running"); (vi) derivative and related forms — stoker (the man, and the machine that feeds a boiler automatically), stoking (the act), stokehold/stokehole (the furnace room). The semantic bridge from fire to feeling is a direct metaphor: emotion is pictured as a fire, and the person who deliberately increases it is pictured as the stoker feeding fuel, so that stoke has become the standard verb of modern political journalism for the deliberate intensification of public passion.

scullery (noun): A scullery is a small back kitchen or utility room, typically in a large house, used for washing dishes, pots, and pans, for rough kitchen work, and for preparing vegetables — the room where the dirtier, wetter work of the kitchen is done, out of sight of the dining room. In the source context (a country house), the scullery is the humble service room of the servants' world, the place where the maid "washed the pots," the counterpart of the grand kitchen above stairs. The word's core is the dish: the scullery is etymologically "the dish-room," the office of the dish-washer, and the scullion is the servant who does the washing-up. Polysemy and extensions: (i) the primary sense — a small room off the kitchen for washing dishes and rough culinary work; (ii) by extension in older usage, the whole department or office of the scullion (the servant who washed dishes); (iii) in modern domestic and architectural language, a utility room, wash-up room, or butler's pantry in a large household; (iv) figurative and historical — the scullery as emblem of servant life, the obscure, unglamorous room of the great house, and "upstairs/downstairs" class geography; (v) in contemporary usage the word has faded with the servant class and survives chiefly in period fiction, in the phrase "scullery maid," and in conversions of old houses where the original scullery plan is preserved. The historical bridge: Latin scutella, "a small dish or bowl" (from scutra, "a flat dish or platter"), gave Old French escuele "dish," and the suffix -erie ("place of") turned it into escuelerie, "the place of the dishes" — the room where the dishes were washed — which English borrowed in the 13th–14th century as squillerye, modern scullery.
