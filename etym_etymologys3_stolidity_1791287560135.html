Stolidity comes to English almost entirely through books, not through the street, and its history is therefore unusually tidy: a Latin adjective of dullness becomes an English noun of impassive endurance, with French as a thin intermediary. The Latin base is stolidus, attested in Plautus, Cicero, and Seneca meaning dull, brutish, insensible, obtuse — applied both to people who cannot feel and to animals that seem not to feel. From it Late Latin forms the abstract noun stoliditas meaning dullness or stupidity. Crucially, Latin already contains the ambiguity that English will inherit: is the stolid person stupid, or merely unmoved?

English first borrows the adjective stolid in the sixteenth century in humanist prose, initially with the frank sense dull, stupid, brutish. Thomas Elyot and later Baconian writers use stolid for lumpish unteachableness. The abstract noun stolidity follows by the mid-seventeenth century, first meaning heaviness of mind. French stolidité in the same period helps fix the spelling with single l and -ity rather than -ness, marking it as learned rather than native.

Phonetically the word barely changes across two millennia, which is typical of learned borrowings that leap over sound laws: Latin /ˈsto.li.dus/ becomes French /stɔ.li.di.te/ becomes English /stəˈlɪdɪti/. Stress moves to the second syllable in English under the Latinate -idity pattern (as in solidity, validity, timidity), and vowels reduce. The morphology is transparent: stolid + -ity, where -ity continues Latin -itas via French -ité, turning a quality into a state or degree.

The great semantic shift occurs in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when British imperial and stoic ideals rehabilitate dullness as steadiness:
  • Early modern dull stupidity: stolidity as a defect — slow-witted, unteachable, brutish. Purely negative.
  • Victorian impassivity: stolidity as a virtue of the lower ranks and of the countryman — uncomplaining, unflappable, able to bear discomfort without grimacing. Still faintly condescending when applied by elites to peasants, soldiers, or colonised peoples.
  • Modern deadpan endurance: Wodehouses quiet stolidity of a stuffed moose — comic, affectionate, and visual. The stolid person wears a mask, gives nothing away, preserves decorum while inwardly uncomfortable. Stupidity has been bleached out; what remains is impermeability.
This is why Wodehouse pairs stolidity with a stuffed moose: both are large, immobile, glassy-eyed, and impossible to read. The joke only works because readers already know stolidity as bovine, northern, Scandinavian, or English-butler impassivity — the quality of not reacting.

Literary attestations confirm the amelioration. Gibbon uses stolid for barbarian dullness. George Eliot uses stolidity for peasant endurance that is both limited and dignified. Kipling uses it for the rank-and-file Tommy who bears heat and boredom without complaint. By the time Wodehouse writes Jeeves and Wooster, stolidity can be comic praise: to preserve quiet stolidity in discomfort is to behave well, to keep the mask on. Contemporary psychology keeps both edges: stolidity can be praised as resilience and calm, or pathologised as alexithymia, flat affect, or lack of empathy, depending on whether the observer admires or pities the mask.
