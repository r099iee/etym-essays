The history of ISLES is really the history of two words that gradually converged on one modern spelling: the native English island and the Romance import isle. The plural isles that appears in the source sentence belongs to the second of these, and its story runs from the salt water of the Indo-European world through Latin, Old French, and the English of Shakespeare and Byron.

The Latin foundation. The chain begins in the vocabulary of the sea. Latin sal "salt" produced salum, "the open sea" — the salt swell of the Mediterranean. On the traditional etymology, insula "island" is literally in salo, "that which is in the sea". The Latin word is first attested in the early republic, in Plautus (2nd c. BCE), and it remained the standard word for an island throughout the classical period and into the Romance languages.

  • From insula came Italian isola, Spanish isla, Portuguese ilha, and Old French ile/isle — the last inherited directly by Middle English.
  • The scholarly debate: an alternative reading connects insula to solum "ground", i.e. "standing land in the sea", an idea favoured by some modern linguists; the in salo analysis remains the traditional and still most-cited one.

The French inheritance. Old French isle (later île, whose -s- was dropped in the 18th century) carried the word into English after the Norman Conquest. Middle English had both ile and isle, alongside the native īegland "island". The two words competed in everyday prose for three centuries, with ilond winning the plain register and isle retreating to verse and elevated writing.

Renaissance respelling. During the Renaissance, humanist scholars, eager to display Latin ancestry, inserted the silent -s- into ile to match insula, and even respelled the native iland as island on the same principle. This "etymological respelling" is the reason both words carry a silent s today. The plural isles inherits the same silent letter, so the spelling — not the pronunciation — is what betrays the word's Latin descent.

Poetic rise of the plural. As island settled into plain prose, isle rose in the other direction, into the diction of verse. The plural isles became a staple of Romantic poetry: Byron's "The Isles of Greece" (Don Juan), Shelley's azure isles, Tennyson's islands of the bay. Its two-syllable shape and the sibilance of the plural made it perfect for metre and rhyme.

  • Fixed collocations: the British Isles, the Channel Isles, the Western Isles (Hebrides), the Isles of Scilly.
  • Imperial usage: "the home isles" for Britain spoken from India and the colonies in the 19th-20th centuries.
  • Modern survival: tourism and heritage copy ("the enchanting isles of the Aegean"), place names, and literary pastiche.

Phonetic development. Old French isle /is.lə/ became Middle English /iːl(ə)/, the -s- falling silent early, and the Great Vowel Shift gave the modern /aɪl/. The plural isles is pronounced /aɪlz/, identical to iles. The -s- remains purely orthographic — a fossil of Renaissance etymology.

Conclusion. From the salt of Indo-European seas to the Romantic English plural, isles has travelled from a plain geographic noun to a word whose very form signals poetry and nostalgia. Its evolution is a living lesson that register — how a word feels — can be the most important change a word ever undergoes, even when its meaning stands still.
