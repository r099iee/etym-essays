1. Middle English throbbe(n), c. 1400, "to beat, palpitate, throb" - first attested in Middle English; origin recorded as obscure (OED). 2. English throb (verb, intransitive) "to beat strongly and steadily, as the heart or a wounded part" - continuation of the Middle English verb, attested from c. 1400. 3. English throb (noun) "a strong steady pulsation; a beating, especially of the heart or of pain" - attested from the 1570s. 4. English derivatives and compounds: throbbing (participial adjective/noun, "a dull pulsating pain; a pulsing sound"), throbby (informal, 20th c., "pulsing, rhythmic"), heart-throb (16th-17th c., "a beat of the heart"), then heartthrob (20th c., esp. 1960s, "a celebrity who makes the heart throb"). Final node: English throb (noun and verb) "a strong regular pulsation; to beat strongly and steadily". Debate/unknown: The OED and standard dictionaries record the origin as "obscure". The most widely accepted explanation is that throb is echoic / sound-symbolic, coined in English to imitate a dull repeated beat, kin to thud, thump, thwack, thrash. No Proto-Indo-European root is reconstructed, and none should be forced; the word appears to be a native English coinage. The doubled consonant in early spellings (throbbe, throbbing) is typical of expressive, echoic words.
