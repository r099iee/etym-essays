Root core: Unknown / expressive (North Germanic). The English verb builds on an expressive Germanic base *fal- "to move the hands or limbs uncertainly, to grope, to waver, to be at a loss." No reliable Proto-Indo-European etymon has been established; the frequently-cited link to PIE *pel- "to fold" (whence English fold) is rejected by the OED and Etymonline as an unprovable speculation. Linear chain to FALTERED: 1. Expressive Germanic base *fal- "to move the hands or limbs uncertainly, to grope, to waver" (early medieval; no secure PIE ancestor). 2. Old Norse falma "to grope with the hands, to be at a loss, to hesitate" (Viking Age, c. 9th–13th century), the attested Scandinavian verb built on the base. 3. Middle English falten "to stagger, totter, hesitate" (14th century), borrowed from the Scandinavian family (Old Norse falma); the borrowing is the likely proximate source of the English verb. 4. English falter (v.) "to stumble, waver, speak hesitantly" — the frequentative form in -er settles in the language from the 15th–16th century. 5. English faltered (past tense and past participle, regular weak form in -ed) — the exact inflected form on this card. Debated / nuanced notes: - The OED and Etymonline reject any connection to PIE *pel- "to fold" (source of fold, fallow): no morphological path leads from *pel- to falten, and the resemblance is an accident of sound. The word is classed with the expressive vocabulary of uncertain, groping motion. - Latin fallere "to deceive, to cause to stumble" (source of fail, false, fallacy, and related to Greek sphállō "to trip up") is a DIFFERENT root family despite the surface resemblance; it is not a relative of falter. - Old Norse falma survived in the North Germanic languages as Danish/Norwegian famle and Swedish famla "to fumble, grope," confirming the Scandinavian origin of the borrowing; English fumble is an expressive sibling from the same "awkward hand" stock, not a direct descendant of falma.
