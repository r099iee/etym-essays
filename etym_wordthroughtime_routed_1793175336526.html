- Latin — rupta: a broken thing, feminine of ruptus, used for a broken-off detachment or a forced path — breaking as origin.
- Old French — route: both a company of men and their breaking — a band that marches together and the defeat that snaps it; the double sense is born.
- 14th–15th c. — Middle English route, rout: a company, a crowd, a tumult — Chaucer's rout is any noisy gathering, not yet a defeat.
- 16th c. — the military fixing: rout hardens into disorderly flight after defeat, and to rout becomes to inflict it; Shakespeare's battles supply the noise.
- 17th–19th c. — the historian's superlative: every crushing victory is a rout, from Naseby to Waterloo, and the word becomes the standard crown of conquest.
- Today — sport and metaphor: teams are routed on Saturdays, scandals rout ministers from office, while carpenters rout grooves with a tool of the same name but different blood.
