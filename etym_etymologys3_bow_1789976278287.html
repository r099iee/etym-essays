Chain A — bend family (/baʊ/ reverence + /bəʊ/ weapon, knot, fiddle-stick): The root is Proto-Indo-European *bheug- (to bend, to bow, to curve), a securely reconstructed verb with reflexes in Germanic, Baltic and Indo-Iranian for pliable bending. In Proto-Germanic it became *beugan (to bend, strong verb) and *bugon- (a bend, a bow), yielding Old English bugan (to bend, bow down, submit) and boga (archery bow, arch, vault). Beowulf draws a boga; Anglo-Saxon law punishes breaking another's boga. Phonetic path: Germanic u + g vocalised to Middle English bowe, bouwe (bow, c.1200), then Great Vowel Shift split the reflexes by following consonant and usage into modern /baʊ/ (verb to bend the body) and /bəʊ/ (noun weapon, knot). The bending-body sense stays close to submission (to bow to a king, to bow to necessity), the weapon sense stays with elastic wood, the knot sense with looped ribbon, the musical sense (violin bow, 17th century, from same bent stick) with horsehair on a curved shaft.

Chain B — nautical bow (/baʊ/ ship's forepart, your context): Entirely different origin in body-part metaphor. Proto-Indo-European *bheh2ghus meant forearm, upper arm or shoulder (Sanskrit bahu, Greek pekhus). In Proto-Germanic this became *bogaz, *boguz (shoulder, upper arm, shoulder-blade), attested as Old English boga (shoulder — homographic but distinct from boga bow-weapon), Old Norse bogr (shoulder) and Middle Low German boch, Middle Dutch boech (shoulder, and by transferred use the shoulder of a ship — its forepart pushing water). Hanseatic sailors in the North Sea (13th–14th centuries) called the forepart boch; Middle English borrowed it as bowe, bow (ship's bow, late 14th century, c.1390, in naval accounts and Chaucerian contemporaries). The metaphor is transparent: as shoulders lead the body through a crowd, the bow leads the hull through waves. Pronunciation merged with the bending bow as /baʊ/, sealing the homography.

  • 1300s–1500s — coexistence: archery bowe dominates Agincourt-era England (longbow as national weapon); nautical bowe spreads with cogs and caravels; reverential bouwen marks feudal deference. Spellings overlap completely.
  • 1600s–1800s — specialisation: firearms eclipse the longbow (verb to bow as reverence survives, weapon becomes sporting/antiquarian); naval architecture refines bow types (clipper bow, bluff bow, bulbous bow, 19th-century iron hulls); bow tie (19th century) and violin bow fix /bəʊ/ in fashion and music.
  • Modern split: RP stabilises /baʊ/ for ship + body-bend vs /bəʊ/ for weapon + knot + music, the only reliable cue. Orthography never disambiguates; dictionaries list them as separate headwords (bow¹, bow², bow³).

Debate note: older popular etymologies derived the ship's bow from bending planks (*bheug-), making all bows one family. Modern historical linguistics (OED, Kroonen, de Vaan) rejects this and keeps Chain B distinct via shoulder (*bheh2ghus → *bogaz), citing Low German nautical attestations where shoulder is primary. The Old English homograph boga (shoulder vs weapon) already shows the collision. Both PIE roots are secure; only their convergence in modern spelling is accidental.
