1. A native English word. Unlike most words on these cards, comely is not a loan from French or Latin: it is inherited directly from Old English. The chain runs cȳme "beautiful, splendid" → cymlic (with the common adjective suffix -lic, the ancestor of modern -ly) → Middle English comely → modern comely. Where cȳme itself came from is a genuine puzzle: the word has no certain cognates outside English, and specialists debate whether it belongs with a Germanic base of "delicacy, frailty" or with an Indo-European base of "strength, vigour". What matters for the career of the word is that it was present in English before the Norman Conquest, and that it survived the great inflow of French vocabulary — a survival that tells us how central "beauty that is also fitting" was to the everyday moral-aesthetic vocabulary of medieval England.

2. The double life of the word: beauty and seemliness. Even in Old English the adjective's two poles were already visible: cymlic could praise a physical form and a moral quality alike. In Middle English prose and verse — Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, the Canterbury Tales, the anonymous lyrics — comely is constantly applied to the bodies and faces of lovers, to courteous and hospitable conduct, to well-ordered households and well-chosen words. The two senses did not compete; they reinforced one another. A comely person was attractive because well-formed, and well-formed because well-ordered: medieval aesthetics, Christian and courtly at once, had no vocabulary that could more economically express the unity of physical grace and moral order.

3. The early modern centuries: decorum institutionalised. From the 16th to the 18th century comely settled into an almost formulaic pairing with words for fitness and decency — "comely and decent", "comely and seemly" — and was used of dress, architecture, ceremonial, and language. The King James Bible uses it of proper order in worship ("all things done decently and in order"); Puritan and Anglican moralists alike made the comely the visible sign of the regulated life. In the same period the word acquired its occasional sense of "kindly, gracious" of persons, and it passed into proverbs of social conduct. Its register was never elevated or learned; it was the plain, warm word of household and parish, of the beauty one could trust.

4. The nineteenth century: wholesome beauty in the novel. Romantic and Victorian novelists found in comely exactly the quiet, unspectacular attractiveness their moral imagination prized. In Scott, Austen, and George Eliot, comely girls and comely cottages, comely manners and comely old men fill the pages as the visual signature of decency: beauty measured by character. This is the literary climate in which Pater writes. When he calls for "a more liberal and comely way of conceiving life" he is reaching back past the prudential Victorian use to the older double sense — a conception of life that would be generous, beautiful, and fitting — and quietly reminding his readers that aesthetics and ethics, in the great tradition of the Renaissance, were supposed to be one.

5. The twentieth century and today: a word in retreat with a fixed smile. After 1900 comely receded from everyday speech. The causes are familiar: the rise of the plain, neutral vocabulary of "attractive" and "good-looking"; the suspicion that moral beauty is a sentimental fiction; and the sheer archaism of a word that does the work of two. Yet it never quite vanished. It survives in literary prose, in period fiction, in formal and legal-sounding phrases, and in the affectionate parody of old-fashioned praise. Used today, it is knowingly antique: it trades on its refusal to separate the pretty from the proper. In Pater's sentence it is neither archaism nor decoration but the precise instrument of his central claim — that the Renaissance idealised a life whose beauty was also its rightness, and that a culture loses something essential when it can no longer call that unity comely.
