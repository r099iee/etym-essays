Flummoxed means utterly bewildered, baffled, or thrown off mental balance — so thoroughly perplexed that clear reaction or planning fails. It is the past tense and past participle of the slang verb flummox (“to perplex, confound, defeat completely”), and in its participial-adjective use it describes a person reduced to a state of helpless confusion. Bilbo, feeling positively flummoxed, is not merely surprised: he is scrambled. Gandalf's knowledge of the larders, the dwarves' appetite, and the sudden rewriting of a quiet evening combine into a colloquial, almost comic collapse of composure. The intensifier positively underlines completeness — no half-confusion, but full disorientation, the word arriving at exactly the moment when a respectable hobbit has run out of internal commentary. The effect is of orderly mental furniture knocked over all at once: not a single puzzling fact but a cascade of them, each new impossibility landing before the last has been processed, until the mind gives up and simply gapes.

The word's polysemy is narrow but real, and the senses shade into one another. First, the mental sense, by far the most common: “to baffle, perplex, nonplus”, and hence flummoxed = “utterly at a loss”. Second, the physical dialect sense preserved in English dialect records: “to throw down untidily, to make a mess, to fling clumsily” — a physical tumbling or mussing that is widely thought to be the older meaning, from which the mental sense developed by metaphor (knocked into a heap → wits in a heap). Third, the noun use a flummox (as in “in a flummox”, a state of confusion) and the rare agent noun flummoxer (one who confounds). Fourth, in modern informal and gaming usage, to flummox an opponent is to outmanoeuvre or wrong-foot them completely — the word's playful, competitive edge. The semantic bridge across all senses is the image of something — a pile of belongings, a train of thought, an opponent — being knocked into disorder and brought to a halt.

The synonym boundaries are worth drawing sharply. Perplexed is the Latinate, intellectual default — puzzled by a problem that still admits of thought; flummoxed is blunter and more physical, suggesting the mind itself has been jostled into a heap, not merely occupied. Bewildered (from be- + wilder) implies being lost in an unfamiliar landscape, directionless; flummoxed implies being beaten down by something absurd or impenetrable rather than genuinely lost. Confounded carries an old-fashioned imprecatory force (confound it!) and a hint of being brought to shame or ruin; flummoxed is purely comic and colloquial, with none of that damnation. Baffled (from Old French bafouer, to mock) suggests being stopped at a single barrier, a puzzle that defeats the attempt; flummoxed is looser, the whole mind in a heap rather than halted at one wall. And nonplussed (literally ‘no more’, from Latin non plus) is the nearest in meaning — utterly at a loss — but it is bookish and Latinate where flummoxed is slangy and auditory, a word that sounds like its own effect: flum-mox, a soft, silly collapse of the wits.

Register, collocation, and usage. Flummoxed takes the intensifiers positively, absolutely, completely, totally, quite, or the semi-archaic clean, and the verb builds flummoxed by: flummoxed by the question, by the sudden change, by the mathematics. Register is informal, conversational, faintly old-fashioned, and gently jocular — at home in dialogue, light narrative, and British comedy of manners, and slightly incongruous in formal prose, which is precisely why Tolkien lets it bruise the narration at this moment. In the nineteenth century it belonged to slang and dialect, the province of servants' gossip and stage farce; by Tolkien's day it had mellowed into a genteel colloquialism with a country-house flavour, ideal for a hobbit whose dignity is intact but whose composure has been knocked sideways — the exact register of a crisis that is real but cannot, by the rules of politeness, be described as one.

  • Mental sense (dominant): utterly baffled, perplexed, nonplussed; at a complete loss.
  • Physical dialect sense (older): to throw down untidily, make a mess, fling clumsily — the semantic seed of the mental sense.
  • Noun and agent forms: a flummox (a state of confusion); flummoxer (one who confounds).
  • Modern informal / competitive: to outmanoeuvre or wrong-foot an opponent completely.
