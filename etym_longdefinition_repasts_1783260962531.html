repasts (noun, plural) — In this programmatic passage from Macaulay's History of England, the word forms part of a catalogue of everyday life: "the revolutions which have taken place in dress, furniture, repasts, and public amusements." Macaulay is announcing a new kind of history — the history of the people, not merely of government — and repasts is his deliberately dignified term for meals. The nuance is significant: a "meal" is neutral and modern, whereas a "repast" carries a studied, literary formality, an invitation to imagine not merely food but the whole social ritual of eating — how tables were set, what courses followed, who sat with whom, what customs governed hospitality. In context the plural does important work, gesturing at a long series of successive generations, each with its own table customs, and at "revolutions" (Macaulay's pointed word) in diet and dining that historians too often pass by. The tone is at once elegant and democratic: by placing repasts beside dress, furniture, and amusements, Macaulay dignifies the material culture of ordinary life as a fit subject for serious history.

Core sense: a repast is a meal — an occasion of taking food — and, in its verbal heritage, the act of taking food itself. The word's full range:

  • Meal / refreshment. The central sense: a meal, especially one taken formally or at a set time. A repast may be grand or frugal — the word marks register, not size: "a light repast", "a hasty repast", "an evening repast", "a substantial repast".
  • The act of taking food. The older, slightly abstract sense preserved in the dictionary gloss "the act of taking food" — eating as an occasion, from the Late Latin repascere "to feed again". In the verb form, to repast once meant "to take food".
  • Formal / literary register. Repast is a consciously elevated synonym for meal, alive in novels of manners, historical essays, and ceremonial prose; using it turns eating into an event, dignifying the humble act.
  • Figurative nourishment. In literary and spiritual usage, repast can denote intellectual or spiritual nourishment — "a repast for the mind", "a mental repast" — food for thought, an extension of the feeding image into the life of the mind.
  • Family words. The noun's kin include refection (a light meal, from Latin reficere "to restore"), and the rare adjective repastive; Modern French retains repas as the ordinary word for "meal", so French speakers hear no elevation where English speakers hear ceremony.
  • Obsolescent and jocular use. In modern English repast is consciously old-fashioned, used for effect or humour — a dinner invitation phrased as "a repast" signals wit or formality. The word refuses to be ordinary about eating.

The historical bridge is the shepherd's feeding. Latin pascere meant "to feed, graze, pasture" — to put flocks out to graze, the herder's daily task; repascere added the prefix re- ("again") to mean "to feed again, to nourish anew, to restore with food". The past participle repastus, "fed again", was turned into a noun for a meal: every dinner table is, etymologically, a pasture renewed. The word rose from sheep-herding to the dining rooms of the nation, and its elevated English tone reflects that long, bookish journey through Old French and Middle English — a word that began as the plain feeding of animals ended as the ceremonious name for human dining.

Register and collocation: repast appears in formulaic qualifiers — a light repast, a hasty repast, a substantial repast, an evening repast, a frugal repast, the daily repast. It is virtually always a count noun and usually singular, so the plural repasts in Macaulay is a deliberate, slightly old-fashioned flourish. Its register is formal and literary — the word lives in novels of manners, historical essays, and ceremonial prose rather than in conversation. Macaulay, who relished a touch of stately diction even when describing the dinner table, chose the word precisely because it turns a mundane fact of life into an object of historical study, the daily meal of a people into a chapter of its civilisation.
