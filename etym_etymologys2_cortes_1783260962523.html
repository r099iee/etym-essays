Derivation chain for CORTES (proper noun; Macaulay's anglicized spelling of the Spanish surname Cortés): 1. Proto-Indo-European *ǵʰer- "to enclose, encircle, surround" (Pokorny *gher-; the root of enclosed places — gardens, yards, courts), c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE. 2. PIE *ǵʰortós "an enclosed place, yard, fence" (a substantivized adjective of *ǵʰer-). 3. Latin hortus "garden, enclosed ground" (attested from the 2nd c. BCE; the same root as Greek khórtos "enclosed farmyard, fodder" and Germanic *garda- "enclosure, yard"). 4. Latin cohors, cohortis (also cors, cortis) "an enclosed yard; a company, band, retinue; a military cohort" (com- "together" + hortus "enclosure" — "those shut in together"), attested 2nd c. BCE. 5. Vulgar Latin *cōrtem / *cortem (accusative) "court, courtyard, retinue" — the spoken-Latin development of cohors; the source of the Romance court-words. 6. Old Spanish corte "royal court; capital" — with the plural cortes "courts, assemblies, the estates-general of Castile/Spain," attested from the medieval period. 7. Spanish cortés "courteous, courtly, polite" (corte + -és "of the court") — the adjective from which the surname is most commonly derived; alternatively derived directly from cortes. 8. [Surname] Cortés "the courteous one / the man of the court" — hereditary surname of the conqueror; English spelling CORTES (accent dropped) in English historical prose from the 16th century onward (Macaulay, Prescott). DEBATED / NUANCED: Two derivations are current, both converging on cohors: (a) from the adjective cortés "courteous," the most widely accepted; (b) from the plural cortes "the assemblies/courts," as a name for a man associated with the court. A false etymology connecting the name to Latin curtus "cut short" (Spanish cortar "to cut") is to be rejected.
