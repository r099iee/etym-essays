The history of predilection is a history of love ranked and ordered. It begins in two Proto-Indo-European roots that never met until Latin joined them: *per- / *preh₂- meaning forwardness and priority in space and time, and *leg- meaning to gather, collect, and by extension to pick out and choose. Latin prae meaning before, in front of, and beforehand preserved the first root in countless compounds expressing precedence. Latin legere meaning to gather, cull, pick out, and eventually to read (as in gathering letters with the eyes) preserved the second. When Latin prefixed dis- in its intensive sense to legere it produced diligere, literally to single out and value highly, which by the classical period meant to esteem, prize, and love with discriminating affection, distinct from amare which was warmer and more passionate.

Early Christian and Medieval Latin moral theology needed a noun for the act of loving one thing before or above another, especially God's preferential love for the elect. It therefore formed praedilectio, praedilectionem from prae- plus dilectio, meaning a preferring beforehand, a special and prior love. The form is first visible in ecclesiastical writers discussing predestination, divine favour, and the preferential love owed to virtue. It carried no sense of mere whim; it denoted a settled, reasoned, even providential preference. Old French did not widely adopt it, but sixteenth-century Middle French humanists and theologians revived it as prédilection, meaning partiality, predilection, especially God's predilection for Israel or for saints. Cotgrave's 1611 French-English dictionary glosses it as a fore-love or special favour.

  • Prae: from space to rank: Latin prae moved from local before to temporal beforehand to evaluative above, so that prae-diligere naturally meant to love above, to set before in esteem. English preserves this in prefer, premise, predict, and predominance.
  • Legere: from harvest to choice: PIE *leg- meant to gather crops or sticks; in Greek legein and Latin legere it extended to gathering words, hence logic, lecture, legend, election, select, collect, and neglect. Diligere thus originally meant to gather apart in the mind, to discriminate in favour of.
  • Dilectio versus amor: Medieval moralists distinguished dilectio as rational, elective love from amor as affective, desiring love. A predilection was therefore dignified: a preference of judgment, not a spasm of appetite.
  • French mediation: The word entered English not directly from Latin but through French prédilection in the seventeenth century, when French court and salon language supplied English with nuanced terms for taste: penchant, predilection, liaison, nuance itself.

English first records predilection in the 1770s, notably in political and literary prose. Horace Walpole, Chesterfield's heirs, and later Samuel Johnson's circle used it for a leaning toward a party, author, or pursuit. Unlike prejudice, which had darkened into pre-judgment against, predilection kept a mildly favourable, indulgent colour. Nineteenth-century novelists — Jane Austen, George Eliot, Henry James — made it a signature word for characterisation: a heroine's predilection for Italian music, a clergyman's predilection for port, a collector's predilection for first editions. It signalled a durable, slightly idiosyncratic bent that explained behaviour without condemning it. Darwin used it in The Descent of Man for animals' sexual predilections, extending it into biology.

Phonetically the word is fully naturalised but retains a French-Latinate profile: /ˌpriːdɪˈlɛkʃən/ with stress on -lec- and reduction of the first two vowels, the -tion pronounced as shun. The spelling with single r and -ilection (not -election) betrays the diligere root rather than eligere, though the two are cousins. Modern usage distinguishes two constructions: a predilection for something (most common: a predilection for spicy food) and a predilection toward(s) or in favour of. In law, psychology, and medicine it denotes predisposition without determinism: a predilection for risk, for violence, for sugary drinks. In arts criticism it is almost affectionate: a director's predilection for long takes. No major semantic pejoration has occurred; if anything the word has softened from theological election to polite euphemism for a habit one half-apologises for, as in Will Durant's phrase a passionate predilection for blood as delicacy, where the clash between polite noun and savage appetite creates deliberate irony.
