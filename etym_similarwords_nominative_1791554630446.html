• Subjective: Similar because subjective case is the modern grammatical term synonymous with nominative in English school grammar (identifying subjects like I, he, she); differs because subjective is a broader functional term used across non-inflected syntactic systems, while nominative is the historical, morphological term in classical and comparative linguistics.
  • Naming: Similar because both relate to the act of calling or identifying by a label; differs because naming is an active, everyday Anglo-Saxon participle/gerund, while nominative is a formal Latinate grammatical and legal classification.
  • Nominal: Similar because both share the Latin root nomen ('name'); differs because nominal means existing in name only (e.g., a nominal fee, a nominal leader) or refers broadly to noun phrases, whereas nominative specifically designates the subjective grammatical case or registered shares.
  • Denominative: Similar because both derive from verbs of naming; differs because denominative is a specialized morphological term for verbs or adjectives derived directly from a noun (e.g., to cloud from cloud).
  • Casus Rectus: Similar because both identify the upright, fundamental grammatical case; differs because casus rectus ('the upright case') is the philosophical Latin category contrasting with all oblique cases (accusative, genitive, dative, ablative), which were seen as 'falling away' from the nominative.
  • Appointee: Similar because both relate to nomination; differs because an appointee is the person who has received an office or position, while nominative is an adjective describing the naming process or case form.
  • Titular: Similar because both involve formal designations or titles; differs because titular denotes holding an official title without real authority or practical duties (e.g., titular ruler), unlike nominative's grammatical or financial registration.
