TOILED is the past tense and past participle of the verb toil — to work hard, continuously, and exhaustingly, typically at physical labour, under strain or duress. "Toiled" describes the completed act of laborious effort: "Farmers toiled under the hot sun", "He toiled for years to complete his book". The word stresses the difficulty, duration, and cost of the work — the sweat and weariness — rather than its skill or reward. Its tone is dignified and often literary; unlike "worked", it insists that the effort was heavy and unrelenting. In the context passage of this card ("I toiled in service, I stole") the word carries the full weight of exhausting servitude and labour.

THE MODERN SENSES IN FULL

  • Literal, physical labour (core): to work long and hard at heavy or menial tasks — "toiled in the fields", "toiled at the loom", "toiled in the mines". The sense of oppressed or grinding work is common in labour history and social writing.
  • Of movement through difficulty: to move with great effort and exhaustion, as through snow, uphill, or rough terrain — "they toiled upward through the drifting snow". Here the verb is applied to a body moving, not to a task.
  • The noun "toil": hard, tiring work or exertion — "years of toil", "the toil of the harvest", "a life of toil". (The plural "toils" meaning "snares, nets" is a different word — see the etymology note.)
  • Figurative, of enterprises and processes: "toiled" of institutions, negotiations, or efforts that grind on painfully — "the talks toiled through the winter".
  • Literary idiom "toil and trouble": Shakespeare's witches in Macbeth chant "Double, double toil and trouble" (IV.i) — the alliterative pairing of exhausting labour with calamity, now a proverbial phrase.
  • Derived forms: "toilsome" (laborious, wearisome), "toilworn" (worn out by labour), "toiler" (one who toils), "toilfully" (rare), and the pair "toil and moil" (an archaic doublet meaning to labour painfully).
  • Register note: "toiled" is formal-to-literary, neutral or even admiring in tone; it dignifies hard work without glamorising it, and is at home in narrative prose, history-writing, and set literary phrases.

THE HISTORICAL BRIDGE: FROM CRUSHING OLIVES TO WEARY LABOUR

"Toil" entered English from Old French, where toillier meant "to stir about, to agitate, to rumple or soil"; behind it lay Late Latin tūdiculāre, "to pound, crush, or stir with a tudicula" — a small mallet or the instrument used for crushing and stirring olives. The physical action of stirring, crushing, and churning — the agitated motion of pounding and mixing — carried the idea of exhausting effort. In Anglo-French the word toiler came to mean "to struggle, to contend, to be harassed", and Middle English toilen (13th c.) took on "to pull about, to struggle, to labour". By the 14th century the sense had settled into the modern one: hard, continuous, wearying labour. The semantic bridge runs from the crushed and stirred olive to the labourer worn down by effort: the root sense of "beating and churning" became the sense of "struggling and toiling".

THE PAST TENSE AND ITS USE

As a past tense, "toiled" reports completed exertion and is used with durations ("toiled for years"), locations ("toiled in the fields"), and adverbs of manner ("toiled ceaselessly", "toiled grimly"). It also works as a participle adjective of a person or life spent labouring ("a toiled and weary hand"). It is less common in casual speech, where "worked hard", "slaved away", or "slogged" are preferred, and it retains a stately, almost biblical ring in phrases such as "they that toiled" or "all his toiled years".
