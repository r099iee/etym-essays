• Stage 1 — Origin uncertain: no PIE root and no securely attested pre-Middle-English ancestor can be reconstructed; the word is very probably echoic or imitative, its shape suggesting the whirr of a rushing or thrown body.
  • Stage 2 — Continental West Germanic (possible source): Middle Low German hurreln "to hurl, push violently" and Middle Dutch hurlen "to hurl, rush" (attested 13th c.); cf. German hurren "to move quickly, hurry" — a family of expressive hurry-and-rush words.
  • Stage 3 — Middle English hurlen "to rush headlong, dash" and "to hurl, fling with force" (first attested c. 1225–1300).
  • Stage 4 — Modern English hurl "to throw with great force and violence" (15th c. onward).
  • Stage 5 — Modern English hurling (present participle of hurl; also the noun for the Irish stick-and-ball game, 19th c.) — the root word itself.
Note: The OED treats the origin as uncertain, "possibly of imitative origin." The Middle English form already carries both the intransitive "rush" sense and the transitive "fling" sense. Hurling is a separate word from the later verb hurtle (also of obscure origin), though the two have influenced each other for centuries; no stage here is invented, and the possible Low German or Dutch borrowing is stated as a hypothesis, not a certainty.
