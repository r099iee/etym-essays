Derivation chain for MUSH (root word one of two): Stage 1 — (Origin debated; no secure PIE root.) Old English māscan "to crush, pound, or mix into a soft pulp" (attested by the 10th–11th century), of Germanic stock; the related noun māsc meant a pulpy mixture, especially the mash used in brewing. Stage 2 — Middle English mashen / masken "to mash, to crush to pulp" (12th–15th century), continuing the Old English verb. Stage 3 — Modern English mash (noun and verb, 16th century onward): "to crush into a soft pulp," and the pulpy mass so made. Stage 4 — English mush (17th–18th century), a dialectal variant of mash, meaning "a soft pulpy mass"; in North America from the 18th century specifically "boiled cornmeal porridge," possibly reinforced by German Mus "soft food, porridge" brought by Pennsylvania Dutch settlers. Stage 5 — Modern English mush "a soft pulpy mass; cornmeal porridge; cloying sentimental material" — TARGET. Note on the debate: the ultimate origin of Germanic māscan is uncertain. Some scholars connect it with the PIE root *mag- "to knead" (cf. Greek mássein "to knead," the source of English "mass" and "masticate" via Latin), but the connection is not secure, and the word may be of imitative origin. The influence of German Mus on the American porridge sense is plausible but not proven. Derivation chain for RICKETY (root word two of two): Stage 1 — Ancient Greek rháchis "spine, backbone" (attested from the 5th century BCE onward; also "ridge" and, in a sea term, the line of surf on the shore). Stage 2 — Ancient Greek rhachîtis (also rachitis) "inflammation or curvature of the spine," the medical name of what we now call rickets — a formation from rháchis attested in the Greek medical writers of late antiquity (e.g. Galen, 2nd century CE). Stage 3 — Medieval and scientific Latin rhachitis / rachitis "rickets," the learned medical name of the disease (16th century). Stage 4 — English rickets "the disease of softened, deformed bones in children" (17th century, c. 1630s); the English form rickets is probably an alteration of Latin rachitis influenced by native English words, though the exact pathway is debated. Stage 5 — English rickety "affected with rickets; hence weak, shaky, unsound, liable to collapse" (17th–18th century; the generalized "shaky structure" sense becomes dominant from the 19th century). Stage 6 — Modern English rickety "shaky or likely to collapse" — TARGET. Note on the debate: the origin of English "rickets" is uncertain. The usual account derives it from Greek rhachitis via Latin, but the distinctive English spelling rick- suggests contamination with native words such as "wricket" (to wriggle) or regional forms, and a wholly native Germanic origin has also been proposed. What is certain is that the adjective rickety derives from the disease name rickets.
