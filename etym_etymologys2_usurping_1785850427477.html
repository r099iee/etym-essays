The target word USURPING is the present participle and gerund of English usurp, so the chain below traces the verb to its base and then notes the -ing form. Derivation chain for usurp (base of usurping): 1. Proto-Indo-European *rep- 'to snatch, to seize, to carry off' (traditional reconstruction, Pokorny; posited as the base of Latin rapere 'to seize'. The reconstruction is conventional — the root is also written *rēp-, and its exact shape is debated — but the Latin family is securely attested). 2. Latin rapere (verb) 'to seize, to snatch, to carry off, to plunder' (attested from the 2nd c. BCE; the same root yields Latin rapina 'plunder' and the participial stem rapt-, seen in raptus 'snatched, seized'; English descendants include rape, rapid, rapture, ravish, rapt). 3. Latin ūsūrpāre (verb) 'to take into use, to make use of, to claim, to assume, to seize for oneself' (first attested in the 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero; a compound of ūsus 'use, employment' (the past participle of ūtī 'to use') + rapere 'to seize' — literally 'to seize for one's own use'. IMPORTANT NOTE: the first element ūsus / ūtī 'to use' has NO secure Indo-European etymology; de Vaan records no convincing PIE root for it, and traditional guesses remain unproven — the uncertainty belongs to the first element only, while the second element rapere has the solid root given in stage 1). 4. Old French usurper (verb) 'to usurp, to seize wrongfully, to take possession of by force' (attested from the 12th c.; from Latin ūsūrpāre, with the vowel of the initial syllable reduced and the medial cluster simplified in Gallo-Romance). 5. Middle English usurpen (verb) 'to usurp, to seize wrongfully' (attested from the 14th c.; borrowed from Anglo-French / Old French; the English form drops the infinitive ending of the Old French -re). 6. Modern English usurp (verb) 'to seize and hold (power, office, rights, etc.) by force or without legal right' (spelling settled in the 16th c.), and its present participle and gerund USURPING (usurp + the participial/gerundial suffix -ing, native English, from Old English -unge / -ende). Note on the two elements: because usurp is itself a compound of ūsus 'use' and rapere 'seize', the derivation properly has two tributaries — the 'seize' element has a solid PIE root (*rep-), while the 'use' element (Latin ūtī, ūsus) has no accepted PIE reconstruction and must be marked as unknown. English, however, borrowed the whole compound from Old French, so English speakers inherit usurp without ever assembling its two parts.
