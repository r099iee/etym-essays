Proto-Germanic *harpǽ / *harpō “harp, stringed instrument” (reconstructed, c. 500 CE; reflected in all early Germanic branches) → Old English hearpe “harp, the plucked instrument of the hall” (attested 8th–11th c.) → Middle English harpe “harp” (attested 12th–15th c.) → Modern English HARP “the framed plucked instrument; also the verb ‘to harp on’” (present day). Debated / nuanced note: The word is pan-Germanic cultural vocabulary — Old Norse harpa, Old High German harpha (modern German Harfe), and Middle Dutch harpe are the well-attested cognates beside Old English hearpe; Gothic does not attest it. Beyond Proto-Germanic the trail is uncertain: most handbooks mark the ultimate origin as unknown, treating the word as a Germanic culture-word (possibly of pre-Germanic substrate origin); older speculation deriving it from a sense of “hook, pluck, grip” (bending or seizing strings) has no consensus, and there is no accepted Indo-European cognate set outside Germanic. It is certainly not a loan from Latin cithara or Greek kithara (those feed the zither/guitar lines) and not a Romance word. The historically attested portion (Old English hearpe through modern harp) is secure.
