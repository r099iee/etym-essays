parataxis is a term of grammar, rhetoric and stylistics denoting the arrangement of clauses, phrases or sentences side by side in coordinate or juxtaposed sequence without explicit subordination — clause placed beside clause, each grammatically independent, the logical relation left to inference, rhythm or mere and. Its textbook opposite is hypotaxis, where one clause is subordinated to another by because, although, when, which and the hierarchy is marked. Caesar's Veni, vidi, vici — I came, I saw, I conquered — is pure parataxis; so is Hemingway's The rain stopped and the wind rose and the clouds broke, and the King James And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep.

In the complaint at issue — students who cannot handle hypotaxis and parataxis — the pair stands for elementary stylistic literacy: the ability to name whether a prose period builds by nesting or by chaining. To lose the terms is, the author argues, to lose perception itself; we praise clarity or complexity without knowing which syntactic architecture produces the effect. Parataxis here is not exotic jargon but ordinary equipment, like periodic sentence, that graduate study ought to command.

The descriptive range is wide and domain-sensitive:

  • Grammatical core: coordination without subordination — He ran, she laughed; bread and circuses; fast, cheap, good. Markers, if any, are coordinators (and, or, but) or mere punctuation (comma, semicolon, full stop). Relation — cause, sequence, contrast — is unmarked.
  • Rhetorical-stylistic effect: swiftness, bluntness, objectivity, biblical starkness, childlike immediacy, or modernist flatness. Paratactic style is often called additive, aggregative, democratic: each unit weighs the same. Critics contrast its drumbeat with hypotactic swell.
  • Literary-historical associations: Old Testament narrative, Anglo-Saxon chronicle, folk tale, Caesar, Defoe's plain record, Hemingway, Carver, didactic and journalistic plainness, and oral storytelling. Each era reinvents parataxis as honesty against hypotactic artifice — or as poverty against hypotactic richness, depending on taste.
  • Linguistic-typological use: languages and stages described as paratactic when they favour juxtaposition over embedding; child language, pidgins, early writing and some oral cultures are characterised as paratactic before hypotactic machinery develops.
  • Poetic and philosophical extension: paratactic image-lists in Pound and Williams, paratactic aphorism in Nietzsche and Adorno — thoughts set side by side without connective tissue, inviting the reader to supply hierarchy. Here parataxis becomes ethic: refusal to subordinate.

It is crucial not to confuse parataxis with mere shortness or simplicity. A paratactic sentence may be very long — I bought bread and I bought milk and I bought eggs and I walked home and ... — while a hypotactic sentence may be brief — Because he ran, she laughed. The distinction is architectural, not quantitative: whether relations are stated by subordinators and embedding or left to juxtaposition. Skilled prose mixes both: periodic hypotaxis to suspend and resolve, paratactic hammer-blows to assert and close. Misuse arises when parataxis is taken to mean bad or primitive; linguists stress it is a choice with costs and gains — vivid immediacy and ambiguity purchased at the price of explicit logic — not a developmental failure.

Pedagogically, parataxis is diagnosed by stripping connectives: if you can replace commas and ands with full stops without grammatical wreckage, the structure is paratactic; if removal orphans a which-clause or a because-clause, it is hypotactic. Mastery lets a critic explain why two passages of equal length feel utterly different: one marches, the other climbs.
