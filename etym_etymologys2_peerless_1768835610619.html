PIE *per- "to allot, assign, apportion — hence a share; also 'to pass through'" (reconstructed c. 4500–2500 BCE) → Latin par "equal" — "evenly apportioned" (the traditional derivation, whose exact semantic mechanism is debated; attested 2nd c. BCE) → Old French per "an equal; a noble of equal rank" (9th–11th c.) → Middle English pere "an equal; a peer" (12th–13th c.) → English peer "an equal; a nobleman of equal rank" (13th c.) → English peerless (c. 1300) — peer + -less, "without a peer" → English peerless (present day) Cognates and relatives within the same economy of shares: Latin paria "equal things" → Old French paire → English pair; Latin comparāre "to put on an equal footing" → English compare; Latin dispar "unequal" → English disparity; Latin pars "part, a share" → English part; Latin portio "portion" → English portion; and English par "the standard level" (18th century, including the golf sense). NOTE ON DEBATE/NUANCE: the derivation of Latin par from PIE *per- is traditional but the semantic mechanism ("evenly apportioned") is debated; some scholars remain agnostic about a PIE link for par. The English history from Old French per onward, however, is secure.
