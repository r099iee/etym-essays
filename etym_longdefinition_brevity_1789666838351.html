BREVITY means conciseness of expression, shortness of duration, or compactness of form: using few words, lasting a short time, or occupying little space. Its most celebrated use is stylistic — brevity in writing or speech — epitomized by Polonius's the brevity is the soul of wit and the modern mantra brevity is clarity. In the source sentence — Baker was never afraid of brevity, repetition or stating the obvious — it denotes a literary virtue: the willingness to write short, plain, even blunt sentences (Nothing happened) rather than inflating them, and to trust that a brief statement can carry full weight.

More broadly, brevity covers three related shortnesses that English groups under one noun while other languages sometimes split: brevity of speech or text (conciseness), brevity of time (brief duration, as in the brevity of life, the brevity of a visit), and brevity of form (compactness, as in the brevity of a pamphlet). All share the schema of much contained in little, whether words, time, or matter.

  • Primary sense — conciseness in language: the quality of expressing much in few words; terseness without obscurity. A brief is short; brevity is the abstract quality of being brief. Examples: admirable brevity, military brevity, legal brevity. The semantic bridge from Latin brevis short is direct: physical shortness mapped onto discourse length. Praise or blame depends on context: brevity as wit versus brevity as abruptness. Collocates: brevity and clarity, brevity and precision, extreme brevity, models of brevity.
  • Secondary sense — shortness of time: brief duration, transience. The brevity of human life, the brevity of summer, the brevity of fame. Here the bridge is from spatial shortness to temporal shortness, a universal metaphor (a short rope → a short meeting). This sense is often elegiac: to invoke brevity is to invoke mortality, as in medieval contemptus mundi and modern memento mori. It underwrites fixed phrases: life's little brevity, the brevity of the encounter.
  • Tertiary senses — compactness, abridgement, summary: (a) shortness of physical extent (the brevity of the Wittenberg edition is rare and bookish); (b) a concise statement or epitome, now largely superseded by brief and summary but surviving in legal history (brevities for memoranda); (c) in phonetics and prosody, the short quantity of a vowel or syllable (brevity versus length), a technical survival of the Latin quantitative sense. Each preserves a different facet of brevis: extent, content, duration.
  • Stylistic and rhetorical nuance: brevity belongs to a family of concision-terms with distinct edges: brevity is neutral-positive (few words, well chosen); conciseness stresses density; terseness adds polish and slight brusqueness; laconicism implies Spartan reticence; pithiness implies forceful substance; curtness implies rude shortness; succinctness implies orderly compression. Baker's brevity in the source is the plain, courageous kind: short declarative sentences, repetition, and obviousness embraced as honesty rather than avoided as faults.
  • Domain usages: in law, brevity is a professional duty (briefs must be brief); in journalism, brevity is survival (headlines, leads); in military communication, brevity codes compress messages; in liturgy, brevity serves attention (the brevity of collects); in social media, brevity is enforced (character limits) and aestheticized (micro-fiction). The word thus moves between virtue, constraint, and technique.
  • Grammar and collocation: uncountable abstract noun (much brevity, not *a brevity, except in the obsolete memorandum sense). Governed by of (brevity of expression) and for (a gift for brevity). Modified by admiring adjectives (elegant, admirable, masterly) or critical ones (excessive, misleading, curt). Proverbial frame: brevity is the soul of wit (Hamlet).

The paradox Baker exploits is that brevity and repetition, ostensibly opposites, are allies: short units repeated (Nothing happened … Nothing happens) gain gravity through recurrence. Brevity supplies the hammer-blow; repetition supplies the rhythm. To be unafraid of brevity is to be unafraid of silence around the words — the white space in which watching, waiting, and honesty happen.
