CAFTAN in its core modern sense means a long, loose, ankle-length garment with long wide sleeves, worn over other clothes, originally a men's tunic in Turkey, Persia, Egypt and Central Asia and now also a women's fashion dress and a Russian peasant coat. In the present context a speaker contrasts rich merchants in boots and fur coats with his own plain linden bark shoes and wretched gray caftan: the word marks poverty, humility and native Russian dress against expensive imported furs. The cut is straight, collarless or small-collared, opening down the front and closed by buttons, hooks, a sash or a belt, made in cotton, wool, linen, silk or brocade according to wealth.

The garment sense divides into three major cultural applications that must be distinguished. First, the Middle Eastern and Ottoman caftan as robe of honour, often silk or velvet, richly patterned, presented by rulers. Second, the Russian kaftan as long-skirted everyday coat of peasants, artisans, coachmen and merchants, usually coarse grey or brown cloth, a symbol of traditional life before Peter the Great ordered Western dress. Third, the Western fashion caftan from the 1950s onward as a loose women's dress, kaftan dress or beach cover-up, often brightly printed, with no buttons and no sash, valued for comfort and exoticism. All share length, looseness and sleeves, but differ in gender, fastening, fabric and social signal.

  • Middle Eastern robe: ankle-length tunic, long sleeves, sash-tied, silk or cotton, male courtly and religious wear, also a ceremonial honour robe.
  • Russian peasant coat: long coat of coarse wool, hooked or buttoned, grey or dark, worn by peasants and townsfolk, sign of poverty or conservatism in nineteenth century literature.
  • Modern fashion caftan: loose unstructured dress for women, often V-necked, brightly patterned, worn at home, on holiday or as evening wear, signalling relaxed luxury.
  • Extended and metaphorical uses: any long flowing over-garment likened to a caftan, and in costume history a quilted under-armour coat, reflecting the Persian sense of mail coat.

Grammatically CAFTAN is a countable noun, plural caftans, also spelled kaftan, kaftans. Collocations include silk caftan, velvet caftan, embroidered caftan, peasant caftan, Moroccan caftan, kaftan dress. Register is neutral to slightly exotic; in Russian-translation contexts it carries ethnographic colour, in fashion contexts glamorous leisure. To call a coat wretched or gray, as here, activates the peasant pole; to call it silk or Ottoman activates honour and luxury.

Polysemy bridges are historical rather than metaphorical. The Russian sense developed because the same Turkish garment type was adopted northward as ordinary outerwear, losing luxury while keeping form. The fashion sense developed by analogy of form: Western designers borrowed the silhouette, not the function, keeping looseness and length while dropping sashes, hooks and male association. The armour-coat gloss survives only in dictionaries from Persian khaftan as mail coat, bridged by quilting: a thick quilted robe protects like light armour. No verb sense exists; to caftan is not standard.
