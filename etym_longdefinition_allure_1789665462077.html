ALLURE as a noun denotes the power to attract, entice or fascinate through charm, beauty, mystery or promised advantage; as a verb it means to entice or tempt by such means. Your context — That, surely, is the allure of all field study — uses the noun in its purest appreciative sense: the magnetic pull that keeps a naturalist returning to cold marshes before dawn, not for pay but for the promise of encounter. It is positive, almost reverent, yet it retains a shadow of its falconry origin: what allures also captures.

The origin explains the duality. Old French aleurer meant to train a hawk to the lure (leurre), a padded decoy of leather and feathers swung to recall the bird. To allure was thus to draw back by offering something desired, with artifice built in. Middle English aluren (c. 1400) already extends this to people: to tempt by the offer of advantage or pleasure, to win over, to seduce. From enticement-act the noun develops for enticement-quality: the allure of the stage, of travel, of wild places. Modern use oscillates between innocent magnetism and calculated seduction depending on context and adjective.

  • Enchanting quality, power to attract (primary noun): the allure of the coast, mysterious allure, rugged allure. Bridge: from the act of luring to the property that lures; the falconer's tool becomes an aura.
  • To entice or tempt (primary verb): allured by high salaries, allured into speculation. Bridge: direct continuation of aleurer — to offer bait, whether worms, wages or wonder.
  • Romantic and sexual fascination: feminine allure, masculine allure, alluring glance. Bridge: narrowing to interpersonal charm; the lure is beauty and manner, the capture is attention or affection.
  • Commercial and touristic appeal: the allure of cheap flights, retail allure, brand allure. Bridge: marketing borrows the hunting metaphor knowingly — customers are hawks to be recalled to the store.
  • Deceptive or dangerous temptation (secondary, often with qualifier): false allure, treacherous allure, the allure of extremism or gambling. Bridge: recovery of the original artifice — the lure hides a hook; what glitters entraps.
  • Intellectual and natural-history allure (your sense): the allure of archives, of mathematics, of field study. Bridge: sublimation — the bait is knowledge itself, the capture is devotion; no victim, only a willing return like the hawk to the fist.
  • Grammatical and collocational notes: countable (an allure, many allures) but usually singular mass (much allure); adjectives alluring, allured; adverb alluringly. To have allure versus to exert allure; to resist, succumb to, trade on allure. Bridge: morphology regularises a French loan into a full English family.

Register matters. Allure is warmer than attraction (which can be physical), softer than seduction (which implies sexual capture), more mysterious than appeal (which can be rational) and less visual than glamour. To speak of the allure of field study ennobles patience, discomfort and uncertainty: mud, waiting and Nothing happened become desirable because they promise authenticity. The semantic bridge from falconry to fieldwork is thus historically exact — J. A. Baker's peregrines are hawks, and the naturalist is himself lured as the hawk is lured, returning daily to the estuary as to a swung lure.

Do not confuse allure with illusion (false perception), delusion, or with gait-allure (Old French aleure, way of walking), a distinct homograph that briefly influenced spelling but not sense. The lure in allure is the falconer's leurre from Frankish *lothr, call or bait, cognate with German Luder and English invitation-words, not the Latin ambulare family. When Baker's commentator writes of allure, he invokes eight centuries of hawking language to say that attention, patiently offered, is always rewarded by return.
