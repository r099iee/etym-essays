• sacrosanct: Similar because both describe something that is holy and immune from attack or interference; differs because sacrosanct carries strong ecclesiastical connotations of religious consecration or dogmatic untouchability (often used today with mild irony for bureaucratic privileges), whereas inviolable is an objective legal, constitutional, and normative standard.
  • inviolate: Similar because both share the same Latin verbal root and denote freedom from violation; differs because inviolate is a descriptive state signifying that something has remained intact, pure, unharmed, or unpolluted up to the present moment, whereas inviolable denotes a normative principle or constitutional immunity stating that something *must not* or *cannot* be legitimately violated.
  • untouchable: Similar because both describe that which cannot be assaulted or challenged; differs because untouchable is an everyday Germanic term that can denote social caste marginalization, criminal impunity, or unapproachability, whereas inviolable conveys solemn legal, spiritual, or ethical majesty.
  • unassailable: Similar because both denote security against hostile aggression; differs because unassailable emphasizes physical, military, or logical invulnerability against assault or counter-argument (e.g. an unassailable fortress or unassailable logic), whereas inviolable relies upon moral, spiritual, or legal interdicts rather than physical defensive strength.
  • inalienable: Similar because both apply to fundamental rights that cannot be surrendered; differs because inalienable specifically means incapable of being transferred, sold, or forfeited to another party (from property and contract law), while inviolable means incapable of being infringed or attacked by outside force.
  • impregnable: Similar because both suggest an inability to be breached; differs because impregnable focuses strictly on military or physical fortifications that cannot be captured by storm, while inviolable applies to persons, treaties, rights, and spiritual boundaries.
  • hallowed: Similar because both involve sacred protection; differs because hallowed emphasizes traditional religious sanctification, ancient reverence, and historical memory (e.g. hallowed ground), whereas inviolable emphasizes active legal or moral prohibitions against breach.
  • immune: Similar because both convey exemption from external harm or legal prosecution; differs because immune is a clinical or procedural term denoting physiological resistance to disease or statutory exemption from taxes and penalties, lacking the deep sacral gravity of inviolable.
