Derivation chain for COLOPHON (single root word; one PIE root):
1. Proto-Indo-European *kel- “to rise, to be prominent, to be tall” (reconstructed; Pokorny’s *kel- “‘hervorragen’, to stand out”).
2. → Ancient Greek kolṓnē (κολώνη) “hill, mound” (attested from Homeric/archaic Greek).
3. → Ancient Greek kolophṓn (κολοφών) “summit, peak, top” (derivative of kolṓnē; classical Greek).
4. → Greek idiom ἐπιτιθέναι κολοφῶνα (epitithénai kolophōna) “to put the finishing touch on a thing” (Aristotle and later writers); the “summit” becomes the capstone of completion.
5. → Late Latin colophōn “a finishing stroke, a conclusion” (borrowed from the Greek idiom).
6. → English colophon, first attested 1774, “an inscription at the end of a book” (the scribe’s/printer’s closing note).
7. → Modern English colophon (20th c.) “a publisher’s emblem or device on the title page or spine” — OUR CARD: COLOPHON.

Debate / nuance: the derivation of Greek kolṓnē and kolophṓn from *kel- is the traditional scholarly view (Pokorny). Beekes is more cautious and allows the possibility of a pre-Greek substrate element in the mountain-word; no competing etymology, however, has displaced the *kel- family of “rise, be prominent” forms. The step from “summit” to “finishing stroke” is secured by the Greek idiom, and the step from “finishing stroke” to the scribe’s closing inscription is the ordinary book-historical development attested from the medieval manuscript to the incunable.
