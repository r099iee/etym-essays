GALLED is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb to gall. In its core physical sense it means "made sore, raw, or tender by rubbing or chafing": a shoulder galled by a knapsack strap, a heel galled by new leather, a horse's back galled by a badly fitted saddle. The word names not the act of friction but its result — the inflamed, abraded patch of skin left where something hard has pressed and worked against the flesh until the surface wears bare and burns. Because a gall is caused by steady, repeated pressure rather than by a single cut, it is a wound of endurance: the sort of injury that develops unnoticed during a long march and announces itself later with a sting of heat and tenderness.

In The Hobbit, the word is used with brutal physical precision. The Ring, hung in a pouch next to Gollum's skin, "galled" him: its hard metal and weight rubbed against his flesh, wearing him raw, until he could no longer bear to wear it and took to keeping it in a hole in the rock. The sentence works on two levels at once. Literally, the Ring is a heavy, hard object that chafed Gollum's skin. Figuratively, it is a torment of the spirit: a secret, an obsession, a precious thing he cannot keep, that galls his mind even as he must keep going back to look at it. Galled, therefore, holds together the flesh-wound and the humiliation in a single word — the Ring rubs against the body and against the soul at the same time.

The word's family tree is rich, and its modern polysemy spreads across several distinct senses:

  • Physical sense (primary): made raw or sore by rubbing, chafing, or pressure — especially of skin under a strap, saddle, harness, or boot. A "saddle gall" is the classic example, a raw spot on a horse's back caused by a chafing saddle.
  • Figurative sense (secondary): deeply irritated, vexed, wounded in pride, or humiliated by something — "it galled him to admit defeat." The sense transfers the physical rawness to the emotions: a galling remark stings like a galled shoulder.
  • The noun "gall" — bile: the bitter yellow-green secretion of the liver; by metaphor, anything bitter or rancorous ("gall and wormwood").
  • "Have the gall" (19th c.): to have impudent, brazen boldness or effrontery — "he had the gall to ask for more." The bitterness of bile becomes the bitterness of nerve.
  • Botanical "gall": the abnormal swelling on a plant (oak apple, gallnut, gall-wasp) caused by an insect or fungus, a hard excrescent growth that mirrors the idea of a hard, irritated lump on the body.
  • As an adjective, "galling": vexing, humiliating, or bitterly irritating — "the galling injustice of it."

The historical bridge between the senses runs through colour. The Proto-Indo-European root *ghel- meant "to shine, be bright, be green or yellow," and it named the yellow-green tints of new growth and of bile. In English the descendant of that root came to mean specifically the bile itself, the bitter fluid; because bile was the "yellow humour" of medieval medicine, the word carried bitterness into the language. The physical sore sense is a fourteenth-century development: the raw wound raised by chafing was thought to fill with a yellow, bilious discharge, and so was called a gall. From that sore the verb was formed (sixteenth century), and from the verb's irritation the modern figurative sense grew (sixteenth–seventeenth centuries), until "galled" could describe a spirit rubbed raw by humiliation just as easily as a shoulder rubbed raw by a strap. Tolkien's sentence is the perfect union of the whole history: the Ring literally galls Gollum's skin, and the memory of it galls his soul.
