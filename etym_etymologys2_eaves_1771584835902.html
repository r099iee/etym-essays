Eaves descends through a single, unbroken Germanic line from the Proto-Indo-European root *upo. The chain below runs from PIE to the present word. (6 attested stages.) 1. PIE *upo "under, up from under, over, near" — a preposition root (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE); the lengthened variant *uper "over" is the source of English over, German über, and Latin super. 2. → Proto-Germanic *ub "under, up from under" < PIE *upo — the Germanic reflex of the preposition. 3. → Proto-Germanic *ubaswō- "porch, vestibule, that which lies under (the roof)" — a nominal formation built on *ub with the suffix *-aswō-; literally "the thing that lies under", attested only by inference from its daughter forms. 4. → Old English efes / yfes (feminine singular) "edge of a roof, that which overhangs" — the attested Old English roofing term; Old High German obasa / obisa "portico, vestibule, eaves" and Old Norse ups "eaves" are Germanic sisters of the same formation. 5. → Middle English eves / ewes / ifes "eaves" (13th–15th c.) — the medieval form of the word. 6. → English eaves "the projecting lower edge of a roof" — plural in form from c. 1500, though the -s is a fossil of the old singular; a new singular eave was back-formed in modern English. Final node: English EAVES (rootWord) — the present-day form, plural-only in shape with singular meaning. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The reconstruction *ubaswō- is not directly attested in any Germanic language; it is inferred from Old English efes, Old High German obasa/obisa, and Old Norse ups, and the exact shape of the suffix is debated. (2) The relation of *upo to *uper ("over") is well established, but the internal details of the Germanic suffix formation are uncertain. (3) The reinterpretation of the final -s as a plural (stage 6) is one of the best-known accidents of English morphology; modern speakers have back-formed the singular eave on the strength of that misreading.
