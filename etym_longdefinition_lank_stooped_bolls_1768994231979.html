lank (adjective). Thin and tall, but with a crucial extra component: slackness, limpness, a body or thing stretched thin without tone. Lank describes hair that hangs limp and greasy, a stalk that droops, a frame hung loosely on its bones. Where lean suggests a taut, pared-down strength, lank suggests a body that has lost its firmness — thin and slack at once, the thinness of something no longer held taut, a sheet left to hang, a stem that has wilted. In the passage, “lank and stooped” fixes the field labourer as vertical body pulled thin, horizontal body folded down, drained of energy: the word is precise, grim, and unheroic, a flat notation of exhaustion. The near-synonyms of lank mark its boundary clearly: lean implies a hard, healthy thinness and is usually admiring; gaunt is a hollowed, haggard thinness from hunger or illness; scrawny is contemptuous and bony; thin is the neutral all-purpose term. What distinguishes lank is the component of limpness — it is also the classic descriptor of hair, precisely because hair is the one part of the body whose thinness is inseparable from its limpness.

stooped (adjective; past participle of to stoop). Bent forward and downward at the shoulders and back, and — in the modern, habitual sense — permanently so: a stooped posture is the body's settled condition, folded by age or by a lifetime of work. The participle carries no ridicule; a writer never mocks a stooped back. The verb's older senses branch widely: to stoop is also to bend down physically (to stoop to pick something up), to descend or swoop (of a bird of prey dropping on its quarry — the falconer's stoop), to condescend or lower oneself morally (to stoop to deceit), and to lower one's head or shoulders in submission or dejection. The participle used adjectivally — a stooped figure, stooped with age, stooped with work — focuses the whole family on the bent body as an emblem of wear and endurance.

bolls (noun, plural of boll). The rounded, swollen seed-capsules of the cotton plant (and of flax), the case that holds the fibre which is the whole point of the crop. The boll is the round pod that bursts open at harvest, and the “cotton boll” is fixed in American agricultural vocabulary — the boll weevil that destroyed the cotton South, the bollworm, cotton-picking time. The word is technical and regional, a word of the picker's hand; in a literary sentence it authenticates the world with botanical exactness. Etymologically a boll is “the round thing,” from the same swell-root that gave ball and bowl — the globe-shape is the word's core image, moved from table vessel to seed-capsule.

How the three words work together. “Blacks in the fields, lank and stooped, their fingers spiderlike among the bolls of cotton”: lank gives the stretched, slack thinness; stooped gives the permanent forward fold; bolls gives the swollen round fruit the thin bent bodies reach for. The three words build one physical grammar — vertical pull, horizontal fold, spherical fullness — and each is drawn from the oldest stratum of native English vocabulary, so the sentence reads as biblical and elemental. The historical bridges are (for lank) the root of bending and flexibility turning to slack thinness, (for stooped) the root of bending and bowing turning into a chronic posture, and (for bolls) the root of swelling producing the round vessel and then the round seed-capsule.

  • lank — primary: thin and slack, stretched without tone; of hair, limp and greasy.
  • lank — secondary: of plants, stalks, grass — thin, drooping, unthrifty.
  • stooped — primary: bent forward and downward at the shoulders, habitually so.
  • stooped — secondary: (of a bird) swooping on prey; (morally) lowered, condescended — “stooped to lying.”
  • bolls — primary: the rounded seed-capsules of cotton (or flax).
  • bolls — secondary: by extension any rounded seed-vessel; the word's roundness is its core image.
