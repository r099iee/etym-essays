Spoil begins securely in Latin spolium (most often plural spolia), meaning the skin stripped from a killed animal and, by famous transfer, the armour and weapons stripped from a defeated enemy. Roman triumphs displayed spolia opima, the richest spoils taken by a general who slew the enemy leader single-handed. The verb spoliare meant to strip of clothing, to rob, to plunder, to despoil. Cicero uses it for fleecing provinces, Virgil for stripping the dead, jurists for unlawful seizure. The core image never wavers: to spoil is to pull off an outer covering and keep it.

Phonologically Latin spolium /ˈspo.li.um/ gives Late Latin *spoliare with regular -iare frequentative. Gallo-Romance palatalises and diphthongises: Old French espoillier / despoillier /es.pwa.je/ with prosthetic e- before sp- (a French requirement, as in escole, estat) and -ill- from Latin -li-. Anglo-Norman carries espoiller to England after 1066. Middle English drops prosthetic e- and simplifies to spoilen /ˈspoi.len/, rhyming with oil, toil. Modern spelling spoil keeps French oi; pronunciation /spɔɪl/ shows the regular Middle English /oi/ preserved. Noun spoil is shortened from verb or from Old French espoille "stripping, booty".

  • Martial borrowing (14th c.): early citations are all of war: to spoil the enemy, the spoil of the Egyptians (Wyclif's Bible renders Latin spoliare). To spoil is to strip the slain, a chivalric yet brutal act. Noun spoils are honourable if dedicated to gods, shameful if hoarded. This is Ruskin's sense: Homeric kings surrender their rightful spoil to followers, proving selflessness.
  • Moral extension to ruining (15th–17th c.): by metaphor a stripped thing is a ruined thing. Spoiled beauty is beauty stripped of bloom, spoiled meat is meat stripped of freshness, spoiled plans are plans stripped of success. Shakespeare already has both: the spoil of beasts and to spoil a beauty. The King James Bible fixes both poles: Israel spoils Egypt of jewels, while moth and rust spoil treasures on earth.
  • Domestication to children and food (18th–19th c.): Locke and Rousseau warn against spoiling children by fond indulgence — stripping them of hardiness by too much softness. Cookery books warn that milk spoils in thunder. The verb becomes household routine, losing martial clang but keeping judgment: spoiled implies blame, whether of cook or parent.
  • Political and industrial extensions (19th–20th c.): American Jacksonian democracy coins the spoils system (1828): offices as loot for victors. Mining creates spoil heaps of stripped overburden. Sport creates spoilsports who strip fun. Cinema creates spoilers who strip suspense by revealing endings, and engineers create spoilers on cars that strip lift by spoiling airflow — a neat return to aerodynamics of stripping.

Deeper pre-Latin origin remains debated. Many handbooks connect spolium to a PIE root *(s)pel- "to split, tear, strip", with Greek spolas "flayed hide", Lithuanian spaliai "flax fibres", and possibly English spill, spillikin. Michiel de Vaan, however, lists Latin spolium as of uncertain etymology, noting that the s- may be mobile and the -ol- unexplained. Honesty requires stating this nuance: the road from Rome to London is paved and dated, the road before Rome is conjectural.

Today spoil balances between condemnation and affection: we condemn spoiled ballots and spoiled brats, but we love to spoil grandchildren a little. The ancient stripping image still governs: every spoil is something pulled off and kept, whether armour, freshness, or discipline.
