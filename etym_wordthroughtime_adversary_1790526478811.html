- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed from Anglo-Norman and Old French *adversarie*, originating in Latin *adversarius*; initially applied in legal contexts (opposing counsel or litigant in a court of law) and in Middle English biblical translations as a title for Satan, the spiritual enemy of mankind.
- 15th to 17th Century - Broadened extensively into military, chivalric, and dramatic literature (such as Shakespeare) to denote any hostile combatant, political foe, or philosophical opponent in public disputation.
- 18th to 19th Century - Formally established in jurisprudence through the development and codification of the Anglo-American "adversary system" (or adversarial system) of justice, while maintaining its solemn theological resonance in sermons and religious literature.
- 20th Century - Expanded into modern military strategy, game theory, sports commentary, and geopolitical analysis (e.g., describing Cold War superpowers as geopolitical adversaries).
- 21st Century - Pervasive across technical domains, notably in cybersecurity ("adversary emulation", "adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures / TTPs", "threat adversary") and cognitive psychology ("the internal adversary").
