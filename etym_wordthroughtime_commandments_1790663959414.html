- 14th c. authoritative orders - feudal and general sense of weighty biddings from kings and lords, binding by status of speaker
- 16th–17th c. biblical fixation - Tyndale to King James makes the Ten Commandments the overwhelming association; memorized, catechized, inscribed; reverent and absolute connotation
- 18th–19th c. moralized extension - commandments of reason, health, grammar; schoolroom prohibitions borrow biblical gravity; tone splits between earnest and ironic
- 20th c. parodic and critical use - Orwell barn-wall parody; style guides attack grammatical commandments as dogma; connotation often oppressive rote learning
- Present hazily recalled set - what ordinary adults retain from schooling: a few thou-shalt-nots about usage, remembered well enough to inhibit, too vaguely to explain; source sentence captures this weary, comic stage
