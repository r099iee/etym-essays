VALE descends from Latin vallis "valley." No secure PIE etymon can be reconstructed (see the debate note); the attested chain is: 1. Latin vallis (also vallum? no — vallum is "rampart, palisade," a different word) / vall-es "valley" (first attested 2nd c. BCE in Ennius; regular in Classical Latin — Caesar, Vergil). The stem is valli-. 2. Latin vallem (accusative singular) — the oblique stem on which the Romance and English forms are modeled. 3. Old French val "valley" (masculine; 9th-12th c., from Latin vallem; cf. Old French valee "valley, dale," the source of the doublet valley). 4. Middle English vale "valley, dale" (attested c. 1300; taken into English from Old French val and/or directly from the Latin stem valle-, the final -e reflecting the Latin feminine stem). 5. Modern English VALE (chiefly poetic and literary from the 13th century onward). Debate note: the ultimate origin of Latin vallis is disputed. De Vaan (Etymological Dictionary of Latin, 2008) regards the etymology as uncertain; older scholarship (Pokorny) grouped it under the PIE root *wel- "to roll, turn," interpreting the valley as a "rolling/winding" landform, and a connection has also been floated to the root of Latin vallus "stake, palisade" (as if the valley were an "enclosed" place) — but neither proposal is certain, and no attested PIE form can be reconstructed. Some scholars even suspect a substrate or loanword origin. The safest statement is that vale enters English as a Latinate literary word in the 13th century without a reliable PIE chain behind it.
