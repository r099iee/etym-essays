• Alsatia: Similar because it is the Latin and early modern English name of the same region, and English writers once used it interchangeably with Alsace; differs because it survives chiefly as an archaic literary term and as the cant name for a London debtors' sanctuary, not as a geographical designation.
  • Elsass: Similar because it is the German name of the region, used officially from 1871 to 1918 and still current in German; differs because it belongs to German discourse and carries the memory of the Reichsland, whereas Alsace is the French and international form.
  • Alsace-Lorraine: Similar because it names the same territory together with its neighbour and is often treated as a single historical unit; differs because it denotes a political construct created in 1871 and dissolved in 1918, not the region as such.
  • Alsatian: Similar because it is the adjective and the demonym derived from Alsace; differs because it describes people, dialects or the German Shepherd dog, and cannot name the territory in a phrase like “the defence of Alsace”.
  • Lorraine: Similar because it is the neighbouring region with which Alsace is constantly paired in history and in the phrase Alsace-Lorraine; differs because it lies to the west, has a different dialectal and political history, and is a separate region.
  • Sundgau: Similar because it is the name of the southern part of Alsace, and the two terms can overlap in historical geography; differs because it denotes only the Jura-facing south of the region, not the whole.
  • Grand Est: Similar because it is the present administrative region that contains Alsace; differs because it is a recent bureaucratic creation (2016) that merges Alsace with Champagne-Ardenne and Lorraine, and it lacks the historical resonance of the older name.
  • Vosges: Similar because the mountain range defines Alsace's western edge and appears constantly in descriptions of the region; differs because it is a physical feature shared with Lorraine, not a political or cultural name.
