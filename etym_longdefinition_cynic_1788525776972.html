A cynic (noun) is primarily defined in modern usage as a person who harbors a pervasive, deeply ingrained distrust of human motives, firmly believing that individuals are driven exclusively by self-interest, vanity, greed, or covert ambition rather than by genuine altruism, integrity, or moral goodness. Characterized by a sneering, contemptuous, or derisive attitude toward professed ideals, a cynic habitually interprets acts of apparent kindness, social reform, or romantic devotion as hypocritical posturing or calculated manipulation. In formal historical, philosophical, and intellectual contexts, however, the capitalized term Cynic designates a member or adherent of a prominent school of ancient Greek philosophy founded in Athens during the 4th century BCE by Antisthenes (a pupil of Socrates) and epitomized by Diogenes of Sinope, which advocated absolute virtue, radical self-sufficiency, and ascetic disregard for societal conventions, material wealth, and artificial decorum.

The term spans several nuanced senses, psychological profiles, and technical applications:

  • Classical Philosophical Sense (The Cynic School): In the history of philosophy, a Cynic (Ancient Greek: Κυνικός Kynikos, literally "dog-like") was a philosopher who sought eudaimonia (human flourishing) through aretē (moral virtue) lived in strict accordance with nature. Rejecting social hierarchies, civic ambition, religious dogma, and material luxury as artificial impediments to freedom, ancient Cynics practiced askēsis (rigorous physical training and ascetic endurance), autarkeia (complete self-sufficiency), and parrhēsia (uncompromising, fearless freedom of speech). Living in public squares, wearing a simple coarse cloak, and carrying only a beggar's staff and wallet, they subjected the hypocrisies and pretensions of Athenian and Roman society to unsparing ridicule.
  • Modern Psychological and Dispositional Sense: In contemporary psychology and common parlance, a cynic is an individual exhibiting high trait cynicism—a chronic cognitive orientation marked by generalized hostility, suspicion of others' intentions, and habitual attribution of selfish, exploitative motives to all interpersonal behavior. Unlike mild skepticism, psychological cynicism functions as a defensive emotional barrier, shielding the individual from vulnerability, disappointment, and gullibility at the cost of chronic interpersonal alienation.
  • Sociopolitical and Institutional Cynicism: In political science and sociology, the term describes a widespread public posture of profound disillusionment with democratic institutions, governance, corporate rhetoric, and public discourse. A political cynic operates under the assumption that all politicians, laws, and institutional promises are fundamentally corrupt, transactional, and deceptive, leading frequently to civic apathy or nihilistic disengagement.
  • Literary, Satirical, and Aphoristic Sense: In drama, satire, and aesthetic literature, the cynic is a recognizable archetypal figure who punctuates social comedy with biting, unsentimental commentary. Most famously, Oscar Wilde defined a cynic in his 1892 play Lady Windermere’s Fan through Lord Darlington's unforgettable aphorism: "A man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing." In Wilde's fairy tale The Nightingale and the Rose, the little green Lizard is described as "something of a cynic" because it immediately laughs at the notion of crying for a mere red rose, evaluating romantic yearning with cold, unfeeling pragmatic absurdity.
  • Epistemic Nuance (Cynicism vs. Skepticism): Lexicographers and epistemologists emphasize a crucial semantic distinction between the cynic and the skeptic: while the philosophical skeptic suspends belief pending verifiable empirical evidence, the cynic dogmatically presumes bad faith and malicious or self-serving intent without requiring proof.

The semantic transition from an ancient philosophical ascetic practicing radical virtue to a modern sneering fault-finder represents one of the most famous examples of semantic pejoration in lexicographical history: because Diogenes and his followers flouted societal decorum, mocked religious rituals, and "barked" at aristocratic vanity like stray dogs, external observers focused on their abrasive, dismissive demeanor rather than their underlying ethical idealism, cementing the word's negative, world-weary connotation by the late 16th century.
