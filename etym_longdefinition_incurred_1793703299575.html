The verb 'incurred' is the past tense and past participle of 'incur,' which fundamentally means to bring upon oneself or to become subject to something, almost invariably something unwelcome, undesirable, or negative. It implies a direct causal link between one's actions, decisions, or circumstances and the resulting consequence. The core idea is that one 'runs into' or 'falls into' a particular state or condition, often one that carries a burden or disadvantage.

One of the most common applications of 'incur' is in financial contexts. For instance, a business might 'incur debt' by borrowing money, or 'incur expenses' through its operational activities. In these cases, the debt or expense is a direct result of the financial decisions or necessities of the entity. Similarly, an individual might 'incur charges' on a credit card or 'incur a penalty' for late payment. Here, the financial burden is a consequence of specific actions or failures to act.

Beyond the financial realm, 'incur' is frequently used in legal and administrative contexts. A person might 'incur liability' for damages caused, 'incur a fine' for a violation, or 'incur a sanction' for non-compliance. These usages highlight the idea of becoming legally or officially responsible for an adverse outcome. The implication is that the individual or entity has, through their conduct, brought themselves under the purview of a rule or law that dictates a negative consequence.

In a broader, more general sense, 'incur' can refer to bringing upon oneself social or emotional displeasure. As in the provided context, one can 'incur wrath,' 'incur displeasure,' 'incur criticism,' or 'incur the disapproval' of others. This signifies that one's actions or words have provoked a negative reaction from individuals, groups, or even deities. The 'wrath' is not simply experienced; it is actively drawn forth or provoked by the actions of the one who incurs it. This usage emphasizes the reactive nature of the consequence, where the 'incurring' party is the agent of their own misfortune.

It is important to distinguish 'incur' from verbs like 'suffer' or 'experience.' While one might 'suffer a loss' or 'experience difficulty,' 'incur' often carries a stronger connotation of direct causation and, at times, a degree of responsibility or agency. To 'incur a loss' suggests that the loss was a direct result of a specific venture or decision, whereas 'suffering a loss' can sometimes imply a more passive reception of misfortune. Similarly, 'incurring risk' means actively taking on or exposing oneself to potential danger, rather than merely being in a risky situation.

The word 'incur' almost exclusively precedes negative or undesirable outcomes. One does not typically 'incur praise' or 'incur joy.' The semantic field is firmly rooted in burdens, liabilities, penalties, losses, and negative reactions. This consistent association reinforces the word's utility in describing situations where actions lead to adverse consequences, making it a precise term for conveying responsibility for one's own misfortunes, whether intended or not. The past tense 'incurred' therefore describes a state where these negative consequences have already been brought about and are now a present reality for the subject.

The nuance of 'incur' also lies in its implication of an unfolding process. While the consequence might be immediate, the act of incurring it often involves a series of decisions or a prolonged state of being. For example, a company might 'incur debt' over months or years, or a person might 'incur the wrath' of a powerful figure through repeated transgressions. This temporal aspect adds depth to the word, suggesting that the negative outcome is not always a sudden event but can be the culmination of sustained behavior or circumstances.

In summary, 'incurred' denotes the past act of bringing upon oneself or becoming subject to something detrimental, burdensome, or undesirable. It is a verb that strongly links agency (or lack thereof) with consequence, making it a powerful term in contexts ranging from finance and law to social and mythological narratives, always emphasizing the direct connection between actions and their often-unpleasant results.
