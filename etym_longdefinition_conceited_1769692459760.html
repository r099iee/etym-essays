conceited: having an excessively high opinion of oneself; vain, self-important, or arrogant; full of self-conceit. Core meaning. Conceited describes a person who thinks too well of himself or herself — who has an inflated sense of their own abilities, attractiveness, or importance. It is always negative: a conceited person is boastful, self-admiring, or condescending, frequently seeking admiration and affronted when it is not given. In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, the Bingley sisters are "very fine ladies... but proud and conceited": their self-satisfied vanity, bred by fortune and rank, is named alongside pride as their great fault. The word's core image is literally "full of conceit" — full of a high opinion of oneself. Primary sense — excessive self-opinion. The everyday modern sense: "He's so conceited about his looks," "a conceited young man who thinks he knows everything." It combines vanity (admiring one's own appearance or achievements) with arrogance (feeling superior). Unlike "proud," which can be positive, conceited is always a fault. It can describe a person, their manner ("a conceited smile"), or their behavior ("a conceited performance"), and it implies that the high opinion is unjustified. Historical senses that have faded or shifted.
  • Literary sense of the noun "conceit": an elaborate, fanciful, witty idea or image — especially in Elizabethan poetry (John Donne's "metaphysical conceit"). The adjective "conceited" once meant "full of fanciful ideas or witty inventions, imaginative, ingenious" — this sense is now archaic but explains the family: a "conceit" was a concept, a clever notion.
  • "Having a good opinion or notion" (obsolete): in Middle English "conceit" meant simply a thought, opinion, or concept; "conceited" could mean "having understanding, quick-witted" (15th–16th c.).
  • The self-regarding shift: from "full of fancies" the word moved to "full of a high opinion of oneself" — first recorded in the 16th century, firmly established by the 17th. The "self-conceit" (an overweening opinion of oneself) is the modern core.
Secondary shades and register.
  • Intensity: between "vain" (admiring one's appearance) and "arrogant" (claiming superiority); conceited blends self-admiration with self-importance.
  • Register: everyday, mildly informal to neutral; common in casual speech and fiction.
  • Collocations: "so conceited," "conceited about," "a conceited smile/air," "proud and conceited" (Austen's pairing).
  • Related nouns/verbs: conceit (noun, "a fanciful idea; self-conceit"), self-conceit ("excessive self-opinion"); the verb "to conceit" (to imagine, archaic).
  • Note on the semantic bridge: Latin concipere "to take in, to conceive" — a "conceit" was a thing conceived in the mind, a notion. To be "full of conceit" originally meant full of clever notions; by the 17th century it meant full of a (false) notion of one's own greatness.
Polysemy summary (all senses, living and historical).
  • Having an excessively high opinion of oneself; vain, self-important — modern, dominant, negative.
  • Full of fanciful, witty, or imaginative ideas — Elizabethan-literary, archaic.
  • Having understanding, quick-witted, intelligent — 15th–16th-century, obsolete.
  • Of a notion: taken into the mind, imagined (from the verb) — archaic.
  • Self-conceited (the fuller form), self-conceit (noun) — the same vain sense.
In Austen's sentence, "proud and conceited" forms a precise moral pair: the sisters' pride is their high self-estimation; their conceit is the vain, self-admiring opinion of themselves that follows from it — the two together make them look down on others while thinking extremely well of themselves.
