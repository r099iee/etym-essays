Single linear derivation chain for FRETFULLY (native Germanic line, with the adverbial suffix at the end):
  • PIE *ed-, "to eat" (c. 4000 BCE) — the basic verb of eating across the family (Latin edere, Greek édein, Sanskrit admi "I eat").
  • Proto-Germanic *etaną, "to eat" (c. 500 BCE – 1st c. CE).
  • Proto-Germanic *fra-etaną, "to eat up, devour, consume completely" (intensive compound of *fra- "completely, forth" + *etaną) — the specific ancestor of English fret and German fressen.
  • Old English fretan, "to devour, eat up, consume" (attested c. 8th–10th c.; used of beasts tearing prey, of corrosion eating metal, and in Beowulf of Grendel's devouring).
  • Middle English freten / fretten, "to devour; to gnaw, corrode, wear away" (12th–15th c.).
  • English fret (verb), "to gnaw, corrode, chafe" (13th c.), and by extension (14th–16th c.) "to gnaw at the mind, vex, worry"; the intransitive sense "to be worried, to chafe with irritation" (16th c.) is the direct base of the adjective.
  • English fretful (adjective), "inclined to fret, disposed to vexation, anxious, peevish" (c. 1590, Shakespeare-adjacent; "fretful" formed with the suffix -ful "full of").
  • English fretfully (adverb), "in a fretful manner" (17th c., formed with the adverbial suffix -ly); our word.
NUANCE NOTES: (1) The PIE root *ed- is beyond doubt (it also gives Latin edere "to eat," whence edible, and Sanskrit ad-). (2) The specifically "devour" compound *fra-etaną is the accepted Proto-Germanic ancestor of fret, and the formal German parallel fressen (Old High German frezzan) shows the same "animal-devouring" vs. "human-eating" (essen) distinction. (3) The homonyms fret "a guitar ridge" (from Old French frete, "a band, ferrule") and fret "interlaced ornament" (from Old French frete/fretis) are entirely separate words and are NOT part of this chain. (4) The Old English verb fretan was lost in its literal form and survives through the Middle English verb fretten/freten, whose senses merged gnawing, corrosion, and worry; the modern noun fret "a state of worry" (in "in a fret") is a back-formation from the verb.
