- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) — Attested as mist in Anglo-Saxon verse (such as Beowulf and the Exeter Book riddles), evoking desolate sea-marshes, fenland darkness, and demonic wilderness prowled by monsters.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) — Spelled mist, myst, or miste; expanded into theological and devotional allegory to represent spiritual blindness, tears of contrition, or sinful delusions clouding the soul's inner eye.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — Flourished in Renaissance drama (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton) and the King James Bible; popularized in philosophical rhetoric contrasting the radiant light of reason against the "mists of error and superstition."
- 18th–19th c. (Romantic era) — Romantic poets and Victorian historians (Wordsworth, Scott, Carlyle) established the iconic historiographical idiom "the mists of time" and "the mists of antiquity" to evoke half-remembered medieval legends and prehistoric origins.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains exact quantitative meteorological definitions in atmospheric science and aerosol pharmacology, while remaining a universal idiom in historical essays, philosophy, and creative literature.
