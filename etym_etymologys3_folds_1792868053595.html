The word folds carries a double inheritance, and its essay must follow two threads that Old English kept apart and modern English has braided into one spelling: the verb of bending and the noun of the pen.

First thread: the bend. PIE *pel-, to fold, is reconstructed from a web of bending words across the family, and Germanic inherited it as *falþaną — Grimm's law turning initial p to f — whence Gothic falþan, Old High German faltan, and Old English fealdan, to fold, wrap, and clasp. The Old English verb was strong, with ablaut grades still visible in fold, folded, and the old participle folden; Middle English levelled the paradigm to the weak folden–folded that we use today. Every crease-sense flows from this thread: folds of cloth, of paper, of geological strata thrust sideways into anticlines, and — by the poet's extension — folds of land.

Second thread: the pen. Beside the verb stood the noun falod, the fenced enclosure where sheep were folded for the night. Whether its -od is an old place-suffix on the same bending root (the place made by bending hurdles into a ring) or an independent formation is the detail scholars still dispute; either way, the pen-word and the bend-word reinforced each other at every turn, since penning sheep is bending a fence around them. Middle English fold absorbed both, and the parables — the lost sheep, the one fold and one shepherd — lifted the farmyard noun into theology, so that returning to the fold has meant coming home to the faith for six hundred years.

Sound and spelling shifts:

  • Grimm's law: PIE *p becomes Germanic *f, so *pel- yields *falþ-; the medial *lþ cluster simplifies in Old English to ld, giving fealdan and fald.
  • Vowel levelling: Old English ea in fealdan monophthongises to Middle English o, and the strong-verb ablaut is abandoned for the regular -ed past, fixing modern fold–folded.
  • Noun–verb merger: falod loses its unstressed vowel to become fald, then borrows the verb's o, so that pen and crease share one spelling by the fourteenth century.
  • The -fold suffix: Old English -feald (twofold, manifold) grammaticalises the crease into arithmetic: each fold doubles the layers, and the suffix still multiplies today.

Socio-cultural context: the sheepfold stood at the centre of the downland economy — hurdles, night-folding for manure, the shepherd's year — while the pulpit made it the centre of the moral economy too: stray, fold, shepherd, wolf. Enclosure and empire later literalised the metaphor, as missions and colonies were gathered into their respective folds, and Dickens could still make a whole novel turn on a prodigal's return to one. The landscape sense that Tolkien uses is the youngest and gentlest of the brood: nineteenth-century poets, walking the chalk downs, saw the hills as folded cloth and named what they saw, so that geology, tailoring, and shepherding meet in a single village disappearing into the dusk.
