- 13th c. — Physical demolition & legal warfare: borrowed into Anglo-Norman and Middle English from Old French abatre, meaning literally to strike down, fell knights from horses, raze castle walls, and quash legal writs.
- 14th–15th c. — Intransitive softening: begins to be applied naturally to storms, fevers, and human passions subsiding of their own accord, shifting from active physical violence to natural diminishment.
- 16th–17th c. — Dramatic and poetic depth: Shakespeare and contemporaries employ abate across tragedies to signify humbling pride, curtailing grief, or cutting short royal power.
- 18th c. — Maritime standard & Enlightenment prose: Swift, Defoe, and nautical logs cement abated as the definitive English sea-term for the calming of tempests and perilous gales.
- 19th c. — Statutory codification: public health legislation formalizes "nuisance abatement" and "tax abatement" into administrative jurisprudence.
- Today — Formal literary and legal prestige: maintains high stylistic dignity in serious prose, journalism, and law, marking the quiet aftermath of turmoil.
