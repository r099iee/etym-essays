Pre-Greek substrate (date unknowable; recorded from the classical period): Aones (Ἄονες) "the Aones," a people regarded by Greek tradition as the earliest inhabitants of Boeotia before the Cadmeans; the name has no convincing Indo-European derivation and is classed by Beekes as a Pre-Greek (Aegean/Anatolian substrate) word.
Ancient Greek (5th–4th c. BCE): Aonía (Ἀονία) "Aonia," the region of Boeotia around Mount Helicon, formed from the ethnic name with the toponymic suffix -ία; the Muses come to be called Aonídes.
Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE): Aonius, -a, -um "Aonian" — the poetic adjective borrowed from Greek, used by Ovid (Metamorphoses) and Statius as a stock epithet of the Muses, Helicon, and poetic inspiration.
English (c. 1600, Renaissance Neo-Latin learning): Aonian — adjective meaning "of Aonia, of Helicon, of the Muses, of poetic inspiration," entering the epic diction of the age.
Modern English (archaic literary): Aonian — survives in Milton scholarship, literary allusion, and archaizing verse.
NOTE ON THE DEBATE: The ultimate etymon is not Indo-European. Because the Aones are among the "pre-Hellenic" peoples of Greece and their name lacks a Greek or PIE derivation, the standard scholarly view (following Beekes on the Pre-Greek substrate) treats *Aones as a relic of an older, non-Indo-European linguistic layer of the Aegean. No PIE root should be reconstructed for it; the chain begins, honestly, at the tribal name itself.
