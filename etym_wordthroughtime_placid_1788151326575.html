- In early 17th-century English (1620s) - borrowed from Latin placidus as a learned literary word meaning both "pleasant, agreeable" and "calm, still"; pleasingness and stillness were twin ideas.
- 18th century - adopted by the nature poets (Thomson, later Cowper) as a favourite epithet for untroubled landscapes, streams, and skies - connotation of pastoral harmony and morally wholesome quiet.
- 19th century - the Romantics (Wordsworth's Prelude: "a clear and placid day") and the Victorians extend it from scenery to character: "a placid countenance", "a placid temper" - the ideal of a composed, unruffled domestic spirit.
- Later 19th century - a faint clinical flavour appears: placid also describes animals and patients, meaning docile and unreactive, and the word begins to acquire an edge of dullness ("placid as an ox").
- 20th century - journalism and travel writing adopt it for places and markets; placid now toggles between admiring ("a placid lake, perfect for a honeymoon") and patronizing ("a placid, uneventful existence").
- 21st century - the word survives in formal descriptive prose, place-name tourism (Lake Placid), and the idiom of the unexcitable; in psychology and wellness discourse it is reclaimed as a positive trait of low-arousal, low-anxiety minds.
