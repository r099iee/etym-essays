JEST (noun) names (1) something said or done to provoke laughter — a joke, a witty or sportive remark, a playful trick; (2) the object of mockery — 'the jest of the town,' a laughing-stock; (3) archaically, the state of being spoken or done playfully: 'in jest' = not seriously. As a verb, (4) it means to joke, banter, or speak mockingly. The source line — 'many a merry _____ and dance' — uses the word in its core, lighthearted sense, and the collocation 'merry jest' is itself a genuinely old one.

1. Modern senses (noun).

  • Joke / witticism: a short, pointed or amusing remark: 'his remark was only a jest.' Now literary or dated beside 'joke' — 'jest' today signals elevation, period flavour, or wry formality.
  • Playful act / prank: 'a merry jest' — a sportive practical joke; the word's archaism suits the fairy-tale register of the flashcard.
  • Object of derision: 'he became the jest of the whole town' — a laughing-stock; 'to make a jest of X' = to ridicule X. This is a live, if formal, sense.
  • 'In jest': the fossil phrase 'in/for jest' = 'not in earnest': 'he said it in jest,' with the ancient contrast pair 'in jest and in earnest.'

2. Archaic and historical senses (crucial for polysemy).

  • Deed, exploit (especially martial): from Old French geste 'exploit,' English gest, jest in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries meant 'a deed of arms, an achievement.'
  • Tale of deeds, romance: the same word meant 'a story of heroic deeds, a romance' — the 'gests' of Arthur or Alexander; this is the sense visible in the French chanson de geste (heroic epic) and in the medieval Latin compilation Gesta Romanorum ('Deeds of the Romans').
  • Idle talk, trifling word: by the fifteenth century 'jest' could also mean 'an idle tale, a piece of trifling gossip' — the bridge stage in which heroic story slid downward to 'a mere tale' and then 'a laughing matter.'
  • These senses were obsolescent by 1600 and dead by 1700; they explain why 'jest' and 'jester' look so much lighter than their heroic ancestor geste.

3. The verb and its derivatives.

  • To jest (v.): to speak or act playfully; to joke; to mock ('jest at' = ridicule). The verb shows the same register slide — 'jesting' in the King James Bible (Ephesians 5:4: 'neither filthiness, nor foolish talking, nor jesting, which are not convenient') already carries moral disapproval, while modern 'to jest' is bookish and mild.
  • Jester: a professional fool at court — a role institutionalized across Europe in the Middle Ages and Renaissance (motley, bauble, the licensed right to mock the king), whose comic freedom is echoed by the modern metaphor of the 'court jester.'
  • Jest-book: collections of comic tales printed from the sixteenth century onward — A Hundred Merry Tales (c. 1526) is the celebrated English pioneer; Joe Miller's Jests (1739) made a comedian's name a byword for a stale joke.
  • Fossil: 'jesting Pilate' — from Bacon's essay Of Truth (1625): 'What is truth? said jesting Pilate, and would not stay for an answer,' endlessly echoed in philosophy and criticism — the one place most modern readers meet the word with complete seriousness.

4. Semantic bridge in one line. gerere 'to do, perform' → gesta 'deeds' → Old French geste 'exploit; tale of exploits' → English 'jest' 'deed; tale; idle tale; joke': the word devalued down the social ladder exactly as its subject matter did — from the deeds of heroes to the quips of jesters. The literal meaning 'something performed' explains everything: a jest is a small performance given for laughter.

5. Boundaries. 'Jest' leans toward spoken wit and concluded jokes; it does not cover elaborate practical jokes ('prank,' 'caper' do), cruelty ('sarcasm,' 'jeer' — though 'jest at' once meant mocking), or formal comedy ('comedy,' 'farce'). Its register today is formal-literary, lightly archaic, and always slightly knowing.
