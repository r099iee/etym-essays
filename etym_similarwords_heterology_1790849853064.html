• Heterogeneity: Similar because both refer to the state of consisting of diverse, disparate, and varied elements; differs because heterogeneity is a descriptive condition of diversity, whereas heterology is the theoretical study, discourse, or deliberate manifestation of radical otherness.
  • Alterity: Similar because it concerns the philosophical condition of "otherness" and radical difference; differs because alterity focuses specifically on the existential and ontological status of being distinct from the self, while heterology provides the systematic methodology or textual practice of engaging with that otherness.
  • Divergence: Similar because it involves departing from a common standard, baseline, or path; differs because divergence implies a linear branching away from a single point of origin, whereas heterology describes a fundamental, structural incompatibility with the dominant norm.
  • Anomalousness: Similar because both highlight deviation from established rules, forms, or expectations; differs because anomalousness emphasizes irregularity or unexpected eccentricity, lacking the profound theoretical, psychoanalytic, or philosophical frameworks intrinsic to heterology.
  • Multiplicity: Similar because it involves a state of having numerous varied components; differs because multiplicity merely emphasizes numerical abundance and plurality, whereas heterology centers on the unassimilable, subversive quality of difference.
  • Aberrance: Similar because it denotes a departure from the normal, standard, or correct type; differs because aberrance carries strong pejorative connotations of error, defect, or moral straying, whereas heterology is frequently celebrated in critical theory as a liberating disruption of totalitarian order.
