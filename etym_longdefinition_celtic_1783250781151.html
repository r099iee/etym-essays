Celtic (adjective; also noun). Primary sense in this context. In the nineteenth-century sentence, drawn from an essay on the history of the Latin language, Celtic stands for the pre-Roman population of western Europe and their languages: the many Celtic-speaking peoples of France, Spain, and Portugal whom Latin “drove out,” and who were replaced by the Latin that “is at this day the basis of the French, Spanish and Portuguese languages.” The pronoun it is Latin, and the whole contrast is with the Teutonic, the Germanic tongues that did not displace Latin. Celtic here is a collective, ethnolinguistic term, slightly sweeping and scientific, used with calm confidence: it names not one tribe but an entire family of peoples and languages that once filled the map of western Europe, and the tone is that of the grand nineteenth-century comparative philologist who surveys the disappearance of one language family and the triumph of another as a single, dramatic arc of history. The full polysemy of the word. Celtic is at once an ethnonym, a language-family label, a cultural badge, and a modern identity term, each sense with its own historical bridge:
  • The ancient peoples. The Indo-European peoples who spread over Europe from the 2nd millennium BC and, by the 5th–1st centuries BC, occupied Gaul, the Iberian peninsula, the British Isles, central Europe, and parts of Anatolia — the Galli of Caesar, the Celtiberians of Spain, the Galatians of Asia Minor.
  • The language family. The branch of Indo-European comprising the Continental Celtic languages (Gaulish, Celtiberian, Lepontic, Galatian) and the Insular Celtic languages, divided into Brittonic (Welsh, Breton, Cornish) and Goidelic (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx) — the scientific, philological sense the nineteenth-century sentence uses.
  • The adjective of the family. “Celtic languages,” “Celtic peoples,” “Celtic art,” “Celtic mythology” — the attributive use linking any cultural or linguistic datum to the family.
  • The cultural-revival sense. From the 18th–19th centuries, “Celtic” came to denote the romanticized heritage of the modern Celtic nations — Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, Brittany, the Isle of Man — the “Celtic Revival,” “Celtic Twilight,” and “Celtic fringe.”
  • The modern identity term. Today “Celtic” names a living linguistic and cultural identity: the Celtic Congress, Celtic languages with state support in Ireland and Wales, the Celtic nations, and the pan-Celtic movement.
  • The pronunciation crux. The word carries a famous phonetic double life: the ancient and learned pronunciation is /k/ (‘Keltic’), while a soft /s/ (‘Seltic’) survives in the sports clubs (Boston Celtics, Glasgow Celtic) — a sound shift that is itself part of the word's modern polysemy.
How the secondary senses developed (semantic bridges). The ethnonym is oldest: Greek writers (Hecataeus c. 500 BC, Herodotus) recorded the Keltoi as a people of the upper Danube and Gaul. From the people came the language-family sense — when Edward Lhuyd in 1707 classified Welsh, Irish, and related tongues together, he called them Celtic, and the term entered comparative philology. The cultural-revival sense is a Romantic reaction: as the Celtic languages retreated before English and French, writers and nationalists of the 19th century revalued the Celtic past as spiritual, artistic, and pre-Roman — a usable inheritance against modern industrial civilization. The modern identity sense continues this, now backed by institutional language policy. The pronunciation split reflects a genuine historical divergence in the reception of the word: the /k/ is the classical restoration, the /s/ a French-derived alternative. Distinctions from related terms. Gallic and Gaulish are confined to Gaul (ancient France), whereas Celtic is the umbrella for the whole family. Gaelic names specifically the Goidelic tongues of Ireland and Scotland. Brittonic covers the Insular branch (Welsh, Breton, Cornish); Goidelic the Irish–Scottish branch. Celtiberian is the Continental language of Spain; Galatian that of the Celts of Anatolia. In the context of the passage, the critical contrast is with Teutonic (Germanic): Celtic is the family Latin displaced, Teutonic the family that did not displace Latin. Register and collocations. Celtic is a formal, scholarly, and cultural adjective, standard in linguistics, archaeology, history, and modern identity discourse. Typical collocations: “Celtic languages,” “Celtic peoples,” “Celtic Christianity,” “Celtic art,” “the Celtic fringe,” “the Celtic Revival,” “Pan-Celticism,” “Celtic Tiger” (the 1990s–2000s Irish economic boom). The word is never merely descriptive today: it always carries, to some degree, the double charge of ancient peoples and living identities.
