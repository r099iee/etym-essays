Chain for thee: 1. PIE *túh₂ ("you", second-person singular pronoun; reconstructed for c. 4500–3500 BCE; the ancestor of Sanskrit tvám, Greek sý, Latin tū, Old Irish tú, Old Church Slavonic ty, Lithuanian tù, and Proto-Germanic *þū) → 2. PIE oblique stem *twé ("thee", the objective case of *túh₂; the *tw- cluster is what surfaces across the objective paradigm of the family) → 3. Proto-Germanic *þek (accusative singular "thee", built on the oblique stem with an emphatic particle *-ke; this *-k marker also appears in Gothic þuk, Old Norse þik, Old Saxon thīk, and Old English þēc; reconstructed for c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → 4. Old English þē (accusative/dative singular of þū "thou", the shorter form that generalized alongside the older þēc; attested from c. 700 CE) → 5. Middle English the(e), þe, þee (merged accusative/dative object form "thee"; c. 1100–1500) → 6. Modern English thee (archaic, poetic, liturgical, and dialectal object pronoun; from c. 1500 onward). Debated / nuanced stages: (a) the exact shape of the Proto-Germanic accusative is reconstructed as *þek, but some scholars reconstruct *þekēn with a longer emphatic ending, and the dative was a separate *þiz / *þizē; (b) the relation between the short Old English þē and the longer þēc is debated — þēc is usually taken as the inherited emphatic accusative whose final consonant was later lost in most contexts; (c) the initial cluster shift PIE *t- → Proto-Germanic *þ- follows Grimm's Law, and the later loss of *-k- is a purely Germanic reduction, but the precise staging of the loss is not directly attested.
