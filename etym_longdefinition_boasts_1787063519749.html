Primary sense. The plural noun boasts denotes statements or claims in which a person proudly asserts their own merits, achievements, possessions, or abilities — words of self-praise, especially of an inflated or overconfident kind. In the source sentence, the dwarves' "confident boasts" are their grand claims and bold promises, made moments earlier and now abandoned in terror. The word is the plural of boast, and it also appears, in exactly this spelling, as the third-person singular present tense of the verb to boast ("he boasts that..."), so the form must be described from both angles.

Senses of the noun.

  • Proud self-assertion. The core noun sense: a statement of one's own excellence, success, or daring, normally implying exaggeration or vanity. "Empty boasts", "idle boasts", "vain boasts" are the standard collocations, and the word in this sense is almost always a mild rebuke: a boast is something a sensible or modest person would not make.
  • A thing to be proud of. In a softer, sometimes positive sense, a "boast" can be a legitimate source of pride — "the castle's chief boast is its great hall", "their new stadium is the town's proudest boast". Here the word means "something worth claiming proudly", and it shades into "pride", "glory", "distinction", or "showpiece". This sense survives mainly in formal, touristic, or journalistic prose.
  • Archaic and dialectal uses. Historically the noun also meant noise, clamour, or idle loud talk (its earliest Middle English sense), and "to make one's boast" once meant simply "to make a solemn promise or declaration". These uses are now obsolete or rare, but they explain the word's ancient link between loudness and bragging.

Senses of the verb "to boast".

  • Intransitive — to brag. "He boasts of his wealth", "she boasted about her children": the verb means to speak proudly or excessively about one's own attainments, usually with disapproval implied, though "proudly boast" can be admiring.
  • Transitive — to claim proudly. "He boasts that he can swim a mile": the verb takes a clause setting out the proud claim.
  • Transitive — to possess with pride. The important modern transfer: "the town boasts two museums", "the hotel boasts a rooftop pool". Here the subject is usually a place, building, or institution, the object a feature worth having, and the meaning is simply "can proudly offer or contain". This sense is wholly neutral and is ubiquitous in advertising and tourism.
  • Reflexive and idiom. "To boast oneself" (to call oneself proudly) is now rare; the fixed phrase "boast of" and the noun phrase "a boast that" carry the load.

How the senses connect (semantic bridge). All of these uses hang on a single image: the proud display of what one has or does. The oldest noun sense, "noise and loud talk", already links sound to self-importance; from "loud proud talk" it is a short step to "a proud statement" (noun) and to "to speak proudly" (verb); and from "to speak proudly of what one has" it is another short step to "to have something worth speaking proudly of", which is exactly the modern "the town boasts...". The word has therefore travelled from loud vanity to neutral possession without breaking the chain of logic.

Register and tone. The plural noun "boasts" is distinctly censorious: no one describes their own words as "boasts" without irony, and the word tends to appear where self-praise is exposed as hollow (empty boasts, idle boasts). In the source passage the word is doing precisely that work: the dwarves' boasts are "confident" only for a moment, and the narrative quickly strips the confidence away — the very structure of the word, proud claim followed by deflation, mirrors what happens in the sentence.

Masonry technicality. A separate, unrelated word "boast" (noun and verb) survives in stonemasonry: "boasting" is the rough-hewing of stone, and a "boast" is a coarse tool or stroke used to begin shaping a block. This word comes from Old French bosser "to rough-hew, to emboss" and is etymologically distinct from the boast of bragging — a useful trap to note, since the two spellings are identical.

Grammar note. As a noun, "boasts" is simply the regular plural. As a verb it is the third-person singular present of the regular verb boast / boasts / boasted / boasting, used with "he, she, it", with a bare object ("boasts a fine cellar"), a prepositional complement ("boasts of his skill"), or a "that"-clause ("boasts that he never sleeps").
