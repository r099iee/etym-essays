- Middle English entry - strait came from Old French estreit, from Latin strictus (tight, drawn narrow), and originally meant simply narrow, tight, confined.
- Nautical specialization - by the early modern period the noun specialized to mean a narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies, a choke-point of currents and perils, from Gibraltar to Messina.
- Figurative extension - the plural straits grew the separate figurative sense of distress or difficulty (dire straits, straitened circumstances), both senses flowing from the same root idea of being drawn tight.
- Contemporary sense - straits is a formal word of hydrography and geopolitics, and the double life of the word - physical narrows and moral distress - remains one of its most distinctive features.
