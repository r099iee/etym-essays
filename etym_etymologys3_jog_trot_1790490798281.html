JOG — a shake of unknown origin: jog appears in the mid-16th century as jogge, shogge, to shake, push, jolt — to jog him on the shoulder, to jog out of sleep — and as to jog on, to trudge, to move heavily. OED, Etymonline, and Liberman agree the deeper origin is unknown and must be stated so. It belongs to a cluster of expressive Northern and Scots verbs — shog, shug, shock, shove, jig, jog — all denoting jerky movement, likely imitative rather than inherited, with no secure Proto-Germanic or PIE root. Middle English shoggen (to shake, 14th c., of uncertain relation) is the closest antecedent, with regular sh- to j- variation seen in shove / jolt pairs, but the link is plausible, not proven. What is secure is the 16th–17th c. stabilisation: jog as a frequentative push (to jog the elbow, to jog the memory) and to jog on as slow riding or walking. Shakespeare has jog on, jog on, the footpath way (Winter's Tale), already both literal and moral.

  • Sound symbolism: initial /dʒ/ plus short /ɒ/ plus /ɡ/ enacts the jolt; compare bob, bog, dodder, judder, joggle. Such words are coined and recoined rather than inherited, which explains the absence of cognates.
  • Memory sense (1620s): to jog the memory — to give recollection a push — becomes the most durable figurative child of the verb, alongside jogging along for easy travel.
  • Sporting descendant: 1960s jogging (Bowerman, Lydiard) revives jog-trot on foot as health exercise: slow running for fitness, directly continuing the jog-trot pace Ruskin rides in.

TROT — from Frankish treading to posting roads: trot is, by contrast, fully traceable. It enters Middle English as trotten from Old French troter, to trot, to bustle — itself from Frankish *trotton, to tread, to step, frequentative of Proto-Germanic *tredanan, to tread, to step — which continues PIE *der- or *dreu-, to run, to tread, to trip (cognate with Greek dromos, course, English tread, trade in its path sense). Old High German trotton (to tread, to go) and Middle High German trotten confirm the Germanic base; Old French borrows it for horse movement in the 12th c. (le trot du cheval), already distinguishing pas, trot, galop. Middle English trotten keeps the French /t/ and adds native infinitive -en, soon nominalised as a trot. Modern /trɒt/ shows the Great Vowel Shift shortening before a geminate and cluster simplification.

  • Gait systematisation: by the 18th c. riding masters fix walk – trot – canter – gallop with subdivisions jog-trot, dog-trot, extended trot, collected trot. Farriery books describe the diagonal two-beat and its jolting effect on the rider's seat.
  • Human extension: to trot about, to trot out excuses, on the trot (three wins on the trot), globe-trotter, trotline. The horse gait supplies a whole tempo vocabulary for busy coming-and-going.
  • Proverbial use: to trot out the old excuses, a well-trodden path keeps the treading root visible beside the gait. The compound jog-trot selects the slowest trot and adds the jog-jolt, fixing it as the emblem of monotony by the early 19th c.

Recombined, jog-trot is first recorded in the early 19th century (Keats 1818, sporting papers 1820s) as jog-trot, a transparent compound for the slowest trot and the dull round it emblematises. Its Victorian career is double: stable-manual literalism (how to sit a jog-trot without posting) and moral-essay figurative life (Carlyle, Ruskin, and George Eliot all contrast jog-trot respectability with galloping genius). The 20th century keeps both — riding schools still teach the jog, managers still deplore jog-trot thinking — while jogging spins off as a health cult. Ruskin's hired postchaise at a jog-trot pace thus sits at the compound's birth-moment: a new phrase for an old gait, carrying a child who will make slowness a philosophy of seeing.
