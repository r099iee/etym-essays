Reduplicative / sound-symbolic Germanic base *jaim- / *jēm- ('to moan, wail, lament'; an expressive, echoic formation native to Germanic — NO secure PIE etymon is reconstructed; a contested comparison with the PIE root *ǵem- 'to groan' (Greek góos 'lament') is floated by some scholars but rejected in the cautious OED account) → Proto-Germanic *jēmaraz (adjective, 'sorrowful, miserable, dejected') → Old English gēomor (adjective, 'sorrowful, sad, mournful') → Old English gēomrian / gīomrian (verb, 'to lament, to mourn, to bemoan'; a denominal verb in -ian formed from the adjective) → Middle English yameren / yomeren (c. 1300; 'to mourn, to complain, to whine, to chatter') → English yammer (verb, 'to utter a continuous querulous whine'; also noun by conversion, 'the sound of one who yammers') → English yammering (present participle, formed with the -ing suffix of continuous action) → YAMMERING Note on debate: the Proto-Indo-European stage is not securely established. The Germanic family — which includes Old Saxon jāmar 'sorrow', Old High German jāmar 'misery, wailing' (German Jammer), Middle Dutch jameren 'to lament' (Dutch jammer 'what a pity!'), and Old English gēomor — is best treated as an expressive, echoic formation native to Germanic; the tempting comparison with *ǵem- is generally rejected. These continental forms are sisters of the English chain, not ancestors.
