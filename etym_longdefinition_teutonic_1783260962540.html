Teutonic (adj.) — of, relating to, or characteristic of the Germanic peoples, languages, or culture; in older usage, specifically relating to the Teutones (also Teutoni), an ancient Germanic tribe, and by extension to the Germanic or "Teutonic" race as a whole. The word is the learned, archaic, and literary synonym of Germanic, with which it overlaps almost completely in scholarly usage.

Primary sense. Pertaining to the ancient Teutons — a tribe (or group of tribes) of the north-west, first heard of when they marched against Rome with the Cimbri in the late 2nd century BCE and were crushed by Marius at Aquae Sextiae in 102 BCE. From this tribal base the adjective was generalized in the 18th–19th centuries by philologists and historians to cover the whole Germanic branch of the Indo-European family: its languages (the "Teutonic languages"), its peoples ("the Teutonic race"), and its mythology and culture. In this scholarly sense Teutonic equals Germanic, and it was the standard term in English philology before Germanic displaced it.

Polysemy and extended senses. The word radiates into several registers:

  • Historical linguistics. "Teutonic languages" (Gothic, Old Norse, Old English, Old Saxon, Old High German, and their descendants) was the ordinary 19th-century term; "Teutonic" appears in the grammars of Grimm's generation and in English textbooks well into the 20th century. The source sentence, from Macaulay's History of England (1848), uses "the Teutonic" as a noun meaning the Teutonic language: Latin "drove out the Celtic; it was not driven out by the Teutonic."
  • The Teutonic Order. The Teutonic Knights (Ordo Teutonicus, founded c. 1190 at Acre during the Third Crusade) were the German crusading military order that conquered and converted Prussia in the 13th century; "Teutonic" is thus also an historical label for this order, its rule, and its state.
  • Mythology and folklore. "Teutonic myth," "Teutonic gods" — the mythology of the ancient Germanic peoples (Odin, Thor, and the northern pantheon), a phrase beloved of the 19th-century Romantics and of antiquarians seeking a northern counterpart to classical myth.
  • Cultural stereotype (colloquial). In modern informal English "Teutonic" is used, usually with a smile, of stereotypical German qualities: thoroughness, efficiency, orderliness, seriousness, rigidity, even heavy-handedness — "Teutonic efficiency," "Teutonic thoroughness." This is the word's only living casual sense.
  • Racialist history (now discredited). In 19th- and early 20th-century racial theory, "Teutonic" named a supposedly superior "race" or "stock" (the "Teutonic race"), a construct of philology and nationalism pressed into service for Anglo-Saxonist and pan-Germanist ideology and reaching its peak in the world wars. Modern scholarship rejects the racial framing; "Teutonic" survives only as a literary or historical word.

Historical-semantic bridge. The Roman name Teutones / Teutoni (from the Germanic tribal self-designation *þeudō, "the people") entered Latin, gave the adjective Teutonicus, and came into English in the 17th century as a learned classical term. Its career since then shows a characteristic arc: from tribal ethnonym (the specific Teutons), to pan-Germanic umbrella (the philological "Teutonic" of the 18th–19th centuries), to cultural and racial catchword (the Romantics and then the racists), and finally to a faded literary and jocular survival, its serious work now done by the neutral word Germanic.
