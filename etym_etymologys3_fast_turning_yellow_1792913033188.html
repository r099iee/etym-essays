The phrase fast turning yellow braids three old threads into one autumn observation, and each word brings its own millennia of drift before they meet under Tolkien elm.

Fast begins as firmness, not speed. Proto-Germanic *fastu- meant fixed and steadfast, surviving in Old English fæst for what is bound secure, from fast friends to fasting vows. The speed sense grew from the image of the firmly-planted runner straining forward: what holds its course goes swiftly. By Middle English fast meant both secure and quick, giving modern English the paradox of holding fast while running fast, and the adverbial haste inside fast turning.

Turning travels from the lathe to the leaf. Latin tornare, to work on a revolving lathe from Greek tornos, passed through Old French torner into Middle English turnen, broadening from rotation to every change of state: to turn traitor, to turn the tide, to turn yellow. The participle turning keeps the process open, naming change still under way rather than finished, which is why leaves are turning for weeks while they are turned only when fallen.

Yellow is among the oldest colour words in the family. Proto-Germanic *gelw-, kin to gold, glow, and Latin helvus, named the gleaming gold-green band of ripe grain, yolk, and autumn leaf. Old English geolu coloured gold, gall, and jaundice alike, already binding beauty, wealth, and sickness into one hue. Later spelling reforms gave modern yellow, but the pigment stayed put while chemistry caught up.

Botanical history then explains why haste and gold belong together:

  • Chlorophyll economics: trees salvage nitrogen from leaves before shedding them, draining green magnesium complexes fast in a cold bright week and unmasking slow-decaying yellow carotenoids beneath.
  • Elm habits: English elms yellow unevenly and often early under drought or disease, so a crown fast turning yellow yet still thick records a precise dated week of early autumn, exactly the walkers calendar.
  • From poetry to physiology: medieval lyrics read yellowing as dying; Victorian naturalists timed it with thermometers; twentieth-century biochemistry traced carotenoid retention to photoprotection, turning elegy into adaptation.

Socio-cultural layers gather round the colour. Yellow warned of plague flags, crowned harvest wheat, gilded altarpieces, and later stained cheap paperbacks and cowardice slang. Tolkien, writing tramps through a watercolour autumn, uses none of the slang and all of the shelter: his yellowing elm is bounty, not blame, a roof still thick enough for lunch and laughter. The three plain words thus carry lathe, gold-hoard, and harvest-field into one lunch-hour, and their very plainness is the point: any walker knows leaves that hurry into gold while still holding the rain.
