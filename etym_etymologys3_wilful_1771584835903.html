Built inside English Wilful is built entirely inside English from the noun will and the adjective-forming suffix -ful ("full of"): a wilful person is literally "full of will". The word first appears in Middle English (recorded by the fourteenth century) as wilful, in the sense "self-willed, obstinate, governed by one's own desires", and it has kept that core meaning for seven centuries. To follow its whole biography, however, we must descend to the root of will itself: the Proto-Indo-European verb *welh₁-, "to wish, will, choose". This root carried the idea of deliberate preference — choosing one thing over another because one desires it — and every branch of the family remembers that meaning. The root: PIE *welh₁- In the Indo-Iranian branch the root became the Sanskrit vṛ- (वृ), "to choose, wish, prefer": vṛṇoti "he chooses", vára "wish, boon, choice", and vara "the chosen one", which survives in Hindi as var, "bridegroom" — the man chosen in marriage. The same root, in the Avestan of ancient Iran, appears as var-, "to choose". At every stop the root keeps its signature meaning: what one wants is what one chooses. The Italic branch
  • Latin velle, "to wish, want" — one of the language's most important verbs; its noun voluntās, "will", and derived adjective voluntārius gave English voluntary, volition, and volunteer.
  • Latin compounds on the same stem: benevolēns "wishing well" → benevolent, benevolence; malevolēns "wishing ill" → malevolent; voluptās "pleasure" (what is wished) → voluptuous, voluptuary.
  • The Romance languages continue the verb directly — French vouloir, Italian volere — so that a modern speaker of French saying je veux ("I want") is still conjugating the same PIE root as the Sanskrit vṛṇoti and the English will.
The Germanic branch and the birth of wilful Proto-Germanic *weljaną, "to want, choose", produced the verb willan (modern English will), the noun will (Old English will, "will, desire, purpose"), and the related wela, "wealth, well-being" — a striking proof that in the old world-view prosperity was simply what one desired, hence English wealth and welfare, German Wohl, and the adverb well, "in a wished-for way". From the noun will, English appended -ful and produced wilful, attested by Chaucer's age with the sense of headstrong self-will that the novel preserves. The spelling split is nineteenth-century: British English keeps wilful (one l), American English uses willful (two l's). Semantic drift and the novel The semantics of wilful drifted from neutral to pejorative: early use could be admiring (abundant in will, determined), but from the seventeenth century the word darkened into firm disapproval — headstrong, disobedient to advice, rules, and convention — and in law wilful misconduct named harm done on purpose. In The Story of the Stone, the word is chosen precisely because it fuses judgment with choice: Bao-yu's wilfulness is not the blindness of the obstinate but the constant, deliberate preference of the self-willed — a personality that chooses its own desires, including the desire to treat girls kindly, against every expectation of his house. Seven centuries of use have not altered the word; it still means, as it always has, "full of will", and Lady Wang's rueful smile in the passage is the smile of a household that has learned, generation after generation, to live with precisely that.
