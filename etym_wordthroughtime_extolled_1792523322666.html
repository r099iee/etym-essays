- [Late 15th c. Late Middle English] — Borrowed directly from Latin extollere during the initial wave of Renaissance humanist translation, signifying the act of physically raising high or elevating someone's social honor and spiritual status - [perception: an elevated, scholarly Latinism associated with aristocratic eulogy and theological adoration]
- [16th–17th c. Elizabethan & Jacobean English] — Adopted into high literary and biblical translations (including the Geneva Bible and King James Version) to denote reverent, unreserved adoration of the Almighty, heavenly hosts, and virtuous monarchs - [perception: a grave, magnificent register of sacred worship and sovereign tribute]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Eras] — Expanded into literary criticism, philosophical essays, and patriotic histories, frequently applied to classical heroes, scientific pioneers, and civic institutions - [perception: formal, dignified, and authoritative rhetorical praise of enduring achievements]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary English] — Maintained in formal prose, literary fiction, journalism, and academic review, often appearing in the past-participial passive ("was extolled by critics"), while occasionally acquiring mild ironical nuance when excessive commercial puffery is exposed - [perception: serious, high-register term of enthusiastic public acclaim and critical endorsement]
