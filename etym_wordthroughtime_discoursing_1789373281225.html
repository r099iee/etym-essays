- Late 14th to 15th Century - Enters Middle English via Old French from Latin; initially utilized in theological translations and scholarly treatises to denote the faculty of human reason running step-by-step through arguments (discursive thought), occasionally retaining physical traces of running to and fro.
- 16th Century (Elizabethan Era) - Rapidly expands into vibrant literary and dramatic registers (Shakespeare, Spenser, Sidney); refers to eloquent conversation, high-minded dialogue between courtiers, and the oral or written communication of complex ideas.
- 17th to 18th Century - Deepens in Enlightenment philosophy and essayistic prose (Francis Bacon, John Locke, David Hume); "discoursing" describes systematic intellectual argumentation, natural philosophy lectures, and civilized salon disputation.
- 19th Century - Retains an elevated Victorian literary register, frequently applied to grand, unhurried conversationalists (such as Samuel Taylor Coleridge holding forth at Highgate) who mesmerized listeners with encyclopedic disquisitions.
- Mid to Late 20th Century - Reconceptualized through structuralism, linguistics, and continental critical theory (Michel Foucault, Jürgen Habermas); "the discoursing subject" becomes a foundational theoretical term designating how speakers are embedded within cultural power relations.
- 21st Century - Preserves its dual presence: in academia and literary criticism, it denotes rigorous ideological and linguistic positioning, while in general prose it describes an erudite, prolonged exposition.
