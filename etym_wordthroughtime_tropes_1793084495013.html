- Classical antiquity — the rhetorician's term: Greek tropos names the turning of a word from its proper sense; Roman schools drill the four master tropes as the crown of eloquence.
- Middle Ages — chant and commentary: tropes multiply as musical interpolations in the liturgy, while allegorists turn every text toward hidden senses.
- Renaissance — the ornament of princes: handbooks of rhetoric teach tropes to schoolboys and courtiers alike; Shakespeare turns them by the hundred, living and deliberate.
- 18th–19th c. — from ornament to origin: philosophers notice that tropes die into literal meanings, and Emerson builds continents of language from their shells.
- 20th c. — theory and cinema: structuralists catalogue tropes as the machinery of meaning, while film critics spot the Western's tropes and the melodrama's.
- Today — fandom's favourite word: tropes names every stock device of fiction, lovingly listed on wikis — the rhetoricians' term turned populist, itself a trope of a trope.
