- [Old English: a word born on the body] - “Ridge” (OE hrycg) first names the back and spine of a man or beast; even in Anglo-Saxon, though, the same word is already mapping that bony line onto the land as the “crest of a hill” — the word begins straddling anatomy and geography at once.
- [Old English–Middle English: the land takes over] - The topographical sense grows dominant as the form settles through Middle English “rigge”: a ridge is a long narrow elevation with ground falling away on both sides. The bodily sense quietly retreats from daily use, surviving only as a fossil in “ridge of the nose” and “ridge of a roof.”
- [Middle English–early modern: a productive landscape word] - The word spreads across English place-names and dialect (northern and Scots “rigg,” “rig”), names the corrugated “ridge and furrow” of medieval fields, and becomes the standard way of describing upland terrain as lines of crest — never single summits.
- [Modern: the scientific ridge] - It enters the technical vocabularies of geology, mountaineering, and building — “mid-ocean ridge,” “ridge line,” “ridge walk,” “ridgepole” — and in cartographic or literary use the plural RIDGES reads as an inventory of parallel crests descending one after another.
- [Contemporary sense] - A wholly standard word in every register, free of archaism and dialect colour; its topographical sense is primary, yet the original anatomical image is still faintly audible — every ridge of earth named still echoes the backbone of a beast.
