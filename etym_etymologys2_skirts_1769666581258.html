Proto-Indo-European: *(s)ker- "to cut, clip, shear, sever" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; a highly productive root also behind Latin caro "flesh," curtus "shortened," and English shear, short, share) → Proto-Germanic: *skurtaz "short, cut short" (adjective; reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Proto-Germanic: *skurtijōn "short garment, shirt, tunic" (noun; reconstructed — a garment made of cut/shorn cloth) → Old Norse: skyrta "shirt, tunic, a short-cut garment" (noun; attested in runic and saga sources, c. 9th–13th c.; the hard sk- is preserved, unlike the English palatalized form) → Middle English: skirt(e) "lower part of a tunic or robe, hanging garment" (noun; borrowed from Old Norse via northern Danelaw dialects, attested from c. 13th–14th c.) → Early Modern English: skirt + plural -s → skirts "outer edges, borders of an area" (spatial sense attested from the late 16th c.; the garment sense coexists throughout) → Modern English: skirts "outer parts, edges, or borders of an area; garment hanging from the waist" ◄ TARGET Debated / nuanced stages: - The derivation of Old Norse skyrta from Proto-Germanic *skurtijōn (in turn from *skurtaz "short," from PIE *(s)ker- "to cut") is standard and secure. The garment is, literally, a "cut-short" piece of cloth. - Important doublet note: native Old English scyrte (with palatalized sh-) gave modern shirt (upper-body garment), while the Norse loan skyrta (hard sk-) gave skirt (lower hanging garment). English thus kept both — a famous case of a borrowed twin word. - The spatial sense "skirts of a forest/town" is an English-internal metaphorical extension of the garment sense (the hanging border = the outer edge), attested from the late 16th century; it is not a separate loan.
