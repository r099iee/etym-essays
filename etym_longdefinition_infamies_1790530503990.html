Primary Definition and Core Lexical Senses

Infamies (/ˈɪn.fə.miz/) is the plural nominal form of infamy, designating extremely disgraceful, monstrous, shameful, or abominable acts, deeds, or events that evoke intense public outrage and universal condemnation. In its countable plural manifestation, it refers to discrete, notorious crimes or abuses that inflict severe damage upon moral standards, human decency, or civil law.

Lexicographically, infamies operates across several distinct yet deeply connected tiers of meaning:

  • Discrete Outrageous Deeds and Transgressions: Plural countable acts of extraordinary cruelty, treason, corruption, or betrayal that result in universal public opprobrium, enduring historical notoriety, and moral condemnation.
  • Historical and Institutionalized Atrocities: Systemic or state-sanctioned abuses (such as despotic purges, tyrannical edicts, or mass exploitations) that stand as monumental stains of dishonor across the historical record.
  • Instances of Profound Public Shame and Humiliation: Specific situations or conditions in which individuals, families, or institutions are subjected to extreme social disgrace, total loss of civic reputation, and enduring ignominy.

Polysemy, Roman Legal Roots, and Semantic Extensions

  • Roman Civic and Legal Status (Classical Infamia): In ancient Roman jurisprudence, infamia was not merely a subjective feeling of shame, but a formal, institutional legal condition. A citizen branded with infamia by the Roman censor or praetor suffered a severe reduction in civic rights: they were stripped of voting privileges, barred from holding public office, disqualified from testifying as legal witnesses or filing lawsuits, and excluded from honorable social orders. Certain professions (such as gladiators, prostitutes, actors, and usurers) automatically incurred infamia.
  • Theological and Canon Law Perspectives: In medieval Catholic canon law, infamia juris (infamy by law) and infamia facti (infamy established by common public notoriety) represented formal impediments preventing individuals from receiving holy orders or holding ecclesiastical benefices due to notorious scandalous conduct.
  • Literary and Dramatic Usage: In high-literary, rhetorical, and historical prose (exemplified by Leo Tolstoy, Edward Gibbon, and Thomas Carlyle), infamies serves as a majestic, elevated indictment against the abuses of despots, conquerors, and corrupt aristocracies. It pairs frequently with words like atrocities, tyrannies, and iniquities to convey cumulative, unbearable moral degradation.

Semantic Reasoning and Historical Bridges

The transition from the Latin abstract quality of being ill-spoken of (in- ["not"] + fama ["fame/reputation"]) to the English plural noun denoting concrete physical acts follows a well-documented trajectory of linguistic reification. In Classical Latin and Early Middle English, infamy was exclusively an uncountable, abstract condition of dishonor and evil repute. During the Early Modern period (16th century), English writers began employing the rhetorical figure of metonymy to transfer the word from the condition of disgrace to the specific actions that produced that disgrace. Thus, individual crimes of treason, murder, or pillage ceased to be merely "deeds producing infamy"—they became, in themselves, infamies.
