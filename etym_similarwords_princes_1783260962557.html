• monarchs: Similar because both denote sovereign supreme rulers of a state; differs because monarch is a narrower constitutional term for a single supreme hereditary ruler, while princes historically includes any ruler — kings, dukes, even non-sovereign royal sons — and is not by itself a constitutional rank.
  • kings: Similar because both name male royal rulers; differs because king specifically denotes the chief sovereign of a kingdom, while prince may denote a ruler of lower rank or a royal son who holds no crown.
  • rulers: Similar because both name those who govern a people or territory; differs because rulers is a general, title-neutral word covering presidents and oligarchs as well as royals, while princes belongs specifically to royal or princely hierarchies.
  • sovereigns: Similar because both denote independent supreme authorities; differs because sovereign emphasizes legal independence and supremacy, while princes often names subordinates in a hierarchy (sons, vassals) who lack full sovereignty.
  • potentates: Similar because both name powerful rulers, often of small or exotic states; differs because potentate is pejorative or grandiloquent, implying arbitrary personal power, while princes is a neutral rank-title.
  • emperors: Similar because both name supreme hereditary rulers; differs because emperor denotes the highest imperial rank, theoretically above kings and princes, while prince is a lower and more flexible rank.
  • dukes: Similar because both are hereditary noble or royal titles below the crown; differs because a duke is a high nobleman whose rank derives from a duchy and is not necessarily of royal blood, while princes traditionally implies royal descent or supreme rule.
  • archdukes: Similar because both are hereditary titles above ordinary nobility; differs because archduke is a specifically Austrian-Habsburg title, while princes is universal.
  • crown princes / heirs apparent: Similar because both denote the designated royal successor; differs because heir apparent is a legal-descriptive term of succession law, while crown prince is the actual royal title, and princes in general covers many non-successors.
  • chiefs / chieftains: Similar because both name leaders who are "first" in a group, echoing Latin princeps "first"; differs because chief is tribal or organizational and carries no dynastic or royal claim.
  • magnates / merchant princes: Similar because both denote wealthy, powerful figures of commerce; differs because magnate is purely economic and title-free, while merchant prince deliberately metaphorizes royal precedence.
  • lords: Similar because both are titles of authority in a hierarchy; differs because lord is vastly broader (a feudal, noble, or divine title) and lacks the specific "first-taker / first in line" sense of princes.
