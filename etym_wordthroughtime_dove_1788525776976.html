- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Inherited as dūfe (Northumbrian dūfe, West Saxon *dūfe), referring strictly to the wild or domesticated pigeon/dove bird, especially in biblical translations - Concrete avian and religious term.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - Evolved into douve, doufe, or dowve, deeply embedded in Christian liturgical literature, chivalric romance, and vernacular love lyrics as a symbol of virgin purity, gentle love, and the Holy Ghost - Spiritual and romantic devotion.
- Early Modern English (16th–18th c.) - Fixed in standard spelling as dove; used as an affectionate term of endearment for a beloved woman or innocent child, and in natural history to classify smaller columbid species - Affectionate and zoological distinction.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Flourished in aesthetic and fairy-tale literature (e.g. Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose, where red roses are compared to the crimson feet of the dove) as an archetype of romantic beauty, innocence, and pathos - High aesthetic and poetic resonance.
- 20th–21st Century - Expanded during the Cold War and Vietnam War into political and macroeconomic jargon ('hawks vs. doves') denoting advocates of peace, negotiation, and monetary easing - Political, diplomatic, and financial idiom.
