Squirmed (the past tense of squirm) means 'to twist, wriggle, or writhe the body from side to side,' here in 'He squirmed through the opening': the figure squeezes through a narrow aperture not by stepping or crawling but by contorting the whole body — shoulders twisted, limbs drawn in, spine flexing — worming his way through the gap. The verb emphasizes at once the narrowness of the opening (which will not admit an upright body), the continuous serpentine twisting this forces upon him (a succession of turning, writhing motions rather than one clean movement), and the undignified, animal quality of the whole process, the body reduced to worm-like undulation, sliding through like a serpent or an eel. The tone is comic-pathetic and vividly physical: the reader feels the tight squeeze and the slow twisting progress through darkness, every movement wrung out of a body too large for its passage. The past tense reports the whole laborious event as completed — he is through, but the memory of the contortion lingers.

Distinguishing it from similar verbs sharpens the picture: wriggled is very close but stresses rapid, small, side-to-side motions, as of a worm on a hook or a child held fast, without the sense of forward progress; writhed implies involuntary contortions, usually of pain or agony, and often in place rather than along a path; twisted names a single torsion without the continuous travel; crawled is limb-based and deliberate, moving on hands and knees; slithered is a smooth gliding, as of a snake's belly over a surface, sleek rather than strained; and wormed (one's way) emphasizes stealth or ingratiation as much as motion, a manner of getting in somewhere one is not wanted. Squirmed uniquely combines sustained twisting with forward progress through constricted space — the exact motion of a body forcing itself through an opening too small for it, all wriggle and no grace.


Polysemy and the full modern range of squirmed / squirm:
  • Physical sense: to twist and wriggle the body; used of worms, eels, and snakes, and of people — squirm out of a hold, squirm through an opening, squirm under a fence. This is the sense in the passage.
  • Figurative moral / emotional sense (nineteenth century): to squirm is to writhe with embarrassment, discomfort, guilt, or anxiety — 'the question made him squirm,' 'she squirmed at the compliment.' Bridge: the involuntary twisting of a caught body is transferred to the involuntary discomfort of a caught conscience; a person cornered by an awkward truth twists inwardly just as a body twists to escape a grasp.
  • Derived forms: squirmy (adjective, nineteenth century) describes a restless, fidgety child or a situation that breeds unease; a squirm (noun) names the motion itself or a pang of embarrassment; a squirmer is one who squirms, colloquially a restless child.
  • Register and sound-symbolism: squirm is informal and vividly physical; its skw- onset and curling -erm syllable enact the very motion it names (echoic / sound-symbolic), so the word feels snake-like in the mouth. Writing chooses squirm when the motion is strained, twisting, and slightly comic, and writhe when it is agonized.
  • Rhetoric and media: 'squirm' is a fixture of political and interview language — 'the witness squirmed under questioning,' 'lawyers watched the CEO squirm' — where the physical image of the cornered body stands for evasive discomfort before scrutiny.

Thus the two living patterns — the physical (squirm through an opening, squirm out of a hold) and the figurative (squirm with guilt) — are equally standard today, and the context sentence activates the physical sense while quietly reserving the word's comic-pathetic, undignified weight: a body that squirmed through an opening is a body that had to wriggle like a worm to get where it was going, and the word lets the reader feel every twist of that ungraceful progress.
