PIE *h₁oḱtṓ(u) "eight" (reconstructed numeral, c. 4000 BCE; the traditional explanation as a dual formation meaning "two sets of four fingers" is widely repeated but debated — see note below) → Proto-Italic *oktō "eight" (c. 2nd–1st millennium BCE) → Latin octō "eight" (attested from the earliest Latin inscriptions and texts, c. 7th–3rd c. BCE) → Italian ottetto "a group of eight musicians" (19th c. CE; formed on Italian otto "eight" with the diminutive suffix -etto; the immediate model for the English word) → English octet "composition for eight performers; group of eight" (first attested 1865 CE; the spelling was latinized to oct- on the model of Latin octō, with the suffix reshaped to the French/Latin-looking -et) → English octet (present-day rootWord) "a group of eight; an eight-line stanza or poem; (chemistry) eight valence electrons; (computing) eight bits" ◄ TARGET Debate / nuance note: The internal structure of the PIE numeral for "eight" is debated. The traditional account (followed by many handbooks) reads *h₁oḱtṓ(u) as a dual formation literally meaning "two sets of four (fingers)," from a root *h₁eḱ- "sharp, pointed" (as in Latin acies, Greek akmē), referring to the two four-finger groups excluding the thumbs. Some modern linguists treat this as folk-etymology and regard the numeral as a lexicalized, unreduced root of unknown internal shape; its semantic analysis is therefore best described as uncertain. The Latin-to-Italian-to-English chain itself is fully attested. Note also that English took the general term octet partly by adaptation of Italian ottetto and partly by neo-classical formation on octo- + -et, and that the poetic sense (eight-line stanza) and later chemical and computing senses were extensions of the numeral's meaning in English itself.
