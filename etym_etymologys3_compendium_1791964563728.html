Latin compendium is a word from the marketplace before it is a word from the library. It joins com-, “together”, to pendere, “to weigh” — the verb behind pondus “weight” and pensum “something weighed out, a task”. To compendere is to weigh things together, to strike a balance; a compendium is the result of that balancing, and hence a “saving” or “gain”. Plautus and the comic poets use it for profit; classical prose uses it for a short way round and for dispatch, as in compendium facere, “to make short work of a thing”.

From “short way” to “short book” the step is small and was taken in later Latin, when scholars needed a name for the digest that saved a reader the labour of many volumes.

  • Classical Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE): “saving, gain; shortcut”. The metaphor is economic and spatial, not yet bibliographic.
  • Late and medieval Latin (4th–13th c.): compendium becomes a title-word for summaries — Compendium theologiae, Compendium musicae — as Christian and monastic schools digest patristic and classical learning into teachable form.
  • Renaissance (16th c.): the printed compendium is a commercial and pedagogical genre; humanist schoolmasters produce compendia of rhetoric, dialectic and Ciceronian idiom for the classroom.
  • English (1580s onward): borrowed directly from Latin, the word enters scholarly English meaning “brief compilation containing the substance of a larger work”.

Form and phonology. English keeps the Latin shape almost untouched — /kəmˈpen.di.əm/ — with the classical plural compendia alongside the anglicized compendiums. The adjective compendious, from Latin compendiosus “short, brief; saving”, entered English in the fifteenth century and survives mainly in formal prose (“a compendious account”). The rare verb compend and the noun compend are archaic curiosities; the modern word family is carried almost entirely by the noun and the adjective.

Literary and cultural context. The compendium is the characteristic book of an age of authority. When a culture believes it already possesses the truth in a canonical set of texts, the useful work is to digest, order and transmit. Medieval compendia of canon law and theology, Renaissance compendia of rhetoric, eighteenth-century compendia of experimental philosophy and Victorian compendia of useful knowledge all share this logic. The genre has a double reputation: it democratizes learning and it encourages second-hand knowledge, so the compendium is always suspected of being what someone knows instead of the original.

The Ciceronian case. Nizzoli’s Thesaurus Ciceronianus (1535) belongs to the high Renaissance industry of Ciceronian aids — dictionaries, phrase-books, commonplace collections and treatises on imitation. Its claim to be a compendium of “all pure Ciceronian language” was both a scholarly boast and a pedagogical promise: master this volume and you possess the words of Cicero. The fierce debates over whether an author might use a word not found in Cicero show how much authority such a compendium could carry, and how quickly a convenience could harden into a dogma.

Modern usage. Today the word survives in three main habitats: the learned handbook; the British “compendium of games”; and digital curation, where “compendium” names an aggregated resource. Its connotation is orderly, slightly old-fashioned and mildly self-important; calling a list a compendium is a small act of dignification, and readers have learned to discount accordingly.
