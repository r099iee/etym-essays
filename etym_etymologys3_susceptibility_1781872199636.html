Susceptibility is a learned abstract noun of the Latin tradition whose deepest image is the hand closing on an object. Proto-Indo-European *kap-, "to grasp, hold, seize," supplies Latin capere, one of the most productive verbs in the language: to take, seize, capture, contain, and, metaphorically, to understand. From capere English inherits a throng of relatives—capture, captive, capacity, accept, receive, concept, perception, princeps, forceps—each preserving some facet of taking or holding. The specific path to susceptibility runs through the compound suscipere, formed from sub-, "up from under," and capere: literally to take up from below, hence to support, undertake, catch, admit, or receive. The prefix assimilates its final consonant before the initial consonant of the base (sub- + capere > sus-cipere), a regular Latin sound process that explains the odd sus- spelling. Roman usage of suscipere is richly practical and social. One takes up a burden (onus suscipere), undertakes a duty, acknowledges a child as one's own, receives a guest, or accepts an obligation; the verb lives at the meeting point of physical lifting and institutional undertaking. The past participial stem suscept- feeds the Late Latin adjective susceptibilis, "capable of receiving, able to be sustained," and the medieval scholastic noun susceptibilitas, "capability of being affected." English forms susceptible around 1600 and susceptibility by the 1640s, initially in the broad sense of capability of being influenced or of receiving impressions. The dating matters: these words enter English during the great age of Latinate scientific and philosophical coinage, when writers in medicine, moral philosophy, and natural science needed precise nouns for capacities, dispositions, and liabilities, and when the modern vocabulary of sensibility and aesthetics was still being built. Scientific and medical English later specialize susceptibility to liability to disease or external agents, so that by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it is a clinical term; psychological and literary English keep the older breadth—openness to feeling, influence, and aesthetic effect. In physics, the nineteenth century's study of magnetism and electromagnetism attaches susceptibility to the ratio of a material's magnetization to an applied field, extending the metaphor of a medium that "receives" an influence into exact quantitative form; electric susceptibility follows the same pattern for dielectric response. In epidemiology, twentieth-century compartment models designate as susceptible those individuals who can still be infected, a sense now familiar from SIR-type diagrams and public-health reporting. The word thus splits into a precise scientific register and a qualitative humanistic register without ever losing the underlying image of something that takes in an influence. Pater stands squarely in the literary line. When he writes that education becomes complete in proportion as susceptibility to impressions increases in depth and variety, he is not diagnosing weakness but prescribing the cultivation of receptive power. The semantic journey thus runs: grasp → take up/support → capable of being taken/received → open to influence → refined capacity for aesthetic impression. At each stage the prefix sub-/sus- colors the action as something that rises to meet or is lifted into the self rather than merely seized by force, and the passive valence of the word—being capable of being affected—is exactly what the aesthetic program needs: the critic does not impose meaning on art but is seized by it, and the depth and variety of that seizure measure the completeness of education. Sister formations from capere—accept, receive, capacity—remain nearby in the lexicon, so the English speaker still half-hears the ancient gesture of taking-in whenever susceptibility is named. In aesthetic criticism that taking-in becomes the measure of a finished education; in medicine and physics it becomes a precisely measurable parameter of response. The word's enduring power lies in this double life: quantitative in the laboratory, qualitative and personal in the study of art. Historical attestations run from seventeenth-century moral and medical prose, through the eighteenth-century language of the nerves and sensibility, into the Victorian criticism of Pater and Walter Horatio Pater's circle, and down to the modern scientific lexicon. The drift from active grasp to passive openness is the whole story of the word: a root that began in the hand ends in the fine-tuned receptivity of the educated mind, and the same Latin path that yields "capture" yields also the quiet virtue of being wonderfully capturable by beauty.
