1. The toponym Trianon. The name is first attested as Triarnum in a papal bull of Alexander III dated 1163, where it designates a hamlet and its lands in the fields south-west of the later palace. No earlier form is known, and the etymology is genuinely disputed. The explanation most often cited reads the name as a Latinised compound meaning “junction of three domains”, comparable to Latin trifinium, “a meeting of three boundaries”; on that view the hamlet grew where three seigneurial territories met. The link is plausible topographically but is not demonstrated by a documented pre-Latin form, and other suggestions — a Gallo-Roman personal name with the suffix -onem, for instance — circulate without secure documentation. The prudent statement is that Trianon is a medieval French place name of uncertain origin, of which only the Latinised shape Triarnum and the later French Trianon can be verified.

2. From hamlet to royal estate. The hamlet gave its name to the estate acquired by Louis XIV, who built the Trianon de porcelaine in 1670 and replaced it in 1687 with the Grand Trianon. In French usage Trianon therefore came to mean a royal house of retreat, and the phrase Petit Trianon was formed in the 1760s when Gabriel’s small pavilion was added to the domain. The adjective petit is contrastive: the new building was small beside the Grand Trianon, and its function was domestic rather than ceremonial.

3. The adjective little. English little descends from Old English lȳtel, “not large, not much; short in distance or time; unimportant”, with cognates in Old Saxon luttil, Old High German luzzil, Old Norse lítill and Gothic leitils. The Germanic adjective is reconstructed as *lūtilaz, and it is often connected with a root meaning “to stoop, crouch”, hence “small”; the deeper PIE link (*leud-) is conventional rather than certain. The word’s semantic range is unusually wide: it covers size (a little house), duration (a little while), youth (a little boy), importance (a little matter), affection (the little ones), quantifier use (little money, a little bread) and adverbial understatement (little did he know).

4. The compound. The French name Petit Trianon was calqued into English as Little Trianon, a transparent rendering of petit by little. The calque belongs to the nineteenth century, when English travel writing and historical prose regularly translated French building names; Carlyle’s use in The French Revolution (1837) is a conspicuous example. Modern English prefers Petit Trianon, keeping the French adjective, and “Little Trianon” now reads as period colour. The compound is endocentric: the head is Trianon, the place, and little restricts it to the small pavilion rather than the estate as a whole.

5. Semantic and cultural extension.

  • Trianon alone can denote the estate, the Grand Trianon, or the whole complex of gardens and pavilions; context decides.
  • The Treaty of Trianon (1920), signed in the Grand Trianon, transferred the name from architecture to diplomacy and made it, in Hungarian memory, a symbol of territorial loss.
  • The Petit Trianon has become a byword for the private life of Marie-Antoinette and for the artificial rustic ideal of the late eighteenth century, especially through the Hameau de la Reine.
  • In English the unqualified “Trianon” is rarer than “Versailles”; the compound survives mainly in historical writing, guidebooks and translations.

6. Why the name matters here. Carlyle’s “Little Trianon” is not a neutral toponym. The English adjective makes the royal retreat sound like a cottage, and the sentence moves the dying king from that small private house back into “his own Chateau of Versailles”, the vast public stage. The name thus carries the chapter’s argument: a king who has withdrawn into a little private world is carried back, at the end, into the ceremonial palace he cannot escape.
