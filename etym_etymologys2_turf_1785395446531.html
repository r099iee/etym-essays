TURF is a native Germanic word with one of the most conservative phonetic histories in the language — its shape has survived essentially unchanged for well over a thousand years. CHAIN: PIE *derbh- "to wind, twist, bind together" (c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; also the base of Sanskrit darbhá- "tuft of bound grass" and Old High German zerban "to wind, spin") → (zero-grade *dr̥bʰ-, with Germanic metathesis of r) Proto-Germanic *turbaz "turf, sod, clod of earth bound by roots, block of peat" (c. 1st c. CE; cognates: Old Norse torf, Old Frisian turf, Middle Dutch turf, Old High German zurba — see note) → Old English turf "turf, sod, earthen surface" (9th c. CE; plural tyrf) → Middle English turf "turf, sod, peat" (12th–15th c. CE) → English TURF (modern form, unchanged since the 16th c.; extended senses "the turf" horse-racing 18th c., "turf war" c. 1950) NOTE ON DEBATE: The Germanic forms are securely attested across the whole branch, but the deeper PIE connection is debated. The usual reconstruction is PIE *derbh- "to wind, twist, bind", from which both Proto-Germanic *turbaz and Sanskrit darbhá- (a tuft of kuśa grass bound for Vedic ritual) are derived on the zero-grade *dr̥bʰ-, the Germanic form showing metathesis to *turb-. Some scholars accept the Greek δρέπω (drépō) "to pluck, gather" as a further cognate; others doubt it, and the whole PIE-level equation rests on the root meaning "to bind" — the image of roots and earth twisted together into a fabric. The connection is probable but not certain and is flagged here rather than asserted as fact.
