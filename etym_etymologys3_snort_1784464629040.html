Snort belongs to one of the oldest and most striking subsystems of English vocabulary: the expressive sn- family of nose-and-breath words. Its history is not the tidy story of a Latin word passing through French into English, but the messier, more human story of a language building words directly out of the sound of the body. 1. The expressive sn- family and the question of a PIE root English possesses a remarkable cluster of words beginning with sn- that all concern the nose, breath, and sharp noise: snore, sniff, snuff, snivel, snarl, snuffle, snout, and snort itself. Historical linguists explain this as sound symbolism: the initial sn- sequence iconically suggests the passage of air through the nose, the flattening of the nasal passages, and the friction of breath. Such words are "expressive" rather than normal inherited lexemes — they are coined, reinforced, and reshaped over and over across dialects because the sound itself carries the meaning. This is why a single neat Proto-Indo-European reconstruction for the whole set is impossible: the family is largely Germanic-internal sound-symbolism, reinforced rather than strictly descended. A loose connection to a broader Indo-European root *sner- "to hiss, whisper, snore" has been suggested, and Greek verbs such as snérkhō "to snore" do look tempting as distant relatives. But most specialists hesitate to call these true cognates, because expressive vocabulary is notoriously prone to independent, convergent creation. The honest position is that snort's deepest pre-Germanic stage is unknown, and no responsible dictionary manufactures a clean single-line PIE pedigree for it. 2. Middle English origins: snorten and the snore cluster The first attested English forms appear in the 14th century. Middle English snorten meant to snore or to breathe heavily through the nose, and by extension to make a harsh nasal sound; it sits directly beside snoren "to snore" and the noun snore. Early senses cluster around the loud, ungoverned breath of sleep and the sharp nasal sounds of irritation or exertion. The word is clearly built on the same expressive material as Dutch snorken "to snore, snort" and German schnarchen "to snore" — parallel creations within Germanic that reinforce one another across the North Sea rather than deriving from a single documented ancestor. 3. Early Modern English: the verb and noun settle In the 16th century snort emerges clearly as both verb and noun with the core meaning it retains today: to force air explosively through the nose, and the sound so made. The equine sense — the horse's loud blowing — is prominent from early on, giving the word a vivid animal anchor that keeps it physical and unrefined. Alongside the raw sound, the expressive senses of contempt, disbelief, and derision develop naturally: a snort is abrupt, unpolished, and impossible to dress in courtesy, so it becomes the perfect audible gesture of dismissal. By the 17th and 18th centuries, "to snort at" a proposal is a recognized idiom of scorn, and "a snort" can be a noise of angry or amused contempt. 4. Semantic extensions in the modern period
  • Laughter — "a snort of laughter" becomes a fixed phrase for a laugh forced through the nose, usually suppressed or ungraceful; the word names the physical failure to contain amusement.
  • Drug slang — from the 20th century, "to snort" means to inhale a powdered drug sharply through the nose, and "a snort" is the dose so taken; the sharp nasal inhalation supplies the bridge.
  • Machinery — "snort" is applied colloquially to loud, throbbing engine and instrument noises by extension from the bodily blast.
  • Nautical — the submarine "snort" (snorkel), an air tube for submerged running, transfers the nose-image to technology.
5. The literary moment in The Hobbit In The Hobbit, Gloin's "Humph!" is glossed as "some snort more or less like that." The narrator deliberately refuses to dignify the dwarf's reaction as articulate language: it is body noise, not rhetoric. The whole history of snort — sound-symbolic, animal, contemptuous, unpolished — is compressed into that one parenthetical. Skeptical air through the nose, not eloquence through the mouth: exactly the right sound for a dwarf weighing whether a comfortable hobbit can ever be a burglar.
