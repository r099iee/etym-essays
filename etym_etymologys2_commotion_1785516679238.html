PIE *mewə- / *meuə- 'to push away, thrust off' (reconstructed; also cited as *mēuə-, the exact ablaut is debated but the 'move/push' semantics are secure) → Latin movēre 'to move, set in motion, agitate' (attested from the earliest Latin literature, 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin commovēre 'to move violently, to stir up, to agitate' (com- 'together, thoroughly' + movēre; attested in Caesar and Cicero, 1st c. BCE) → Latin commōtiō (genitive commōtiōnis) 'a violent moving, agitation, upheaval, disturbance' — a verbal noun formed from the participle stem commōt- of commovēre with the suffix -iō (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE; the medical commōtiō cerebrī 'shaking of the brain, concussion' is a later technical extension) → Latin accusative commōtiōnem (the case form that fed the Romance and Old French borrowings) → Old French commocion / commotion 'tumult, agitation, uprising' (12th–13th c.) → Middle English commocioun, commotion 'violent agitation, public disorder, upheaval' (attested c. 1380–1400) → modern English COMMOTION (current form and full sense range). Debated/nuanced stages: The PIE root behind Latin movēre is reconstructed as *mewə-/ *meuə- 'to push away' by Pokorny and others, but whether it is further related to Greek ameibō 'to exchange' or to other 'move' roots is disputed; the stages from Latin movēre onward are all securely attested. The prefix com- in commovēre functions as an intensifier ('to move thoroughly') as much as a marker of 'togetherness' — both glosses are given in the grammars, and both feed the English sense of motion spreading through a crowd.
