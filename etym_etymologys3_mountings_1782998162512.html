MOUNTINGS: A MOUNTAIN SMALL ENOUGH TO FIT IN THE MIND The noun mountings is the plural verbal noun of the verb mount, and that verb carries a mountain inside it. Its ultimate source is the Proto-Indo-European root *men-, meaning 'to stand out, project, rise' — the root from which the ancient languages built their words for heights and prominences. In Italic the root, extended with a -t-, produced *mont-, and Latin mons (genitive montis), 'mountain', is attested from the earliest Latin literature; Plautus already uses mons freely of heights both literal and figurative. The same root made the Celtic word for mountain independently: Welsh mynydd, Cornish meneth, and Breton menez are all 'mountain' and all descend from PIE *men- — proof that the equation 'projecting mass = mountain' is as old as the language family itself.
  • Homonym warning: there is a second, completely unrelated PIE root *men- meaning 'to think, have in mind', which gave Sanskrit manas, Greek ménos, Latin mens, and English mind, mental, mention, and comment. A mountain is not a mind; the two roots are homonyms and must never be merged — a trap lazy dictionaries fall into constantly.
  • The -t- extension: *men- + -t- gives *mont-, a shared Italo-Celtic innovation; its precise function (a nominal suffix marking the prominent thing) is debated, but the resulting 'mountain' sense is firm.
It was in Vulgar Latin, the everyday Latin of soldiers, farmers, and traders, that the mountain became a verb. From mons, montis the Romans coined *montāre, 'to climb, to go up a mountain', and as the empire dissolved into the Romance languages, each one carried the verb forward: Italian montare, Spanish and Portuguese montar, French monter, all meaning 'to go up, mount, rise'. Through Anglo-French, the Norman form of Old French, the verb entered Middle English in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries as mounten, and by the early modern period it had become the ordinary English mount — to climb, to rise, to go up onto a horse, to ascend stairs or a throne.
  • Sound and form: Latin mons, montis → Vulgar Latin *montāre → Old French monter shows the regular loss of the final syllables of the infinitive and the stability of the -nt- cluster; English mounten simply naturalizes the French verb with the Middle English infinitive suffix -en.
  • Derivatives cluster: the same French verb bred a rich family — amount ('to rise to a total', from Old French amont 'upward'), surmount ('to rise above, overcome'), remount, dismount, and paramount ('supreme', literally 'above all'), all built on the mountain-image of rising.
The verbal noun mounting came naturally from the verb: a mounting is literally 'a rising'. For centuries the word's senses were concrete and technical — the act of ascending, the putting of a gem or picture in its setting (a mount), the supporting frame in which an instrument is fixed. The modern dictionary still leads with these: a mounting is a bracket or support, and in photography and engineering the word is entirely ordinary.
  • Wordsworth's transference (1805): The Prelude's 'mountings of the mind' is a decisive figurative step. Wordsworth pluralizes the verbal noun and turns it on the inner life: the mind does not merely rise; it 'mounts', with all the physical energy of climbing a fell or vaulting onto a horse. The plurality ('mountings') makes the rises recurrent and involuntary — visitations that 'come fast upon' the speaker. This is a distinctively Romantic coinage, aligning psychological elevation with the sublime landscape of the Lake District.
  • Modern residue of the figure: outside Wordsworth, the participle mounting supplies the familiar figure of steady increase — 'mounting anger, panic, costs, tension' — where the image of something climbing higher and higher is now so worn that we barely notice it is a metaphor at all.
Mountings therefore condenses a full arc of linguistic history: a PIE root for 'protruding', a shared Italo-Celtic mountain, a Vulgar Latin climbing verb, a Norman French loan into Middle English, centuries of concrete 'rising' senses, and finally one poet's stroke that turned a mountain into a metaphor for inspiration itself.
