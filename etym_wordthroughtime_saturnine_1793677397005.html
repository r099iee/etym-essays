- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed into English within the framework of medieval astrology and Galenic medicine (notably appearing in Geoffrey Chaucer's *The Knight's Tale*); applied strictly to individuals born under the astrological ascendancy or malefic transit of Saturn, believed to suffer from an excess of black bile, coldness, and heavy sorrow.
- 15th to 16th Century (Renaissance Alchemy & Hermeticism) - Broadened under Paracelsian and alchemical philosophy to encompass lead (Latin *plumbum*); used by physicians and occult philosophers to describe the heavy, dull, poisonous properties of lead and its melancholic correspondences in nature.
- 17th Century (Jacobean Literature & Anatomy of Melancholy) - Secularized beyond literal horoscopes into psychological character portraiture (Robert Burton's *The Anatomy of Melancholy*, Shakespearean drama); came to designate an individual of dark, grave, taciturn demeanor and sardonic worldview, contrasting with the witty *mercurial* type.
- 18th to 19th Century (Gothic Romanticism & Victorian Pathology) - Embraced by Gothic novelists (the Brontës, Ann Radcliffe) and Victorian authors (Charles Dickens) to depict brooding, shadowed, morally ambiguous figures; concurrently formalized in medical toxicology as a clinical term for chronic lead poisoning (*saturnine colic*, *saturnine gout*).
- 20th Century (Modern Literary & Psychological Realism) - Astrological and humoral literalism completely faded into metaphor; solidified in modern English as an elevated, highly literary epithet for a dark, dryly cynical countenance, brooding intellectual distance, and slow, unsmiling gravity.
- Contemporary Usage - Retains an aristocratic, evocative literary resonance in sophisticated prose for solemn, piercing, or sullen characterization, while persisting in historical toxicology as a venerable synonym for plumbic illness.
