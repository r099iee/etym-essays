Jogging is the present participle and verbal noun of the verb to jog, meaning the action of moving along at a steady, gentle, bouncing pace — a rhythmic, rocking motion that is neither as fast as a run nor as stately as a walk. As a verbal noun it names both the gait of an animal being ridden and the motion of a traveller setting out in a relaxed, unforced way: a jogging movement is a series of small shakes, nudges, and bounces repeated evenly, like a continuous gentle jolt. The word belongs first to the world of horses and roads — a rider jogs, a horse jog-trots, a company "jogs along" on laden ponies — and only in the mid-twentieth century did it acquire its second, now-dominant life as the name for running slowly and regularly for exercise. The full range of senses:
  • Of a horse or rider — the easy, bouncing trot (16th–19th c.). To jog is to move at a slow, steady, jolting trot; jogging names this gait — neither the brisk two-beat trot nor the walk, but the easy rocking pace of a rider in no hurry.
  • Of a traveller — to proceed at an easy, unforced pace. "To jog along", "to jog off", "to jog on": to set out or continue a journey in a relaxed, bouncing rhythm, without haste and without ceremony.
  • To nudge, shake, or jolt (16th c. onward). The root physical sense from which the gait derives: to give something a small shake or push — the sense alive in "to jog one's memory", to rouse a recollection with a gentle mental nudge.
  • The modern exercise sense (1948 onward). Running slowly and steadily for physical fitness, popularised internationally from the late 1960s; the human runner took over the trot of the horse, and the morning jog became a mass pursuit.
  • Jogtrot. The steady, monotonous trot, and by metaphor any humdrum, unchanging routine of life — "the jogtrot of daily existence".
  • Noun — "a jog". A small push or nudge; a spell of running for exercise ("a morning jog"); "to give someone's memory a jog".
  • Joggle (frequentative). To shake with small, repeated motions — the frequentative twin of jog, of the same expressive family.
In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the nuance is cheerfully domestic and unhurried: "That's how they all came to start, jogging off from the inn one fine morning just before May, on laden ponies; and Bilbo was wearing a dark-green hood (a little weather-stained) and a dark-green cloak borrowed from Dwalin." The phrasal verb "jog off" means to set out or depart at an easy trot, and the whole sentence is designed to sound easy: a fine morning, just before May, laden ponies, a weather-stained hood. There is no drum, no trumpet, no solemn leave-taking; the company simply jog off, bouncing along at a comfortable rhythm, and the word's small repeated motion mimics the ponies' jog-trot and the lighthearted spirits of travellers who do not yet know what the road holds. The comedy of the departure — Bilbo in a borrowed hood, the baggage still a jumble — is carried entirely by that undramatic verb. Jogging should be distinguished from its neighbours in the equestrian and pedestrian vocabulary. A trot is the horse's standard two-beat diagonal gait, brisk and workmanlike; a canter is a smooth three-beat gait faster than a trot; a gallop is the horse's full four-beat speed; an amble is a smooth lateral gait; a lope is a relaxed, bounding canter; and a plod is a slow, heavy, weary tread. Running, as a human activity, is faster and more strenuous than the jog, which is regular, light, and sustainable; ambling is smooth and relaxed with no urgency; and plodding is slow and weary where jogging is light and even cheerful. Jogging alone names the steady, gentle, bouncing rhythm — the small repeated shake that is the word's oldest meaning, kept forever in "to jog one's memory".
