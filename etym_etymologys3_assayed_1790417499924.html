The verb assayed represents the historical past tense and past participle of assay, a word whose rich etymological trajectory spans the fundamental physics of weighing, the regulation of medieval coinage, the development of Renaissance literature, and the frontiers of modern biochemical analysis. Etymologically, assay and essay are doublets—descendants of the very same Latin root that diverged into distinct orthographic forms and specialized semantic domains over centuries of linguistic evolution.

1. PIE Roots and Classical Latin Morphological Genesis:

  • The Root *h₂eǵ-: The deep ancestral source is the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eǵ-, meaning "to drive, lead, draw out, conduct, or move." This ubiquitous root produced Greek agein ("to lead," as in demagogue, pedagogy) and Latin agere ("to drive, do, act," giving English agent, action, agenda, and act).
  • Compound Formation in Latin: By prefixing the preposition ex- ("out, thoroughly") to agere, Latin formed the compound verb exigere, literally meaning "to drive out, force out," which rapidly evolved the secondary senses of "to demand, require, measure against a standard, examine, or weigh accurately" (the source of English exact and exigent).
  • From Action to Instrument: In Late Latin and Imperial Roman commerce, the verbal stem generated the neuter noun exagium (attested in the 4th century CE in the writings of Ammianus Marcellinus), meaning "a weighing" or "a standard weight used to verify the integrity of coins." This formed the denominative verb exagiare, "to test by weight, to balance on a scale."

2. Gallo-Romance Shifts and Anglo-Norman Transmission:

  • Vowel Changes and Lenition: In the transition from Vulgar Latin to Old French, the initial unstressed syllable underwent prosthetic modification and vowel shifts, producing the Old French verb forms essaiier, essaier, and variant assaier (influenced by association with Latin ad- or phonetic opening before sibilants), alongside the noun essai ("trial, test, attempt").
  • The Norman Conquest and Middle English Adoption: Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman administrators brought both variants across the English Channel. By the early 14th century (c. 1300–1330), Middle English records attest the verb as assaien or asaien. In medieval England, where the royal mint at the Tower of London and goldsmiths' guilds strictly regulated currency integrity, the "Trial of the Pyx" was an institutionalized public assay to ensure gold and silver coins met legal purity standards.
  • Chivalric and Literary Senses: In Middle English romance and chivalric literature (such as Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales and Sir Gawain and the Green Knight), assaien was used equally for testing the purity of a sword's steel, testing a knight's virtue or courage, and "attempting" a bold deed (e.g., "he assayed to smite").

3. The Lexical Split: Assay vs. Essay:

  • Renaissance Divergence: During the late 16th century, the French philosopher Michel de Montaigne published his groundbreaking reflective writings under the title Essais (1580), using the word in its sense of "attempts, trials, exploratory endeavors of the mind."
  • Orthographic Specialization: Francis Bacon popularized the literary form in English with his Essays (1597). Over the 17th and 18th centuries, English lexicographers and writers gradually distinguished the two spellings:
    • Essay became fixed for the literary composition and general exploratory attempts.
    • Assay was retained for the precise metallurgical testing of ores, chemical analysis, verification of hallmark standards on precious metals, and elevated metaphorical examinations of human character.

4. Modern Scientific and Metaphorical Trajectory:

  • Laboratory and Biotechnology Expansion: In the 19th and 20th centuries, as analytical chemistry and biology industrialized, assay became the standard scientific term for experimental determination of substance concentrations (leading to compounds like bioassay, immunoassay, plaque assay, and radioreceptor assay).
  • Stylistic Register: In non-technical modern prose, the past participle assayed functions as a deliberate, stylish rhetorical device. When a writer notes that a glance, speech, or letter assayed a certain quality, the metallurgical heritage of the word infuses the observation with an aura of forensic, quantitative precision—dissecting human behavior down to its constituent elements.
