- 1620s: berth as convenient sea room for ships — navigational safety term; to give wide berth means keep well clear
- 1660s-1706: berth as allotted anchoring ground (verb to berth a ship) and as place on board to stow chests and sleep — shifts from empty water around a ship to assigned slot and then to bunk itself
- Late 1700s: extends ashore — any sleeping place, bed, billet, railway sleeper berth; still working-class and military
- 1800s to present: berthing as gerund for the act, cost and logistics — marina berthing fees, alongside berthing, Mediterranean mooring; in yachting a prestige cost-item next to outfitting and maintenance; in transport a booked bunk (train, ferry, spaceship in science fiction)
