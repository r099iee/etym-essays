IMPELLED — the past tense and past participle of the verb "to impel": to drive, force or urge (someone) to do something. 1. The sense in this passage. In David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone (Hong lou meng), Aroma's "simple devotion frequently impelled her to remonstrate" with Bao-yu. The verb names an inward pressure that drives her to speak even though she finds his character "strange and incomprehensible". It is not casual persuasion and not cold external command: it is the sense that simple devotion itself pushes her into speech. The tone is psychological and almost mechanical — force applied to the will — yet the force is affection and duty, not violence. Because the remonstrance "invariably" fails, the word also carries a note of pathos: she is driven to act by loyalty, then worried and hurt when the drive meets indifference. The past form impelled locks the pressure into repeated narrative habit rather than a single whim — this is what she does again and again. 2. Core meaning. To impel is to drive or urge someone forward into action by some force or pressure, above all an inner motive such as conscience, duty, curiosity, fear or love; the verb can also describe physical propulsion, but in modern English its primary home is the moral and psychological register. 3. Full polysemy inventory.
  • Psychological and moral driving: to urge or force a person to act through an inner motive — "impelled by conscience / duty / curiosity / love / fear".
  • Physical propulsion: to drive a body forward by force; in modern use this sense is largely restricted to technical and mechanical contexts.
  • Mechanical / engineering jargon: the "impeller" is the rotating blade or rotor that drives fluid in a pump, fan or turbocharger — the noun formed on the same Latin root, keeping the physical sense alive.
  • Quasi-passive construction: "feel impelled to do something" — a felt necessity without any external commander; the resistance overcome is hesitation or reluctance.
  • Figurative extension: events, circumstances or emotions impel a course of action, as in "economic necessity impelled the reform".
  • Rhetorical / literary use: in elevated prose, impel names the force of fate, destiny or inspiration pressing a character toward a deed — the mechanical image transferred to the will.
4. Register and collocations. Collocations favour passive or quasi-passive frames: "impelled by duty / conscience / curiosity / fear / love"; "feel impelled to" + infinitive; less often the active "X impelled Y to do Z". The register is literary, formal and essayistic rather than conversational. In this novelistic use, the collocation with "simple devotion" and "remonstrate" places the word firmly in the lexicon of moral psychology: affection as an engine, speech as the motion produced when that engine fires against worry and incomprehension. 5. The semantic bridge. Latin impellere combines in- "into, against, upon" with pellere "to drive": literally to strike against something and so set it in motion. The physical shove was metaphorically transferred to the will, so that conscience or devotion "pushes" a person into action — a mechanical image pressed into service for psychological necessity. The verb therefore sits at the junction of internal motive and felt necessity, stronger than "urged" and more inward than "compelled".
