RASPBERRY (noun) — The soft, red (or rarely yellow, purple, or black) aggregate fruit of plants of the genus Rubus in the rose family, borne on a prickly shrub and made up of many tiny fleshy drupelets clustered around a central core; the word also denotes the plant itself and, by a famous figurative leap, a spluttering, derisive noise made with the tongue and lips ('to blow a raspberry'). Core sense and grammatical range. As a noun, raspberry names the fruit, the plant (the bramble), its colour, and — in the idiomatic phrase 'to blow a raspberry' — a rude gesture-sound. The noun is also used attributively ('raspberry jam,' 'raspberry vinegar'). Botanically, the raspberry is an aggregate fruit: each little knob is a separate drupelet, each with its own tiny seed, clustered on a conical core from which it separates when picked — which is why a picked raspberry is hollow, unlike the blackberry, which keeps its core. Polysemy and secondary senses. The word carries a striking range of senses:
  • Botanical and culinary sense: the fruit itself and the plant; raspberry jam, tarts, syrups, vinegars, and liqueurs (e.g., the classic combination of raspberry jam with scones and clotted cream).
  • Colour sense: the clear red colour of the fruit, used of cloth, cosmetics, and foodstuffs ('raspberry pink').
  • Derisive noise — 'to blow a raspberry': a rude spluttering sound made by protruding the tongue and blowing air through vibrating lips, used as a gesture of derision or mockery; the British term for what Americans call a 'Bronx cheer.' The expression originated in Cockney rhyming slang: 'raspberry tart' rhyming for 'fart,' shortened to 'raspberry.'
  • Computing and technology sense: the Raspberry Pi, an inexpensive credit-card-sized single-board computer (from 2012), named by metaphor for a small, friendly, berry-like device rather than for the derisive noise.
  • Colloquial and family register: 'blowing a raspberry' on a baby's tummy is a harmless game, while the same gesture aimed at an adult is an insult — showing how the same word spans playfulness and derision.
  • Idiomatic 'Raspberry Award' / 'Razzie': the Golden Raspberry Awards ('Razzies') parody the Oscars, naming the year's worst films; the name plays on the derisive 'raspberry' gesture, and its informal clipping 'razzie' (also a verb) extends the joke.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The fruit-name is a compound of rasp- + berry. The second half, berry, is native Germanic (Old English berie). The first half is famously murky: medieval documents attest Latinate raspecia/raspis, first as a sweet reddish wine and then as the reddish berry associated with it; by the sixteenth century English added the native berry to form raspis-berry, later fused as raspberry. Popular speech has long connected the name to the verb rasp ('to scrape') because of the rough, pebbled surface of the fruit — a folk-motivated association with the drupelet texture. The derisive sense is a much later, purely English invention: Cockney rhyming slang of the 1890s used 'raspberry tart' as slang for 'fart,' then clipped it to 'raspberry' for the rude lip-noise itself. Connotation and register. In the kitchen raspberry is a warm, homely, summery word — jam, cream, hedgerow picking, cottage comfort. In the idiom 'blow a raspberry' it is cheeky, childish, and derisive. In technology it is unexpectedly modern and friendly (the Raspberry Pi). The word thus spans the nursery, the pantry, the insult, and the electronics bench — an unusually versatile berry. Related words. The family includes the clipping razz (US slang for 'to deride' or the derisive sound), Razzie, the colour adjective raspberry, and the compounds raspberry jam, raspberry vine, and raspberry bush; related aggregate fruits are the blackberry, loganberry, dewberry, and cloudberry.
