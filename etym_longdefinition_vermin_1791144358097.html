Vermin denotes small noxious animals and insects that infest houses, stores, crops or bodies, damage food and property, spread disease or parasitize humans and livestock — such as rats, mice, cockroaches, lice, fleas, bedbugs, weevils and similar pests.

In the source context — granaries secure from rain and damp, vermin and thieves — vermin means exactly those store-pests that eat or foul hoarded grain and meat: rats, mice, grain weevils, moths and worms. The farmer's progress is measured by exclusion: weatherproofing against damp, proofing against gnawing and creeping eaters, and guarding against human stealers. Vermin here is collective and uncountable, a mass threat rather than one animal.

More broadly, vermin covers any animal deemed a pest in a given human enterprise. To a farmer, rabbits, crows, foxes and slugs may be vermin; to a gamekeeper, stoats, hawks and foxes; to a householder, rats, mice and cockroaches; to public health, lice, fleas, ticks and mosquitoes as vectors. The category is anthropocentric and shifting: the same fox is vermin to a poultry keeper and protected wildlife to a conservationist. Legal definitions therefore vary by statute and locality.

  • Store and household pests (core): Rats, mice, cockroaches, ants, grain weevils, moths, silverfish that consume or contaminate food, clothing and buildings.
  • Parasites and vectors: Lice, fleas, bedbugs, ticks, mosquitoes that live on or bite humans and animals, causing itching and transmitting typhus, plague, Lyme disease and malaria. Historical bridge: worms in the belly were the prototype of internal vermin.
  • Agricultural and game pests: Rabbits, pigeons, crows, slugs, aphids, foxes when they damage crops, game or livestock. This extends the worm-prototype by function: whatever eats our share is vermin.
  • Figurative insult for persons: Contemptuous term for people considered vile, parasitic, or socially harmful; e.g., to call criminals, informers or political enemies vermin. Bridge is dehumanization through disgust, implying they breed, swarm and should be exterminated. Now recognized as hateful dehumanizing language.
  • Hunting and pest-control jargon: Vermin control, vermin shooter as technical terms for lawful culling; vermin-proof for bins and buildings. Here the tone is neutral-technical rather than insulting.
  • Archaic medical sense: In older texts, worms and worm-like parasites causing colic or disease, and by extension disease itself. Survives in veterinary verminous meaning infested with worms.

Grammatically vermin is usually collective: one says vermin is or vermin are without adding -s, though vermins occurs historically. Connotations are strongly negative — filth, swarming, stealth, contamination — which is why the figurative use wounds. Understanding the word requires seeing that it classifies not by zoology but by human hostility: vermin are animals with whom we are at war.
