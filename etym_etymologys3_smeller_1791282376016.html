Smeller is a transparent Modern English formation from the verb and noun smell plus the Germanic agent suffix -er, but its base carries one of the more stubborn mysteries in English etymology. Middle English smellen appears around 1150-1200 meaning to emit an odour and to perceive odour, with noun smell for odour following shortly after. Before that, Old English has stenc for odour and a different set of verbs, with no clear ancestor for smell. The OED, Etymonline and Beekes alike label the pre-Middle-English history obscure. Proposed links to Middle Dutch smelen, Low German smelen to smoulder or fume, or an unattested Old English *smyllan or *smellan, remain conjectural because sound correspondences and textual attestations are thin.

Phonetically, what is secure starts in Middle English. The cluster sm- is stable, the vowel e shortens and centralises to modern /ɛ/, and final -ll simplifies. Modern smell is /smɛl/, smeller adds the agent suffix -er pronounced in RP as schwa /ˈsmɛlə/ with non-rhotic loss of /r/. The suffix itself has a clear pedigree: Old English -ere, as in fisher, baker, from Proto-Germanic *-ari, borrowed very early from Latin -arius. It attaches freely to verbs to mean one who does, and to nouns to mean thing for doing. Smeller therefore patterns exactly like singer, reader and nose-blower.

Semantic evolution branches because smell itself and -er each contribute:

  • Odour perception in Middle English: to smell sweet or foul, to smell out treason. The base establishes both active sniffing and passive stinking, a duality English keeps.
  • Agent: one who smells 16th-17th c.: a smeller is one who sniffs or tests by odour, as in professional tea or tobacco smellers. Neutral occupational sense.
  • Organ: nose late 17th-19th c.: slang and colloquial use makes smeller mean the nose itself, the thing with which one smells. Grose's slang dictionary and pugilistic writing record smeller for nose, especially as a target for blows.
  • Blow or fall 19th-20th c.: to come a smeller, to take a smeller, means to take a heavy fall or a punch on the nose, then any crash. The bridge is metonymic: a blow on the smeller fells a man, so the fall itself becomes a smeller. Wodehouse's comes a smeller in the ditch belongs precisely here, meaning comes a cropper, takes a tumble.
  • Flower and joke extensions: a smeller for a nosegay or bouquet, and in schoolboy slang a stinker as a difficult problem. Minor playful transfers.

Socio-cultural context explains why nose and fall senses flourished. Bare-knuckle boxing, music-hall slang and public-school slang all favoured body-part metonyms: conk, snout, smeller. Comic writers from Dickens to Wodehouse loved these to deflate high-flown metaphors. A lofty motoring metaphor of brain as car ends, bathetically, with a smeller in the ditch, a thumping fall. The humour depends on readers knowing both the elevated and low senses.

Literary attestations confirm the arc. Occupational smellers appear in trade manuals. Slang smeller for nose appears in 1780s-1840s cant dictionaries. Sporting papers describe pugilists receiving smellers. Wodehouse and P. G. Wodehouse contemporaries use to come a smeller or a purler for comic pratfalls. Modern use keeps nose as dated slang and fall as archaic comic idiom, while occupational smeller survives in perfumery. The word thus shows how a morphologically regular form can acquire vivid idiomatic life far beyond its literal agent meaning.
