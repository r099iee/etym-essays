Primary sense — Tolkienian geography. Moria is the Sindarin (Elvish) name, meaning “the Black Chasm”, for Khazad-dûm, the greatest of the Dwarven kingdoms of Middle-earth in J.R.R. Tolkien's legendarium, delved for millennia beneath the Misty Mountains. It is the name by which the ruined, orc-haunted remnant of that realm is remembered in The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings — a byword for lost grandeur, buried treasure and the hubris of digging too deep.

The word in its context. The sentence of this card's context is spoken by Roäc, son of Carc, the wise old raven of the Lonely Mountain, in chapter 17 of The Hobbit (“The Clouds Burst”), as he brings the dwarves news of the goblin army marching on Erebor. “Whose father you slew in Moria” addresses Dáin Ironfoot: it refers to the Battle of Azanulbizar (Third Age 2799), the decisive engagement of the War of the Dwarves and Orcs, fought in the Dimrill Dale before Moria's East Gate, where Dáin struck down the orc-chieftain Azog — whose son Bolg now leads the goblins of the North. The name therefore enters the published story already loaded with blood-feud and vengeance.

In-universe semantics and naming layers. Tolkien analyses the name within his invented Elvish: mor “dark, black” + iâ “void, abyss, gulf”, hence “the Black Chasm”. It is a name given by outsiders, and it encodes their fear:

  • Khazad-dûm (“Dwarf-mansion”) is the Dwarves' own Khuzdul name for the living city; the Dwarves never call it Moria.
  • Hadhodrond (“Dwarf-cave”) is the older Sindarin name used by the Elves of Eregion while the realm still stood.
  • Moria is the post-disaster Elvish name — the “Black Chasm” — applied after the city's fall, and it is the word that survives in the mouths of men, hobbits and ravens.

History of the realm. Founded by Durin the Deathless, eldest of the Seven Fathers of the Dwarves, who awoke beside the Mirrormere (Kheled-zâram), Khazad-dûm was for ages the centre of Dwarven craft and the chief source of mithril, the “true-silver” more precious than gold. Its mansions ran for miles beneath the mountains; its Great Gates opened onto the Dimrill Dale, and the Doors of Durin on the western side bore the famous riddle “Speak, friend, and enter”. In T.A. 1980 the Dwarves delved too deep in their hunger for mithril and awakened a Balrog of Morgoth, “Durin's Bane”, which slew Durin VI and his son Náin I and drove the survivors into exile; from then until the War of the Ring, Moria was the lair of Orcs and of nameless things.

Secondary sense — the Fellowship's ordeal. In The Lord of the Rings (1954–55), Moria is the setting of the Fellowship's darkest passage: the Watcher in the Water at the gate, the Chamber of Mazarbul, and the Bridge of Khazad-dûm, where Gandalf confronts the Balrog, breaks the bridge, and falls into the abyss with the monster — dying and returning as Gandalf the White. In this register Moria means trial, sacrifice and resurrection.

Tertiary sense — biblical homograph. The same letters spell the Latinised form of the Hebrew Moriyyāh, “Moriah”, the mountain in the land of Moriah where Abraham was commanded to offer Isaac (Genesis 22:2) and the traditional site of Solomon's Temple (2 Chronicles 3:1). The Hebrew name's etymology is uncertain — tradition connects it with rā'āh “to see” (hence “the LORD will see/provide”, Genesis 22:14). Asked about the resemblance, Tolkien replied that his name is Sindarin “Black Chasm” and treated the likeness as fortuitous, though he admitted the biblical name may have influenced him unconsciously — a celebrated case of apparent cognation between an invented word and an ancient one.

Quaternary sense — Greek homograph. Greek μωρία (mōría) means “folly, madness”, from μωρός (mōrós) “dull, foolish” — the word of Erasmus's Moriae Encomium (The Praise of Folly, 1511), whose title puns on the name of his friend Thomas More. Entirely unrelated to Tolkien, it nonetheless shares the spelling, so “Moria” can mean folly in a classical library and a black chasm in a fantasy novel.

Modern cultural afterlife. Since the 1970s “Moria” has detached from Middle-earth: it names a landmark 1975 roguelike computer game, appears as a dungeon archetype across gaming and fantasy, and serves in journalism as shorthand for vast, dangerous underground complexes or for the disaster of digging too deep. The word thus carries two lives — an Elvish lament for fallen splendour within the fiction, and a global meme for subterranean peril outside it.
