Squirmed is one of the youngest and most mysterious words in this batch: it has no known family tree at all. First attested in 1640s English, the verb squirm, 'to twist and wriggle the body,' appears suddenly — without Germanic cognates, without a Latin or Greek source, and without any reconstructible Proto-Indo-European ancestor. The honest scholarly verdict, recorded in the OED and repeated by Etymonline, is that its origin is obscure; the likeliest explanation is that it is echoic or sound-symbolic. The s- followed by the kw- cluster and the curling -erm syllable mime the twist-and-turn motion it names, much as wriggle and worm do by different phonetic means; one feels the word coil in the mouth as one says it, the tongue and lips enacting the very motion the word denotes.

Dialectal forms such as squirn and squirmy suggest that the word may have had a long underground life in spoken English before it entered the written record, surfacing first in colloquial speech and only later in print. The written history that we do have is short but eventful, and it moves steadily from the body outward into the moral life.

  • 17th–18th centuries: the body: squirm appears chiefly in physical description — of eels, snakes, and worms, and of people wriggling out of tight places, out of ropes, or out from under restraint. Its natural habitat is confinement; the motion is ungainly, animal, and undignified, the wriggle of a creature that cannot simply walk away.
  • 19th century: the moral transfer: to squirm gains its famous transferred sense — to writhe with embarrassment, discomfort, or guilt, as in 'the question made him squirm.' The physical contortion of a caught body becomes the inward unease of a caught conscience; a person cornered by an awkward truth twists inwardly just as a body twists to escape a grasp, and the word slips from the domain of eels to the domain of awkward interviews and embarrassing compliments.
  • The family grows: squirmy (a restless child; a situation that breeds unease) and the noun squirm (the motion, or a pang of embarrassment) extend the word beyond the verb; a squirmer is colloquially a restless child.
  • Modern register: the word is informal and vividly physical; formal writing prefers writhe for agony and wriggle for motion, leaving squirm for the strained, twisting, slightly comic version of either — the word of choice when dignity is at a low ebb.

In the context at hand — 'He squirmed through the opening' — the verb is used in its oldest, physical sense: a body too large for its passage twists and wriggles its way through a narrow gap, shoulders and limbs drawn in, spine flexing, the whole motion serpentine and undignified. The word's sound — the skw- onset and the curling -erm — enacts the motion it names, and its history, from worm to body to conscience, means that even a purely physical use carries a faint comic-pathetic overtone of the creature reduced to undulation. Squirm is the word for getting through when one cannot walk, crawl, or climb: a worm's progress, completed and past, the memory of the tight squeeze and the slow twisting journey through the dark left to linger in the reader's body as vividly as in the character's.
