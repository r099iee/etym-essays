RESTRUNG (/ˌriːˈstrʌŋ/) is the past-tense and past-participle form of the verb restring — to fit with a string or strings once again, or to thread a new cord through something. The word is built from the prefix re- (“again, back, anew”) and strung, the irregular past participle of string. It describes a completed action whose durable result is a freshly strung object: the old strings have been taken off and new ones put in their place.

Core sense — musical instruments: The most familiar use concerns chordophones — harps, violins, cellos, guitars, lutes, mandolins, banjos, ukuleles. A luthier or player “restrings” an instrument when the existing strings have broken, worn thin, gone dead, or been removed; the participle restrung reports that the work is done: “After the concert the harp was restrung with silver wire.” The card’s source context (“His harp shall be restrung… To songs of yore re-sung”) plays directly on this sense: the restored instrument is the token that the kingdom’s old songs can be sounded again.

  • Sense 1 — fresh strings on an instrument: the primary modern sense; involves choosing new gut, metal, or nylon strings, winding them onto tuning pegs, and tensioning them to pitch.
  • Sense 2 — re-corded jewellery: necklaces, bracelets, rosaries, and pearl strands are “restrung” when their cord or wire thread wears out; a jeweller restrings beads on new silk or wire.
  • Sense 3 — new tension cords for sport and war: tennis, squash, and badminton racquets are restrung with fresh gut or synthetic stringing; archery bows are restrung when the bowstring is replaced.
  • Sense 4 — figurative renewal and restored voice: because a restrung instrument can sing again, the participle metaphorically denotes emotional or spiritual restoration — a life “restrung” after grief, a community “restrung” after disruption. This is the oldest poetic echo of the word, reaching back to the psalmist’s harp hung on a willow (Psalm 137).
  • Sense 5 — rare technical back-formation: in computing and data work, “restring” occasionally occurs as a verb meaning to join or sequence elements into a string again; this is a fringe jargon use, not part of the general vocabulary.

Grammar and morphology: The verb string preserves two past-participial forms in modern English: irregular strung (from the old strong-verb pattern string–strang–strung) and regular stringed, which survives chiefly in the adjective stringed instruments. Restrung follows the irregular pattern; the regular restringed is rare and generally avoided. The prefix may also be written with a hyphen (re-strung) when the sense of repetition is being emphasised.

Historical and literary bridge: The chain runs from the noun string (“a slender cord”) through the verb to string (“to fit with a cord”) to the prefixed restring (“to fit anew”) and finally to the participle restrung. The poetic resonance is ancient and biblical: in Psalm 137 the exiled Levites “hanged our harps upon the willows” and refused to sing, so the restrung harp — the instrument re-strung so that music can resume — is a symbol of restoration after silence. The word thus carries a double life in English: a plain, practical term for maintenance and repair, and a resonant metaphor for the recovery of voice, joy, and harmony.
