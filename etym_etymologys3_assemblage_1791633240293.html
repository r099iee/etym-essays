The evolution of assemblage spans over five millennia, tracing a path from an ancient Proto-Indo-European root for unity and togetherness, through Vulgar Latin phonetic sound shifts and Old French suffixation, to the avant-garde manifestos of twentieth-century art and contemporary post-structuralist philosophy.

Indo-European Origins and Latin Developments:

The ultimate ancestor of the word is the prolific Proto-Indo-European root *sem-, signifying 'one, as one, together with'. This root generated an extraordinary constellation of cognates across Indo-European languages: Sanskrit sam ('together') and sama ('same, equal'), Ancient Greek hama ('at the same time') and homos ('same'), Old English same ('same'), and Latin semel ('once'), simplex ('simple'), and simul ('together, at the same time').

In Classical Latin, the adverbial form simul combined with the directional prefix ad- ('to, toward') to form the verb adsimulare (or assimilare), meaning 'to make similar, liken, compare'. In Late and Vulgar Latin, as spoken across the crumbling Western Roman Empire, the verb drifted from the abstract notion of 'making like' toward the concrete, physical action of 'bringing together into one place, gathering together'.

Gallo-Romance Phonetics and the Genesis of *assembler*:

During the transition from Vulgar Latin to Early Old French (sixth to tenth centuries CE), the unstressed medial vowel of *assimulare was lost through syncope, creating an unstable consonant cluster */sm-l/. To resolve this articulatory difficulty, Gallo-Romance phonology developed an epenthetic /b/—an intrusive bilabial plosive that naturally bridged the transition from the bilabial nasal /m/ to the lateral liquid /l/. This regular phonetic sound law produced the Old French verb assembler ('to bring together, collect, unite, assemble'), cognate with modern French ressembler and English resemble.

In the thirteenth century, the nominal suffix -age (derived from Latin -aticum, designating an action, collective state, or fee) was appended to the verbal stem, generating the abstract noun assemblage. In Old and Middle French, it signified the act of gathering, a marital union, an alliance, or a collective crowd of people.

Middle English Adoption and Mechanical Specialization:

The word crossed the English Channel in the late fifteenth century during the final stages of the Hundred Years' War. Early occurrences in late Middle English prose (found around 1480–1500) carried the French sense of a meeting of persons, a formal convocation, or a troop gathering. By the seventeenth century, as the Scientific Revolution took root, artisans and natural philosophers began applying assemblage to the physical world: clockmakers referred to the assemblage of gears, while carpenters and shipwrights used it to describe the interlocking joints of wooden vessels.

The 20th-Century Artistic Revolution: Dubuffet and MoMA:

The word underwent a radical semantic transformation in the post-World War II art world. Although artists had been pasting papers (collage) and constructing sculptures from scrap metal since Cubism and Dada, there was no distinct, universally recognized term for three-dimensional art built from found matter. In August 1953, the French artist Jean Dubuffet created a series of collages made from butterfly wings, calling them assemblages d'empreintes. Dubuffet deliberately chose the term to differentiate his works from the decorative connotations of traditional collage, declaring that assemblage was a raw, unpretentious confrontation with physical matter.

In 1961, curator William C. Seitz organized the seminal exhibition The Art of Assemblage at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. The exhibition featured 250 works by 130 artists, including Marcel Duchamp, Kurt Schwitters, Man Ray, Joseph Cornell (whose glass-fronted shadow boxes became iconic), Robert Rauschenberg (his 'combines'), and Edward Kienholz. Seitz officially codified assemblage as a distinct medium: three-dimensional works constructed predominantly from non-art, pre-fabricated, or natural materials that had not been created for an aesthetic purpose. This exhibition established the word permanently in international art history.

Philosophical Canonization in Post-Structuralism:

In the final decades of the twentieth century, assemblage underwent yet another historic leap into high philosophy. When Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari wrote Mille Plateaux (1980), they made central use of the French term agencement—meaning arrangement, fitting together, or layout. When translator Brian Massumi rendered the work into English in 1987, he famously translated agencement as assemblage, drawing upon the term's rich artistic heritage of joining heterogeneous fragments. Today, Assemblage Theory represents one of the most vibrant paradigms in sociology, political geography, and anthropology, standing as a testament to the word's enduring power to describe how unity emerges from diversity.
