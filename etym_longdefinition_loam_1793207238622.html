Loam is, in its dominant modern sense, the ideal soil: a friable, fertile mixture of sand, silt, and clay (usually with humus), combining drainage with moisture-holding, warmth with substance — the dark earth that crumbles in the fist yet holds its shape when squeezed. In the source sentence Cobbett reads the Thames-side country through it: the soil is a gravel at bottom with a black loam at top near the Thames, and the blackness matters — it signals rich, humus-fed topsoil over hungry gravel, the good land of the riverside against the spewy poverty further back. For an agricultural writer the word loam is never casual: it is a verdict on the land's capacity to feed people.

Core meaning in detail: Soil scientists define loam by texture triangle — roughly balanced proportions of sand (grit, drainage, warmth), silt (smooth fertility, moisture), and clay (nutrient-holding, body), with up to about a quarter clay and the rest divided between sand and silt; gardeners define it by hand — soil that holds a squeezed ball briefly then breaks apart, that digs easily in March and never bakes to brick in August. True balanced loam is rare; most working loams lean sandy (light, early, hungry) or clayey (heavy, late, generous), and the textbooks name a dozen blends — sandy loam, silt loam, clay loam. Cobbett's black loam is the gardener's rather than the surveyor's term: topsoil darkened by rotted dung, leaf-mould, and centuries of manuring, lying like a pocket of wealth over the Thames gravels.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Fertile mixed soil of sand, silt, and clay (primary modern sense): the gardener's ideal and the soil scientist's texture class described above. The bridge from the Old English sense is refinement: lam once meant any clay or mud, and centuries of husbandry writers narrowed the best of those muds — the workable, fruitful mixture — into loam proper, leaving barren clays behind.
  • Rich dark topsoil generally (Cobbett's and the gardener's loose sense): black loam, garden loam — any dark, humus-rich earth regardless of exact texture. The bridge is colour as quality: darkness signals rotted organic matter, and organic matter signals fertility, so blackness becomes shorthand for goodness.
  • Clay or mud for bricks, pottery, and plaster (older technical sense, 14th–19th c.): brickmakers dig loam, plasterers spread loam, the potter wedges his loam. The bridge preserves the Old English root meaning: before loam was the ideal mixture it was simply workable earth — mud with enough clay to hold shape and enough grit to fire without cracking — and the brickfields of Middlesex and Kent were dug in exactly such earth.
  • Foundry loam; moulding mixture for casting (industrial sense): founders pack loam moulds of sand, clay, dung, and water around patterns for bells and cannon. The bridge is functional: the same plasticity that lets loam take a seed lets it take an impression, and the vocabulary of the brickfield transfers wholesale to the foundry.
  • To cover or dress with loam; to make into loam moulds (verb): to loam a vine border, to loam a pattern. The bridge is the ordinary noun-to-verb slide: the material becomes the act of applying it.
  • Mortal clay; the earth of the body (figurative, literary): poets reach for loam where the Bible reaches for dust and clay — our native loam, this mortal loam. The bridge runs through Adam, formed of the earth's mud: loam as soil becomes loam as flesh, the ground we till and the ground we become.

Register and connotation: Loam is a warm, approving word — the vocabulary of gardeners, allotment holders, estate agents praising kitchen gardens, and poets. No one curses loam; even heavy clay loam is spoken of with respect, as land that pays back work. Against sand (hungry), chalk (thin), peat (sour), and clay (stubborn), loam is the golden mean, and saying a soil is loamy is the highest compliment an English grower can pay dirt. Cobbett uses it as an instrument of judgment: where he finds black loam he expects fat crops and independent labourers; where he finds spewy gravel he expects tax-eaters' tea-garden boxes and gin-drinking poverty — soil as moral evidence.
