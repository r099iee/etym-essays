• Decay: Similar because both name the process of decomposition or decline; differs because decay is physical and natural (teeth, wood, organic matter), while decadence is moral, cultural, and historical — a judgment on a civilisation or style, not a chemical event.
  • Decline: Similar because both describe a falling off from a peak; differs because decline is neutral and general (numbers decline, health declines), while decadence implies the late, luxurious, corrupting stage and a moral verdict.
  • Deterioration: Similar as the gradual worsening shared by both; differs because deterioration is clinical and measurable (materials, conditions deteriorate), without the glamour, art, or historical romance of decadence.
  • Degeneration: Similar because both name a falling away from type or health; differs because degeneration is biological-hereditary (or, in older sociology, racial — now discredited), where decadence is cultural and aesthetic.
  • Corruption: Similar because both can denote moral rottenness in high places; differs because corruption is active wrongdoing — bribery, abuse of office — while decadence is a passive condition of over-ripeness and weakened vigour.
  • Effeteness: Similar because both denote the enervated, over-refined, exhausted quality; differs because effeteness is a narrower stylistic trait (the exhausted aesthete) and rarely the grand historical noun that decadence is.
  • Over-refinement: Similar in naming the excessive subtlety of late art styles; differs because over-refinement names a quality of manner, while decadence names a whole historical or moral process.
  • Fall / Ruin: Similar as the catastrophic end implied by decadence; differs because fall and ruin are sudden or final, while decadence is slow, glittering, and can even be enjoyed (as Pater's 'comely decadence' shows).
  • Languor: Similar as the world-weary indolence of the decadent mood; differs because languor is an emotional state, not a period or process — it is one symptom of decadence, never its meaning.
