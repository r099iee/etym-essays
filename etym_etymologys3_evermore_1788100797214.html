Introduction. Evermore is a compound of two words whose histories are each a small masterpiece of Indo-European comparative study, fused into one of the language's most solemn adverbs of time. The ever of the compound carries the Indo-European word for a lifetime; the more carries an ancient comparative of size; and the two were joined — first as a phrase, then as a single word — to name time without end. Both halves deserve separate histories, and the compound has a history of its own.

1. Ever: from *h₂eyu- to Old English ǣfre

The story of ever begins with a Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eyu-, "a long time, a lifetime, an age". Its reflexes are among the grandest words of the ancient languages: Latin aevum ("age, eternity", whence aeternus, "eternal", and English eternal), Greek aiṓn ("age, lifetime, eternity", whence English eon and aeon), and Sanskrit āyu- ("life", as in the Rigveda's wish for long life). From this root the Germanic branch made an adverb of eternity: Proto-Germanic *aiwi-, "age, eternity; ever", attested in Gothic aiw ("ever, always") and continued in Old English ā and āwa, "ever, always" — the word that survives today in the poetic "for aye" and in "aught" (from Old English āwiht, "ever a whit").

Old English then produced the mysterious ǣfre (later æfre), "ever, at any time, always", attested from the 9th–10th century writings and glosses. Its first element is generally agreed to be the same ā < *aiwi-, "ever"; but the second element, the -fre / -re, has defeated explanation. The Oxford English Dictionary records the formation as of uncertain origin; various conjectures — an old adverbial suffix, a fossilised comparative or case-form, some lost particle — have been offered, and none has won general acceptance. It is a famous small puzzle of Germanic philology, and an important one to state honestly: the modern word ever is certain in its first element and genuinely obscure in its tail. Middle English contracted and levelled the form through euere, evre, euer to the modern ever, with the poetic contraction e'er following in verse.

2. More: the ancient comparative that became a degree-word

The second half of the compound is more, from Old English māra (adjective, "greater" — the comparative of micel, "great, much") and mā (adverb, "more, further"). These continue a Proto-Germanic comparative *maizō / *maiz, "greater, more", whose cognates are widespread: Gothic maiza ("greater"), Old Saxon mēro, Old High German mēro (modern German mehr, "more"), and Old Norse meiri. The Germanic comparative points back to an Indo-European comparative stem usually reconstructed as *mē-is- (or *mo-is-), built on a root *mē-, "big, great, abundant" — the base that appears in the Celtic comparatives Old Irish mó ("greater, bigger") and Welsh mwy ("more"). Whether this *mē- has any ancient connection with the great-roots of Greek mégas and Latin magnus (the *meǵh₂- family) is debated; modern etymologists tend to treat them as separate bases, and the honest statement is that the exact ultimate relations of more are a matter of continuing discussion rather than settled fact. What is certain is the marriage of the two Old English words in medieval phrase and compound.

3. The compound: from phrase to word (Old English to Middle English)

Old English already used the phrase ǣfre mā, "ever more", in homiletic prose for unending time; the two words stood together, meaning loosely "at every further moment". In Middle English the phrase fused into the compound evermore (with the variants evermo, ever mare, euer more), recorded from the 13th century and abundantly from the 14th: Chaucer uses evermore as a plain, strong adverb — lovers remember evermore, saints serve evermore, the world turns evermore — and in the religious and lyric literature of the period the word names the eternity of God and heaven, the medieval English of "world without end". The two senses coexist from the start: the eternal ("for evermore") and the habitual ("always, continually"), and both survive into modern literary use.

4. The Bible, the Prayer Book, and the solemn register (16th–17th centuries)

The seventeenth century fixed the word's register. The Authorized Version of the Bible (1611) uses evermore in its natural voice — "Lord, evermore give us this bread" (John 6:34), the crowd at Capernaum asking for the bread of life at every hour — and the Book of Common Prayer built the word into the English of eternal praise: "from this time forth and for evermore" (the Gloria Patri), and the "world without end" of the doxologies. The word became, in effect, the English adverb of worship: solemn, forward-looking, without irony, used in vows, prayers, and royal and civic formulas. Its negative twin, nevermore ("never again", Middle English never mo, following the same pattern), travelled the same path and would be given its immortal line by Poe's raven in 1845.

5. Romanticism and the modern career (18th–21st centuries)

The Romantics and Victorians made evermore the word of unending longing: poets vow to love evermore, elegists mourn evermore, and popular song and balladry keep the word glowing — "for evermore" in vows and farewells. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the everyday language has ceded the ground to forever, leaving evermore to the registers it has owned for four hundred years: liturgy and hymnody, formal prose, poetry, and self-conscious archaism. Its meaning has not shifted since Middle English — "at all times henceforth, eternally" — but its social position has: from ordinary adverb to solemn poetic word. And it is in that solemn register that Wilde sets the word in the close of The Happy Prince: the little bird that sang and froze "shall sing for evermore" in the garden of Paradise — the compound's whole history, from Indo-European lifetime to English eternity, compressed into the story's last gift of hope.
