brigandage is the practice, trade, or way of life of brigands — armed outlaws who rob travellers, plunder villages, and terrorize the countryside, typically operating in organized, quasi-military bands rather than as solitary criminals. The word denotes not merely the isolated act of robbery but an entire system of predatory lawlessness: a mode of existence in which violence is routinized, mobility is essential, and the band, not the individual, is the operative unit.

In the literary context of this card — drawn from the opening chapters of the Qing-dynasty novel Dream of the Red Chamber (Hong lou meng) — brigandage names the lawlessness that erupts after a series of crop failures caused by flooding and drought. Starving and desperate, peasants rise in mutiny, and the state answers with troops who 'hunt down the mutinous peasants and making arrests.' The word here is a barometer of systemic collapse: famine produces brigandage, and brigandage produces brutal state repression. It is not a small, local crime but a rupture in the social contract, a society turning against itself.

Core senses:

  • Primary sense — armed robbery and plunder: the organized seizure of goods by force, typically on highways, mountain passes, and remote rural roads where travellers are vulnerable and escape is easy.
  • Systemic sense — banditry as a way of life: brigandage implies an ongoing livelihood or social structure, not a one-off crime; brigands maintain hideouts, hierarchies, lookouts, informants, and internal codes, often with local protection.
  • Historical-political sense — irregular warfare and resistance: in Italian, Greek, Balkan, and later South American contexts, brigandage blurred into guerrilla resistance, peasant revolt, and warlordism; governments used the word precisely to criminalize insurgency.
  • Legal-administrative sense: in 19th-century law and colonial administration, 'brigandage' was a specific crime category that justified deploying troops and creating special tribunals — e.g. the anti-brigandage laws of post-unification Italy and the repressive campaigns of the British in India.
  • Figurative and extended sense: the word is used loosely of any predatory, exploitative behaviour that preys on the vulnerable — the 'brigandage of warring warlords,' the 'economic brigandage of corrupt officials' — transferring the image of the armed highwayman to political or economic predation.

Semantic history and bridges:

  • Brigandage descends from brigand 'highwayman,' which originally meant 'irregular foot soldier.' The bridge is the medieval mercenary: when wars ended, hired soldiers did not quietly return to farming; they kept their weapons and martial skills and turned them on civilians, robbing travellers and extorting villages.
  • The suffix -age (from Latin -aticum) forms abstract nouns of action, collectivity, or system, so brigandage = 'the system or activity of brigands,' in parallel with piracy, banditry, and rapine.
  • The word retains a strongly rural, quasi-military flavour; it is not used of urban street crime or pickpocketing but of armed bands that dominate lawless regions, often romanticized in 19th-century travel writing as colourful outlawry in the mountains of southern Italy, the Balkans, or colonial territories.

Usage and register:

  • In modern English the word is mostly historical or literary; journalism prefers banditry, armed robbery, insurgency, or organized crime.
  • It is strongly collocated with rural, highway, endemic, widespread, and suppression.
  • It is a mass (uncountable) noun in ordinary use — 'an outbreak of brigandage' — though a countable plural is possible in scholarship ('the brigandages of the Papal States').
  • Pronunciation follows the normal stress of the suffix: /brɹˈɪɡandɪdʒ/.
