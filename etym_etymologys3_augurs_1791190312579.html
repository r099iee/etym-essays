The word augur, whose English plural is augurs, belongs to the oldest stratum of Latin religious vocabulary. Latin augur, auguris (m.) meant diviner from birds, member of the augural college. From it derive augurium, observation of birds and the omen itself, auguror, augurari, to act as augur and then to predict, and inaugurare, to install after taking auspices, source of English inaugurate, inauguration. The noun is already central in early Roman tradition: Romulus and Remus take competing auguries at the foundation, Numa is said to institute the college, and the Twelve Tables presuppose auspical law.

Etymology is famously debated, and honesty requires presenting the dispute. Ancient Romans derived augur from avis, bird, and gerere or a speaking root, the bird-speaker. Varro, Festus, and Servius repeat versions of birds plus speech or bearing. Modern comparative linguistics finds this plausible but phonologically difficult and offers a rival: connection with augere, to increase, prosper, through a lost noun *augos-, increase granted by gods, making the augur the functionary of divine increase who guarantees growth. De Vaan marks the formation as uncertain, listing both the traditional avi- + -gur and the aug- hypotheses without firm decision. Etymonline summarizes as of unknown origin, possibly from avis plus root of garrire, to talk. The debate matters because it mirrors two Roman self-understandings: augur as bird-reader versus augur as prosperity-guarantor.

  • Proto-Indo-European background (disputed): either *h₂ewis, bird, plus *ǵerh₂-, to cry, proclaim, or *h₂ewg-, to increase, grow; both roots are well attested elsewhere (Latin avis, garrire; augere, auctor), but the compounding and sound changes to augur are irregular and therefore flagged as uncertain.
  • Archaic Latin (c. 8th–3rd c. BCE): augur as priestly title; augurium as rite and sign; foundation legends and regal period institutions retrojected by later writers but reflecting genuine early priesthood.
  • Republican institutionalization (c. 5th–1st c. BCE): lex Ogulnia 300 BCE opens college to plebeians; Sulla expands to fifteen; Cicero holds augurate and writes De Divinatione and De Legibus preserving technical vocabulary of templum, tabernaculum, obnuntiatio.
  • Late Republic to Empire (1st c. BCE–3rd c. CE): political weaponization of obnuntiation, then imperial control; inaugurare generalizes to any solemn installation; Suetonius, Livy, Tacitus fix literary image.
  • Late Latin to Old French to Middle English (4th–14th c.): augur, augurium persist in Christian polemic against pagan divination (Augustine) and in Isidore; Old French augure for omen; Middle English augur, augurie from late 14th century via French and Latin, first for classical priests then for omens.
  • Early Modern English pluralization: regular -s plural augurs replaces Latin augures; Shakespeare, Jonson, and Dryden popularize transferred pundit sense and verb to augur.

Sound development is straightforward learned borrowing: Latin au- diphthong anglicized, -gur with reduced vowel, stress on first syllable, spelling stable. The English plural -s marks full naturalization. Semantically the trajectory is from technical priesthood to general prophecy to secular forecasting metaphor. Auspice, auspicious, inaugural branch off the same stem and carry the religious procedure into modern civic language: every presidential inauguration unknowingly reenacts an augural founding. Shakespeare's line about augurs and understood smiles cemented the ironic modern overtone that experts may not believe their own signs yet profit from them.

Literary attestations chart the path: Ennius and Livy for foundation auguries, Cicero for skeptical insider practice, Juvenal for satire of superstition, Augustine for Christian rejection, Chaucer and Lydgate for medieval transmission, Shakespeare Julius Caesar and Antony and Cleopatra for the sinister Roman color, and modern leader-writers for economic and electoral augurs. The plural in your passage is the pure historical sense, the college as holders of the lituus.
