DERIVATION CHAIN FOR "NAPE":
Stage 1 — ORIGIN UNKNOWN (isolate within Germanic): no PIE root can be established for "nape," and no secure cognates exist in Latin, Greek or Sanskrit; the OED marks the word's origin as obscure. The chain therefore begins with the first attested English form rather than with a reconstructed root.
Stage 2 — Middle English: nape (c. 1300) "back of the neck," first recorded in the fourteenth century with exactly the modern sense.
Stage 3 — Modern English: NAPE "the back of the neck" — unchanged in meaning for over seven hundred years.

DEBATED ORIGIN (explicit notes):
Hypothesis 1 (favoured by the OED): "nape" may be related to the expressive Germanic base behind English "nap" (n.) "the soft, fuzzy surface of cloth" — Middle English noppe, Middle Dutch noppe, Middle Low German noppe (14th c.). The semantic idea: the downy, fuzzy area at the back of the neck was likened to the nap or pile of cloth. This is a plausible but unproven link, and no intermediate form survives to confirm the transfer.
Hypothesis 2 (rejected): older dictionaries derived "nape" from Old French nape / hanap "goblet, cup," on the fancy that the roll of flesh at the back of the neck resembled the rim of a bowl; both the OED and Etymonline treat this as a dead end.
Anti-merging guardrail: English "nape" is NOT descended from the PIE root *mon- "neck, nape, mane," which produced Sanskrit manyā, Latin monīle and English "mane." Those are genuine cognates of one another, but "nape" has no connection to them.
