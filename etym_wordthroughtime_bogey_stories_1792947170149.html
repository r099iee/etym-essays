- Middle English — bugge frights the nursery: a frightening spectre, the raw goblin-word behind bugbear and bogey, already used to scare children.
- Scots 16th c. — bogle and boggart haunt fields and farms: local sprites blamed for lost tools and strayed cattle, half feared and half laughed at.
- 19th c. — nurses coin the compound: bogey-stories and bogey-man enter print as the standard names for nursery scares, cosy Victorian terror at bedtime.
- Early 20th c. — Tolkien childhood: Fatty nurses embody the Edwardian nursery where such tales were daily discipline, goblins and wolves the standard cast.
- Mid 20th c. — the word goes public: golf adopts bogey for the dreaded extra stroke, airmen adopt it for the dreaded unknown blip, fear generalised.
- Today — dismissal and delight: to call a panic a bogey-story is to dismiss it as childish, yet horror writers and fantasy lovingly revive the nursery goblin for grown-up shivers.
