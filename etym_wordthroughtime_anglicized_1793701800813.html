- **Late 16th Century (Verb "Anglicize" emerges)** - Coined to describe the process of making something English in character or form, often in the context of language or customs. Initially a neutral, descriptive term for linguistic or cultural adaptation.
- **17th-19th Centuries (Expansion of British Empire)** - The term gains broader usage, often associated with the imposition or adoption of English language, laws, and customs in colonial territories. It begins to carry connotations of cultural assimilation, sometimes viewed positively as civilizing, other times negatively as cultural suppression.
- **20th Century (Post-colonial era & globalization)** - Continues to be used for linguistic adaptation (e.g., anglicizing foreign names). In cultural contexts, it can still refer to assimilation, but with increased awareness of cultural identity and diversity, the term can sometimes evoke discussions about cultural loss or homogenization, especially when applied to individuals or groups. It remains a descriptive term but is often used in academic or historical contexts to analyze cultural shifts.
- **21st Century (Digital Age)** - Primarily used in linguistics to describe the adaptation of foreign words/names into English spelling and pronunciation. In broader cultural discussions, it describes the adoption of English/Western cultural norms, often in the context of global media and cultural exchange, maintaining its descriptive function but with continued sensitivity to issues of cultural identity and influence.
