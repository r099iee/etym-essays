- [Late Old English, c. 11th c.] — Attested once as dott, glossing Latin pustula to describe the tiny head of a boil, speck, or skin eruption — [perception: obscure medical and physical blemish]
- [12th–15th c. Middle English] — Vanished from surviving literary manuscripts; preserved strictly in oral regional dialects of eastern and northern England — [perception: dormant vernacular folk word for small specks]
- [Late 16th c. Elizabethan English] — Re-emerged in print to denote a tiny round mark or point made on parchment by a pen — [perception: practical scribal and typographic mark of punctuation]
- [18th c. Enlightenment & Picturesque Era] — Verbalized and adopted into landscape aesthetics; travelers viewing vistas from hilltops described valleys dotted with cottages, trees, and sheep like ink-dots on a surveyor's map — [perception: celebrated visual aesthetic of picturesque harmony between nature and human settlement]
- [19th c. Victorian & Industrial Eras] — Extended into music notation (dotted notes), textile manufacturing (dotted swiss, polka dots), and legal contracts (the dotted line) — [perception: familiar cultural, musical, and commercial descriptor]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Ubiquitous in telecommunications (dotted decimal notation in computing), cartography, and high-register travel and epic literature — [perception: universal term for harmonious spatial interspersion]
