- 11th to 13th Century - Forms in Old French from Vulgar Latin *traversare; functions as a physical verb of spatial movement in medieval chansons de geste, describing knights crossing rivers, traversing mountain passes, and crossing swords in combat.
- 14th to 16th Century - Inflected forms such as the imperative *traversez* appear in travel narratives, chivalric romances, and military treatises; commands troops to ford waterways, cross bridges, or advance athwart enemy formations.
- 17th to 18th Century - Solidified in classical French drama and urban planning; used in stage directions commanding actors to cross the boards (*traversez le théâtre*), and in civic descriptions of newly constructed royal bridges across the Seine.
- 19th Century - Emerges prominently with the Haussmannization of Paris; the creation of wide, multi-lane grand boulevards creates the modern urban pedestrian experience, making crossing wide thoroughfares a newly complex, regulated civic act.
- 20th Century - Mechanized and electrified in Parisian municipal street signaling; the specific phrase *"traversez en deux temps"* becomes an iconic fixture of modern Paris street furniture, featured in red LED lights on boulevard median safety islands.
- 21st Century - Celebrated in comparative literature, Proustian criticism, and urban flânerie; recognized by scholars as a rich philosophical allegory for crossing the fragmented, discontinuous dimensions of memory, mortality, and modern life.
