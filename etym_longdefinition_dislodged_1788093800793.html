Dislodged is the past tense and past participle of to dislodge: to force, knock, drive or shift something out of the position in which it was firmly lodged, settled, stuck or established. In the source passage — “they it was who dislodged the goblins from the mountain-slopes” — the sense is the classic military one: the eagles drove the goblins out of the positions they had occupied on the slopes, casting them over precipices. The word always implies prior fixity: what is dislodged was lodged first, and it leaves its place by external force, not of its own accord.

1. Physical senses (the everyday core). A stone dislodged from a wall, a brick dislodged by the storm, a dislodged cork, debris dislodged by an avalanche, a dislodged splinter, a dislodged tooth, rocks dislodged from a cliff face. The verb takes both objects that were stuck or wedged (a pebble in a boot-sole, a bone caught in a throat) and objects that were simply resting in a place (a nest, a tile). In first aid it is the standard word for freeing an airway obstruction — the Heimlich maneuver is said to dislodge the food or object blocking the windpipe.

2. The military and tactical sense (the historical core). To dislodge an enemy is to drive him from his ground, his post, his entrenchment, his heights or his town — the objective being not necessarily to destroy him but to make his position untenable and force him out of it. The phrase “to dislodge the enemy from the heights/wood/redoubt” is a staple of 17th–19th-century drill manuals, dispatches and campaign histories, and it is this sense that colours the Tolkien passage: the eagles do not merely defeat the goblins, they dislodge them — pry them out of the mountain-slopes they held. The paired military noun is “lodgement” — the foothold or position seized and held (“to effect a lodgement on the beach”) — so the vocabulary of entrenching and un-entrenching runs in exact pairs.

3. Political and institutional senses. By extension, to dislodge a government, an incumbent, a monopoly or an entrenched interest is to remove it from power or dominance — “the scandal finally dislodged the minister”, “the challenger dislodged the champion”. The force may be electoral, economic or rhetorical, but the image is the same: something settled and dug in is levered out of its place.

4. Medical and technical senses.

  • Medicine: a dislodged catheter, line, stent, tube or intra-uterine device (i.e., moved from its correct position — usually unintentionally, a complication); a dislodged blood clot (an embolus — a clot dislodged from its original site travelling to block a vessel); a dislodged tooth or dental crown; in airway emergencies, a dislodged obstruction.
  • Geology and engineering: dislodged boulders, strata and scree; dislodged bridge bearings; dislodged ballast on a railway line.
  • Mind and habit: a dislodged fixed idea, prejudice, belief or memory — something entrenched in the mind shaken out of it by evidence or experience. This psychological use is frequent in intellectual and therapeutic prose.

5. The semantic bridge via “lodge”. The whole word is built on the verb to lodge, which has four relevant senses the prefix dis- (apart, away, reversal) negates: (a) to become fixed or stuck — a bone lodged in the throat, a splinter lodged in the hand; (b) to provide or take shelter — to lodge travellers; (c) to deposit formally — to lodge a complaint, an appeal, an objection; (d) to place firmly in the mind — the image lodged in my memory. Hence dislodge: (a) to free what is stuck (the Heimlich sense); (b) to displace from shelter or quarters (the old military sense); (c) (rarely) to withdraw a deposit; (d) to remove from the memory or mind. Every use of dislodged thus implies that the thing displaced was previously lodged — settled into a niche, a position, a socket or a mind.

6. Register and connotation. “Dislodged” is a neutral-to-formal physical verb with a distinctly tactical heritage; it is at home in news prose, medicine, geology and narrative description, and slightly above everyday casual speech, whose blunter equivalents are “knocked loose”, “shifted”, “prised out”. It carries a quiet suggestion of resistance overcome: dislodging takes effort because the lodged thing did not want to move. In the passage, that effortful, violent transfer is exactly the point: eagles, not weather, dislodge goblins from slopes.
