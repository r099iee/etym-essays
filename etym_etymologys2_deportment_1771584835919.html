ROOT: PIE *per- "to lead, pass over, carry" (reconstructed) — a root of passage and conveyance: to go across, to bring across, to carry through. From it Europe built its verbs of travel (Germanic) and of carrying (Italic).

CHAIN (the route into English deportment):
1. PIE *per- "to lead, pass over, carry" (reconstructed; no direct attestation). Same root: Sanskrit pṛ-/par- "to bring over, carry across" (píparti "brings across, rescues"; pārá "the far side, the opposite shore" → Hindi pār पार "across, beyond"); Proto-Germanic *faraną "to travel, go" → Old English faran → English fare, wayfarer, welfare; *ferjaną "to carry across" → Old Norse ferja → English ferry.
2. Latin portāre "to carry, convey, bear" (Classical Latin) — from the Italic reflex of the root; also the source of portus "harbour" (English port), porta "gate" (English portal, porch), portātor "carrier" (English porter).
3. Latin deportāre "to carry away, carry off; to carry (oneself)" (Classical Latin) — de- "away, down from" + portāre "to carry". In classical usage the verb meant physical removal, e.g. of a prisoner.
4. Old French deporter "to carry away, remove; to behave, conduct oneself; to divert, amuse" (c. 11th–13th centuries) — the reflexive se deporter developed the sense "to conduct oneself," literally "to carry oneself."
5. Old French déportement "behaviour, conduct, demeanor" (suffix -ment) — the nominalised manner of carrying oneself.
6. Middle English deportement "behaviour, conduct, manner of carrying oneself" (borrowed c. 1600s) → English deportment "manner of carrying oneself; bearing, demeanor." ◄ OUR WORD.

SISTER FORMATION (same Latin verb, split sense): English deport "to banish, transport away from a country" (16th–17th centuries) preserves the classical physical sense of deportāre "to carry away"; the modern pair deportment "how one carries oneself" and deportation "how the state carries one away" descend from the same Latin verb along two different lines. Related English loans from the same family: comport, comportment (Latin comportāre "to carry together"), porter, port, portal, porch, fare, ferry.

NOTE: no stage of this chain is debated; the derivation from Latin portāre is standard and secure in the OED and etymological handbooks. The PIE form of the root is sometimes cited as *per- and sometimes as *perh₂- / *preh₂- in recent handbooks; the reconstruction *per- follows the traditional presentation (Pokorny, Watkins).
