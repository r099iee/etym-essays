The biography of NAPE is short and strange: a word that has meant one precise thing for more than seven hundred years yet cannot tell us where it came from. It first appears in Middle English around 1300 with the sense "back of the neck," and from that day to this it has not shifted its meaning by a hair's breadth — remarkable stability for an English word, and all the more remarkable given that its origin is lost. Where most English vocabulary can be traced back through Old English to Proto-Germanic and beyond into the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European family, "nape" stands alone: no cognates, no clear ancestor, no agreed root, and the OED pronounces its origin obscure. There is no Old English ancestor, no Latin cousin, no Germanic relative that shares its shape, and no way to push its history back beyond the fourteenth century.

Two hypotheses have been offered for the mystery, and neither is secure:
  • Hypothesis A (favoured by the OED): a link to the expressive Germanic base behind English "nap" (n.) "the soft, fuzzy pile of cloth" — Middle English noppe, Middle Dutch noppe, Middle Low German noppe, all of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. On this view the back of the neck, the downy place where the hair begins to grow, was likened to the fuzzy nap of cloth, and the word for the one was carried over to the other. The phonetic fit is plausible and the semantic analogy is attractive — both denote a soft, fuzzy, short-fibred surface — but there is no surviving intermediate form to prove the transfer, so the link remains a hypothesis.
  • Hypothesis B (rejected): an old fancy, repeated in nineteenth-century dictionaries, derived "nape" from Old French nape or hanap "goblet, cup," on the idea that the roll of flesh at the back of the neck resembled the rim of a bowl. The OED and Etymonline both treat this as a dead end — an attractive fancy without a shred of evidence and semantically far-fetched to boot.

The word's isolation is part of its character. French uses nuque, which descends through medieval Latin nucha from an Arabic word for the spinal cord or marrow; German has Nacken, Italian nuca, Spanish nuca — none of them related to English "nape," which stands alone in its corner of Germanic like a linguistic isolate. It is tempting, and wrong, to pull it into the Indo-European family of *mon-, "neck," the root that gave Sanskrit manyā "nape of the neck" (attested in the Rigveda), Latin monīle "necklace," and English "mane." Many older dictionaries flirted with that idea, and the earlier condensed version of this card displayed it side by side; but the historical evidence does not support it, and the two families must be kept strictly apart. The Sanskrit, Latin and Germanic words are genuine cognates of one another; "nape" is not their relative.

What the record does allow is a history of usage rather than of form. Throughout Middle and early modern English, "nape" was the plain everyday word for the back of the neck, used in medical, anatomical and ordinary description. From the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it acquires a second, literary life: the Romantic and Victorian novelists discover the nape as the most intimate and vulnerable part of the body — the place where hair begins, where a neck curves, where a kiss may land — and it becomes a standard prop of love scenes and of tender physical description. In the twentieth century the word broadens slightly in professional registers: hairdressers speak of the nape of a haircut, tailors of the nape point, zoologists of an animal's scruff (the loose nape skin by which mothers carry their young). Yet no matter how technical the setting, the word has never lost its reserve of delicacy, and it is precisely that reserve that a translator like David Hawkes can exploit in a sentence about pearls and gold.

The semantic stability of "nape" is itself worth a paragraph, for it is almost unique among English body-part nouns. Words for other parts of the body drifted constantly — "thigh," "hip," "loin" narrowed and broadened and narrowed again — but "nape" has meant one thing, the back of the neck, without deviation for seven centuries. This suggests that the word filled a genuine lexical gap: Old English had no single everyday noun for the back of the neck (the area was simply part of "neck" or "hals"), and once Middle English acquired a precise monosyllable for it, the language kept it because it had no reason to let it go. A word that means exactly one thing, and says it compactly, survives; a word whose origin is unknown but whose function is irreplaceable does not need an etymology to thrive. The mystery of its birth has never cost it a single day of use.
