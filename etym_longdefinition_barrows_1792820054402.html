Barrows, plural of barrow in its vehicle sense, are small open carts for moving heavy loads by hand: a shallow box or tray with handles behind and one wheel (or two) in front, pushed by a walker and tipped forward to empty. They are the humblest freight vehicles in English, the garden, building site, and market stall's answer to the lorry.

In the source sentence the road to the gate is blocked with barrows and handcarts, and Tolkien means exactly this everyday clutter: party preparations have filled Bag End's lane with borrowed garden barrows, delivery carts, and emptied boxes, the cheerful traffic jam of a village celebration. The plural matters — one barrow is a tool, but a lane of barrows is an event.

Core meaning in detail: The classic wheelbarrow combines lever and wheel: the load sits over or just behind the axle, the carrier takes only part of the weight on the handles, and tipping does the unloading. Hand-barrows, the older form, have no wheel at all and are carried by two people, one at each end, like a stretcher. Both types share the idea of a hand-scale load-carrier, distinguished from wagons and carts only by size and by being pushed or carried rather than pulled by animals.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Wheelbarrows for garden and building loads (primary modern sense, from 14th c.): barrows of soil, manure, gravel, and rubbish. The bridge is direct inheritance: Old English bearwe, a carrying basket-frame, gains a wheel in the Middle Ages and becomes the one-wheeled garden servant everyone knows.
  • Hand-barrows carried by two bearers (older sense): a barrow of fish, a sedan-like litter for the injured. The bridge is the pre-wheel original: before wheels, loads travelled slung between two people, and the word named the frame itself.
  • Market and street-vendor barrows (commercial sense): the costermonger's barrow of apples, the flower-seller's barrow. The bridge is trade on foot: the same garden tool, fitted with displays and scales, becomes a mobile shop for sellers too poor for premises.
  • Industrial barrows (technical): mortar barrows, slag barrows, works barrows on rails or with pneumatic tyres. The bridge is mechanisation: factories and mines scale the garden shape up in iron, keeping the name while losing the garden.
  • A burial mound (famous homograph, different word): ancient barrows on the downs, chambered barrows of the Bronze Age. The bridge here is spelling only, not history: mound-barrow descends from Old English beorg, hill, while vehicle-barrow descends from bearwe, carrier. Tolkien, who knew both, plays in the borderland between them, and learners must keep the twins apart.
  • A heap or load itself (transferred, colloquial): a barrow of compost, to shift barrows of mud. The bridge carries the container into its contents, as cup and spoonful do: the measure takes the name of the measurer.

Register and connotation: barrows is workmanlike and cheerful — allotments, harvest festivals, and market mornings — with honest dirt on its hands. It never sounds grand or martial; even full, a barrow is modest, and to call something barrow-work is to praise patient, unglamorous labour. The burial-mound twin lends an accidental poetry: a lane blocked with barrows sounds faintly like a lane of ancient tombs until the handcarts clarify the shopping list.
