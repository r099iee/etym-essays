APOTHEOSIS enters English in the 1600s as a learned borrowing of Late Latin apotheosis deification, especially of an emperor, itself borrowed whole from Greek apotheosis, action-noun in -sis from the verb apotheoun to deify. That verb is transparently apo- plus theos god plus factitive -oun, meaning to make into a god, with apo- here carrying its completive or transformative force change into, as Etymonline notes, rather than simple motion away. The morphology is therefore classical and undisputed, resting on two of the commonest Greek elements: a preverb found in apology, apostasy and apocalypse, and a noun found in theology, theism, atheism and pantheon.

The preverb has a secure Indo-European pedigree. PIE *apo-, variant *h2epo in laryngeal notation, meant off, away from, and appears in Sanskrit apa away, Latin ab from, Gothic af, English of and off. In Greek apo it governs the genitive and builds hundreds of compounds where removal shades into completion, disappearance and transformation. De Vaan and Pokorny document the Latin branch extensively. By contrast the noun theos god is philologically troubled. Etymonline repeats the older Pokorny assignment to PIE *dhes- forming words for religious concepts, linking theos to sacred language. Beekes in his Etymological Dictionary of Greek rejects this, listing theos as without secure etymology and possibly Pre-Greek substrate, noting dialectal variants and the absence of regular cognates. All authorities agree on one negative: despite look and meaning, Greek theos is not related to Latin deus god, whose true cognate is Greek Zeus from PIE *dyew- to shine, sky. Zero-guesswork therefore presents theos with an explicit debate flag.

  • Greek to Roman ritual: In Hellenistic courts flatterers voted divine honours to living kings; Rome systematised this as consecratio, called in Greek sources apotheosis. Upon an emperor's death the senate decreed divinity, a witness swore he saw the soul ascend, an eagle was released from the pyre, and the new divus received priest, temple and festival. Writers from Cicero to Suetonius and Cassius Dio use apotheosis for this legal-religious promotion. Phonetics pass unchanged into Latin except for accent; semantics narrow from any deifying to imperial procedure.
  • Christian and art-historical mediation: Patristic authors denounce pagan apotheosis while transferring its imagery to Christ's ascension, the Virgin's assumption and saints' glory. Renaissance and Baroque ceilings literalise it: Titian, Rubens and Tiepolo paint apotheoses where foreshortened bodies spiral into luminous heaven, fixing the modern visual sense of figures lifted into light. French apotheose mediates the word to England alongside theology and connoisseurship.
  • English literary career: First attested in the seventeenth century for deification in theological controversy, apotheosis soon broadens. Dryden, Pope and Gibbon use it for poets and emperors with mingled admiration and irony. Nineteenth-century critics apply it to Shakespeare, Napoleon and steam; twentieth-century journalism democratises it to the apotheosis of denim, brunch or the fast bowler. Pronunciation settles as RP /əˌpɒθiˈəʊsɪs/ with stress on -o-, plural apotheoses. The falcon passage exploits the full arc: a pagan emperor's rite crowns a bird, making DDT-poisoned helplessness feel like sacrilege, the fall of a god.

Thus apotheosis preserves its spelling across twenty-five centuries while its reverence migrates from altar to museum label to sports page. Its etymology is half sunlight, half shadow: apo- traced to a secure PIE adverb, theos confessed as mystery. That asymmetry is itself instructive, reminding learners that even words for gods can have unknown gods of origin.
