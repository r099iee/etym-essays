spindly, rheumy, and flabby are three adjectives of physical frailty and decay, which Jack Vance arrays in a single devastating portrait: "It was a thing to arouse pity—a great head on a small spindly body, with weak rheumy eyes and a flabby button of a nose." Each word attacks a different axis of the creature's weakness — structure, moisture, and tone.

Part 1: SPINDLY — Definition

spindly (adjective): long, thin, disproportionately slender, and noticeably weak or fragile; shaped like the slender rod of a textile spindle. Etymologically rooted in Old English spinel ("spindle"), it describes physical frames, limbs, stems, or structures whose height or length far outstrips their mechanical strength or supportive breadth.

Polysemous Spectrum of SPINDLY

  • 1. Physical Limbs and Body Anatomy: unnaturally long, thin, and weak legs, arms, or torsos. Contextual example: "The newborn calf wobbled uncertainly on its spindly legs."
  • 2. Botanical Etiolation and Plant Morphology: in horticulture, plants that grow weak, pale, and unnaturally tall due to light deprivation (etiolation); "spindly seedlings" are stretched stems too weak to stand.
  • 3. Mechanical and Furniture Architecture: furniture legs, scaffolding, or structures that are thin to the point of fragility — "spindly chair legs" that look as though they might buckle.
  • 4. Figurative Fragility: by extension, any argument, institution, or scheme that is stretched too thin to support its own weight — "a spindly alibi."
  • 5. The Historical Spindle Metaphor: the whole word rests on the image of the drop-spindle — the tapering wooden rod on which thread was wound — the ancient standard of slenderness in Western technology and proverbial thinness ("spindle-shanks" for skinny legs).

Part 2: RHEUMY — Definition

rheumy (adjective): watery, running, or discharging thin moisture, especially of the eyes; affected with or producing rheum — the thin, mucous, often watery discharge of the eyes and nose. Descending through Latin and Old French from Greek rheuma, "a flow," it is a technical word of old medicine that survives chiefly as a pitying literary adjective of age and feebleness.

Polysemous Spectrum of RHEUMY

  • 1. Weeping, Discharging Eyes (Literary Sense): the primary modern use — weak, filmy, moisture-laden eyes that leak thin fluid, the classic mark of old age, sickness, or feebleness. Contextual example: "The old man blinked at us through rheumy eyes."
  • 2. Medical / Historical Humoral Usage: in old medicine, rheum was the thin discharge of the eyes and nose read as a leak in the body's humoral economy; a rheumy condition was one of excessive or weakened flows.
  • 3. Figurative / Poetic Extension: anything watery, misty, or weeping — a rheumy dawn, rheumy light — transferring the moisture of the eyes to atmosphere and mood.
  • 4. The Rheumatism Homonym Trap: rheumy must be distinguished from rheumatic: both derive from the same Greek root of flowing, but rheumy concerns discharges, rheumatic concerns the aching of joints (the old theory that joint pain was caused by a flow of humours into the joint).

Part 3: FLABBY — Definition

flabby (adjective): soft, loose, hanging, and lacking firmness or muscle tone; yielding and flaccid to the touch. Formed in the late 17th century from flab + -y, of the imitative family of flap and floppy, it describes flesh, objects, and by extension arguments, prose, and character that lack vigour and structure.

Polysemous Spectrum of FLABBY

  • 1. Flesh without Tone (Primary Physical Sense): soft, loose, unresilient flesh that yields under pressure and flops about — the tactile opposite of firm, taut, muscular. Contextual example: "He grew flabby from lack of exercise."
  • 2. Figurative — Flabby Argument, Prose, Character: since the 18th–19th centuries the word has stretched beyond flesh: a flabby argument is one with no structure or force; flabby prose is loose and wordy; a flabby character is weak, indecisive, lacking moral backbone.
  • 3. Objects and Materials: any soft, puffy, unresilient object — a flabby cushion, flabby dough — that does not spring back to shape.
  • 4. Economic and Corporate Metaphor: in modern usage a "flabby" organisation, budget, or bureaucracy is one that is over-stuffed, inefficient, and lacking discipline — the body's lost tone applied to institutions.

Combined Portrait

Vance's three adjectives work as a single diagnostic trio: spindly names the creature's broken structure (too long and thin to bear its great head), rheumy its broken moisture (eyes that weep without strength), and flabby its broken tone (flesh without firmness). Together they depict a life assembled badly — a body whose every system fails to hold itself together, and which therefore arouses the pity Vance describes.
