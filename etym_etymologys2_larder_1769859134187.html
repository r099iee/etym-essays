Latin lardum (also laridum) "cured pork fat, bacon, lard" (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus; classical Latin; the word's own pre-Latin origin is uncertain — see note) → Old French larde "bacon, cured pork fat" (attested 11th–12th c. CE; also source of English lard), with the derived noun lardier "a storeroom for meat, especially cured fat" (attested 12th c. CE) → Anglo-Norman larder / lardier "a room where meat is kept" (12th–13th c. CE; Anglo-Norman was the form that passed the word into English) → Middle English larder "a storeroom for meat and provisions" (attested 14th c., c. 1300, in the OED) → Modern English larder "a cool room or cupboard for storing food, especially meat and provisions" (the sense widened from meat-store to general food-store as domestic life changed; "larder fridge" appears in the 20th c.) → Modern English LARDER "a room or large cupboard for storing food; a pantry" ◄ TARGET rootWord Debate / nuance note: The etymology of Latin lardum itself is not settled. Some authorities connect it to Greek larinós "fat, plump" or to a broader Mediterranean substrate word for fat, but no Indo-European root is securely established for it; it is safest to treat Latin lardum as a word of uncertain, possibly substrate, origin. By contrast, the path from Latin lardum through Old French lardier to English larder is fully attested and certain — the room was named for the cured pork fat (lard) that was its most valued contents.
