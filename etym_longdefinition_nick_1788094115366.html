NICK (noun and verb) is a short, versatile English word whose oldest surviving sense is a small cut, notch, or indentation made in the surface of something — a blade, a stick, a piece of wood, or the skin. In the reading context from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit ("down the gale in the ___ of time"), the word appears in the fixed idiomatic phrase in the nick of time, meaning "at the exact critical moment, just before it is too late". Here nick does not denote a physical cut at all, but an exact point in time; the phrase describes the Eagles arriving at the decisive instant of the Battle of Five Armies. This idiom is the word's most famous survival in modern English and the sense the reader must grasp: a hair's-breadth of timing, the precise last possible moment.

Beyond that idiom, nick carries a rich cluster of related and divergent senses across different registers of English:

  • Physical cut or notch (noun): a small incision, groove, or indentation, especially one accidentally made (e.g., "a nick in the razor blade", "a nick on his chin from shaving"). This is the core concrete sense from which the others branch.
  • To cut slightly (verb): to make such a small cut or notch; in cricket, to nick means to deflect the ball with the edge of the bat, and the resulting deflection is itself called a nick — a specialized sporting-jargon use.
  • British criminal slang — to steal (verb): since the 19th century, to nick something means to steal it ("someone nicked my bike"). The semantic bridge runs from "to cut off, to snatch away" — cutting a purse or picking a pocket — into thieves' cant.
  • British slang — to arrest; and "the nick" = prison or police station (verb and noun): to nick a criminal means to catch and arrest him; the nick is the police station or prison. The bridge is again the sense of "seizing, grabbing" developed inside the criminal register.
  • In the nick of time (idiom): at the exact critical moment. First attested in the late 16th century (in the nick, 1580s), the phrase probably derives from the old practice of marking time or tallying on a notched stick — the nick being the precise notch that marks the point — so "the nick of time" is literally "the exact notch of time".
  • Old Nick (proper-noun use): a folk name for the Devil, attested from the mid-17th century, probably a euphemistic application of the personal name Nicholas, reshaped by popular etymology and by the word's existing overtones of cutting and sharp practice.
  • Nickname (derivative): nick is the surviving core of nickname, which arose by misdivision of an eke-name ("an additional name", from Old English ēaca "addition, increase") into a neke-name, then a nick-name; the nick here is purely phonetic, not semantic.
  • In good nick (idiom, British and Australian): "in good condition", said of a person, animal, or object; the bridge is the image of a well-trimmed, well-kept state, as of a groomed horse or a maintained tool.

Semantic bridges and secondary meanings. The unifying thread is "a small precise cut or mark". From "cut" the word moved in two directions: (1) toward precision and exactness — the notch as a tally mark giving "the exact point" and hence "the nick of time"; (2) toward seizure — cutting something off, snatching it, hence stealing and arresting in British slang. In woodworking and metalwork a nick is a deliberate notch cut as a guide or marker, reinforcing the tally-mark sense. In pathology and daily life, "a nick" is a minor wound, far milder than a cut or a gash — the register is informal, physical, and usually trivial, which is why "a nick" rarely alarms anyone.

Register and usage today. In contemporary English nick is informal-to-neutral. The physical senses (small cut) are everyday; the slang senses (steal, arrest, prison) are strongly British, colloquial, and casual; in the nick of time is a fully idiomatic, slightly literary phrase used in narration and speech alike, and is the sense most likely to appear in literary texts such as Tolkien's. The word's brevity and hard monosyllabic shape (/nɪk/) make it a favourite of headline writers and idiom hunters.
