COMPONENT 1: WITH
↓
PIE (c. 4000–3500 BCE)
*wi-
"apart, away, in two"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE–200 CE)
*wiþra-
"against, opposite, toward"
↓
Old English (8th–11th c.)
wiþ
"against, opposite, toward"
↓
Middle English (12th–15th c.)
with
"in company with, by means of"
(sense flips from 'against' to 'alongside',
influenced by Old Norse við)

COMPONENT 2: OUT
↓
PIE (c. 4000–3500 BCE)
*úd
"up, out, away"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE–200 CE)
*ūt
"out, outside"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c.)
ūt
"out, forth"
↓
Old English (c. 10th c.)
ūtan
"from outside, from without"
↓
Old English (c. 1000)
wiþūtan
"outside, on the outside"
(compound: wiþ + ūtan)
↓
Middle English (13th–15th c.)
withoute
"outside; not having, lacking"
↓
Modern English
WITHOUT

Note: the PIE root *wi- is reconstructed on strong comparative evidence (Sanskrit ví, Latin vitare 'avoid', Greek a(h)- in 'a(h)ekon' 'unwilling'), but some scholars propose a laryngeal-laden form *h2wi-; the form of the root is debated but its meaning 'apart/in two' is secure. The exact vowel of the Old English wiþ and the degree of Old Norse influence on the Middle English shift of meaning are also points of scholarly discussion.
