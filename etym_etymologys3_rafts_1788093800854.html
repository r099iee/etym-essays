A Germanic beam. The story of rafts begins with a single log. The Proto-Germanic noun *raftaz meant “log, beam, rafter” — a piece of timber — and its descendants are scattered across the Germanic world: Old Norse raptr (a log, a rafter), Old English ræfter (a roof beam, the ancestor of modern rafter), Middle Dutch raefter, Old High German rafter. No generally accepted Indo-European root has been securely established for *raftaz: older attempts to connect it with a PIE base meaning “to break off” (a log as a broken-off branch) remain speculative, and honest etymology therefore begins at the Germanic stage rather than inventing a PIE form.

Norse and the Danelaw. The English word came in with the Vikings. In the centuries of Scandinavian settlement (9th–11th centuries), northern England was bilingual in English and Old Norse, and Norse timber-words entered the dialects. Old Norse raptr, “log, rafter”, is the standard source given for Middle English raft (attested c. 1300 in the sense “beam, rafter, log”), while the native Anglo-Saxon cognate ræfter survived independently in the south and gives us the modern word rafter — the sloping beam of a roof. The pairing is a classic Danelaw doublet: two cognates, one borrowed, one native, both ultimately from the same Germanic stem.

The part names the whole (late 15th century). The decisive semantic shift occurred in the late 15th century: raft moved from “a log” to “a floating platform of timber”. The bridge is metonymy of the most natural kind — a raft is built of logs, and the word for the building material became the word for the structure. The new sense spread quickly because the object was ancient and universal: rafts of lashed timber, reeds or inflated skins are among humanity's oldest watercraft, known from every river culture from Mesopotamia to the Americas. By the 16th–17th centuries, travellers' accounts made the English word familiar across Europe and the New World: armies crossed rivers on rafts, settlers floated their goods downstream on them.

The New World and the lumber era. In 18th–19th century America, the raft became an industrial instrument. The great rivers — the Mississippi, the Ohio, the Delaware — carried immense log rafts, sometimes acres in extent, floated by lumbermen from the forests to the sawmills and cities downstream; “rafting” was a skilled trade, and “raftsmen” a recognised class of river workers. Mark Twain's Huckleberry Finn (1884) gave the log raft its enduring literary form: Huck and Jim's raft, drifting down the Mississippi, became an American symbol of freedom. It is in this same riverine world that the colloquial sense “a raft of” (a great number) is first recorded, in 1837 American English: the image is of a piled mass — a raft of logs, hence a raft of anything. Scholars debate the exact pedigree: some derive the quantity sense from the platform word, others from raff, “heap, large amount” (as in riffraff); the matter is not settled, and the doubt belongs in any honest account.

The 20th century: rescue and sport. The twentieth century added two technical chapters. First, the life raft: inflatable buoyant craft, carried on ships and aircraft for emergency evacuation, standardised in maritime safety practice in the decades after the Titanic disaster and the World Wars — the raft became a life-saving device, the very opposite of the improvised timber platform. Second, the sporting raft: from the 1970s onward, whitewater rafting — paddling inflatable rafts down rapids — grew into a global recreational industry, fixing the verb “to raft” and the gerund “rafting” in the popular vocabulary. Parallel technical extensions multiplied the image: “raft foundations” (concrete slabs floating a building on soft ground), “ice rafting” (geological transport of debris by floating ice), “rafts” of floating vegetation in biology.

Summary. From a Germanic log, through a Norse loan, a Danelaw doublet with rafter, a metonymic leap from timber to platform, an American industrial heyday, a colloquial quantity sense, and a modern career in rescue, sport and science — rafts is a word that has floated a remarkable distance from its first, solid, single beam.
