Touchstone is a transparent compound with a cloudy half. Stone is straightforwardly Germanic: Old English stān, Proto-Germanic *stainaz, usually referred to PIE *steyh₂- “to stiffen, become solid” (what stiffens into rock), though some handbooks leave the deeper root as uncertain. Its phonetic journey — long ā rounding and shortening to modern stone /stəʊn/ — is textbook Great Vowel Shift plus pre-consonantal shortening in the compound. Touch is the puzzle: Middle English touchen comes from Old French tochier/tuchier “to touch, hit, try,” itself from Vulgar Latin *toccare “to knock, strike a bell,” first attested around the 7th century. Where did *toccare come from? Two rival accounts persist: (a) borrowing from Frankish or another Germanic *tokk- “to tug, touch lightly” (cf. Old High German zucchen), or (b) an imitative creation from the knock-sound tok-tok. Most Romance etymologists now favor the Germanic source with imitative reinforcement — a compromise that explains both the form and the hit/touch doublet of meaning. This debate must be stated openly: touch has no secure PIE pedigree.

The compound’s thing-history is older than its word-history. The stone itself — Greek basanos, lapis Lydius (“Lydian stone”) — was in use by 600 BCE in Lydia, the kingdom that invented coinage and therefore needed instant forgery detection. Theophrastus (On Stones, c. 300 BCE) describes it precisely; Pliny (Natural History 33.126) repeats him for Rome. Rub gold on black jasper, compare the yellow streak to needles of known karat, read fineness to within a karat or two — all without furnace. Because the test was quick, cheap, and honest, moralists seized it early: Pindar, Euripides, and later Stoic preachers call adversity or a true friend the basanos of character.

English word milestones:

  • c. 1500 literal tool: Caxton-era goldsmith ordinances mention the “towchestone” alongside weights and scales; Caxton and Elyot use it literally before daring metaphor.
  • 1530s–1600s moralized tool: Tyndale, Latimer, and Shakespeare (“now do I play the touchstone” is punned in As You Like It through the clown Touchstone) make it the standard figure for trial by experience; “the touchstone of truth” becomes pulpit cliché.
  • 1860s Arnoldian canon: Matthew Arnold’s Study of Poetry (1880, lectured earlier) proposes “touchstone” passages — Homer’s “rosy-fingered dawn” lines, Dante, Milton — to be memorized as instant detectors of poetic value, shifting the noun from the testing stone to the tested gold, a productive ambiguity still with us.
  • 1900s–2000s promiscuous criterion: every discipline, brand deck, and op-ed anoints its touchstone; philosophers of science reply, as in the source sentence, that all-purpose touchstones contradict what perception science shows — judgment is comparative and contextual, not single-stone.

That sceptical use is the word’s maturity: only a culture saturated with touchstones can complain of an “all-purpose” one. The pronunciation is fully native (/ˈtʌtʃstəʊn/, two stresses collapsing to one), the spelling fused since the 16th century (earlier touch-stone, touch stone), and the meaning has traveled from workshop to study without losing its tactile promise — truth known by contact, not by syllogism.
