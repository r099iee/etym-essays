Satirists is the plural of satirist: a person who practises satire, the literary and artistic mode that exposes human folly, vice, and pretension through irony, ridicule, parody, and scorn. Where flattery tells the powerful what they wish to hear, the satirist tells them what they need to hear, exaggerated until it stings. Juvenal scourged Roman corruption, Swift proposed eating Irish children to shame English indifference, Austen punctured genteel self-deception with cool irony, and in the source sentence Thorstein Veblen — economist as satirist — turns the leisure class’s lawns, silver spoons, and conspicuous leisure into anthropology, mocking status games until the reader feels the ornament for the emptiness it is.

The central sense is literary mocker with a moral mission. Satirists differ from mere jokers because their ridicule claims a corrective purpose: the classical formula is to lash vice so that society may mend. This gives the role its characteristic double posture — superior enough to judge, engaged enough to care — and its characteristic defences: I am only joking shielding I am entirely serious. Practitioners range across verse satirists (Horace, Pope), novelistic ironists (Swift, Twain, Waugh), essayists and economists (Veblen, Mencken, Orwell), cartoonists, and in modern times stand-up comedians and television hosts who inherit the same licence to mock the mighty.

A secondary sense extends the label to any habitual mocker or ironist, whether or not they publish: a dinner-table satirist, the office satirist whose emails drip with irony. Here the corrective mission thins toward temperament — a sharp, deflating tongue — and the word can shade from praise (fearless truth-teller) to blame (sneering cynic who tears down without building up). Closely related is the visual and performing satirist: caricaturists, cartoonists, and filmmakers whose medium is image rather than sentence but whose operation — exaggerate the telling feature until pretension collapses — is identical. Roman theory already distinguished the smiling Horatian satirist, who corrects with urbane laughter, from the raging Juvenalian, who denounces with indignation; modern usage preserves that spectrum, calling some satirists gentle, others savage, but always assuming an audience complicit enough to get the joke and guilty enough to deserve it.

  • Literary satirist: author of formal satires — verse, novels, essays — attacking folly with ridicule; the core sense.
  • Horatian (gentle): the amused, conversational mocker who corrects with a smile, in Horace’s manner.
  • Juvenalian (savage): the indignant denouncer whose mockery burns, in Juvenal’s manner; Veblen’s incessant mocking leans this way.
  • Visual / performing satirist: cartoonist, caricaturist, parodist, stand-up — same corrective mockery in non-prose media.
  • Colloquial temperament: a habitually ironic, deflating person; praise when brave, blame when merely cruel.
  • Scholarly / economic satirist: the analyst — like Veblen — whose ostensibly scientific description is sharpened into sustained irony.
