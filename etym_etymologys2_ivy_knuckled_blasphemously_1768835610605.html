ivy — linear derivation chain: 1. Pre-Indo-European substrate stem *ibah- “the ivy plant” (pre-Germanic Europe, before c. 1000 BCE) — no secure PIE root can be established; the leading handbooks treat the stem as a western-European substrate (non-Indo-European) botanical name. 2. Proto-West Germanic *ibah- / *ibahō “ivy” (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–5th c. CE). 3. Old English īfig, īfeg “ivy” (attested c. 725 in the Epinal and Corpus glossaries). 4. Middle English ivi, ivye, ivy “ivy” (attested 13th–14th c.). 5. English ivy (spelling fixed in the 16th c.). Debate note: a traditional minority hypothesis connects the word with the yew (Old English īw, Proto-Germanic *īwaz), proposing a semantic bridge “yew / evergreen tree” → “clinging evergreen.” The required phonological change (*-w- to *-b-) is irregular, so most authorities prefer the substrate explanation and label the ultimate origin uncertain. knuckled — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *ken- “to compress, pinch” (reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE) — the uncertain base proposed for the Germanic “knob / bone” group (Pokorny’s gen-/ken- set; debated). 2. Proto-Germanic *knuk- “bone, knob, protuberance” (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–100 CE) — source of Middle Dutch cnoke, Middle Low German knoke, Old High German knoh(a), German Knochen “bone.” 3. Proto-Germanic *knukilaz “little bone, small knob” (reconstructed) — diminutive formed with the suffix *-il-. 4. Old English cnucel, cnycel “knuckle of the hand, a small knob” (attested c. 1000, Ælfric’s Glossary). 5. Middle English knokel “bony joint of the finger” (attested 13th c.). 6. English knuckle (noun, spelling settled 15th–16th c.); verb knuckle “to bend or touch with the knuckles” (16th c.); past participle / adjective knuckled “having knuckles; rough with knobs” (16th c.). Debate note: the ultimate Indo-European base of *knuk- is uncertain. Some scholars group it with the “lump–knob–nut” cluster, linking it to Latin nux “nut” via an extended root *knu-k-, but the connection is speculative and not universally accepted. blasphemously — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *bʰeh₂- “to speak, say” (reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE). 2. Greek phēmē “speech, saying, report, reputation” (Homeric, 8th c. BCE) — built on the same root as phanai “to speak.” 3. Greek blasphēmos “speaking evil, slanderous, profane” (attested 5th c. BCE, Sophocles; the verb blasphēmein “to speak profanely” is common in classical prose) — a compound of blapsis “an injury, harm” (from blaptein “to injure, damage, impede,” of uncertain origin) + phēmē “speech.” 4. Latin blasphemus “speaking evil, reviling, profane” (classical Latin; the Christian sense is fixed by the Vulgate, 4th c. CE; also blasphēmia “blasphemy”). 5. Ecclesiastical Latin blasphemāre “to blaspheme” → Old French blasfemer, blasmer “to blaspheme, to blame” (10th–12th c.). 6. Middle English blasfemen “to blaspheme” (13th c.); Middle English blasfemous, blasphemous “blasphemous” (14th c., from Latin blasphemus or Old French). 7. English blasphemously — blasphemous + adverbial suffix -ly (attested 15th–16th c.; Shakespeare uses “blasphemous” in the 1590s). Debate note: the first element blapsis is itself of uncertain origin, so the compound’s analysis (“injury” + “speech”) is secure in meaning but its ultimate root is not; Middle English spelling fluctuated between -ou- and -o- before settling on -ou- under Latin influence.
