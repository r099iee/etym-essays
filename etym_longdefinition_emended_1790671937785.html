emended is the past tense and past participle of the verb emend, a formal, chiefly textual verb meaning to remove errors from a text, document or work in order to restore or improve its correctness, typically by careful scholarly comparison, conjecture or reference to authority. One emends a manuscript, a proof, a constitution, a poem, a musical score. The action presupposes a corrupt or faulty witness and a truer reading to be recovered: the scribe miscopied, the printer mis-set, the author slipped, and the emender repairs. In the acknowledgement at issue — students who read drafts and amended and emended — the pairing is deliberate and artful: they both amended, i.e. improved the substance and conduct of the argument, and emended, i.e. corrected its letter, purging slips, solecisms and misquotations with philological care.

The amend / emend distinction, endlessly confused, is load-bearing. Both descend from Latin menda, a fault or blemish, but diverge in prefix and province. Amend (from a- + mendare via French) means to change for the better in morals, behaviour, law or general condition: you amend your life, amend a bill, amend a motion. Emend (from e-/ex- + mendare, to free from fault) is narrower and textual: you emend Homer, Shakespeare, a code base. Amending alters the thing to make it better than it was; emending restores the thing to what it should have been. Editors therefore speak of an emended edition, an emendation adopted, a conjectural emendation where no manuscript supports the fix but sense demands it. To collapse the two is itself an error calling for emendation.

Secondary and extended senses cluster tightly around this textual core:

  • Textual-critical core: to correct readings — the passage was emended from ye to the; Bentley's emended Horace. Neutral-scholarly, often with apparatus, sigla and notes. Connotation is learned diligence, sometimes pedantic daring.
  • General editorial correction: to proofread and tidy — she emended the typescript for spelling and punctuation. Slightly elevated synonym of copy-edit; differs from amend in touching letter, not policy.
  • Legal-historical usage: formerly, to emend a law could mean to purge it of scribal error as distinct from amending its substance; modern statutes prefer amend, leaving emend to antiquarians.
  • Figurative-moral extension (rare, literary): to emend one's life or soul as if it were a corrupt manuscript — Donne, Milton and modern poets play on the book-of-life conceit. Self-consciously bookish; an ordinary moralist would say amend or reform.
  • Computing and genetics by analogy: to emend code or emend a sequence is occasionally used for surgical correction of single-character errors, borrowing textual prestige. Jargonistic and marked.

Grammar and collocation are distinctive. Emended is almost always transitive and passive-leaning: texts are emended, readings stand emended, editions are newly emended. It takes from X to Y, in favour of, on the basis of, after a manuscript: emended after the Ellesmere manuscript; emended from quarto to folio. The noun is emendation, the agent emender or emendator, the adjective emendatory. Register is invariably formal-academic; to use emend in casual speech — let me emend what you just said — is either jocularly lofty or plainly wrong, and copy-editors reserve it for work that invokes authority and conjecture rather than mere tidying. Evaluatively it is laudatory but risky: a brilliant emendation wins fame, a rash one earns the gibe of corrupting while claiming to correct.

In contemporary humanities, emended also signals method and humility. A critical edition advertises itself as emended to promise that variants have been weighed, errors expelled and the author's hand restored so far as possible, with every intervention documented. Acknowledgements like the present one therefore do double duty: they thank helpers and certify process — drafts were not merely read but amended in thought and emended in letter, improved and purified. The doublet has classical ring, echoing Renaissance title-pages that boast castigatus et emendatus, chastised and emended, scourged free of fault.
