As a verb, to harbor means to give lodging, refuge, or enclosed protection to someone or something — or, figuratively, to keep a thought, feeling, or suspicion within oneself. The physical sense pictures a safe interior held against a hostile exterior: a port that shelters ships from storm, a house that shelters a fugitive, a body that harbors a pathogen. The psychological sense treats the mind as just such an interior: one harbors a grudge, a doubt, a hope, or a secret design. Across both domains the verb implies sustained keeping — something is taken in and allowed to remain, not merely met or touched in passing. As a noun, harbor means a sheltered body of water where ships can anchor and take refuge, and by extension any place of refuge or safety.

  • Core verb sense — give refuge/shelter: To shelter a person, animal, or vessel: "harbor refugees," "harbor a fugitive," "harbor stray dogs." The object is received into a protective space and allowed to remain there.
  • Legal/political sense — criminal sheltering: Statutes criminalize harboring fugitives, criminals, or enemy agents; international and political rhetoric speaks of states harboring terrorists, militants, or dissidents. Bridge: sheltering becomes culpable when the object is illegal or hostile — the value-neutral verb takes on accusation from its object.
  • Psychological sense — retain thoughts/emotions: To keep deep-seated feelings: "harbor a grudge," "harbor doubts," "harbor hopes," "harbor ill will," "harbor a secret design." Bridge: the mind is treated as an interior that can take things in and keep them — the lodging metaphor transferred inward.
  • Biological/medical sense — host pathogens: An organism "harbors" a virus, parasite, or infection; "reservoir" hosts harbor pathogens without necessarily falling ill. Bridge: the body is a container that shelters an inner inhabitant.
  • Noun sense — sheltered waters: A harbor is a port, bay, or inlet where ships find safety from wind and sea. The verb is historically bound to this idea of giving safe lodgment; "safe harbor" is now also a legal and financial metaphor for protected status.
  • Figurative noun extensions: "safe harbor" in law and finance (protection from liability or regulation); "a harbor of peace"; "harbor of refuge." Bridge: the maritime shelter generalized into any refuge.
  • Register and evaluative tone: The verb is standard and neutral-to-suspicious: protective when the object is sympathetic (refugees, orphans), accusatory when the object is crime, disease, or malice. This ambivalence is the word's defining modern character. Antonyms and contrasts: expel, evict, banish, expose, abandon, dispel (thoughts), refuse asylum.
  • American/British spelling: American harbor, British harbour; identical pronunciation and sense, differing only in orthography.

Collocations are highly patterned: harbor a fugitive, harbor refugees, harbor doubts / resentment / ill will, harbor a virus, harbor terrorists, harbor a grudge, safe harbor. The verb unifies the maritime image of the port and the psychological image of the keeping mind: in both, an enclosed interior protects and sustains what it holds against a more dangerous outside.
