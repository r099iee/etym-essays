At the root of destitute stands one of the oldest and most productive words of the Indo-European inheritance: the Proto-Indo-European root *steh₂- (traditionally written *stā-), 'to stand'. The family that descends from it is everywhere: Sanskrit sthāti 'he stands', Avestan stā-, Greek histēmi 'I set up', Latin stāre 'to stand'. From Latin alone English has drawn state, status, station, constant, circumstance, substance, statue, statute, stable and institute. Our word comes from a specific Latin verb built on this root: statuō, 'to set up, place, establish', the causative of 'to stand' — one 'makes stand' what one sets up. To this verb Latin added the prefix dē-, 'down, away from', forming dēstituō: literally 'to set down, put down, put away'.

The semantic step is a small and very human one. To set a thing down and leave it is to abandon it; so dēstituere came to mean 'to forsake, desert, leave in the lurch', its past participle dēstitūtus meaning 'abandoned, forsaken, left without support'. In classical Latin the verb already does both jobs — destituere aliquem, 'to abandon someone'; destituere spem, 'to disappoint hope' — and the family is witnessed from Cicero and Caesar onward. The prefix dē- is the same one seen in desert (from Latin dēserere) and decline, and it is what turns 'standing' into 'standing away from', 'left standing outside' — precisely the image the card's mnemonic preserves. The shift from physical 'set down' to social 'abandon' is a shift from space to relationship: what is set down apart from the community is abandoned.

English received the word through the Romance channel of Latin learning. Old French and Anglo-French passed on destitut 'deprived, abandoned', and Middle English adopted it in the fifteenth century in the plain sense 'forsaken'. Its semantic life in English then unfolded in three recorded stages. First came the literal Latin sense, 'abandoned, deserted' — a destitute friend or widow — now obsolete. Second, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the word took on its complement construction, destitute of followed by an abstract noun, meaning 'entirely devoid of': destitute of taste, destitute of merit, destitute of the common feelings of humanity. It is precisely this sense that Gibbon employs in the sentence of this card, where 'laborious pedants, themselves destitute of taste' labour over Polycletus and Apelles without the one gift that would let them understand what they study — the vocabulary of statute, sermon and Augustan satire, a complete vacancy where refinement should stand.

Third, in the nineteenth century, as social questions pressed on the national conscience, destitute was applied overwhelmingly to persons utterly without means — the destitute poor, destitute families, destitution as a condition of law and philanthropy. The direction of the shift is worth dwelling on: the word began as a passive participle about being put down and left behind, then became an adjective of total absence, and finally a social category of extreme poverty. Sound change was minimal — the form survived nearly unchanged from Latin through French into English — so the whole story is semantic drift, the gradual migration of a physical image into moral and economic registers.

  • Sibling words from the same root. Institute (in- 'in' + statuere), constitute (con- 'together'), substitute (sub- 'under'), restitution (re- 'back'), prostitute (pro- 'before, for sale'), superstition (from superstes, 'standing over'), statue and statute. All are built on the same idea of standing or setting.
  • False-friend warning. Do not confuse destitute (dē- 'away' + statuere) with substitute (sub- 'under' + statuere): the prefixes give opposite directions, 'stood away' vs 'stood under'.
  • Literary attestations. Gibbon's Decline and Fall; the legal and philanthropic prose of the 19th century; Dickens and the Victorian novelists for the social sense; the Book of Common Prayer and the homiletic tradition for 'destitute of'.
  • Contemporary note. In modern use, sense 2 (material poverty) dominates the newspapers and the census; sense 1 survives as a formal, slightly literary way of saying 'utterly lacking'; sense 3 is extinct. The word retains its absoluteness: nothing in English is merely 'somewhat destitute' — the prefix and the participle between them guarantee total abandonment.
