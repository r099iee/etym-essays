- Earliest English sense - "shod" is the plain, workaday past participle of "to shoe": feet — or hooves — simply furnished with footwear; a practical, unremarkable word for being equipped for the road
- Medieval to Early Modern - the practical sense holds while the register rises: in scripture and chronicle the shod man is the prepared man, "shod with sandals" like a soldier or messenger girded for a mission, while the farrier's "shod" stays the everyday word for horses
- 17th–19th c. - the word recedes from ordinary speech as "wearing shoes" takes over, surviving in set phrases — well-shod, poorly shod (footwear as a mark of prosperity), roughshod, shod with iron — and gaining a faintly elevated, almost epic flavour
- Contemporary - "shod" is a deliberately archaic, literary participle: standard of horses and the shod traveller, and in prose with a high or fairy-tale tone it signals quiet readiness — feet made comfortable, concealed, and silent for the night's work
