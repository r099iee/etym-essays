- 1605–1615 - Created by Miguel de Cervantes as *Don Quixote de la Mancha*, the protagonist of his celebrated parodic novel; in early 17th-century Spanish, *quixote* (Old Spanish spelling) was also recognized as the literal military term for a piece of armor protecting the thigh (*cuisse*), chosen as an absurd, comic in-joke by the protagonist.
- 17th to 18th Century - Rapidly adopted into English and other European languages (initially pronounced in English as /ˈkwɪksət/), where *Quixote* nominalized into a common noun denoting an eccentric, extravagantly chivalrous person chasing foolish enterprises, giving rise to the adjectival form *quixotic* (attested c. 1718) and the noun *quixotism*.
- 19th Century (Romantic Transcendence) - The 19th-century Romantic movement redefined *Quixote* from an object of simple farcical ridicule into a sublime, tragic cultural archetype symbolizing the holy madness of pure faith and human spirit resisting the brutal, calculating utilitarianism of industrial society.
- 20th Century (Philosophical & Psychological Paradigm) - Adopted by philosophers and critics (Ortega y Gasset, Unamuno, Shklovsky, Foucault) as a vital philosophical paradigm representing the crisis of representation, the collision of heroic myth with modern banality, and the archetype of stubborn existential resistance.
- 21st Century - Retained across global culture both as a timeless literary reference and as a widespread common noun and adjective for individuals embarking on noble, hopeless crusades against entrenched systemic odds.
