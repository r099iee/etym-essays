Will is a cornerstone Germanic noun and modal auxiliary verb in the English language, expressing volition, conscious determination, legal testament, and future temporality. Its extensive polysemy spans philosophy, psychology, law, and grammatical syntax:

1. Volition, Mental Agency, and Determination:
The conscious mental power, faculty, or capacity by which a person decides upon, initiates, and directs their own deliberate actions and choices:

  • Philosophical Volition: Self-directed agency and purpose, central to centuries of discourse on moral responsibility and autonomy (e.g., free will versus determinism).
  • Psychological Resolve: Firm determination, perseverance, mental discipline, and self-regulation (e.g., iron will, willpower, the will to live, a battle of wills).
  • Desire, Mandate, or Pleasure: An individual's deliberate wish, command, intent, or decree (e.g., "against the will of his father", "not my will, but thine be done", "at will", "working one's will").

2. Legal Instrument and Testamentary Disposition:
A formal, legally executed and witnessed written declaration in which an individual (the testator) specifies how their real property, personal estate, financial assets, and affairs are to be managed and distributed after death:

  • Legal Formula: Commonly termed a "last will and testament", designating an executor to administer the estate and nominating guardians for minor dependents.
  • Transitive Verb Use: To bequeath, transfer, or demise property to another by testamentary instrument (e.g., "he willed his extensive library to the university").

3. Modal and Auxiliary Verb Functions:

  • Future Tense / Predictive: Expressing future occurrence, certainty, or expectation (e.g., "the ship will arrive tomorrow", "accidents will happen").
  • Volitional Intent and Promise: Expressing willingness, consent, promise, or determination (e.g., "I will help you", "we will not surrender").
  • Habitual / Characteristic Behavior: Expressing characteristic propensity or customary tendency (e.g., "boys will be boys", "wood will float on water").
  • Direct Mental Action: To try to cause something to happen through sheer concentration of mind (e.g., "she willed herself not to cry").

Historical Semantic Bridges:
The bridge uniting these diverse senses is the ancestral Indo-European concept of desiring, choosing, and intending (*welh₁-). Over centuries of Germanic and English development, the expression of subjective desire naturally evolved into active mental resolution, formal legal intent regarding one's posthumous legacy, and grammaticalized future expectation.
