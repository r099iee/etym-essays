English maxim descends from the Latin superlative maximus 'greatest', itself formed on magnus 'great', and ultimately from the widespread PIE root *méǵh₂- 'great, large'. That same root is among the most productive in the family, yielding Greek megas (the source of English mega-), Sanskrit mahā- 'great' (as in maharaja), and the Germanic words behind English much and archaic mickle. In classical Latin, maximus is an ordinary adjective of degree and rank — 'greatest', 'oldest', 'most important' — as in Jupiter Optimus Maximus, 'Jupiter the Best and Greatest'. The superlative itself is irregular: the comparative maior and superlative maximus use a stem built on the root of magnus with a suffix -is- / -im-, one of Latin's suppletive degree systems.

THE SCHOLASTIC BRIDGE: FROM ADJECTIVE TO NOUN

  • In late Antiquity and the Middle Ages, Latin-speaking logicians spoke of the maxima propositio — the 'greatest proposition', the most general premise on which an argument could rest.
  • Shortened to maxima (sc. sententia or propositio), the word became a noun naming a leading principle or a fundamental axiom of reasoning. Scholastic Latin of the 12th–13th centuries used maxima technically in the theory of argument and demonstration.
  • This is the crucial semantic step: a word meaning 'greatest (thing)' becomes the name of a 'greatest, most general truth'.

THROUGH FRENCH INTO ENGLISH

  • Old and Middle French developed maxime as a noun for a basic principle or axiomatic saying, extending the scholastic usage into moral and practical guidance.
  • English borrowed the word in the late medieval / early modern period as maxim; the first attestations (15th c.) show it meaning 'a fundamental principle', already sliding toward 'a concise general rule or saying'.
  • The great moralists of the 17th century — above all La Rochefoucauld's Maximes (1665) — cemented the modern association of the word with a terse, memorable, often worldly observation about conduct.
  • Phonologically the word shows the ordinary route of French borrowings into English: Middle French maxime (with stress on the first syllable and a final -e later lost) gives modern English maxim, with the initial /m/, lax a, and final /-ɪm/.

THE SEMANTIC ARC

  • greatest (adjective) → greatest proposition / leading principle (scholastic noun) → concise general rule or saying (vernacular).
  • A parallel, more quantitative branch of the same Latin superlative gave English maximum, so that maxim and maximum are etymological siblings that specialized differently — one toward proverbial wisdom, the other toward measurable extremes.
  • In modern English the word keeps two registers: the technical (legal maxim, logical maxim) and the moral / literary (maxims of prudence). The legal sense is ancient and settled; the moral sense dominates everyday use.

LITERARY AND SOCIAL LIFE

  • The word's prestige history still colours it: a maxim claims the status of something 'highest' or most important, not a casual remark. People who quote maxims present themselves as keepers of distilled wisdom.
  • Maxims have been a genre of moral writing — from ancient sententiae and the proverbs of the Bible to La Rochefoucauld, Franklin's 'Poor Richard', and modern self-help — always compressing ethics into quotable form.
  • In narrative, quoting a maxim is a rhetorical move: it borrows impersonal authority and closes debate. That is precisely how Li Shou-zhong uses 'a stupid woman is a virtuous one' — as a 'good old maxim' that ends all discussion.

CONCLUSION

In the story, founding a household policy on a 'good old maxim' is rhetorically powerful and morally double-edged. The word itself promises the 'greatest' truth, yet the narrative exposes how a high-sounding fragment of inherited wisdom can freeze inequality into common sense. The etymology — from 'greatest' to 'greatest truth' to 'pithy rule' — is the whole story of how a Latin superlative became a license to stop thinking.
