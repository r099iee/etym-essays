In Gibbon's imperial vocabulary, provinces are not quaint regions but the great administrative bodies of Roman rule outside Italy — territories entered by conquest or diplomacy and thereafter governed under Roman law and manners. When he writes that the "influence of laws and manners had gradually cemented the union of the provinces," the plural names a political achievement: diverse lands bound into one structure by shared legal habit and cultural practice, not by geography alone. The word carries the weight of empire — extraction and order, local variety and central authority — and the metaphor of cementing treats provincial union as masonry: slow, artificial, and strong when successful.

Core meaning: a principal administrative division of a country or empire, originally the sphere of duty (and then the governed territory) assigned to a Roman magistrate. The word derives from Latin prōvincia, which began as an abstract of office — the "charge" a consul or praetor received — and only secondarily named the territory that formed that charge.

Senses and uses:
  • Roman imperial province — an administered territory outside Italy, ruled by a governor (proconsul, propraetor, or imperial legate) under Roman law; the plural provinces was the standard name for the empire's limbs.
  • Senatorial vs. imperial provinces — under the Principate, provinces were split between those governed by the Senate's proconsuls (older, settled ones) and those held directly by the emperor through legates (border and military provinces).
  • Administrative division of a modern state — the standard term for the principal subdivisions of certain countries, above all Canada (its provinces), but also used historically for divisions of France, Spain, China, and various empires.
  • Ecclesiastical province — in church organization, the territory under the jurisdiction of an archbishop or metropolitan, a sense inherited from the early Church's adoption of Roman administrative geography.
  • "The provinces" (with a pejorative tint) — the regions of a country outside its capital, viewed from the capital as backward, narrow, or unsophisticated; the source of the adjective provincial in the sense "narrowly local, uncultured."
  • Province of (figurative, singular) — a person's proper sphere of activity, authority, or competence: "that question falls outside my province"; "the provinces of art and science."
  • Historical provinces of empires — used for the administrative territories of later empires (Ottoman, British, Mughal) wherever Roman-style provincial administration was borrowed or paralleled.
Polysemy and semantic bridges:
  • The bridge from "office" to "territory" is the key historical move: a magistrate's prōvincia was first a charge (typically a military command), and the land in which he discharged it took the name of the charge. Thus "province" conflates assignment and place — the same fusion visible in words like "office" and "diocese."
  • The pejorative "provincial" sense grows out of the imperial polarity between Rome (the center) and everything outside it: what is of the provinces is by definition not of the capital, hence rustic, narrow, and behind the times. This meaning attaches to the adjective, not to the institutional noun, and is absent from Gibbon's neutral-to-positive plural.
  • The ecclesiastical sense is a direct institutional inheritance: the Church re-used Roman provincial geography as its own administrative map, and archbishops' territories are still called provinces today.
Boundary contrasts:
  • Regions and territories are broader and lack the Roman administrative specificity.
  • Colonies imply settlement by the metropolis's people; many provinces were governed peoples rather than settler colonies.
  • Dependencies stress subordination but miss the technical sense of a prōvincia as a magistrate's charge.
  • States and departments import modern constitutional frames; dominions work for later empires but blur the classical term Gibbon inherits.
Collocations: Roman provinces; senatorial and imperial provinces; provincial governor / proconsul / legate; the union of the provinces; provincial elite; provincial capital. Register in Gibbon is high historiography; in modern politics "province" remains standard, especially for Canadian subdivisions, while "provincial" carries its double life — neutrally "of a province," pejoratively "unsophisticated."
