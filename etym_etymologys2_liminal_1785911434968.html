Chain for the single root word LIMINAL (the derivation runs from Latin to the modern English coinage; the ultimate PIE stage is not recoverable and is stated explicitly as unknown): - Latin līmen, genitive līminis (neuter) "threshold, doorway, entrance; lintel", attested from Plautus (early 2nd century BCE) and common in classical prose and verse. Etymology uncertain: no securely reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root is agreed for līmen. A connection with Latin līmus "oblique, transverse" (i.e. the transverse beam set across a doorway) has been proposed, but the link is debated and modern authorities (e.g. de Vaan) record no convincing etymology. The popular claim of a PIE root *lei- "to bend, lean" behind līmen is unproven and should be treated as speculation. State: the earliest recoverable stage is the Latin noun itself. - Latin līmināris (adjective) "of, or belonging to, the threshold", formed from the stem līmin- plus the suffix -āris; a late, productive adjectival formation, the natural base for the modern English coinage. - English liminal (adjective) "of, pertaining to, or situated at a threshold; in psychophysics, at the threshold of sensation", coined in 1884 in English psychological writing, on the Latin stem līmin- plus the English suffix -al. Note the nuance: the modern word is an English (scientific) coinage on the classical noun, not a word that descended through an unbroken Latin → French → English channel; it was created to translate the psychophysical concept of the sensory threshold (German Schwelle, "threshold"). - LIMINAL Note on the debate: because līmen itself has no agreed Indo-European etymology, every attempt to give the word a PIE root is conjecture. The chain therefore begins at the earliest attested form, Latin līmen (2nd century BCE), and any claim of a deeper stage should be regarded with caution.
