Proto-Indo-European: *ghrebh- "to scrape, dig, carve, gouge" (reconstructed; attested indirectly through derivatives; c. 4500–3500 BCE) → Proto-Germanic: *grobaz "coarse, heavy, thick, rough-grained" (adjective; reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Middle Dutch: grof "coarse, rough, thick" (attested, Middle Ages; also Middle Low German grof) → Early Modern English: gruff "coarse, rough" (adjective; borrowed from Low German/Dutch, first attested 1530s) → Modern English: gruff + -ly (adverbial suffix from Old English -līce "in the manner of") → gruffly "in a rough, low-pitched, surly manner" ◄ TARGET Debated / nuanced stages: - The PIE root *ghrebh- "to scrape/carve" is the root most authorities cite for Proto-Germanic *grobaz, but the semantic bridge (scraping → rough surface) is an inference, not an attested form; some scholars instead set up a separate expressive root *grob- for "coarse." The Germanic form itself, however, is secure and attested through descendants. - The precise donor dialect is debated: both Middle Dutch grof and Middle Low German grof (Hanseatic trade language) are plausible sources for the 1530s English borrowing. - The -ly suffix is a native English adverbial ending (Old English -līce), so gruffly is an English-internal formation on the borrowed adjective gruff, not itself a loanword.
