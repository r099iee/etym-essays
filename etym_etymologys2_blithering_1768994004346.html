blithering - PIE root *bhle- "to blow" (reconstructed; debated) — the ancient equation of speech with breath and wind; the connection to the babbling words is plausible but partly expressive/imitative, and must be stated as a hypothesis rather than a certainty. - Old Norse blaðra "to babble, to mutter" (attested, medieval) — an expressive formation of the Germanic north, standing beside Old English blǣdrian "to blather." (Old English blǣdre "a wind-filled bag," the bladder, belongs to the same root-world: talk as wind in a bag.) - Old English blǣdrian "to blather" (attested, Old English period) — the native English member of the pair. - Middle English blathren "to talk foolishly" (attested, 12th–15th c.) — the medieval verb from which blather, blether, and blither descend. - English blather / blether "to talk foolishly" (attested from Middle English onward) — the two surviving dialectal forms. - English blither (18th c.) — a variant of blather, hardened into a distinct form. - English blithering (present participle, attested; then intensive adjective from the late 19th–early 20th c.) — at first "babbling," then "utter, complete, hopeless," in the collocation "blithering idiot." - English blithering (the form required by the passage: "a blithering sandstorm"). Note on debate/nuance: the whole family is expressive — words for babbling are coined on the sound of babbling — so the PIE *bhle- "to blow" equation is a reasonable but not a strict descent; the forms may be independently imitative. Sanskrit bhaṣate "he speaks" is sometimes adduced as a cognate, but its origin is uncertain and it is best set aside. The English word bleat (imitative, of sheep) belongs to the same expressive world of animal cries but is not a direct ancestor.
