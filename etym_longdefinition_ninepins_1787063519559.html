Ninepins (historically known also as skittles or kegel) is a traditional bowling sport played on an indoor wooden lane, alley, or outdoor bowling green, as well as the plural noun designating the set of nine wooden upright pins arranged in a diamond-shaped formation (3 × 3 grid turned on its point). The objective of the game is for players to roll a wooden or hard rubber ball (historically without finger holes) down the alley to knock down as many of the nine standing pins as possible.

The rich polysemous, historical, cultural, and idiomatic facets of ninepins encompass:

  • 1. The Traditional Diamond-Formation Bowling Game:
    • Historic European Ninepin Bowling: The direct ancestor of modern ten-pin bowling, ninepins has been played across Germany, the Low Countries, England, and Scandinavia since the Middle Ages. In traditional continental Kegeln, the game is played on narrow wooden or asphalt tracks.
    • The American Ban and the Birth of Ten-Pin Bowling: In 19th-century America (especially New York and New England), ninepins was so heavily associated with tavern gambling and public disorder that several state legislatures outlawed it. To circumvent the ban, alley owners famously added a tenth pin and rearranged the diamond formation into a triangle—inventing modern ten-pin bowling.
  • 2. The Physical Wooden Pins (The Nine Pins):
    • The Kingpin and Corner Pins: The set of nine carved wooden pins, with the central and tallest pin traditionally designated as the kingpin (which frequently awarded bonus points if knocked down while leaving the other eight standing).
  • 3. Idiomatic and Metaphorical Expressions:
    • "Falling Like Ninepins": A prominent English idiom meaning to collapse, fall down, be defeated, or succumb rapidly in rapid succession, as under heavy enemy fire, epidemic disease, political scandal, or exhaustion (e.g., "Under the scorching sun, the marathon runners began dropping like ninepins"). The semantic bridge directly equates the sudden, cascading tumble of wooden bowling pins with human collapse.
  • 4. Folklore and Literary Legend:
    • The Rip Van Winkle Myth: Immortalized in Washington Irving's 1819 classic American story Rip Van Winkle, in which the ghostly crew of Henry Hudson plays ninepins in the Catskill Mountains, with the rolling of their heavy balls creating the sound of pealing thunder across the peaks.

In literary styling (such as Tolkien's depiction of the recreational games of the Shire), ninepins evokes an idyllic, timeless English pastoral culture of village pubs, friendly wagers, and gentle athletic skill.
