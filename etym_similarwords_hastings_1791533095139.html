• Battle: The town actually built on the battlefield, named after the event; similar as the true site of 1066, but it is a different settlement, founded around the abbey, and its name is a common noun turned proper.
  • Senlac: The medieval name of the ridge where the battle was fought; similar as the battlefield, but it is a descriptive Old English name (“sand-stream”) that never became the standard label for the battle.
  • Pevensey: The landing place of William’s fleet; similar as a 1066 site on the Sussex coast, but it is a village with a Roman fort and a Norman castle, not the battle.
  • Bexhill: The neighbouring seaside town; similar as a Sussex coastal resort, but it has no battle and a much smaller historical footprint.
  • Rye: A medieval Cinque Port near Hastings; similar as a Sussex port with medieval walls, but it lies on the Rother and is not the site of 1066.
  • Winchelsea: The other ancient Cinque Port town in the region; similar as a planned medieval port, but it was rebuilt inland after the sea destroyed the old town and is not associated with the conquest.
  • Hæstingas: The Old English name of the folk-group; similar as the etymological ancestor, but it denotes people, not a place, and its first element is a personal name of uncertain origin.
  • Hastinges: The Middle English and Anglo-Norman spelling; similar in reference, differing only in orthography and in the records in which it appears.
  • Hastings, New Zealand: The largest namesake; similar in name and in colonial British naming practice, but it is a city in Hawke’s Bay named after Warren Hastings, not after the Sussex town.
  • Hastings Cutoff: The emigrant trail; similar only because it is named after Lansford Hastings, but it is a route in the American West, not a place in England.
  • Warren Hastings: The eighteenth-century statesman; similar as the most famous bearer of the surname, but he is a person, not a place, and his name reached New Zealand before it reached Nebraska.
