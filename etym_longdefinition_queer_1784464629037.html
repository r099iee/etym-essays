Queer in the world of The Hobbit means strange, odd, unusual, or peculiar — off the expected center of behavior or appearance — and it carries none of the specialized identity sense that dominates some modern registers. In the dwarves' character sketch, "Gets funny queer fits, but he is one of the best, one of the best — as fierce as a dragon in a pinch." The adjective softens and marks intermittent oddity: hobbit nervousness and Tookish spells read as "queer fits" from a dwarf's practical point of view, yet loyalty and courage remain undoubted. The tone is colloquial early-twentieth-century English, mildly affectionate and slightly dismissive of eccentricity. In Tolkien's period diction this is the ordinary "odd" sense — the adjective points to something off the expected axis, momentarily askew. Primary senses in historical order.
  • Strange, odd, peculiar, eccentric: the oldest and central English sense (from c. 1500) — "a queer feeling," "a queer smell," "queer fits" — things off the expected center.
  • Unwell, faint, uneasy: the physical sense — "I feel queer" — a sudden oddness of the body, close to dizziness or nausea.
  • Doubtful, questionable, suspect: the suspicious sense — "queer business," "queer goings-on" — odd in a way that invites distrust.
  • Counterfeit, false (historical cant): "queer money" meant forged or counterfeit notes; "to queer" came to mean to spoil or put wrong — "queer the pitch."
  • Homosexual (modern slang, now often reclaimed): a twentieth-century sense that developed from the odd/suspicious branch, became a slur, and has since been reclaimed as an umbrella identity term — "queer theory," "queer community."
Semantic bridges. The semantic history is a chain of deviations from the straight or expected. The Germanic core is the idea of the transverse, the diagonal, the crosswise — that which is not straight-on is "queer." From "crosswise" the word generalized to "odd, strange," then narrowed to "suspicious, questionable," then to "counterfeit" (something not genuine), and, in the twentieth century, to a term for those whose identity or behavior was socially coded as off-center. The modern identity sense must not be read back into Tolkien's dialogue: in 1930s narrative English, "queer fits" is transparent — strange turns, funny spells. Usage note. On this card queer modifies fits (intermittent odd episodes), not the whole person: Bilbo is "one of the best" despite being occasionally queer. Preserve the period sense — strange, peculiar — exactly as the dialogue uses it. The context in full. In the company's conversation about their prospective burglar, "Gets funny queer fits, but he is one of the best, one of the best — as fierce as a dragon in a pinch," the speaker balances two judgments in one breath. The clause with queer concedes the oddity: Bilbo suffers strange spells, funny turns, moments when he seems off the expected axis — nervousness, Tookish wanderlust, unexplained fancies. The repetition "one of the best, one of the best" then overrides the concession, insisting on his worth, and the fierce comparison closes the argument. The word fits is crucial: it makes the queer quality intermittent, something that comes over him in episodes, not a permanent defect. This is the standard colloquial use of early-twentieth-century British English, where "a queer turn" or "queer fits" meant passing peculiar spells and carried a mild, affectionate tolerance of eccentricity rather than moral judgment. Register and pitfalls. Because queer has acquired powerful modern identity senses, modern readers can misread this line; the correction is to hold the period register. In Tolkien's diction, queer is an everyday word for "odd," a synonym of peculiar and strange with a slightly comic or wary tint — nothing more. The word is also freely used of objects, feelings, and situations ("a queer smell," "a queer feeling that she was being watched"), and of bodily unease ("I feel queer"). All of these are offshoots of the same core: deviation from the straight, the normal, or the expected.
