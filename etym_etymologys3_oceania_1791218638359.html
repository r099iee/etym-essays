Oceania is a young learned coinage, barely two centuries old, built from a very old word for sea. Its evolution therefore has two halves: the three-thousand-year career of ocean from Homeric cosmology to modern geography, and the brief Napoleonic-era invention of Océanie / Oceania as a fifth part of the world alongside Europe, Asia, Africa and America.

Ocean's deep past: Homeric Greek Ōkeanos was both a Titan god and the fresh-water river supposed to encircle the earth. Classical geographers — Herodotus, Eratosthenes, Ptolemy — turned myth into oceanography: Oceanus in Latin became the outer sea surrounding the inhabited oikoumene. Etymologically Ōkeanos is opaque; despite many attempts no convincing PIE root has been established, and Beekes and others treat it as Pre-Greek substrate, like many Aegean sea-words. Latin borrowed it as Oceanus, Old French as ocean, Middle English as ocean by the late 1300s. By the Age of Sail ocean in English meant any of the great sea basins — Atlantic Ocean, Pacific Ocean, Indian Ocean — and metaphorically anything vast: ocean of troubles.

  • Need for a fifth continent (mid-1700s–early 1800s): Cook's voyages (1768–1779), Bougainville, La Pérouse and Flinders revealed thousands of Pacific islands plus the continent of New Holland that did not fit the four-part scheme. Geographers cycled through names: South Sea Islands, Australasia (de Brosses 1756), Polynesia, Notasia, Ulimaroa.
  • Malte-Brun's coinage (1804–1812): Danish-French geographer Conrad Malte-Brun, in his Précis de la géographie universelle, proposed Océanie as a collective for all lands bathed by the Pacific — Australia, New Zealand, New Guinea, the Malay Archipelago in his widest definition, and the small atolls. The suffix -ie / -ia followed Australie, Polynésie, Mélanésie, Micronésie.
  • English adoption (1810s–1840s): British reviews and atlases at first wrote Oceania, Oceanica, Oceanic region interchangeably. By the 1840s Oceania stabilised in English atlases, missionary reports and emigration handbooks. Early pronunciation followed Latinate /ˌəʊʃiˈeɪniə/ before settling to modern /ˌəʊsiˈɑːniə/.
  • Rival Australasia: throughout the 19th century Australasia competed as Australia + New Zealand + neighbours, while Oceania was wider, including Polynesia, Melanesia and Micronesia. The source sentence — natives of Oceania made real houses of carefully cut boards — uses the wide ethnological sense typical of early-20th-century evolutionist anthropology.

Semantic drift and institutionalisation: in the 20th century Oceania acquired sporting, political and statistical definitions. The United Nations geoscheme defines Oceania as Australia, New Zealand, Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia. Rugby, football and Olympic bodies have an Oceania confederation. Anthropologists subdivide it into Near and Remote Oceania by Lapita pottery and settlement chronology. Orwell in Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949) famously reused the name for his superstate Oceania — Airstrip One, London — giving the word a dystopian second life wholly unrelated to the Pacific, which still colours literary associations.

Phonetics and usage today: RP /ˌəʊ.siˈɑː.ni.ə/, General American /ˌoʊ.ʃiˈɑː.ni.ə, ˌoʊ.siˈæni.ə/. Always capitalised as a proper region name; adjective is Oceanian or Oceanic, the latter also naming the Austronesian subgroup. To say Oceanic houses of cut boards, as the source does, is to invoke a classic culture-area generalisation now refined by archaeology into distinct Lapita, Polynesian and Micronesian traditions.
