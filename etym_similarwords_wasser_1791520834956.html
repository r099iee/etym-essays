• Water: the exact English cognate, from the same Proto-Germanic source; similar because it denotes the same substance and shares the same idioms and compounds; differs because English uses water as a verb (“to water the garden”) and in the plural for territorial seas, while German normally uses bewässern for the verb and Gewässer for bodies of water.
  • Gewässer: the collective and legal term for a body of water, such as a river, lake or canal; similar because it denotes inland waters and is often translated simply as “water”; differs because it is a count noun for delineated bodies and belongs to geography, law and administration, not to the substance itself.
  • Flüssigkeit: liquid in general; similar because water is the prototypical liquid and the word is used in scientific definitions of Wasser; differs because it covers any liquid, from mercury to oil, and has no element of life, landscape or ritual.
  • H₂O: the chemical formula for water; similar because it denotes exactly the same compound and is used where precision is required; differs because it is a formula, not a word, and it excludes the everyday, literary and idiomatic senses entirely.
  • Mineralwasser: bottled mineral water; similar because it is a kind of Wasser sold for drinking; differs because it specifies dissolved minerals and commercial bottling, and in restaurants it is the ordinary way to ask for still or sparkling water.
  • Trinkwasser: drinking water; similar because it names water fit for human consumption; differs because it is a regulatory and public-health category, defined by purity standards rather than by source or taste.
  • Abwasser: wastewater, sewage; similar because it is water after use and belongs to the same hydraulic vocabulary; differs because it is defined by contamination and disposal, and it carries a negative, sanitary charge that plain Wasser does not.
  • Grundwasser: groundwater; similar because it is water held beneath the earth's surface; differs because it is a hydrogeological term for the saturated zone, not water visible in a glass or a river.
  • Hochwasser: flood, high water; similar because it describes an excess of water and can be rendered literally as “high water”; differs because it denotes a dangerous event or state, and the compound is fixed in insurance, hydrology and disaster reporting.
  • Nass: wetness, the wet; similar because it names the quality associated with water and is often used where English says “water” (im Nassen stehen); differs because it is an adjective or a mass noun for the condition of being wet, not the substance itself.
  • See: lake; similar because a lake is a body of water and the word often appears alongside Wasser in landscape description; differs because it denotes a specific geographical feature, and the masculine See (lake) must be distinguished from the feminine See (sea).
  • Aqua: the Latin word for water, used in German in technical and cosmetic names (Aqua destillata); similar because it is the international scientific designation for water; differs because it is a loanword confined to pharmacy, chemistry and ingredient lists, with no vernacular or emotional life.
