The history of shrouds is the history of one cut of cloth pulled in two directions — toward the grave and toward the masthead. Philologists trace both to the ancient cutting-root PIE *(s)ker-, to cut or shear, which runs through a great family of severed and shaped things: Latin cortex, bark stripped from a tree, scalpels that cut, shards broken off, and in Germanic the cut pieces of fabric themselves. From this root Proto-Germanic shaped a noun for cut cloth, variously reconstructed as *skraud- or *skrud-, a garment precisely because garments begin as lengths cut from the loom. Old English scrūd still meant any garment or dress without funereal colour: charters and homilies dress bishops and beggars alike in scrūd, and the word sat beside clāþ and hrægl as an everyday clothing-word.

From everyday dress to the grave and the mast:

  • Old English — the general garment: scrūd clothes the living in wills, laws, and saints' lives. Its plural could mean clothes in general, and no burial association dominates; the dead are wrapped, but so is everyone else against the cold.
  • Old Norse — gear and tackle: the cognate skruð drifts from clothing to equipment: a ship's furniture, rigging, and ornamental gear. This is the sense Viking seamanship needed, for a mast must be dressed as surely as a man, and it is the direct ancestor of the nautical shroud.
  • Middle English — narrowing to the burial sheet: as French loans (garment, robe, attire) seize fashionable dress, native shroud retreats to the one garment French cannot decently rename: the winding-sheet. By the fifteenth century shroud alone increasingly means grave-clothes, and the adjectives gather round it — winding, dead, burial — like mourners.
  • 15th–17th c. — the ropes take the name: English seamanship, saturated with Norse-derived rigging terms, fixes shrouds as the standing lateral ropes from masthead to channels. Naval lists count them carefully — fore-shrouds, main-shrouds, mizzen-shrouds, topmast-shrouds — and deadeyes and lanyards tension them. Landsmen meeting the word hear mourning; sailors hear only rope, tar, and strain.
  • Sound shifts: the initial cluster simplifies from Old English scr- (pronounced skr-) through Norman-influenced palatalisation to modern shr-, exactly as scrēawa became shrew and scrēadian shed its cluster toward shred; the long vowel rounds and shortens from scrūd to shroud with its modern diphthong. Spelling wobbles through schroud and shrowd before settling in the seventeenth century.
  • Modern extensions: the verb to shroud, to wrap or conceal, grows from the cloth noun and carries it into meteorology and espionage alike; engineers adopt shroud for fitted casings that clothe machines; aeronautics borrows shroud lines straight from the rigging-loft. Each new trade rediscovers the same metaphor: a working thing needs its clothes.

Tolkien's stray shrouds of mist show the cloth sense at its most lyrical and least mournful. The hill is not buried but half unveiled, its windows burning through torn weather-linen — the grave-word turned to atmosphere, with the masthead ropes standing just out of sight behind the spelling, reminding the philologist-reader that one word can dress a corpse, a mast, and a hillside at once.
