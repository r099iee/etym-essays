KINDRED — the biography of the kin-condition.
The word is a compound, and its two halves tell two stories. The first half is kin, from Old English cynn "family, race, kind," descending from Proto-Germanic *kunją and ultimately from the PIE root *ǵenh₁- "to give birth, to beget." That root is one of the great fertility roots of Indo-European: it is the source of Latin genus and gens (hence English genus, gender, general, generate, generation, generous, genius, genuine, indigenous, congenital), of Greek génos and geneá (hence gene, genesis, genealogy, genetics, genotype), and of Sanskrit jánas "race, people" and jāti "birth, caste" — which live on in modern Hindi जाति and Odia ଜାତି. The same Germanic line that produced cynn also produced cyning "king," literally "a son of the royal kin" (cynn + the patronymic -ing), and cynd "nature, kind," the ancestor of the English word kind itself, so that "what kind of thing" is etymologically "of what birth" — a remarkable demonstration of how a root meaning "to beget" came to mean "sort, nature, quality."

The second half is the suffix -rǣden, which in Old English formed nouns of condition or state: cynrǣden "the condition of being kin," frēondrǣden "friendship," þēowrǣden "servitude," hyldrǣden "allegiance." The suffix descends from a noun built on the same base as rǣde "ready, ordered," which goes back through Proto-Germanic *raidaz to the PIE root *reydʰ- "to ride, to move, to set in order." The root's other English descendants — ride, road (originally "a riding, a journey"), ready (from the "ordered, prepared" sense) — show how travel and arrangement grew from one source; the same "ordering" sense appears in German bereit "ready" and bereiten "to prepare." A neighbouring but distinct root, *h₂reh₁- "to think, reason," produced rǣd "counsel," German Rat, and English read — homophonous with the rǣden material but genetically unrelated, a classic trap for the unwary, and a reminder that the -red of kindred is not the colour and not "counsel" but "condition."

By Middle English kynrede had fused into a single word for "kinship," the condition of being kin, and then — the decisive semantic step — the abstract condition became the people who share it: "my kindred" meant "those joined to me by the condition of kin," and by extension the whole body of one's family and relations. The same development produced the adjective: those of one kin are of one kind, so "kindred" came to mean "alike in nature or character," and the modern idiom "a kindred spirit" (solidly established by the nineteenth century, beloved of the Romantics and Victorians for souls that recognise each other) is the direct descendant of that step. The word's warmth and solemnity made it the natural choice for fantasy, fairy-tale, and religious prose, where peoples are bound by blood and origin, and for legal and anthropological language, where a "kindred" is the bilateral network of kin traced through both parents.

In this passage the whole history is live. The half-Dwarf Doctor Cornelius claims kinship with "the true Dwarfs" — my kindred — in the very breath in which he admits they would despise him and call him a traitor. The word's Old English roots make the claim one of birth (kin, from *ǵenh₁- "to beget") and of condition (kindred, the state of being kin), and its warmth makes the anticipated contempt cut deeper: these are his people by blood and origin, yet he cannot be claimed by them. "Kindred" gathers a whole race into a single intimate noun, and the tragedy is precisely that the condition and the people have come apart.

  • The kin element: PIE *ǵenh₁- "to beget" → Proto-Germanic *kunją → Old English cynn → kin; the same root gives Latin genus, Greek génos, Sanskrit jāti (Hindi जाति), English king and kind.
  • The -red element: PIE *reydʰ- "to set in order" → *raidaz → Old English rǣden "condition, state" → cynrǣden → kynrede → kindred; the same family gives ready, road, ride.
  • The false friend: rǣd "counsel" from PIE *h₂reh₁- "to think" (English read, rede) is unrelated to the -rǣden suffix.
  • The semantic step: from the abstract "condition of being kin" to the people who share it — one's kindred.
  • The adjective: "alike in nature," as in "a kindred spirit" (19th-c. idiom).
  • The passage: the half-Dwarf's "my kindred, the true Dwarfs" — blood, origin, and the tragedy of mixed blood.
