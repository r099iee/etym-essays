Derivation chain for MANIFEST (root word one of two): Stage 1 — PIE *man- "hand" (reconstructed; the plain Indo-European root of the hand, attested by its reflexes in Latin manus and Sanskrit mánus) — dating is limited to the reconstructed proto-language, conventionally placed in the 4th–3rd millennium BCE. Stage 2 — Latin manus "hand" (attested from the 3rd century BCE onward in Old Latin inscriptions and in Plautus). Stage 3 — Latin manifestus (also manufestus) "palpable, evident, clear, caught in the act" (attested from the 2nd–1st century BCE, e.g. in Plautus and Cicero), formed from manus "hand" + -festus, a second element of debated origin traditionally connected with a verb meaning "to strike, to grasp," the whole meaning "seized by the hand." Stage 4 — Old French manifeste "evident, clear, open" (12th century), a learned loan from Latin manifestus. Stage 5 — Middle English manifest "clearly visible, evident" (14th century), borrowed from Old French. Stage 6 — Modern English manifest (adjective: clearly visible or obvious; also noun and verb) — TARGET. Note on the debate: the -festus element of Latin manifestus is of uncertain origin. It has been connected with prehendere "to grasp" (giving "grasped in the hand") or with roots of striking, but de Vaan and other authorities regard its precise source as unknown. Germanic has no cognate; manifest is a purely Italic/Romance inheritance. Derivation chain for VATS (root word two of two): Stage 1 — Proto-Germanic *fatą "vessel, container" (reconstructed on the basis of its Germanic reflexes; the ultimate origin is unknown, with no secure PIE root established). Stage 2 — Old English fæt "vessel, container, jar, cup" (attested from the 8th–9th century; the word occurs in Beowulf). Stage 3 — Middle English fat / vat "a large container, a cask, a tub" (12th–15th century); the spelling with initial v- reflects a southern English dialectal pronunciation in which f- became v-. Stage 4 — Modern English vat "a large open-topped container for liquids," plural vats — TARGET. Note on the debate: the ultimate origin of Germanic *fatą is unknown. Some scholars have speculated about a pre-Indo-European substrate or a connection with the root of "fathom" or "to fit," but no established PIE etymology exists. Cognates include Old Norse fat, Middle Dutch vat, and Old High German faz, all meaning "vessel" (a container).
