- 12th–13th c. - mend is clipped from amend (Old French amender, Latin emendare, 'to free from fault'); in Middle English it already covers correcting an error, curing the sick, and putting damaged things to rights.
- 14th–15th c. - the moral sense glows: to 'mend one's ways' is to amend conduct, and the word moves freely between pulpit, lawbook, and workshop.
- 16th–17th c. - mend establishes itself as the homely everyday repair word for clothes, tools, roads, and health - modest beside the formal repair - while the intransitive 'to mend', of a patient, comes to mean recovering, climbing back to usefulness.
- 18th–19th c. - mending becomes the quiet domestic labour of needle and thread - darned sleeve, patched pot; proverbs like 'never too late to mend' turn the word into folk-wisdom.
- 20th–21st c. - casual speech hands the work to fix, leaving mend a deliberately modest, tender word; the participle mended describes a thing or a body patched to working order - whole enough to be discharged from care, no heroics attached.
