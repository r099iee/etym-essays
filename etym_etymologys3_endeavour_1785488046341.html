Evolution essay for ENDEAVOUR The root: to seize, to have, to owe. Endeavour does not descend from a word for effort but from a word for debt. Its chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *gʰeh₁bʰ-, "to grab, take, receive" — a root of the hand, of grasping and seizing. In Latin this root produced the verb habēre, "to have, to hold": to have is, etymologically, to keep grasped in the hand. From this most ordinary of verbs, Latin built one of its most interesting compounds. By prefixing the preposition dē, "away from, down from," the language formed dēbēre, literally "to have away from oneself" — to hold something only as something that must be given back, hence "to owe." The conceptual leap is elegant and deeply Roman: possession conceived as obligation. What you have that is not really yours, you owe; what you owe, you are bound to give. Out of dēbēre grew the whole Romance family of verbs of owing and obligation — Italian dovere, Spanish deber "to owe, must, should," and French devoir — and with them a whole moral vocabulary in which duty, devoir, is literally "that which is owed." The medieval French devoir the noun meant "duty"; the verb devoir meant "to owe, to be obliged to, to must." The Strasbourg Oaths of 842, among the earliest French texts, already use the descendant forms; the word runs unbroken through Old French literature as one of the great moral nouns of the language. Into English. The word reached English through the channel of the Norman Conquest. After 1066 the French of the Anglo-Norman court and nobility poured into English, and with it came devoir in its concrete phrases. The decisive expression was se mettre en devoir, "to put oneself to one's duty" — the act of setting oneself earnestly to a task one is bound to perform. Middle English borrowed the phrase whole: endeven, endeveren, attested in the late fourteenth century, meaning "to make it one's duty, to strive." The modern spelling endeavour was regularised by the fifteenth century. The semantics shifted by stages: "to owe" became "to have as a duty," which became "to set oneself to one's duty," which became "to strive earnestly," and finally, as a noun, "a sustained serious attempt." The idea of obligation never quite vanished: an endeavour is still, at its root, an attempt one owes — to oneself, to one's standards, to one's task — which is why the word carries a gravity that "try" and "attempt" lack. Spelling: the -our and the -or. The word's spelling is a small history of English in itself. The British spelling endeavour keeps the -our inherited from Old French devoir, in the same family as savour, favour, honour, and colour — the French-derived nouns whose medieval spelling in -our was retained by the print culture of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In the early nineteenth century Noah Webster, in his campaign to rationalise American spelling, reduced the group to -or, giving American English endeavor, color, honor. The two spellings have since become national badges: endeavour marks British, Canadian, Australian, and New Zealand English; endeavor marks American. The word is otherwise identical in both. Literary and cultural career. From its entry the word moved into the dignified registers of English. It is the verb and noun of earnest effort in the moral and civic vocabulary: of Milton's "life-long endeavour," of the "strenuous endeavour" of Victorian official prose, of the plural endeavours for the collected labours of a person or a nation. It appears in philosophy and political writing (the "endeavour" of a commonwealth in Hobbes's Leviathan, where endeavour is even given a quasi-technical sense as "conatus," the internal striving of bodies), in the language of law ("best endeavours" clauses, where a contractual party binds itself to use its best efforts), and in the language of science and exploration. The name Endeavour was borne by James Cook's bark on his first Pacific voyage (1768–71), and the Space Shuttle Endeavour was named in homage to it in 1991 — the word, in each case, carrying the connotation of serious, high-purposed enterprise. Semantic register today. Modern English keeps the word formal and slightly elevated. It is the natural choice in official prose, education, journalism, and literary criticism; it is not colloquial. The noun ranges from the singular ("a serious endeavour") to the collective plural that this context uses ("the literary endeavours of Englishmen") — a phrase whose dutiful gravity is exactly the word's inheritance from devoir, "that which is owed." The sixteenth-century English poets, scholars, and educators who set Greek and Roman verse "on the highest of pedestals" and then submitted their own verse "to fresh scrutiny" were, etymologically as well as morally, putting themselves to their duty. The word has carried that moral weight for six hundred years: an endeavour is a debt of effort, and this passage's "literary endeavours" are the attempts a culture owed to its own self-respect — found wanting against the pedestalled classics, but never wanting in earnestness.
