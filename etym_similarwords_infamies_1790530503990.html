• Atrocities: Similar in describing acts of horrifying cruelty and moral depravity; differs because atrocities emphasizes savage violence, physical brutality, and bloodshed (from Latin atrox, "fierce/cruel"), whereas infamies highlights the utter destruction of reputation, profound dishonor, and scandalous notoriety of the evil deeds.
  • Abominations: Similar in denoting deeply detestable and offensive transgressions; differs because abominations (from Latin abominari, "to turn away in omen/horror") carries strong religious, ritualistic, or existential disgust, whereas infamies focuses on public disgrace and civic opprobrium.
  • Scandals: Similar in involving public disgrace and widespread outrage; differs because scandals (from Greek skandalon, "stumbling block") can arise from indiscretions, gossip, or financial impropriety without necessarily involving the monumental, heinous evil implied by plural infamies.
  • Ignominies: Similar in describing conditions or instances of deep public shame and loss of honor; differs because ignominies (from Latin in- + nomen, "name stripped away") stresses personal humiliation and degradation, while infamies leans more strongly toward active villainy, wickedness, and notorious criminality.
  • Outrages: Similar in representing shocking violations of decency, morality, or law; differs because outrages emphasizes the emotional shock, furious resentment, and violation of boundaries, whereas infamies emphasizes the enduring historical stigma and infamous reputation left behind.
  • Iniquities: Similar in denoting gross moral injustices and wickedness; differs because iniquities (from Latin iniquus, "unequal/unjust") carries biblical and ethical gravity focusing on intrinsic unrighteousness, whereas infamies centers on public fame of an abhorrent nature.
  • Opprobriums: Similar in referring to the scornful reproach attached to shameful conduct; differs because opprobrium is primarily a mass noun for the collective disdain and contempt directed toward someone, while infamies can be counted as discrete, heinous deeds or institutions.
  • Villainies: Similar in pointing to vicious, depraved acts; differs because villainies evokes deliberate, treacherous malice (historically originating from peasant status, villanus), whereas infamies emphasizes public, notorious disgrace.
