• spired: Similar because it too describes a building crowned with a tall pointed feature; differs because spired names only the spire, the tapering roof, whereas steepled implies the whole tower-and-spire structure and, by extension, a whole town’s silhouette.
  • towered: Similar because both are denominal adjectives describing vertical architecture; differs because towered is neutral about form and is often secular or military (castle towers, tower blocks), while steepled is specifically ecclesiastical and pointed.
  • pinnacled: Similar in evoking a jagged, upward-pointing outline; differs because pinnacles are small ornamental finials, so pinnacled suggests intricate Gothic detail rather than one landmark steeple.
  • domed: Similar as a skyline adjective formed from a roof feature; differs in shape and culture — a dome is rounded and typically classical, Byzantine or Islamic, whereas a steeple is tapering and Western Christian.
  • minareted: Similar because it describes a place marked by slender towers; differs because minarets belong to mosques and carry a different religious and geographical frame.
  • belfried: Similar in referring to the bell tower; differs because a belfry is the part that houses the bells and may sit in a squat square tower with no spire, so belfried says nothing about height or pointedness.
  • steep: A near-homophone and historical cousin; differs completely in sense — steep describes gradient, or (as a verb) soaking, never the presence of a tower, and the two are constantly confused.
  • church-crowned: Similar as a poetic description of a town dominated by its churches; differs by being a transparent compound rather than a lexicalised adjective, and by making the religious claim explicit instead of metonymic.
  • steeped: Similar only in sound; differs because it means soaked, saturated or deeply imbued, and comes from a different Germanic verb meaning “to pour, soak”.
