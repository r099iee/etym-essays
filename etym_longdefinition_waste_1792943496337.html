Waste in the source sense is wild untilled ground: moor and waste are the open, barren stretches the riders hurry across — land yielding nothing, owned by no plough, the empty country between settlements where travellers push on and outlaws lurk.

This landscape noun is only one face of an exceptionally many-sided word. As noun, verb, and adjective, waste spans geography, morality, economics, and ecology: whatever is desolate, whatever is squandered, and whatever is thrown away all answer to this one short Anglo-French syllable.

Core meaning in detail: The geographical waste is land unfit or unused for tillage — heath, marsh, desert fringe — historically the commons, the marches, the king's waste open to grazing and foraging. From this barrenness flows the moral metaphor: as untilled land yields no crop, so squandered time, money, and talent yield no fruit. The ecological noun completes the circle: waste is also what is left after use — rubbish, offcuts, effluent — matter itself turned barren.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Wild uncultivated land (source sense, from 13th c.): moors, marches, and commons — the empty tract between tilled fields. The bridge is Latin vastus, empty and desolate: land emptied of crops.
  • To squander or spend recklessly (verb, central modern sense): to waste money, time, or a golden chance. The bridge runs from barren land to barren use — expenditure that yields no harvest.
  • To wear away or destroy gradually (verb): disease wasted him; grief wastes the frame. The bridge is bodily desolation: the body becomes a landscape emptied of strength.
  • Refuse and discarded matter (noun, modern): household waste, industrial waste, nuclear waste. The bridge runs through worthlessness: what no use remains in is cast out like barren soil abandoned.
  • Grown thin and enfeebled (adjective, medical-literary): wasted limbs, a wasted face. The bridge is the verb's completed state — flesh consumed as by a slow inner fire.
  • Superfluous or rejected material (technical): waste products, waste heat, waste paper. The bridge is industrial: the useful extracted, the remainder named for the emptiness of its value.
  • To lay waste — to ravage (verb phrase, military): invaders laid waste the countryside. The bridge returns to the source sense: armies manufacture the very desolation the noun names.
  • Ground surrendered to neglect (literary): gardens running to waste, farms gone to waste. The bridge is neglect as slow invasion: without the plough's yearly defence, the waste returns like a tide over every field.

Register and connotation: waste is stern and moralising in the verb — to call spending waste is to condemn it — but neutral and bureaucratic in the ecological noun, where waste management merely sorts the bins. The landscape sense is poetic and faintly menacing: wastes are where ballads lose their travellers and riders hasten through, as in our line, eager for tilled and peopled ground.
