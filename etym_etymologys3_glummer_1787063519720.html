Glummer is the comparative of glum, a short, blunt English adjective for a quiet, visible gloom. The word’s history begins not with an adjective but with a verb: Middle English gloumen (also glomen), attested around 1300, meaning “to become dark, to be gloomy, to look sullen”. The earliest image is of a face darkening — the same picture you get in Modern English “to scowl”. The ultimate origin of gloumen is uncertain; scholars have floated a Scandinavian or Low German source (compare Norwegian glom, Low German glum “murky, muddy”), but nothing has been proven, and no Indo-European root can be reconstructed. It is one of those homely northern words whose deep ancestry is simply lost.

From verb to adjective (1300s–1500s)

  • Around 1300 the verb gloumen meant to grow dark or look sullen — a face clouding over.
  • By the 1540s the adjective glum was in use meaning “sullen, morose, gloomy” — the state of one who gloumens.
  • The noun “gloom” (1590s, “a gloomy look, a sullen frown”) grew from the same stock; glum and gloom have been near twins ever since.

Gloom from the person to the weather (1600s–1800s)

  • For two centuries glum described people and their tempers: a glum fellow, a glum silence, a glum face.
  • In the 19th century the word broadened to scenes and weather — a glum sky, a glum landscape — spreading the personal mood across the whole atmosphere.
  • The comparative glummer and superlative glummest are attested from the 16th century onward and have always been entirely regular.

The comparative in action

  • Glummer (RP /ˈɡlʌm.ə/) doubles the -m- before -er to protect the short vowel — the same rule as dimmer, slimmer, grimmer.
  • The doubled m is nicely expressive: the lips press shut, as they do on a face refusing to smile.
  • In the pattern “glummer and glummer” the comparative is repeated to show steady, unbroken worsening — the standard English idiom for a mood or state sinking deeper and deeper.

The word in Tolkien

  • In The Hobbit (1937), the dwarves sit and think, wander aimlessly, “and glummer and glummer they became” — the repeated comparative stretches across days of hopeless waiting.
  • Tolkien picks the plainest possible word: not “more melancholy” or “increasingly despondent”, but the monosyllabic glum, doubled for emphasis, exactly matching the heavy, simple, silent despair of the scene.
  • The sentence is a small masterpiece of escalation: sit, think, wander, and then the steady comparative — mood hardening into mood as hope drains away.

Modern profile

  • Today glum is everyday and slightly humorous; it deflates grand sadness into a simple, visible case of the blues — you look glum, you feel glum, the day is glum.
  • Glummer still means “more gloomy, more sullen, more dejected”, and the doubled-comparative “glummer and glummer” remains a vivid way of showing a mood sliding downhill without any dramatic break.
  • The word has not changed its shape in four centuries: the same four letters, the same doubled m, the same face grown dark — exactly the image with which its story began.
