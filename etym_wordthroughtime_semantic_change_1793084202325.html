- Antiquity — change unnoticed: classical rhetoricians note that old poets use words oddly, but treat it as decay from a golden age rather than a process.
- 19th c. — the science begins: comparative philologists map sound laws, then turn to meaning; Michel Bréal founds semantics (1883–1897) and names the study of sense-evolution.
- Early 20th c. — textbooks and types: manuals classify narrowing, widening, metaphor, and metonymy, and semantic change enters English curricula as the standard chapter heading.
- Mid-20th c. — structuralism reframed: meaning seen as systems of relations, so change becomes system-shift — whole fields (kinship, colour, rank) moving together rather than lone words.
- Late 20th c. — cognition and corpus: metaphor theory and computer corpora reveal the mental pathways (body to mind, space to time) along which meanings travel.
- Today — watched live: the internet lets speakers observe semantic change in real time (viral, cloud, ghost), turning a historical science into a spectator sport.
