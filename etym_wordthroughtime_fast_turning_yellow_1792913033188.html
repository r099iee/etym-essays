- Old English — everyday colour work: fast means firm and speedy, turn names rotation and change, yellow names gold pigment; no single phrase yet.
- Medieval — autumn gold in verse: poets describe leaves yellowing and falling as emblems of mortal life, though the exact collocation stays loose.
- Early modern — gardeners watch haste: herbals note how fast some elms and limes yellow in a cold snap, reading speed as a weather-glass.
- 19th c. — nature writing fixes the phrase: walkers and novelists write leaves fast turning yellow to date a scene within days, haste becoming atmosphere.
- 20th c. — science explains the gold: chlorophyll chemistry shows the yellow was there all along, unmasked as green withdraws, giving the old phrase a laboratory footing.
- Today — season, stress, and symbol: the phrase covers healthy autumn, drought warnings, and metaphors of ageing, united by the image of green hurrying into gold.
