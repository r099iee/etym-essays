- 1850s to 1860s - Charles Baudelaire emerges in Parisian literary circles as a brilliant, provocative art critic and scandalous lyric poet; the 1857 publication of *Les Fleurs du mal* leads to judicial condemnation for public immorality, stamping his name with immediate notoriety as a dangerous, corrupting bohemian decadent.
- 1870s to 1880s - Following his tragic death in 1867 from paralysis and aphasia, his reputation undergoes a dramatic resurrection; celebrated by the young Symbolist and Decadent generation (Paul Verlaine, Arthur Rimbaud, J.-K. Huysmans) as an angelic martyr, visionary master, and the undisputed prophet of modern poetry.
- Late 19th to Early 20th Century - Baudelaire's renown achieves global reach through translations and critical studies; in Germany, Stefan George and Rainer Maria Rilke absorb his poetic rigor, while in England, Arthur Symons, Walter Pater, and T.S. Eliot credit him with creating the distinctive idiom and psychological landscape of modern urban poetry.
- Mid-20th Century - Walter Benjamin cements Baudelaire's foundational status in cultural theory and urban sociology through his monumental *Arcades Project* and essays on 19th-century Paris, establishing Baudelaire as the essential interpreter of historical commodity capitalism, modern alienation, and the wandering *flâneur*.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Fully canonized as one of the supreme masters of world literature; his work is studied globally not only for its flawless verse architecture, but as a prophetic meditation on consumer society, psychic fragmentation, and the tragic struggle for spiritual dignity within the modern megalopolis.
