- c. 1200 — dreden, to fear and shrink from: a stark word of terror, doom, and religious awe, used of sinners before judgement and subjects before wrath.
- 14th–16th c. — terror and reverence fused: kings, judges, and God are dread and dreaded at once, both feared for punishment and revered for majesty, with no contradiction felt.
- 17th–18th c. — the awe sense peaks and then thins: dread sovereign hardens into courtly formula while sermons and tragedies keep the terror sense burning at full heat.
- 19th c. — the participial adjective spreads into daily life: dreaded examinations, diseases, and visits, as schooling, medicine, and bureaucracy give everyone new futures to fear.
- 20th c. — comic bleaching begins: the dreaded mother-in-law, the dreaded dentist, where mock horror advertises affection and the dread is performed, not felt.
- Today — a two-tier word: sincere dread for diagnoses, disasters, and confrontations, and cheerful hyperbole for Mondays, meetings, and in-laws, with tone of voice deciding which tier is meant.
