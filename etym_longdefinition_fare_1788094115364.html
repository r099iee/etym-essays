FARE (verb) is a verb of motion and of condition. As a verb of motion it means to travel, journey, or proceed along a way ('to fare forth'), and, in a slightly archaic or literary register, to go or to be in transit ('wherever you fare'). As a verb of condition it means to get on, to succeed, or to be situated in a particular state ('how did you fare?', 'the travellers fared well'). The word weaves together the sense of making one's way through the world and the outcome of that journey — the continuum between travelling and how one is treated or how one thrives along the way.

Primary senses.

  • To travel, journey, proceed: to go on a journey or make one's way ('fare forth to the mountains'). This is the root sense of motion, now mostly literary or poetic.
  • To get on, succeed, turn out: to experience a particular fortune or condition ('he fared badly in the dispute'; 'she fared well in the exam'). This is the dominant modern sense of condition.
  • To eat / to be fed (archaic): to fare is also, in old usage, to be nourished or to have food set before one ('we fared sumptuously'), from the same root as the food sense of the noun 'fare'.

Secondary and tertiary senses.

  • 'Farewell': the farewell salutation is literally an imperative 'fare well' — 'travel well', 'may it go well with you' — a frozen compound preserving the verb's oldest wishes.
  • Related nouns: the verb's kin include the noun 'fare' ('the price of a journey', 'passengers paying for a ride') and 'food/household fare' ('simple fare', 'homely fare'); these nouns branch directly from the verb's senses of travelling (and thus of a journey's cost) and of being fed.
  • 'Thee / thou fare': archaic formulas such as 'fare thee well' preserve the full archaic conjugation and register.

Semantic bridge. The entire family derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *per-, 'to go across, to pass over, to travel'. The core idea is motion through the world. Because how one journeys — safely, richly, or poorly — was closely tied to food, shelter, and fortune, the verb naturally extended from 'to travel' to 'to be fed, to thrive, to get on'. It is this extension that yielded both the modern 'how did you fare' (how did things go for you) and the noun senses of food (that which one is served) and of a journey's cost (that which one pays to travel). The farewell blessing unites the two: it wishes the traveller a good journey and good fortune, all in two words built on 'fare'.

Register and usage. In modern English the motion sense of FARE is archaic and literary, surviving mainly in poetry, fantasy, and set phrases; the condition sense is fully current and neutral ('to fare well or badly'). The word's noun and salutation relatives are far more frequent in everyday speech than the bare verb itself.

The condition sense in detail. The modern condition sense of FARE is subtle but precise. To say 'they fared well in the negotiations' is to report on the outcome or state they reached, not merely on their actions. The verb here behaves almost like a linking verb, taking an adverb of degree or quality ('well', 'badly', 'better', 'worse') and answering the question 'how did things go for them?'. It is this sense that makes the idiom 'how did you fare?' interchangeable with 'how did it go for you?' or, depending on register, 'how did you cope?'. Because the word spans success and failure neutrally — one can fare well or fare badly with equal grammatical ease — it is a uniquely balanced way of reporting fortune, untinged by the optimism of 'succeed' or the gloom of 'fail'.

The farewell and its kin. The impact of FARE on everyday English is most visible through its close relatives. Most famous is 'farewell', a frozen compound of the imperative 'fare well', which for centuries has been the standard sincere leave-taking salutation, and whose noun form ('a fond farewell') names the act of parting. Related formulas like 'fare thee well' and 'to bid farewell' keep the archaic imperative alive. The noun 'fare' also carries two important modern senses: the price one pays for a journey ('bus fare', 'a cheap fare to the city'), and food or provisions considered as what is set before one ('plain fare', 'country fare'). Both of these descend from the same Proto-Indo-European root as the verb, via travelling and being fed, so that the single root 'to go across' ultimately underwrites tickets, meals, and valedictory blessings alike.

Summary of the word's character. FARE is thus a word of unusual richness hiding behind a short, plain form. In its original and still-poetic role it is a dignified verb of journeying; in its everyday role it is an impartial verb of fortune; and through its noun and salutation relatives it reaches into transport, food, and parting. The reader who meets 'wherever you fare' in a text is meeting the verb in its oldest, fullest dress — a travelling, a proceeding, and a wishing of good fortune, all concentrated into a single word that means, at bottom, to go across the world and see how one gets on.
