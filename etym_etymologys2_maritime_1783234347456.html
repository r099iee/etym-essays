PIE *móri- "sea, standing body of water" — reconstructed (gen. sg. *mr̥-yés); attested in Italic, Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic; NO secure Greek or Indo-Aryan reflex (Greek uses thálassa, Sanskrit sāgara/samudrá) → Proto-Italic *mari- "sea" — reconstructed → Latin mare (n.) "sea" — attested classical Latin (also in Romance daughters: Italian mare, Spanish mar, Portuguese mar, French mer, Romanian mare) → Latin marītimus (adj.) "of or belonging to the sea; situated by the sea" — attested; formed with mare + suffix *-timus, as in fīnitimus "neighbouring" → Late Latin maritime (adj.) — attested late antiquity → Old French maritime (adj.) — attested 12th c. → Middle English / English maritime — borrowed late 14th–15th c.; current Note on the family: Latin marīnus "of the sea" (mare + -īnus) is a sibling adjective, source of English marine (1580s), mariner, submarine, and of marinade/marinate (via marīnātus "pickled in brine"). Proto-Germanic *mari-/*marja- "sea, lake, standing water" is a cognate line: Gothic marei "sea," Old Norse marr, Old English mere "lake, pond" (Windermere, Grasmere), mermaid, and OE mersc → English marsh. Neither Greek (thálassa) nor Sanskrit (samudrá) descends from *móri-. A supposed link to a root *mer- "to gleam" or *mer- "to die" is speculative, though the sea/death pun was exploited in Celtic and Slavic poetic language.
