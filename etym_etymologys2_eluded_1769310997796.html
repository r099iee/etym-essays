1. PIE *leid- / *loid- "to play, sport, jest; to slip mockingly away" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3500 BCE) — debated, see note. 2. Latin ludere "to play, sport, jest, mock" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE; Plautus, Terence). 3. Latin eludere "to play out of a game, to parry, dodge, evade, mock, deceive" (e-/ex- "out of" + ludere; attested 1st c. BCE; Cicero). 4. French éluder "to evade, elude, circumvent" (attested from the 16th c.). 5. English elude "to escape or slip away from by dexterity, trickery, or cunning; to baffle understanding" (attested from the 1530s; borrowed from Latin eludere and/or French éluder). 6. English eluded — past tense and past participle of elude, formed by regular English -ed inflection → ELUDED. Debate / nuance notes: - The PIE root *leid- "to play" is the traditional reconstruction but is debated; alternative proposals connect Latin ludere to a root meaning "to revile / mock" (cf. Greek loidoréō "to revile, abuse") or treat it as lacking a clear Indo-European etymology. Flag this stage as uncertain rather than securely attested. - English elude may have entered by the Latin route directly, via French éluder, or both (OED gives Latin eludere, likely through French); both routes are historically plausible. - The change to the past form eluded is regular grammatical inflection, not a new borrowing.
