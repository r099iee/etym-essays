• Brook: Similar because it refers to a small stream; differs because a brook is generally larger and more permanent than a rill, typically flowing year-round.
  • Creek: Similar because it's a natural watercourse; differs because a creek is usually larger than both a rill and a brook, often a tributary to a river, and typically permanent.
  • Streamlet: Similar because it denotes a very small stream; differs primarily in its formation as a diminutive of "stream," often used interchangeably with "rill" but perhaps with slightly less emphasis on ephemerality or erosional origin.
  • Trickle: Similar because it describes a small, slow flow of liquid; differs because "trickle" can refer to the *act* of flowing or the *amount* of liquid, whereas "rill" refers to the *channel* or *body* of water itself.
  • Rivule: Similar because it means a very small stream; differs as it is a less common and somewhat more formal or literary term for a small stream, often implying a slightly more established flow than a transient rill.
  • Gully: Similar because it's a channel carved by water erosion; differs significantly because a gully is much larger and deeper than a rill, typically too large to be crossed easily or removed by tillage, often formed by the coalescence of many rills.
  • Channel: Similar because it refers to a path for water; differs because "channel" is a much broader term, encompassing anything from a tiny rill to a major river or even an artificial canal, lacking the specific diminutive and natural connotations of "rill."
  • Furrow: Similar because it's a long, narrow trench or groove; differs because "furrow" is typically created by plowing or digging, often in agricultural contexts, and does not inherently imply the presence of water, though water might collect in it.
