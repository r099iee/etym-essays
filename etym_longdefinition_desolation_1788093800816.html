desolation (pronounced /ˌdɛsəˈleɪʃn/) names the state of being deserted, ruined and empty — a place from which life, inhabitants and usefulness have been stripped — and, by a well-established extension, the emotion of utter loneliness and grief that feels like inner ruin. The word is built on Latin dēsōlātiō, literally "a leaving alone", from dēsōlāre "to abandon, lay waste", itself from dē- "completely" + sōlus "alone". Etymologically, then, desolation is the condition of being left completely alone — and every modern sense radiates from that single image of abandonment.

Sense 1 — physical desolation: a ruined, empty, barren place.

  • A landscape emptied of people and their works: "the desolation of the bombed city", "a desert of total desolation".
  • Waste land that was once inhabited or productive: ruined towns, abandoned farms, scorched earth. The word always implies loss — the place was not always empty.
  • In geography and ecology, "desolation" describes barren regions — ice fields, salt flats, volcanic wastes — in a literary rather than purely technical register.

Sense 2 — the act of laying waste (archaic/literary). Historically desolation also meant the process of destroying: "the desolation of the countryside by the invaders". This sense survives mainly in historical prose and biblical quotation; the agent noun desolator is rare.

Sense 3 — emotional desolation: utter loneliness, grief or spiritual emptiness. Since the seventeenth century the word has described a state of mind:

  • Crushing loneliness after bereavement or abandonment ("the desolation of widowhood").
  • Spiritual desolation: in Christian devotional writing (Ignatius of Loyola's "desolation of soul") a state of dryness and distance from God, set against consolation.
  • Modern literary and clinical prose: "a sense of desolation" = the feeling that one's inner world is empty and ruined, mirroring the ruined landscape of sense 1. The bridge is the etymology itself — to be left alone is to be emptied.

Sense 4 — biblical and prophetic register. The King James Bible fixed "desolation" as the word for divinely decreed ruin: "the abomination of desolation" (Matthew 24:15; Daniel 9:27, 11:31, 12:11), "a desolation and an astonishment" (Jeremiah), the "desolations" of temple and land. In this register desolation is the visible proof of divine abandonment — a city God has left alone. It is the most solemn single use of the word in English.

Sense 5 — the Romantic and modern sublime. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century writers made desolation an object of aesthetic awe: ruined abbeys, empty moors, Arctic and desert wastes (Shelley's Ozymandias, the polar explorers' journals). War reporting today uses "desolation" for devastated cities and regions — Dresden, Grozny, Aleppo — fusing the physical and emotional senses into one image of human ruin.

Derivatives and relatives. Desolate (adjective and verb), desolately, desolateness, desolator. Latin sōlus "alone" also yields sole, solitary, solitude and solo — a whole family of "aloneness" words, of which desolation is the most extreme: solitude is chosen or enjoyed, desolation is suffered and ruinous.
