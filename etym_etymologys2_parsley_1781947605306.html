Derivation chain for PARSLEY (single root word, from a Greek compound): 1. Greek sélinon "celery, parsley" — the ancient name for the parsley-like umbellifers, i.e. celery and its close relatives; attested already in Homer (8th c. BCE). Origin debated: a traditional PIE derivation from *seu- "to press, to extract juice" (with the idea of the plant's expressed sap) is questioned by modern scholars (e.g. Beekes), who note the suffix -in-, typical of Mediterranean plant names, and suspect a Pre-Greek substrate word; the deepest origin must be marked unknown. 2. Greek petroselinon "parsley, rock-celery" — a compound of pétros / pétrā "rock, stone" + sélinon, i.e. "the celery that grows on rocks and stony ground"; attested in Theophrastus (c. 4th c. BCE) and Dioscorides (1st c. CE). The element pétros is itself debated: often derived from PIE *per- "to strike through" (a rock as "that which is struck, cleft"), but a Pre-Greek origin has also been argued. 3. Latin petroselīnum "parsley" — borrowed from Greek; attested 1st c. CE, Pliny the Elder. 4. Vulgar Latin *petrosilium / petroselinum "parsley" — syncopated late-Latin form (4th–6th c. CE), the base of the Romance words (French persil, Italian prezzemolo, Spanish perejil). 5. Old English petersilie "parsley" — a loan from Latin petroselinum via the continental form; attested c. 1000 in Ælfric's glossary. 6. Middle English percil, persely, parsely "parsley" — with syncope and reshaping of the first syllable; attested 13th–14th c. (e.g. perselye, c. 1380). 7. Modern English PARSLEY — spelling fixed in the 16th century; 16th c.–present. Note: The compound nature of the word means two debated elements (sélinon and pétros) sit side by side; neither has a fully secure Indo-European ancestry, and the substrate hypothesis (Pre-Greek) is the current scholarly position for at least sélinon. The chain from Greek petroselinon onward is secure and uncontested.
