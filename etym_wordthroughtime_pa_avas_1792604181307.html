- [Late Vedic & Sutra Literature (c. 1000–600 BCE)] — Earliest textual attestations in late Vedic rituals and domestic sūtras, where the term emerges as a specialized rhythmic tabor distinct from the primordial earth-drum (bhūmi-dundubhi) — [perception: sacred ceremonial instrument used in domestic and sacrificial rites]
- [Classical Epic Period (Rāmāyaṇa & Mahābhārata, c. 500 BCE–400 CE)] — Celebrated as a hallmark of opulent city life in Ayodhyā and Dvārakā, as well as an indispensable martial instrument struck to signal heroic vanguard maneuvers across roaring battlefields — [perception: prestigious acoustic emblem of kingly glory, courtly luxury, and epic martial valor]
- [Classical Sanskrit Dramaturgy & Nāṭyaśāstra (c. 2nd c. BCE–3rd c. CE)] — Canonized by Bharata Muni as an elite member of the tripuṣkara theatrical ensemble, possessing elaborate playing techniques, specialized fingering styles, and designated dramatic moods (rasas) — [perception: sophisticated classical orchestral instrument central to drama, dance, and high art]
- [Medieval Lexicons & Regional Musicology (c. 6th–13th c. CE)] — Codified in lexicons like the Amarakośa; gradually superseded in popular performance practice by newer regional drum types (such as the mardala and dholak), with the term occasionally applied loosely to small brass cymbals or tabors in vernacular dialects — [perception: venerated classical term gradually shifting into scholarly, archival, and poetic memory]
- [Modern Indology & Historical Musicology (19th–21st c.)] — Reconstructed by organologists, ethnomusicologists, and temple epigraphers through ancient sculptures and dramatic texts as a vital link in the evolutionary history of South Asian percussion — [perception: celebrated historical relic of ancient India's Golden Age of musical culture]
