Single root word: clamour (UK spelling; clamor in US) — from Old French clamour/clamor, from Latin clāmor, "a shout, outcry," noun from clāmāre, "to cry out." Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *kelh₁- (also written *kel-, with lengthened-grade *klā-), "to call, shout, summon, cry out" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives: Ancient Greek kaléō "I call, summon" (and ekklēsía, "assembly of those called out"), Sanskrit kalakala "a confused noise, shouting," Old English hlōwan "to low, bellow" (via a Germanic form of the same root). 2. Latin calāre, "to call out, proclaim, announce solemnly" — attested from the earliest Latin ritual language; its feminine gerundive calendae gave the "calends," the proclaimed first day of the month (source of English calendar); the same strand produced concilium, "a calling together, assembly" (source of English council). 3. Latin clāmāre, "to cry out, shout, proclaim," a lengthened-grade form of the same root, "to shout" — attested from Plautus, c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE; the source of English exclaim, acclaim, proclaim, reclaim. 4. Latin clāmor (accusative clāmōrem), "a shout, loud cry, outcry, clamour" — noun formed from clāmāre + the suffix -or — attested from the 1st c. BCE (Cicero, Virgil). 5. Old French clamour / clamor, "loud cry, outcry, appeal, petition" — attested from c. 12th c. CE. 6. Middle English clamour / clamur / clamor, "a loud outcry, a confused noise of voices, a great shout" — attested from c. 14th c. CE (Chaucer uses the word); the verb "to clamour" appears in the same period. 7. Modern English clamour, "a loud and confused noise, especially of people shouting; a vehement public demand" — the "demand/petition" sense consolidates in the 15th–17th c. — final node. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The root *kelh₁- is a large and productive "call" root, and the relation between calāre (ritual proclamation) and clāmāre (outcry) is that clāmāre is generally analysed as a lengthened-grade variant of the same root, sometimes with an extension whose detail is debated. The Sanskrit kalakala is an onomatopoeic formation within the same semantic field, not a direct regular cognate of the Latin verb; the two are grouped by sense rather than by regular sound correspondence. None of these nuances affects the solid Roman-to-English chain, which is attested at every step.
