Note on orthographic and semantic bifurcation: The Greek stem phantasía generated a famous triplet in English: fancy, fantasy, and phantasy. In Middle English, the French loanword fantasie contracted colloquially into fantsy and then fancy, which came to denote personal taste, whimsical preference, or romantic inclination ("to take a fancy to someone"). During the 16th-century Renaissance, humanists deliberately revived the classical Greek spelling with ph- (phantasy) to signal intellectual sophistication, distinguishing serious creative and philosophical imagination from lighthearted fancy. In the 20th century, mainstream English adopted fantasy (with f-) as the standard spelling for imaginative literature and daydreams, while phantasy was preserved in psychoanalysis and Romantic poetry for unconscious and visionary mental structures.
