CHAIN 1 — THE PREFIX "AWAY" (de-): Stage 1 — PIE *de- "off, away, from" (reconstructed prepositional/adverbial root of separation; the base of the Latin preposition dē and prefix de-, and of Greek and other daughter forms meaning "from, away"). Stage 2 — Latin dē "from, down from, away from" (preposition attested from the earliest Latin; also the inseparable verbal prefix de- marking separation, removal, reversal, or descent). Stage 3 — Old French de- (the inherited prefix of separation, from Latin dē-, as in departir "to part away, to separate, to go away"). Stage 4 — Middle English de- (the prefix in departen, borrowed from Old French; retained in Modern English depart). CHAIN 2 — THE VERBAL ROOT "TO SHARE, DIVIDE" (partir): Stage 1 — PIE *per- "to hand over, allot, distribute, sell, exchange" (reconstructed root of allotment and exchange; the proposed base of Latin pars "a part, share" — DEBATED, see note below). Stage 2 — Latin pars (genitive partis) "a part, share, piece, portion" (attested from the 2nd century BCE; a nominal formation, "that which is apportioned or dealt out"). Stage 3 — Latin partīre "to share, divide, distribute, separate" (denominal verb from pars, "to make into parts"; attested in classical Latin). Stage 4 — Old French partir "to divide, share" and departir "to separate, divide; to go away, leave" (de- "away" + partir "to part"; by the 12th century departir already had the sense "to go away, depart"; the source of the noun départ, "departure"). Stage 5 — Middle English departen "to separate, part, divide; to go away, leave" (borrowed from Old French departir, attested from the 13th century). Stage 6 — Modern English depart "to go away, leave; to deviate" (16th century and later) and its past participle departed, used as adjective and noun: "the departed" = the dead (euphemistic use attested from the 16th century). FINAL NODE: DEPARTED. DEBATE NOTE: the derivation of Latin pars from PIE *per- "to allot, distribute, exchange" is standard but not airtight; some scholars reconstruct a separate root or derive pars from *per- "to pass over, go through" with a different intermediate sense. What is securely attested is the chain within Latin and Romance: pars "part, share" → partīre "to divide, share" → Old French departir "to separate, leave" → English depart → departed, with the funerary euphemism developing in English from the 16th century.
