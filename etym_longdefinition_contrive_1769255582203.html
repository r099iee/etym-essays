contrive (verb, /kənˈtraɪv/) — Here the verb does double duty: "He managed to contrive a way to escape from the dungeon." The complement "manage to" is the tell — contrive names successful, effortful problem-solving, the turning of wit and available means toward a practical end. The word implies deliberation, skill, and a degree of artifice: the prisoner does not stumble on a way out but works one out, turning the problem over mentally until a route presents itself. In this context, under duress and secrecy, contrive shades toward cleverness and even cunning — resourcefulness born of necessity, the kind of plotting that suits a dungeon escape in a fairy-tale key. There is nothing clumsy or accidental in the word; the contriver is always in command, fitting means to ends the way a craftsman fits a joint, and that image of the craftsman-planner is what gives the sentence its air of quiet heroism.

Primary senses

  • To devise or plan with skill: to work out and arrange a plan, scheme, or method by deliberate use of intelligence and craft — "they contrived a plan to surprise her." The planning is active, intentional, and means-tested; success is implied.
  • To bring about by artful management: to cause (an object or situation) to come into being or to happen through contrived arrangement — "he contrived to be at the meeting," "she contrived a way out." The sense of "bring about against difficulty" is the core modern use.
  • To fabricate or construct: older, more literal sense of building or making something by craft — "contrive a machine," "contrive a shelter" — the physical crafting sense from which the abstract ones grew.
  • To manage or succeed despite obstacles: with an infinitive ("contrive to do") it means to succeed in doing something, often against the odds or with ingenuity — the exact nuance of the dungeon sentence.

Polysemy, register, and shifted senses

  • Neutral-procedural sense: in ordinary modern prose "contrive" can simply mean "arrange or bring about" without any sneer — "the team contrived a victory" (or, ironically, "contrived to lose").
  • Negative-artifice sense: the family strongly shades toward artificiality. The adjective contrived condemns something as forced, strained, unnatural, or too obviously constructed — "a contrived plot twist," "a contrived smile." The noun contrivance is neutral (a device, a plan) but can also carry the taint of manipulative scheming — "a mere political contrivance."
  • Cunning / scheming shade: in older and literary usage (16th–18th c.) "contrive" is the plotter's verb — to contrive a scheme, a ruin, a secret design — with unmistakably underhanded, even wicked associations. Shakespeare uses it of machinations and villainy.
  • Resourceful/heroic shade: the "manage to against difficulty" sense softens the sneer: the prisoner, the escape artist, the survivor who "contrives" a way through — cunning redeemed as heroism. This is the register of the sentence.
  • Humorous/jocular sense: in modern idiom "I'll contrive something" means "I'll figure something out, somehow" — cheerful, pragmatic improvisation with a wink.
  • Technical/literary-critical jargon: in literary criticism "contrivance" names the visible machinery of plot — when a coincidence or device feels author-imposed rather than organic. The word here is diagnostic and mildly pejorative.

Related words in the family

  • contrivance (noun): (1) a device or machine; (2) a plan or scheme; (3) an artificial or strained arrangement — covering the full neutral-to-negative range.
  • contrived (adjective): forced, unnatural, artificial, over-planned — almost always negative.
  • contriver (noun): one who contrives; an inventor or schemer.

Boundary comparisons with the verbs of planning

  • devise: the neutral planner's word — "devise a plan" — with no hint of craft, secrecy, or difficulty; contrive adds effort, artifice, and the hint of making do against obstacles.
  • invent: emphasizes creating something genuinely new, often playful ("invent an excuse"); contrive stresses arranging existing means toward a goal rather than creating the novelty itself.
  • scheme: carries clear underhanded intent and disapproval — a scheme is at best self-interested, at worst wicked; contrive is neutral-to-positive when it means "manage to succeed," and only the adjective contrived carries the sneer.
  • plot: adds secrecy and narrative intrigue, the classic word of conspirators; contrive is broader — it covers planning, building, and managing, not just clandestine conspiracy.
  • engineer: implies technical, behind-the-scenes arrangement, sometimes manipulative ("engineer a meeting"); contrive is less industrial and more craft-like, closer to "figure out a way."
  • concoct: informal, suggests mixing ingredients, physical or figurative ("concoct a story"); contrive is more precise and purposeful, with the connotation of deliberate plan.
  • maneuver / manoeuvre: suggests strategic positioning and outwitting opponents; contrive emphasizes the successful bringing-about of the end itself.
  • wangle / finagle: informal, suggests getting something by persuasion or trickery; contrive is more dignified and intellectual, less about social manipulation.

Connotation summary

  • Deliberate, skillful, effortful — nothing accidental or clumsy.
  • Double valence — admires resourceful success ("contrive an escape") yet condemns artificiality ("contrived"); the positive reading is activated by phrases like "manage to."
  • Crafting metaphor — etymologically and in feel, the contriver works like a craftsman, fitting means to ends.
