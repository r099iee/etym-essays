- Middle English compound (14th–15th c.) - "court" + "yard" fuse into a plain architectural noun naming the enclosed open space inside a castle, inn, or great house; neutral, spatial, purely descriptive.
- Early modern urban use (16th–17th c.) - the word spreads to modest domestic and civic buildings; its power becomes positional — what it separates (the private inside from the public outside) — and it takes on a value of seclusion and defence.
- Industrial-era crowding (18th–19th c.) - courtyards become the airy interior lungs of tenements, inns, and colleges; the word acquires connotations of cramped but hidden sanctuary tucked behind the street.
- Modern design register (20th–21st c.) - "courtyard" migrates into real-estate, landscape, and hospitality language (courtyard house, hotel courtyard, courtyard garden), shifting from mere enclosure into a marketed amenity of privacy and calm.


- Medieval English drinking-house (13th–14th c.) - "tavern" names a licensed public house selling wine; morally suspect in pulpit and law — the place of drunkenness, gambling, and low company.
- Renaissance civic institution (15th–17th c.) - taverns are distinguished from inns (no lodging) and alehouses (beer, lower rank); they become quasi-respectable urban meeting-places for merchants, writers, and politics.
- Colonial and frontier shift (18th–19th c.) - in American English the tavern doubles as roadside inn and gathering place, then edges toward the rough saloon of the frontier.
- Contemporary archaic-literary register (20th–21st c.) - "tavern" survives as an old-fashioned, atmospheric word — candlelit, smoky, faintly dangerous — used in fiction and in bars trading on historic charm; it keeps a stage-like gravity that ordinary "pub" or "bar" lack.
