Chain — ilk 1. Proto-Indo-European *h₁ey- "that, that one" (demonstrative / anaphoric particle; the basis of Latin is, id, and of the English pronoun it). 2. Proto-Indo-European *leyg- "body, shape, form" (yielding Proto-Germanic *līką "body, form", seen in English lich and in -like, such, which). 3. Proto-Germanic *īlīkaz "having that form, the same" — a compound of the demonstrative stem *ī- "that" + *-līkaz "like, formed" (reconstructed; some scholars propose *igalīkaz with a different ablaut grade; the exact shape is debated). 4. Old English ilca (also ylca) "the same", a weak demonstrative adjective, used in the phrase se ilca "the same one" (8th–11th c.; attested in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, in Ælfric's homilies, and in the Old English Gospels). 5. Middle English ilke "the same" (as in this ilke day, that ilke), 12th–15th c. 6. Early Scots (15th c.): in the title style of landed gentry, X of that ilk = "X of the same (name and place)", i.e. "Macintosh of Macintosh" (attested in Scottish charters and later in Sir Walter Scott's novels). 7. Modern English ilk "kind, sort, class" — a reanalysis of the phrase, first clearly established in English usage from the 18th century (Johnson's Dictionary, 1755, records "ilk; Scotch. Same"). ILK. Debated / nuanced note: the reconstruction of the Proto-Germanic compound is not settled — the first element may be the bare demonstrative *ī- or a fuller grade *ega- (cf. Old Norse glíkr "like", from *ga-līkaz, which preserves a prefix ga- that Old English ilca lacks). The modern "kind, sort" sense is universally agreed to be a post-medieval misunderstanding of the Scottish gentry formula; the point of origin (eighteenth-century glosses) is well documented.
