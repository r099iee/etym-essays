“Fender” began as the low guard set before the hearth — the thing that “fends” or defends the floor from rolling coals, embers, and ash.
“Fender” in the 18th–19th centuries became a fixed, polished part of the Victorian hearth tableau — brass or iron rail, fender-seat and stool, where the master of the house rested his feet in comfort.
“Fender” in the 20th century retreated from the hearth as open fires declined, while the same word moved out to the edges of transport — rope fenders on ships, wheel-guards on cars, and the buffer beam of railways.
“Fender” today is far more common in its nautical and automotive senses, with the hearth-rail now a cosy, period-flavoured memory.
