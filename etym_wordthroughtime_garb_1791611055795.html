- Late 16th c. (c. 1590s) – Borrowed from Middle French garbe and Italian garbo; originally meant graceful demeanor, elegant deportment, courtly manners, and outward bodily posture in Elizabethan high society.
- Early 17th c. (c. 1600–1620) – Underwent metonymic semantic shift from personal bearing and posture to the physical cut, style, and fashion of clothing worn to express that bearing (frequent in Ben Jonson and late Shakespeare).
- 18th c. – Broadened into distinctive national, occupational, and religious costume (e.g., Highland garb, Quaker garb, Roman garb); began to acquire metaphorical senses denoting rhetorical disguises and political pretexts.
- 19th c. – Solidified in romantic fiction (Sir Walter Scott) and historical ethnography to describe the colorful, traditional dress of exotic cultures or past epochs; established in literary reviewing to describe the cover, typeface, and binding format of published books.
- 20th–21st c. – Preserved in formal, journalistic, and academic English for symbolic dress (judicial, ecclesiastical, military garb) and metaphorical guises (e.g., tyranny in the garb of democracy; an art treatise in its new hardbound garb).
