In the line identifying the Modern Library’s illustrated edition of Gibbon, “etchings” names the specific intaglio prints by the great Italian artist Giovanni Battista Piranesi (1720–1778) that were used to illustrate the text — works drawn above all from his celebrated Vedute di Roma, the views of ancient Roman ruins whose soaring, melancholy grandeur are among the most famous images in the history of printmaking. The word is a precise art-historical term: an etching is not a drawing or a painting but a print pulled from a metal plate — usually copper — whose design has been bitten into the surface by acid. The nuance in this context is one of antiquarian gravitas and aesthetic contrast: the fine, wiry, freely drawn lines and the dramatic flicker of light and shadow in Piranesi’s plates perfectly complement the “grand” and “melancholy” themes of Gibbon’s narrative of Roman decay. “Etchings” thus evokes both an exact reproductive technique and the whole romantic atmosphere of ruined grandeur that the illustrations conjure. The plural form, moreover, signals a body of work — a suite of plates — rather than a single print, exactly what an illustrated edition requires. As a dictionary word, “etching” is a noun with two linked senses: (1) the art or process of producing a design on a metal plate by the corrosive action of acid, in which the artist draws through a protective ground of wax so that the acid bites only the exposed lines; and (2) the print itself, pulled from such a bitten plate by inking the sunken lines and pressing paper into them. The plural “etchings” is the collector’s and cataloguer’s word for such prints as a class or a body of work — “the etchings of Rembrandt,” “a portfolio of etchings,” “illustrated from the etchings by Piranesi.” The word also extends, by the same technique, to images made in glass, silicon, and other materials (see the technical senses below). Polysemy — the full range of senses:
  • The print sense (core): a print made by the intaglio process of biting a design into a metal plate with acid — the sense used in the Gibbon line, where Piranesi’s etchings illustrate the text.
  • The process sense: the technique or craft itself — “she studied etching under a master” — the etcher’s needle, the wax ground, the acid bath, the press.
  • The plural as a body of work: a suite or collection of such prints, catalogued as a class — “the artist’s etchings of Roman ruins.”
  • Industrial and technical extension: the biting of designs or circuits into metal, glass, or silicon by chemical agents — “circuit-board etching,” “glass etching” — the modern descendants of the printmaker’s acid.
  • Figurative use: the notion of being “etched” in memory or on the face — a lasting, bitten-in mark — though the common verb form is “etched”; “etchings” itself stays close to the physical prints.
  • Metonymic and gallery use: an etching as a signed, limited-edition work of art, collected and framed — the connoisseur’s object.
The boundary words sharpen the profile:
  • “Engraving”: Similar because both are prints from a metal plate; differs because an engraving is cut with a sharp steel burin, producing a clean, flowing, calligraphic line, whereas an etching is bitten chemically by acid, freeing the artist to draw with a needle as spontaneously as a pen.
  • “Drypoint”: Similar because both are scratched plate prints; differs because drypoint scratches the plate directly with a needle, raising a velvety burr of metal, while an etching is bitten by acid for a wiry, sketch-like line.
  • “Print”: Similar because both are images pulled from a block or plate; differs because print is the broadest term for any impression from wood, metal, or stone, while an etching is one specific acid-bitten technique within that family.
  • “Aquatint”: Similar because both are acid-bitten intaglio techniques; differs because aquatint produces tonal washes of light and shade through a resin ground, while etching produces the line itself.
  • “Lithograph”: Similar because both are printed images; differs because a lithograph is drawn on flat stone with grease and printed from a planographic surface, while an etching is bitten into metal in intaglio.
  • “Woodcut”: Similar because both are prints; differs because a woodcut is cut in relief on a block of wood and printed from the raised surface, while an etching prints from lines bitten into the plate.
  • “Drawing”: Similar because both are graphic images by an artist’s hand; differs because a drawing is a unique work on paper, while an etching is a multiple — many impressions pulled from one plate.
The word’s history is the history of the technique it names: etch comes from the same root as “eat” — the acid eats into the metal — and the word preserves that ancient image of corrosion, so that an etching is, etymologically, a print made by eating. In the Piranesi passage the word carries the full weight of this history: the technical precision of the acid-bitten line, the romantic grandeur of the eighteenth-century ruin-views, and the collector’s body of work that makes an illustrated edition possible. The plural “etchings” is exactly the cataloguer’s form — the word one uses when listing the plates that illustrate a grand book.
