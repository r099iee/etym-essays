doctrinaire: describing a way of thinking or writing in which a fixed body of theory or ideology dictates conclusions in advance, so that evidence is fitted to doctrine rather than doctrine to evidence; and, as a noun, a person who applies a doctrine rigidly in all circumstances without regard to practical considerations. In the sentence praising Gibbon's Decline and Fall, doctrinaire describes a way of writing and thinking in which a fixed body of theory or ideology dictates conclusions in advance, so that evidence is fitted to doctrine rather than doctrine to evidence. The passage claims that few works in the social sciences have had "so small a dogmatic or doctrinaire ingredient" as Gibbon's masterpiece — that is, that the work is almost uniquely free of the rigid, theory-driven cast that so often hardens scholarship into polemic. The nuance is precise: dogmatic stresses the refusal to question and the assertion of truths without proof; doctrinaire stresses the slavish application of a doctrine to every case regardless of practical consequences. Gibbon is praised on both counts at once — he neither asserts unproved truths nor forces his favorite theories onto the past — and the pairing of the two adjectives makes the compliment exhaustive. The word's senses divide into an adjective, a noun, and a history.
  • Sense 1 — adjective: rigidly applying a doctrine or ideology to every case. The primary modern sense, always pejorative: a doctrinaire approach, doctrinaire communism, doctrinaire liberalism, a doctrinaire insistence on theory. The doctrinaire person fits facts to theory rather than theory to facts.
  • Sense 2 — noun: a doctrinaire person. One who is doctrinaire — a theory-monger, an ideologue who applies a fixed system regardless of consequences. The noun can be used of individuals, schools, and parties.
  • Sense 3 — historical: les Doctrinaires. The French constitutionalist political group of the Bourbon Restoration (1815–1830), including Pierre-Paul Royer-Collard and François Guizot, who sought to reconcile the monarchy with the Revolution through abstract principles and were mocked by their opponents as men who ruled by doctrine. The English word is borrowed directly from this nickname.
  • Sense 4 — of arguments, policies, and writings. A doctrinaire ingredient, as in the Gibbon passage: theory-driven, a priori, closed to empirical correction, and therefore hardened into polemic. The adjective applies to the cast of a work as well as to a person's temperament.
  • Sense 5 — derived forms. Doctrinairism and doctrinarianism name the habit of mind; doctrinairely is a rare adverb; doctrinarian is an older variant noun and adjective. The -aire suffix (as in millionaire, concessionaire) marks a person characterized by the noun.
  • Sense 6 — register. The word is always critical or contemptuous. It names the ideology-driven form of inflexibility, and it is cool and systematic rather than hot: the doctrinaire is not inflamed like a fanatic but coldly applies a system.
The semantic bridge is a political birth. The English word is not an ancient inheritance but a recent loan: it was coined in France in 1815 as the nickname of a party, spread as an insult from the party's enemies, and was borrowed into English around 1840 just as Guizot's reputation peaked. From naming a faction it generalized to name any person or work that rules by abstract principle, and the contemptuous overtone it carried at birth has never left it. Because doctrine descends from Latin docere, "to teach", the word keeps the image of the professor applying a syllabus to a class — the doctrinaire teaches reality how it ought to be, and blames reality when it will not comply. In usage the word collocates with approach, attitude, ingredient, cast, spirit, and minded; it is preceded by the adverbs purely, rigidly, hopelessly; and it appears in the paired formula "dogmatic or doctrinaire", as in the Gibbon passage, where the two adjectives together exclude both unexamined assertion and rigid application. Its register is essayistic and scholarly, comfortable in political and historical prose, and its value is that it names, with a single contemptuous word, the intellectual vice of preferring a system to the evidence.
