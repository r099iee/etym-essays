• Wessex: Similar because it is the other great Anglo-Saxon kingdom named in the same passage and the other pole of Macaulay's still-pagan England; differs because Wessex lay in southern England and eventually unified the English under Alfred's line, while Mercia held the Midlands.
  • Northumbria: Similar because it is a major Heptarchy kingdom of comparable power and culture; differs because Northumbria lay north of the River Humber and was the centre of the 7th-8th century Northumbrian Renaissance (Bede, Lindisfarne), whereas Mercia was the Midlands power of the later 8th century.
  • East Anglia: Similar because it is one of the seven Heptarchy kingdoms; differs because it lay on the eastern coast and was smaller and more often a tributary to Mercian or West Saxon overlords.
  • Essex / Sussex / Kent: Similar because they are the southern Heptarchy kingdoms; differs because they were smaller, coastal, and more exposed to Continental influence, whereas Mercia was the large interior kingdom of the Midlands.
  • The Marches: Similar because 'march' is the exact English cousin of the word behind Mercia, both meaning 'borderland'; differs because the Welsh Marches is a later medieval border region of the England-Wales frontier, whereas Mercia was the Anglo-Saxon kingdom named from its border position.
  • The Danelaw: Similar because it is a region of England defined by political division and Viking settlement; differs because the Danelaw was the area of Scandinavian law and settlement created in the 9th century, whereas Mercia was an Anglian kingdom of the 6th-9th centuries.
  • Mark / March / Margin: Similar because they are etymological cousins of Mercia, all from PIE *merǵ- 'boundary'; differs because they denote abstract or general 'boundary, edge' rather than the specific Anglo-Saxon kingdom.
  • Deira / Bernicia: Similar because they are early Anglian kingdoms that merged into Northumbria; differs because they were northern kingdoms of the 6th century whose union created Northumbria, while Mercia remained a separate Midlands power.
