Chain for ZEAL: 1. Proto-Indo-European *yā- "to seek, request, desire" (Watkins's reconstruction of the base of Greek zēlos; tentative — the deeper Greek etymology is debated, Beekes is cautious, so treat this PIE stage as a hypothesis). 2. Greek zēlos (ζῆλος) "ardour, eager rivalry, emulation; jealousy" (classical; personified as Zēlos, child of Styx, in Hesiod's Theogony, 8th–7th c. BCE). 3. Late Latin zēlus "zeal, emulation, jealousy" (Church Latin; the Vulgate, 4th c. CE, uses it in the biblical senses). 4. Old French zel "zeal, passionate ardour" (12th c.; modern French zèle). 5. Middle English zele "ardent passion, religious fervour" (late 14th c., in Bible translations). 6. English ZEAL "great energy or enthusiasm in pursuit of a cause" — OUR CARD. Notes: - The PIE base *yā- "to seek, desire" is the traditional reconstruction (Watkins), but zēlos's pre-Greek history is uncertain; some scholars allow an Aegean/Pre-Greek element. State explicitly: the derivation from Greek zēlos onward is secure; the PIE stage is probable but debated. - Side-channel of the same Greek family: zēlōtēs "one who is a zealous follower" (from zēloōn "to be zealous") passed via Late Latin zēlōtēs into English as zealot (early 14th c.); the Zealots (Greek Zēlōtai) were the first-century Judean sect fiercely resisting Rome (Josephus). - Second side-channel: Late Latin zēlōsus "full of zeal" passed through Old French jalos / gelos "keen, zealous; avaricious; jealous" (12th c.) into Middle English jelous (c. 1200) — English jealous and jealousy are therefore cousins of zeal, not the same word. - No Indo-Aryan branch is shown: no secure descendant of *yā- "to seek" demonstrably underlies Greek zēlos in Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia; the branch is omitted for accuracy.
