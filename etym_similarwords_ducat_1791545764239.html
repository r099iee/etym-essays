• florin: Similar because both were preeminent gold trade coins minted by rival Italian republics in the thirteenth century (Florence and Venice) that became international currency benchmarks; differs because the florin bore the heraldic lily of Florence and was first struck earlier (1252), whereas the ducat bore St. Mark and the Doge.
  • sequin (zecchino): Similar because it is the exact same Venetian gold coin under its later sixteenth-century Italian name; differs because zecchino (from la zecca, the mint) was officially adopted by Venice in 1543 to distinguish the gold coin from its new silver ducat, and later entered English to mean a small glittering dress ornament.
  • thaler (dollar): Similar because both were legendary international trade currencies that dominated European and colonial commerce for centuries; differs because the thaler was a heavy silver coin originating in sixteenth-century Bohemia (Joachimsthal), whereas the classic ducat was universally famed as pure gold.
  • doubloon: Similar because both were prestigious European gold coins celebrated in Atlantic commerce and maritime history; differs because the doubloon was Spanish (valued at two escudos or four silver dollars), larger and heavier, whereas the ducat originated in medieval Italy and Central Europe.
  • sovereign: Similar because both were premier official gold coins carrying supreme state authority and gold backing; differs because the sovereign was English (originally worth twenty shillings), featuring the enthroned English monarch and struck under distinct insular alloy standards.
  • guinea: Similar because both served as prestigious gold currencies recognized across global trading ports; differs because the guinea was struck in Britain between 1663 and 1814 from West African gold, valued at twenty-one shillings, whereas the ducat was a continental coin of medieval origin.
  • bezant: Similar because both were gold coins used as standard media of exchange throughout Mediterranean commerce; differs because the bezant was Byzantine (the solidus or hyperpyron), dominant in the early Middle Ages before the Venetian ducat supplanted it following the Fourth Crusade.
  • groat: Similar because both were widespread medieval European coins circulating extensively in trade; differs because the groat was a small silver denomination (worth fourpence in England), whereas the ducat was primarily an elite high-value gold bullion coin.
  • moidore: Similar because both were gold coins celebrated in transatlantic trade and classic literature; differs because the moidore was Portuguese (moeda de ouro), circulating heavily in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in Portugal, Brazil, and the West Indies.
  • pistole: Similar because both were continental gold coins used in European commerce and literature; differs because the pistole was the French and English name applied to the Spanish two-escudo coin or Italian double-ducat.
