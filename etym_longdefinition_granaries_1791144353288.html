Granaries is the plural of granary: buildings or chambers specially constructed and secured for storing threshed grain and other staple foods against moisture, pests and theft until needed in leaner months.

In its core agricultural sense, a granary is not any shed but a purpose-built store: raised off the ground or ventilated, weatherproof, rat-proof, often with bins, silos or lofts, where wheat, barley, rice, maize or millet is kept after threshing and winnowing. The source context uses exactly this sense: he built granaries secure from rain and damp, vermin and thieves, and gathered food into them. The emphasis is on foresight, surplus management and the transition from immediate consumption to planned storage that underlies farming civilization.

Beyond the farm building, the word extends to any reserve or productive source of grain. A country or region can be called the granary of an empire or continent, meaning its breadbasket. Institutions speak of strategic or famine granaries, and development projects of village granaries. Figuratively, writers speak of granaries of knowledge, memory or culture, meaning rich stored reserves to be drawn upon.

  • Farm storehouse (primary): A ventilated, dry, secure building for threshed grain; e.g., wooden granaries on stilts, Roman horrea, Egyptian pit granaries, modern concrete silos called granaries in general prose.
  • Surplus and food security: Public or communal stores held against famine, siege or winter; e.g., Joseph's granaries in Genesis, Roman annona granaries, Chinese ever-normal granaries.
  • Breadbasket region (metonymic): A fertile area supplying grain to others; e.g., Sicily as Rome's granary, Ukraine as Europe's granary, the Midwest as America's granary.
  • Figurative store / repository: Any abundant reserve; e.g., a library as a granary of learning, memory as a granary of experience. This bridge is metaphor: grain sustains the body, so stored wisdom sustains the mind.
  • Archaeological / historical type: Specific forms such as raised granaries, underground pit granaries, and granary barns; archaeologists classify them by ventilation, elevation and capacity to infer social complexity.
  • Adjectival and commercial extensions: Granary bread containing whole grains, evoking the granary; granary moth / weevil as pests of stored grain. These are associative, not separate buildings.

The polysemy is bridged by the ideas of harvest, surplus, security and provision. Because grain was life, the building that preserved it became emblematic of prudence, civilization and power. To build granaries is to plan for the future; to fill granaries is prosperity; to empty them is famine or war. Hence economists, historians and moralists all use the word with overtones of foresight, stewardship and communal responsibility far beyond carpentry.

Such buildings also shaped law and morality: granary receipts served as currency and tax records in Egypt and Mesopotamia, hoarding in granaries could be condemned as selfish monopoly in famine years, while open granaries signified generous lordship. To speak of granaries is thus to speak of civilization choosing foresight over immediacy.
