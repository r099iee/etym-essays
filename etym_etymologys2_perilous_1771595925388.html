Chain for PERILOUS: 1. Proto-Indo-European *per- "to pass through, lead across, go forward" (with the experiential extension "to try, risk"; one of the richest roots in Indo-European). 2. Latin periculum "a trial, a test; risk, danger" (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus; the semantic slide from "trial" to "danger" is already complete in classical Latin). 3. Latin perīculōsus "dangerous, perilous" (classical Latin, from Cicero). 4. Old French perilleus "perilous, dangerous" (11th–12th c.). 5. Middle English perilous "dangerous, fraught with risk" (13th c., c. 1250). 6. English PERILOUS "full of danger or risk" — OUR CARD. (English also borrowed the noun peril directly from Old French in the 13th c.) Notes: - The semantic bridge is metonymy: PIE *per- "to go through" → Greek peira "trial, attempt" and Latin periculum "a trial, a test" → because a test is dangerous, "risk, danger." The same stem produced Latin experiri "to try out" (English experience, experiment, expert) and Greek peiratēs "one who attempts," the source of English pirate. - Sibling reflexes of *per- in the spatial sense: Greek peri "around," Sanskrit pari "around," Latin per "through," Germanic *faran "to go" (English fare, ferry, ford, fjord). - Variant to note: Middle English also had parlous, a corruption of perilous used from the 14th to the 16th c. and surviving as a jocular or dialectal word; the direct Latin loan periculous (periculosus) was briefly used in English and is now obsolete. No major scholarly dispute attaches to this chain.
