Note on historical debate & linguistic origin: The ultimate etymological provenance of Sanskrit daṇḍa (and its derivative daṇḍaka) is the subject of one of the most prominent philological debates in South Asian historical linguistics. Indo-Europeanists (such as Manfred Mayrhofer in Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Altindoarischen and Julius Pokorny) have historically attempted to connect daṇḍa to the PIE root *del- / *delh₁- ("to split, carve, fashion with an adze"), positing a reduplicated or nasalized formation *dṇ-d-. However, prominent Dravidianists and comparative linguists (including Jules Bloch, Thomas Burrow, and Murray Emeneau) argue convincingly that daṇḍa represents an early prehistoric borrowing from Proto-Dravidian *taṇṭu, which signifies "a stick, staff, stalk, mace, or marching army" (surviving in Tamil taṇṭu, Kannada daṇḍu ["army, stick"], and Telugu daṇḍu). Whether rooted in Indo-European wood-cleaving or ancient Dravidian substratum borrowing, the noun evolved into the ultimate Sanskrit symbol of judicial authority, punishment, and the sovereign rod of law.
