The verb sift begins with one of the oldest technologies known to settled life: the woven mesh that turns a heap of threshed grain into food. Proto-Germanic *sibi, the sieve or strainer, named a hoop strung with haircloth, rushes, or pierced hide, and Old English siftan named the rhythmic shaking that drove the fine meal through while husks danced on top. Every bakery, brewery, and building site in Anglo-Saxon England knew the gesture, and the word sat squarely in the vocabulary of daily bread.

The leap from kitchen to conscience: already in Middle English the physical act lent itself irresistibly to thought. Preachers told congregations to sift their deeds as housewives sifted meal, keeping the wholesome and casting out the chaff of vanity, and Chaucerian English could speak of sifting counsel, sifting rumours, and sifting the scriptures for doctrine. The semantic bridge needed no explanation because every listener's arms knew the motion: truth, like flour, is what remains after honest shaking. By the sixteenth century courts and colleges had adopted the metaphor wholesale, witnesses were sifted, authorities were sifted, and thoroughness itself took the sieve as its emblem.

Tools that changed, hands that did not: the industrial centuries mechanised the literal half of the word without touching the figurative half. Millers fitted graded wire meshes and rotary sifters, builders specified sifted sand for fine plaster, assayers sifted crushed ore for gold dust, yet the mental sense stayed stubbornly manual: one still sifts evidence the slow way, heap by heap, doubt by doubt. Darwin among barnacles and Lyell among gravels both described their science as sifting, giving the word a permanent place in the self-image of patient inquiry.

  • War and archives (20th c.): codebreakers sifted intercepts, auditors sifted ledgers, and journalists sifted leaks, until the verb became shorthand for democratic hygiene: no claim seated until sifted.
  • Domestic survival: through every change the kitchen sieve survived, so that children watching flour snow into a bowl still learn the founding image first-hand, exactly as their Anglo-Saxon forebears did.
  • Digital wordplay (1999): computer vision researchers, needing a name for an algorithm that shakes images down to their stablest keypoints, christened it SIFT, the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform, a learned wink proving the metaphor still delights engineers.

Sound and kin: sift belongs to a whispering family of Germanic sieve-words: German sieben and Sieb, Dutch zeven and zeef, Old Norse sia, all turning on the same ancient mesh. The consonants never shifted far because the tool never changed shape, and the modern verb still sounds like its own action, a soft shaking hiss ending in a clipped stop.

Modern standing: today sift works as hard in metaphor as in meal: bakers aerate flour, detectives aerate alibis, and search engines sift billions of pages between blinks. Gandalf's sentence catches the word at its wisest, advising not cleverness but patience before a liar, the oldest method in the book: pour out the words, shake them hard, and trust only what stays in the mesh.
