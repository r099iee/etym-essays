- Mid-16th Century (c. 1550s–1600) - Borrowed into English from French and Latin; initially applied primarily to human character and speech to describe untrustworthy, frivolous, or talkative individuals who leak confidences and chatter vainly, reflecting the classical Latin image of a leaky pitcher.
- 17th Century - Undergoes semantic shift from persons to intellectual discourse, writing, and argumentation; philosophical writers like Francis Bacon and John Locke employ it to condemn baseless scholastic logic, hollow disputes, and insubstantial theories that retain no truth.
- 18th to 19th Century - Generalizes into its dominant modern meaning of practical operational inefficacy; widely used in military chronicles, political treatises, and dramatic fiction to characterize doomed resistance, hopeless heroic efforts, and wasted human labor.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Deepens in existential and literary gravity under the influence of World War I, modernism, and existentialism (Marcel Proust, Franz Kafka, Albert Camus); "futile" becomes an emblem of the human struggle against time, mortality, and bureaucratic absurdity.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Expands into rigorous technical and scientific domains, acquiring precise formal definitions in biomedical ethics ("medical futility" in palliative care) and biochemistry ("futile cycle" in metabolic substrate regulation).
