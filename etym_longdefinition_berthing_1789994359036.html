Berthing is the gerund and verbal noun of berth: the act of assigning, occupying or paying for a berth — and by extension the system of bunks, marina slots, fees and manoeuvres around them. In the source sentence it is the middle item of a yachtsman's ruinous triad — outfitting, berthing, and maintaining — where it means marina charges for keeping Hinano (45 foot) on her pontoon or mooring, plus the labour of warping her in and out. Together with failing eyesight, these recurring costs force sale.

A berth itself is famously two things at once: (a) room for a ship — sea room, anchoring ground, a slot alongside — and (b) room in a ship — a bunk, a cabin place, a seaman's chest-space. Berthing inherits both: to berth a vessel is to park it; to berth a crew is to bed them. Context decides, and yachting contracts exploit both (berthing fees cover the pontoon; berthing plan shows who sleeps where).

  • Primary nautical-manœuvre sense: action of bringing a vessel into its allotted place (to berth alongside, to berth stern-to, Mediterranean berthing, alongside berthing). Includes lines, fenders, thrusters and harbourmaster's orders. Opposite: unberthing.
  • Mooring-contract and cost sense (the context sense): provision of and payment for a permanent or visitor's slot — marina berthing, swinging mooring versus pontoon berth, liveaboard berthing, winter berthing. Measured in metres, charged per night or annum, the yachtsman's standing charge beside insurance.
  • Sleeping-place sense: assigning bunks — troop berthing, crew berthing, train berthing (sleeper compartment), ferry berthing, even spaceship berthing in science fiction. From 1706 (place on ship to stow chests and sleep) extended ashore 1778. Example: six-berth cabin means sleeps six.
  • Figurative sea-room sense: preserved in to give a wide berth (keep well clear), first navigational (do not crowd another ship's manœuvring room), now general avoidance. Berthing in this idiom is abstract clearance, not rope.
  • Logistical and commercial sense: port berthing — allocation of quay, draft, cranes and tides to merchantmen; berthing master, berthing schedule, berthing window. A port sells berths the way a hotel sells beds.
  • Grammatical note: berthing is uncountable for the process (berthing is expensive) but countable in the plural for instances (two awkward berthings today). Attributive: berthing fees, berthing plan, berthing party.

Polysemy radiates from one debated core: empty room that becomes assigned room that becomes furnished room. Sea room around a hull to anchoring ground allotted to a hull to bunk built into a hull to bed booked ashore — each step narrows and domesticates space while keeping the idea of a safe, sufficient place. The bridge is administrative: harbours and pursers allot room, then charge for it, then draw it on plans. A learner who pictures first an empty circle of water that no other ship may enter, then a painted rectangle on a marina pontoon, then a narrow bunk with a lee-cloth, has the whole word. In the Hinano sentence only the middle picture — the rented rectangle of water and its annual invoice — is active, but the bunk-picture lingers below deck, and the wide-berth idiom sails overhead.
