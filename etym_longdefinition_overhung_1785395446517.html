OVERHUNG is the past tense and the past participle of the verb overhang, and, as a participial adjective, it means "covered, roofed, adorned, or shaded by something that hangs or projects from above." Where a thing is said to be overhung, something else, a branch, a rock, a cliff, a cloud, an awning, a brow, extends over it and leaves space beneath: the covering is arched, not flush. In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses the participle in a single carefully weighted clause: the dells below the level of the path stand "overhung with bushes and low trees." The picture is of hollows scooped out of the sloping ground, their mouths half-closed by vegetation that has pushed out from the banks above them, so that each dell reads like a cave-like pocket of green pressed into the hillside. The participle insists that the covering comes from a height and leaves space beneath, something arched over the hollow rather than lying flush upon it, and the past-participial form renders the state as already completed: the dells stand enveloped, the canopy already over them, before the travellers have even arrived to see it.

Grammatical range. As the past tense, overhung narrates the moment of covering ("the boughs overhung the path"); as a participle and adjective it describes a completed state ("an overhung porch", "a wall overhung with ivy"). Both uses carry the signature geometry of the family: a projection from above with open space beneath, the brow, the lintel, the canopy, the cliff-edge.

  • Sense 1 (core physical sense, transitive): "to project or extend over (something); to hang suspended above," of cliffs, rocks, trees, branches, clouds, buildings, and brows: "the crag overhung the stream", "the balcony overhung the street."
  • Sense 2 (participial adjective, of things): "covered, roofed, or shaded from above; standing beneath a canopy or projecting mass": "an overhung dell", "a doorway overhung with vines", "windows overhung by a jutting roof." This is the sense in the Tolkien passage.
  • Sense 3 (adornment and draping): by a natural extension, "adorned or decked with something hanging from above," a wall overhung with ivy, a shrine overhung with garlands; the covering is ornamental as much as protective.
  • Sense 4 (of the face, especially the brow): "projecting forward with a heavy or threatening look": an overhung brow, overhung eyebrows that shadow the eyes, the same geometry, a projection above with space beneath, read as menace, secrecy, or brooding.
  • Sense 5 (figurative, suspended threat): "impending, hovering, threatening to fall": a danger or fate that overhangs a life, a catastrophe that hangs overhead; the overhung thing is not yet struck, the threat is suspended in time, and the participle names the state of waiting beneath it.
  • Sense 6 (technical and commercial extensions of the noun family): though the rootWord is the participle overhung, its noun twin overhang has acquired precise technical senses that illuminate the family: in mountaineering an overhang is an outward-projecting rock face; in finance and markets an overhang is a supply of unsold stock or shares pressing down on a market and threatening prices; in architecture and engineering an overhang is the projecting part of a roof, storey, or structure. All reproduce the same geometry, a mass projecting over a space below.

Register and stylistic bridge. Overhung is a word of descriptive, spatial writing, of landscape, architecture, and the human face, and of the period novel and literary narrative that inherits its eye for enclosing, shadowed space. Its figurative life ("overhung by dread", "overhung with gloom") transfers the picture of a canopy overhead to the pressure of something imminent and unfulfilled. The bridge between the physical and the figurative senses runs through the shared image of a weight suspended above that has not yet fallen: the canopy, the brow, and the pending catastrophe are all the same shape, a mass overhead with living space beneath it.
