• Harold Godwinson: The full personal name, without the royal title; identical in reference and more precise in scholarly writing, but it does not by itself assert his kingship.
  • Harold II: The regnal name; identical in reference, differing in register — it is the numbering used in dynastic lists and is neutral about his character.
  • The last Anglo-Saxon king: A descriptive epithet; similar in identifying the same man, but it is a claim (and a slight simplification, since Harold was of mixed English and Danish descent) rather than a name.
  • Earl Harold: His title before 1066; similar in referring to the same person, but it locates him in the reign of Edward the Confessor and denies the royal status he later claimed.
  • Hereweald: The Old English form of the name, “army-ruler”; similar as the etymological ancestor, but it is a linguistic reconstruction of the name’s elements, not a name he is likely to have been called in that spelling.
  • Harold: The bare given name; similar in form, but it is shared by many later bearers and lacks the specific historical reference of the full phrase.
  • William the Conqueror: His opponent; similar as the other protagonist of 1066, but William won, reigned for twenty-one years and founded a dynasty, whereas Harold is defined by defeat.
  • Harald Hardrada: The Norwegian king killed at Stamford Bridge; similar as a Harold-like warrior-king and a rival claimant, but he is Norwegian, not English, and his death belongs to a different battle.
  • Edward the Confessor: Harold’s predecessor and brother-in-law; similar as an Anglo-Saxon king of the same crisis, but he died in his bed and was canonised, whereas Harold died in battle and was never a saint.
  • Edgar the Ætheling: The royal prince passed over in 1066; similar as a claimant, but he was never crowned king, so he cannot be called “King”.
  • Tostig: Harold’s exiled brother; similar in family and in the 1066 invasions, but he was an earl and a rebel, not a king.
