• configuration: Similar because both refer to an arrangement and structural disposition of interrelated parts; differs because configuration emphasizes the spatial or functional positioning of parts relative to one another, without necessarily implying an irreducible, emergent psychological whole.
  • totality: Similar because both describe an entire encompassing entity; differs because totality is primarily quantitative, emphasizing completeness, absolute sum, or all-inclusiveness, whereas gestalt emphasizes qualitative internal organization and emergent properties.
  • synthesis: Similar because both involve the combination of diverse elements into a unified whole; differs because synthesis emphasizes the active historical or intellectual process of combining disparate elements (thesis and antithesis), whereas gestalt refers to an immediate, perceived structural state.
  • organic whole: Similar because both describe a system whose components function harmoniously together; differs because organic whole is often a biological or philosophical metaphor emphasizing living growth and mutual interdependence of organs, whereas gestalt is grounded in precise perceptual and cognitive laws.
  • pattern: Similar because both involve recognizable arrangements and regularities; differs because pattern typically denotes a recurring decorative, geometric, or behavioural repetition across time or space, whereas gestalt can refer to a single, unique, holistic perceptual entity.
  • assemblage: Similar because both bring together multiple items; differs because assemblage implies an additive, often loose or eclectic collection of disparate artifacts (as in modern art), contrasting sharply with the unified irreducibility of a gestalt.
  • structure: Similar because both concern internal organization; differs because structure focuses on the underlying framework, skeleton, or formal rules governing how parts connect, whereas gestalt focuses on the resulting integrated perception and holistic impact.
