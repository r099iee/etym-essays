The evolution of ostensibly is a study in how a perfectly honest word about showing came to be the standard instrument of suspicion. The chain descends from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch, extend, spread." This was one of the family's most prolific roots. Vedic Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches" lies behind the word tan in Indian music and behind tension via Latin; Ancient Greek teínein "to stretch" feeds tone, tendon, extend, and the whole ten-/tend-/tens- vocabulary of English; Old English þennan "to stretch" (Germanic *þanjaną) gives the noun thin and the verb thawn in its idea of spreading; and Latin tendere "to stretch, extend, aim" stands behind tend, attend, intend, and tent (something stretched). The root's core meaning was the physical act of drawing something out to its length, whether a rope, a bowstring, or a cloth.

Latin built its compound ostendere by prefixing the preposition obs- (a variant of ob-) "towards, in front of, against" to tendere: literally "to stretch out in front of", hence "to present, show, display, manifest". The word is attested from the earliest Latin comedy — Plautus and Terence (third–second centuries BCE) use it freely of showing, pointing out, and displaying — and it becomes one of Cicero's favourite verbs for "pointing out" an argument or "displaying" a quality. Its participial stem ostēns- also produced the frequentative ostentāre, "to show off, display ostentatiously", whose English descendant ostentatious keeps the idea of showing without modesty. Already in Latin, therefore, the "show" family had split into an honest branch (ostendere, to show plainly) and a showy branch (ostentare, to flaunt).

From the participle the Romance languages and medieval Latin built an adjective meaning "that can be shown, displayable, apparent". Latin's ostēnsibilis is thinly attested, but the pattern was obvious, and French created ostensible in the seventeenth century (recorded around the 1660s) with the senses "that can be shown; apparent, professed". English borrowed the adjective ostensible in the eighteenth century — the first records cluster in the 1730s — with the meaning "capable of being shown, plausible, professed". At this point the word was still fairly neutral: it described whatever could be put on display, whatever was on the surface.

The critical change came in the nineteenth century, when the adverbial form ostensibly hardened into its modern skeptical sense. In part this was a consequence of the word's own logic: if a thing is merely "showable" or "on the surface", it invites the question of what lies beneath. Nineteenth-century novelists, journalists, and politicians exploited exactly that gap. A speaker could state a purpose "ostensibly" while winking at the real one; a government could act "ostensibly" for the public good while serving other interests. The adverb became the workhorse of the unreliable narrator — the grammatical equivalent of "so he said". It carries no accusation on its face, which is why it is so beloved of careful writers: it imputes doubt without asserting deception.

The word's career shows a perfect inverse of its root's honesty. PIE *ten- described stretching cloth taut, an image of openness and clarity; Latin ostendere meant to hold something forward so that all could see it; but modern ostensibly holds something forward precisely so that we may wonder what is being hidden behind it. The surface remains, but its trustworthiness has drained away. In the source passage, Yu-cun's quatrain is "ostensibly on the subject of the moon, but really about the ambition he had been at some pains to conceal": the moon is the stretched-out, showable surface; the ambition is the truth held back. The sentence is a textbook deployment of the word.

The adverb has also acquired a journalistic and legal afterlife. In reportage, "ostensibly" has become a standard hedge for second-hand claims and cover stories; in legal argument, it distinguishes the professed purpose of a rule from its effect; in scholarship, it separates what a text claims to be about from what it actually does. Its register remains formal, and its prevalence in modern public prose makes it one of the most useful sceptical adverbs in the language.

Summary of the evolution:
  • Formal chain: PIE *ten- → Latin tendere → ostendere → ostēnsus → (Latin ostēnsibilis, thinly attested) → French ostensible → English ostensible → English ostensibly (-ly).
  • Sound and morphology: the -ns- participle stem ostēns- passes unchanged into French and English; English adds the ordinary adverb suffix -ly.
  • Semantic drift: "stretched out in front" → "shown, displayed" → "that can be shown, apparent" → "professed, put forward for show" → "apparently but (probably) not really".
  • Socio-cultural engines: Roman rhetoric and law; seventeenth-century French rationalism; nineteenth-century journalism and the novel's ironic narration; twentieth-century political and legal discourse.
  • Literary attestations: Plautus, Terence, and Cicero; seventeenth-century French prose; eighteenth-century English letters; nineteenth-century novels (where "ostensibly" marks the narrator's doubt); modern news, law, and scholarship.
