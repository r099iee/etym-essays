- [14th c.: literal fire] - Borrowed from Old French ardur (Latin ardor), the word first means burning heat and flame itself, a purely physical, almost meteorological heat.
- [14th-16th c.: figurative passion] - The fire-metaphor extends: ardour becomes the burning intensity of love, desire and devotion, glowing with romantic and religious warmth.
- [18th-19th c.: intellectual and moral zeal] - It migrates to learning and enterprise: the ardour of scholars, reformers and soldiers, elevated and honourable, still fiery but directed at causes and crafts.
- [Modern: literary register] - Today ardour is formal and bookish, naming a sustained, glowing commitment to a cause or study, and it can carry a gently ironic edge when the devotion is comic or excessive.
