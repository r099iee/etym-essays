- Late 16th c. — Adjectival textile and chromatic borrowing: borrowed from French incarnadin and Italian incarnadino to describe delicate flesh-pink, carnation, or pale peach-tinted silk fabrics, carrying courtly, decorative, and gentle domestic connotations.
- 1606 — Shakespearean dramatic transfiguration: William Shakespeare in Macbeth radically converts the noun/adjective into an active transitive verb, reimagining it not as delicate carnation-pink, but as the monstrous, guilt-laden, blood-red staining of the entire global ocean; forever imbuing the word with dark, tragic, and awe-inspiring poetic violence.
- 17th–18th c. — Literary emulation & poetic elevation: adopted by metaphysical poets (such as John Donne and Thomas Carew) and later 18th-century writers as a quintessentially sublime, Latinate epithet for blushing cheeks, sunrises, or battlefields, balancing its bloody theatrical dread with romantic crimson passion.
- 19th c. — Victorian gothic & historical prose: widely employed by Romantic and Victorian authors (e.g. Keats, Shelley, Melville) to evoke rich, saturated dyes, fatalistic wounds, and stained waters, establishing it as an aristocratic, hyper-literary marker of crimson immersion.
- 20th–21st c. — Historiographical & modern poetic register: rarely used in spoken speech, reserved for elevated historiography (such as Will Durant describing primitive eras stained by violence) and stylistic literature to characterize landscapes, oceans, or eras indelibly marked by violence or fiery red luminescence.
