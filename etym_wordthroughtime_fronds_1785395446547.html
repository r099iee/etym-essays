- Before 1784 - absent from English; the foliage of ferns and palms was simply called 'leaves', with no distinct organ-name.
- 1784 - borrowed directly from botanical Latin 'frons'/'frondis' ('leaf, foliage, leafy branch') into English scientific vocabulary, when naturalists needed a name for fern leaves - whole, rolled, divided into leaflets, leaf and stem in one.
- 19th century - expands beyond ferns to palms, cycads and even seaweeds; botanists speak of the fronds of kelp as readily as of fern fronds.
- Modern English - keeps a learned, literary aura: 'fronded' became a favourite of Romantic nature poetry, and in literary prose fronds imply scale, strangeness and something primeval, older than flowers - foliage that dwarfs the observer.
