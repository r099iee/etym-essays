nettles (plural noun; singular nettle; RP /ˈnɛt.l̩/) — the common stinging nettle, Urtica dioica, a bristly herb whose stems and leaves are armed with fine, hollow, hair-like trichomes that deliver a sharp, burning, itching sting on contact. The plural form in the text signals a whole clump or drift of the plants — an obstacle to be fought through, not a single specimen to be noticed. 1. The word in this literary context. In this passage from C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian, the four Pevensie children have just been sucked out of a railway platform and deposited in a wild wood; they "struggle out of the thicket" with "stings from nettles and pricks from thorns." The word is doing precise sensory work: it anchors the magical transition in ordinary physical pain. Narnia does not arrive as soft wonder but as a bramble-scratched, nettle-stung immediate reality, and the tactile shape of the word (its double /t/, its clipped two syllables) mimes the sharp, smarting touch of the plant. It is bodily, concrete, and faintly comic — the heroic transport is delayed while the children pick stings out of their ankles. The tone is one of physical inconvenience rather than real danger: the nettle is the least glamorous of plant hazards, so the word quietly undercuts any too-solemn reading of the scene. It also secretly flatters the children: getting stung is proof that they are really, corporeally there in the wood. 2. Polysemy — the word's three lives in modern English. The noun has stayed rock-steady for a thousand years, but it has branched into a verb and into technical registers:
  • The plant (primary sense). Any of the herbs of the genus Urtica — chiefly the stinging nettle Urtica dioica and the annual Urtica urens (the "small nettle," fiercer for its size) — plus the look-alike dead nettle (Lamium spp.), which does not sting, and the white and purple dead nettles grown in gardens. The family name Urticaceae is formed from Latin urtica, "nettle," itself from urere, "to burn."
  • The verb (from the 16th century). To nettle someone is to irritate, provoke, or annoy them, as if stung: "His casual remark nettled her." From the verb come the adjective nettlesome ("irritating, provoking, prickly") and the past participle nettled ("annoyed, piqued, vexed").
  • Technical and botanical terms. Nettle-rash is the everyday name for urticaria, the burning, itching skin eruption — medical Latin urticaria being formed from the same urtica, "nettle." Nettle fibre and nettle cloth are the sustainable bast-fibre textiles now revived from ancient practice. Nettle water is a nitrogen-rich liquid fertiliser that gardeners brew from soaked nettle leaves. Urtication is the historical medical practice of whipping a limb with nettles to stimulate circulation.
3. Synonym boundaries. Nettle is constantly paired with thorn, but the two hurt in opposite ways. A thorn is a hard, woody spike that wounds by piercing — it draws blood and breaks off in the skin. A nettle has no sharp edge at all; its sting is chemical, delivered by injected irritants (histamine, formic acid, acetylcholine) that raise a burning, itching rash. A bramble (the blackberry bush, Rubus) wounds with backward-curving prickles that snag clothing and rip the skin; a thistle is a coarse spiny weed whose prickles are passive armour, with no stinging apparatus. Only the nettle actively "stings" in the allergic sense, which is why the word for the plant became the word for any sharp, smarting irritation. 4. The historical semantic bridges. The bridge from plant to verb is transparent: the plant's chemical sting becomes the figure for any sharp, irritating provocation. The older bridge runs the other way, into utility — the ancestors of the English wove nettle-stems into thread and cloth, and the very name "nettle" descends from a word meaning "the little binder," a plant used for binding and cordage. Modern practice completes a circle: nettle soup, nettle tea, nettle fibre, and the proverb "to grasp the nettle" (to confront a difficulty boldly, since a nettle seized firmly stings far less than one brushed lightly) all return the word to its oldest, most practical and most characterful register. The plant that stings and the plant that binds were, from the start, one and the same.
