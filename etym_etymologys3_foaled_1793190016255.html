The story of foaled begins with one of humanity's oldest partnerships, since the reconstructed Indo-European root *pōlo-, young animal, was already naming the offspring people watched most closely. Its witnesses span the family: Greek pōlos for a foal, Latin pullus for the young of any creature (the ancestor of French poulet and English pullet and poultry), and Germanic *fulan- specialised to the young horse. The Germanic branch shows Grimm's Law in miniature: initial p hardening to f, so that cousins across Europe say the same word with shifted lips — pullus in Rome, fola in Winchester.

Old English fola lived the ordinary life of a farm word, naming colts in pastures and appearing in compounds and glosses without ceremony. The Norman Conquest overlaid French horse vocabulary — courser, palfrey, destrier — for war and status, yet the native fole held the nursery ground: whatever grand French name the warhorse earned, it began life with an English word. By the sixteenth century the noun performed the most English of tricks and verbed itself: mares foal, first recorded around 1600, joining calve, lamb, whelp, and farrow in the farmyard parliament where each species keeps its own birth verb.

The past form foaled might have stayed a humble farm fact had racing not made it golden. James Weatherby's General Stud Book of 1791, recording every thoroughbred's descent, fixed the eternal formula — foaled so, by such a sire, out of such a dam — and with it the economics of blood: a foaling date decides a racehorse's age class, a foaling place its prizes and passports, a foaling prefix its breeder's brand. Two centuries of sale catalogues have chanted the participle until it gleams like brass, and modern breeding science — covering dates, gestation tables, foaling alarms that wake the stud groom at midnight — keeps the old word at the centre of high technology.

Shifts and survivals:

  • Young animal to young horse: the PIE generalist narrowed in Germanic to horse-kind alone, while Latin kept the broad sense that became poultry — one root, two farmyards.
  • Noun to verb: the seventeenth-century conversion turned the baby into the birth, a characteristically English economy also seen in to lamb and to calve.
  • Event to record: the stud book transformed was foaled from something that happened in straw to something inscribed in ledgers, sale rings, and now databases governing millions in bloodstock.
  • Stable to story: wonder tales like Koshchei's borrow the breeder's precision — find where it was foaled — to make the supernatural steed feel pedigreed, as if magic too ran in families.
  • Sound constancy: from fola to foal the word barely changed its shape across twelve centuries, the long vowel gliding but the foal staying recognisably itself.

Literary foals run from Shakespeare's comparisons through Black Beauty's meadow idyll to the Koshchei wonder-horse whose foaling place is a quest object. Few participles carry such commercial weight so lightly: foaled looks like a farm note and functions as a title deed, proving that in horse society, birthplace is destiny.
