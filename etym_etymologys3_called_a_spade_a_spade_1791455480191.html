The phrase called a spade a spade looks like a piece of immemorial English folk wisdom, but its history is bookish, cosmopolitan, and slightly accidental. It descends from a Greek saying recorded by Plutarch, passed through Erasmus’s Latin, and was fixed in English by a Tudor translator. The journey explains both its plain-spoken image and its lingering air of the study rather than the field.

The Greek original

In Plutarch’s Apophthegmata Laconica (“Sayings of the Spartans”), the phrase is attributed to Philip II of Macedon: he is said to have praised men who “call figs figs and a trough a trough” — in Greek, sŵka sŵka, tēn skaphēn skaphēn. The point is the same as the English idiom: name things by their plain names. The objects are humble and domestic, and the repetition gives the saying a proverbial, almost comic bluntness.

  • Greek skaphē: “trough, basin, boat,” a hollowed object.
  • Rhetorical structure: the doubling (“figs figs”) enacts the very refusal of ornament it recommends.
  • Attribution: Plutarch puts it in a royal mouth, giving blunt speech a paradoxical aristocratic pedigree.

Erasmus and the Latin turn

Desiderius Erasmus included the saying in his Apophthegmata (1531), a collection of memorable Classical utterances. Rendering the Greek for Latin readers, he used ligonem ligonem vocare, “to call a mattock a mattock.” A ligo is a hoe or mattock, a digging implement close to a spade. Whether Erasmus was working from a variant text, conflating skaphē with a similar-sounding tool word, or simply choosing the nearest Latin farm implement is debated. What matters is that the tool replaced the trough at this point, and the tool was destined to survive.

Udall and the English fixation

Nicolas Udall translated Erasmus’s collection into English in 1542. His rendering, “to call a spade a spade,” gave the language the form it has kept ever since. The choice of spade rather than mattock may reflect dialect, euphony, or the greater familiarity of the spade; whatever the reason, the English phrase is Udall’s gift. From the sixteenth century onward it appears in sermons, conduct books, and political polemic as a warrant for plain speech.

Reformation and plain style

The phrase thrived in a period that prized “plain style” in religion and politics. Protestant polemicists contrasted their own honest naming of Rome’s errors with what they saw as Catholic verbal evasion. At the same time, humanist education taught the value of perspicuitas, clarity of diction. The spade idiom fitted both currents: it was Classical in origin and bluntly vernacular in effect.

18th and 19th centuries

Enlightenment and Victorian prose use the phrase as a marker of masculine common sense. It appears in parliamentary debate, in letters, and in novels of rural life. The nineteenth century also gave it an imperial and sporting colouring: the plain-speaking Englishman who calls a spade a spade became a national type, contrasted with foreigners imagined to be more given to flourish and evasion.

  • 18th c.: common in political and clerical argument as a claim to honesty.
  • 19th c.: absorbed into the rhetoric of British national character.
  • Australian English: intensified into “call a spade a bloody shovel,” a phrase that adds aggression and comic excess.

The word “spade” itself

The tool noun is much older than the idiom. Old English spadu descends from Proto-Germanic *spadōn, with cognates in Frisian, Dutch, Low German, and German Spaten. It is probably related to a PIE notion of a long, flat piece of wood, and to Greek spathē “blade, spatula.” The idiom thus rests on a genuinely ancient farm word, even though its English form is a Renaissance translation.

Modern usage and controversy

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the phrase is a staple of political journalism: “let’s call a spade a spade” precedes a blunt diagnosis. It can be a genuine appeal to candour or a rhetorical shield for rudeness. A separate complication arose in the 1920s, when “spade” became a derogatory slang term for a Black person, apparently from the black suit in playing cards. That slur is unrelated to the agricultural idiom, but it has made some speakers cautious about the phrase. The historical record is clear: the idiom is attested in 1542, centuries before the slang, and its imagery is that of digging, not race.

Why it lasts

The idiom survives because its image is perfectly matched to its meaning. A spade is ordinary, useful, and slightly dirty; calling it by its name requires no cleverness, only the decision not to pretend. That combination of humility and defiance keeps the phrase available whenever euphemism becomes oppressive.
