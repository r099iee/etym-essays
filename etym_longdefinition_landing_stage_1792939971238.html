Landing-stage names in its plainest sense a broad fixed or floating platform reaching to or over the water, built so that boats can come alongside and people and goods can step between vessel and shore without wading. Piles driven into the riverbed or pontoons riding the tide carry a deck of planks at about gunwale height; steps, rails, bollards, and lamps complete the small architecture of arrival.

In the source sentence the compound arrives as the end of a short journey: a path runs a hundred yards or so to the river-bank, where there was a broad wooden ______. The adjectives do honest work — broad promises room for baskets and feet, wooden promises tarred planks, weather-silvered piles, and a hollow knock underfoot. The reader hears, before any boat appears, the place where water-travel touches land-travel.

Core meaning in detail: A landing-stage differs from its cousins by breadth and business. A jetty or pier may run far out for promenade or deep water; a landing-stage exists to land at — to receive wherries, ferries, barges, and river-steamers, hold them against pile or pontoon while passengers embark, and release them. On tidal rivers the stage often floats, rising and falling with the stream and joined to the bank by a hinged brow or gangway; on quiet reaches it stands fixed on posts. Either way it is working furniture, not ornament.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • River or harbour landing platform (primary, from 19th c.): the broad wooden deck at the bank where boats land passengers and cargo. This is the source sense and the one every dictionary leads with.
  • Floating pontoon stage (technical): a raft-like landing carried on floats or hulks, moored off the bank and reached by a gangway. The bridge is adaptation to tide: where the water rises and falls, the landing must rise and fall too.
  • Ferry and steamer terminus (extended): the named stopping-place of a service — the Westminster landing-stage. The bridge runs from structure to station: the platform lends its name to the timetable point.
  • Construction and scaffolding platform (allied trade sense): builders call a projecting loading platform a landing-stage where hoists land their loads. The bridge is functional analogy: goods land from the hoist as passengers land from the boat.
  • Theatrical and ceremonial platform (rare, figurative): a low stage set for an arrival scene or river-pageant. The bridge is metaphor: any boards where entrances are made become a landing for actors.
  • Moment of transition (figurative, literary): a landing-stage of life, youth, or exile — the boards between one element and another. The bridge carries the timber image into biography: every crossing needs somewhere to step.

Register and connotation: landing-stage is plain Victorian working English — hyphenated, practical, unshowy. It smells of creosote, rope, and river-mud rather than of yachting varnish. Against grander quay and embankment it stays modest and local: the parish’s boards, the ferryman’s pitch, the place where the path meets the tide. Novelists love it for exactly that modesty — an arrival can be staged without a harbour.
