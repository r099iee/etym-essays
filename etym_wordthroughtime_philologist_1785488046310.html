- 17th-century debut (c. 1650s) - borrowed through Latin philologus and French philologue from Greek philologos 'lover of words, fond of learning'; means a student of language and literature, broad and respectable.
- 19th-century golden age - philology becomes the organised science of historical linguistics (comparative grammar, sound change, textual criticism); the philologist reads texts as documents of linguistic and cultural history, a figure of the highest academic prestige.
- 20th-century narrowing - universities split the discipline into literary criticism and linguistics; 'philologist' retreats as a professional title, displaced by 'linguist' and 'historical linguist'.
- Modern register - now somewhat old-fashioned and idealising; used to praise someone who unites literary values with linguistic technicality, evoking the lost unity of the old discipline.
