The story of willow begins with the very quality that makes the tree remarkable: its pliancy. The Indo-European base *wel-, meaning "to turn, roll, wind, wrap", was applied across the ancient world to everything flexible and coiling — to rolling wheels and rolled volumes, to coiled ropes and wrapped sheaths — and, in several branches, to the one tree whose wood twists and bends without snapping. In the Germanic branch the base produced a derived noun with a -g- extension, reconstructable as *walg-ijōn- or *wilig-ōn, "the bending tree", which entered Old English as welig (also wilig), a word recorded from the earliest period of the language. The history of the English form is one of gradual erosion: welig became wilghe in Middle English, then, by the fourteenth century, wilowe, with the loss of the velar consonant and the influence of the common ending -owe reshaping the word's tail; modern willow is the direct descendant of that form. The continental Germanic relatives kept the consonant intact — Dutch wilg, Low German wilge — while the German Weide, ironically the most famous Germanic willow name, belongs to a different root entirely. Meanwhile, on other branches of the Indo-European family, the same base was flowering differently. Latin volvere "to roll" gave the vast Romance and learned English family of revolve, involve, voluble and volume (literally "a rolled scroll"); Greek eilyein "to wrap" produced elytron, the sheath of an insect's wing; and from helissein "to turn, roll" came helikē, the Greek name of the willow itself, "the spiralling tree", cousin of our word helix. In Sanskrit the root appears as valati, "he turns or rolls", with valaya, a ring or bracelet, and probably the creeper-name vallī, which lives on in Hindi as bel or vel, "a twining plant". The willow, in short, is the one tree that the whole Indo-European world agreed to name after motion rather than shape. Key stages and shifts:
  • PIE *wel-: "to turn, roll, wind, wrap" — the root of all the coiling, wrapping words of the family.
  • Proto-Germanic *walg-ijōn- / *wilig-ōn: "the bending tree" — the -g- extended derivative applied to the pliant willow.
  • Old English welig / wilig (8th c.): "willow, osier", recorded in the earliest English texts.
  • Middle English wilghe → wilowe (14th c.): the velar consonant is lost and the ending reshaped.
  • Modern English willow (16th c. onward); willows as the ordinary plural.
In English, by the early modern period, willow had accumulated a second, wholly literary life: from the harps hung upon the willows of Psalm 137 to the drooping "weeping" willow of European poetry, the tree became the very emblem of sorrow, mourning and forsaken love — Ophelia's willow, the willow of the forsaken lover, the willow that bends and sighs by the water. The word therefore works in both registers at once: a precise botanical type of flexible tree used in craft (osiers, withes, cricket bats) and a storehouse of literary sadness. In the context sentence from The Hobbit — "Wind got up, and willows along its banks bent and sighed" — Tolkien draws on both at once: the natural, pliant tree of the riverbank, and the melancholy, sighing tree of poetry. The word's ancient meaning, "the bending tree", is reborn in that image: the willows bend and sigh, named, as they always have been, after their motion.
