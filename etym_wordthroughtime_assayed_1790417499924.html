- 1300s–1400s: Entered Middle English from Anglo-French as assaier, meaning both to test/examine the purity of precious metals and to attempt or dare a difficult physical or military endeavor. - Regarded as a standard term for both royal mint oversight and chivalric trials.
- 1500s–1600s: Widely used in Early Modern English across drama and poetry (e.g., Shakespeare, Spenser) for attempting noble actions and probing human loyalties. - Carried strong poetic, romantic, and technical resonance.
- 1700s–1800s: As essay split off to claim the general sense of "attempt" and the literary genre, assay became increasingly specialized in metallurgy, chemistry, and mining. - Perceived as a precise scientific and commercial discipline of verification.
- 1900s–Present: Cemented in modern biochemistry/pharmacology (e.g., bioassay, immunoassay) while surviving in literary and high-register English as a sophisticated metaphor for analyzing sentiment, character, or subtle discourse.
