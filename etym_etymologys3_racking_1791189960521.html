Racking is the present participle of rack in the sense to torment or strain to the utmost, whose noun rack names both a framework for holding or stretching things and the infamous torture engine that stretched victims by the limbs. The earliest secure forms are Middle Dutch rac and raecke for a frame, rack, or bar, cognate with or borrowed into Middle English rak and rack around 1300 for a hay-rack, a frame for stretching cloth, and soon the torture rack. Before Dutch the trail goes cold: dictionaries propose Proto-Germanic *rak- or *rekk- for a straight pole or stretching, related to reach, rake, reck, but no Old English or Old Norse form securely proves it, so responsible etymology stops at Middle Dutch with a debated-deeper note. It is explicitly distinct from wrack and wreck, ruin and destruction, from Old English wrecan to drive or avenge, with which it is constantly confused.

From frame to torment the shift is metonymy: to put on the rack became to rack, then to rack any person or faculty. By the sixteenth century rack as verb meant to torture judicially, racked and burned, then figuratively to afflict cruelly: racked with pain, racked with guilt, racked with coughing. The collocation rack one's brains and wits appears by the late sixteenth and early seventeenth century in Dekker, Burton, and others, picturing the mind stretched as on the frame to extract an idea. The spelling variant wracking with w arose by confusion with wrack as ruin and is now often preferred for nerves and wits, but racking remains standard, especially in racking pain and in the present context of brains racked for invention.

  • Medieval frame (1300s): agricultural and artisanal noun: hay-rack, cloth-rack, spit-rack; neutral technology.
  • Judicial torture (1400s-1600s): the Rack as engine in the Tower of London; verb to rack as interrogate by stretching; terror and state power.
  • Generalized torment (1500s-1700s): racked by gout, conscience, grief; medical and moral extension in Shakespeare, Donne, Bunyan.
  • Mental exertion (1600s-present): racking brains, wits, invention; self-inflicted intellectual stretching celebrated as ingenuity.
  • Modern participle-adjective: racking cough, sobs, doubt; attributive adjective for anything shaking or straining the frame.
  • Orthographic split: racking versus wracking division of labour debated by style guides; descriptively both live for mental idioms.

Phonetically the word is stable: short ash vowel plus voiceless velar plus velar nasal participle; no Great Vowel Shift effect on the short vowel. Culturally it preserves memory of judicial cruelty inside everyday hyperbole: students say racking brains without picturing the Tower, patients report racking coughs without thinking of the engine. The source sentence redeems the violence as progress: the primitive brain torturing itself to invent tools and machines. Literary attestations include Shakespeare racked lovers, Poe racked nerves, Orwell racked bodies, keeping the torture-frame vivid beneath the common examination-season phrase.
