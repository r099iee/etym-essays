Primary sense — the bird. An ibis is a medium-to-large wading bird of the family Threskiornithidae (about 28 living species), characterised by long legs, a long neck and — above all — a long, slender, down-curved (decurved) bill used to probe mud, sand and shallow water for insects, worms, crustaceans and small fish. Ibises fly with neck and legs outstretched in loose lines or V-formations, feed and nest colonially in marshes, estuaries and riverbanks, and often gather in the long silent rows that have made them a fixture of Nile and tropical imagery since antiquity. The plural “ibises” is the ordinary English plural (Latin and Greek used ibides; English simply adds -es) and is used of flocks, of species collectively, and of the birds as a class — as in Wilde’s red ibises standing in rows on the banks of the Nile.

  • The sacred ibis of Egypt. The sacred ibis (Threskiornis aethiopicus) — white plumage, bare black head and neck, black curved bill — was holy in ancient Egypt and appears in Egyptian art from the Old Kingdom onward. It was the living emblem of Thoth, god of wisdom, writing, measurement and the moon, who is shown ibis-headed with a scribe’s palette; the ibis hieroglyph writes the bird’s name and, in compounds, Thoth’s. The Egyptians kept, and in later periods bred, ibises for mummification as votive offerings; vast catacombs at Saqqara and Tuna el-Gebel hold the mummies, and a 2019 ancient-DNA analysis (Wasef et al.) showed that most were wild-caught birds sacrificed to the god — evidence of a truly industrial devotion. Greek writers made the ibis famous as the serpent-killer: Herodotus (Histories 2.75–76) describes how the ibises destroy the winged serpents that invade Egypt from the desert, and Pliny (Natural History 10.87) repeats the admiring lore of the self-purging ibis that uses its long bill to cleanse its own body.
  • Species and range. Modern ibises are cosmopolitan: the glossy ibis (Plegadis falcinellus) is a dark bronzy wader found across Europe, Africa, Asia, Australia and the Americas; the white ibis and the brilliant scarlet ibis (Eudocimus ruber) live in the New World tropics; the crested ibis (Nipponia nippon) of China and Japan was rescued from near-extinction in the 1980s and is a national conservation emblem; and in Australia the white ibis has become the urban “bin chicken” of parklands and tips — a sacred name attached to a rubbish-bin raider. Wilde’s “red ibises” blend the scarlet species of the Americas with the Nile setting of the sacred ibis — poetic licence perfectly in keeping with the story’s orientalism.
  • Cultural afterlife. The ibis motif — that curved bill, that crested head — is a favourite of Egyptian Revival jewellery and Art Deco design; the ibis appears in heraldry and hotel branding (the Ibis hotel chain takes its logo from the bird); the scarlet ibis gives James Hurst his celebrated 1960 short story “The Scarlet Ibis”, in which the exotic bird stands for beauty that cannot survive its environment; and the University of Miami’s mascot Sebastian the Ibis is one of the few bird mascots in American college sport.
  • Word-shape. “Ibis” forms its plural “ibises” in English (the Latinate “ibides” is rare); it is used attributively in “ibis-bill”, “ibis-wood”, and ornithological compounds; and it contrasts within its family with the spoonbills, which feed with flat spatulate bills instead of probing curved ones. The word is one of the few common English bird-names of direct Egyptian origin.

Semantic bridge. Every sense of “ibises” is anchored to Egypt: the word is Egyptian (hb), the bird’s fame is Egyptian (Thoth, the mummies, the serpent-wars), and even the scientific family name Threskiornithidae means “religious-worship bird” (Greek thrēskeia, “worship”, + ornis, “bird”) because the sacred ibis gave the family its genus. From “holy Nile wader” the plural “ibises” reaches outward to all the curved-bill waders of the world — the oldest sanctity in ornithology.
