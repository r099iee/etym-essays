- [14th c. — the acrobat] “Tumbler” names the court and fairground entertainer who flips, leaps, and somersaults; perception: an admirable wonder-worker, half-buffoon.
- [c. 1660 — the drinking glass] Glasses with rounded bases appear; they cannot stand, so the drinker drains them at one draught or watches them tumble — the vessel inherits the acrobat's name.
- [17th–18th c. — locks and pigeons] Lockmakers call the falling lever a “tumbler”; fanciers breed “tumbler pigeons” that somersault in flight; the word quietly becomes a homonym of parts and breeds.
- [19th–20th c. — industry] Tumbler switches, tumble dryers, rock tumblers, and foundry test barrels multiply the mechanical senses; the acrobat sense retreats to circus and historical usage.
- [21st c. — the portable cup] The insulated travel “tumbler” becomes a global commodity word; the acrobatic origin is forgotten, and the glass sense rules everyday speech.
