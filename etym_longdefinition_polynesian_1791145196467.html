Polynesian as an adjective means of or relating to Polynesia — the vast triangular region of the central and southern Pacific (Hawaii, Easter Island, New Zealand outward) — and its peoples, languages, and cultures; as a noun it means a native or inhabitant of Polynesia or a speaker of a Polynesian language.

In everyday use Polynesian chief, as in the source sentence, evokes nineteenth-century European travel writing about Tahiti, Samoa, Tonga, Hawaii, and Māori New Zealand: tattooed chieftainship, navigation, and island custom. Anthropologically it is precise: Polynesians form a closely related ethnolinguistic group within Austronesian, sharing closely cognate languages (Tahitian, Samoan, Tongan, Hawaiian, Māori), outrigger-canoe navigation, and hierarchical chiefdoms (ariki/aliʻi).

Polysemy and extended senses:

  • Geographical: of Polynesia proper (Polynesian Triangle). Example: Polynesian islands. Strict sense, contrasted with Melanesian and Micronesian.
  • Ethnic: of Polynesian descent. Example: Polynesian community in Auckland. Modern identity sense, often capitalised.
  • Linguistic: of the Polynesian subgroup of Oceanic Austronesian. Example: Polynesian languages, Proto-Polynesian. Technical sense used by linguists; includes subdivisions Tongic vs Nuclear Polynesian.
  • Cultural: characteristic of Polynesian culture — voyaging, tattoo (tatau), hula/ori, tapa cloth, umu/hangi earth oven. Example: Polynesian dance, Polynesian tattoo. Also commercialised in Tiki culture.
  • Physical-anthropological (dated): nineteenth-century writers used Polynesian as a racial type. Now obsolete and offensive if used racially; modern usage prefers ancestry/culture.
  • Commercial-kitsch: Polynesian sauce, Polynesian restaurant, Polynesian resort — mid-twentieth-century American Tiki appropriation. Loose, exoticising extension, often criticised as stereotyping.
  • Biological: Polynesian rat (Rattus exulans), Polynesian chestnut, etc. — species distributed by Polynesian voyagers. Technical biogeographical sense.

Grammar: adjective (Polynesian culture) and countable noun (a Polynesian, Polynesians). Capitalised for people/region/language; lower-case only in derived commercial coinages is discouraged. Pronunciation stresses third syllable. Near neighbours: Melanesian (western Pacific, darker-skinned stereotype in old literature), Micronesian (north-western small islands) — the three-part division coined in the 1830s by Dumont d'Urville, retained with qualification.

Register is neutral-descriptive in scholarship and identity discourse, but literary-colonial examples like the source (cannibal anecdote via Pierre Loti) reflect an older exoticising gaze now read critically. Modern careful usage names specific peoples — Tahitian, Samoan, Native Hawaiian, Māori — rather than generic Polynesian where possible, and respects self-identification (e.g. Pasifika, Kānaka Maoli).

For study purposes, anchor Polynesian to the Triangle plus language proof: if peoples speak mutually recognisable Polynesian languages descended from Proto-Polynesian and trace voyaging ancestry to western Polynesia (Tonga-Samoa), they are Polynesian in the strict sense. This test excludes Fijians (largely Melanesian with Polynesian overlay) and includes outliers like Tikopia and Kapingamarangi outside the Triangle. Holding this linguistic anchor prevents the word drifting into vague tropical kitsch and keeps usage respectful and precise.
