- Early Modern learned borrowing (16th c.) - Rare, bookish Latinism used by scholars and moralists to condemn gluttony; connotation elevated, disapproving, distant from everyday hunger.
- 17th–18th c. natural history - Adopted by naturalists for animal and elemental devouring (sharks, fire); perception shifts from sin to natural fact, still awe-tinged and fearful.
- 19th c. anthropological use - Used for primitive savagery and pre-civilised appetite (as in Durant); connotation primitivist — voracity marks the beast-in-man before fire, cooking, and farming.
- Late 19th–20th c. metaphorical widening - Extends to mind and market: voracious reader (admiring), corporate voracity (condemning); perception splits into positive intellectual hunger vs. negative economic greed.
- Contemporary usage - Formal, literary word; literal sense (overeating) still known but figurative senses dominate; connotation intense, slightly archaic, morally loaded — signals excess that alarms or impresses.
