The biography of grumpy begins not in writing but in sound. Its ultimate ancestor is the echoic Germanic base *grum-, an imitation of the low, muffled rumble of a growl or a grunt of dissatisfaction — the kind of noise a bad-tempered person makes under the breath rather than in words. This base appears in Middle Dutch grommen, "to growl, mutter", whose frequentative formation grommelen gave Middle French grommeler, and from that continental source English took the verb grumble in the 1580s. For two centuries grumble flourished as the ordinary word for voicing discontent in a low, surly murmur. But out of grumble grew, in the dialect speech of eighteenth-century England, the clipped back-formation grump — first a verb meaning "to sulk", then a noun naming a fit of ill humour or the surly person who suffers one. From grump the adjective was formed by the ordinary suffix -y (as in sleepy, stony): grumpish appears by about 1725, and the now-standard grumpy is recorded from 1778. The semantic movement is from sound to disposition: what begins as an audible growl becomes, by degrees, a silent state of being. By the time grumpy reaches modern English it no longer needs a noise at all — a grumpy person may say nothing whatsoever, and the word names the mood rather than the mutter. Key stages and shifts:
  • The echoic base *grum-: the low, muffled rumble of growls and grunts of dissatisfaction, independently formed across the Germanic and Romance dialects.
  • 1580s: grumble enters English from the continental echoic family, meaning to rumble low or to murmur discontent.
  • Early 18th century: the clipped dialectal back-formation grump arises — verb "to be sulky", noun "a fit of ill humour" and "a surly person".
  • c. 1725: grumpish, the first adjectival form.
  • 1778: grumpy, the standard modern adjective, is recorded.
The suffix itself deserves a word. English -y is one of the oldest and most productive Germanic suffixes for making adjectives from nouns, and it carries a specific tone: sleepy, stony, windy, hearty. Attached to grump it produced not merely a description of bad temper but a characterisation of a person as one who has a grump inside them, rather as a windy day has wind inside it. This is why grumpy feels so homely: unlike the Latinate surly or the heavy sullen, it is built from a native noun by a native suffix, and its whole sound and shape belong to the ordinary speech of kitchen and hearth. The word in society and literature: the little family deserves note. Grim, far older, descends from a different though possibly allied Indo-European base meaning "to be angry" (PIE *gʰrem-, Proto-Germanic *grimmaz, Old English grim), and grunt shares the echoic habit of the family without belonging to the grumble lineage. Grumpy itself entered literary English as a homely, affectionate word, more apt to raise a smile than a shudder, and that is exactly the tone Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit. His company has been soaked for a day: hoods dripping, cloaks sodden, the pony stumbling on stones. To say the dwarves were "too grumpy to talk" is to treat their magnificent discomfort with the kindly irony of a fireside storyteller. The word's recentness is part of its effect: it is a modern, domestic, comic word for a bad temper that never quite becomes anger, and it lets the reader smile at the drenched travellers while sharing their misery — a grumpiness that is a mood, not a menace.
