- [late 14th century: roving] - The isolate verb prollen (c. 1386) means "to rove stealthily, to move about in search" — a word of obscure, native origin with no known kin.
- [c. 1500: the spelling] - An inorganic -w- gives prowl, though the word is still pronounced "prôle" well into the 18th century; Scots and northern dialects keep prole and provle.
- [1580: the quartering] - A transitive sense appears — to prowl over an area, going stealthily across ground in search of prey or plunder — and the agent noun prowler follows by the 1510s.
- [19th century: on the prowl] - The phrase "on the prowl" is fixed by 1803; the prowler slips from beast-of-prey to burglar and, by the 20th century, to the night-time sexual predator — the word darkens and loses neutrality.
- [Contemporary sense] - Prowling is roving, opportunistic hunting with no fixed quarry — literal big-cat patience and human menace at once, always carrying stealth and hunger.
