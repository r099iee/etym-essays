- [8th–10th c.] - Old English forms the adjective *Cornwielisc / Cornwylisc* from the Celtic tribal root *Corn-* ('horn-dwellers') and Germanic *-wielisc / -isc* ('foreign, Celtic, Welsh') - [perception: the distinct Celtic Britons holding the southwestern peninsula against Saxon Wessex]

- [11th–14th c.] - Middle English refines the name into *Cornyssh / Cornisshe*, establishing Cornwall as a semi-autonomous duchy rich in tin mining and home to the living Celtic tongue *Kernewek* - [perception: proud maritime and mining people with a thriving native language and miraculous mystery plays]

- [15th–17th c.] - British chroniclers and folklorists (e.g., Geoffrey of Monmouth, Michael Drayton) cement the image of Cornwall as an enchanted land of giant-killers (*Jack the Giant Killer*), King Arthur's court at Tintagel, and legendary Cornish ogres - [perception: mythical, rugged borderland steeped in Arthurian romance and giant lore]

- [18th–19th c.] - The Industrial Revolution makes the *Cornish engine* and Cornish tin miners world-famous, while Victorian literature and fairy tales (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Selfish Giant*) celebrate the colorful folklore of *Cornish* ogres - [perception: industrial engineering pioneers paired with romanticized Celtic folklore]

- [20th–21st c.] - Cornish language (*Kernewek*) undergoes a dramatic cultural revival, and Cornish heritage gains official recognition as a distinct Celtic national minority within the UK - [perception: celebrated Celtic cultural identity, artistic haven, and vibrant heritage]
