shod is the past tense and past participle of the verb to shoe, and as an adjective it means "wearing shoes or other foot-coverings; furnished with footwear; fitted with protective coverings for the feet." The word describes a completed, equipped state: feet — human or animal — finished with a covering of leather, cloth, or iron. It is a strong participle in the modern sense only by custom; historically it is the old weak past participle of a verb built on the noun "shoe," and it has kept an archaic, slightly elevated flavour in modern prose. In the passage from C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian that generated this card, "shod" is loaded with stealth. Caspian and Doctor Cornelius are not merely wearing shoes — they are "both shod so that they made almost no noise." The word here carries a tactical, hushed tone: the soft buskins just given to Caspian are the acoustic equipment of an escape, converting bare feet (or echoing formal footwear) into silent instruments. Lewis uses the archaic, slightly elevated participle rather than the plainer "wearing shoes," which lends the sentence the flavour of romance and old-fashioned chronicle. "Shod" also quietly signals preparedness: a shod man is equipped, outfitted for the road or the night, as a horse is "shod" for hard travel. The warmth of the buskins and the muffling of the robes converge on the same idea — feet made comfortable, concealed, and quiet.

Principal senses (polysemy). Shod is not a one-note word. Its senses branch from a single core image — a protective covering fitted to the feet — into several distinct provinces:

  • Human footwear (primary sense): "wearing shoes or other foot-coverings." The plain literal core: "warmly shod," "well shod for the winter," "lightly shod." Here shod functions as a passive participle of the verb to shoe and as a pure adjective describing the feet's state.
  • Farriery (the horse-sense): "fitted with horseshoes." For the equine world shod is the everyday technical word — "a shod horse," "the mare was newly shod," "shod with iron." Because farriery was a daily reality for centuries, the horse-sense kept the word alive in ordinary speech long after "shod" for humans had begun to sound archaic; it is arguably the most frequent modern use.
  • Figurative "equipped, furnished, prepared": from furnishing the feet, shod extends to being outfitted in general. The metaphor survives in fixed compounds and phrases such as "shod with" (furnished or armed with: "shod with iron," "shod with steel") and in transferred uses like a walking-staff "shod with a metal ferrule" or a pole "iron-shod." The semantic thread is always protective reinforcement of an extremity.
  • Idiomatic prosperity and class markers: "well-shod" and "poorly shod" have long been shorthand for social standing, footwear being among the most visible signs of wealth and respectability; the collocation is recorded from the sixteenth century. To be "ill shod" was to be exposed, humble, unprepared.
  • The fossil idiom "roughshod": the adjective survives chiefly in the idiom "to ride roughshod over" (to trample something with contemptuous disregard). Historically a rough-shod horse wore shoes with projecting nail-heads or sharp points roughened for grip on ice and hard roads; the idiom images a nail-studded hoof trampling opposition — a fossil of the farrier's craft now used almost entirely metaphorically, and one of the most vivid of all horse-words in modern English.
  • Transferred and technical uses: in industry and craft, shod describes anything fitted with a hard wearing tip, casing, or shoe-like part: the "shod" end of a sounding-line or pile, "brass-shod" furniture feet, "rubber-shod" tools, the metal shoe of a brake pad. The same core — a fitted protective covering — is extended mechanically.

Register and distribution. In modern English shod is deliberately formal and often consciously literary or archaic. Ordinary speakers say "wearing shoes" or "put shoes on"; the participle is reserved for special effects — the horse-world, elevated or fairy-tale prose, and the figurative idioms. Its use in Prince Caspian is exactly that: the word supplies period flavour, hushed tension, and the romance of an older world, all in a single syllable. In negation ("unshod," "barefoot") it carries the same weight: the unshod foot is the vulnerable, earth-touching, primitive foot.

Near-synonyms and boundaries.

  • Booted is the close sibling but implies high, rigid footwear with military or riding associations; it lacks the quiet-preparedness sense and can never describe an animal — a horse is shod, never booted.
  • Footed describes how a creature walks or what its feet are like ("sure-footed"), not what covers them; it is a weak, mostly poetic near-synonym.
  • Sandaled names a specific open foot-covering, narrower and lighter than the general shod, and is rarely used of animals.
  • Sheathed images the foot as a blade in a scabbard; it works only figuratively and loses shod's practical, completed-furnishing idea.
  • Covered and clothed are far too general; they say nothing of feet or of being equipped for walking.
  • Equipped / furnished / fitted are generic "provided with what is needed" words; shod is the foot- and hoof-specific member of the set.
  • Barefoot is the true antonym and sets the whole contrast: to be shod is to be separated from the raw ground by a layer of crafted material, whereas barefoot denotes direct, unprotected contact.

The nuance that distinguishes shod from all its near-synonyms is the completed-furnishing idea yoked to the foot or hoof — a quiet, thorough, old-fashioned word for readiness that even "equipped" cannot match.
