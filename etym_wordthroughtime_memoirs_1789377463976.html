- Late 15th Century (Late Middle English / Early Tudor) - Borrowed into English from Old and Middle French *mémoire*, initially functioning as a written memorandum, note of record, or commemorative inscription created to preserve institutional memory.
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Early Modern English) - Expanded across diplomacy and natural philosophy to designate official state briefs, diplomatic position papers, and transactions of learned societies (such as the philosophical memoirs of European academies).
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Solidified into a dominant European literary genre (under the influence of French court writers like Saint-Simon and Retz), celebrated as an elegant, subjective window into political intrigue, aristocratic salons, and military campaigns.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely popularized among public figures, explorers, and statesmen, while simultaneously acquiring a satirical connotation of vanity and self-aggrandizement (as mocked by Oscar Wilde in portraits of pompous speakers who talk as if dictating memoirs).
- 20th to 21st Century (Modern & Contemporary Era) - Underwent a democratic revolution in publishing, moving beyond famous politicians to become a preeminent form of creative nonfiction, embracing intimate personal narratives, trauma, identity, and literary experimentation.
