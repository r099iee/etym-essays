- The body's side — the word enters Middle English (late 14th c.) purely anatomical: the fleshy side of man or beast between ribs and hip; the heaving flanks of the ridden horse are the early texts' favourite image.
- The soldier's side — 16th-century warfare converts it: the flank of an army, the flank attack, flanking fire; 'to flank' (1590s) means both to guard a side and to strike it from the side.
- Generalisation — 17th–18th centuries: the flank of a mountain, a glacier, a building, a column of men — every lateral surface borrows the word; tactical idiom (flanking march, exposed flank, oblique order) matures in the age of Marlborough, Frederick and Napoleon.
- Modern spread — flank steak (19th c.) in the butchery, 'flanker' (1880s) in rugby, 'flank speed' (20th c.) in the US Navy, the flank as a zoological and clinical landmark; 'cover your flanks' enters everyday idiom.
- Today — 'flanks' is the standard word for exposed sides everywhere: anatomy, military science, sport, business and epic battle narrative ('His flanks were unguarded'), always carrying the undertone of vulnerability.
