Single root word: deprecatingly, the adverbial form of the English verb deprecate. Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *prek- "to ask, beg, pray, entreat" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives include Vedic Sanskrit praccháti "he asks", Avestan pərəsaiti "he asks", Old Irish arco "I ask", Gothic fraihnan "to ask", Latin poscō "I demand, ask for" (from *prek-sk-). 2. Latin precārī (deponent verb) "to pray, beg, entreat" — classical, attested from the 1st millennium BCE; alongside prex "prayer, entreaty" and precārius "obtained by asking, uncertain" (source of English precarious). 3. Latin dēprecārī "to pray (something) away, avert by entreaty, plead against, deprecate; to excuse oneself" — dē- "away, down from" + precārī — attested in classical prose (Cicero); noun dēprecātiō "aversion by prayer; plea for pardon". 4. Latin dēprecātus / dēprecāns "prayed off, averted by entreaty; entreating against" — past and present participles, classical. 5. English deprecate "to pray against, to express earnest disapproval of" — borrowed from Latin dēprecātus, first attested c. 1620s. 6. English deprecating "expressing disapproval; (later) humbly self-disparaging" — present participle, attested from the 17th century; the reflexive self-deprecating is recorded from the early 20th century. 7. English deprecatingly — deprecating + adverb suffix -ly, "in a deprecating manner" — attested from the 19th century. Final node: deprecatingly. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The PIE root *prek- "ask, pray" is well established across the Indo-European languages. The interesting nuance is semantic rather than formal: English moved the family from "pray against / formally protest" (17th century) to "self-effacingly modest" (20th century), a drift driven by politeness culture rather than by any change in form. A usage debate also swirls around the spelling self-deprecating vs. the frequent misspelling self-deprecating; the standard form follows the verb deprecate. Where a stage is unknown none has been invented; the chain above is fully attested at every step from Latin onward.
