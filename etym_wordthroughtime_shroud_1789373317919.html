- 8th to 11th Century (Old English) - Recorded as *scrūd*, signifying an ordinary article of clothing, everyday garment, vesture, or general protective fabric covering.
- 12th to 14th Century (Middle English) - Formed as *shroud* / *schroud*; gradually bifurcated between general clothing, naval standing rigging (the ropes supporting a mast), and the specific custom of wrapping the dead in a linen winding-sheet.
- 15th to 16th Century - The general meaning of "everyday clothing" died out (supplanted by *garments*, *clothes*, and *apparel*), leaving *shroud* firmly specialized as the mortuary burial sheet and the maritime mast-rigging.
- 17th Century (Jacobean & Restoration) - Enriched by metaphysical poets and dramatists (Shakespeare, Donne, Milton); the verb *to shroud* expanded dynamically into figurative realms of enveloping, veiling in darkness, and shielding secrets.
- 18th to 19th Century (Gothic & Romanticism) - Became a quintessential motif of Gothic terror and Romantic melancholy (Baudelaire, Poe, Shelley); concurrently established in early mechanical engineering as protective casings and funnel coverings.
- 20th to 21st Century - Broadly diversified across aerospace engineering (payload fairing shrouds for spacecraft), turbofan jet engines (acoustic and fan shrouds), nuclear reactor core shrouds, and forensics (the Turin Shroud), while maintaining its profound literary role as the archetypal symbol of mortality and concealment.
