prejudices is the plural of prejudice: preconceived judgments, opinions held before or without sufficient evidence, especially leanings for or against persons, parties, creeds or customs. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita opening his account of Toryism, I have never been able to trace these prejudices to any royalty of descent — these prejudices are his violent Toryism of the old school, his love of kings, Scott and Homer, his desire for sweet pippins and Living Kings. He calls them prejudices with disarming irony: what a Liberal age dismisses as irrational bias he cherishes as inherited affection, yet honestly admits he cannot ground it in blood — his father’s ancestors unknown, his maternal grandmother a Croydon inn landlady. The plural matters: not one prejudice but a bundle — monarchy, church, home, Scott — confessed together.

In general modern use prejudices almost always means biases, usually invidious: judgments that pre-judge. One has prejudices against foreigners, against a class, in favour of one’s school; one harbours, nurses, betrays, overcomes or confirms prejudices; a trial is prejudiced, a jury is prejudiced, an employer is prejudiced. The word is countable in the plural (this prejudice, those prejudices) and uncountable in the singular abstract (prejudice spoils judgment). Moral colour is normally adverse: to call something a prejudice is to condemn it as irrational and unfair, though writers from Burke to Ruskin defend prejudices as the latent wisdom of affection and habit — prejudice renders a man’s virtue his habit.

  • Core social-moral sense: preconceived adverse opinion, bias: e.g. racial prejudices, class prejudices, to be free from prejudice, blinded by prejudice. This is the head sense in modern dictionaries. The semantic bridge from Latin praeiudicium (a prior judgment, a precedent at law) is straightforward: a judgment made prae- (before) the evidence becomes a judgment against evidence. Law gives the metaphor (pre-judging a case), society generalises it to persons.
  • Favourableanted sense: preconceived liking, loyalty, partiality: e.g. his prejudices in favour of old schools, a prejudice for country over town, Ruskin’s Tory prejudices. Eighteenth-century writers use prejudice neutrally for any prepossession: Hume writes of prejudices of education, Johnson of innocent prejudices. The bridge is the same etymology without moral condemnation — a judgment formed before reasoning, which may be affectionate and true. Ruskin deliberately revives this older, Burkean sense while acknowledging the modern condemnatory overtone.
  • Legal-technical sense: detriment, injury to rights (without prejudice): e.g. to act to the prejudice of a claim, without prejudice to further proceedings, a letter written without prejudice. Here prejudice is harm or impairment, and without prejudice means without loss of rights, often in settlement negotiations. The bridge is Roman law: praeiudicium was a preliminary inquiry whose finding prejudged the main action and could harm a party; hence prejudice as damage. This sense survives chiefly in lawyers’ formulae, not everyday plurals.
  • Philosophical and psychological senses: epistemology speaks of prejudices as pre-judgments that make understanding possible (Gadamer’s rehabilitation: all interpretation starts from pre-judices); psychology measures implicit prejudices, prejudice reduction, contact hypotheses. The bridge is descriptive rather than condemnatory: the mind must anticipate before it can perceive, and prejudices name those anticipations, good and bad.
  • Verbal and adjectival kin: to prejudice is to bias or to harm (the delay prejudiced his case); prejudiced means biased (a prejudiced referee); prejudicial means harmful to a fair trial or interest (prejudicial publicity); unprejudiced means impartial. All keep the double thread of unfair opinion and material harm.
  • Stylistic note: prejudice in the singular abstract condemns (victim of prejudice); prejudices in the plural confesses or catalogues (his prejudices were Tory, rural and literary). Ruskin’s plural invites inventory and forgiveness; modern anti-bias prose prefers the singular mass-noun for condemnation.
