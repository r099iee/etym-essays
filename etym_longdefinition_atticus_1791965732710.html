Atticus here is a proper name, not a common noun: it denotes Titus Pomponius Atticus (110–32 BCE), the Roman equestrian, Epicurean, man of business and letters who was Cicero's closest friend and most intimate correspondent. The name by which he is known is an agnomen, a distinguishing surname awarded for his long residence in Athens: Latin Atticus means "of Attica, Athenian". In the sentence at hand — "he encouraged his friend Atticus in this pursuit" — the reference is to Atticus's role in the publication of Cicero's letters, a role that made him, in effect, the first great literary publisher of the Western tradition.

Who he was

  • Birth and family: born in Rome in 110 BCE into the Pomponii, an old and wealthy equestrian family. He was educated in Rome with Cicero and with Lucius Manlius Torquatus, and remained on terms of intimacy with both for life.
  • Athens: in 86 BCE, during the civil violence of Marius and Cinna, he moved to Athens and stayed for roughly twenty years. He took no part in Roman politics, devoted himself to philosophy and antiquarianism, and earned the nickname Atticus — "the Athenian" — which stuck.
  • Wealth and independence: he inherited a large estate from his uncle Quintus Caecilius, who adopted him, and added to it by prudent management and by lending. He remained an eques, refusing the senatorial career and the offices that went with it, and so preserved a position of independence in an age when independence was almost impossible.
  • Epicureanism: he was a professed follower of Epicurus, and his withdrawal from public life was in part a philosophical choice: the garden rather than the forum, friendship rather than ambition.
  • Death: he died in 32 BCE at the age of seventy-seven. Cornelius Nepos, his biographer, records that when an incurable illness made his life unbearable he refused food and so ended it — a death consistent with the Epicurean calm he had cultivated.

Why he matters in this reading

The passage concerns the publication of Cicero's correspondence. Cicero wrote to Atticus for decades — the surviving collection, the Epistulae ad Atticum, runs to sixteen books and covers the years 68 to 44 BCE — and in those letters he wrote with a freedom he allowed himself nowhere else: gossip, panic, calculation, affection, literary criticism, and the raw politics of the fall of the Republic. Atticus preserved them, and the passage suggests that he did so with an eye to publication, and that he encouraged his friend in the project. Without Atticus there would be no Ad Atticum, and without the Ad Atticum our picture of Cicero — and of the last generation of the Roman Republic — would be immeasurably poorer.

Atticus was also a publisher in the concrete ancient sense. Wealthy Romans kept households of trained slaves, librarii, who copied texts by hand, and Atticus's establishment was known for the accuracy of its copies. Cicero relied on him to have his works reproduced and circulated, and the relationship illustrates how literature moved in the ancient world: not through a market of booksellers in the modern sense, but through networks of friendship, patronage and household labour.

A man of letters in his own right

  • He wrote a Liber annalis, a chronological outline of Roman history down to his own day, which Cicero used and praised.
  • He composed an account of Cicero's consulship in Greek, a gesture of friendship as much as of scholarship.
  • He wrote works on the genealogies and portraits of the great Roman families, drawing on the imagines preserved in their halls.
  • He was a collector and connoisseur of art and antiquities, and a patron of Athens itself, where his generosity was commemorated.

The name and its significance

Roman names carried meaning. A citizen had a praenomen, a nomen and a cognomen, and a fourth element, the agnomen, could be added for a distinction — a victory, a conquest, or, as here, a place of residence. Atticus belongs to the class of place-agnomina alongside Africanus, Germanicus, Creticus and Numidicus, but with a difference: those commemorated conquests, while Atticus commemorated a voluntary exile and a love of Greek culture. The name is thus a small emblem of the man — a Roman who chose Athens.

Modern reference

Atticus survives in English in several directions. The name is now a moderately common given name, largely because of Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird (1960), whose Atticus Finch is a lawyer of quiet moral courage rather than a Roman publisher. In classical scholarship he is a permanent presence through the Epistulae ad Atticum and through Nepos's Life of Atticus. In the history of the book he is remembered as the friend who copied, kept and circulated the words of the ancient world's greatest orator.
