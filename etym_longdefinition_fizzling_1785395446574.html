FIZZLING is the present participle of the verb fizzle, and it describes making a weak, sputtering, hissing sound — the thin, damp protest of something burning or discharging badly — and, by extension, failing or dying out after an unpromising or half-hearted start. A fizzling thing does not go out silently or all at once; it spits, sputters, and hisses feebly, throwing off small bursts of sound and light that promise far more than they deliver, until it subsides into nothing. The word is applied above all to weak flames — a fizzling torch, a fizzling candle, wet gunpowder, a damp fuse — and, in its dominant modern use, to plans, careers, romances, and movements that begin with noise and end with a whimper: a performance that fizzles out, an economy that fizzles, a boom that fizzled before it ever caught fire. In the goblins' torch-song of J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit — 'A fizzling torch / To light the night / for our delight, Ya hey!' — the participle paints a lamp so weak it barely serves its own purpose. A torch that fizzles does not burn cleanly or cast a steady glow; it hisses, spits, and sputters, throwing off bursts of damp, uncertain light that promise far more gloom than illumination. The word is doing triple work here: its buzzing, sibilant onset (fiz-) mimics the sound of water meeting flame; its frequentative -le and present participial -ing build the action as small, repeated, and faintly pathetic; and its modern afterlife — every enterprise that 'fizzles out' — quietly supplies the song with a comic-deflationary irony, as though even the goblins' fire can only manage to fail. The tone is mock-heroic in reverse: the goblins sing of delight while their torch cannot even light the night, and the very word becomes a joke at their own expense — a miserable, rain-damp flame that embodies the pitch-black setting of the whole scene. The full polysemy of the word:
  • Weak sputtering sound (acoustic core): "the fizzling fuse", "a fizzling candle", "fizzling fireworks" — the thin, repeated hiss of something damp or defective burning or discharging.
  • Weak flame (visual core): "a fizzling torch", "the fizzling embers" — a flame that spits and flickers without burning steadily, close to going out.
  • Fizzle out (figurative failure, the dominant modern sense): "the campaign fizzled out", "his career fizzled", "the celebration fizzled before midnight" — to fail or end weakly after a promising or loud start, first attested in American English in 1848.
  • Of electrical or mechanical discharge (technical slang): "the fizzling speaker", "a fizzling connection" — a crackling, sputtering sound of bad contact or interference.
  • Of carbonated liquid (informal): a soda that fizzles is one audibly releasing gas, a sense folded into the broader fizz family — "fizzling soda".
  • Fizzling as adjectival participle: "a fizzling performance", "fizzling hopes" — anything that sputters toward failure carries the word's damp, deflationary colouring.
  • Humour and comic deflation: the word's register is inherently deflationary; to call anything fizzling is to mock it as damp, feeble, and destined to fail — the opposite of blazing or sizzling.
The neighbouring words mark out fizzling's exact territory:
  • Hissing: similar in being a high-pitched stream of escaping sound; differs because hissing is continuous, steady, and often deliberate, with no implication of weakness or extinction — a kettle hisses indefinitely without failing.
  • Sputtering: similar in naming a rapid series of small explosive bursts; differs because sputtering stops at the noise and says nothing about the outcome, while fizzling implies a flame or venture that is failing or dying out.
  • Sizzling: similar in the hissing sound of something hot; differs because sizzling is the loud, appetizing hiss of fat in a hot pan — energetic, attractive, almost celebratory, the exact opposite of fizzling in spirit.
  • Crackling: similar in a dry, snapping noise from fire; differs because crackling sounds vigorous and lively, while fizzling is damp, feeble, and on the verge of going out.
  • Sputtering vs. fizzling out: sputtering describes the sound; fizzling out describes the sound plus the failure.
  • Whimpering: similar in the sense of ending weakly; differs because whimpering is a human or animal sound of distress, while fizzling is mechanical, gaseous, and deflationary.
  • Waning: similar in the sense of growing weaker; differs because waning is gradual and neutral (the moon wanes), while fizzling is noisy, damp, and comic.
Collocations and register: "fizzling torch", "fizzling fuse", "fizzling fireworks", "fizzling candle", "fizzle out", "fizzled campaign", "fizzled boom", "fizzling connection". The register is informal to conversational, with a strong comic-deflationary colouring: the word belongs to the vocabulary of damp squibs, failed bangs, and parties that end before midnight. Morphologically it is built on fizz (the imitative core) + the frequentative -le (fizzle, "to fizz repeatedly and feebly") + the present participial -ing, and its sound is the whole of its meaning: the sibilant buzz of a soft, repeated hiss, smaller and feebler than the confident hiss of steam or the appetising spit of a hot pan — the acoustic signature of something giving out.
