• News: Similar because both denote reports of recent events; differs because news is the ordinary modern word, neutral and uncountable, while tidings is plural, archaic and literary.
  • Word: Similar because both can mean “information conveyed” (“word came that...”); differs because word is far broader (a promise, a command, a single lexical unit) and informal, while tidings always means reported news.
  • Intelligence: Similar because both mean gathered information about events; differs because intelligence is formal and often military or governmental, while tidings is general and literary.
  • Information: Similar because both denote communicated knowledge; differs because information is abstract, neutral and a mass noun, with no suggestion of importance or drama, while tidings implies significant news.
  • Report: Similar because both denote an account of events; differs because a report is a formal, structured document, while tidings is spoken or circulated news.
  • Message: Similar because both involve communication; differs because a message is a single directed communication from a sender to a recipient, while tidings is news that spreads broadly.
  • Dispatch: Similar because both denote news sent from afar; differs because a dispatch is an official or military communication sent by a courier, while tidings is less formal and more literary.
  • Bulletin: Similar because both announce events; differs because a bulletin is a brief official statement (news bulletin), while tidings is informal in origin and archaic in register.
  • Announcement: Similar because both convey news; differs because an announcement is a deliberate public statement made by those involved, while tidings is news that travels, often without an official source.
  • Communiqué: Similar because both report events; differs because a communiqué is an official diplomatic or military statement, technical and cold, while tidings is warm, old and general.
  • Rumour / hearsay: Similar because both involve circulating reports; differs because rumour and hearsay are unverified or false by implication, while tidings is neutral as to truth and usually significant.
  • Advices (archaic): Similar because both are old-fashioned plurals for news (“advices from abroad”); differs because advices is commercial or diplomatic jargon of the 18th–19th centuries, now wholly obsolete, while tidings still lives in set phrases.
