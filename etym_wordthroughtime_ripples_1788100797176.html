- 14th century – a sound, not a sight: “to ripple” describes the soft murmur of a brook running over stones; the word is auditory.
- 1660s – a homonym appears: “a ripple” is a toothed comb for stripping flax, loaned from Dutch “repel”, later merging into the same spelling and fading.
- 1755–1798 – sight takes over: the verb gains “to flow in small waves” (1755), and the noun “very small wave” is first recorded in 1798, at the height of Romantic nature-writing.
- 19th century – migration from water to voice: “a ripple” in speech and laughter (1842); geology adopts “ripple-marked” strata; the word becomes standard in nature description.
- 1950–1957 – abstraction: “ripple effect” (1950) for consequences spreading from small events; electronics and computing borrow “ripple” for successive small pulses of current or data.
- Present – the metaphor nearly outruns the word: scandals, tax changes and rumours “send ripples” through markets and ministries; yet the anchor sense remains Wilde’s “silver ripples” — light, calm water and the smallest wave.
