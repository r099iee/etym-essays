cuppe is one of the oldest Latin loanwords in English, and its history illuminates the ordinary, unglamorous side of the Roman inheritance: not law or theology, but the tavern, the kitchen and the table. The word entered Germanic before the Anglo-Saxons came to Britain, was naturalised in Old English, and then expanded into an extraordinary range of senses that its Latin ancestor never had.

Latin origins

The starting point is Latin cōpa (also written cūpa), a noun for a cask, tub or barrel. It belonged to the vocabulary of storage and transport rather than of drinking, and it is the source of Italian coppa, Spanish copa and Old French coupe. In Late Latin a variant cuppa appears with the narrower sense "drinking vessel"; this is the form that spread into the Germanic languages and eventually into English. The ultimate origin of cōpa is uncertain. A connection with a PIE root *kup- "hollow, pit" has been proposed on the basis of Sanskrit kūpa- "pit, well" and Greek kypellon "cup", but the reconstruction is not secure and should be treated as a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated fact.

Borrowing into Germanic

The word was taken into Proto-West Germanic as *kuppa, and it appears across the family: Old English cuppe, Middle Dutch cuppe, Old High German kopf, and Old Norse koppr. The wide distribution shows that this was not a learned or ecclesiastical borrowing but a trade word, carried by merchants, soldiers and settlers along the Roman frontier and into Scandinavia. It belongs with win, ciese and pund to the small pre-migration Latin layer of English vocabulary.

  • Old English — cuppe, attested in glosses and glossaries from the ninth and tenth centuries.
  • Old Norse — koppr "cup, small vessel", which reinforced the word in the Danelaw.
  • Old French — coupe "cup, goblet", from the same Latin cuppa and the source of the later English coupe.
  • Middle Dutch — cuppe, a parallel borrowing that produced Dutch kop "cup, head".

Old and Middle English attestations

In Old English the word is modest but well attested. It appears in glosses where monks glossed Latin calix, cupa and vas, and in religious prose where the chalice of the Mass is described. In Middle English it becomes fully domestic: poetry and romance are full of cups, and wills bequeath silver cups by name. The word also develops the figurative sense "one's portion" in religious writing, modelled on the biblical cup of wrath and the cup of salvation.

Sound history

The phonological development is regular and instructive. Old English cuppe had a short /u/ and a geminate /pp/, pronounced roughly /ˈkup.pe/. In Middle English the final -e was weakened and eventually lost, and the geminate was simplified, giving /kup/. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the short /u/ in a closed syllable underwent the change known as the FOOT-STRUT split, producing the modern /ʌ/ of cup. The spelling cuppe survives in place names and in archaic or antiquarian writing, while the modern spelling cup reflects the loss of the final vowel.

  • Old English /ˈkup.pe/ — short vowel, geminate consonant, final vowel pronounced.
  • Middle English /ˈkup(ə)/ — loss of the final vowel, simplification of the geminate.
  • Early Modern /kʊp/ — the FOOT-STRUT split gives the modern vowel.
  • Modern RP /kʌp/ — a single syllable, with the old final -e preserved only in spelling history.

Semantic expansion

The semantic history of cuppe is a case study in metonymy and metaphor. The vessel gives its name to its contents (a cup of wine), then to a measure (a cupful), then to the object awarded as a prize (a silver cup), then to the competition for that prize (the World Cup), then to anything shaped like a cup (the cup of a flower, a bra cup, a protective cup). Each step is licensed by a different trope: metonymy for contents and measure, symbolism for the trophy, and shape-based metaphor for the anatomical and technical senses.

  • Container for contents — "a cup of tea" names the drink by naming the vessel.
  • Vessel for measure — the cup becomes a unit in recipes and medicine.
  • Object for honour — the trophy cup stands for victory.
  • Object for contest — the cup becomes the competition itself.
  • Shape for anatomy — the bra cup and the protective cup are named from form.

Modern life of the word

In contemporary English cup is everywhere, and its senses are distributed across registers. In sport it is public and celebratory; in the kitchen it is domestic and practical; in medicine and engineering it is technical; in idiom it is intimate and evaluative. The environmental campaign against single-use cups has given the word a new moral charge, while the specialty-coffee boom has made "cup" a marker of taste and connoisseurship. The Old English cuppe is thus still doing the work it did in the refectory: naming the small vessel through which a society shares what it drinks.
