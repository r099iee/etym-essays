ANOINTS (third-person singular present indicative of the verb anoint) describes the act of applying, smearing, rubbing, or pouring oil, unguent, ointment, pomade, or scented balm onto the human body, hair, head, or a sacred ritual object. Historically and conceptually, it bifurcates into two major spheres: sacred ceremonial consecration and practical somatic grooming or medicinal treatment.

Beyond sacred ritual and daily hair care, ANOINTS encompasses rich polysemous, political, medical, and metaphorical dimensions across various domains:

  • Sacred, Liturgical, and Monarchical Consecration: In Judeo-Christian theology, ecclesiastical liturgy, and monarchical statecraft, to anoint is to apply consecrated holy oil (chrism) to a priest, prophet, monarch, or sick believer as a sacramental sign of divine election, spiritual dedication, and the bestowal of the Holy Spirit. In British coronation rituals, the monarch is privately anointed on the hands, chest, and head before being crowned, signifying holy sanctification. The term Messiah (Hebrew Mashiach) and Christ (Greek Christos) literally mean 'the Anointed One.'
  • Cosmetic Grooming and Hair Care: In personal grooming and Victorian cosmetology—prominently featured in Arthur Conan Doyle's The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle ('has grizzled hair which he has had cut within the last few days, and which he anoints with lime-cream')—the verb describes applying scented pomades, brilliantines, or hair oils to moisturize, tame, shape, and perfume the hair. Sherlock Holmes uses the olfactory evidence of lime-cream ointment on the hat's inner sweatband to deduce the gentleman's grooming habits.
  • Political and Organizational Succession: In modern political journalism, corporate leadership, and institutional succession, anoints is used metaphorically to describe an incumbent leader or ruling elite choosing, endorsing, and designating an un-elected favorite as their official successor (e.g., 'the party elders anointed the young governor as the presidential nominee'). The semantic bridge transfers the ancient royal divine right of succession to modern institutional sponsorship.
  • Medicinal, Therapeutic, and Balneological Treatment: In historical medicine, dermatology, and physical therapy, to anoint wounds, burns, or stiff joints with soothing herbal salves, liniments, or animal fats was the primary method of topical healing and pain relief.
  • Athletic and Gladiator Preparation in Classical Antiquity: In ancient Greco-Roman athletics, athletes and gladiators routinely anointed their entire bodies with olive oil prior to wrestling in the palaestra, which protected their skin from sand and made them harder for opponents to grip.
  • Literary and Poetic Blessings: In poetic imagery, natural elements like morning dew, sunlight, or rain are depicted as anointing the earth, bestowing vitality and natural grace upon the landscape.

Etymologically derived from Latin *in-ungere* ('to smear on, rub with oil'), the verb carries an inherent sense of solemnity, tactile intimacy, and transforming grace.
