Isidore in the source context unambiguously denotes Isidore of Seville, c. 560-636 CE, Archbishop of Seville, Doctor of the Church, and compiler of the most influential encyclopedia of the early Middle Ages. Born into a Hispano-Roman family in Cartagena after the Byzantine reconquest, educated by his brother Leander, he succeeded as metropolitan bishop, presided over the Councils of Seville and Toledo, reformed Visigothic liturgy and schools, and wrote prolifically to convert Arian Visigoths and organise Catholic Spain. Dante places him among theologians in Paradise, and the Catholic Church venerates him as patron of scholars, students and, by modern extension, the internet.

His keeper status rests on sheer transmission: when libraries were shrinking and Greek was fading in the West, Isidore assembled excerpts from Pliny, Varro, Cicero, Virgil, Augustine and lost handbooks into accessible Latin compendia. His Etymologiae or Origines in twenty books covers grammar, rhetoric, mathematics, medicine, law, chronology, theology, languages, peoples, animals, stones, agriculture, ships, houses and furniture, each entry often beginning with an etymology that claims the primitive shape of a word reveals its essence, exactly the belief cited in the source: if we traced a word back far enough, its form would reflect its meaning. Medieval schoolmen copied nearly a thousand manuscripts, making him the textbook of the Carolingian renaissance and of Chaucer's England.

The name Isidore itself means gift of Isis in Greek, a theophoric pagan survival Christianised by saints, and it recurs in other keepers such as Isidore of Miletus, architect of Hagia Sophia, and Isidore of Alexandria, Neoplatonist philosopher. In Anki terms Isidore is therefore never an obscure NPC to be culled but a central node linking etymology, encyclopedism, Visigothic Spain and medieval education. To forget him is to forget how antiquity reached modernity.

  • person - archbishop and saint: administrator who unified Spanish church and converted Visigoths; similar because it is his office; differs because it stresses pastoral power, not writing.
  • person - encyclopedist and etymologist: author of Etymologies, Differentiae, De natura rerum; similar because it is his intellectual function; differs because it stresses compilation and word lore.
  • doctrine - Isidorean etymologism: belief that origo vocabulorum discloses res, that homo from humus or rex from regere proves nature; similar because it is his method; differs because modern linguistics rejects it as folk etymology.
  • era - Isidorean age: shorthand for Visigothic golden age of learning c. 600; similar because it names his influence; differs because it is period label, not man.
  • name - Isidore as given name: from Isidoros to Isidro, Isidora; similar because it shares form; differs because bearers like Isidore Isou or Isadora Duncan are distinct persons.

In current scholarship Isidore is revalued as an active theorist of signs and education rather than a mere scissors-and-paste compiler, and his etymologies are read as mnemonic theology and early semantics. The source sentence rightly makes him emblem of pre-modern faith that language is motivated, not arbitrary, a faith Saussure would overturn yet poets and lettrists would revive.
