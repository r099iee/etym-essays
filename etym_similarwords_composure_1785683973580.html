• Calm: Similar because both denote freedom from agitation; differs because calm is the general and often passive word — it can describe weather or seas and says nothing about effort or discipline — whereas composure implies self-command, actively gathering one's emotions into order.
  • Equanimity: Similar because both denote evenness of mind under stress; differs because equanimity stresses a settled, unshakeable evenness of temperament as a stable philosophical trait, while composure highlights the moment-by-moment act of staying composed in a particular crisis.
  • Self-possession: Similar because both mean control of oneself under pressure; differs because self-possession focuses on not betraying fear through limbs and voice, while composure adds the image of inner ordering and arrangement — putting the scattered faculties together.
  • Poise: Similar because both suggest graceful self-command; differs because poise emphasizes outward physical and social grace — carriage, bearing, ease — while composure is inward, the ordering of the emotions themselves.
  • Collectedness: Similar because both capture the gathered-together image of a mind kept whole; differs because collectedness is rarer and more literary, and leans slightly on the sense of gathering scattered thoughts, while composure is the standard, everyday noun for calm self-command.
  • Sangfroid: Similar because both mean coolness in the face of danger; differs because sangfroid is the borrowed French term for keeping one's blood cold, more Gallic, more specifically about peril, and slightly more theatrical, while composure is the plain English word for ordered calm.
  • Serenity: Similar because both denote inner peace; differs because serenity suggests a deep, spiritual, unbroken calm that can exist in solitude, while composure is the calm of a person actively self-possessed amid disturbance — the one ordered thing in a scene falling apart.
  • Placidity: Similar because both denote a calm temperament; differs because placidity implies a mild, undisturbed, almost bovine evenness, lacking the effort, dignity, and strain-resistance that composure always implies.
  • Tranquillity: Similar because both denote freedom from disturbance; differs because tranquillity is a state of surroundings or mind (a tranquil evening, a tranquil spirit), while composure is specifically a person's self-command under trial.
  • Control: Similar because both imply keeping oneself in hand; differs because control is a broad word for command over anything and anyone, while composure is the specialised noun for self-command over one's emotions in particular.
