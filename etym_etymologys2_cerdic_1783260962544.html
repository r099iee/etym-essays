CERDIC is a personal name; the mainstream scholarly reading derives it from a Brittonic (Celtic) name, though the deep origin is debated. Linear chain on the Celtic reading: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *keh₂- "to desire, wish, love" (3rd millennium BCE; the root also of Latin cārus "dear", Vedic Sanskrit kāma "love, desire"). Stage 2 — Proto-Celtic *karā- "to love" (reconstructed; also the root of Welsh caru "to love", Old Irish caraid "he loves"). Stage 3 — Proto-Celtic *Caratācos "beloved one" (a common personal name, literally "loved", formed with the familiar Celtic name-suffix -tācos; attested across the Celtic world in Roman-period inscriptions). Stage 4 — Latin Caratacus (romanized form of *Caratācos; 1st century CE — borne by the British chieftain of the Silures whose resistance to Claudius is reported by Tacitus). Stage 5 — Old Welsh Caradog, and the closely related derivative Ceredig "beloved" (medieval Welsh forms of the same name; Ceredig also named a 5th–6th-century ruler of the territory later called Ceredigion / Cardiganshire). Stage 6 — Old English Cerdic (attested in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle for the year 495 as the founder of Wessex; the un-palatalized initial C- before e is a phonological fossil showing the name entered Old English already containing a velar /k/, not a native formation). Stage 7 — Modern English CERDIC (the modern historical spelling, transmitted through the chronicle tradition and later historiography). FINAL NODE. DEBATE / NUANCE NOTES: - The derivation is not universally accepted. No attested Brittonic form *Cerdic is known exactly, and the route *Caratācos → Cerdic requires the loss of interior syllables; parallels like Welsh Caradog and Ceredig show how Brittonic names contracted over the centuries, making the derivation plausible though not strictly provable. - A minority of scholars attempts a native Germanic origin, but the hard initial before a front vowel is phonologically difficult for a native Old English formation (a native name would show palatal ch-), which is exactly the evidence pointing to a Brittonic loan. - The historicity of the bearer is independent of the name's etymology: Cerdic may be semi-legendary or a back-projection, but the linguistic form of the name is evidence of Brittonic–Saxon culture-contact regardless.
