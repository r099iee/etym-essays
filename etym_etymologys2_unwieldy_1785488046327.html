1. PIE *wal- "to be strong, to be powerful, to rule" (reconstructed) — the base of a family of words for strength and governing in Italic, Germanic, and Baltic; the exact shape and unity of the root are debated (see note). 2. Proto-Germanic *waldaną "to rule, to govern, to have power over" (reconstructed) — the strong verb of governing, inherited by every major Germanic language. 3. Old English wealdan "to rule, to govern, to control" and its close relation wieldan "to control, to govern, to wield" (attested from c. 700–1000 CE). 4. Middle English welden / wielden "to control, to handle, to use" (attested from the 13th century). 5. English wield "to hold and use with the hands; to control, to manage" (14th century). 6. English wieldy "capable of being wielded, manageable, easy to handle" (recorded from the 14th–15th centuries; now rare except in the negative). 7. English unwieldy "lacking strength, weak, infirm" (of persons, c. 1400, the earliest sense) and "hard to control, clumsy, cumbersome" (16th century; the modern sense) → English UNWIELDY (this card). Note on debate: the PIE root *wal- "to be strong, to rule" is secure in Germanic (*waldaną) and is traditionally extended to Latin valēre, "to be strong", and Baltic Lithuanian valdyti, "to rule, to govern", but the unity of the root is debated: some scholars reconstruct two distinct bases, *h₂welh₁- "to be strong" (Latin valēre) and *wal- / *wald- "to rule" (Germanic *waldaną, Lithuanian valdyti), and their identity is not universally accepted. The Germanic line to English, however, is unambiguous: wealdan → wieldan → wield → wieldy → unwieldy. No Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia cognate of *wal- is securely established.
