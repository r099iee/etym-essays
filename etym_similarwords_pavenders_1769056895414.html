• trout: Similar because both are familiar edible fish-names of the kind pavender is engineered to resemble; differs because trout is a real species with a long biological and linguistic history, while a pavender is a coinage with no referent outside Narnia — it only mimics the sound of a real name.
  • salmon: Similar because both are prized, edible fish remembered from an older, better time; differs because salmon names an actual migratory fish of northern seas, while a pavender is fictional — beautiful, rainbow-colored, and existing only in Lewis's invented world.
  • mackerel: Similar because both are sea-fish named with the same plain, single-word confidence; differs because mackerel is a genuine, commercially central Atlantic fish rooted in centuries of usage, while pavender was invented in 1952 to sound as ordinary and established as mackerel does.
  • pike: Similar because both are fish-names of the same blunt, single-syllable or two-syllable shape; differs because pike is a real fresh-water predator with a descriptive history (named for its pointed "pike" snout), while a pavender has no biology and no descriptive etymology at all.
  • roach: Similar because both are fish-names that sound plain and native; differs because roach is a genuine European fresh-water fish with a long English record, while a pavender is a Narnian invention that borrows the plainness of such names without their history.
  • plaice: Similar because both end in a soft, two-syllable -er/-ice shape that sounds like an old species name; differs because plaice is a real flatfish of European seas with an attested etymology, while pavender is a closed-system word of the Narnian lexicon.
  • prawn: Similar because both are two-syllable sea-food words ending in a consonant cluster that reads as natural; differs because a prawn is a real crustacean, while a pavender is a fictional fish — the comparison is one of sound-shape, not of reference.
  • gudgeon: Similar because both are species names whose shape feels antique and dialectal; differs because gudgeon is a real small fish (and also a nautical fitting and a gullible person), while pavender exists only on Lewis's pages.
  • Reepicheep: Similar as an invented Narnian name with a plausible, folkloric ring; differs because Reepicheep is a talking animal with character and agency, while a pavender is a mere animal — beautiful but silent, a creature of appetite rather than of character.
  • pavonine: Similar because the rainbow colouring makes pavonine ("peacock-coloured," from Latin pāvo) a plausible echo that may have flavoured the coinage; differs because pavonine is a real English adjective with a Latin ancestry, while pavender is a neologism — the resemblance is inspiration, not inheritance.
