- Late Latin / Early Christian theological use - annihilation as bringing to nothing in contrast to creation from nothing; solemn, philosophical, tied to God's power to create and uncreate.
- 1520s–1600s Early Modern English borrowing - annihilation enters English via French/Latin as learned formal term for utter destruction and annulment of laws and souls; elite, legal-theological register, connotation of absolute power.
- 17th–18th century philosophical and theological debate - annihilationism vs eternal torment, annihilation of souls, of matter; abstract, doctrinal, feared as ultimate punishment worse than pain.
- 19th century scientific and colonial extension - annihilation of space by rail/telegraph, annihilation of tribes and species; both celebratory (progress conquers distance) and ominous (empire erases peoples), industrial-age scale.
- 20th century nuclear and physics sense - mutual annihilation, electron-positron annihilation, fear of human annihilation; dominant connotation becomes existential dread, Cold War apocalypse, yet also precise lab jargon.
- Contemporary metaphorical inflation - to annihilate in sports, debate, gaming means to dominate totally; still extreme but often playful hyperbole, alongside continued literal use for ecocide and war.
