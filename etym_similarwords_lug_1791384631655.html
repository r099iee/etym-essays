• Haul: Similar because both mean pulling heavy or cumbersome loads with effort; differs because haul is more systematic, industrial, and sustained (hauling freight over distances), whereas lug emphasizes clumsy awkwardness and hand-carried strain.
  • Tote: Similar because both describe carrying something; differs because tote is regional American, informal, and can apply to lighter bags (tote bag) or casual carrying, whereas lug inherently involves heavy, awkward strain.
  • Drag: Similar because both denote pulling something along the ground against friction or pulling an unwilling person; differs because drag specifically implies surface contact and friction along the ground, while lug can mean carrying off the ground with exertion.
  • Cart: Similar because both mean transporting cumbersome goods; differs because cart implies using a vehicle or wheelbarrow (or figuratively trudging with a load) and often involves moving things repeatedly.
  • Tug: Similar because both involve pulling force; differs because tug implies short, sharp, intermittent jerks or pulls rather than sustained, cumbersome transportation.
  • Schlep: Similar because both colloquially describe carrying awkward, heavy burdens wearisomely; differs because schlep is Yiddish in origin and heavily emphasizes personal grievance, weariness, and long tedious journeys.
  • Heave: Similar because both involve substantial muscular exertion; differs because heave emphasizes lifting or tossing upward with a major surge of strength, or the rhythmic rise and fall of seas.
  • Draw: Similar because both mean applying force to move something behind oneself; differs because draw is elegant, smooth, and formal (drawing a carriage, drawing a curtain), lacking the gritty awkwardness of lug.
  • Manhandle: Similar because both convey moving something through raw physical muscle; differs because manhandle implies rough, aggressive, or forceful handling of someone against their will, often bordering on violence.
  • Humph: Similar because Scottish dialect uses humph for carrying a heavy load on one's back; differs because humph is strictly dialectal and emphasizes a hunched back posture.
