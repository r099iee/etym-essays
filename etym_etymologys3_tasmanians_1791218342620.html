Tasmanians is the regular English plural of Tasmanian, meaning a native, inhabitant or attribute of Tasmania, and its history is entirely modern, beginning with a 17th-century Dutch sailor's surname. The surname Tasman combines Dutch tas, bag, purse or satchel, with man, man or person, hence bag-man or purse-maker, an occupational or nickname surname common in the Netherlands. Dutch tas itself was borrowed from Old French tasse, cup or small vessel, which came via Arabic tass and Persian tast, cup or tray, showing an oriental thread even in this northern name. The man element continues Proto-Germanic *mann-, person, one of the most stable Germanic words.

Abel Janszoon Tasman, 1603-1659, employed by the Dutch East India Company, sighted the island in November 1642 and named it Anthoonij van Diemenslandt after his patron Antonio van Diemen. For two centuries English charts called it Van Diemen's Land, a name that became infamous as a penal colony after 1803. On the proposal of John Franklin and others the colony was officially renamed Tasmania in 1856 to honour its European discoverer and to shed convict stigma. From Tasmania English formed Tasmanian with the Latinate suffix -ian, meaning belonging to, and then the plural Tasmanians for the people.

  • Surname formation: Dutch tas plus man creates Tasman as an occupational byname, later a famous explorer's family name.
  • Discovery naming: Tasman voyage 1642 to Van Diemen's Land creates a patronymic toponym honouring Van Diemen rather than the discoverer.
  • Penal era: Van Diemen's Land 1803-1853 accumulates convict infamy in British imagination through transportation and bushranger lore.
  • Renaming: Van Diemen's Land to Tasmania in 1856 shifts honour from patron to navigator and rebrands the colony for free settlement.
  • Adjectival derivation: Tasmania to Tasmanian adds -ian, enabling both a person noun and an attribute for tiger, devil, timber and culture.
  • Pluralisation: Tasmanian to Tasmanians adds -s for collective peoples, both Indigenous Palawa and settler islanders.

Phonetically Tasmanian follows English stress on the second syllable, taz-MAY-nee-an, with the Dutch short a lengthened and the -ia- forming a separate syllable. The spelling preserves Dutch s single and adds Latinate -ia for place plus -n for people. Semantically the word bifurcated: Tasmanian Aborigines or Palawa for the Indigenous peoples, nine nations speaking related languages, and Tasmanians for modern island residents generally. Context usually disambiguates, but capitalisation and phrases like Aboriginal Tasmanians versus Tasmanians mark the distinction.

Culturally the term carries heavy history. Nineteenth-century writers used Tasmanians, especially full-blood Tasmanians, as the classic example of an extinct race after the death of Truganini in 1876, long presented as the last Tasmanian. This extinction myth, now rejected, made Tasmanians a moral shorthand for colonial genocide, elegy and scientific racism, with skulls and vocabularies preserved in museums. The source sentence, grouping Tasmanians who slept in hollow trees with Veddahs and Bushmen, reflects this era's stadial anthropology. Contemporary usage reclaims Tasmanian Aboriginal identity, with revived palawa kani language, Bass Strait islander communities and recognition of survival and continuity. In natural history Tasmanian tiger and Tasmanian devil extend the name to extinct and endangered marsupials, adding ecological poignancy to the human story.
