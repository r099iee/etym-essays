- 3rd century BCE (Historical Origin) - King Pyrrhus of Epirus defeated Roman armies at Heraclea (280 BCE) and Asculum (279 BCE) at unbearable cost to his own phalanxes, uttering the famous warning recorded by Plutarch.
- 16th–17th centuries (Early English) - Initially used in English in reference to classical poetic prosody (*pyrrhic foot*) and ancient Greek weapon dances (*pyrrhic dance*).
- Late 18th–19th centuries - The phrase *Pyrrhic victory* emerged in English political commentary and military historiography, rapidly conventionalizing *pyrrhic* as a lowercase adjective for any self-ruining success.
- 20th–21st centuries - Extended across modern corporate finance, geopolitical analysis, legal disputes, and everyday cultural discourse to denote any contest where winning costs more than conceding.
