Single linear chain for DISCONTENTED
Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European root *ten- (also *tenH-), "to stretch, extend, draw taut," c. 4500–3000 BCE. Reflexes: Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches," Greek teínein "to stretch," Latin tendere "to stretch," and, via the zero-grade with a stative suffix, Latin tenēre.
Stage 2 — Latin tenēre, "to hold, keep, grasp, possess," attested 2nd–1st c. BCE (Plautus, Cicero); from PIE *ten- "to stretch" — the one who holds keeps something stretched tight and under control.
Stage 3 — Latin continēre (contineō, continēre, continuī, contentum), "to hold together, contain, restrain, keep within bounds," attested 2nd c. BCE; con- "together" + tenēre.
Stage 4 — Latin contentus, -a, -um, past participle of continēre, "held together, contained, restrained; hence satisfied, content," attested 1st c. BCE. The psychological metaphor is already Latin: the person whose desires are "contained" is the content person.
Stage 5 — Old French descontent (adjective and noun), "displeased, dissatisfied; dissatisfaction," 12th c.; des- "un-" + content, alongside the verb descontenter "to discontent."
Stage 6 — Middle English discontent (adjective, noun, verb), "dissatisfied, displeased; dissatisfaction," 15th c., from Anglo-Norman.
Stage 7 — English discontented, formed with the past-participial suffix -ed on the noun/adjective discontent, "dissatisfied, ill at ease, restlessly displeased," attested late 16th c. (Shakespeare, Richard III, c. 1592–94, uses the noun "discontent"; the adjective is well attested by the 1590s) — FINAL NODE discontented.
Debate note: The etymology is transparent and undisputed (dis- + content + -ed). The only nuance is the route of the negative: English may have formed "discontented" directly from its own noun/adjective "discontent" plus -ed, or inherited a participial model from Old French; both channels probably reinforced one another in the 15th–16th centuries. The derivation of Latin tenēre from PIE *ten- "to stretch" is standard (via *ten-eh₁-, "to be kept stretched, held").
