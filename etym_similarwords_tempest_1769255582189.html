• storm: Similar because both name violent weather of wind (usually with rain or thunder), and both carry figurative uses of uproar. Differs because storm is the plain, everyday, generic term for any violent weather, while tempest is the elevated, classical, literary word implying greater scale and drama.
  • gale: Similar because both are strong, destructive winds. Differs because a gale is, technically, wind of a particular measured force (Beaufort scale 8–9), without the dramatic overturning of the elements implied by a tempest; gale is meteorological, tempest literary.
  • squall: Similar because both denote violent weather. Differs because a squall is brief, sudden, and local — a sharp burst of wind, often with rain — while a tempest is a prolonged, full-scale storm.
  • hurricane: Similar because both are violent, destructive windstorms. Differs because hurricane is the precise technical term for a tropical cyclone of a defined intensity, while tempest is general and non-meteorological.
  • typhoon: Similar because both are violent tropical storms. Differs because typhoon names the specific West-Pacific cyclone, while tempest is a generic literary storm of any region.
  • cyclone: Similar because both are violent storm systems of rotating winds. Differs because cyclone is a scientific term for a low-pressure system (especially tropical), while tempest is a classical, dramatic word for a storm's fury.
  • tornado: Similar because both are violent, destructive atmospheric events. Differs because a tornado is a narrow, rapidly rotating funnel with extreme localized winds, while a tempest is a broad storm of wind and weather over a wide area.
  • whirlwind: Similar because both name violently moving air, and both are used figuratively (a whirlwind tour, a tempest of emotion). Differs because a whirlwind is a rotating column of air, quick and local, while a tempest is a wide field of stormy weather.
  • blizzard: Similar because both are violent storms. Differs because a blizzard is specifically a storm of wind and driving snow, while a tempest is not tied to any particular precipitation type.
  • thunderstorm: Similar because both are violent storms with atmospheric fury. Differs because a thunderstorm is the specific phenomenon of thunder and lightning, while a tempest emphasizes wind and upheaval rather than electrical display.
  • downpour: Similar because both can describe violent weather. Differs because a downpour is heavy rain alone, while a tempest centers on wind and general storm violence.
  • deluge: Similar because both are violent, overwhelming natural events used metaphorically for flood-like force. Differs because a deluge is a flood of water (or, figuratively, of things), while a tempest is a storm of wind.
  • monsoon: Similar because both are powerful weather systems with violent wind and rain. Differs because a monsoon is a seasonally reversing wind with torrential rain in a specific climate, while a tempest is a single storm, not a season.
  • uproar: Similar because both name violent disturbance, and both are used of noisy public commotion. Differs because uproar is wholly figurative and social — a clamor of voices — while tempest retains its literal weather sense.
  • turmoil: Similar because both denote violent agitation and disorder, and both apply to emotions or politics. Differs because turmoil is entirely abstract (confusion, upheaval) and has no weather meaning at all.
  • frenzy: Similar because both name violent, uncontrollable excitement or fury. Differs because frenzy is a state of the mind or of a crowd, while tempest is an atmospheric event used metaphorically.
  • outburst: Similar because both can describe a sudden violent release of emotion (a tempest / an outburst of rage). Differs because an outburst is brief and singular, while a tempest suggests sustained, overwhelming force.
  • eruption: Similar because both describe a violent, explosive release of force (of a volcano, or figuratively of anger). Differs because an eruption is sudden and localized, while a tempest is a sustained meteorological event.
  • disturbance: Similar because both name an interruption of calm, and archaic tempest could mean any atmospheric disturbance. Differs because disturbance is far weaker and more general, applicable to a ripple of any kind, with none of tempest's grandeur.
  • cataclysm: Similar because both name violent, earth-shaking upheavals, literal and figurative. Differs because a cataclysm is a great disaster that overturns everything (a deluge, an earthquake), broader and more catastrophic than the strictly weather-bound tempest.
