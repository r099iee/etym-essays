A ROOT ABOUT HANGING

The root from which limped descends is a root about hanging. Indo-European *lemb- meant "to hang down, to droop, to be slack" — the posture of something unstiffened, sagging, not quite held up. In India the root prospered: Sanskrit lambate means "it hangs down, clings, sinks", and from its participle lamba-, "hanging down, dangling", comes modern Hindi lambā, "long" — a thing that hangs down is also a thing stretched out, and so a word of slackness became a word of length. Sanskrit ālamba, "support, prop", is the same root turned useful: that on which one hangs. These are the Indo-Aryan half of the family, and they show that the root's fundamental meaning was never "to walk badly" but simply "to hang slack".

A GERMANIC SURPRISE: FROM HANGING TO HAPPENING

In the Germanic world the root took a surprising turn. Proto-Germanic *limpanan meant not "to hang" but "to happen, to befall, to turn out" — a shift from physical drooping to the outcome of events, as though what comes to pass "hangs down" or "falls out" of the unknown. Old English limpan had exactly this sense: hit limpeð, "it happens, it befalls", and with ge-, gelimpan, "to happen to, to come about". Old English also had lemphealt, "halting, lame" — built on the same "slack" root — so the two ideas, happening and lameness, existed side by side in the earliest records. But the same verb could also mean "to fall short, to fail" — the thing that comes out badly — and this negative shading is the seed of the modern word.

THE BRIDGE TO WALKING

In Middle English, limpen carried both "to happen" and "to fail, to fall short". From "falling short" the meaning was ready to slip from events to bodies: a leg that falls short, that comes up short with every step, is a leg that limps. The walking verb is first recorded in English in the 1560s, and by the 17th century it was firmly settled in its modern sense. Along the way, sound and form settled too:

  • Old English limpan was a strong verb (limp / lamp / lumpon); by the 1560s the new walking verb had regularised to a weak past, giving limped.
  • The adjective limp, "flaccid, without stiffness", resurfaces in 1706 — a separate formation from the same "hang slack" idea, so that after a thousand years the root's original meaning was reborn in English.
  • The noun limp, "an uneven gait", appears in the 19th century ("a slight limp", "to walk with a limp").

THE FAVOURABLE HALF: GERMAN SUCCESS

German took the favourable side of the same inheritance. Old High German gilimpan "to befit, suit" became Middle High German gelingen and modern German gelingen "to succeed": a gelungener Tag is a day that has turned out well. So the same root meaning "to turn out" divided cleanly — German kept the positive ("to turn out well"), English drifted to the negative ("to turn out badly, to fall short, to limp"). This is a textbook case of how one inheritance splits into opposites across two languages.

LITERARY AND CULTURAL ATTESTATIONS

The Old English verb limpan is common in the homiletic and historical prose of the period — it is, quite literally, the verb of fate, of what befalls a person. By the modern period the walking verb belongs to the plain, physical vocabulary of walks and wounds: it appears throughout English fiction whenever a damaged character must keep moving — soldiers, refugees, injured animals and, in The Hobbit, a company of dwarves and one hobbit limping south through the pine forest. The figurative use (a "limping economy", a ship that "limps into port") dates from the 19th–20th centuries and treats an enterprise as a wounded creature favouring its damaged leg. Modern English keeps the word plain, physical and unsentimental: a word of walks and wounds, not of hospitals, combining physical damage with dogged onward effort.

SUMMARY

From a PIE root meaning "to hang slack", through Sanskrit's lambā "long" and a Germanic verb of what befalls us, the root drifted from drooping to happening, took a negative turn in English ("to fall short"), and in the 1560s stepped into the street as the walking verb — so that limped carries, under its surface, four thousand years of hanging, happening and coming up short.
