- 14th c. - Formed in Middle English (unfeyned) as a direct moral descriptor for faith, love, and truth that was uncorrupted by falsehood or deceit.
- 16th & 17th c. - Solidified into the religious and literary vernacular through Tudor translations of the New Testament (Tyndale, Cranmer, and the King James Bible) in iconic collocations like 'faith unfeigned' and 'love unfeigned'.
- 18th & 19th c. - Widely employed in classic English prose (such as Jane Austen, George Eliot, and Charles Dickens) to denote deep, transparent emotional reactions (unfeigned joy, unfeigned admiration, unfeigned regret) in contrast to polite social facade.
- 20th c. to Present - Retained as an elevated, elegant literary adjective for profound sincerity, raw emotional authenticity, and uncompromising truth in expressive arts.
