- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Emerged as nippen / nypte, meaning to pinch, squeeze tightly between two surfaces, or bite sharply with claws or teeth - Physical pinching action.
- 15th–16th Century - Extended to describe the biting effect of severe frost and bitter wind freezing plant shoots or stinging flesh (originating the famous phrase 'nipped in the bud' by 1600) - Horticultural blighting and environmental metaphor.
- 17th–18th Century - Broadened into idiomatic usages for checking, halting, or suppressing growth, vice, or rebellion at an early stage; also used for taking quick sips of spirits ('a nip of gin') - Moral suppression and social colloquialism.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Featured in literary depictions of nature's harshness (e.g. Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose: 'the frost has nipped my buds') alongside British colloquial uses for darting quickly ('nipped into town') or petty theft - Poetic melancholy and vernacular flexibility.
- 20th–21st Century - Pervasively used in British and Commonwealth English for rapid brief journeys ('he nipped out for lunch'), alongside the enduring standard idiom 'nipped in the bud' in management, medicine, and law - Ubiquitous idiomatic, botanical, and colloquial sense.
