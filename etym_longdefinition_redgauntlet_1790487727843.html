Redgauntlet is primarily the title and eponymous hero of Sir Walter Scott's 1824 Waverley novel Redgauntlet: A Tale of the Eighteenth Century, and by extension the Border family and Jacobite type it creates. Sir Edward Hugh Redgauntlet is a fanatical legitimist who plots in 1765–66 to land Charles Edward Stuart in the Solway country and restore the Stuarts. Stern, sombre, sarcastically courteous, with a horseshoe-shaped red mark or blood-stained gauntlet legend on his hand, he kidnaps his nephew Darsie Latimer (the true heir) to reclaim him for the Cause, and collapses tragically when the Prince himself proves petty and the enterprise dissolves at Crackenfell. The source sentence recalls his physical prowess: Redgauntlet speared more salmon than any of the Solway fishermen — Scott's scene of night-time salmon-leistering in the Solway Firth, remembered by a boy as proof that gentlemen-kings out-work commoners.

The novel is epistolary and autobiographical for Scott: Darsie and Alan Fairford are his own divided self (romance versus law), Nanty Ewart the repentant smuggler, Wandering Willie the blind fiddler whose tale of Hell is the most famous inset ghost story in English, and Peter Peebles the mad litigant. Historically it treats the last Jacobite gasp after 1745, arguing that legitimism, however gallant, is now obsolete — a theme Ruskin half-resists, admiring the labour while conceding the cause lost.

  • Literary head sense: Scott's novel and hero: e.g. have you read Redgauntlet? Redgauntlet is Scott's best late novel. Always capitalised, often italicised as title (Redgauntlet) versus roman for man (Redgauntlet). Pronounced red-GAUNT-let.
  • Familial-genealogical sense: the fictional House of Redgauntlet of Redgauntlet Castle, with its curse — that the heir's forehead bears a red horseshoe — and its Border reiver past. Readers speak of the Redgauntlets as a clan like the Bradwardines or Osbaldistones. The bridge is synecdoche: one man's nickname extends to kin.
  • Moral-type sense (Ruskin's use): a king-loving person who does harder work than anybody else and takes less; a disinterested loyalist. Ruskin explicitly generalises: both the author of the Iliad and the author of Waverley made their kings, or king-loving persons, do harder work. Here Redgauntlet stands for Scott's whole chivalric sociology — salmon-spearing lairds, smugglers turned repentant, advocates riding all night — contrasted with modern governors who govern less and get more. To call someone a Redgauntlet in Ruskinian circles means a gallant anachronism.
  • Historical-Jacobite sense: shorthand for the 1765–66 plot and for intransigent Jacobitism itself; e.g. the Redgauntlet conspiracy, a Redgauntlet Tory. The bridge is epitome: the novel's plot stands for the real waning Cause. Historians of Jacobitism cite Scott while warning that the novel romanticises a squalid minor intrigue.
  • No common-noun or technical senses: unlike ivanhoe (racehorse, operation) or waverley (station, novel-type), redgauntlet lower-cased has no dictionary entry as object or slang. It remains a proper-name keeper, globally significant because Scott is globally significant, not an obscure NPC to be culled. The task instruction rightly retains it.

Knowing Redgauntlet is thus knowing late Scott, late Jacobitism, Solway salmon, and Ruskin's boyhood moral that true kingship is hardest labour.
