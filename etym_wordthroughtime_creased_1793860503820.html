- Late Middle English (ca. 14th–15th c.) - Originated as an alteration of 'crest' (Middle English creste, from Old French creste, Latin crista), originally signifying a tuft, a plume, or the prominent ridge along a helmet; a 'crease' was the ridge formed along a folded sheet.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - Transitioned fully into a verb (to crease) and adjective/participle (creased); denoted making an intentional or accidental ridge by folding cloth, paper, or leather; adopted into English field sports (particularly cricket) for the whitewashed boundary lines marking batsman and bowler positions.
- 18th to 19th c. - Widely applied to human physiognomy to describe faces weathered by wind, age, or smiling; developed an equestrian and hunting sense ('to crease a horse', meaning to graze a mustang along the neck ligament to temporarily stun and capture it without injury); formal tailoring elevated sharp pressed creases as the hallmark of gentlemanly elegance.
- 20th to 21st c. - Preserved all physical and sartorial meanings; blossomed in British and Commonwealth colloquial slang into a vivid idiom meaning overwhelmed with laughter ('it creased me up', 'creased with laughter') or thoroughly exhausted/worn out.
