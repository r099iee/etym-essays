In the sentence "Habit had given a melancholy cast to her tender face; Nature had bestowed a sickly constitution on her delicate frame," bestowed is a gift-word that has been turned against itself. To bestow is to confer something graciously, as a king bestows a title or a patron a favour; here the thing conferred is a "sickly constitution" — an illness, or at least a constitutional fragility. The irony is deliberate and tragic. The verb raises Dai-yu's frailty from an accident of biology to a conferred destiny: Nature herself, the one force above all human authority, has chosen to "place" this delicacy upon her. It is simultaneously a mark of grace and a sentence of suffering.

The word is genuinely polysemous, and its senses unfold along a single historical thread that runs from physical placing to gracious giving:

  • To confer or present as a gift, honour, or favour (the dominant modern sense): a king bestows a title, a patron bestows a favour, Nature or God bestows qualities and blessings. The verb takes the recipient with on or upon (bestow a knighthood upon him), and more rarely with with. This is the sense carried by the participle in the passage.
  • To put, place, or stow (the older, physical sense): in 14th–15th-century English a thing is bestowed in a chest or a place — to bestow is to lay it up, to stow it away safely. The word is in origin simply be- + stow, and the physical "placing" sense survives in the memory of the verb and in the phrase "bestow a glance upon" (to direct, to place).
  • To give lodging or quarters (archaic sense): to bestow a guest is to house him, to find him a place; Shakespeare's "If he be not in love with some woman, there is no believing old signs: he bestows a whole goodly mansion" plays on placing a person in a dwelling. The archaic sense links the physical and the social: placing a person is also giving him a gift of hospitality.
  • To apply, spend, or expend (obsolete-to-archaic sense): one could bestow labour, time, or care upon a task — to "place" one's effort. This sense shows the word's slide from physical placement to the investing of a resource.
  • The noun bestowal / bestowment: the act of giving or conferring; a thing bestowed. These derivatives extend the participle's grammar into abstract nouns.
  • Figurative and elevated register: the word is now almost exclusively formal and lofty, used of giving something weighty — honours, gifts, qualities, blessings — and it is deliberately archaic when applied to ordinary, everyday giving.

Note too that the clause stands in parallel with "Habit had given...": habit merely gives a melancholy cast, while Nature bestows, and the escalation of the verbs suggests that what habit can only lend, Nature permanently confers. Bestowed keeps the image of the hand setting something down upon someone — it is giving with placement, a gift that "stands" upon its recipient, and the word's deep kinship with stand and stow is what makes the placement so physical.
