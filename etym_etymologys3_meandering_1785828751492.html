A RIVER THAT NAMED A LANGUAGE

Few words in English carry their geography so openly as meandering. The word is a fossil of a real river — the Maiandros — and its entire modern meaning is the memory of that river's habits. The Maiandros rose in the mountains of Phrygia and wound down through Caria, in western Asia Minor, to the Aegean near Miletus, and ancient travellers were astonished by its course: instead of running to the sea by the shortest road, it doubled back on itself in huge, slow, ever-growing loops, wandering across the flat valley floor like a serpent taking its time. So striking was this behaviour that the river's name became proverbial in Greek — to call something maiandros was to call it crooked, labyrinthine, wandering. Greek artists abstracted the look of the river into the geometric border of interlocking right angles that English still calls the meander or the Greek fret; the pattern, endlessly repeated around the rims of vases and the friezes of temples, is the river's shape copied by hand.

FROM GREEK TO LATIN TO ENGLISH

The ultimate origin of the river's name is a mystery and should be frankly acknowledged: Maiandros is a pre-Greek, probably Anatolian place-name, and no Proto-Indo-European etymology commands any authority. The word's Indo-European life begins only when Greek turned the name into a common noun — 'a winding course, a labyrinthine line' — and Latin borrowed it as Maeander, keeping the river and its figurative meanings together. From Latin, and through Middle French méandre, the word reached English in the late sixteenth century (first recorded in the 1570s–1590s) as a learned noun and verb of winding. Shakespeare and his contemporaries knew the river of the classical geographers; the Restoration and eighteenth-century travel-writers knew its figurative geography of ornament and digression; and the spelling meander (from the earlier mæander, maeander) settled in the seventeenth century.

THE MODERN FIGURATIVE EXPLOSION

The really interesting development is English, not Greek: the shift from water to speech. Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries meander kept its physical senses — rivers meandered, paths meandered, and gardens were laid out with meandering walks in the picturesque style, where the curve was itself the point. In the nineteenth century, with Romantic taste for wandering and the natural landscape, the word prospered in pastoral prose, describing streams and strolls with affectionate warmth. At the same time science was taking the word in: nineteenth-century geomorphology adopted 'meander' as the technical term for the sinuous loop of a river and its valley, distinguishing meander belts, meander scars, and the processes of erosion and deposition that make bends grow and eventually cut through. And then the figurative senses that had been latent for two centuries finally blossomed: a meandering narrative, a meandering argument, meandering thoughts — a style that follows association rather than line, that rambles, digresses, and turns back on itself like the river.

SOUND, FORM, AND CULTURE

  • Sound and form: the pronunciation /miˈæn.də.rɪŋ/ preserves the Greek disyllabic base (Mai-andros) with the stress on the second syllable; the spelling settled in the 17th century as meander.
  • Socio-cultural pressure: the picturesque garden movement, Romantic landscape prose, and Victorian travel-writing all drove the positive, pastoral charge of 'meandering walks'; modern conversational use extends it to speech, careers, and plots with a mildly amused tolerance for digression.
  • Literary attestations: the word appears in Romantic and Victorian prose as a staple of landscape description, and in twentieth-century fiction as a favourite way to characterise a rambling narrator or an unmethodical mind; the technical geomorphological use runs in parallel in scientific writing.
  • Register today: 'meandering' is informal-to-literary, equally at home describing a river's loops and a politician's speech; it is almost always slightly affectionate or indulgent, though it can tip into criticism when it means needlessly roundabout.
