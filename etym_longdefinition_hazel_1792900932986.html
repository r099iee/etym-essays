Hazel is first and foremost a tree that refuses to behave like one: Corylus avellana, the common hazel, a many-stemmed shrub of the birch family that colonises wood-edges, hedgerows, and hillsides, throwing up slender poles from a single stool and raining catkins in February long before its broad round leaves appear. In the source sentence it fills the landscape itself — ‘many deep brakes of hazel on the rising slopes’ — the hillsides furred with its rods, the lane running between walls of coppice green. This is England’s most companionable woody plant: everywhere, useful, and quietly ancient.

The tree earns its fame twice over: in autumn it bears hazelnuts, each sweet kernel jacketed in a ragged leafy cup, beloved of squirrels, dormice, children, and chocolate-makers; and its wood — pale, supple, straight-grained — has been coppiced for millennia into wattles, hurdles, thatching spars, walking-sticks, and divining rods. A hazel stool cut to the ground throws up new poles within the year, so the plant is effectively immortal: some stools in English woods have been harvested since before the Norman Conquest.

Core meaning in detail: Botanically the hazel is a deciduous shrub or small tree, rarely above six metres, monoecious — male catkins dangling yellow in late winter while tiny crimson female flowers wait on the same twig — fruiting in September. Ecologically it is a woodland-edge keystone, feeding rodents, birds, and insects, and housing dormice that seldom leave its branches. Culturally it is the hedgerow tree of memory: nuts gathered in September gangs, rods cut for water-divining, leaves turning butter-yellow in October.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The shrub or small tree Corylus avellana (primary, from Old English): coppice stools, hedgerow hazels, hazel poles. The bridge is direct inheritance: what the Anglo-Saxons called hæsel is what walkers still push through.
  • The nut, the hazelnut (metonymy): a pocketful of hazel, hazel kernels. The bridge is tree-for-fruit: the plant lends its name to its most desirable product, gathered for food since the Mesolithic.
  • The wood (material sense): hazel wattle, a hazel wand. The bridge is tree-for-timber: rods and poles take the name of their source, prized for springiness and straight grain.
  • The colour hazel (transferred, from 16th c.): reddish-brown, green-brown — hazel eyes. The bridge is visual: the polished nutshell’s mottled brown becomes the name for eyes and hair of that changeable shade.
  • Divining and magic rods (folkloric): hazel twigs for finding water, hazel wands in folk magic. The bridge is pliancy made mystical: the rod that bends in the hand is imagined to bend toward hidden springs.
  • Wisdom and the otherworld (Celtic literary): the nine hazels of wisdom dropping nuts of knowledge into the sacred well. The bridge is mythic botany: a tree that feeds the body comes to feed the mind, and salmon that eat its nuts become the wisest of fish.

Register and connotation: Hazel is warm, golden, and English — autumn pockets, hedgerow light, eyes that change colour with the weather. As a tree-word it is practical and affectionate, free of the oak’s majesty and the yew’s gloom; as a colour-word it is intimate and admiring, almost always of eyes. Folklore gilds it further with wisdom and water-finding. Few plant names carry so little menace: hazel shades walkers, feeds children, colours glances, and asks nothing in return but a September afternoon.
