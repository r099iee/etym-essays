The history of manor is the history of staying put — a word that began as a simple verb for "remaining in a place" and became the name of an entire feudal world. It descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *men-, "to remain, stay, wait, abide." This is one of the oldest human words, expressing the opposite of wandering: the decision to stay where one is, to remain fixed. (It must not be confused with the homonymous PIE root *men- "to think," which gave Latin mens "mind" and English mental; the "remain" root is a separate, older word.) In Greek the root produced ménō, "I remain, I stay," a verb central to Homeric and classical poetry; in Armenian it gave mnam "to remain"; and in Latium it gave Latin manēre, "to remain, stay, dwell, abide."

Latin manēre was a verb of extraordinary durability. From it Romans formed mansio, "a staying, a dwelling, a stage of a journey" — the imperial road system placed mansionēs, official posting-houses, at intervals along the roads where travelers and couriers could remain overnight. The English word mansion comes directly from this root: a great house is, etymologically, a place where one remains. Even the modern word remain is re- "back" + manēre "to stay" — to remain is literally "to stay back." And permanent is per- "through" + manēre: that which stays through everything.

The turn from verb to noun happened in the Romance world of the early Middle Ages. In Old French the infinitive manoir, "to dwell, to stay," was pressed into service as a noun — un manoir, "a dwelling, a residence" — exactly as English uses "a home" or "an abode." A lord's manoir was his dwelling place. As feudalism crystallized after the Carolingian period, the word acquired legal weight. Medieval Latin formed manerium, and with the Norman Conquest the concept crossed the Channel to England. The Domesday Book of 1086 records thousands of maneria — the units of land, each centered on a lord's residence, by which England was governed and taxed.

Under English common law a manor became far more than a house. It was a territorial corporation: the lord's demesne (his own farmland), the tenant holdings worked by free and unfree peasants, the common pastures, the manorial court that settled disputes, and the mill and the oven whose use the lord controlled. The "lord of the manor" held jurisdiction over this whole world. It is from this institutional sense that the modern meaning descends: the lord's residence at the center of the estate was the "manor house," and by the 19th century the word alone — manor — had come to mean the great country house itself, the stately seat of landed gentry. Austen's use in Pride and Prejudice (1813) sits exactly at this hinge: Bingley, a tenant, is "provided with a good house and the liberty of a manor" — he has the house and the rights of the estate, though he does not own it; the word carries both the building and the feudal privilege.

The modern slang has carried the word in an unexpected direction. In British police and criminal usage, "on my manor" means "on my patch, in my area of operation" — a police officer's beat or a criminal's territory. The word has thus come full circle: from a verb of remaining, to a feudal estate, to a great house, and finally to a colloquial name for one's local territory. The same ancient idea of dwelling and staying put — of a place one owns and remains in — persists in every sense, from the stately manor to the beat a constable patrols.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *men- → Latin manēre → mansio → Old French manoir → Anglo-French maner → Middle English maner → English manor [ˈmænə]; the -oir/-our/-or endings are the same Romance suffix spelled differently over time.
  • Semantic drift: "to remain, dwell" → "a dwelling, residence" → "the principal house and estate of a feudal lord" → "a unit of feudal land tenure" → "a great country house" → (slang) "one's local territory."
  • Socio-cultural factors: feudalism and the Norman Conquest made the manor the organizing unit of English land; the manorial system's decline left the word to name the surviving great houses; police slang revived the territorial sense.
  • Literary attestations: the Domesday Book (1086, maneria); medieval legal records; Austen's Pride and Prejudice (1813) — "the liberty of a manor"; 20th-century British crime and police fiction — "on my manor."
  • Modern situation: the dominant everyday sense is "large country house"; the feudal-legal sense survives in history and property law; the slang sense is alive in British English.

Few words carry their whole social history inside themselves as compactly as manor. It begins as a verb of staying, becomes the feudal unit of land, becomes the great house, and finally shrinks to a slang word for the patch of a street — always, in every form, about the place one dwells in and calls one's own.
