Aztecs has no Indo-European root; it is wholly Uto-Aztecan. The base is Classical Nahuatl Aztlan (often Aztlān), the legendary northern homeland from which seven nahuatlaca tribes were said to have migrated south to the Basin of Mexico. Nahuatl morphology is transparent: aztatl “heron, egret” or iztac “white” + locative -tlan “place abounding in” yields “place of herons / whiteness,” and the inhabitant suffix -tecatl / -ecatl (“person from”) yields aztecatl (plural aztecah) “one from Aztlan.” The Crónica Mexicayotl, Codex Boturini, and Codex Aubin depict the departure, with the Mexica as one Aztlan group among Xochimilca, Chalca, Tepaneca, Acolhua, Tlahuica, and Tlaxcalans.

Ironically the imperial Mexica of Tenochtitlan seldom called themselves Aztecs; they said Mexica, Tenochca, Nahua. Colonial Spaniards likewise wrote mexicanos, indios, tenochcas far more than aztecas. The modern umbrella is an 18th–19th-century learned revival: Jesuit Clavijero (Storia antica del Messico, 1780), Humboldt (Vues des cordillères, 1810), and above all William Prescott’s History of the Conquest of Mexico (1843) popularized French aztèque / English Aztec for the whole Triple Alliance civilization, needing one word where sources had many. English added the regular plural -s to give Aztecs, pronounced /ˈæztɛks/, and the adjective Aztec, Aztecan.

  • Mythic Aztlan to 1325: origin story: migration codices stage Aztlan as island mountain; aztecatl is a prestigious ancestral label.
  • 1325–1521: Mexica empire: foundation of Tenochtitlan (1325), Triple Alliance (1428), expansion under Ahuitzotl and Moctezuma; insiders say Mexica, outsiders pay tribute.
  • 1521–1780: eclipse: mexica survives as colonial Nahua identity; azteca rare in Spanish records.
  • 1780–1850: invention of the Aztecs: antiquarians resurrect azteca / aztèque / Aztec as classical parallel to Greeks and Romans; Calendar Stone (1790) and Kingsborough’s Antiquities fuel fascination.
  • 1850–present: global Aztec: from Chicano “Aztlán” to museum blocks, mascots, and games; linguists clarify Nahuatl belongs to Uto-Aztecan (with Hopi, Shoshoni, Yaqui), while activists reclaim Mexica over exonym Aztec.

Phonologically Nahuatl [asˈteːkatɬ] → Spanish [asˈteka] dropped the lateral affricate tl and vowel length, then English [ˈæztɛk] voiced the s to z by assimilation. Semantically the word widened from “emigrant from Aztlan” to “imperial Nahua” to “all Nahuatl high culture” to “brand for anything stepped-pyramid.” Debates persist on two points which the S2 note flags: the etymon of Aztlan (heron vs. white) and its geography (mythic vs. Straws Canyon vs. Mexcaltitán), plus the politics of Aztec vs. Mexica today — whether to keep the familiar exonym or adopt the emic name. What is undisputed is the non-PIE, fully Indigenous American origin, a reminder that English vocabulary for the Americas often bypasses Europe entirely.
