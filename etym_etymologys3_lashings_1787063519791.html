The difficulty of "lash." Lashings is the plural of lashing, formed from the verb to lash. That verb is actually two or three different words that have fallen together in English, and its history is unusually tangled. The oldest and most prominent sense — "to strike or flog with a whip, to move violently" — first appears in Middle English in the early fourteenth century as lasshen or lashen. Its origin is debated: the most common explanation is that it is imitative or echoic, reproducing the whistling sound of a whip cutting through the air; some scholars have also suggested connections to Germanic verbs meaning "to move violently" or to Old Norse words for whipping. No secure ancestor has been proven, and dictionaries accordingly mark the origin as uncertain.

The separate verb: to lash = to bind. A second, historically distinct verb lash means "to bind or fasten with cord or rope." This one is not of obscure Germanic stock but descends through Old French lacier ("to lace, to tie, to bind") from Latin laqueare, derived from laqueus, "noose, snare" — a Latin word built on the idea of a loop or snare. English took this lash into nautical use in the seventeenth century, and from it comes the noun lashing, "a rope or cord used for binding," still the standard word in sailing for the lines that secure cargo, spars, and masts. The near-homophony of the two verbs meant that from early on, "lashing" could mean either a beating or a binding, and both senses have always been present in the same word-family.

Lashing as beating. Once the strike-sense verb existed, the natural -ing noun lashing developed in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, meaning the act of striking and, by extension, a stroke or a beating. In the centuries of legal and naval flogging, "a lashing" was a real, everyday punishment: sailors and soldiers were given lashings as judicial discipline, and the word carried the full brutal weight of corporal punishment. This is the sense that survives in the fixed idea of "giving someone a good lashing," and it is the sense whose plural, lashings, Tolkien uses of the tail-blows in the mountain scene — blows that fall like judicial strokes but belong to a dragon rather than a court.

From punishment to weather. From beating, the word's energy passed into descriptions of violent natural movement: the sea "lashes" the shore, rain "lashes" the windows, a tail "lashes" from side to side. In these uses lashing loses all punitive feeling and simply means violent, repetitive striking. Tolkien's sentence sits exactly on this seam: Smaug's tail does not whip him as a punishment — it strikes and smashes with whipping force, so the "lashings of his huge tail" are blows without a punisher, pure destructive energy expressed in the language of the whip.

The strange birth of "lashings of." The most curious chapter is the idiom lashings of, "plenty, an abundance," first recorded in 1835 in British colloquial use. Its origin is genuinely uncertain: some have suggested it refers to a large quantity of something being "lashed up" or tied into bundles, others that it plays on the sense of liquid "lashing" about in a vessel, and still others that it is simply a jocular intensifier built on the forceful sound of the word. Whatever its source, the idiom had an astonishing career: it became the signature phrase of generous Victorian and Edwardian hospitality — "lashings of cream," "lashings of toast," "lashings of tea" — and remains alive today as a warm, nostalgic British idiom, a world away from the punishing lashings that gave the word its start.

Today. Modern English therefore carries all the accumulated senses at once: lashings as whip-strokes (literary and archaic), lashings as violent weather-striking, lashings as nautical rope-bindings, and lashings as "heaps of something good." Few English words switch so dramatically from violence to cosiness depending on context, and the plural form lashings — the form that survives in all of them — is the hinge on which the whole family turns. It is a reminder that one spelling can carry the history of a flogging, a storm, a ship's rigging, and a tea-party all at once.
