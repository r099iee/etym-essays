RE-PERUSAL is a compound: the prefix re- ("again") + the noun perusal ("a thorough reading"). Each component has its own chain. Chain 1 — the prefix re- "back, again": - PIE *wert-/*wret- "to turn" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; cf. Sanskrit vártate "turns", Latin vertere "to turn", the Germanic root of English -ward "turned toward") — uncertain, see note → Latin re- (before vowels and certain consonants red-) "back, again, anew; against" (prefix; Old Latin red-, e.g. reddere "to give back", redīre "to go back"; classical Latin re-, 3rd c. BCE onward) → English re- "again, back" (borrowed into English through Old French and learned Latin loans; productive as a living prefix from the 14th–15th c.) → RE- of RE-PERUSAL, "a perusal undertaken again." Note on debate: the ancestry of Latin re- is disputed. The traditional view connects it with PIE *wert- "to turn" (the idea of turning back); other scholars treat it as an Italic isolate or connect it with the Umbrian ret-; no attested stage stands between any PIE form and Latin re-, and the reconstructed root is a hypothesis. Chain 2 — the noun perusal "a thorough reading": - PIE *per(i)- "through, forward, beyond" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; cf. Sanskrit pári "around", Greek perí "around, concerning", and the Germanic *fer-/for- behind English for, forth, fore) → Latin per "through, throughout, by" (preposition, attested from the earliest Latin) → Latin per- (intensive prefix "thoroughly, to completion") → Anglo-Norman / Old French peruser "to use up, exhaust, finish; to examine thoroughly" [per- "thoroughly" + user "to use"] (attested 12th–13th c.) → Middle English perusen "to go through, examine item by item, read carefully and completely" (first recorded c. 1470) → English perusal "the action of reading through carefully and completely; a careful inspection" [peruse + suffix -al] (first recorded 16th c.) → English re-perusal "a repeated, thorough reading or examination of a text" [re- + perusal] (compound; the hyphenated form emphasizes the deliberate second pass; 19th-c. usage, as in the Mill biography) → Modern English RE-PERUSAL. Note: the verb base user "to use" descends from Latin ūtor, ūtī, ūsus "to use, employ, enjoy" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE); the ultimate origin of ūtor is disputed (sometimes connected with a PIE root of "use, enjoy"), and no intermediate stage is securely attested.
