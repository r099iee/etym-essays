Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *h₃er- 'to rouse, set in motion, stir up, arise' (reconstructed root; also the source of Greek órnumi 'to rouse', Latin orior 'to rise, be born', origin, and orient; some older sources cite a simpler *er-). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *ernustuz 'seriousness, zeal, vigor, struggle, fight' (reconstructed abstract noun, built with the *-ust- abstract suffix on a base related to *arniz 'efficient, capable, vigorous'; the root sense is energetic engagement). Stage 3 — Old English eornost, eornust 'earnestness, zeal, seriousness; a fight, battle' (attested from the Old English period, c. 9th century), alongside the adjective eornoste 'earnest, zealous, serious'. Stage 4 — Middle English ernest 'seriousness, zeal' (noun) and erneste 'serious, sincere' (adjective) (attested from the 13th century). Stage 5 — Modern English EARNEST 'serious, sincere, intense in purpose' (adjective) and 'seriousness of purpose' (noun, chiefly in the fixed phrase in earnest) (continuous from Early Modern English onward). Important homonym note: the legal and commercial noun earnest (in earnest money) is a DIFFERENT word of unrelated origin — a medieval loan through Old French erres 'pledge, deposit', from Latin arrha, from Greek arrhabṓn 'pledge, earnest money', ultimately from a Semitic root meaning 'pledge, surety'. It collided with the native word in Middle English spelling and is not part of the Germanic chain traced above. The derivation of the native word is secure; the only debated point is the precise internal structure of the Proto-Germanic abstract (whether directly from *arniz or from a parallel base), which does not affect the attested Old English onward sequence.
