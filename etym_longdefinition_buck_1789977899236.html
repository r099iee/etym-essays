buck is one of the most polysemous short words in English, and its senses fall into several historically connected clusters: an animal noun, a money noun, and a resistance / leaping verb, plus a set of compounds and idioms. The core, oldest sense is zoological: an adult male of certain mammals, especially deer, antelope, goats, rabbits, hares, rats and kangaroos. A buck deer is the male counterpart of a doe, typically bearing antlers; a billy-goat may be called a buck; a male rabbit is a buck to the female doe. This animal sense carries connotations of vigour, rutting energy, leaping and untamed masculinity, and it is the source of most of the extended senses.

The second great modern sense, chiefly American, is monetary: a buck is one US dollar, extended to dollars generally and to money itself (to make a quick buck, to earn big bucks). This comes from colonial buckskin as a unit of trade, shortened to buck. From there it broadened to any dollar, then to any unit of money in slang (a hundred bucks, twenty bucks). It is informal, everyday, and neutral in register, though commercial and sometimes slightly boastful. Related is the poker sense: the buck as a marker (buckhorn knife, silver dollar) passed to indicate the dealer, giving pass the buck and the buck stops here.

  • Animal noun — male deer / antelope / goat / rabbit: the primary dictionary sense. A buck is a mature male red deer, white-tailed deer, roe deer, pronghorn, gazelle, goat, hare or rabbit. The semantic bridge is maleness plus leaping, horned or vigorous wildness. Example: a six-point buck. Literary and hunting usage keeps this dominant in British English.
  • Money noun — dollar: American slang for one dollar, plural bucks for dollars generally. Bridge: frontier traders valued deerskins (buckskins) as currency units; one buckskin = one buck; then transferred to the dollar bill that replaced it. Now fully bleached of animal meaning for most speakers.
  • Verb — to resist, oppose, defy: to buck authority, to buck the trend, to buck the system. Bridge: from the image of a bucking horse or mule violently arching its back to throw a rider — hence to throw off, to strive against, to refuse to submit. Now used for people resisting trends, odds, conventions or orders.
  • Verb / noun — to buck / a buck (of a horse): a horse bucks when it leaps with back arched and head down to dislodge a rider; a buck is such a leap. Bridge: direct observation of male deer / wild goats leaping, transferred to broncos. Extended to bucking bronco rodeos, buckjumping, and metaphorically to any jerky upward jolt.
  • Sawhorse / support noun — buck: a frame for sawing logs (sawbuck), a gymnastics vaulting buck. Bridge: the four-legged animal stance gives its name to a four-legged wooden support; the X-shaped sawbuck recalls legs and antlers.
  • Rank noun — buck private / buck sergeant: the lowest enlisted rank in the US Army (buck private). Bridge: probably from buck as lowest / youngest male animal, or from buck as a common undistinguished man; hence the most junior, unadorned grade before stripes or chevrons.
  • Adjective / adverb in compounds — buck naked, buck teeth, buckshot, buckskin, buckwheat: buck naked means stark naked (perhaps from buckskin colour, or from butt-naked); buck teeth are large protruding upper teeth like a buck rabbit or beaver; buckshot is coarse lead shot for shooting bucks; buckskin is soft deerskin leather; buckwheat is beech-like grain (Dutch boeckweite, unrelated but absorbed by folk association). Each shows how the animal root ramified into material culture.
  • Idioms — pass the buck, the buck stops here, buck up, buckle down (related): pass the buck means to shift responsibility (from the poker buck marker); buck up means cheer up or brace oneself (brace like a buck resisting); to buckle under is to collapse. These preserve older social practices in frozen form.

In the reading context — “Perhaps it’s a __________?” — the dealer is guessing at an animal identity, almost certainly debating whether a pelt, carcass, pair of ears or hunted creature is a male deer or hare rather than a doe, consistent with the poaching / game-dealing scene. The hesitation marks the countryman’s expert eye: antlers, size and musculature distinguish buck from doe and determine price. The modern learner must therefore hold all senses at once: zoological maleness first, monetary slang second, and resistant leaping third, recognising that the dollar and defiance senses are American-grown metaphors from the leaping, tradable frontier animal.
