Chattering is the present participle and gerund of chatter: making rapid, light, repetitive sounds, or engaging in rapid, trivial speech. The word is fundamentally onomatopoeic — it sounds like what it names — and this imitative core explains why it applies so comfortably to birds, people, teeth and machines alike. As an adjective it describes the source of such sounds (“chattering teeth”, “chattering magpies”, “a chattering crowd”); as a gerund or noun it names the sound or activity itself (“the chattering of the monkeys”, “endless chattering”).

  • Sense 1 — animal sounds: of birds (magpies, jays, starlings), squirrels and monkeys, to chatter is to utter rapid, high-pitched cries; naturalists and nature writers use the word as the standard term for such calls, and it is the oldest attested sense of the verb.
  • Sense 2 — human speech, pejorative: of people, chattering is rapid, continuous, trivial talk — idle gossip or nervous gabble. The word's tone is mildly to strongly dismissive: a “chattering” person is not conversing but making noise. This human application is nearly as old as the bird sense (Middle English chateren meant “to talk rapidly, jabber”).
  • Sense 3 — teeth: “chattering teeth” are teeth clicking together rapidly and involuntarily from cold, fear or fever (the shivering reflex). The sound is exactly like rapid speech, which is the semantic bridge — the teeth seem to be “talking”. The image is a stock of Gothic and Victorian fiction and of medical writing on ague, malaria and exposure.
  • Sense 4 — machinery: in engineering, chatter (often “chattering”) is the unwanted self-excited vibration of a cutting tool or machine part during operation, producing a rough surface and a rattling noise; the word was extended from sounds to the physical vibration itself in 20th-century technical usage (tool chatter, relay chatter, brake chatter).
  • Sense 5 — objects rattling: historically the verb could also mean simply “to rattle”, of doors, windows, carts and loose fittings (“the window chattered in the gale”); this general rattle sense is the bridge between the animal sound and the machine sense.
  • Sense 6 — figurative noise: “the chattering of the crowd”, “chattering phones and keyboards” — any rapid, jumbled, low-level soundscape; in media and intelligence usage, “chatter” denotes the unverified rumour and small talk circulating on networks (“online chatter”, “radio chatter”).
  • Sense 7 — fixed phrases: chatterbox (a persistently talkative person, especially a child; recorded from 1774), chitter-chatter (reduplicated idle talk, 18th century), and the British political sneer the chattering classes (coined in 1980s journalism for media-friendly, liberal, middle-class commentators).

Semantic bridges: every sense is an extension of the sound. The word was coined to imitate the rapid clicking and twittering of birds; applying it to people's rapid talk treats speech as bird-noise — trivial, unthinking; applying it to teeth transfers the same clicking sound to the body's involuntary response to cold or terror; and engineers, hearing the same rattle in a lathe or drill, borrowed it for the vibration itself. The pejorative charge — noise without meaning — runs through all the human and social uses: chattering is sound without substance, whether it comes from a gossip, a monkey or a milling machine.
