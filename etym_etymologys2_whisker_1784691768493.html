PIE *weis- “to flow, ooze” or *wisk- “to turn, twist, whirl, sweep” (debated, see note; the Germanic family of whisk / whisker is often treated as of obscure Germanic origin without a certain PIE etymon) → Proto-Germanic *wiskaz / *wiskōn “a bundle of twigs or hay; to sweep with a bundle” (reconstructed; the Scandinavian and continental Germanic forms are well attested) → Old Norse visk “a bundle of hay or straw” and visp “a wisp” (→ English wisp, a loan); Old High German wisc, Middle Low German / Middle Dutch wisch “a wisp, a small brush”; Old Swedish viska “to wipe” (11th–13th c.) → Middle English whisken “to move with a quick sweeping stroke” (c. 1375) → English whisk (noun) “a small bundle of twigs used for sweeping; a quick sweeping motion” (c. 1400) → English whisker (agent noun) “a whisk-broom; one who whisks” (early 16th c.) → English whisker “the hair of the moustache and side-whiskers, from its likeness to a whisk-broom” (1580s) → English whisker “a vibrissa of a cat, dog, rat, or weasel; figuratively by a whisker, by the narrowest of margins” (17th–20th c.) => whisker (modern English noun) DEBATE / NUANCE: The Germanic line (whisken → whisk → whisker → hair) is fully attested and secure. The deeper origin is debated: the family is often connected to PIE *weis- “to flow / ooze” via the sense of sweeping motion, or set up as a separate Germanic root *wisk-, and many dictionaries mark the ultimate Indo-European source as uncertain or obscure. The English word was reinforced by Scandinavian forms (Old Norse visk, visp). The animal-sensory sense is a late transfer: whiskers (vibrissae) are named after the human face-hair, not the reverse.
