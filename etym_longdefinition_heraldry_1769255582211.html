heraldry (noun, uncountable): the ancient and highly codified system by which coats of arms, armorial bearings, badges, crests, and related hereditary insignia are devised, granted, blazoned (described), and regulated — the art, science, and legal discipline that surrounds hereditary symbols of identity, rank, and lineage.

1. Primary sense — the systematic art of armorial bearings

In its central, dictionary sense, heraldry is the whole apparatus of inherited visual identity built around the coat of arms: a shield (the escutcheon) charged with figures (the charges), coloured by tinctures (metals such as argent = silver/white and or = gold; colours such as gules = red, azure = blue, sable = black, vert = green, purpure = purple), and often accompanied by a crest, helm, mantling, supporters, and motto. It is not mere decoration; it is governed by strict traditional rules — including the famous rule of tincture (metal must not be laid upon metal, nor colour upon colour) — that determine what may legally and conventionally appear on a shield, who may bear it, and how it must be drawn.
  • Blazonry — the precise, compact, quasi-grammatical language used to describe a coat of arms in words ("Azure, a lion rampant Or"); a skilled herald can reproduce a shield exactly from its blazon alone.
  • Arms, crest, and badge — the armorial achievement normally includes the shield (arms proper), the crest above the helm, and the badge, a simpler device used on livery, banners, and seals.
  • Cadency and differencing — marks (such as the label, crescent, and mullet) that distinguish the branches of one family so that different sons and descendants do not bear identical arms.
  • Marshalling and quartering — the combining of two or more coats on one shield (impalement, quartering, augmentation) to display marriage alliances and inherited honours.
  • Regulation — in the English-speaking world heraldry is administered by officers of arms (the College of Arms in England and Wales, the Court of the Lord Lyon in Scotland), who grant, confirm, and record arms in their registers.

2. The historical function — identity, lineage, and property

Heraldry grew out of the practical needs of medieval warfare and court life, not out of pure aesthetics:
  • From the 12th century the closed great helm hid a knight's face, and the painted shield became his identity card on the battlefield and in the tournament; by about 1250 hereditary arms were spreading across the European nobility.
  • Arms were marks of status and of legal title; before surnames functioned as full legal records a coat of arms could serve as a signature, a seal, and a sign of ownership of land and goods.
  • Heralds — the officers who gave the science its name — began as messengers and masters of ceremony who announced battles, declared war, and conducted tournaments; by the later Middle Ages they were the genealogists, record-keepers, and court officials who granted and adjudicated arms, and their records (the Tudor visitations in England) became the foundation of English genealogy.

3. Technical jargon and its modern afterlife

Heraldry has bequeathed a rich vocabulary to general English — blazon, blazonry, tincture, crest, achievement, supporter, motto, charge, quartering, cadency — and the discipline survives today not only for royalty and nobility but also for civic arms (towns, universities, guilds), military and regimental badges, corporate logos, national flags, and sports emblems, many of which are still designed and registered according to heraldic rules.

4. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings

  • Ceremonial pageantry — by extension, heraldry can name grand, colourful, formal ceremonial display: the pageantry and heraldry of a state occasion, the pomp of a coronation, a royal wedding, or a great procession, where banners, tabards, and officers of arms make the spectacle.
  • Figurative "heraldry" — in modern, often journalistic or metaphorical use, the word can denote the distinctive symbolic insignia of any institution, brand, or cause — "the heraldry of a football club," "the corporate heraldry of a tech giant" — the badges and emblems by which an organisation announces and remembers its identity.
  • Genealogical sense — loosely, heraldry can stand for the study and record of noble lineage generally, the whole antiquarian science of pedigree and family honour of which the arms are the visual emblem; "heraldry and genealogy" is a standing pairing in the title of learned societies.

5. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge

The secondary senses grow naturally out of the primary one. Because heraldry is above all the art of regulated, hereditary symbols of identity and rank, it easily widens in two directions: inward, toward the whole science of pedigree and family record that the heralds administered; and outward, toward the colourful ceremonial apparatus (tunics, banners, trumpets, processions) in which heralds and their arms appear. The medieval officer who cried a tournament and the modern badge of a nation or a corporation are, in the word's eye, the same thing: a display of formal, hereditary, symbolic identity.

Words in the same family: herald (the officer, and by metaphor any announcer or forerunner — "a herald of spring"), heraldic (the adjective), heraldist (one versed in the science), and blazon (both the verb "to blazon" and the noun).
