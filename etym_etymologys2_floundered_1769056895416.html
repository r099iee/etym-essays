The origin of flounder is genuinely obscure, so the chain below follows the leading scholarly hypothesis (the OED's founder + blunder blend) and flags the alternatives explicitly. Chain via the standard blend hypothesis: - *bhudh- "bottom, foundation" (Proto-Indo-European, reconstructed; cf. Sanskrit budhná "bottom, ground", English bottom) → Latin fundus "bottom, ground, base" (classical) → Vulgar Latin *fundorāre "to found, to sink" (Late Latin / Romance) → Old French fondrer "to fall, to sink, to founder" (Medieval French) → Middle English foundren → English founder (verb) "to sink, to collapse" (14th century; the nautical sense, a ship taking water) - Old Norse blundra "to shut the eyes, to stumble" (Old Norse) → Middle English blundren "to stumble blindly" → English blunder (verb) "to act clumsily, to make a mistake" (14th century) → blend of founder + blunder → English flounder (verb) "to struggle clumsily, to move with difficulty" (first recorded 1592) → flounder + past-tense suffix -ed → floundered (the inflected form on this card) Note (debate and nuance): The verb's origin is disputed. (1) The founder + blunder blend is the standard OED view. (2) A second hypothesis derives flounder from Middle Low German / Dutch flodderen "to flounder, to splash about in mud or water", making it a borrowing rather than a coinage. (3) A third treats it as an independent English onomatopoeic / expressive formation imitating splashing and thrashing. All three remain live possibilities, and the honest scholarly answer is that no single origin is proved. English flounder (noun) "the flatfish" is a different word — from Anglo-French floundre / Old French flondre, from Old Norse flyðra "flatfish" — and is deliberately kept separate here.
