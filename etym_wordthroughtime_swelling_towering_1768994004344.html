- swelling — Old English swellan; the participle swelling (attested from the 14th century) first describes living distension from within — a wound, a river in flood, bread in the oven: growth under pressure, organic and liquid.
- — From the 16th–18th centuries the participle turns figurative: swelling pride, swelling rage, swelling sound — inward pressure spilling over into emotion and volume, still carrying the feel of something alive inflating itself.
- — In late 19th-century American slang the word inverts: a 'swell' fellow is excellent, elegant — the pressure drained away, the participle reduced to praise.
- — Contemporary: swelling returns to body, water, and statistics — medical distension, rising tides and numbers, growing mass — its old organic energy intact, its slang glamour forgotten.
- towering — First attested in the 17th century as the participle of tower: 'rising like a tower,' height that dominates everything around it — static and monumental rather than airborne.
- — 18th–19th centuries: applied to cliffs, trees, and figures that overtop the world, and turned figurative — towering rage, towering ambition — height converted into magnitude of passion.
- — Contemporary: overwhelmingly figurative (a towering achievement, a towering intellect); the concrete sense survives in geology and architecture, always with the awestruck, slightly menacing dignity of a thing that holds height rather than merely reaches it.
