- Early 17th c. (c. 1620s–1650s) - Borrowed into Early Modern English initially as a neutral historical and political descriptor referring to the administrative or dynastic titles of Byzantine and Ottoman rulers (*despotēs* / *despot*).
- Mid to Late 18th c. (Enlightenment & Revolutions) - Under the profound influence of Montesquieu's *L'Esprit des Lois* and the American and French Revolutions, the term acquired an intensely pejorative moral and constitutional charge, crystallizing as the ultimate antithesis of constitutional liberty and designating any arbitrary rule unconstrained by law.
- Late 18th to 19th c. (Victorian Literature & Domesticity) - Widened through philosophical essays and domestic literature to denote not merely imperial sovereigns, but tyrannical familial authority (the Victorian "despotic father") and intellectual or religious intolerance in ecclesiastical bodies.
- 20th c. (Bureaucracy & Psychology) - Extended into psychological, corporate, and organizational discourse to describe micromanaging executives, dogmatic institutional cultures, and the tyrannical command of obsessive habits or destructive passions over the human will.
- Contemporary Usage - Maintained in geopolitical analysis to characterize autocratic regimes that subvert democratic institutions, while colloquially serving as an evocative descriptor for anyone who exercises petty, uncompromising dominion within a localized sphere.
