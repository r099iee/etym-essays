PROVENANCE (noun): the place of origin, source, or documented history of something — the record of where a thing comes from and through whose hands it has passed. In this passage from a treatise on English poetic rhythm the author explains that since his examples serve only to illustrate general points about metre, "questions of provenance and textual detail are usually irrelevant". Provenance here means the full history of a text's origin and transmission — in what manuscript or early edition it is found, who attributed it, and how it reached modern readers. The tone is scholarly pragmatism: the author declines the whole apparatus of textual criticism because his subject is rhythm, not bibliography.

The word entered English as a neutral, bookish term for "origin or source", and its dominant modern use is specialised: in art history and archaeology, provenance is the documented chain of origin and ownership that guarantees an object's genuineness.

  • Primary sense: "the place of origin or earliest known history of something" ("the provenance of the term", "the manuscript's provenance").
  • Art and the antique trade: "the documented chain of ownership of a work of art" — the biography of a painting or sculpture from its creation through collectors, dealers, and exhibitions; a work with full provenance commands higher prices and resists forgery claims.
  • Manuscript and textual scholarship: "the history of a text's transmission — scribe, copyist, library, collector — establishing how a text survived".
  • Archaeology and museums: the record of an artefact's excavation site, stratigraphic context, and custody; provenance is now inseparable from provenance ethics, the demand that artefacts be shown to have been lawfully exported.
  • Geology and mineralogy: "the origin site of a mineral or rock" (the provenance of sediments).
  • Forestry and seed science: "the geographic origin of seed stock", which determines the hardiness of planted trees.
  • Computer science and data: "data provenance", the documented lineage of data from its source through transformations, used for verification and audit.

The historical bridge from "origin" to "authenticated ownership" is the museum's need to verify: once a thing's full history is documented, its genuineness is established. The word's uniqueness is that it belongs to objects and texts rather than to living beings: a painting, manuscript, or coin has provenance; a person has ancestry, parentage, or pedigree. The register is formal and increasingly technical, carrying an aura of authentication, connoisseurship, and curatorial expertise.

In collocation, provenance takes the prepositions of and for: "the provenance of the painting", "provenance research", "a question of provenance", "to trace the provenance". It is frequently attributive — "provenance information", "provenance records", "a provenance statement" — and it appears characteristically in the vocabulary of auction catalogues, museum accession files, art criticism, and, increasingly, forensic computing. In academic writing the word is often used with a hint of self-exculpation, as here: to declare questions of provenance "irrelevant" is to say that one is not practising textual or art-historical criticism. The distinction between provenance and its synonyms is consistently one of custody: origin points to the beginning, source to the outlet, derivation to the route, but provenance alone names the authenticated record of ownership and transmission — the chain of hands through which a thing has passed — and it is precisely this documentary, institutional sense that the modern word has made its own.
