ALLUDED (/əˈluːdɪd/) is the past-tense and past-participle form of the verb allude — to make a covert, passing, or oblique reference to something or someone, without naming it directly. When something is “alluded to”, the speaker or writer points toward an object by implication, echo, or suggestion, leaving the audience to recognise the referent. In the card’s source context (“no songs had alluded to him even in the obscurest way”), the word carries exactly this force: no song had so much as hinted at the hobbit, even in the faintest fashion.

Core sense — indirect reference: The verb normally takes the preposition to: one “alludes to” a person, a text, an event, or an idea. The allusion may be a polite veiling, a literary homage, a knowing joke, or an evasive dodge — but in every case the referent is not spelled out.

  • Sense 1 — to refer indirectly or in passing: the central modern sense; e.g. “the novel alluded to the war without naming it”. The emphasis is on obliqueness and lightness of touch.
  • Sense 2 — to hint at or suggest: closely allied, but pointing toward something not yet revealed; an author may “allude to” a future event, a politician “allude to” coming changes.
  • Sense 3 — literary allusion: in criticism, the noun allusion names the deliberate echo of another text (biblical, classical, folkloric); “alluded to” is the action verb behind such references — a hallmark of works dense with tradition, such as Tolkien’s, whose source context is a good example of a text that alludes constantly to older heroic song.
  • Sense 4 — archaic “to play upon words; to jest or mock”: the oldest English sense, inherited from Latin alludere (“to play with, jest at”); a writer might “allude” a word — play upon it — and the mockery sense survives into the 16th century before dying out.
  • Sense 5 — rare, “to compare or liken”: an early secondary sense in which allusion shades into simile or comparison, now obsolete.

Register and nuance: Alluded is a deliberate, literary verb. It implies choice and craft: the referent was pointed to on purpose, yet left undecoded. Compare the bluntness of mentioned and the directness of referred: to allude is to leave a trail rather than a name. In journalism the verb often softens unwelcome content (“he alluded to, but did not confirm, the scandal”), and in criticism it is the technical word for intertextual echoing. The past form alluded is morphologically regular (allude – alluded – alluded) and always takes to before its object.

Historical bridge: Latin alludere (from ad- “to, toward” + lūdere “to play”) literally meant “to play toward”, hence “to jest at, to touch upon playfully”. English borrowed the verb in the 1530s with the mockery sense, then shifted it, under the influence of French and of the image of “playing upon” a subject, toward its modern meaning of touching a matter obliquely. The allusion is thus, etymologically, a game — a playful brushing of the surface of a thing rather than a direct grasp of it.
