The first half of merrymaking carries one of the prettiest semantic journeys in English, beginning with brevity itself. The reconstructed root *mregh-u- meant short, and its Germanic descendant *murgijaz meant short-lived or fleeting, a word for things that do not last. How fleeting became joyful is the poet's logic of transience: pleasures are sweet precisely because they pass, so the word for brief duration slid into the word for the delight that brief things give. Old English myrge or mirige already shows the crossing complete, meaning pleasant, agreeable, and delightful, applied to music, weather, and company, with the old sense of shortness surviving only as a faint etymological perfume. Few English words hide so wistful a philosophy inside so cheerful a surface: merry remembers that joy is short, and loves it for exactly that.

Middle English made cheerfulness active and slightly intoxicated. The adjective mirie broadened across songs, tales, and companions, while the fixed invitation to make mirie, to be merry, to feast and rejoice, became one of the period's great festive formulas, commanded in carols and promised in romances. In the same centuries the tipsy sense crystallized: a merry cup was a strong one, and merry men might be Robin Hood's loyal band or simply drunk ones, the ambiguity usually deliberate. The Great Vowel Shift lifted the vowel toward its modern height, and spelling settled through mery to merry, while the meaning stabilized as cheerful with intoxication hovering permanently at the edges, ready to colour any festive use.

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were the golden age of making merry. Shakespeare's clowns and revellers make merry across the comedies, the phrase carrying both innocent feasting and disreputable roistering depending on who speaks it; the Authorized Version's eat, drink, and be merry gave the idiom scriptural dignity; and the nostalgic invention of Merry England located national virtue in imagined medieval festivity. In this climate the gerund compound merrymaking fused and flourished, naming the whole business of communal rejoicing as a recognized social art. Puritan moralists attacked it as profane excess, which only confirms how central it had become: to denounce merrymaking was to denounce the people's calendar of pleasure itself.

The second half of the compound has a humbler but equally ancient pedigree. The root *mag-, to knead or fashion with the hands, gave Proto-Germanic *makōjan, to make or construct, thence Old English macian and Middle English maken, one of the commonest verbs in the language. In the festive idiom maken does light causal duty, to make mirie being simply to produce merriment, cause cheer, put joy into action; the compound then nominalizes the whole event, parallel to peacemaking and mischief-making. Phonetically macian lost its palatal fricative and levelled its vowels into modern make, while the compound kept the older double r of merry and the participial -ing that marks ongoing collective action.

From the eighteenth century onward the compound drifted toward the rustic and the literary as polite vocabulary moved on to entertainment, amusement, and eventually partying. Harvest-home descriptions, Christmas sketches, and children's stories preserved it as the sound of traditional England rejoicing, and twentieth-century fantasy adopted it wholesale: taverns, inns, and Shire fields merrymake in fiction precisely because the word refuses modernity. Journalism rediscovered the same property, deploying New Year's Eve merrymaking whenever street crowds should sound hearty rather than threatening. So the fused noun now lives a charmed double life, archaic enough to be picturesque and familiar enough to need no gloss, carrying inside it a four-thousand-year meditation that begins with shortness, passes through cheer, and ends, on Bilbo's birthday field, as the sound of other people's happiness heard smilingly from afar.

  • Shortness to sweetness: PIE *mregh-u-, short, becomes Germanic *murgijaz, fleeting joy, the founding metaphor that pleasure is precious because it passes.
  • Old and Middle English cheer: myrge to mirie, pleasant and then actively festive, with drink joining the family and the idiom to make mirie commanding celebration.
  • Golden age and fusion: Shakespeare, scripture, and Merry England entrench the idiom while merrymaking fuses into the name of communal rejoicing as a social art.
  • Humble making: PIE *mag-, to knead, through Germanic *makōjan and Old English macian, supplying the causal half of the compound.
  • Rustic retreat and literary return: polite society abandons the word to villages and storybooks, then fantasy and journalism revive it as deliberate nostalgia.
