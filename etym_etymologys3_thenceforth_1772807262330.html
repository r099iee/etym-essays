The history of thenceforth is a history of English deixis, of adverbial morphology, and of register. The word is young as compounds go — it crystallized in late Middle English and early Modern English — yet it bundles together processes that took more than two thousand years to produce. The demonstrative foundation. The story begins with the PIE demonstrative stem *to-, the same material that yields the English articles and pronouns the, that, and there. In Proto-Germanic this stem supplied *þan-, the base of the temporal adverb *þana "at that time," and English inherited it as Old English þonne and þænne, both meaning "then, at that time." The Old English form fluctuated between back and front vowels (þonne vs. þænne) — a fluctuation inherited from the ablaut patterns of the demonstrative paradigm — and Middle English kept both reflexes as thenne and thanne before they settled, by the 15th century, on the single form then. Alongside its temporal reading, Old English þær / ther and related forms carried spatial senses, so that the "then" family straddled place and time from the beginning. The -s adverb. Old English formed a small set of adverbs with a genitive -s (or -es) suffix — words such as dæges "by day," nīhtes "by night" — and this suffix was extended to pronominal and deictic adverbs. The combination of thenne with -s produced thennes, meaning "from that place, from that time," attested by the 13th century. The same process produced hennes "hence" and whennes "whence," and the three forms together (thennes, hennes, whennes) show the analogical spread of one morphological trick across a whole deictic paradigm. Over the 14th and 15th centuries the double -n- of thennes eroded and the spelling settled as thence, sometimes thens, with the unetymological -ce spelling of the standard form coming from the parallel of hence and once. The older spatial sense "from that place" remained alive well into the 16th century — Shakespeare still uses thence with a fully locative meaning ("I came from thence," "go hence") — so that when the compound was built, the spatial flavour was still available to be carried along. The second element: forth. Forth descends from PIE *per- / *pro-, the root of "forward, before, in front," which in Proto-Germanic gave *furþ- and in Old English forþ "forth, forward, onward." Old English forþ was an extremely productive word: it combined with verbs (forthbringan "to bring forth," forþgangan "to go forth") and appeared in phrasal compounds of motion and continuity. Middle English forth kept this full range. The root's relatives — for, fore, from, first, former — all descend from the same PIE stock, making the family one of the deepest layers of the native vocabulary. The compound and its rivals. By the late 14th century, thennes "from that place/time" and forth "forward" were fused into thennesforth, a temporal adverb meaning "from that time forward." The 15th century shows a lively competition of spellings and near-doublets: thennesforth, thensforth, thensseforth, thenseforth, and thennesforward, alongside the parallel forms hennesforth (later henceforth) and whennesforth. The spelling thenceforth, regularized on the model of henceforth, had won the field by the 16th century; thenceforward and thenceforwards continued as rarer variants. This morphological elasticity is typical of the period: English had a rich stock of deictic particles (-ce, -s, -ward, -forth, -after) and combined them freely, and only the fixing of the standard language in the 17th–18th centuries froze the winners in place. Sound change. The word's phonology tracks the broader history of English. Old English þ [θ] survives as modern th; the fronting and shortening of the vowel in thenne reflects the general unrounding and shortening of vowels in late Old and Middle English; and the -ce spelling, though historically a scribal convention, later conditioned the [s] that remains in the pronunciation /ðɛnsfˈɔːθ/. The medial cluster nsf proved durable, and the final -th of forth kept its voiceless fricative, so that the compound's pronunciation today is almost transparently a sum of its parts. Register and literary life. Thenceforth was never a colloquial word. It flourished where permanent change had to be recorded: in chronicles of reigns and successions, in legal instruments where a new status was ratified, and in the King James Bible, where covenant language needs an adverb of irrevocable consequence ("And they were thenceforth no more strangers"). Malory's Morte Darthur and the English prose of the 15th–17th centuries use it to close an episode and open a new dispensation. As spoken English moved toward more analytic time-adverbials (from then on, after that), thenceforth retreated into the written and the formal. Today it is best understood as a stylistic instrument: a single syllable-light word that compresses "and this new state of affairs, once established, held permanently from that point on" into the register of the statute book and the historical novel. In the Red Chamber passage its use is precisely that: the narrator ratifies, in the formal language of record, a change of identity that will govern the rest of the story. Summary of the drift. The overall trajectory is from spatial pointing to temporal pointing to stylistic elevation. What began as a demonstrative "that" (PIE *to-) became a temporal adverb "at that time" (then), then a spatial adverb "from that place" (thence) through the genitive -s, then a compound meaning "from that time forward" (thenceforth) when the temporal reading absorbed the spatial one, and finally an archaic marker of ceremonial finality. Each shift is a small step in deixis — from place to time to register — and the word preserves all three layers like sediment in its own structure.
