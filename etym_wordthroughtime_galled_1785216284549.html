- [Old English: the bitter fluid] - gall is the English name for bile, the bitter yellow-green secretion of the liver; it also names bitterness of spirit, in 'gall and wormwood,' a bodily term already leaning toward metaphor.
- [14th century: from fluid to wound] - the noun gains a new physical sense: the raw, inflamed sore raised on a horse's back by a chafing saddle, so named because the abraded flesh was thought to fill with a yellow, bilious discharge; the word now names an unglamorous, gritty wound.
- [1550s-1570s: the verb and its two faces] - to gall is formed meaning 'to chafe, make sore'; within two decades the figurative sense 'to irritate, vex, humiliate deeply' grows beside it, so that a galling remark stings like a galled shoulder - flesh-wound and humiliation said in one word.
- [19th century: impudence] - 'to have the gall' adds the flavour of bitter audacity, the same bilious bitterness turned into brazen nerve.
- [Contemporary: two senses held together] - galled still works literally (skin abraded by friction) and figuratively (deeply irritated or humiliated), and its grittiest power is keeping both alive at once - as with the Ring that galled Gollum, wearing his flesh raw and his spirit alike.
