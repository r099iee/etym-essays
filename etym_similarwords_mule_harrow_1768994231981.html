• Donkey / ass: Similar to mule as a sturdy, stubborn work-animal of the same hybrid family; differs because the donkey is a separate species (the mule's parent), slower and smaller, while the mule is the deliberate hybrid combining donkey endurance with horse size and strength.
  • Hinny: Similar because it is also a horse-donkey hybrid; differs because the hinny is the reverse cross (stallion × jenny), generally smaller and less prized than the mule, which comes from a jack × mare.
  • Horse: Similar because both are equines and the mule's other parent; differs because the horse is prized for speed, status, and display, while the mule is the unglamorous draft and pack animal of endurance and hardscrabble labor.
  • Jack / jenny: Similar because both are donkeys, the mule's parents; differs because jack and jenny name the male and female donkey specifically, while mule names the sterile hybrid offspring rather than either parent species.
  • Plough / plow: Similar to harrow as a primary tool of tillage that follows the mule's work; differs because the plough cuts and turns the soil in furrows, while the harrow crushes clods and levels the surface after the plough — the second, finishing stage of cultivation.
  • Rake: Similar because both are toothed implements drawn over soil to break and level it; differs because the rake is a light hand-tool for gathering or smoothing, while the harrow is a heavy animal-drawn frame of iron teeth for breaking plough-clods at field scale.
  • Drag / clod-crusher: Similar because both level and break soil after ploughing; differs because these are heavier, often blunt smoothing implements, while the harrow's teeth actively tear and break the clods rather than merely pressing them down.
  • Harry / harass: Similar to the figurative harrow because both mean to distress or trouble repeatedly; differs because harry (from Old English hergian) means to raid, pester, or press with repeated attacks, while to harrow the heart is to wound or lacerate it in a single devastating blow.
  • Torment / rack: Similar to the figurative harrow in inflicting intense distress; differs because torment and rack name deliberate, often prolonged infliction, while a harrowing experience is one that tears at the emotions — the figure preserves the image of iron teeth raking the heart.
  • Lacerate: Similar because both involve tearing or wounding deeply; differs because lacerate is literal bodily tearing (of flesh), while harrow is the metaphorical application of the same tearing image to the mind and feelings.
