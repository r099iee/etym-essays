• Anger: Similar because both name the emotion of strong displeasure; differs because anger is the everyday, general noun usable of trivial irritations, while ire is formal, literary, and reserved for intense, notable anger.
  • Wrath: Similar because both denote intense anger, often of great persons or gods; differs because wrath is the epic Germanic and biblical word carrying overtones of divine or vengeful punishment, while ire is the latinate, colder, more controlled form.
  • Fury: Similar because both mean violent, intense anger; differs because fury implies frenzied, nearly uncontrollable rage, while ire can be cold, dignified indignation as well as hot anger.
  • Rage: Similar because both mean violent anger; differs because rage stresses the violent, often uncontrolled outburst and can also mean a furious passion or craze, while ire is more composed and formal.
  • Indignation: Similar because both name strong displeasure; differs because indignation is specifically anger at what is perceived as unjust or unworthy, while ire need not carry that moral judgement.
  • Resentment: Similar because both name lasting displeasure; differs because resentment is a brooding, long-held sense of grievance, while ire is an acute, usually momentary blaze of anger.
  • Choler: Similar because both derive from the old humoral theory of anger (the yellow bile, the choleric temperament); differs because choler is now archaic and technical, while ire remains a live literary and journalistic word.
  • Outrage: Similar because both can name a public eruption of anger; differs because outrage also names the offensive act itself (an outrage) and stresses a reaction to insult or injury, while ire simply names the anger.
  • Vexation: Similar because both name a state of displeasure; differs because vexation is mild irritation or annoyance, a far weaker emotion than the intense anger that ire denotes.
