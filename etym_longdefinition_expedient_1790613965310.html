Expedient functions both as adjective and as countable noun, united by a single relational idea: serving to promote the end in view under present circumstances. As an adjective it means conducive to advantage, suitable to the occasion, practical and effective for achieving an immediate purpose: it is expedient to compromise, an expedient solution, politically expedient timing. As a noun, often in the plural expedients, it means a means, measure, device, or contrivance employed to achieve an end, especially a stopgap, makeshift, or emergency resort adopted for want of something better: carrying sacks of objects as an expedient to abolish words, a wartime expedient, desperate expedients. Both forms presuppose a goal, constraints, and a calculation of fitness.

In its primary adjectival sense of practically advantageous, expedient evaluates an action as useful, fitting, and prudent relative to circumstances rather than in the abstract. The Authorized Version gives the classic theological use: It is expedient for us, that one man should die for the people (John 11:50, Caiaphas), and It is expedient for you that I go away (John 16:7). Here expedient does not mean morally ideal but instrumentally necessary for a larger good as perceived by the speaker. Modern collocations preserve this prudential flavour: expedient course, expedient policy, expedient to wait, expedient to delay. The semantic bridge from etymology is transparent once known: Latin expedire literally to free the feet from a snare, to disentangle, to make ready for marching. What frees the feet clears the way, what clears the way advances the march, what advances the march is useful. From untying feet to clearing obstacles to being advantageous is a chain of generalization by pragmatic inference.

In its secondary adjectival sense, strongly pejorative in contemporary English, expedient means governed by immediate self-interest or short-term advantage rather than by principle, justice, honesty, or long-term wisdom. A merely expedient lie, an expedient marriage, expedient silence, politically expedient hypocrisy all imply sacrifice of the right to the useful. Collocation with mere, sheer, purely, cynically, shamefully marks disapproval, as does the abstract noun expediency or expedience in phrases like the politics of expediency, sacrificed principle to expediency. The bridge from neutral usefulness to moral dubiousness is evaluative narrowing driven by cultural suspicion of consequentialism: if what is useful right now can conflict with what is right, then calling something expedient hints that it may be useful but wrong. Philosophy from Cicero's De Officiis contrasting honestum the honourable with utile the useful, through Machiavelli, Bentham, and Weber on the ethics of responsibility, provides the intellectual backdrop for this split between praise for prudence and blame for opportunism.

In its tertiary nominal sense, an expedient is a contrived practical means itself, often with overtones of ingenuity under pressure and provisionality. Lexicographers distinguish expedient from near-synonyms by this temporary-improvised profile: unlike a solution which resolves, an expedient merely gets by; unlike a strategy which plans, an expedient improvises; unlike a stratagem which deceives, an expedient need only work. Swift's satire in Gulliver's Travels, Voyage to Lagado, weaponizes exactly this dignity: An expedient was therefore offered, that since words are only names for things, it would be more convenient for all men to carry about them such things as were necessary to express the particular business they are to discourse on. The professors solemnly dub lugging sacks of furniture on one's back an expedient, exposing how bureaucratic and academic language sanctifies absurd inconvenience as clever practicality. Swift exploits both layers — ostensibly a device, actually an encumbrance — to show that calling something expedient does not make it sensible.

Domain usages refine the core. In law and governance, expedient belongs to statecraft and emergency powers: expedient measures, meet the exigency by suitable expedients, martial-law expedients. In military history, Roman expeditus meant light-armed, unencumbered by baggage (impedimenta, whence impediment), ready to march; the modern echo is rapid-deployment pragmatics. In business and management, expedient praises resourcefulness — an expedient workaround, expedient outsourcing — but warns against short-termism. In ethics and theology, it anchors the perennial debate whether the end justifies the means. In everyday conversation, it is expedient to... sounds formal and slightly archaic, lending bureaucratic weight to advice. Across domains the constant relational structure remains: an agent, a desired end, constraining circumstances, and a means judged by fitness rather than intrinsic worth, with moral colour supplied by context.

  • Adjective: practically advantageous and fitting: tending to promote a desired object under present conditions — it is expedient to postpone the decision, a wise and expedient compromise. Bridge is etymological generalization from freeing feet to clearing the way to being useful; reasoning is means-end fitness.
  • Adjective: self-interested and unprincipled (pejorative): sacrificing principle to immediate advantage — a merely expedient alliance, politically expedient lies. Bridge is moral narrowing and implicature: emphasis on usefulness at the expense of rightness invites inference of opportunism.
  • Noun: contrived means or makeshift resort: a device adopted to meet a difficulty — carrying sacks as an expedient to abolish language, a temporary expedient, wartime expedients. Bridge is reification: the quality of being expedient embodied in a thing or plan; reasoning is nominalization of the adjective.
  • Political and legal expedients: emergency measures of state — legislative expedients, fiscal expedients, expedients of war. Bridge is institutionalization: what is expedient for a ruler becomes an official measure, often temporary and extra-legal, revealing the link between expediency and exception.
  • Philosophical expedient vs right: the expedient as the useful in tension with the good — Ciceronian honestum vs utile, Benthamite expediency. Bridge is ethical abstraction: lifting the practical calculation into a principle of action, then debating its limits, which explains why expediency can sound both wise and cynical.
