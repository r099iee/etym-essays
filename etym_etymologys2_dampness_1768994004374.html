DAMPNESS — linear derivation chain (with debate notes): - Proto-Indo-European root *dʰewh₂- (also written *dheu- / *dheuə-), "to blow, to breathe, to emit smoke or vapour; to rise in a cloud" (reconstructed, ca. 4500–3000 BCE). This is the family of smoke-, breath-, and vapour-words: Sanskrit dhūmá- "smoke", Greek thūmós "breath, spirit", Latin fūmus "smoke". - Proto-Germanic *dampaz, "vapour, steam, exhalation, mist" (ca. 500 BCE – 200 CE) — a nasalized extended formation (*dʰembʰ- → *damp-) within the wider *dʰewh₂- family. NOTE: The grouping of Germanic *dampaz under *dʰewh₂- follows Pokorny and Kluge; the exact PIE configuration is debated, so this linkage is reconstructed rather than strictly attested. - Middle Low German / Middle Dutch damp, "vapour, steam, smoke" (medieval period) — the immediate source of the English loan. - Middle English damp, "noxious vapour, foul air, gas in a mine" (borrowed 14th c., c. 1300s, via Flemish/Low German trading and mining contacts). The earliest English senses were all about choking gas, not water: the dread firedamp and chokedamp of the pits. - Modern English damp, adjective "slightly wet, moist, humid" (17th c.) — a semantic transfer from the noun: a "damp" place was one filled with vapour, and the word slid from the vapour itself to the moisture it deposited. - Modern English dampness, "the state or condition of being damp" (17th c.), formed as damp + -ness, where -ness continues Old English -nes/-nysse, the Germanic suffix of state and condition — the final node, DAMPNESS.
