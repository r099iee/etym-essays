- 849-899 - Ælfrǣd is a West Saxon prince and king; contemporaries see a hard-pressed war leader, a lawgiver and a patron of scholars, with no hint of "the Great".
- 893-10th c. - Asser's Life and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle construct the wise Christian king who saved his people; the legend begins to grow.
- 11th-15th c. - Alfred is remembered in chronicles as a lawgiver and defender of the English; the cake story appears in the twelfth century and adds a homely, moralising touch.
- 16th c. - Tudor antiquaries rediscover him as an English proto-Protestant and a model of godly rule; the epithet "the Great" is first recorded.
- 17th-18th c. - Whig historians make him the founder of English liberty and limited monarchy; he becomes a constitutional icon.
- 19th c. - Victorian medievalism turns him into a national hero: statues, stained glass, schoolbooks and the standard title "Alfred the Great".
- 20th c. - Scholarship reassesses the sources and the legends; the 1969 film and popular history keep the name visible, while "Alfred" declines as a given name.
- 21st c. - Prestige television (The Last Kingdom, Vikings) reintroduces him as a complex political operator; "Alfred" survives mainly in the short form Alfie, and the epithet is both honoured and debated.
