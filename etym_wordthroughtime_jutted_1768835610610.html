- Entering English as a spelling-variant of 'jet' (16th c., c. 1560) - jut appears meaning 'to thrust out, to protrude,' and still smells of its French ancestor jeter, 'to throw': the thing that juts is something that has been thrown out of a surface
- A verb of rocks and battlements (17th-18th c.) - the 'throwing' image fades and the word hardens into pure description: crags jut, towers jut, cliff-edges jut; it is the plain, muscular word of topographical and architectural writing, always with a hard angle
- Literary and nautical use (19th c.) - the participle 'jutting' becomes a stock descriptive ornament of romantic prose and sailing journals, loved for its abrupt, shelf-like force; the preterite 'jutted' fixes a mass arrested mid-thrust
- Contemporary - the word remains firmly literary and physical, never fully absorbed into neutral scientific speech; a jut is still a protrusion with attitude - stark, angular, unsoftened by curve, the residue of a throw preserved in stone
