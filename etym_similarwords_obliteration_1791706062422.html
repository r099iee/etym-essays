• Annihilation: Similar because both denote total destruction resulting in nonexistence; differs because annihilation carries an existential, physics-based, or cosmic finality (reducing matter literally to nothing, Latin nihil), whereas obliteration retains a tangible nuance of wiping clean, blotting out, or erasing visible form and record.
  • Eradication: Similar because both convey thorough and complete removal; differs because eradication derives from pulling up by the roots (Latin radix), typically applied to the systematic extermination of diseases, social pests, or weeds, while obliteration emphasizes blotting out surfaces, forms, or structural cavities.
  • Extinction: Similar because both describe the disappearance of an entire entity; differs because extinction focuses specifically on the dying out of biological lineages, species, fires, or family lines, whereas obliteration implies an active, violent, or administrative wiping out.
  • Effacement: Similar because both involve the erasure of surface marks or identities; differs because effacement represents a gradual, subtle wearing away or modest receding into the background (e.g., self-effacement), whereas obliteration is catastrophic, absolute, and forceful.
  • Extermination: Similar because both signify total destruction; differs because extermination is strictly applied to living populations, humans, or vermin driven beyond borders or slaughtered to the last individual, while obliteration encompasses inanimate structures, curricula, and medical lumens.
  • Decimation: Similar because both describe devastating loss; differs because decimation strictly signifies severe damage or the historical killing of one in ten, whereas obliteration demands one hundred percent total destruction with zero remnants.
  • Expunction: Similar because both derive from textual deletion; differs because expunction is a formal legal or bureaucratic procedure of striking out records or criminal entries by puncturing or formal order, while obliteration is broader, visceral, and physically total.
  • Liquidation: Similar because both denote the final termination of an entity; differs because liquidation carries financial, commercial, or sinister clandestine totalitarian overtones (settling debts or covertly eliminating political dissidents), while obliteration suggests an overt, complete leveling.
  • Abolition: Similar because both involve ending something established; differs because abolition is the legislative or moral nullification of an institution, law, or custom (such as slavery), whereas obliteration involves physical destruction or complete erasure of existence.
