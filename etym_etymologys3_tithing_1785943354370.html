The story of tithing begins with a number. The Indo-European word for "ten," reconstructed as *déḱm̥, is among the most securely attested roots in the entire family: it appears as Sanskrit dáśa, Greek déka, Latin decem, Gothic taihun, and Old English tīen "ten." The numeral is everywhere because counting — and above all counting in tens on the fingers — belongs to the deepest layer of human society. From this single root the Germanic peoples built their ordinary word for ten, *tehun, and from it an ordinal, *tehundô, "the tenth." What is remarkable is that this abstract ordinal, a mere number-word, should have grown the thick institutional biography that attaches to tithing. The Germanic and Old English formation. As Proto-Germanic developed into the separate Germanic languages, the ordinal *tehundô was simplified: final syllables were lost and consonants reshaped, so Old English inherited tēoþa "tenth," with the characteristically English voiced dental þ. From this ordinal Old English then formed a verbal noun, tēoþung or tēoþing, using the native -ung/-ing suffix that turned the idea of "taking a tenth" into a standing noun. The same formation gives English tithe itself, which is simply tēoþa transformed by regular sound change and by the medieval spelling conventions of the word. The religious tithe. The practice of paying a tenth to God is far older than the English word. The Hebrew scriptures command a tenth of harvest and herd (Leviticus 27:30; Deuteronomy 14:22), and early Christianity carried the practice west. In the eighth and ninth centuries the Carolingian church and Frankish legislation made the tithe compulsory, and the obligation crossed the Channel into Anglo-Saxon England, where church councils and royal charters enforced the payment of a tenth of grain, livestock, and their increase to the parish clergy. For a thousand years, from the tenth century to the nineteenth, the tithe was the fiscal backbone of the English parish: it was collected in kind, stored in the tithe barn, and divided among the incumbent, the church fabric, and the poor. The Tithe Commutation Act of 1836 replaced payment in kind with money rents, and in the twentieth century the system withered, but the word and the percentage survived in voluntary religious giving, most conspicuously in Mormon practice, where a literal 10% of income is expected of every member. The administrative tithing. Independently — but from the same numeral — the word named a human grouping. In Anglo-Saxon and early Norman law a tithing was a unit of ten households, the smallest subdivision of the hundred, bound by mutual surety: each man was answerable for his fellows, and the group as a whole had to produce any member accused of crime. Under the frankpledge system systematized after the Norman Conquest, every freeman over twelve was enrolled in a tithing under its tithingman, or headborough. The two senses — "a tenth of produce" and "a group of ten men" — are twin growths from the single root, one religious and fiscal, the other municipal and legal, and both show how a number-word can become an institution. Sound changes and semantic drift at a glance.
  • PIE *ḱ → Germanic *h (as in *déḱm̥ → *tehun), the regular Germanic "centum" shift shared with Latin and Greek.
  • Loss of unstressed final syllables produced Old English tēoþa from *tehundô.
  • The noun suffix -ing converted the ordinal into an abstract institution, tēoþing.
  • Medieval orthography (tithe, tything) and the Great Vowel Shift of the 15th–17th centuries gave the modern pronunciation /ˈtaɪ.ðɪŋ/.
  • Semantically the root moved from pure arithmetic (ten) to proportion (a tenth) to duty (the church tax) to society (the ten-man unit) to modern voluntary charity.
Literary and historical attestations. The word appears in Anglo-Saxon law-codes and charters of the 9th–11th centuries, in the Domesday Book's manorial records, in medieval church registers, and in the legal texts of the frankpledge system. Shakespeare uses tithe for the tenth in All's Well That Ends Well, and the ecclesiastical sense runs through English literature wherever parishes, parsons, and harvests are described. In modern English the word retains its precise arithmetic core while its institutional weight has faded into history — a survival of the day when a tenth of everything belonged to God, and a man was answerable for his neighbors in groups of ten.
