BOAST means to speak with excessive pride, to brag or vaunt about oneself, one's deeds or one's possessions, especially to impress or provoke others. In the prompt he fears his brother will dare to boast of his riches to me: the enriched sibling would flaunt wealth to humiliate the poor one, so the poor one plots to loose Misery to ruin him first. The core is self-exalting speech that claims superiority.

Grammatically BOAST is both verb and noun. As verb it is intransitive with of, about and that-clauses, and transitive in boast a record: to boast of riches, to boast about promotion, to boast that one won. As noun it is the act and the thing vaunted: an idle boast, his boast was his garden. Pronunciation is BOHST. Register ranges from condemnatory bragging to neutral possession in marketing.

Primary verb senses:

  • To brag of personal advantage: to boast of riches, strength, children and victories; the bridge is speech that elevates self over hearer, exactly the brother daring to boast to his sibling.
  • To vaunt possessively with audience: to boast to me, at table, in the tavern; the bridge is directed display where wealth needs witnesses to wound.
  • To possess and display neutrally: the hotel boasts a view, the town boasts a museum; the bridge is inanimate subject shift where having becomes showing without moral blame.

Noun and extended senses with bridges:

  • An act of bragging: empty boasts, drunken boasts; the bridge is action nominalised as countable utterance.
  • Grounds for pride: his garden was his boast, her boast was her voice; the bridge is object-for-act metonymy where the prized thing stands for the vaunting.
  • Threat and menace in older use: Shakespearean to boast can mean to threaten loudly; the bridge is big speech as intimidation, surviving in blustering boasts.
  • Online and marketing vaunt: to boast followers, specs and awards; the bridge is broadcast boasting where audience is indefinite but envy remains the aim.

Collocations and usage notes: boast of is classic and formal, boast about is colloquial; never boast without complement needs context; to boast that introduces reported triumph. Boast, brag, vaunt, crow and bluster form a scale from neutral possession to noisy menace. In the folk-tale moral economy, to boast of riches to kin violates humility and invites Misery as punishment, so not daring to boast marks restored hierarchy through ruin.

Social psychology refines the folk-tale warning: humblebragging, where complaint masks vaunting, and straightforward boasting both seek status, but audiences punish the former more because insincerity doubles the offence. The brother who dares to boast of riches to a ruined sibling violates kin solidarity twice, by having and by displaying, so Misery as avenger embodies the community sanction that modern research still finds in downvotes, unfollows and distrust of braggarts.

  • Boast versus toast distinction: to boast is to raise self, to toast is to raise another; folk wisdom prefers toasts at feasts and condemns boasts, which is why royal viands and meads should prompt thanks rather than vaunts.
