- Ancient Rome (c. 5th c. BCE–4th c. CE) - Pertaining to the solemn, sacrificial duties of the *pontifices*—the supreme Roman priestly college—overseeing state rituals, sacrificial calendars, and the sacred bridge over the Tiber.
- Early Christian Church & Medieval Latin (4th–15th c.) - Christianized as a sacred liturgical verb (*pontificare*), meaning to celebrate Pontifical High Mass or to exercise the sovereign episcopal or papal governance of the Church.
- Post-Reformation Era (16th–17th c.) - Protestant polemicists attacked 'pontifical' decrees as tyrannical, ceremonial idolatry, associating the root with haughty ecclesiastical authoritarianism.
- 19th Century British Literature - Satirically extended by Victorian essayists and novelists (such as Thackeray and Dickens) to secular blowhards, academics, and politicians who delivered self-important monologues with papal airs.
- 20th to 21st Century - Exclusively recognized in colloquial and journalistic English as a biting pejorative term for pompous, unearned, dogmatic opinionating.
