Stag is a word of blunt, native English character, and like many such words its deeper history is frustratingly dark. It appears in Middle English around 1200 as stagge, "an adult male deer," and behind it stands an Old English *stagga that survives chiefly in place-names - so the word is ancient in the ground if thin in the manuscript. Its ultimate origin is uncertain. The Oxford English Dictionary points to a probable Scandinavian connection, Old Norse steggr, "a male animal, a male bird," and to a possible membership in the great Indo-European family of *stegh-, "to stick, to prick, to pierce." If that membership is right, the stag is "the pricking animal": the beast defined by its antlers, the sharp sticks growing from its head. The logic of such naming is widespread - animals are often named for their most salient physical feature - and it would explain why the word shares its family with a surprising company: English stick and sting, stake and stitch; Latin stimulus, "a goad" (whence stimulate and stimulus) and instigare, "to goad on" (whence instigate); Greek στίζω (stízō), "to prick, to tattoo" (whence stigma); and Sanskrit तिज् (tij-), "to be sharp," with its brilliant derivative तेजस् (téjas), "sharpness, radiance." Each of these is a facet of the same ancient idea of piercing sharpness, and the stag is the facet that grew antlers. Because the *stegh- connection is probable rather than proven, however, the word must be described honestly: a native Germanic noun of uncertain origin, possibly Scandinavian in form and possibly a member of the great "pricking" family - but no more. Once formed, stag settled into a compact but surprisingly productive set of meanings. Its primary sense - the adult male deer, especially the red deer with its full branching antlers - carried the word into the hunting field, where a stag is a specific animal of a specific age with a full head of antlers, and from the hunt it passed into heraldry and architecture as a motif of masculine strength, as in this card's "stag's head roofing," a translator's compound that gives a Chinese building the antlered emblem of an English country house. The secondary senses all radiate from the image of the solitary, powerful male. The male-only gathering is Victorian: the "stag party" or "smoker" of the 1840s-50s, an entertainment for men alone, and in America "going stag" to a dance without a female partner. The financial sense is contemporary with it: in the railway speculation boom of the 1840s an investor who subscribed to a new issue and sold at once was called a stag - the creature that takes a swift, speculative bound outside the herd, without the staying power of the steady "bull." The stag beetle transfers the antlered head to the insect that wears horn-like mandibles, a folk naming that shows how vivid the animal image remained. Even "stagflation," the 1965 coinage of Iain Macleod, plays on the word's sound if not its derivation, the stag of stagnation arriving by accident beside the stag of the stock market. The word's register has stayed robust. Unlike many hunting terms it never became archaic: the core sense is still the ordinary word for a male red deer, and the secondary uses - stag do, stag night, stagflation, to go stag - keep the old noun busy in everyday British and American speech. Its one great competitor, buck, covers smaller deer and the males of other species; hart, the older native word (Old English heorot, source of Hartford, Hertford, and the hart-beest antelope), retreated into poetry. The literary life of stag is long: it is the quarry of the medieval and Renaissance hunt in Chaucer's boar-and-stag imagery and in the deer parks of Shakespeare, where the wounded stag of As You Like It stands for the exile's grief; it is the emblem of the forest in heraldry and of the gentleman sportsman in nineteenth-century country fiction; and in the modern novel it is the word a translator chooses when a Chinese roof corner must grow a stag's head. Over eight hundred years the sound has barely moved - stagge to stag is a single syllable - while the meaning has branched like the antlers themselves, one animal naming the hunt, the wedding night, the stock market, and the beetle, all at once.
