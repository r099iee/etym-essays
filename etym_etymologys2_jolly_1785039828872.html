Linear derivation chain for JOLLY (single line of descent; a rival theory is noted below): 1) Germanic *jōl- "midwinter festival, feast" — the reconstructed feast-word of the Germanic peoples, source of English Yule; the traditional theory followed for this note. 2) Old Norse jól "a winter feast, Yuletide" — attested in the Old Norse-Icelandic record; a heathen midwinter festival of feasting and merriment. 3) Old French jolif / joli "festive, merry; amorous; pretty" — attested from the 12th c.; on the traditional account the Germanic feast-word was loaned into Gallo-Romance and applied to festive mood and handsome appearance. 4) Middle English joly "merry, cheerful" — c. 1300, borrowed from Old French; the terminal -f of jolif was dropped in English, as hastif became hasty. 5) Early Modern English jolly "merry, festive" — with the intensifier sense "great, remarkable, splendid" developing from the 1540s and the colloquial "somewhat drunk" sense from the 1650s. 6) Modern English JOLLY "merry, buoyant, festive." Debated note: the Oxford English Dictionary calls the Norse/Germanic link "extremely doubtful" on historical and phonetic grounds and prefers instead to see Old French jolif as a Romance formation built on Latin gaudēre "to rejoice" (from PIE *gau-), the family that produced English joy through Old French joie. The Italian word giulivo "merry" is cited both for and against the Germanic theory. The true origin is genuinely uncertain; the Germanic chain is the traditional account and is retained here with this caveat.
