strangle in the source passage — Our good and sublime emperor has the greatest part in the world to play, and he is so virtuous and noble that God will not desert him, and he will fulfil his mission—to strangle the hydra of revolution, which is more horrible than ever now in the person of this murderer and miscreant.… Whom can we reckon on, I ask you?… England with her commercial spirit will not comprehend and cannot comprehend all the loftiness of soul of the Emperor Alexander (Leo Tolstoy, War and Peace, Book 1, Chapter 1, capturing Anna Pavlovna Scherer’s impassioned political declamation against Napoleon Bonaparte and the French Revolution) — is used in a ferocious, mythological metaphor meaning to extinguish, crush, and violently suppress a monstrous political uprising. By pairing strangle with the hydra of revolution, the speaker evokes the ancient heroic labour of Hercules throttling deadly monsters, depicting the Tsar's war not as ordinary geopolitics, but as a righteous act of choking out the breath of revolutionary anarchy before it destroys Christian European civilization.

In standard, legal, medical, and metaphorical English, strangle is a transitive and intransitive verb denoting the act of killing, injuring, or incapacitating a person or animal by squeezing, compressing, or constricting the neck, throat, or trachea, thereby cutting off the intake of air (asphyxiation) and cerebral blood flow. Figuratively, it signifies the deliberate, forceful suppression, stifling, smothering, or termination of any developing idea, movement, speech, economic activity, institution, or natural growth so that it cannot flourish or survive.

  • Physical and forensic sense (anatomical asphyxiation): Compressing the windpipe and carotid vessels by hands (manual strangulation) or by a ligature such as a cord, rope, or cloth (ligature strangulation) to cause death or unconsciousness. For example: the assassin attempted to strangle the guard from behind; forensic investigators determined the victim had been strangled with a silk cord. In pathology and law, it is distinguished from drowning or chemical suffocation by the direct mechanical compression of the cervical structures.
  • Figurative political and social suppression (source sense): Crushing, silencing, or destroying a rebellion, free expression, ideological movement, or nascent policy before it gains unstoppable momentum. For example: the autocratic regime sought to strangle the nascent democratic movement in its cradle; censorship was enacted to strangle independent journalism; high tariffs threatened to strangle international trade. The metaphor relies on denying the 'breath' or 'lifeblood' necessary for an organization to grow.
  • Botanical and horticultural sense (strangler plants): Describing parasitic vines, epiphyte trees, or aggressive weeds that encircle, constrict, and eventually choke the host tree to death by preventing radial growth and blocking sunlight. For example: the roots of the strangler fig slowly strangle the host palm until it rots away; dense ivy began to strangle the ancient fruit trees.
  • Mechanical, engineering, and fluid dynamics sense: Restricting, narrowing, or choking the passage of fluid, steam, air, or fuel through a valve, pipe, or engine throat, thereby starving the mechanism of essential flow. For example: a blockage in the fuel line threatened to strangle the engine; the valve was closed halfway to strangle the steam pressure. (Closely related to throttle and choke).
  • Psychological and emotional inhibition: Suppressing one's own voice, tears, screams, or impulses through intense self-restraint or overwhelming grief. For example: she had to strangle a sob as she took the witness stand; he strangled his rising fury and forced a polite smile.
  • Collocations and idiomatic expressions: Frequently collocates with strangle in its cradle / in its infancy (destroy at the very beginning), strangle the economy, strangle free speech, strangle a cry. Its register spans serious legal and medical texts, vivid historical narratives, political oratory, and dramatic literature.

Across all domains, the unifying conceptual core of strangle is lethal constriction: applying tight, crushing pressure around a vital conduit — whether a biological throat, a shipping lane, or a political movement — until life and motion cease.
