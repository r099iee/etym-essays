- c. 1400 - the frequentative mynglen enters the language, and its participle mingled simply means 'mixed' — waters, dust, colours stirred together; perception: a plain, neutral verb of daily physical life.
- 15th–16th c. - extends to people: armies and crowds mingle; mingled describes bodies, households and peoples thrown together; still unmarked, everyday speech.
- Late 16th c. - the social sense crystallises (to mingle with the guests), and the emotional sense opens ('mingled joy and fear'); the word begins to sound softer, more intimate.
- 17th c. - the Authorized Version fixes mingled in sacred prose (Psalm 106:35: 'were mingled among the heathen'), lending the participle a solemn, moral resonance.
- 18th–19th c. - Romantic and Victorian prose canonise 'mingled feelings' and the harmony of mingled sounds, lights and waters; the word climbs into the literary register and starts to leave daily speech.
- 20th–21st c. - everyday English prefers 'mixed'; mingled survives as the dignified, faintly archaic alternative in literary narration (Tolkien) and in the fixed idiom 'with mingled feelings'.
