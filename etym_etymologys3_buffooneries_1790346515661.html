The term buffooneries captures a colorful linguistic and cultural evolution originating in bodily onomatopoeia, flourishing through Renaissance street theater and the commedia dell'arte, and culminating in Victorian moral and aesthetic critique.

1. Onomatopoeic Roots in Late Latin:

  • Proto-Indo-European Echoic Base: The word emerges from the expressive, echoic Indo-European root *bu- (or *buff-), imitating the sound of exhaling, blowing, or puffing out the cheeks with air.
  • Late Latin buffare: In Late and Vulgar Latin, this gave rise to the expressive verb buffare ("to puff, blow out the cheeks"). In popular Roman pantomime and rural comedy, actors routinely puffed out their cheeks so that a comic blow from a fellow actor would produce a loud popping sound or a ridiculous expulsion of breath, provoking laughter from the crowd.

2. Italian Renaissance and the Commedia dell'Arte:

  • Old Italian buffa and buffone: By the 13th and 14th centuries in Italy, buffa came to mean a "puff of wind," and by extension a "joke, jest, or trick." A person who engaged in such jesting, particularly a professional clown, jester, or mountebank, was called a buffone.
  • The Rise of Italian Farce: The buffone became a staple archetype of the Italian popular stage, performing physical slapstick, acrobatics, grotesque mimicry, and crude bawdry. This comic genre also gave rise to the musical term opera buffa (comic opera).

3. French Transmission and the Suffix -erie:

  • Middle French bouffon and bouffonnerie: In the 16th century (c. 1540s), during the height of Italian cultural prestige under Catherine de' Medici and the Valois court, the French borrowed bouffon. By attaching the productive French suffix -erie (designating an art, practice, conduct, or collective instances of an action), they formed bouffonnerie ("clowning, ludicrous jesting, or comic antics").

4. Adoption into Early Modern English:

  • Jacobean Borrowing: The word entered English in the late 16th and early 17th centuries (c. 1580s for buffoon; c. 1610s for buffoonery), appearing in the works of Ben Jonson and contemporary Jacobean playwrights who drew heavily on continental theatrical traditions.
  • Plural Form buffooneries: The plural form was quickly established to describe discrete acts, repeated routines, or varied manifestations of clownish folly.

5. Neoclassical Aesthetics: Wit versus Buffoonery:

  • 18th-Century Pejorative Shift: During the Augustan Age of English literature, critics like John Dryden, Alexander Pope, and Samuel Johnson drew a strict boundary between noble, refined wit (which instructed and delighted through intellectual insight) and low buffoonery (which merely amused the rabble through vulgar distortion and slapstick). Buffoonery became an aesthetic pejorative associated with intellectual vacuity.

6. Victorian Polemics and Ruskin's Indignation:

  • Critical Weaponry in the 19th Century: In the 19th century, romantic and moral essayists such as Thomas Carlyle and John Ruskin harnessed buffooneries to denounce the superficial cynicism of the popular press. In the opening preface to Modern Painters (1843), Ruskin famously deplored how the press vented its "ribald buffooneries" upon J. M. W. Turner's transcendent landscapes, using the term to signify a shameless, grotesque desecration of sublime truth.
  • Modern Usage: Today, buffooneries retains this vivid dual character: denoting both physical clownish antics in dramatic arts and contemptible, circus-like conduct in political and public discourse.
