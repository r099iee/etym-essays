- 16th–17th c. — “Ailing” is rare and literal, the participle of a common verb meaning “to trouble” or “to be ill”; “what ails thee?” belongs to ordinary speech and to balladry.
- 18th c. — The adjective settles into the sense “chronically unwell”, and “ailment” is coined to give the family a medical noun; the verb begins its retreat into set phrases.
- 19th c. — “Ailing” becomes the usual polite term for the long ill health of the old; the verb survives in dialect and in Scottish and Irish English.
- 20th c. — The adjective extends from bodies to institutions: “ailing industry”, “ailing economy”, “ailing currency” become journalistic staples.
- 21st c. — Attributive use dominates, usually of organisations or of elderly relatives; the verb is archaic or regional, and the medical sense is softened by euphemism.
