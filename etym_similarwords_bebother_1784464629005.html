• Bother: Similar because both words mean to trouble or vex someone; differs because bother is the ordinary mild verb with no curse-force, while bebother adds the be- prefix that makes the annoyance directional and complete.
  • Bedevil: Similar because both are intensified be- verbs of harassment; differs because bedevil suggests deliberate diabolical malice, whereas bebother is mere pestering raised to a wish.
  • Beset: Similar because both use the intensive be- pattern; differs because beset means to surround and attack from all sides, military and serious, with none of bebother’s comic lightness.
  • Bewilder: Similar because both are be- intensives describing a troubled state; differs because bewilder denotes mental confusion (from wilder, “to be lost”), while bebother denotes vexation and annoyance, not puzzlement.
  • Vex: Similar because both mean to annoy; differs because vex is old-fashioned but lacks the intensive prefix’s theatricality and the oath’s jocular mildness.
  • Pester: Similar because both mean to trouble repeatedly; differs because pester is colloquial but not imprecatory, describing nagging, while bebother is an oath-like wish.
  • Plague: Similar because both mean to afflict with trouble; differs because plague implies a sustained, serious campaign of affliction, far stronger than bebother’s single comic volley.
  • Harass: Similar because both mean to disturb persistently; differs because harass is neutral-to-serious and legal-register, without the gently dated humour of bebother.
  • Annoy: Similar because both mean to cause irritation; differs because annoy is polite and understated, with none of bebother’s intensive prefix or oath-force.
  • Confusticate: Similar because the two words form Tolkien’s paired oath and both are comic imprecations; differs because confusticate is the mock-Latin “grand” half meaning to confuse, while bebother is the homely native half meaning to vex thoroughly.
