- Old English — syll / syl: the ground-beam or threshold of a hall, the timber a whole timber building stands on; foundational, structural, load-bearing.
- Middle English — sille / sulle: the bottom piece of any framed opening, door or window, as stone replaces timber and houses grow glazed windows to lean on.
- Early Modern — the window sense takes over daily life: the sill becomes a shelf for cushions, plants, and elbows, entering proverbs and domestic scenes.
- 19th c. — engineers borrow it outward: geologists name flat rock intrusions sills and dock-builders name lock ledges sills, honouring the flat-underlying logic.
- 20th c.–today — motor cars gain sills below their doors, and the word sits at every scale from bedrock to bodywork; in stories it remains what it was in the source sentence, the springboard ledge of windows, climbed, leaned on, and sprung from.
