- Late 14th c. — Harmful and injurious to life: The adjectival base noisome emerges from aphetic noy + -some, primarily describing destructive frosts, poisonous serpents, or fatal wounds.
- 15th c. — Pestilential climates and unwholesome airs: Begins designating disease-bearing swamps, marsh miasmas, and atmospheric conditions believed to corrupt bodily humors.
- 16th c. — Olfactory focus and biblical adoption: Specializes sharply toward unbearable stench and rank putrefaction; William Tyndale and the Geneva Bible describe 'noisome beasts' and 'noisome stenches.'
- Late 16th–17th c. — Coining of the abstract noun: Noisomeness enters early modern print in medical treatises on plague contagion and Puritan theological tracts characterizing the foul stench of human depravity.
- 18th c. — Sanitary and urban critiques: Deployed in Enlightenment travel accounts and municipal reports to document the horrific stench of open urban sewers, slaughterhouses, and unventilated prisons.
- 19th c. — Victorian aesthetic and moral polemic: John Ruskin and contemporaries utilize the noun as a devastating aesthetic category, weaponizing it against the envious vitriol of incompetent critics.
- 20th–21st c. — High-register literary preservation: Disappears from casual speech while persisting in judicial rulings, classical scholarship, high journalism, and historical fiction as an elevated emblem of visceral disgust.
