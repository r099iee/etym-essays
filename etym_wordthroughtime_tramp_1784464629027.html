“Tramp” entered English as the verb for heavy, rhythmic treading — to stamp or walk heavily — and the noun for that sound and gait itself.
“Tramp” by the 17th–18th centuries made the walker the meaning: a “tramp” was a person who walked the roads, and the word settled on the homeless wanderer, gaining a harsh social edge.
“Tramp” in the 19th century also became the tramp steamer — a cargo ship that roamed wherever trade took it — extending the wandering image to ships.
“Tramp” today has a vagrant sense that is dated and often offensive, and the word most naturally survives as the heavy, ominous footfall — “the tramp of doom” — or as a noun for the sound and rhythm of marching feet.
