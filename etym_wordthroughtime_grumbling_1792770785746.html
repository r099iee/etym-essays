- Late Middle English (c. 1350–1450) — Emerged from the onomatopoeic Germanic base *grum- / *grom- combined with the frequentative suffix *-le*, designating the physical rumbling of an upset stomach or low, half-suppressed vocal growls of ill-temper — [perception: visceral physical manifestation of digestive distress or surly peasant temperament].
- Tudor & Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — Expanded into political, civic, and military domains to denote covert disaffection, brewing mutiny, and subversive murmurings among troops or subjects against royal decrees, taxes, and harsh officers — [perception: dangerous undercurrent of civil disobedience and sedition].
- 18th & 19th Century Domestic & Literary Prose — Softened into a ubiquitous social trait of the freeborn Englishman, describing harmless, habitual, and cathartic complaining about weather, railway timetables, prices, or local gossip (epitomized in Tolkien's Shire tradesmen) — [perception: mild, petty, everyday human foible of provincial life].
- Victorian Industrial & Mechanical Age (19th c.) — Metaphorically transferred to machinery, boilers, steam locomotives, and geological phenomena to describe continuous, vibrating, low-frequency mechanical noise ("the grumbling boiler") — [perception: evocative acoustic marker of heavy mechanical strain and natural power].
- Contemporary Global English (20th–21st c.) — Pervades corporate culture, digital forums, and interpersonal relations to describe informal venting, passive dissatisfaction, and persistent low-level organizational friction — [perception: pervasive background noise of everyday workplace and civic life].
