Thesaurus is a noun of Greek origin meaning literally “treasure, storehouse”. It is capitalised in the source because it is the first word of a book title: Thesaurus Ciceronianus, the Ciceronian treasury of Mario Nizzoli. In modern English the word most often denotes a dictionary of synonyms, but that is the youngest of its senses, and the Renaissance title belongs to an older and grander one.

The Thesaurus Ciceronianus of Mario Nizzoli (1498–1576) is a Latin lexicon organised around the vocabulary of a single author. It inventories Cicero’s words, phrases and constructions so that a student may write like Cicero; it is a treasury in the literal sense, a hoard of Ciceronian diction laid up for use. In the passage it stands as the technical apparatus behind the whole phenomenon under discussion — the European imitation of Ciceronian prose.

Sense 1 — a dictionary of synonyms (modern primary)

A reference work that lists words grouped by meaning and supplies equivalents, usually with antonyms and sometimes with related terms. Two arrangements are traditional:

  • Alphabetical — entries look like dictionary entries, each followed by a run of synonyms; this is the format of most modern desk thesauri and of the thesaurus function in a word processor.
  • Conceptual — words are grouped under ideas rather than letters, so that the user starts from a meaning and searches for a word; this is the arrangement of Roget’s original Thesaurus (1852) and of its many successors.

The essential difference from a dictionary is that a thesaurus does not define: it offers alternatives. It answers the question “what else could I say?” rather than “what does this mean?”.

Sense 2 — a comprehensive lexicon of a language or an author

The Renaissance and scholarly sense, and the one active in the source: a systematic treasury of the entire word-stock of a language, a period or a writer. Works of this kind are not lists of synonyms but total inventories, with citations and parallels.

  • Thesaurus linguae Latinae — the great Latin dictionary begun in 1894 and still incomplete, the most ambitious thesaurus ever attempted.
  • Thesaurus Graecae linguae — Henri Estienne’s Greek treasury (1572), a monument of Renaissance philology.
  • Thesaurus Ciceronianus — Nizzoli’s lexicon of Cicero, the model of an author-thesaurus designed for imitation.

Sense 3 — a treasury, storehouse or hoard (literal and archaic)

The oldest sense, and the etymological core of all the others: a place where treasure is laid up, and by extension the treasure itself.

  • Architecture. In ancient Greece a thēsauros was a small monumental building in a sanctuary in which votive offerings were kept — the treasuries of Delphi, Olympia and Delos, several of which still stand.
  • Roman law. Thesaurus inventus, “treasure found”, is the legal category of buried treasure; Roman jurists debated how it should be divided between finder and landowner.
  • Theology. Thesaurus ecclesiae, the treasury of the Church, and thesaurus meritorum, the treasury of merits, are medieval terms for the accumulated grace and merit dispensed through the sacraments.

Sense 4 — any rich store (figurative)

Because a thesaurus is a hoard, the word lends itself to metaphor: a thesaurus of anecdotes, a thesaurus of insults, a thesaurus of forgotten melodies. The usage is literary and slightly arch, and it always implies abundance plus arrangement — a collection that can be drawn on, not a mere heap.

Sense 5 — a controlled vocabulary (technical)

In information science, a thesaurus is a structured list of indexing terms with explicit relationships: broader terms, narrower terms, related terms, preferred and non-preferred forms. Medical Subject Headings, the ERIC thesaurus and the UNESCO thesaurus are examples. This sense is unrelated to synonym-hunting: its purpose is to make retrieval consistent.

Register and connotation

The word is dignified and bookish; as a title it confers prestige, which is why dictionaries of Greek and Latin were called thesauri rather than dictionaries. In modern writing advice it carries a mild stigma — “don’t just raid the thesaurus” — because a synonym chosen for variety rather than precision is the mark of uncertain prose. The technical sense is neutral and professional.

Semantic reasoning

The bridge from treasure to words is a metaphor that Renaissance humanism took literally: eloquence was wealth, and a copious vocabulary was a fortune. Erasmus’s De copia (1512) taught the accumulation of words and things as a moral and rhetorical discipline, and the thesaurus was its instrument. Once language is conceived as treasure, a dictionary is naturally a treasury, and a treasury of synonyms is only one step further.
