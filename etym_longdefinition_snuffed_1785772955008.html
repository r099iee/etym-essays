Snuffed is the past tense and past participle of the verb to snuff, whose core image is the abrupt end of a small flame. The oldest mechanical sense is very specific: to snuff a candle is to crop or pinch off the charred, smoking tip of the wick — the "snuff" — so that the flame burns clean and bright again; from this came the broader sense of putting the flame out entirely, by pinching, by a snuffer (a small cone or scissors-like tool), or by smothering. In narrative prose the word is tactile and domestic: a quick pinch, a wisp of smoke, sudden darkness. By extension, and much more colorfully, snuffed names any sudden, decisive ending — of life, hope, a career, a rumor — often with a cool, brutal finality. In slang and crime idiom "snuffed out" means killed, and British colloquial "snuffed it" means died.

  • Core literal sense — candle and wick: To trim the charred wick, or to extinguish the candle; "she snuffed the candle" means she put it out, by pinch or snuffer. Bridge from trimming to extinguishing: the same gesture that trims a wick can crush the flame entirely.
  • Extinguishing sense (light and fire): Any flame or light snuffed — by a draft, water, fingers, or a snuffer. "The gust snuffed the candle" (this card's context). The action is abrupt and complete: the light does not fade; it is pinched off.
  • Metaphor of termination — "snuffed out": Life, hope, resistance, a career, a movement, or a rumor ended abruptly and decisively. Bridge: the flame is the ancient image of life, so putting out a flame means putting an end to vitality; often implies brutal, unsentimental, or callous suddenness.
  • Slang sense — "snuffed" = killed (19th c.–present): Crime fiction and hard-boiled idiom: "he got snuffed," "snuffed out by the mob." Bridge: the life-as-flame metaphor hardened into a slang verb of murder, retaining the candle image that "wasted," "offed," or "whacked" lack.
  • British colloquial — "snuffed it": Died (humorous to fatalistic). "He snuffed it in his sleep." Now largely archaic-sounding but still recognizable from older fiction.
  • Distinct tobacco sense — "snuff": The noun for powdered tobacco taken by sniffing (from Dutch snuf, related to sniff) is a separate word, and the verb "to snuff" tobacco (to inhale it) is likewise separate; both share the sn-/nose imagery but have no wick history.
  • "Snuff film" (20th c.): A sensational media term for a film supposedly recording a real murder; it shows how far the death metaphor can travel. Semantically marginal but lexically notorious.
  • Register and emotion: From household realism (candles) to hard-boiled or fatalistic idiom (death). The word always carries abruptness. Antonyms and contrasts: kindled, relit, kept alive, nurtured, fanned, preserved, survived.

Collocations cluster around light and life: snuffed the candle, snuffed out the flame, snuffed out hopes / resistance / a life, snuffed it. The phonesthemic sn- cluster (sniff, snout, snip, snap, snub, snatch) makes the word sound quick, small, and sharp, matching its sense. Contrast with extinguished (neutral, formal, technical), quenched (liquid or cooling), smothered (deprivation of air over an area), doused (wet), and stifled (breath or voice).
