THE EVOLUTION OF VOLITION: A WORD BUILT IN THE MEDIEVAL SCHOOLS **The ancient root: *welh₁-, "to wish, choose, desire"** The story of *volition* is the story of a very young word built out of very old materials. The noun itself is a medieval creation — it did not exist in classical Latin — but the root beneath it is one of the most ancient and widespread in the Indo-European family: *welh₁-, "to wish, choose, desire." Every branch of the family built its will-vocabulary from this root:
  • In Germanic it produced Proto-Germanic *weljaną, whence Old English willan and Modern English will; the adjective well also belongs here, meaning originally "wished-for."
  • In Greek the base gave (h)elésthai "to choose, take," the verb of choosing and selecting.
  • In Indo-Iranian it yielded Sanskrit vr-/var- "to choose, desire," whose noun vara "wish, boon; the chosen one" survives in Hindi var, "bridegroom."
  • In Latin it produced the irregular verb velle "to wish, want, be willing" — the source of all the Romance and English will-words that pass through Latin — together with voluntās "will, inclination" (whence voluntary, volunteer) and voluptās "pleasure, delight" (whence voluptuous, voluptuary).
**The classical gap and the medieval coinage** Classical Latin had a rich vocabulary for wanting and willing — velle, volō, voluntās — but no abstract noun meaning "the act of willing." The Romans spoke of the will through verbs and phrases, never through a dedicated noun; that abstraction simply did not exist in the language. The need for it arose in the medieval schools: - Scholastic philosophy, building on Aristotle and the church fathers, developed a detailed psychology of the soul's powers: intellect, appetite, and will. To analyse the will it needed a name for the individual act of choosing and willing, as distinct from the desires that precede it. - The Scholastics therefore coined vōlitio on the stem vol- of volō "I wish," adding the abstract suffix -itio — the same formation as petitio "a seeking" — to yield literally "an act of willing." - The coinage was systematic, not accidental: vōlitio took its place beside intellectio "an act of understanding," and the pair named the twin executive acts of the mind in medieval psychology. **The road into English** - From Medieval Latin, vōlitio passed into the learned vocabulary of modern Europe. French adopted it as volition in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and English borrowed the word in the mid-seventeenth century, during the great expansion of English philosophical vocabulary. - In English it entered the language of metaphysics and psychology: Hobbes, Locke, and their successors used volition to name the executive act of the will, the step that follows desire and intention and issues in action. - The word's birth in the schools is still audible in its register: volition has never been a street word. From the start it was bookish, formal, and technical, and it has kept that dignity for nearly four centuries. **The modern life of the word** Since the seventeenth century volition has had a curious double career: - In philosophy and psychology it remains the standard term for deliberate, willed action, contrasted with reflex, habit, impulse, and coercion; "volitional control" is today a working concept in cognitive science and law. - In ordinary English it has contracted almost entirely into a single fixed phrase, "of one's own volition," meaning freely and by one's own choice. Outside that idiom it is distinctly formal and slightly archaic. - Its literary use, as in Carlyle's "royalist volition," exploits precisely this doubleness: the word carries the philosophical weight of the medieval coinage and the dignity of the fixed phrase at once, letting a writer name a will that is sovereign, proud, and self-determined in a single elevated noun. **Sound and spelling over time** - Latin vōlitio, with its long o, gave the Romance forms in o (Italian volizione, French volition) and the English noun volition; the English pronunciation /vəˈlɪʃən/ shows the usual reduction of the unstressed first syllable and the palatalization of the -ti- sequence to -sh-, exactly as in petition, position, and condition. - The spelling has been stable since the seventeenth century, and the family has grown the adjective volitional and the adverb volitionally by ordinary English suffixation. **Why the word means what it means today** The full range of volition — the philosophical act of willing, the idiomatic "of one's own volition," and the heroic agency of Carlyle's romance heroine — is a single continuous history from a prehistoric root that meant "to wish," through the medieval coinage that named the act of willing, into the modern lexicon where it survives as the formal, dignified word for deliberate self-determination. Every stage kept the core idea: volition is the will in action — the moment a wish becomes a decision and a decision becomes a deed.
