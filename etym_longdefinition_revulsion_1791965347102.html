“Revulsion” is a mass noun of violent feeling: the mind’s involuntary recoil from something perceived as loathsome, repugnant, or morally intolerable. It names not a considered judgement but a reflex of the whole person — often felt in the body as a shudder, a tightening of the stomach, a turning away of the face. In the source passage, which traces the swing of Cicero’s posthumous reputation, the word carries exactly this charge: “After centuries of admiration and acceptance, it is not surprising that there was somewhat of a revulsion.” The implication is that taste did not calmly revise its estimate of Cicero but swung violently against him, and that the swing was as much emotional as scholarly.

1. The dominant modern sense: disgust, repugnance, recoil

In current English revulsion denotes a strong, usually sudden feeling of disgust combined with a physical or moral shrinking-back. Dictionaries place it at the intense end of the aversion spectrum, stronger than distaste or dislike and typically more visceral than disapproval. It can be roused by physical things — filth, wounds, vermin, certain foods — or by moral ones: cruelty, betrayal, an ideology’s atrocities. The word does not require an object in the grammar of the sentence, but it almost always has one in thought.

  • Core lexicographic wording: “a sense of violent disgust and loathing”; “a sudden, forcible change of feeling, especially one of disgust.”
  • Typical constructions: revulsion at (a sight), revulsion against (a person, party, practice), revulsion for (an object of loathing), revulsion from (something one turns away from); “in revulsion,” “a wave of revulsion,” “physical revulsion,” “moral revulsion.”
  • Intensity and register: strong and largely formal; in journalism it often reports public reaction to atrocity or scandal (“revulsion at the images”), where it implies that the reaction is spontaneous and collective.
  • Bodily dimension: unlike most synonyms, revulsion commonly collocates with nausea and shuddering, because its etymology is a physical tearing-away rather than a judgement.

2. The medical sense: revulsion of blood and humours

For three centuries revulsion was first and foremost a technical term of Galenic medicine. It denoted the deliberate diversion of blood or other humours from a diseased part of the body to another part, usually by cupping, bleeding, or counter-irritation at a distance from the seat of the disease. The aim was to empty the affected region indirectly, pulling the morbific matter away rather than letting it accumulate. In the humoral system this was distinguished from derivation, where the evacuation was performed near the affected part. The sense survives only in histories of medicine, but it is the historical seed of every later meaning: the body’s fluids are forcibly drawn back from where they are doing harm.

  • Technical definition: “the diverting of a flow of blood or humours from one part of the body to another,” a practice also known as revulsion therapy or counter-irritation.
  • Historical contrast: revulsion (drawing away) versus derivation (drawing towards); both were standard operations in classical and early modern therapeutics.
  • Modern survival: the term is obsolete in clinical practice but remains in medical history and in the figurative vocabulary of drawing off a harmful excess.

3. The general sense of drawing back, and the “revulsion of feeling”

Between the medical and the modern senses lay a general one: a turning or drawing back of anything, including affection, sympathy, or fortune. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century writers speak of a revulsion of feeling, a revulsion of public opinion, or a revulsion in the tide of events — a sudden reversal of direction rather than a physical disgust. This is the bridge sense, and it explains how a word built from a surgical image could come to describe a change of heart. The modern emotional sense narrows the reversal to one specific direction: away from the object, with loathing attached.

  • Political and social usage: “a revulsion against the government” — a sharp swing of public feeling, still current and only lightly tinged with disgust.
  • Literary usage: Victorian novelists use “a revulsion of feeling” for a character’s sudden change of mind, with no implication of nausea.

4. The semantic bridge: why tearing-away became disgust

The connecting logic is the gesture of recoil. Latin revulsio was a tearing or wrenching away; medical revulsion pulled the body’s fluids back from a diseased part. Both images contain the same motion: something is forcibly drawn away from where it was. Applied to the mind, that motion becomes the whole self shrinking back from an object it cannot tolerate. The step from “a drawing back” to “disgust” is therefore not arbitrary, since disgust just is the emotion of drawing back. English speakers completed the metaphor in the early nineteenth century, when revulsion began to appear in literary prose as a synonym for strong repugnance. The older neutral sense has not entirely vanished — one can still speak of a revulsion of feeling — but it now reads as archaic or specialised.

5. Register, connotation, and company

  • Connotation: involuntary, intense, and often moralised; it suggests that the object is not merely unpleasant but contaminating.
  • Common collocates: wave, surge, shudder, instinct, physical, moral, widespread, public, utter; verbs: feel, provoke, inspire, cause, express, conceal.
  • What to avoid: revulsion is too strong for mild dislike; for aesthetic preferences use distaste, and for considered opposition use aversion or opposition.
  • Related forms: revulse (rare verb), revulsed (archaic adjective), revulsionary (obsolete).

6. In this reading

Applied to Cicero, revulsion describes the nineteenth- and twentieth-century reaction against the ideal of the fluent, copious, self-dramatising orator. The passage’s “somewhat of a revulsion” is deliberately understated: after centuries of admiration, the rejection was not a calm revaluation but a recoil, and the word quietly suggests that the critics reacted to Cicero’s style as one reacts to something physically repellent. Reading the word in its full etymology — a tearing away — sharpens the point: the same culture that had swallowed Cicero whole now could not stomach him.
