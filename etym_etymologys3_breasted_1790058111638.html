The evolution of breasted starts with swelling. PIE *bhreus- to swell, sprout, bud, bulge — Pokorny's root for breasts, buds, bubbles, brewing — gives Proto-Germanic *breustam breast, chest: the swelling front of the torso that swells with breath, milk, muscle. The form is oddly plural (neuter -am) used collectively, as if the chest were two swellings. Cognates across Germanic confirm unity: Old Frisian briast, Old Saxon briost, Dutch borst, Old High German brust, German Brust, Old Norse brjost, Gothic brusts. No certain cognate outside Germanic survives; Latin and Greek use other roots for chest, so the English word is deeply native, bodily, nursery-close.

Old English breost (c.800, Beowulf, Psalms, leechdoms) means chest, bosom, heart as seat of feeling, conscience, song: breost-hord heart-hoard, breost-cofa breast-chamber of thought. Pronounced /breost/ with breaking diphthong eo. Gender neuter, often plural breost for singular. Christian poetry makes the breast the house of soul; medical texts the house of lungs. Middle English brest, breest, breste (c.1200, Ancrene Wisse, Chaucer) levels diphthong to /brest/, extends to brisket meat, hill-breast (slope like chest), ship's breast (bow side), and verb to breast: to face with the breast. To breast one's foe in romances means to meet chest-to-chest; Lydgate, Malory use it chivalrically.

  • Climbing and stemming: 17th-18th c. fell-walkers, soldiers and sailors regularise to breast a hill, to breast the tide, to breast the waves — to meet and overtop by chesting. Milton, Defoe, Cook's voyages use it for slopes and surf. Breast-work, breast-wall, breast-rope, breast-oar technicalise the front as fortification and tackle. Pronunciation /brest/ with DRESS vowel; spelling breast with ea for short e freezes.
  • Sporting tape: 19th c. athletics codifies to breast the tape — to win by torso touch. Rules define torso as scoring part; photography judges chests. Breasted past reports victories in newspapers: she breasted the tape. Rugby keeps to breast the tackle — to run chest into defender.
  • Tailoring homograph: single-breasted, double-breasted coats (19th c. naval reefer to lounge suit) and deep-breasted, full-breasted birds, voices, women use -breasted as possessive adjective (having such a breast), distinct from verbal past but reinforcing chest-front imagery. Your sentence uses verbal past: Lanarck body as tugboat.

Modern breasted thus carries Stone-Age body, medieval heart, industrial hill, and stopwatch chest in one syllabic -ed (/ˈbrestɪd/). From swelling flesh to shoving ships, the word never leaves the front of the body — it merely turns that front into action, making confrontation and ascent a matter of leading with the most vulnerable, most courageous part.
