Ochils is a place-name whose rock is far older than its language. The hills themselves are Lower Devonian lavas some 410 million years old; the name is at most 1,500 years old and belongs to the tangled succession of Pictish, Gaelic, and Scots speech in central Scotland. To trace it is to trace who held the carse below.

The most widely accepted account derives Ochil from Brittonic *uxel, high, the ancestor of modern Welsh uchel, Cornish ughel, and Breton uhel, all meaning high, lofty. Proto-Celtic reconstructs *ouxsello-, high, itself tentatively referred to PIE *h₃ewps-, lofty, though Celticists argue over the laryngeal. Pictish, the non-Gaelic Celtic language of eastern Scotland before c. 900, shared this Brittonic vocabulary; a Pictish *ochel meaning high place or high ridge is therefore entirely plausible as the name for the conspicuous south-facing scarp that dominates the Forth valley. Medieval charters first write it Ochel (13th century, lands and barony), with ch for the guttural /x/ and o for /ɔ/. Scots speakers add the guttural fricative /x/ still heard in Scottish loch and the plural -s for the range: the Ochils on the analogy of the Grampians, the Cheviots.

  • Phonetic path: Brittonic /uxel/ with back fricative /x/ and dark /u/ fronts slightly in Pictish to /oxel/, Gaelic speakers hear /ɔxɪl/ and spell Ochail, Ochil, Scots anglicise the vowel to /ɒ/ or /əʊ/ while keeping /x/ in Scotland (RP speakers often replace it with /k/, giving /ˈɒkɪlz/). The final -s is English collective plural, not Gaelic; Gaelic would say Monadh Ochail (moor of Ochil).
  • Gaelic overlay and folk-etymology: after Gaelic replaced Pictish (9th-11th c.), speakers reinterpreted the opaque Ochel as Gaelic uachail (high, genitive of uachal) or ochaill (projection, edge). Pont's and Blaeu's maps (1590s-1650s) waver between Ochell, Ochill, Ochils. This is normal: an inherited pre-Gaelic name is given a plausible Gaelic meaning after the fact. It does not disprove the Brittonic origin; it shows bilingual ears at work.
  • Scots fixation (1700-1900): Old Statistical and New Statistical Accounts, Ordnance Survey (1860s), and geologists (Murchison, Geikie) standardise Ochil Hills for maps and Ochils for speech. Geikie makes Ochil Volcanic Formation a world type, fixing the singular attributive Ochil in science while the people keep the plural. Scott never sets fiction in the Ochils proper, but tourists and children like Ruskin attach his White Lady and monks to every convenient glen, so glen of the Ochils becomes romantic shorthand.
  • Modern survival: hillwalking guides, whisky labels (Glenochil), bus routes, and conservation partnerships all use Ochils without gloss. The guttural /x/ marks insiders; outsiders who say O-kills are gently corrected.

The responsible etymology is therefore: probably Pictish/Brittonic high (hills), first written Ochel, Gaelicised in spelling, Scotticised with plural -s, now pronounced with /x/ in Scotland. The Gaelic-high derivation is not wrong in sense — both theories mean high — but the Brittonic cognate set (Welsh uchel and kin) makes a pre-Gaelic origin more economical, and scholars explicitly warn that no charter proves the chain link by link. The hills remain, whatever tongue named them, high above the carse.
