- Old English — a boy, nothing more: cnafa names a lad or serving youth, honest work, no stain attached.
- Early Middle English — the servant: household menials and labouring lads; humble but not yet wicked, though masters already grumble about pilfering.
- 13th–14th c. — low birth becomes low character: knave marks the common fellow against the gentleman, and contempt for the station curdles into suspicion of the man.
- 16th c. — the rogue is born: rogues, vagabonds, and cheats are knaves by definition; Shakespeare's age fixes dishonesty as the word's core.
- 17th–18th c. — cards and politics: the Jack is the knave at every card table, while pamphleteers wield the word against informers, stock-jobbers, and ministers.
- Today — the archaic thunderbolt: no longer everyday speech, but kept loaded for polemic and period drama, where it strikes harder for its age.
