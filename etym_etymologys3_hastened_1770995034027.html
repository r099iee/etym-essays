The biography of hastened is the biography of urgency - of how a Germanic word for violent struggle was tamed into a civil word for pressing speed. The chain begins, on the most widely accepted reconstruction, with the Proto-Indo-European root *kei-, "to set in motion, to stir, to rouse." This was a root of activation: in ancient Greece it produced kinéo "to set in motion", the source of our scientific kinetic and cinema; in Rome it produced ciere "to summon, rouse" and its frequentative citare, ancestor of cite, excite, and incite. Across the family the root describes the same primal act: something inert being stirred into action, a still thing being set moving. In the Germanic north, however, the root took on a markedly harder colour. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic form *haifstiz did not mean "to stir" but "strife, struggle, violent contention." The attested relics are all aggressive: Gothic haifsts "strife, contest", Old English hæst "violence, fury, impetuosity", Old Norse heipt "feud, hatred", Old High German heisti "violent, forceful". Here the original "setting in motion" has narrowed to the most intense kind of motion there is - the adrenaline-charged lunge of combat, the fury of a raid, the ferocity of a feud. Some scholars flag this last step as conjectural: the Germanic root could be an independent formation whose deep pre-Germanic etymology is simply unknown, and the link to *kei- rests on Pokorny's broad root-bundling rather than on unambiguous evidence. What is not in doubt is the Germanic word family itself, with its consistent meaning of struggle and violent exertion. The word's great softening happened when it crossed the Channel through Frankish. The Franks, a West Germanic people, gave Old French a large stratum of Germanic vocabulary, and among these loans was the noun for violent impetuosity, which entered Gallo-Romance as haste. In Old French (12th century) haste already meant "haste, urgency, speed" - the violence had been shed, leaving only the residue of extreme urgency and rapid motion. English borrowed haste in the early 13th century (first attestations cluster around 1200-1225), and within a generation the word was firmly at home in Middle English, giving rise to hasty, hastily, and the phrase-world around them. The verb hasten is an English native formation. Middle English attached the productive infinitive suffix -en to the noun haste, producing forms like hastien (c. 1300) and, in the early modern period, the standard hasten (1560s). The formation is of a thoroughly ordinary type - the same pattern as frighten, heighten, strengthen - which is why the verb feels so transparently native despite its Romance-looking ancestry. Hastened is simply the regular past and past-participle form of this verb, marking completed or narrated acceleration. Semantically, the word has always walked a line between efficiency and risk. The medieval and early modern inheritance included the moral proverb "haste makes waste", which fixed the double valuation of speed: to hasten may be wise when time is short, but it may equally be rash. Literature exploited both faces. Shakespeare's plays ring with haste and hasten in scenes of conspiracy, flight, and declaration, where speed is morally charged; in later narrative prose, hastened became the novelist's verb for a character or a fate being hurried toward its end ("he hastened his ruin", "her departure was hastened"). The transitive sense - to cause an event to happen sooner - settled into formal, historical, and literary prose from the 17th century onward. In modern English the two central senses coexist. Intransitively, to hasten is to move quickly and deliberately ("she hastened to reassure them"); transitively, to hasten is to accelerate an outcome ("the storm hastened their departure"). The register has drifted upward: in spontaneous speech people hurry, rush, or dash, while hastened survives in writing, journalism, and narrative where the deliberate, anxious acceleration of events is being underscored - exactly the weight it carries in the card's context, where Feng Su's hastened urgency to comply with the mandarin's request is a matter of family honor and social survival rather than mere speed.
