- 13th to 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed from Old French *marjorane* / *majorane* (derived from Medieval Latin *majorana*, ultimately of uncertain Semitic or Sanskrit origin); established in monastic herb gardens as a prized culinary seasoning and medicinal carminative.
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor & Renaissance Era) - Widely cultivated across English manor and cottage gardens; featured prominently in Shakespearean references and domestic receipt books for "sweet bags" and strewing herbs to freshen household floors and linens.
- 17th to 18th Century (Augustan Era & Linnaean Botany) - Nicholas Culpeper's *The English Physician* (1652) praises it as an herb of Mercury for warming cold stomachs and soothing the brain; Linnaeus formally classifies it as *Origanum majorana* (1753).
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes a beloved staple of sentimental pastoral literature (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*) and traditional British butchery, permanently established in sausage seasonings and savory poultry stuffings.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as an indispensable gourmet culinary staple in European and Mediterranean cooking, widely celebrated in essential oil aromatherapy, natural perfumery, and organic gardening.
