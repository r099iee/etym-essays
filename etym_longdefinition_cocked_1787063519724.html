Cocked (/kɒkt/) is the past tense and past participle of the verb cock, meaning to raise, tilt, or set at an angle, and as an adjective describes something raised, turned up, or drawn back into a ready, alert position. In the reading context the thrush's head is cocked on one side — tilted sharply sideways, the classic listening pose of a bird.

Primary senses

  • Of the head: tilted or turned sharply to one side — the universal gesture of listening, suspicion, curiosity, or playful inquiry (head cocked on one side, a cocked head). This is the sense of the present text.
  • Of ears: raised, pricked forward, or held upright — as a dog or horse cocks its ears to listen; hence ears cocked as a general metaphor for alert attention.
  • Of a firearm: with the hammer drawn back into the firing position, so that the weapon is ready to fire — a cocked gun, cocked and locked (drawn and on safety). This is the origin of the idiom to go off half-cocked.
  • Of a hat: with the brim turned up at one or more points — a cocked hat is the three-cornered (tricorne) or two-cornered (bicorne) style of the 17th–18th centuries, its brims raised like a rooster's crest.

Secondary and extended meanings

  • Of limbs: bent at an angle, poised for action — a cocked fist, a cocked elbow, a cocked wrist drawn back for a throw, stroke, or punch.
  • Of eyebrows and eyes: raised or directed sidelong — cocking an eyebrow expresses scepticism or surprise; cocking an eye at something means glancing at it sharply and appraisingly.
  • Sports and crafts: cocking the wrist is deliberate positioning before a swing, serve, or stroke — the wrist is raised and locked back to generate power.
  • Mechanical: a lever, pawl, or spring cocked into a raised, tensioned, ready position before release; clockmakers speak of cocked mechanisms.
  • Slang: cocked (as in half-cocked) is used in some dialects and periods for being drunk or unsteadily intoxicated, a jocular extension of the not-fully-ready, not-fully-in-control idea.

The historical bridge

The verb cock grows out of the noun cock, the male fowl. A rooster's comb stands upright on its head and the bird holds its head alertly, tilting and jerking it as it watches. From that image the verb came to mean to set erect, turn up, raise jauntily: by the 17th century English was cocking hat brims, gun hammers, heads, and ears. Every modern sense of cocked descends from that single visual metaphor of the strutting, crest-raising bird — a compact family of meanings all tracing back to one barnyard image.

Register and nuance

  • Cocked head and cocked ears are neutral, vivid, everyday description.
  • Cocked hat is historical costume terminology; cocked gun is technical and carries the durable idiom half-cocked.
  • The drunken sense is dialectal and dated, and is now eclipsed by the gun idiom.
