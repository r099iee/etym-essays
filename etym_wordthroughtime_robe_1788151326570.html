- [13th–14th c.] - Enters English via Anglo-French as the fine loose outer garment of the wealthy; connotation of rank, display, luxury.
- [15th–16th c.] - Stabilises as the official attire of church, court, university, and the professions; “the long robe” becomes shorthand for the law and its officers. Perception shifts from fashionable garment to badge of office.
- [17th–18th c.] - Academic and legal ceremony standardises; “robed in ...” enters literary idiom as a metaphor of envelopment (grief, light, silence).
- [19th c.] - Romantic poetry (Wordsworth, Keats) uses “robe” for priestly and natural imagery; the garment itself begins to feel antiquated in ordinary daily dress.
- [20th c.] - In North America unqualified “robe” slides downmarket to bathrobe/dressing gown, creating the comic polarity of courtroom black and hotel terrycloth; “robed” grows in journalism as metaphor.
- [21st c.] - “Robing room,” “robed in secrecy,” “graduation robes” keep the word alive across ceremonial and figurative registers; the bathrobe sense dominates casual speech.
