tankard is a word whose meaning has moved from the cellar to the table. Its earliest English sense is a large tub or cask; its modern sense is a large drinking mug, usually with a handle and a lid. Behind that shift lies a long history of coopering, pewter-working, tavern life and the ritual of drinking beer, and behind the word itself lies one of the most stubborn etymological puzzles in English.

The earliest evidence. The Latinised form tancardus appears in a British source in 1266, and the English word is found in surnames from the late thirteenth century and in the sense “tub, cask” from the early fourteenth. A tankard in this period was a large wooden vessel, made of staves and bound with iron hoops, used for holding and carrying liquids. It was not yet a personal drinking cup. The sense “drinking vessel” is recorded from the late fifteenth century, and by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries tankards could be enormous: London water carriers used tankards holding two gallons or more. The modern pint or quart tankard is a later, smaller descendant of a much more capacious word.

The etymological puzzle. Middle English tan(c)kard(e) corresponds to Middle Dutch tanckaert, and related forms appear in Old French as tanquart and in Medieval Latin as tancardus. None of these can be derived with certainty from anything else. The OED’s tentative suggestion is that the English word is a metathesis of an unattested Medieval Latin *kantard, from Latin cantharus, a large drinking cup borrowed from Greek kantharos. Metathesis of this kind is attested elsewhere, and the semantic fit is excellent, but the intermediate form is not documented. The Dutch and French forms complicate the picture: if the word was coined in England, how did it reach the Continent so quickly? If it was borrowed from Dutch, why is the Dutch form not attested until the early modern period? The safest statement is that the origin is unknown and that the cantharus connection is an attractive hypothesis.

  • Latin cantharus: a large drinking cup, from Greek kantharos; the proposed ultimate source.
  • Medieval Latin tancardus (1266): the earliest attested form, in a British source.
  • Middle English tan(c)kard(e): “tub, cask”, the earliest English sense.
  • Middle Dutch tanckaert / Old French tanquart: related forms of uncertain priority.
  • Modern tankard: a large lidded drinking mug, especially of pewter or silver.

Rejected explanations. The idea that tankard is tank + -ard is repeatedly proposed by amateur etymologists and repeatedly rejected. Tank “a large container for liquid” is not recorded in English until the seventeenth century, long after tankard, and it comes from a different source (Portuguese tanque, ultimately Latin stagnum “pool”). The suffix -ard is real enough, but it does not explain the stem. Klein’s derivation from French tant quart “as much as a quarter” is a clever pun rather than a documented history, and it cannot account for the early Latin and Dutch forms. The word’s origin remains a genuine mystery, which is unusual for such a familiar object.

Materials and social history. The tankard’s history is also a history of materials. Wooden tankards were cheap and strong, but they affected the taste of the drink and were difficult to clean. Pewter, an alloy of tin with lead or copper, became the standard tavern metal in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: it was malleable, could be cast or hammered, and took a dull shine that was associated with good housekeeping. Silver tankards were luxury objects, often engraved and used for display as much as for drinking. Glass tankards, sometimes with metal mounts, became popular in the nineteenth century, and ceramic versions served the lower end of the market. Each material carried its own social meaning: pewter for the inn, silver for the family, glass for the modern bar.

Form and function. The hinged lid is the tankard’s most distinctive feature. It keeps the drink from spilling when the vessel is passed around, preserves the head on beer and keeps out dust and insects. The handle is large enough for a gloved or heavy hand, and the body is often slightly tapered so that it can be gripped and drained. The shape is designed for conviviality rather than delicacy: a tankard is meant to be raised, clinked and emptied. That is why it became a symbol of hospitality and good fellowship in literature and art.

Literature and modern life. The tankard appears wherever English literature stages a tavern: in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama, in eighteenth-century comic prints, in Dickens’s inns and in Wodehouse’s country houses. It is an object that instantly establishes a mood of warmth, tradition and slightly old-fashioned masculinity. In the twenty-first century tankards survive as antiques, as personalised gifts, as historical re-enactment equipment, as craft-beer glassware and as fantasy props. The word has never become archaic, but it has acquired a strong flavour of the past — the flavour of a pewter pot on a dark oak table, with a fire burning somewhere behind it.

Conclusion. Tankard began as a coopered tub and became a lidded drinking mug. Its origin is obscure, its cantharus connection is only a hypothesis, and its history is a record of materials, manners and the social life of drink. The word survives because the object survives, and because the object carries a set of associations — hospitality, solidity, tradition — that English speakers still find useful.
