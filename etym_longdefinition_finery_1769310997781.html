finery (noun, uncountable): showy, elegant, or expensive clothes, ornaments, and decorations — the brilliant dress worn for display on festive or formal occasions — and, as a separate technical homograph, a furnace or forge used to refine pig iron into wrought iron.

1. Primary sense — showy and elegant dress

In ordinary modern use, finery is the sum of one's best, most eye-catching clothing and adornment, worn when one wishes to be seen at one's most splendid:
  • "dressed in all her finery," "Sunday finery," "wedding finery," "the ladies in their finest finery for the ball" (the card's context);
  • it is a collective mass noun, covering clothes, jewels, ribbons, feathers, lace, and the whole apparatus of festive display;
  • it can extend to decoration and ornament generally, not only dress: the finery of a ballroom, the finery of a peacock's tail.
Key features of the primary sense:
  • It implies splendour and expense: finery is the "fine" exterior carried to its most brilliant, finished state.
  • It carries a double emotional temperature — admiration for the beauty, and a knowing smile at the effort, vanity, or occasion that produces it; "in all one's finery" is affectionate, lightly satirical, or admiring depending on tone.
  • Register: informal-literary, a little too warm for cold formal prose; it thrives in novels, period prose, and idiom.

2. Polysemy — the technical metallurgical sense

Entirely separate from the clothes word, a finery is a hearth, furnace, or forge in which pig (cast) iron is refined into wrought iron — the "finery process" and "finery fire" of iron-making (attested from the 17th–19th centuries in industrial English). Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the two words converge on the same idea of refinement. The dress sense descends from the adjective fine, "of high quality, delicate, finished"; the metallurgical sense descends from the verb refine (to make fine, to purify), itself built from fine + the prefix re-. In the word finery the personal and the industrial meet: both are about taking raw material — a person's appearance, or pig iron — and bringing it to a state of fineness and perfection. The doublet is a reminder that "fine" names the completed, polished condition of a thing in almost any domain.

3. Related words and register

  • fine (adjective) — the base: "of high quality, pure, delicate, excellent," and (adverb) "in a satisfactory way";
  • refine, refined, refinement — the process and quality of purification and perfecting;
  • finish, final, finite, define, confine, infinite, finance — the wider family from Latin finis "end, limit," where "to bring to an end" equals "to perfect" (the semantic bridge that made Old French fin mean both "end" and "fine");
  • In the eighteenth–nineteenth-century novel — the age of Austen — finery is the standard word of the marriage market and the ballroom, mingling spectacle with satire; it remains alive today chiefly in idioms such as "in all one's finery."
