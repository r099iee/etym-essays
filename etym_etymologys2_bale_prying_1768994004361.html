BALE — derivation chain PIE *bhel- "to swell, to blow, to inflate" (reconstructed; the root of ball, balloon, bellows, folly) → Proto-Germanic *balluz "ball, round object" (cf. Old Norse bǫllr "ball"; Old English *beallu, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Frankish *balla "a round bundle" (reconstructed; the form taken into Romance, c. 4th–8th c. CE) → Old French balle "a round bundle of goods" (attested 12th c.) → Middle English bale "a round bundle, a bale" (attested 13th c., c. 1300) → Modern English bale "a large tightly bound bundle" (the verb "to bale," to pack into bales, is a 19th-c. back-formation).

NUANCE NOTE: The word family is securely Germanic → Romance → English, and the PIE root *bhel- for "ball" is well established. "Bale" (bundle) must NOT be confused with Old English bealu "evil, misery," a different, unrelated word; nor with "to bail/bale out" (to scoop water), from Old French baille "bucket."

PRYING — derivation chain Middle English prien "to look closely, to peer narrowly" (attested late 13th c., c. 1290; origin uncertain — no secure PIE or Romance etymology; the OED marks it as unknown, possibly related to "peer" from Old French perer "to appear," or of expressive origin) → Modern English to pry "to look curiously and intrusively" (16th c.) → prying (participial adjective, "inquisitive, intrusive"; the moral coloring acquired in the 17th c.).

SEPARATE HOMOPHONE (influence, not ancestry): Old French prise "a grip, a hold; the act of taking" → English prize / prise "a lever; the act of levering" → to prise / to pry "to force open with a lever" (17th c.; US spelling "pry"). The two verbs became homophones and are now often felt and used as one word, which is why a "prying eye" feels like a crowbar working on secrets.

DEBATE / NUANCE NOTE: The origin of the "to look" pry is genuinely unknown and is stated as such rather than guessed; the lever-pry is a separate word whose history is well documented.
