- Late 14th c. — snar snaps: Middle English has the short imitative verb for dogs snapping and men speaking sharply, a bare sound-word with no fixed spelling.
- 1590s — the snarl lengthens: the -l frequentative ending stretches the snap into a sustained growl — not one bite but a continuous threat — and Shakespeare's age gains its word for the bared-teeth rumble.
- 17th–18th c. — humans borrow it: villains, scolds, and ill-tempered masters snarl their words in plays and novels; the dog's voice becomes the standard acoustic for human brutishness.
- 19th c. — machines join the pack: saws, engines, and storms snarl in industrial prose, while the twin tangle-sense (traffic, thread, tape) tangles happily alongside.
- 20th c. — the traffic report: snarled becomes the classic broadcaster's word for gridlock — traffic snarled for miles — carrying the dog's temper into the daily commute.
- Today — threat in one syllable: snarled remains the instant signal of bared-teeth hostility, from guard dogs to headlines, unchanged because the sound it imitates has never changed.
