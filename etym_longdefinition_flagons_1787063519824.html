Core meaning. A flagon is a large vessel used for holding and pouring liquids, chiefly alcoholic drink such as wine, ale, or cider. The word is used both for the historical container of medieval and early-modern tables and, in modern usage, for any large bottle or jug of drink, especially one with a handle, a narrow neck, and usually a lid or stopper. A flagon is bigger than a bottle and typically bigger than a mug or cup; it is meant to supply several servings rather than one. Flagons were commonly made of metal (pewter, silver, brass), pottery, or, in modern times, glass or plastic.

Primary senses.

  • Historical table vessel: in medieval and early-modern England and continental Europe, a flagon was a large drinking vessel with a handle, a broad base, and often a hinged or loose lid, used for ale, wine, or mead, and passed around a company or used to fill smaller cups and goblets.
  • Church / liturgical vessel: flagons (typically of silver) hold the wine for Communion in many Christian churches; in Anglican practice a flagon is a large covered vessel from which the chalice is filled.
  • Modern / retail usage: a large glass or plastic bottle of wine, beer, cider, or other drink, often with a handle and a screw cap, used for casual bulk purchase.
  • Loose colloquial: by extension, any large container of liquid — e.g. a big flask of coffee — though this usage is rare and slightly jocular.

Distinctions from related vessels.

  • From a bottle: a flagon is traditionally larger, usually has a handle, and may have a lid; the words overlap in modern retail use.
  • From a jug / pitcher: a pitcher is an open pouring vessel; a flagon is more often covered and is associated with drink rather than water or general pouring.
  • From a tankard: a tankard is a personal drinking cup with a handle; a flagon is a bulk container that fills tankards.
  • From a flask: a flask is a smaller, flat or pocket vessel; flagon and flask share the same Germanic root but diverged in size and shape.

Materials and tavern culture. Historically flagons were made of pewter, brass, wood, leather, earthenware, or silver; a tavern flagon held a quart or more of ale and sat on the bar to serve several customers, while the master of a house kept a personal cup for drinking. In the medieval economy the flagon was a basic item of household inventory, appearing in wills, inventories, and church accounts. Church flagons of silver held the communion wine and were often among the most valuable items of church plate.

Modern word-family. The family includes flagon, the older doublet flask (both from the same Germanic source), the diminutive flacon (a small stoppered perfume bottle, re-borrowed from French in the modern era), and the informal plural flagons for rows of bottled drink. The modern retail flagon — a large handled bottle of wine or cider — is usually plastic or glass with a resealable cap.

Register and literary role. Because flagons belong to taverns, feasts, and epic halls, the word has a distinctly old-fashioned, literary, or fantasy-flavoured tone today: it appears in historical fiction, Tolkien-style fantasy, and poetry far more often than in ordinary conversation. In the source context — flagons lying among skulls and bones on the floor of a dragon’s hall — the word evokes desolation and the wreck of former feasts. This archaic register is the word’s main modern habitat.

Secondary and figurative uses. The word is occasionally used figuratively for abundance or heavy drinking (“a flagon’s worth of ale”), and in fantasy gaming (tabletop RPGs) a flagon is a standard piece of tavern equipment. There is no technical scientific sense; the word remains firmly within the domain of vessels for drink.
