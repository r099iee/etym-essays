Quintilian is the English name of Marcus Fabius Quintilianus (c. 35 – c. 100 CE), the Roman teacher of rhetoric whose twelve-book Institutio Oratoria is the fullest surviving ancient treatise on the training of an orator. In the source, Donald Bryant ranks him second only to Cicero among the sources of the rhetorical tradition that ran through the Renaissance into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: “after him Quintilian and Aristotle.” The name thus denotes a man, a book and a whole method of education at once.

Life. Quintilian was born at Calagurris in Hispania Tarraconensis, modern Calahorra in northern Spain, the son of a rhetorician. He was educated at Rome, returned to Spain, and came back to the city in the entourage of Galba in 68 CE. He taught rhetoric with such distinction that Vespasian, as part of his policy of imperial patronage for education, endowed a public chair of rhetoric for him — the first such salaried post at Rome, worth 100,000 sesterces a year from the treasury. He retired around 88 CE and spent his leisure on the Institutio. Domitian made him tutor to the two sons of his cousin Flavius Clemens, boys the emperor had marked out as his heirs, and granted him the consular ornaments. He died around the end of the century. Among his pupils was Pliny the Younger; Tacitus is sometimes thought to have studied with him, though the evidence is indirect.

The Institutio Oratoria. The work, dedicated to the lawyer Vitorius Marcellus and published in the mid-90s, follows the orator from the cradle to retirement.

  • Book 1: the education of the child — the nurse, the first lessons, the choice between Greek and Latin, grammar, music, geometry and astronomy as liberal supports to eloquence.
  • Book 2: the transition from grammar to rhetoric and the elements of the art.
  • Books 3–6: invention, the theory of status, the parts of a speech, emotion and character.
  • Books 7–9: arrangement, style, ornament and the figures of thought and speech.
  • Book 10: the famous survey of Greek and Latin literature and the theory of imitation — what to read, and how to make it one’s own.
  • Books 11–12: delivery and memory, then the moral and philosophical formation of the perfect orator, defined by Cato’s formula as vir bonus dicendi peritus, “a good man skilled in speaking”.

Ideas and temperament. Quintilian is humane, practical and moralizing. He prefers public schooling to private tutors for its social training, condemns corporal punishment, urges that early learning be made a game, and insists that eloquence without goodness is mere weapons in the hands of a madman. He is not a narrow Ciceronian: he admires Cicero above all, but recommends a wide canon of models and warns against the sterile imitation of a single author — a point worth remembering beside the purism of Renaissance Ciceronians such as Nizzoli.

The name itself. Marcus Fabius Quintilianus follows the Roman three-name pattern. Marcus is a common praenomen of uncertain origin, linked by the ancients to Mars; Fabius is the gentilicium of the ancient Fabian clan, traditionally connected with faba, “bean”, though that is folk etymology rather than proof. Quintilianus is a cognomen formed with the suffix -iānus, “belonging to”, from the nomen Quintilius, itself built on the praenomen Quintus, “fifth”, from Latin quīntus and ultimately PIE *penkʷe, “five”. Roman praenomina such as Quintus, Sextus and Decimus began as birth-order names, but by the classical period they were inherited labels; the name does not mean that Quintilian was a fifth child. The spelling Quinctilius, with a c, also occurs for the clan, and the relation between the two forms is orthographic and gentilicial rather than a separate etymology.

Reputation and modern reference. “Quintilian” is a bookish proper noun. It appears in literary history, in the history of education and in bibliographic citation (“Quintilian, Institutio 10.1”), and it can stand attributively for the tradition he represents — “Quintilian rhetoric”, “the Quintilian curriculum”. To invoke him is to invoke the ideal of the morally serious, comprehensively educated speaker, and the schoolroom world of imitation, declamation and canonical reading that dominated European education for centuries.

Why he matters here. Bryant’s sentence reflects the actual architecture of Renaissance and early modern schooling: Cicero supplied the model of style, Quintilian the theory and sequence of instruction, and Aristotle the philosophical analysis of persuasion. The recovery of Quintilian’s complete text in 1416 gave humanists a classical blueprint for the grammar school, and his authority helped make Cicero the centre of the curriculum. When Nizzoli’s Thesaurus claimed to be a compendium of pure Ciceronian language, it was working within a tradition that Quintilian had done as much as anyone to shape.
