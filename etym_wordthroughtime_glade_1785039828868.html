- 16th century - 'glade' (probably from the bright root of 'glad,' as in Norse 'gladr') appears meaning a bright open space in a wood, a luminous gap where light falls through the canopy; the earliest sense is one of shining, of openness and gleam.
- 17th-18th century - the word settles into landscape description as a level, open space in a forest, prized in estate design and garden poetry as the spot where the gloom breaks; it remains a quiet, poetic word.
- 19th century - Romantic and Victorian nature-writing makes the glade a set-piece of gentle, sunlit refuge — a place of dappled light and relief within the dark wood — and it appears constantly in pastoral verse and fiction.
- 19th-20th century (American extension) - in American English 'glade' is also applied to open, wet grassland among trees, as in the Florida Everglades, stretching the old brightness into marsh country.
- Contemporary - 'glade' remains literary and gentle: an open, grassy, sunlit clearing, the threshold between forest gloom and light; more luminous and poetic than the plain, functional 'clearing.'
