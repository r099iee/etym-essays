- Mid-16th Century - Borrowed into Early Modern English from Latin and French; initially utilized in natural philosophy and alchemy to describe physical bodies, viscous fluids, or sticky particles adhering tightly together.
- 17th Century (Scientific Revolution) - Extended by natural philosophers and logicians (Bacon, Hobbes, Locke) into epistemology and rhetoric to describe chains of reasoning, axioms, and philosophical treatises whose arguments hang together without logical rupture.
- 18th to 19th Century (Enlightenment & Philological Age) - Flourishes across textual criticism, jurisprudence, and classical scholarship; editors strive to assemble "coherent texts" from corrupt, fragmented classical recensions, while jurists evaluate "coherent witness testimony."
- 20th Century - Revolutionized across quantum physics, laser optics, and communications engineering (Einstein's stimulated emission, lasers, quantum phase coherence, coherent radar), becoming an indispensable technical term for wave-train synchronization.
- 21st Century - Deeply integrated into cognitive neuroscience, artificial intelligence (evaluating large language model narrative coherence), and modernist genetic criticism, remaining the supreme hallmark of intelligible, unified human thought.
