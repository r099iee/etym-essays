• Beams: Similar in describing columns of light; differs because beams are generally broader and more diffuse, while shafts are sharp, narrow, spear-like rays with distinct structural edges.
  • Rays: Similar in lines of light; differs because rays are abstract mathematical vectors of light, while shafts evoke a solid, physical, lance-like visual presence.
  • Lances: Similar in piercing linear form; differs because lances are specifically military weapons used metaphorically for light only in rare poetic contexts, while shafts is an established optical term.
  • Columns (of light): Similar in a vertical body of light; differs because a column is broad and supports an opening, whereas a shaft is narrow, sharp-edged, and penetrating.
  • Spears (of light): Similar in the piercing, pointed line of brightness; differs because spears is a purely poetic transfer from the weapon, while shafts names the weapon itself in its oldest sense and is standard in describing light.
  • Pillars: Similar in an upright columnar form; differs because a pillar implies mass and support, while a shaft of light is slender and penetrative.
  • Bolts (of light): Similar in a sudden, straight flash; differs because a bolt is instantaneous and blinding, whereas a shaft is a sustained, defined column.
  • Sodded: Similar in being covered with living grass sod; differs because sodded is purely agricultural and utilitarian, while turfed carries richer literary, sporting, and territorial connotations.
  • Grassed: Similar in being covered with grass; differs because grassed specifies simple vegetation, while turfed emphasizes the deep, interwoven root-mat and the underlying humus cushion.
  • Swarded: Similar in being covered with a grassy surface; differs because swarded is a literary term for a smooth grassy expanse, while turfed names the laid or living sod itself with its thickness.
  • Verdant: Similar in green and grass-covered; differs because verdant stresses the fresh green color and growth, while turfed stresses the physical mat of roots and soil.
  • Mat of grass / Turfed-out (ejected): Similar to the slang turfed out in meaning thrown out; differs because expelled is formal institutional removal, while turfed out is vivid vernacular ejection, from the image of flinging a sod.
