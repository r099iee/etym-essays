aboard — On or into a ship, aircraft, or other vehicle; within the operational space of a vessel or conveyance, committed to its voyage. The word marks the transition from land to water (or rail, or air) — the moment a person is taken into the company and keeping of a moving craft.

The literary moment. In the sentence "He is taken on for New Orleans aboard a flatboat," aboard is the hinge between land and water, childhood and river-labor. The flatboat is the crude, drifting cargo raft of the pre-steamboat Mississippi, and to be taken on — a phrase that is at once a hiring and an embarking — aboard such a vessel is to enter the fluid, mobile world of the river itself. The word converts the boy from a stationary, land-bound figure into a participant in the great drifting commerce of the frontier, one who will spend "forty-two days on the river" moving toward New Orleans. The tone is quiet, factual, almost documentary: the sentence registers the transaction without sentiment, and aboard supplies the exact spatial word that makes the boy's transition both physical and contractual.

Primary sense — spatial. In its oldest and still central use, aboard means "on board a ship," and by extension "on or into" any vehicle of passage — a train, an aircraft, a bus. It differs from the bare preposition on in implying enclosure within the vessel's working space: to be aboard is to be part of its company and committed to its voyage, not merely standing on its deck.

Secondary senses — extension and abstraction. From the literal vessel the word has climbed to any institution one "enters": welcome aboard (a new member of a crew or team), take this on board (accept an idea or responsibility), and the modern corporate verb to onboard, which inducts a new employee exactly as a new hand was signed onto a vessel. The spatial fact has become a metaphor for institutional belonging.

  • The nautical core: aboard a ship — committed to the vessel, surrendered to its voyage; the word of embarkation and of the crew's shared life.
  • The vehicular extension (19th–20th c.): aboard a train, a plane, a bus — the fixed cries of transport ("all aboard!") carry the old nautical force into the railway station and the airport.
  • The figurative / institutional sense (20th c.–present): "welcome aboard," "take this on board," and the verb to onboard — inducting a person into an organization as a new hand was once signed onto a vessel.
  • The contracted phrase: aboard is historically the phrase "on board" (a- "on" + board "the ship's plank"), contracted and reshaped; the phrase and the word still run in parallel in modern English.
  • The ship's grammar: aboard belongs to a family of board-words from the same nautical plank — overboard (over the side into the sea), starboard (the steering side), larboard (the loading side) — all the same cut plank, seen from different places on the hull.

Why aboard says more than "on." The historical bridge is a single piece of wood. English board descends from a Germanic word for a plank cut from a log; because a ship's hull was built of boards, the word came to mean "the side of a ship," and from that, "to go on board" meant to commit your weight to the vessel's wooden fabric. Aboard is thus, etymologically, "on the plank" — and the plank survives in every modern use, from the boarding pass of an aircraft to the onboarding session of a corporation. The word is a small monument to the fact that ships were built of cut wood before they were built of steel.

Register. Aboard is standard, slightly formal, and inherently dynamic — it almost always accompanies a verb of motion or state of commitment (go aboard, come aboard, climb aboard, be aboard). It is the vocabulary of embarkation in every register, from the sailor's logbook to the flight attendant's announcement, and its long figurative reach makes it equally at home in the sentence of the card and in the human-resources memo.
