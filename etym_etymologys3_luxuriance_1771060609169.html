The evolution of luxuriance: from a dislocated joint to the riot of the garden. 1. Proto-Indo-European: the twisted and the broken (c. 4500–3000 BCE). The story of "luxuriance" begins at the reconstructed root *leug-, whose earliest meaning was markedly physical and violent: "to bend, to twist, to turn aside, to break." In the ancient ancestor language this root described distortion — the bending of a branch, the wrenching of a joint, the breaking of a limb — and its earliest reflexes are mostly negative. Sanskrit "rujáti" means "he breaks, he shatters, he causes pain," and from it classical Sanskrit formed "roga," "disease, illness," literally "that which bends or breaks the body" (Hindi "rog," sickness). Greek preserves "lugrós," "mournful, wretched, bent in spirit." The Germanic branch kept the tactile image of something twisted in "lock" (of hair, from the idea of a curl or twist, Old English "locc") and possibly in the verb "lock." At this stage there is no trace of luxury, excess, or lush growth: the root means deviation from a straight line, and deviation is understood as damage. 2. Latin: a word out of joint (2nd c. BCE – 1st c. CE). In Latin the root surfaces as "luxus," and its earliest attested sense is medical: "dislocated, thrown out of joint." The Roman encyclopaedist Celsus uses "luxare" and "luxus" of a joint slipping from its socket. But Roman culture was supremely metaphorical, and the leap was made early: something physically "out of joint" stands outside its proper, healthy bounds, and Latin extended "luxus" to anything that departed from the strict boundaries of traditional Roman morality — extravagance, self-indulgence, deviation from frugal ancestral virtue. The derived noun "luxuria" came to mean "rankness, excessive growth, wantonness, excess": it could describe over-lush fields, unbridled desire, or moral licence at once. The verb "luxuriāre" ("to grow rank, to run riot") is used by Virgil of vines and corn shooting up with uncontrollable vigour, and the participle "luxuriāns" ("growing rank, running riot") is the direct ancestor of our word. Both the botanical and the moral senses were present in Latin from the start, and both would travel to the modern languages. 3. Romance divergence: lust on the Continent, growth in the North (13th–17th c.). The descendants of "luxuria" took divergent paths. In the Romance languages the moral sense triumphed almost completely: Old French "luxurie," French "luxure," Italian "lussuria," and Spanish "lujuria" all mean "lust, lechery, wantonness." English inherited both strands. In Middle English, "luxurie" (via Old French) meant "lust, sexual sin," and "luxury" long meant "lustful pleasure or self-indulgence" before drifting toward "sumptuous comfort." But the botanical branch also entered English from French: the adjective "luxuriant" (16th century) and the noun "luxuriance" (17th century) were taken from Middle French "luxuriance," which preserved the Latin sense of rank, excessive, riotous growth. In English the moral and botanical meanings split cleanly: the moral charge went to "luxury" and the obsolete "luxurie," while "luxuriance" and "luxuriant" were freed to mean lush, thriving abundance. 4. The aesthetic rehabilitation (18th–19th c.). Freed of its heaviest moral condemnations, "luxuriance" in English became a favourite word of naturalists, travellers, and critics. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century writers apply it enthusiastically to tropical vegetation — "the luxurious luxuriance of the East Indian flora," "the rank luxuriance of the jungle" — and then extend it by metaphor to everything that grows, elaborates, or overflows: "the luxuriance of her hair," "the luxuriance of his imagination," "the luxuriance of the decorative style," "the luxurious luxuriance of a Gothic interior." The semantic bridge is the old image of a plant "running riot" beyond its bed: anything that overflows its proper bounds with rich vitality is luxuriant. The word also drifted into a milder, almost wholly approving sense of "healthy, vigorous fullness," losing almost all its ancient undertone of intemperate excess, so that the modern user is usually surprised to learn that "luxuria" meant wantonness and lust. 5. Present condition and register. Today "luxuriance" is a formal, literary, and slightly old-fashioned noun. It names the quality of being lush, thriving, and abundant — chiefly of vegetation, but also of hair, health, prose, and ornament — and it retains a heavy, opulent, sensual register that distinguishes it from the plain "abundance." The sound history is regular: the Latin "u" in "luxus" gave French and English "u/y" spelling, so the word is pronounced with a velar "g" sound before the "u" ("lug-zhur-iance") in the English spelling pronunciation, and the suffix "-ance" is the standard Latin-derived abstract-noun ending. The story of the word — from a dislocated joint, to moral excess, to the riot of the garden — is a striking example of how an ancient image of physical deviation was successively reinterpreted as moral deviation and then as glorious abundance.
