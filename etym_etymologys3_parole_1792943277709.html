The journey of parole begins with throwing. Its deepest ancestor is the reconstructed PIE root *gʷelh₁-, meaning to throw or cast, which supplied Ancient Greek with ballein, to throw, and hence parabolē — literally a throwing-beside, a comparison in which one thing is set alongside another — the source of English parable.

Latin borrowed the Greek noun as parabola, comparison or allegory, and then something remarkable happened in the mouths of ordinary late-imperial speakers: the learned word slid down the social scale and became everyday paraula, then paravola, meaning simply speech or talk. Scholars call such refugee learned wordsplebeianised — the same fate that turned Latin parabola into French parole, Spanish palabra, and Italian parola, every one of them meaning word.

Old French parole carried the full dignity of the spoken pledge: a knight's parole was his mouth-bond, and military custom across Europe freed captured officers on their parole — their signed promise not to serve again until exchanged. English borrowed the word twice, first as legal parol for oral pleading and testimony, then as military parole for the honour-promise and its daily password twin, the watchword passed by mouth.

The decisive modern turn came with nineteenth-century penal reform. At Elmira Reformatory in New York, Zebulon Brockway built a system of earned supervised release that borrowed the old honour-word for a new bureaucratic machine: indeterminate sentences, marks for good conduct, and release on licence. France's libération conditionnelle and Britain's ticket-of-leave ran on parallel tracks, but it was the American parole, with its boards and officers, that fixed the global sense — freedom as a contract, revocable at a signature.

  • Sound shifts: Greek parabolē lost its middle consonant in Vulgar Latin speech (parabola to paraula), the intervocalic b softening to a glide — the same weakening that gave French its fluid music; English then stressed the second syllable in the French manner.
  • Semantic drift: throwing-beside to comparison to allegory to ordinary talk to pledged word to supervised liberty — each step narrows the mouth's power from describing the world to binding the speaker.
  • Socio-cultural context: chivalric honour made the military parole thinkable; industrial-era faith in rehabilitation made the penal parole administrable; twentieth-century risk politics made it controversial, with boards, scores, and victims' hearings.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare's soldiers give parole on the battlefield; Napoleonic memoirs are thick with officers on parole in country towns; Saussure's Course in General Linguistics (1916) canonised the langue-versus-parole distinction; modern true-crime and courtroom drama have made the parole hearing a stock scene of suspense.

So the prisoner who walks out on parole carries twenty centuries of thrown words with him: a Greek comparison, a Roman story, a French promise, and an American contract — all kept alive in the mouth, where the word began.
