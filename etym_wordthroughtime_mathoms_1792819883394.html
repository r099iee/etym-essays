- 8th–11th c. — kingly treasure: Old English māþum means swords, rings, and jewels, the shining gifts lords give heroes in Beowulf, grave and glorious.
- 11th–19th c. — sleep: the word dies with Old English, surviving only in manuscripts and glossaries while its thing, the treasure-gift, lives on under French names like jewel and present.
- 1937 — Tolkien wakes it: The Hobbit revives mathom as the Shire word for presents too good to throw away, treasure sunk deliciously low into comedy.
- 1954–55 — the museum opens: The Lord of the Rings gives mathoms a home, the Mathom-house at Michel Delving, turning private clutter into public heritage.
- Late 20th c. — fandom adopts it: readers name their own attic boxes and spare-dice drawers mathoms, a wink that says I too keep things of forgotten use.
- Today — the affectionate clutter-word: rare outside Tolkien circles but instantly loved there, mathoms dignifies hoarding as Hobbit thrift rather than waste.
