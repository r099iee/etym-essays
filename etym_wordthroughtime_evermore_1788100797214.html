- Old English — the phrase "ǣfre mā" ("ever more") is used in homilies and prose for unending time; it is not yet one word, but the two halves already mean "for ever".
- 13th c. — the compound "evermore" (evermo, euer more) is recorded; religious prose and lyrics use it of God, heaven, and the soul's eternity — "world without end" territory.
- 14th c. — Chaucer makes "evermore" a routine literary adverb for "always, continually": narrators and lovers remember, serve, and sorrow evermore; the habitual and the eternal senses run side by side.
- 16th–17th c. — the Bible (KJV, 1611: "Lord, evermore give us this bread", John 6:34) and the Prayer Book fix "evermore" as the solemn word of eternal duration; vows and doxologies take it up ("from this time forth and for evermore").
- 18th–19th c. — Romantic and Victorian poetry light it with longing: lovers vow evermore; Poe (1845) coins the dark twin "nevermore"; the word becomes the poetic standard for unending hope and unending grief.
- 20th–21st c. — "evermore" survives as formal and literary: hymns, vows, solemn prose, and deliberate archaism; everyday speech prefers "forever", while "evermore" keeps its eternity intact for poetry — and for Paradise.
