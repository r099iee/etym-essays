Paperoles (frequently spelled in French as paperolles) is a specialized literary, philological, and art-historical noun designating the long, accordion-folded strips of manuscript paper pasted onto notebook leaves, typescripts, or printer's galleys to accommodate massive textual accretions, marginal expansions, and second-thought revisions. In modern literary theory, genetic criticism (*critique génétique*), and European cultural history, the term is universally recognized as the iconic material emblem of Marcel Proust’s creative praxis during the composition and endless expansion of his seven-volume magnum opus, À la recherche du temps perdu. Rather than viewing a draft or a printed proof as a fixed, stable boundary, Proust treated every page as a modular, porous lattice, gluing supplementary paper slips to margins and fold-outs that unrolled like medieval scrolls or accordions across his cork-lined bedroom.

In the specific narrative and biographical context of the novel—as highlighted in the source citation—the term is placed into the mouth of Françoise, the narrator’s loyal, formidable, and delightfully rustic family cook and housekeeper (a fictional composite drawn from Proust's real-life domestic caretakers Félicie Fitau and Céleste Albaret). Françoise, observing the narrator's bedside desk submerged beneath a blizzard of pasted paper strips, applies the domestic, slightly condescending term paperoles to these precarious linguistic constructions. The contrast between Françoise's earthy, dismissive household vernacular and the profound, monumental architecture of modernist memory that those strips embodied constitutes one of the novel's most celebrated meta-literary touches.

The noun encompasses distinct historical, technical, and metaphorical dimensions across multiple domains:

  • Proustian Genetic Criticism and Manuscript Studies (Primary Sense): In the archives of the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF), where Proust's manuscripts are preserved in the Department of Western Manuscripts, the paperoles constitute an official cataloging and analytical category. Proust created over one hundred of these complex folded inserts. When a revision outgrew the page margin, Céleste Albaret would cut strips of paper, apply paste, and attach them to the top, bottom, or sides of the notebook leaves (cahiers) or printer's proof sheets (placards). Some paperoles reached lengths of nearly two meters, containing multiple sub-layers of pasting that documented the multi-year geological accretion of entire characters, philosophical discourses, and social episodes.
  • Historical Craft and Religious Paper Filigree (Art History): Prior to its Proustian immortalization, paperolle (often spelled paperolle or paperoles) designated a delicate decorative handicraft known in English as paper quilling or paper filigree. Beginning in the Renaissance and flourishing through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries across French, Italian, and Spanish convents, impoverished nuns rolled narrow strips of gilded or colored paper around bird quills. These tightly spiraled paper coils were then assembled into intricate, lacelike rosettes, cartouches, and scrolls used to frame religious relics, holy portraits, and altarpieces, ingeniously imitating expensive goldsmith work (*orfèvrerie*).
  • Nineteenth-Century French Administrative and Household Slang: In general French colloquial speech throughout the nineteenth century, paperole functioned as a dismissive or affectionate diminutive of papier (paper), formed with the diminutive suffix -ole. In household, legal, and bureaucratic parlance, it referred to worthless scraps of scribbled paper, ephemeral slips, trivial chits, or cluttering bureaucratic paperwork (closely akin to paperasses). When Françoise uses the term in Proust's novel, she invokes precisely this rustic, skeptical connotation of messy, useless scrap-paper cluttering a clean bedroom.
  • Metaphor of Modular, Montage-Based Literary Composition: In contemporary literary theory and aesthetic philosophy, paperoles has expanded into a powerful metaphor for non-linear, modular, and assemblage-based artistic creation. Critics employ the term to describe any creative methodology—from the collages of the Dadaists and Walter Benjamin's Arcades Project (*Passagenwerk*) to modern hypertext and digital montage—wherein a work is continuously disassembled, spliced, augmented, and reorganized through physical or virtual accretion.

The semantic bridge uniting religious nun's quilling, domestic scrap-paper, and Proustian genetic philology is the physical form of the narrow strip of paper (papier + diminutive -ole). Whether rolled around a quill to frame a saint's relic, discarded on a parlor table by an exasperated housekeeper, or unrolled across a nocturnal bed to house an immortal sentence on time and memory, the word fundamentally denotes a modest paper slip transfigured by human ingenuity.
