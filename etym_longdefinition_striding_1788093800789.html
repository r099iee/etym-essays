Striding is the present participle and gerund of the verb to stride, and in the source passage it acts as a vivid active participle: the man who speaks is advancing with long, firm, deliberate steps even as he talks, so that his gait itself telegraphs authority, urgency and purpose. To stride is to walk with a markedly lengthened step, the legs moving in a wide, regular, unhurried rhythm that carries the body high and covers ground economically — the opposite of shuffling, dawdling, hobbling or running.

Grammatical functions of the -ing form. As a participle it builds the progressive tenses (“he was striding away”): it describes an on-going manner of motion. As a participial adjective it labels an attribute (“a striding gait”, “striding legs”). As a gerund it names the action as an event or process (“the striding of the column”), exactly the pattern of the parallel gerund “mustering” in the companion sentence of this same source text.

Primary senses of the underlying verb.

  • To walk with long steps — the core physical sense (intransitive or transitive): “she strode across the hall” — the emphasis falls on the length and regularity of the step rather than on speed.
  • To bestride, straddle or mount — an older sense preserved from Old English: to place one leg on either side of something, especially a horse; “bestride” (as in Shakespeare) keeps this alive.
  • To step over or cross in one stride — “to stride a brook” = to clear it in a single long step; this spanning idea underlies the figurative “to stride the world” = to dominate or encompass a whole domain.

Secondary, technical and metaphorical senses (all of which appear in the -ing form as well):

  • Figurative progress — “striding towards peace”, “striding ahead of rivals”: the image of covering ground in long, unforced steps becomes the metaphor for confident, unstoppable achievement; the fixed idiom “to make great strides” (to progress) rests on the same image, and the participle generalises it: a firm “striding toward profitability” is advancing with assurance and momentum.
  • Spanning a domain — “striding the globe”, “striding across the centuries”: the one-step-crossing sense expands to occupying or affecting a whole territory, era or field.
  • Computing: the array stride — in computer science a “stride” is the fixed distance in memory between successive elements of an array or successive samples of a sequence (for example when iterating a sub-sampling pattern); “strided access” patterns are a standard topic of cache- and SIMD-performance engineering. The word transfers the measured, evenly spaced step onto addresses in memory.
  • Stride piano — a jazz piano style of the 1920s (James P. Johnson, Fats Waller) in which the left hand “strides” back and forth between deep bass notes and mid-range chords, covering a wide span; the name captures the long-reach, wide-step motion of the hand.
  • Athletics and biomechanics — “stride length”, “stride frequency”, “striding technique”: quantified coaching terminology for running and speed-walking.
  • Idioms — “to take (something) in one's stride”: to absorb a difficulty without breaking pace, as a walker simply steps over the obstacle and continues; “to hit one's stride”: to reach the point where effort and rhythm become easy and efficient; “to stride the deck” (naval, of officers pacing the quarterdeck).

The semantic bridge. The entire family hangs on one image: a long, regular, deliberate step. Walking with long steps (sense 1) is the ground; because a long step forces the legs wide apart, the word extends to straddling and bestriding (sense 2); because one long step can clear an obstacle, it extends to crossing (sense 3); because a person who covers ground steadily and quickly appears to surmount effort and distance effortlessly, the word extends to confident progress and mastery (sense 4); the technical uses (memory stride, stride piano, stride length) simply measure or imitate the identical measured, repetitive, wide-step motion in other media.

Register and connotation. “Striding” is markedly more literary and assertive than neutral walking verbs. It implies purpose, vigour and often dignity or menace; in narrative prose (as here) it characterises a decisive, authoritative figure. In everyday speech it survives chiefly inside idioms (“make strides”, “hit your stride”, “take it in your stride”) and in fitness and business prose; the bare verb “to stride” is at home in written narrative, while the -ing form is the standard image for unstoppable, self-possessed advance.
