To hurtle is to move with enormous speed and violent, barely controlled momentum, with mass, noise, and the constant threat of impact. In the narrative aside describing the Great Goblin's fall — “as he came hurtling down like a thunderbolt” — the word condenses the entire physics of doom: the goblin-king, struck down in the dark, plunges from a great height among his own subjects, too heavy and too fast for anyone to catch or check. The simile 'like a thunderbolt' doubles the force, lending the fall the velocity and flash of divine wrath, as though the crash itself were the judgment, while the participle hurtling supplies the raw, unstoppable mechanics — speed, weight, rushing air, the sickening inevitability of the landing far below. The tone is epic and grimly comic at once: Tolkien delivers the death of a tyrant in the language of slapstick disaster, the verb so vivid that the reader almost hears the descent before the blow.

1. The core sense and its grammar.

The verb is essentially intransitive in modern English: a thing hurtles — a meteor hurtles through the atmosphere, a train hurtles down the track, a body hurtles from a cliff. The older transitive sense, 'to dash or fling violently, to cause to collide', recorded from the fourteenth to the sixteenth centuries, is now archaic; what survives is the intransitive sense of violent, uncontrolled, high-speed movement that became standard from the sixteenth century onward. The -ing participle (hurtling) functions both as a progressive verb (the rock is hurtling down) and as an attributive adjective (the hurtling rock), and it is this participle that the card's sentence uses — the form in mid-flight, the action caught at its most unstoppable.

2. The standing confusion with hurl.

Hurtling is constantly confused with hurling, and the boundary between them is worth fixing. To hurl is to throw — the subject is the thrower, the verb transitive: a pitcher hurls a ball, an angry man hurls abuse. To hurtle is to be thrown, to move or fall at terrifying speed — the subject is the thing in flight, the verb intransitive: the ball hurtles toward the fence. The two descend from the same Middle English frequentative verb built on Old French hurter, which is why they are so easy to swap, but modern usage keeps them apart, and the Oxford dictionaries register hurl (throw) and hurtle (speed/fall) as separate words. The confusion is a standing usage trap precisely because their histories ran side by side for two centuries before diverging in the sixteenth.

3. The near-synonym field.

  • Plummet emphasizes a straight vertical fall under gravity but lacks the roaring, crashing volume and the sense of mass that hurtling insists on — a feather plummets, a boulder hurtles.
  • Plunge suggests violent movement, often into water or darkness, and keeps a direction or destination; hurtling is more purely about uncontrolled speed and impending impact.
  • Careen (American use) means to rush or career wildly, swaying from side to side, but without the sheer destructive momentum; career as a verb means to move swiftly and uncontrollably, but is less weighty and less menacing.
  • Speed, race, rush imply deliberate, controlled velocity, usually with an engine or a competitive purpose, and carry no menace; a racing car speeds, a runaway boulder hurtles.
  • Dash and charge imply a directed, purposeful burst of motion, whereas hurtling is direction given by mass and gravity, not by intention.
  • Whiz, tear, fly name speed and noise but lose the weight and the threat of impact that are essential to hurtle.

4. Why the word fits the passage.

Only hurtle unites unstoppable speed, physical mass, and imminent catastrophe. The Great Goblin does not fall, plunge, or drop — he hurtles, because his fall carries the full weight of a body launched into darkness, with the crash as its only possible ending. The word's phonetics — the hard h-, the burred -r-, the clipped -tle — enact the percussion of descent, and its frequentative history (a repeated violent motion) makes the single fall feel like a whole series of collisions compressed into one. It is the thunderbolt word for the thunderbolt simile: speed, weight, noise, and doom in a single syllable-group.
