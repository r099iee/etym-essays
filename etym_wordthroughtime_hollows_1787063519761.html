- hollows (plural noun) — Old English holh: a cave, den, or hollow place in the earth; the early sense is subterranean and sheltering.
- Middle English: the word becomes general — any scooped-out place, a pit, a cavity in a tree, a depression in the ground; "hollow" also serves as an adjective meaning empty inside, and that sense begins to colour the noun.
- 16th–18th century: the topographic sense is fixed — a "hollow" is a low-lying basin, dell, or small valley between hills; place-names proliferate (countless English "Hollows", and later "Sleepy Hollow" in America).
- 19th century: the anatomical and physical sense — the "hollows" of the body (hollow of the throat, of the hand, of the eye sockets); the word enters natural science for cavities, excavations, and concavities.
- 20th century to present: still dual — everyday topography (hollows in the land) and interior cavities; also idiomatic, as in "the hollows of the night" and "hollows of the waves". The adjectival sense "empty, insincere" (a hollow promise) and the verb sense "to make hollow" remain active, enriching the plural noun's resonance.
