PIE (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE)
*en + *swā + *meǵ-
‘in’ + ‘so, thus’ + ‘great, large’
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*in + *swō + *mikilaz
‘in’ + ‘in that manner’ + ‘great, much’
↓
Old English (c. 8th–10th c.)
in + swā + mycel
‘in’ + ‘so, thus’ + ‘much, great’
↓
Anglo-Norman / Old French influence (c. 13th c.)
en tant (que)
‘to such an extent (that)’ (semantic model)
↓
Middle English (c. late 14th c.)
in so moche
‘to such a degree, so greatly that’
↓
Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)
insomuch
‘to such an extent that; inasmuch as’
↓
Modern English (current)
insomuch
‘to such an extent; inasmuch’

Note: Insomuch represents a fused univerbation of the Middle English prepositional phrase in so moche, created as a syntactic loan-translation (calque) of Anglo-Norman en tant que and Late Latin in tantum. Its three constituent elements are native Germanic reflexes of Proto-Indo-European roots: the locative preposition *en (‘in’), the pronominal demonstrative stem *swā- (‘so, thus’), and the root of magnitude *meǵ- (‘great, large’), which developed via Proto-Germanic *mikilaz into Old English mycel. In Middle English, the northern dialects retained the hard velar in mickle, while southern and Midland dialects palatalized the consonant group into muche / moche.
