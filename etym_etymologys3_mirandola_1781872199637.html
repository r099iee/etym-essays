Mirandola enters English as a toponym that has become a byword for a person. The name begins as a place: the town of Mirandola in the Po plain of Emilia-Romagna, near Modena, seat of the Pico counts. Its etymology is traditionally traced through Medieval Latin forms such as Mirandula, explained from Latin miranda—neuter plural of the gerundive mirandus, "to be wondered at, admirable"—or more broadly from the verb mirari, "to wonder at, admire, gaze with astonishment." Local historical tradition links the name to attentive watching: a strategic stronghold that looks, or is to be looked at, with regard, fitting a fortified town of military and ecclesiastical interest. The diminutive or place-forming shape Mirandula → Italian Mirandola is ordinary in northern Italian toponymy, where many settlements carry such derived names. Behind mirari stands Proto-Indo-European *smei-, "to smile, laugh," a root whose semantic path runs from the facial gesture of pleasure to the attitude of delighted or astonished regard. Sister reflexes include Sanskrit smayate, "smiles," English smile and smirk on the Germanic side, and the Latin cluster mirus, "wonderful," miraculum, "miracle," admirari, "to admire," and the Romance looking-verbs such as Spanish mirar and Italian mirare. Wonder, admiration, and attentive looking are thus historically one family: to smile at the world and to stare at it in marvel share a deep root. English miracle and admire are learned cousins of the same Latin branch that named the town. The proposed derivation of mirari from *smei- is traditional, yet specialists differ in confidence, and the town's name is open to substrate or folk-etymological explanations; the honest account is that the deep root is probable but not certain, while the Latin gerundive chain is secure. The Pico family ruled Mirandola as counts and later as dukes of Mirandola and Concordia. Giovanni Pico (1463–1494), a younger son of that house, is known in English as Pico della Mirandola or Pico of Mirandola. His fame—polyglot learning, Kabbalistic and Platonic syncretism, the Oration on the Dignity of Man, the audacious offer to defend nine hundred theses in Rome, and early death at thirty-one—made the place-name a portable emblem of Renaissance humanism. The Oration's doctrine that man is a creature of free self-formation, "neither of heaven nor of earth," crowned him in later centuries as the laureate of human dignity, and his name was attached to the very idea of the free, self-making individual. Walter Pater, who devoted a celebrated essay to him in The Renaissance (1873), could therefore list "Pico of Mirandola" beside La Gioconda and the Carrara hills as a source of unique pleasurable impression: the man, like the painting and the landscape, has a virtue that strikes the susceptible observer. In Pater's aesthetic historiography Pico represents the spirit of the Renaissance itself—the many-sided, curious, self-fashioning mind—so that the toponym becomes shorthand for an entire cultural episode. The evolution is complete when a PIE smile-root, filtered through Latin wonder-vocabulary and an Emilian fortress name, ends as a one-word allusion in Victorian aesthetic prose. Sound and form. The phonology is modest and mostly Latin-to-Italian: Latin mirandus (with short i in the gerundive stem) passes through the neuter plural miranda into the Romance toponym, where the Italian vocalism of -anda/-ola and the typical feminine place ending -a complete the modern form. No dramatic sound shift is needed, because the word is a proper name fossilized early; the interesting change is lexical rather than phonological—the gerundive "to be wondered at" hardens into a fixed town-name and then into a title. The semantic drift is thus: "admirable things" → "the town of admirable things" → "the lordship of that town" → "the philosopher from that lordship" → "a byword for Renaissance genius." Each stage compresses the last, until a single capitalized word carries the whole episode with it—place, dynasty, philosopher, manifesto, and cultural legend—so that Mirandola in English is less a geography than a signature of the humanist ideal of human freedom.
