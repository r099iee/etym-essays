suppositions — the plural of supposition: beliefs, theories, or assumptions held without conclusive proof, entertained as the basis of reasoning or behaviour. A supposition is an idea "set down" provisionally — accepted for the sake of argument, explanation, or conjecture, but not yet established. It occupies a middle zone between a fact (verified) and a fantasy (arbitrary): a supposition is grounded in some evidence or plausibility, yet remains unproven. In Austen's phrase "ingenious suppositions", the word carries both admiration (the cleverness of the guesses) and gentle irony (their lack of foundation).

Core senses

  • Assumption without proof: a proposition taken for granted in reasoning or action — "the conclusion was based on supposition".
  • Hypothesis / proposed explanation: a theory offered to account for facts, awaiting confirmation — "they made several suppositions about the cause".
  • Plural as collective conjecture: suppositions often denotes the accumulated guesswork of a group — the Bennets' "ingenious suppositions" about Mr. Bingley.

Polysemy and secondary meanings

  • Philosophical / logical sense: in logic and rhetoric, a supposition is a premise laid down for argument — "granting the supposition that X, it follows that Y". Medieval Scholasticism developed a full "theory of supposition" (suppositio), analysing how terms in a proposition stand for things in the world.
  • Scholastic technical term: suppositio in Ockham and Buridan concerns reference — which individual a general term "supposes" (stands for). This sophisticated technical sense is the direct ancestor of the modern word.
  • Legal / statistical hypothesis: in law and science, a supposition is a working assumption to be tested or rebutted.
  • Contrast with related words: an assumption is taken for granted and feels more settled; a hypothesis is formal and testable; a speculation is more freely exploratory; a presumption leans on probability or precedent. Supposition is the broad, neutral term: something placed under an argument as its foundation.
  • Register: formal and bookish; everyday speech prefers "assumption", "theory", or "guess". The word retains a scholarly, cautious flavour, and in modern usage often implies a mildly sceptical judgement on the guesser.

Historical semantic bridge

Latin supponere is literally "to place under" (sub- "under" + ponere "to place"). The noun suppositio meant "a placing under, a substitution" and was used as the Latin calque of the Greek hypothesis ("a placing under": hypo- "under" + thesis "placing"). The bridge: an idea "placed under" a problem serves as the foundation on which reasoning is built — exactly as a physical support placed under a structure holds it up. What begins as a builder's image becomes the logician's premise.
