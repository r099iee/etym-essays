Drinking is the present participle of one of the oldest verbs in English, and its history reaches back to the common Germanic stock. The Proto-Germanic verb is reconstructed as *drinkaną, and it appears in every branch of the family: Old Saxon drinkan, Old Frisian drinka, Old High German trinkan, Old Norse drekka, Gothic drigkan and Old English drincan. The verb was a strong verb of the third class, with the ablaut series drincan, dranc, druncen, and that pattern survives in the archaic forms drank and drunk.

The uncertain root. Beyond Germanic the picture is murky. A comparison is often made with a PIE root *dʰrenǵ-, "to draw or pull", on the idea of drawing liquid into the mouth, and possible Indo-Iranian cognates are cited in support. The connection is attractive but not secure, and some handbooks frankly record the verb as having no agreed extra-Germanic etymology. What is certain is the Germanic word and its unbroken descent into English.

The Anglo-Saxon drinking culture. In Old English poetry drinking is a defining activity of the hall. Beowulf is full of mead and ale, of cups carried round, of the queen pouring for the warriors, and the shared cup passed from hand to hand is the very image of loyalty. To drink together was to be bound in fellowship; to refuse the cup was to risk exclusion. The word at this stage carries no shame whatever. It is the vocabulary of hospitality, celebration and heroic community.

  • Strong verb: the ablaut forms drincan, dranc, druncen show the ancient Germanic pattern of vowel alternation.
  • Ritual centrality: the shared cup and the ceremonial drinking of the hall bound lord and retainer together, and the poetry returns to it constantly as an image of loyalty and joy.
  • Neutral moral tone: drinking is a normal, expected and public act; the modern anxiety about alcohol has not yet appeared.

The narrowing to alcohol. In the Middle English period the verb remains broad, but the absolute construction, "he drinks", gradually acquires its modern implication of habitual alcohol consumption. The mechanism is a familiar euphemistic narrowing: because alcohol is the socially marked thing one drinks, the unmarked verb can be used to gesture at it without naming it. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the implication is fully established, and it produces the modern cluster of euphemisms: he has been drinking, she likes a drink, the drink.

The temperance era. The nineteenth century transformed the word's moral colour. The gin craze of the eighteenth century had already made cheap spirits a public scandal, and the temperance movement of the nineteenth gave the language a whole counter-vocabulary: teetotal, the pledge, the demon drink, intemperance. From this period onward drinking carries a double charge. It can be warm and convivial, as in "a drinking song" or "drinking with friends", or dark and medical, as in "a drinking problem". The word's emotional temperature depends entirely on which of these frames the speaker invokes.

  • Prohibition (1920 to 1933): American law criminalised the sale of alcohol, giving English bootlegger, speakeasy and a lasting association between drinking and defiance.
  • Public health: the twentieth century produced units, guidelines, drinking age, binge drinking and drink-driving, turning the word into an instrument of regulation.
  • Medicine: alcoholism and alcohol use disorder reframed heavy drinking as disease rather than sin, and drinking problem became the polite bridge between the two models.

The figurative branch. Side by side with the alcohol story runs a gentler metaphor: to drink in, meaning to absorb eagerly through the senses or the mind. The image is ancient, biblical and classical, and it treats the mind as a thirsty body. One drinks in a view, a book, a piece of music, a moment. In the Wodehouse passage on this card the phrase is used of a woman watching a duck: she stands drinking it in, wholly absorbed, and the idiom gives the scene its quiet intensity. This branch has no connection with alcohol, and it shows how the same bodily verb can be turned toward contemplation.

Sound and form. The Old English drincan loses its infinitive ending in Middle English, giving drinken and then drink. The present participle ending -ende was replaced by -ing in Middle English, producing drinking, which serves both as participle and as gerund. The initial dr- cluster and the short i have been stable for a thousand years, and the word's phonetic shape is one of the most conservative in the language.

Literary presence. Drinking runs through English literature from the mead-hall of Beowulf to the tavern scenes of Shakespeare, the punch bowls of the eighteenth century, the gin palaces of Dickens, the temperance tracts of the Victorians and the cocktail parties of Wodehouse and Fitzgerald. It is one of the small words through which English literature has recorded almost every form of human sociability, and almost every form of human self-destruction.
