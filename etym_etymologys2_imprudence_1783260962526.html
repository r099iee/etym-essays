Derivation chain for IMPRUDENCE (single root word): 1. Proto-Indo-European *weyd- "to see; to know" (Pokorny IEW 1125; also *weid-/*wid-; the perfect stem *woyd- "I have seen" semantically = "I know" — the SEEING = KNOWING metaphor), c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE. 2. Latin vidēre "to see" (from PIE *weyd-), attested 3rd c. BCE. 3. Latin providēre "to see ahead, foresee, provide" (pro- "before/forward" + vidēre), attested 2nd c. BCE. 4. Latin prōvidēns, -entis "foreseeing, prudent" (present participle of providēre), attested 1st c. BCE. 5. Latin prūdēns "wise, foreseeing, prudent" — contracted from *prōvidēns with the loss of the middle syllable (-vi-), attested 1st c. BCE. 6. Latin imprūdēns "without foresight, heedless, rash" (in- "not" + prūdēns), attested 1st c. BCE. 7. Latin imprūdentia "want of foresight, indiscretion" (abstract noun in -ia from imprūdēns), attested 1st c. BCE. 8. Middle French imprudence "imprudence, want of foresight" (12th c.), from Latin imprūdentia. 9. Middle English / Modern English IMPRUDENCE "lack of caution, wisdom, or foresight; rashness" (attested from the early 15th century, c. 1400–1425). Note: The word is a learned Latin-French loan; the contraction prōvidēns > prūdēns (loss of -vi-) also produced English prudent and prudence in the 14th century, and the family includes providence, provident, and providential — all built on the same root *weyd- "to see."
