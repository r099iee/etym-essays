Fresco is an Italian freshness word turned into a world art term, and its history must follow that double life: a Germanic adjective for the cool and new, then a Tuscan workshop noun for painting on the wet.

The story opens with Proto-Germanic friskaz, fresh, lively, new, vigorous. It survives as Old English fersc (fresh, unsalted, new), Old High German frisc, Old Norse ferskr, Dutch vers, German frisch, modern English fresh. Its deeper Indo-European root is not agreed; etymologists note possible links to per- or preis- but mark them as conjectural, so responsible dictionaries stop at Germanic. What matters is the sense: what is frisk is cool to the touch, newly made, full of sap and life, whether water, meat, wind, or youth. Lombards, Franks, and Goths carry the word into late-antique Italy, where Medieval Latin scribes latinise it as friscus, frescus, frisca aqua (fresh water) to name what classical recens, novus, frigidus divided among three words.

  • Italian naturalisation (12th-14th century): Tuscan and Lombard speech reshape friscus to fresco (masculine), fresca (feminine), freschi, fresche (plurals), meaning fresh, cool, new, crisp. Dante writes l'aura fresca (the cool breeze), pan fresco, sangue fresco. Phonologically Germanic initial fr- survives intact, short i raises and lowers to e, final -us to -o by regular Romance vocalisation. The adjective is everyday, sensory, and approving.
  • Workshop specialisation (14th century): masons and painters need a term for the narrow window when lime plaster is still damp and receptive. They say dipingere a fresco, to paint on the fresh, opposed to a secco, on the dry. Cennino Cennini's Libro dell'Arte (c. 1400) gives the first full recipe: lay intonaco for only what you can finish in a day (giornata), grind earth pigments in water, paint boldly, no retouching except with secco. The phrase nominalises: un fresco, gli affreschi (a fresco, the frescoes). Giotto, the Lorenzetti, and Andrea di Bonaiuto in the Spanish Chapel prove the system can cover whole chapter-houses with theology that outlives six centuries.
  • Monumental triumph (15th-16th century): Masaccio, Piero della Francesca, Ghirlandaio, Perugino, Raphael's Stanze, and Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling (1508-1512, nearly 500 square metres of buon fresco) make fresco the public papal medium. Vasari's Lives theorises it as the most manly, difficult, and moral painting, because it forbids repentance. French travellers borrow fresque; English Grand Tourists in 1598 borrow fresco directly, first spelling it fresco, fresko, freesco.
  • English borrowing and broadening (1598-present): John Florio's Italian dictionary glosses fresco as fresh, cool. Evelyn, Vasari in translation, and 18th-century virtuosi use fresco for any Italian wall-painting. Victorian Ruskin moralises the chemistry: fresco equals truth to material, humility before lime, collective guild labour, while oil equals secrecy and selfishness. Modern conservation adds pathology: salts, damp, and air pollution eat carbonated lime, so restorers speak of strappo, desalination, and climate control.
  • Parallel cool-life (al fresco): alongside the painting, the adjective lives on in al fresco (in the fresh, outdoors), stare al fresco (stay cool, also slang for in prison), mettere in fresco (put in a cool place). English menus and travel brochures keep these to sell shade, wine, and evenings outside.

Together the evolution binds tongue, nose, and hand: what tastes fresh and feels cool becomes, in a mason's hands, the damp wall that drinks colour and keeps it for half a millennium, which is why Ruskin could venerate an Emperor's head in a Dominican chapter-house as both theology and weather.
