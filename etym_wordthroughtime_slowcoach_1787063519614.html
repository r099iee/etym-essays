- Late 18th Century (c. 1790s–1810s): [Literal Stagecoach Distinction] - Coined in Regency England as a literal vehicular term (*slow coach*) for heavy, low-fare stage wagons and plodding passenger carriages that traveled safely but slowly, in contrast to high-speed mail coaches.
- 1820s to 1840s: [Metaphorical Human Personification] - Adopted into British colloquial slang and Regency sporting circles (e.g., Charles Dickens's early novels) to describe a person who moves, dresses, or speaks with exasperating slowness.
- Mid-to-Late 19th Century: [Victorian Household Idiom] - Solidified as a ubiquitous household idiom throughout Victorian Britain and the British Empire, widely used by parents and teachers to gently chide dawdling children.
- 20th Century to Present: [Endearing British Colloquialism & Literature] - Retained as a classic, mildly humorous British/Commonwealth colloquialism, famously preserved in children's literature, period dramas, and everyday conversational banter.
