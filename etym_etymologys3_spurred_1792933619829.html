To be spurred is to feel one of humanity's oldest technologies of speed. The spur itself — a prick of bone, bronze, or steel strapped to the rider's heel — appears in the archaeological record with mounted warfare, and the Indo-European root behind it is the plain bodily act of kicking: reconstructed PIE *(s)per-, to kick, stomp, or struggle with the feet, the same stamp that survives in words for footprints and struggles across the daughter languages. Germanic narrowed the kick to its most purposeful form: Proto-Germanic *spurōn- is both the instrument and the mark it leaves — the spur and the track — because a ridden horse announces its urging in the dust behind it.

Old English spora already carried this double life. It was stable equipment, taxed and tallied, and it was also social rank: the laws and wills of Anglo-Saxon England treat fine spurs as treasure, and the burial of a thegn with his spurred heels aimed at the afterlife tells us the object outranked mere tools. When the Normans arrived with their own esperons — from the same Germanic stock via Frankish — the word was waiting for them, and Middle English spure absorbed the full glitter of knighthood. To win one's spurs became the defining promotion of medieval masculinity; to have them hacked off by a pursuivant was degradation, dishonour made audible in the chapel. Hacking off a knight's gilt spurs unmade him as surely as dubbing had made him.

The leap from heel to heart was inevitable, and English made it early:

  • The moral spur (1500s–1600s): preachers spurred dull consciences, poets spurred lagging muses, friends spurred each other to valour. The metaphor worked because every listener had seen a horse answer the rowel — the congregation knew exactly how sharp grace could feel.
  • Spur-pricks everywhere (1700s): naturalists found spurs on roosters, petals, and mountain ridges — anything hooked and prodding borrowed the rider's noun — while on the spur of the moment (first recorded 1786) froze the spur's instantaneity into an idiom for decisions as fast as a startled horse.
  • The economic spur (1800s): industrial England spurred mills, markets, and manias. Political economists discovered that demand pricks supply like a heel pricks a flank, and the verb colonised the financial pages it has never left.
  • Spurred on (modern): the phrasal extension grammaticalised persistence — one spur starts, many spurs sustain — giving coaches, causes, and film franchises their favourite verb of continuous motivation.

Phonetically the word is a miniature of its meaning: the initial sp- spits like a spark, the vowel is short and flat, and the doubled -rr- plus stopped -d lands the past tense like a hoofbeat — spurred is over almost before it begins, exactly like the burst of speed it causes. Tolkien, a rider's son in imagination if not in fact, deploys it at the precise hinge of terror: the Black Rider's horse is already great, already aimed right at its victim, and the single verb spurred releases the whole coiled menace. No other word — not galloped, not charged, not drove — carries steel inside it. The spur is still there, two thousand years after the first kicking root, doing what it has always done: turning mass into missile.
