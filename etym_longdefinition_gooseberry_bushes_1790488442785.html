gooseberry-bushes is the plural of gooseberry-bush: a low, thorny, deciduous shrub of the currant family (Ribes uva-crispa, syn. R. grossularia) bearing round, green-to-purple, hairy, sharply acid berries, and by extension the fruit itself. In the source sentence — had a garden full of gooseberry-bushes, sloping down to the Tay — Ruskin recalls his Perth aunt's kitchen garden crowded with these clipped, spiny bushes running down to the river. A garden full signals abundance and domestic economy: gooseberries for pies, jams, fools, and children's picking, thorns that scratch, branches that need pruning, berries that children eat green and sour. The plural matters: not one ornamental specimen but rows of productive bushes, a working Scottish garden.

Botanically the gooseberry-bush is straggling, 1–1.5 m high, with triple spines under each bud, lobed leaves, inconspicuous green-pink bell flowers in spring, and fruits 1–3 cm, veined, hairy or smooth, ripening June–August in Britain. Cultivars number in the hundreds — old rough red, hairy amber, whitesmith, invicta, hinnomaki — selected since Tudor times for size, sweetness, and mildew resistance. Wild plants haunt hedgerows, copses, and old ruins across Europe and western Asia; gardens perfected them, especially in the damp north: Lancashire gooseberry shows, Scottish manse gardens, and Norwegian coastal plots to 63 degrees north all testify that cool, moist summers suit them, while hot dry plains do not. Picking demands gloves; pruning demands resolve; cooking demands sugar.

  • Core horticultural sense (head sense): the shrub and its fruit: e.g. to plant gooseberry-bushes, to pick gooseberries, gooseberry jam, gooseberry pie, gooseberry fool (stewed fruit with cream). The bush is the plant, the berry the product, but everyday speech blurs them: gooseberries are ripe means bushes bear. Ruskin's sense is exactly this — productive, thorny, child-high bushes in a sloping town garden.
  • Culinary sense: tart fruit for cooking and preserving; e.g. gooseberry sauce for mackerel, gooseberry fool, gooseberry wine, green gooseberry crumble. The acid cuts fat (pork, goose, oily fish), the pectin sets jam, the juice ferments. Elderflower is the classic partner. Unripe berries are proverbially sour — sour as a green gooseberry — ripe dessert kinds eat fresh.
  • Idiomatic third-wheel sense: to play gooseberry / play gooseberry-bush: to be the unwanted third person with two lovers, a chaperone; e.g. I don't want to play gooseberry on your date, they left me doing gooseberry. Attested from the early 19th century. Theories link it to chaperones picking gooseberries while lovers court, or to the fool (dupe) sense, or to Old Gooseberry as devil; the mechanism is clear even if the origin is debated: the odd one out is the gooseberry among sweeter fruit. The plural bushes rarely appears in the idiom, but the image of hiding among bushes fits.
  • Slang and dialect senses: gooseberry as gooseberry fool and fool person (a dimwit), Old Gooseberry as the devil (1790s slang), gooseberry eyes (goggling, prominent eyes like berries), gooseberry bush as pubic hair or hiding place in coarse jest, gooseberry season in journalism for silly-season stories. Each shows the berry's homely strangeness inviting metaphor: hairy, green, thorny, sour.
  • Bushes as thicket sense: bushes alone means woody shrubs, clumps, and cover; e.g. to hide in the bushes, to beat about the bush (originally to beat beaters around bushes to flush game). In gooseberry-bushes the generic bush is specified by fruit, turning wild thicket into garden row. The hyphen marks compound status: fruit-bush as single concept, pluralised as bushes.
  • Figurative abundance sense (source nuance): a garden full of gooseberry-bushes evokes Victorian self-sufficiency, auntly thrift (cold mutton on Sundays, garden fruit for puddings), and child's adventure — thorns, berries, river below. To know the phrase is to smell green fruit, feel scratches, and see the Tay beyond.

Together gooseberry-bushes thus compresses botany, cookery, idiom, and memory. The child sees infinite Tay water beyond finite thorny rows; the adult tastes fool and jam; the reader hears third-wheel embarrassment. All live in one hyphenated plural.
