1. The gemstone sense. A ruby is a precious gemstone: a transparent to translucent variety of the mineral corundum, crystalline aluminium oxide (Al2O3), whose deep red colour comes from trace chromium ions substituting in the crystal lattice. With a hardness of 9 on Mohs' scale it is the hardest gemstone after diamond, and the finest stones — the legendary "pigeon-blood" rubies of the Mogok tract in Burma — have long ranked among the most valuable gems on earth, occasionally fetching more per carat than diamonds of equal size. Other classic sources include Sri Lanka, Thailand, Vietnam, Tanzania, Madagascar and Mozambique. Rubies may show asterism, the six-rayed "star" of a star ruby, caused by fine included needles of rutile, and stones are routinely heat-treated to deepen or even their colour; since 1902 synthetic rubies have been manufactured by the Verneuil flame-fusion process.

2. The colour sense. By extension, ruby is a deep, rich red with a faint inclination toward crimson — the red of the gem itself: ruby-red lips, ruby-red wine, ruby port, ruby glass. In fashion, cosmetics and design, ruby red is a darker, more luxurious red than scarlet, and the word transfers the gem's associations of rarity and cost to plain colour, so that a "ruby" lipstick is priced and marketed as precious, not merely red.

  • Jewellery sense: rubies are cut as brilliants, cabochons and stars; historically the term also covered other red stones — the "balas ruby" (fine red spinel, as in the Black Prince's Ruby in the British Crown) and garnets sold as "Cape rubies" — trade names that modern mineralogy sorted out, but which still appear in old inventories and crown jewels.
  • Technical senses: the ruby laser, built in 1960 by Theodore Maiman, uses a synthetic ruby rod pumped by a flash lamp; ruby is used for watch and instrument bearings ("jewels") for its hardness and low friction; ruby masers preceded lasers in microwave amplification; and synthetic ruby is a standard material of precision optics.
  • Festival and anniversary senses: the ruby wedding anniversary (40th), the ruby jubilee (40 years of a reign), and the ruby as the traditional birthstone of July, symbolising passion, protection and permanence.
  • Popular-culture senses: Dorothy's ruby slippers in the 1939 film The Wizard of Oz (the famous shoes are actually sequinned and dyed, but "ruby slippers" became a household phrase of magical homecoming), the Rolling Stones' "Ruby Tuesday", the given name Ruby, and the idiom "a ruby among ..." for the finest example of its kind.
  • Technology sense: the Ruby programming language, released in 1995 by Yukihiro Matsumoto, is named partly as a playful successor to the language Perl — the name puns on the birthstone and on the idea of a gem, and the RubyGems package manager makes the joke permanent; Ruby on Rails then carried the name around the world.
  • Typography sense: ruby characters (ルビ, rubi) are the small furigana glosses printed beside or above kanji in Japanese text; the name comes from an English typefounders' term for a 5.5-point type size, "ruby", itself borrowed from the gem by the same logic that named types "pearl" and "diamond".

3. The semantic bridge. The word has never left its core: redness plus preciousness. From the stone, the colour sense grew, the gem's most salient property becoming a colour name; from the colour grew the cosmetic, festive and cultural senses, the colour of passion and celebration, and the ruby wedding honours forty years precisely because the gem symbolises endurance and value; and from the gem's rarity grew the modern technical and linguistic extensions, which keep the old associations alive in lasers, software, typography and stage magic.
