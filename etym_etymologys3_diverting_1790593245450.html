Diverting traces a graceful arc from road-junction to drawing-room, from the physical act of turning aside to the social art of entertaining. Its root is one of Indo-European most fertile turning roots, *wert-, to turn, rotate, wind, which gives English worth and worthy via turning into being, German werden to become, Latin vertere to turn, verse, version, vertigo, advertise, and a host of turning words. To turn is to change direction, and change of direction is change of fate.

Latin vertere is the workhorse: to turn the plough, to turn the page, to turn the enemy. Prefixed with dis-, apart in different directions, it becomes divertere, devertere, to turn apart, to turn aside, to turn into lodgings off the main road. Romans spoke of diverting a river, diverting a journey, diverting suspicion. The noun diverticulum was a side road, a detour, a wayside inn where travellers turned off. Already the seed of entertainment lies in the wayside inn: the place you turn aside to rest and refresh.

Old French inherits this as divertir, and in feudal and court society the sense of turning aside acquires psychological depth:

  • Military diversion: to divert the enemy by feint, to create a diversion, turning their force aside. This remains the grave, strategic sense, preserved in modern diversionary attack.
  • Medical and moral diversion: to divert humours, to divert melancholy, turning bad fluids or thoughts aside toward healthier channels, a common medieval prescription to divert grief by occupation.
  • Courtly entertainment: by the 14th-15th centuries, to divert a prince means to turn his mind agreeably from cares of state by music, hunting, jests, and masques. Diversion becomes pastime, divertissement becomes a formal light ballet or musical interlude. Pleasure is conceptualised as a legitimate detour.

English borrows diverten in the 15th century, first in the literal and military senses: to divert rivers, to divert armies. The entertaining sense follows in the 17th century under French cultural prestige after the Restoration, when Charles II court imports French notions of polite diversion. Pepys speaks of diverting company, Dryden of diverting stories. By the 18th century, diverting as present-participle adjective is established as a term of polite aesthetic judgment: a diverting prospect, a diverting companion, a diverting novel. It belongs to the vocabulary of sensibility, where feeling must be expressed with moderation.

The 19th-20th centuries democratise and ironise the word. What once diverted princes now diverts railway travellers, seaside crowds, and cinema audiences. Guidebooks promise diverting excursions; reviewers damn with faint praise as diverting. Wodehouse, master of faint praise, uses it to mark the detached, amused superiority of the English gentleman observer: aquaplaning is diverting to those who like splashing, silly to those who watch. The -ing form preserves ongoing aspect: diverting is actively turning you now, not diverted and done.

Phonetically, diverting keeps its Latin stress on vert, /daɪˈvɜːtɪŋ/, with the French-derived di- weakened. Orthography stabilises after vacillation between divert and devart. Semantically, it never loses its turning image: to be diverted is still to be turned, and diverting things are still side-roads from duty. From PIE turning to Roman detour to French court pastime to English faint praise, diverting shows how a concrete spatial metaphor can become a whole philosophy of leisure, where pleasure is not the main road but the agreeable turning aside.
