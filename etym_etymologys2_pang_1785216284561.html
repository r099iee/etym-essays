West Germanic (Low German / Dutch) *prang- "to press, pinch, squeeze" (ultimate origin unknown; no certain PIE antecedent) → Middle Low German prange (n.) "narrowness, distress; an instrument of torture (the press)" (c. 14th c.); beside the verb prangen "to press, pinch" → Middle English prange "a sharp, pinching, throbbing pain" (mid-15th c.) → (loss of -r- before the consonant) Middle English pange "a sudden sharp pain" (recorded 1520s) → Modern English pang (n.) "a sudden sharp pain or spasm, physical or emotional" — pangs of conscience (1530s), pangs of childbirth/labour (1580s), pangs of hunger, pangs of love, regret, longing Note: the ultimate origin is unknown; the word is a Germanic family with no certain PIE antecedent, and the -r- was lost in the early sixteenth century (prange → pange → pang). The relative prong, "a pointed, projecting spike" (15th c.), is probably the same Dutch or Low German word for a pinching instrument, repurposed.
