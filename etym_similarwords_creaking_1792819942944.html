• Squeaking: Similar because both are thin high frictional sounds of stiff things; differs because a squeak is shorter, shriller, and often wetter (mice, pumps, new shoes), while a creak is drier, longer, and grating.
  • Groaning: Similar because old timbers and old men both groan and creak under strain; differs because a groan is low, deep, and sustained, while a creak is higher, thinner, and intermittent.
  • Grating: Similar because both describe harsh frictional scraping; differs because grating stresses the abrasive rubbing itself (a grating voice rasps constantly), while creaking stresses the drawn-out wooden note with tiny catches in it.
  • Croaking: Similar because the two are ancient expressive cousins, ravens croak where gates creak; differs because croaking belongs to throats (frogs, ravens, the dying), while creaking belongs prototypically to wood, hinges, and joints.
  • Rasping: Similar because both are dry harsh sounds of rubbing; differs because rasping is a rough file-like scraping (breath, sandpaper), while creaking has pitch and rhythm, almost a squeaky melody of strain.
  • Grinding: Similar because worn joints and gears both grind and creak; differs because grinding implies heavy crushing friction and damage, while creaking can be harmless stiffness that oil will cure.
  • Crackling: Similar because both are dry intermittent sounds of stiffness (fire, joints); differs because crackling is a shower of tiny sharp pops, while creaking is a sustained twisting note.
  • Moaning: Similar because wind in rigging both moans and makes timbers creak; differs because a moan is a smooth low wail, while a creak is broken and frictional, full of little catches.
  • Seized-up: Similar because creaking joints and seized engines share the stiffness story; differs because seized-up means movement has stopped entirely, while creaking means it still moves, noisily protesting.
