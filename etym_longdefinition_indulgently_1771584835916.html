Indulgently is the adverb of indulgence, and it describes the manner of a person who is ready to overlook faults, gratify wishes, and grant favour with a yielding, affectionate kindness. In this scene from Chapter 3 of Dream of the Red Chamber, Grandmother Jia "chided indulgently" on seeing Bao-yu return without having changed his clothes — and the adverb transforms the scold into a caress. Her chiding is a performance of affection, not a true reproach: by scolding indulgently she engages herself in Bao-yu's whims, choosing to yield to his unruliness rather than enforce conformity. The tone is warm, doting, amused, and matriarchal — the soft power of a woman who rules the household through love, letting the wild boy's energy flourish precisely because he is safe under her indulgence.

The core meaning of the adverb can be unfolded into several linked strands:
  • Overlooking faults: indulgently describes doing so with ready kindness, without exacting punishment or correction — the manner of one who notices the fault and waves it away smiling.
  • Gratifying wishes: it describes granting another person's desires freely and gladly, pampering and spoiling with affection rather than reluctant permission.
  • Yielding with pleasure: the adverb implies that the yielding is itself enjoyed — indulgence is not merely tolerance but active pleasure in giving the beloved what they want.
  • Kind, lenient manner: as a pure manner-adverb it colours any verb — to smile, nod, look, chide, or shake one's head indulgently — painting the face of a fond spectator.

Secondary and extended senses across the family. Indulgently inherits the full polysemy of its noun and verb parents. Indulge means to gratify a desire ("indulge a whim"), to pamper or spoil another ("indulge the children"), and, reflexively, to give oneself over to pleasure ("indulge oneself," "self-indulgence," both from the eighteenth century). The adverb extends to self-regarding leniency: one may "live indulgently," "drink indulgently," or "feed one's imagination indulgently," describing gratification of one's own appetites with a clear conscience. In ecclesiastical usage the family carries a wholly separate freight: an indulgence (from Church Latin indulgentia) is the remission of temporal punishment for sin, the famous commodity of the 1517 controversy that drove Luther to his Ninety-five Theses. This religious sense is a sister branch, not a sense of the adverb, but it shapes the word's gravity and explains why indulgence can mean both a grandmother's pampering and a papal pardon. The historical bridge is Latin indulgēre, "to be kind, complaisant; to concede, grant, yield": the same yielding that pardons a sinner also grants a child his wish, and the modern adverb preserves the root's double motion — a kindness that concedes.

Register and collocation. Indulgently is a literary, reflective adverb, common in narrative prose of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and rare in the plainest speech, where kindly or leniently would serve. It appears in a small stable set of frames — to smile indulgently, to nod indulgently, to look indulgently, to chide indulgently, to shake one's head indulgently, to pat indulgently — all describing the gesture of a fond, amused superior. Its register is warm but faintly patronising in the older sense of the word: the speaker stands above, granting what might be withheld. In the novel it is the perfect instrument of Grandmother Jia's soft authority: her scolding is suffused with delight in the boy, and the adverb tells us that even her rebukes are gifts — the gesture of a woman whose whole management of the household runs through affection rather than fear.
