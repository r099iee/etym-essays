The evolution of subvert is a textbook case of a concrete physical metaphor becoming an abstract political, moral, and aesthetic concept while retaining its spatial logic of attack from below.

Its ultimate root lies in Proto-Indo-European *wert- meaning to turn, wind, rotate, a hugely productive root that gives English verse, version, convert, invert, revert, vortex, worm and many others. The notion of turning is central: to change direction, to turn something into something else, to turn over.

  • PIE *wert- “to turn, bend, wind”: the deep image of rotation and turning, preserved across Indo-European languages in words for turning, becoming, and twisting.
  • Latin vertere, versus “to turn, turn around, overturn, overthrow”: a core verb of motion and change. Compounds proliferate: avertere to turn away, convertere to turn together, invertere to turn upside down, pervertere to turn thoroughly awry, subvertere to turn from below, to overturn, demolish.
  • Latin subvertere, subversus: formed from sub- under, from below, beneath + vertere to turn. In Classical Latin (Cicero, Caesar, Livy, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) it meant literally to overturn, capsize, demolish, raze — to subvert walls, ships, chariots — and by extension to overthrow states, ruin fortunes, upset order. Tacitus uses it for ruining reputations and destroying political power.
  • Late Latin and Christian Latin: Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine extend the moral sense: to subvert faith, to subvert souls, to subvert truth. The Vulgate uses subvertere for divine destruction of wicked cities and for heretical perversion of minds. This moralization prepares the English semantic range.
  • Old French subvertir / souvertir: borrowed from Latin, attested 12th–13th c., meaning to overthrow, ruin, pervert. Anglo-Norman legal and religious texts carry it into England after the Conquest as a learned, clerical word.
  • Middle English subverten (14th c.): first attested c. 1374 in Chaucer and contemporary Wycliffite prose as subverten meaning to overturn, overthrow, destroy, especially kingdoms and churches. John Wycliffe writes of those who subvert holy church. The physical sense of turning upside down coexists with political and religious ruin.

Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) massively expands the word under pressure of Reformation polemic and civil war. Protestants accuse Catholics of subverting true religion; royalists accuse Parliamentarians of subverting monarchy and vice versa. Shakespeare uses subversion in Henry VI and Troilus and Cressida for ruin of order. Milton in Paradise Lost (1667) and his pamphlets makes subversion a cosmic principle: Satan seeks to subvert heaven, to subvert mankind. The noun subversive begins to denote persons.

18th-century Enlightenment and revolutionary era politicize the term further. Burke in Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) warns that Jacobin principles will subvert altar and throne across Europe. Conservative discourse makes subversion a crime of ideas, not just deeds — books, pamphlets, clubs can subvert. At the same time radicals embrace the charge proudly.

19th-century industrial capitalism and empire add economic and colonial senses: to subvert discipline in factories, to subvert native loyalty, to subvert markets by fraud. Marx and Engels argue that capitalism itself subverts all fixed relations; the bourgeoisie cannot exist without constantly revolutionizing and subverting production.

20th century gives the word its modern Cold War charge. Subversion becomes a technical term in law, espionage, and counterinsurgency: subversive activities, subversive organizations, House Un-American Activities, Soviet subversion, CIA warnings about communist subversion. Parallel to this, literary modernism revalues subversion positively: Dada, Surrealism, Brecht, Bakhtin explicitly aim to subvert bourgeois art, to subvert the establishment, exactly as in your passage. Puncturing stale balloons and exposing rotten foundations is the classic avant-garde programme.

Phonetically the development is conservative: Latin subvertere with stress on vert- passes through French weakening of final syllables to Middle English subverten /sʌbˈvɛrtən/, then loss of final -en infinitive gives modern subvert /səbˈvɜːt/ with reduced first vowel and voiced /v/ preserved. Spelling retains Latin b though syllabified as sub-vert. American /səbˈvɝːt/ rhymes with avert, convert, invert, pervert, keeping the family audible.

Today subvert lives a double life: in security and moral discourse it remains condemnatory — to subvert democracy, elections, justice — while in cultural criticism it is celebratory — to subvert genres, stereotypes, expectations is to be creative and free. Both rest on the ancient Roman image: turn it from underneath and the whole edifice falls.
