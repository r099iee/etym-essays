agony — Extreme physical or mental suffering, and specifically the kind of suffering that is experienced as a struggle: the throes, the wrestling of a creature against whatever overwhelms it. Unlike neutral pain or passive suffering, agony implies a crisis at the limit of endurance — the body or the mind at the very edge of what it can bear, and often beyond it.

The literary moment. In the sentence "A shadowed agony in the garden," the word is doing two things at once. It summons the oldest agony the English ear knows — the Agony in the Garden, Christ's mortal anguish in Gethsemane before the Passion — and it reworks that liturgical memory into a feature of the landscape. The agony is "shadowed," drained of sound and struggle; it is no longer an event but an atmosphere, a dim, ambient distress absorbed into the garden itself. The biblical frame is what makes the adjective so potent, and the pairing with "the slower dusk" and "a paper skyline" turns the scene into a stage of terminal, silent suffering. This is agony as a condition of the world rather than of a moment.

Primary sense — physical extremity. In its oldest modern use, agony is bodily suffering at its maximum pitch: the throes of death, the pains of childbirth, the effect of torture or disease. The word is traditionally linked to the death-struggle — "the agony of death" was among its earliest English senses — and it retains that finality: an agony is pain that seems to have no exit.

Secondary sense — mental and emotional anguish. By the eighteenth century the word had moved inward. "The agony of suspense," "the agony of waiting," "the agony of doubt" use the old physical crisis as a figure for psychological torment, and the word began to pair with ecstasy as its emotional opposite. The register is elevated and literary, with a persistent liturgical undertow.

Tertiary sense — everyday hyperbole. In the twentieth century the word generalized into ordinary intensification: "it was agony sitting through the lecture," "the wait was agony." The back-formed adjective agonizing ("agonizing pain," "agonizingly close") has softened the old death-struggle into casual intensification, though the noun retains its gravity.

  • The death-struggle sense: the word's earliest recorded English meaning — the throes of dying, still alive in phrases like "the final agony."
  • Christ's Agony in the Garden: the Vulgate's agonia for the Gethsemane anguish fixed the word as the suffering of the soul at its extremity; the liturgical echo survives in art, hymnody, and in this card's sentence.
  • The Greek agon beneath it: at its root the word descends from Greek agōn, "a contest, a gathering for competition" — so agony is etymologically a struggle one must fight; the arena sense survives in protagonist, antagonist, and agonistic.
  • Modern technical uses: in sports psychology and literary criticism, agon and agonistic name competitive or conflict-driven structures; agonize means to struggle mentally with a decision; these re-borrowings keep the classical root productive.
  • Collocations: "in agony," "an agony of suspense," "the agony column" (an old British newspaper term for a personal-ads page), "the agony aunt" (a journalist who answers readers' personal problems) — evidence of how thoroughly the word has been domesticated.

Why the word means so much more than pain. The historical bridge is entirely Greek. The PIE root *ag- meant "to drive, lead, guide"; Greek ágō meant "I lead, I drive"; from it came agōn, the contest or gathering where people were driven together to strive, and then agonía, the struggle for victory — which slid, in the medical and ecclesiastical Greek of the Hellenistic world, from "contest" to "anguish of mind and body." The Latin Church adopted agonia for Christ's Gethsemane anguish, and that is the sense that entered English. Agony, therefore, is a word whose etymology is a struggle and whose literary history is a garden — and both are active in the card.

Register and tone. Agony is a strong, elevated, literary word. It is out of place in clinical prose (medicine prefers acute pain) and in casual complaint (where agony has become hyperbole); its natural home is tragedy, memoir, devotional writing, and fiction at moments of extremity. This makes it exactly right for the card's hushed, liturgical line — a word that still carries the weight of a death-struggle and a garden.
