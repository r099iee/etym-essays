Proto-Indo-European *kwet- ("to shake, strike, beat"; reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE) → Latin quatere ("to shake, strike, agitate"; attested 1st c. BCE; source of the many English -cuss words) → Latin frequentative quassare ("to shake violently, shatter, break in pieces") → Vulgar Latin *quassicare ("to shatter, crack, break into pieces") → Old Spanish cascar ("to crack, break open, shatter"; attested medieval) → Spanish casco ("potsherd, skull, helmet, shell, hull, and by extension cask, barrel"; the semantic hub of the family) → Middle French casque ("helmet," borrowed from the 'helmet' sense; 16th c.) → English casque ("an open helmet"; 17th c., doublet of cask) — and in parallel — Spanish casco → French / English cask ("a barrel for liquids"; first recorded 1560, via French) → Modern English cask (plural casks), with the brewing and distilling compounds cask ale, cask-strength, cask-conditioned. Note on debate and doublets: the route from Latin quatere through Vulgar Latin *quassicare to Old Spanish cascar and the noun casco is the standard and generally accepted account, but the final extension from casco "potsherd / skull / shell" to casco "cask, barrel" — a leap from a broken piece to a whole vessel — is semantically debated; some scholars derive the barrel sense instead from an older Iberian word for a leather or wooden container, and the OED notes the origin of Spanish casco itself as uncertain. English has two doublets from this family: cask ("barrel," from the vessel sense) and casque ("helmet," from the head/shell sense), which are easy to confuse. The Latin compounds of quatere (concutere > concussion, discutere > discussion, percutere > percussion) are sister formations, not stages of the cask chain. No stage between PIE *kwet- and Latin quatere is directly attested.
