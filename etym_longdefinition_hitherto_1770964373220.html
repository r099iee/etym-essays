The adverb hitherto means "up to this time; until now; before this point". It marks a boundary between the past and the present moment, usually in a formal, scholarly, legal, or historical register. When a writer says that something had "hitherto been concealed", the word draws a firm line across time: everything up to now was one way; now something changes. It is a temporal bookend, the dignified scholarly counterpart of "so far" or "up to now", and in the source sentence — "the ambition he had hitherto been at some pains to conceal" — it fixes the boundary precisely at the moment of the quatrain, before which Yu-cun's ambition stayed hidden.

The word's anatomy is transparent: it is a compound of the spatial adverb hither, "to here, to this place", and the preposition/particle to, joined as hither + to = literally "up to here". Old English hider meant "to this place"; Middle English wrote hider-to and then hither-to; and the compound meant, at first, purely spatially, "up to this spot". The transfer from space to time is one of the oldest metaphors of language: time is imagined as a journey, and "up to this place" becomes "up to this time". By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the temporal sense was firmly established, and the spatial one receded into archaism.

Core senses:
  • Until this time, up to now, before this point in time.
  • (Rarely and archaically) up to this place: the original spatial sense of "hither" + "to", now almost entirely superseded by the temporal meaning.
Exhaustive polysemy and modern senses:
  • Historical-narrative use: "up to the moment of narration", used to establish what was true before a turning point (e.g. "the manuscript had hitherto been unknown").
  • Legal and parliamentary use: "as of now, previously", marking the state of affairs before a ruling or statute takes effect.
  • Academic use: the standard way to introduce prior scholarship or evidence ("hitherto unexamined evidence", "hitherto neglected sources") — a favourite of theses and monographs.
  • Contrastive-ironic use: pairing "hitherto" with a sudden reversal emphasises the completeness of the change ("a quiet town, hitherto undisturbed by tourists, was overrun in a single summer").
Historical bridge: the word is a pure English formation. Hither descends from Old English hider "to this place", from Proto-Germanic *hidrē (Gothic hidrē, Old Norse heðra), built on the ancient demonstrative "this, here" that also gives us he and here. To descends from Old English tō, from Proto-Germanic *tō, from the PIE directional particle *do "to, toward" (Greek -de "toward", Old Church Slavonic do "up to"). Once a language measures distance in time as a journey, "up to this place" naturally becomes "up to this time"; the compound made exactly that crossing. The mnemonic — "HITHER-to: until HITHER/here" — preserves the original spatial metaphor perfectly.

Register and usage:
  • Now distinctly bookish and slightly archaic; in ordinary speech it has been replaced by "previously", "so far", or "up to now".
  • Retains formal gravity in legal, historical, and academic prose, where it signals precision about the boundary of the past.
  • An uninflected, non-comparable adverb: no plural, comparative, or superlative forms.
