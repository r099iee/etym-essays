Vespers is the evening office of prayer in the Western Christian Church — the canonical hour appointed for the worship of the close of day, traditionally sung as daylight falls (about 6 p.m.). In the Roman Catholic and Lutheran Divine Offices, vespers consists of five psalms, a short reading, the hymn, and, as its crown, the canticle of Mary, the Magnificat. It is the most musically celebrated of the offices: Vespers settings (Monteverdi's Vespro della Beata Vergine, 1610; Mozart's Vesperae solennes de confessore, 1780) are among the glories of the repertoire. In the Anglican tradition its descendant is Evensong, formed in 1549 by fusing vespers with compline.

In Wordsworth's Prelude (the source context of this card), the word closes a liturgical metaphor: "Matins and vespers of harmonious verse!" — the poet's daily craft keeps the two great offices of the day, morning and evening, and both are "harmonious verse".

Principal senses today:

  • Liturgical (Roman Catholic, Orthodox-rite terminology in the West, Lutheran): the evening office, the first of the canonical hours as they begin the day at sunset; its psalms and Magnificat are proper to it.
  • Historical fixed phrase: "the Sicilian Vespers" (1282) — the rising of the Sicilians against their Angevin ruler, begun at the hour of vespers on Easter Monday, giving the word a permanent political echo.
  • Figurative: any evening ritual, especially of birdsong — the "vespers of the birds" is a stock poetic phrase for the dusk chorus.
  • Adjectival kin: "vespertine" (of the evening: vespertine flowers, vespertine animals such as bats) and the Vesper sparrow, named for its evening singing — the word family radiates from the evening star Venus, called Vesper in Latin.
  • Anglican context: in England after 1549 the daily evening worship is "Evening Prayer" or "Evensong"; "vespers" there is a historical/Catholic term, increasingly also used loosely for choral evening services.

The historical bridge is chronological and physical: the early Christians prayed at dusk, the hour of the lamp-lighting (lucernarium); the office was named from vesper, the evening star that appears at dusk; Old French vespres brought it into English in the 13th century. The word thus preserves in its very shape the image of Venus rising in the western sky — the "west" itself is cognate (Latin vesper, "evening; west", is kin to Germanic west).

Register: vespers is formal, liturgical, and poetic; in Catholic usage it is the ordinary name of the evening office, in colloquial speech it appears mainly in church notices, poetry, and fixed phrases.

In common parlance the word is also softened into metaphor: "the vespers of autumn", "a vespers of quiet" designate any settling, closing, or gradual end of a period, echoing the office's function of closing the day with thanksgiving. The plural form itself (vespers, like matins) preserves the ancient habit of naming an office by its recurring celebrations — the evening (prayers) — and the word never appears as a singular noun in liturgical English, only in the Latin-derived adjective "vesper" (as in vesper bell) and its learned derivative "vespertine".
