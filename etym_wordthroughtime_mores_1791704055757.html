- Classical Antiquity (Rome) — Mos Maiorum: the singular mōs and plural mōrēs formed the bedrock of the Roman state, denoting the ancestral customs, unwritten constitution, and austere ethical character of the Roman citizen.
- Renaissance & Early Modern European Latin: preserved in humanist Latin treatises and legal codes (contra bonos mores), maintaining the classical Ciceronian sense of public manners and civic morality.
- Late 19th c. (c. 1898) — Entry into Anglophone social thought: adapted into early English sociological writings to discuss comparative customs and primitive social organization.
- 1906 — Institutionalization by William Graham Sumner: Sumner's landmark book Folkways transformed mores into a foundational, technical scientific concept in American sociology, distinct from folkways and laws.
- Mid to Late 20th c. — Anthropological relativism & cultural revolutions: widely popularized in post-war media, journalism, and cultural studies to analyze generational shifts (e.g., 'the changing sexual mores of the 1960s', 'bourgeois mores').
- 21st c. — Digital ethics & culture wars: deployed in contemporary critique to examine digital mores, internet cancel culture, and the fragmentation of national moral consensus into polarized online subcultures.
