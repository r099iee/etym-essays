FIDELITY: The quality of being faithful — steadfast loyalty, devoted adherence, and unwavering allegiance to a person, a cause, a belief, an institution, a vow, or a duty; and, by extension, exact and accurate correspondence to an original, the precise reproduction of a source in sound, image, data, text, or transmission. Sense in this context. The card's sentence — "The white cloak is a symbol of our purity, our __________" — deploys fidelity in its oldest and most moral register. The white cloak is an emblem of ceremonial purity, and fidelity is named as its spiritual twin: the keeping of one's faith and vows, the unstained devotion that a white garment visually promises. Here fidelity means the active virtue of holding true — to a knightly oath, a religious commitment, a ruler, or a loved one — and the pairing with "purity" stresses that fidelity is not merely agreement but an uncorrupted, unbroken loyalty, something that can be soiled or betrayed and therefore must be guarded. The tone is elevated and honorific, the language of chivalric or religious ceremony, and the blank word stands as the climactic abstract noun toward which the whole sentence builds. Primary sense — faithfulness and loyalty. In its central modern meaning, fidelity is the settled disposition of being true to someone or something over time: a spouse faithful to the marriage, a friend faithful through hardship, an employee faithful to an employer, a soldier faithful to commander and country, a believer faithful to God and to the doctrines of a church. This sense is essentially a synonym of "loyalty" and "faithfulness," though with a slightly graver, more formal tone; it names the virtue as a standard kept, a trust honoured. It appears in set collocations such as "fidelity to one's word," "fidelity to the cause," "a lifelong fidelity," and "pledge fidelity." Polysemy and secondary modern meanings. The word has branched into several distinct registers, all of which derive from the same Latin core of "faith, trust":
  • Marital fidelity: sexual faithfulness of a spouse — the vow of monogamy, freedom from adultery or infidelity; the opposite is "infidelity." This is arguably the most frequent everyday sense, as in "marital fidelity," "he pledged fidelity to his wife."
  • Political and feudal allegiance: loyalty to a state, ruler, constitution, party, or cause — the modern heir of the medieval oath of fealty; "fidelity to the crown," "fidelity to the party line."
  • Religious fidelity: steadfastness in faith and obedience to God or a church; in Catholic usage "fidelity" overlaps with "faith" itself and with the fidelity of God to his covenant promises.
  • Fiduciary and professional trust: in law and finance, fidelity to a client's interests — the "fiduciary duty" of trustees, guardians, and executives; a "fidelity bond" is an insurance bond protecting an employer against employee theft or fraud.
  • Technical accuracy (the great 20th-century extension): exactness with which a device or medium reproduces a source — "high fidelity" (Hi-Fi) audio, "wireless fidelity" (Wi-Fi), data fidelity, digital fidelity. The word here means "truth to the original signal": a hi-fi system is faithful to the recorded sound wave; a scan is faithful to the original document.
  • Translation and textual fidelity: in literary studies, fidelity to the original text or meaning — a translation judged by how faithfully it renders the source; a transcription faithful to the manuscript.
  • Historical and scientific fidelity: a historical account faithful to the facts; in genetics, replication fidelity — the accuracy of DNA copying; in computer science, model fidelity.
The historical bridge. All these senses flow from Latin fidēs "faith, trust, belief," which itself continues the Indo-European root meaning "to trust, confide." The moral sense — holding true to a person or a vow — is the original and remains central. The technical sense is a striking modern metaphor: beginning in the 1920s and 1930s, audio engineers adopted "fidelity" for the accuracy of a sound-reproduction system, conceiving of the machine as a "faithful" servant that keeps its trust by reproducing the original recording with minimal distortion; from "high fidelity" the metaphor spread to every kind of exact replication, culminating in the deliberate 1999 coinage "Wi-Fi," which was never itself a true abbreviation but was marketed on the prestige of "high fidelity." Collocations and register. Fidelity collocates with the verbs "pledge," "swear," "vow," "keep," "maintain," "observe," and "breach," and with the adjectives "absolute," "unswerving," "unwavering," "strict," "mutual," "sexual," "historical," and "high." Its register ranges from the grave and ceremonial (marital, religious, feudal) to the precise and technical (audio, digital, scientific), and it carries a consistently positive connotation of trustworthiness; the word rarely appears without some implication of an obligation kept or betrayed. Derived forms include faithful, faithfully, faithfulness, infidelity, unfaithful, and the adjectival fidelity-related compounds in technical usage (high-fidelity). Its closest antonym is "infidelity," and its moral field includes honour, integrity, loyalty, and constancy.
