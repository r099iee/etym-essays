Introduction. The word foundry condenses four thousand years of pouring. It descends from an Indo-European root for "to pour", passes through the Latin verb that named the Roman caster's art, crosses into French as the workshop of the medieval metal-melter, and arrives in English — twice: first as the name of an art, then as the name of a building. Its history is a history of technology, craft, and industry, and its newest senses (the silicon foundry, the idea foundry) are still the oldest sense in disguise: melt, pour, mould, cool.

1. The root: *ǵʰew-, "to pour"

The chain begins in Proto-Indo-European with a root reconstructed as *ǵʰew-, "to pour", and its extended form *ǵʰew-d-. The cognates are visible across the family: Greek khéō ("I pour, pour out, shed"), Sanskrit juhóti ("he pours", especially a libation into the sacrificial fire — the ritual verb of the Rigveda), and Latin fundō, which combines the plain sense "to pour, pour out, shed" (tears, blood, wine) with the technical sense "to melt (metal) and cast it into a mould". The Roman evidence is rich: Pliny the Elder's Natural History (Book 34) describes the casting of bronze statues (fundere aera), the lost-wax method, and the work of the fusor, the caster; Latin had already made the leap from pouring liquid to pouring liquid metal, and the vocabulary of the foundry existed in the Roman world — furnaces, crucibles, moulds, and the smell of poured bronze.

2. Old French: fondre, and the birth of fonderie

From Latin fundere the Romance vernaculars took the verb: Old French fondre, "to melt; to cast (metal)", attested from the 11th–12th centuries (the same verb also means "to dissolve, to collapse", a separate branch of "melting"). Around the verb grew a noun with the suffix -erie: French fonderie, attested in the 14th century meaning the workshop where metal is melted and cast. The medieval foundry was the bell-founder's and armorer's shop: church bells, cannon, cauldrons, and bronze vessels were cast in pits and moulds through the Middle Ages, and the craft words (fundere, fondre, fonderie) travelled with the trade from town to town and language to language.

3. English takes the word: from art to building (c. 1600–1700)

English borrowed the noun in the early modern period: foundery (also foundrie, fonderie) is recorded around 1600, first for the art or process of casting metal — "the art of foundery" — and only later for the place. The 17th century is also the century of the type founder: printing demanded cast metal type, and the "letter foundry" (later the type foundry) became a distinct institution, casting punches, matrices, and founts; the casting of type is a chapter of foundry history that kept the word alive in the printing trades for four hundred years. By about 1700 the shift was complete: foundry meant the building — the casting house — and the industrial age found the word waiting.

4. The Industrial Revolution (18th–19th centuries)

The 18th and 19th centuries made the foundry an emblem of manufacture. The iron foundry of the industrial revolution — cupola furnaces charged with pig iron and coke, moulding floors of damp sand, patterns, ladles, and the roar and glare of the tapping furnace — supplied the world with engine cylinders, girders, pipes, and rails; foundries clustered in the iron districts and foundry towns of Britain and America; and the foundryman became a recognised tradesman with his own unions, his own occupational diseases, and his own hierarchy of moulders, coremakers, and fettlers, each answerable to the overseer of the workmen. The word also grew a sister industry: the bronze foundry of art-casting, from which public statues issued, and the great 19th-century art foundries that still flourish. It is in this industrial world that Wilde sets the destruction of the Happy Prince: the broken statue is melted in a furnace, and the foundry's overseer stares in wonder at the leaden heart that alone refuses to melt — the workshop of transformation turned, in the tale, into a machine of reduction.

5. New materials, new foundries (20th–21st centuries)

The twentieth century enlarged the word's domain: precision investment casting, die-casting, continuous casting, and the "glass foundry" (casting and blowing studios) extended the founding arts; the type foundry reinvented itself as the digital font foundry; and, from the 1980s, the "semiconductor foundry" appeared — the contract factory that manufactures silicon chips designed by other companies, the archetype being Taiwan's TSMC (founded 1987), whose "foundry model" split design from manufacture and turned "foundry" into one of the most valuable words in the technology economy. Beyond industry, "foundry" has become a general metaphor for productive pouring-out: talent foundries, idea foundries, startup foundries — each one claiming, in metaphor, the furnace and the mould of the old pour-house.

6. The semantic career summed

From the Indo-European pour, through the Roman caster and the French fonderie, to the Industrial Revolution's ironworks and the silicon foundry of the present, foundry has never left its root: the word means, at every stage, a place — or an art — where something is melted and poured into shape. The overspilling career — art, building, trade, industry, technology, metaphor — is a single story of pouring, and the leaden heart of Wilde's tale, refusing to pour out, is the one thing a foundry cannot process: the word's ancient logic in one image.
