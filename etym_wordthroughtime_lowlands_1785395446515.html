- [Late 18th century: a compound against the heights] - lowland is coined as a late form of low + land in systematic opposition to highland, first and most firmly of Scotland — the Lowlands against the Highlands; it is at first a purely geographic tag, describing relative elevation without praise or blame.
- [19th century: social and historical colour] - the Lowlands of Scotland come to stand for the anglicised, arable, commercially connected south in contrast with the Gaelic Highlands; the word quietly absorbs a whole social geography — cultivation, roads, towns, industry — while still naming land.
- [19th–20th century: the geographer's systematic term] - lowland generalises into formal physical geography: areas near sea level or in river basins, flat or gently undulating, marsh, river-flat, or rolling ground — a whole region defined by low elevation, opposed to upland and mountain.
- [Contemporary sense] - lowlands is a neutral, systematic term of geography and ecology (and the poetic echo of the Low Countries), used for broad sunken, habitable country; in literary use it keeps its promise-and-distance colouring — the level, open, terminal horizon glimpsed from the heights.
