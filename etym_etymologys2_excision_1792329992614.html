Note on historical nuance: The Latin compound verb excīdere displays regular Latin vowel reduction (apophony): when the prefix ex- was attached to the simplex verb caedere ("to cut"), the unstressed root diphthong -ae- weakened to long -ī- (*ex- + caedere* → *excīdere*). Care must be taken in Latin philology not to confuse excīdere (with long -ī-, from caedere "to cut", past participle excīsus, giving English excision) with the homophonic compound excidere (with short -i-, from cadere "to fall", past participle excāsus, meaning "to fall out, escape, perish"). From Latin excīsiō, the word passed into Middle French as excision, where it denoted military razing and early surgical cutting, before entering Late Middle English in the 15th century. Modern comparative linguists (de Vaan 81) connect the base verb caedere to PIE *kae-id- / *kheid- ("to strike, cut"), cognate with Lithuanian skíesti ("to divide, separate").
