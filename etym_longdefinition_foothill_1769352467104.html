foothill (noun, very commonly plural "foothills"): a low hill at the base of a mountain or mountain range, forming the transitional zone where the flat lowland begins to climb toward the high peaks. Primary sense: topography — a low hill standing at the foot of a mountain range; the plural "foothills" is the normal, collective form naming the whole belt of such hills. Foothills are the low, rolling hills that rise gently between a valley or plain and the steep front of a mountain range — often the last easily habitable and traversable ground before the terrain becomes rugged and alpine. In Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, "the golden foothill slopes curve up to the strong and rocky Gabilan mountains" uses the singular form attributively (foothill slopes) to describe the golden, grass-covered lower slopes that rise toward the rocky Gabilan peaks above the Salinas Valley. Secondary and extended senses:
  • Collective geography: the foothills are the broad, often gentle, rolling belt of low hills and ridges immediately in front of a mountain range — the most accessible, habitable, and farmable fringe of a mountain region, where vineyards, oak woodland, ranches, and suburbs cluster before the land steepens into true mountains; loosely equivalent to "piedmont country." Geologically, foothills mark the boundary where uplifted mountain blocks give way to lowland basins; their soft ridges are often eroded outliers of the range's own rock, mantled with gravel fans and alluvial slopes.
  • Figurative: "the foothills of X" — the early, preliminary, or beginner stages of a large undertaking ("the foothills of a career," "the foothills of an economic recovery"), by analogy with standing at the foot of a mountain you have not yet begun to climb.
  • Planetary and technical (rare): "foothills" is used in planetary geomorphology for the low flanking hills and ridges at the margins of mountain masses on the Moon, Mars, and other bodies.
  • Compound morphology: foot + hill. "Foot" carries its ancient transferred sense of "the base or lower end of a mountain" (a metaphor found in Old English fot and in Latin pes montis, Greek podos orous, and Sanskrit pada), and "hill" is the ordinary word for a natural elevation of land.
Historical and semantic bridge: the compound was coined in 19th-century American English (first recorded c. 1856) by surveyors and settlers of the westward expansion, who needed a precise word for the low hills standing "at the foot" of a range. The formation is fully transparent — foot + hill — and it fuses two very old Germanic words, each with an ancient "landscape-as-body" metaphor already built into it. The plural quickly became the standard form because the feature itself is a series of hills, not a single one. Register: neutral, standard geographical vocabulary, slightly scenic in literary and travel writing; in technical geography it remains a plain, matter-of-fact label for the lower elevations of a range.
