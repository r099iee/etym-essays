Skulking is the present participle (and gerund) of the verb skulk, meaning to move in a stealthy, sneaking manner, or to hide or linger in secret, especially with a sly, guilty, cowardly or malevolent purpose. In Tolkien's passage, Smaug contemptuously imagines the dwarves “skulking outside” — lurking invisibly in the dark at the mountain's entrance, too cowardly or scheming to enter, leaving all the dangerous work to Bilbo. The word's characteristic flavour is the combination of secrecy with a disapproved motive: one skulks when one does not want to be seen because being seen would expose guilt, cowardice, or treachery.

Core meanings.

  • Sense 1 — to move stealthily: to walk, creep or slink with a furtive, avoiding gait, keeping to shadows and cover: “He skulked along the back wall of the castle.”
  • Sense 2 — to lurk or lie in wait: to stay hidden, watching, often with hostile or predatory intent: “The assassin was skulking in the doorway.”
  • Sense 3 — to hide from duty or danger (shirk): to keep out of sight to avoid work, responsibility, or battle: “He was accused of skulking behind the lines.” The noun skulker historically meant a soldier who shirked fighting.
  • Sense 4 — figurative, to be present in a hidden, threatening way: “danger skulking beneath the surface” — things (fears, dangers, evils) can skulk as well as people.

Connotation and register. The word is pejorative. Unlike neutral creep or steal, skulk always implies a disreputable reason for the stealth — guilt, cowardice, spite, or villainy. It is a favourite of crime fiction, gothic and fantasy literature (Tolkien, Poe, Lovecraft), where villains, thieves, spies and beasts skulk in shadows and alleys. As an adjective/participle, skulking describes the agent: “a skulking figure”, “skulking thieves”, “a skulking beast”. The word often carries a sense of contempt on the part of the speaker — as in Smaug's sneering taunt — because the skulker is judged a coward as well as a schemer.

Grammar and morphology. Skulking is the -ing form of the regular verb skulk. It functions as a participle (describing a noun), as a progressive verb form, and as a gerund (noun: “his skulking about the corridors alarmed the guards”). The noun skulker means one who skulks (historically a shirker in the military). The verb is usually intransitive, taking prepositions: skulk about / around / behind / in / off. There is no common adverb derived from it.

Phrases and collocations.

  • “Skulking in the shadows / in the dark / behind a tree / around corners.”
  • “A skulking figure / shape / beast / thief.”
  • “Skulk about / around / off.”
  • Military-historical: “skulker” — a soldier who evades battle.

Note on the source. Smaug's contempt is central to the passage: the dwarves, he sneers, are cowardly freeloaders skulking in the dark while making Bilbo risk his life for a “fair share” they will never give him. The word's built-in blend of stealth, hiding and cowardice makes it the perfect instrument of the dragon's mockery, and the narrator uses it precisely because it fuses movement, concealment and moral condemnation into a single gesture.
