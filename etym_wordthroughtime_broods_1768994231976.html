- Old English (brēdan): the verb means “to keep warm, cherish, nurture”, while the noun brōd is the brood — the young hatched at once — so the earliest sense is bodily and tender, the warmth that covers the nest.
- Middle English (broden): “to sit on eggs”, to incubate — a hen broods her clutch; the word is still purely literal, an act of heat and patience, with no thought in it yet.
- 16th–17th c.: the mind takes over the image — to brood on or upon a thought is to sit on it, to incubate it silently; Shakespeare's age uses the word of both hens and meditative men, the bird still visible in the metaphor.
- 18th–19th c.: the figurative sense hardens and turns sullen — to brood is to dwell gloomily, resentfully, on a grief or a wrong; the word collects the morose, silent register that defines it today, and the nest recedes into the background.
- 20th–21st c.: the split is complete — zoology keeps the literal sense (a bird broods its eggs) while everyday English uses the verb for obsessive, unhappy rumination; a literary writer can reactivate the old incubating heat, as here, fusing the sullen thought with the hatching of a temperament.
