FRONDS are the leaf-like organs of ferns, palms, cycads and similar plants: the large, usually divided, feathery leaves that unroll from a coiled fiddlehead in ferns and spread in great arcs from the crowns of palms. The word is the technical and poetic name for foliage that is not quite a leaf, and in the passage from The Hobbit the tall fronds rising right above the hobbit's head complete the picture of a company drowning in vegetation: the travellers push through a sea of bracken while fern fronds tower over Bilbo like living pillars, and the forest swallows him whole. Fronds, unlike leaves, imply scale and strangeness — something primeval, ferny, older than flowers — and the word turns the forest floor into a cathedral of ancient greenery, a quiet primeval nave through which the company marches "quiet as quiet" over pine-needles. The tone is one of hushed, enormous age: the fronds rise like pillars, the gloom deepens, and the forest-silence becomes a presence.

SENSES AND USES

  • Botany, of ferns (core): the leaf of a fern, characteristically a whole compound organ made up of many leaflets (pinnae and pinnules), growing from a creeping rhizome or a short trunk; a young fern frond is the coiled "fiddlehead" that unrolls in spring.
  • Botany, of palms and cycads: the large leaf of a palm or cycad, spread in great arcs from the crown; botanists use "frond" for the leaves of all these groups because they are single compound leaves of unusual size and structure.
  • Of seaweeds and large algae: the leaf-like blade of a kelp or similar seaweed is called a frond — the word extends to any large, divided, leaf-like structure that is not a flowering plant's leaf.
  • Literary and poetic sense (19th century onward): "fronds" implies scale, strangeness and the primeval — foliage that dwarfs the observer; "fronded" became a favourite of Romantic nature poetry, and in literary prose the word carries the resonance of age, the forest and the tropics.
  • Adjectival and technical derivatives: "fronded" (furnished with fronds), "frondless", "frond-like", and botanical "frondose" (leafy) from Latin frondōsus.
  • In taxonomy and field guides: "fronds" is the standard word for the vegetative leaves of pteridophytes (ferns and their allies), and in horticulture for the foliage of palms and cycads.

The word's power lies in its avoidance of the ordinary word "leaf". Where leaves are flat, small and familiar, fronds are large, divided and strange — the foliage of a world before flowers, of fern forests and palm groves. That is exactly its effect in The Hobbit: fronds rising above a hobbit's head turn the everyday into the overwhelming.

CONTRASTS AND BOUNDARIES

  • "Leaves" are the ordinary flat blades of flowering plants; a frond is characteristically a whole compound structure made up of many leaflets.
  • "Sprays" and "branches" are woody supports that bear leaves, whereas a frond is both support and leaf in one.
  • "Blades" suggests the flat, narrow, sword-like leaves of grasses; fronds are broad and divided.
  • "Needles" are the narrow, pointed leaves of conifers like pines — functionally the foliage, but a completely different shape and a different word.
  • "Fiddleheads" are the young, coiled fronds of ferns before they unroll — the same organ at an earlier stage.
  • "Pinnae" and "pinnules" are the leaflets into which a fern frond is divided — parts of the frond, not the whole organ.
  • "Palm" and "fern" name the plants, while "fronds" names their foliage — the word denotes the organ, not the plant.
