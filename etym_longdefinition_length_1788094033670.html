Primary sense. Length is the property or measurement of how long something is — the greater extent or dimension of an object, measured end to end (“the length of the table”, “a rope of great length”), as opposed to its width, breadth or height. It is both a physical quantity (measured in metres, feet, etc.) and the abstract noun for the dimension itself. The word is the noun corresponding to the adjective long, formed with the same Germanic abstract suffix -th that gives width, depth, strength and warmth; the vowel alternation long/length is a relic of the Old English sound change called i-umlaut, by which the *-i- of the suffix fronted the root vowel — the same phenomenon as strong/strength and broad/breadth.

Duration in time. From spatial extent, length extends naturally to time: “the length of the meeting”, “the length of a life”, “a film of feature length”. This spatial-to-temporal transfer is ancient and universal (time is measured as a “long” or “short” extent in most Indo-European languages) and is the semantic bridge to the idioms below.

Idiomatic senses:

  • At length — the sense of this card's context, from The Hobbit: two distinct meanings. (a) “After a long time, eventually, at last”: “At length they reached the hill path.” (b) “In full detail, at great length”: “he spoke at length about his travels.” The first grows from the idea of a process running to its full extent; the second from “to the full length” of speech.
  • Go to great lengths: to make great efforts, spare no exertion — from the image of travelling a long way, attested since the 18th century.
  • At full length / full-length: stretched out completely (“lying at full length”), or of a portrait or film showing the whole figure.
  • A length: a piece or stretch of something (“a length of rope”, “a length of cloth”, “a length of pipe”); in horse-racing and swimming, a unit equal to the length of a horse's or boat's body (“won by two lengths”); in cricket, the place where the ball pitches (“a good length”).
  • Other idioms: “at arm's length” (kept at a safe distance), “the length and breadth of” (everywhere in), “measure one's length” (fall flat), “to any length” (without limit).

Technical senses. In phonetics and linguistics, “length” is the duration of a sound — vowel length distinguishes words in many languages (Latin, Japanese, Finnish, classical Arabic). In geometry and engineering, length is one of the three spatial dimensions; in printing, line length is the measure of a line of type; in sewing, the length of a garment is its vertical extent.

Word family. The derivatives lengthen “to make or grow longer” (16th c.), lengthy “very long” (18th c., originally American and slightly informal), and longish “somewhat long” complete the family; the adjective long, the adverb long, and the verb to long (“yearn”, from a different root) are separate words.

Grammar and form. Old English lengþu (also lengð) is an abstract noun built on the adjective lang/long with the suffix *-iþō; the modern spelling length (with -ngth) is regularised from Middle English lengthe. The word is a noun only; the verb is lengthen, the adjective long.
