Definition. A respite is a temporary interval of rest, relief, or exemption — a pause in which something hard, painful, or relentless briefly lifts. The word differs from plain “rest” in that it points away from what it interrupts: a respite is always a respite from something, whether labour, pain, grief, warfare, weather, or obligation. It also has a precise legal life, where it means the postponement of a sentence, penalty, debt, or payment.

  • Core sense: an interval of relief. “A respite to this passion” in the source context — Wordsworth’s Prelude — is a pause granted to an overwhelming feeling: the speaker deliberately gives his passion a holiday before settling into gentler happiness. The noun characteristically collocates with from (“a respite from the heat,” “a respite from care”) and with without (“without respite” = unremittingly, without pause), where the word achieves its most powerful negative use: a battle, a storm, or a persecutor that allows no respite is utterly relentless.
  • Legal sense: postponement or reprieve. In law, a respite is the postponement of a fine, debt, judgment, or — historically and still today — of an execution, in which function it is a reprieve. This is the oldest documented English sense: the medieval legal term respit (from Old French) named the time granted to a debtor or defendant to pay or answer, and “respite of the day of judgment” already appears in Middle English legal prose. Etymologically reprieve is a different word (from Old French repris, “taken back”), but in ordinary speech the two converge on the idea of a temporary release from a doom.
  • Respite care (modern institutional sense). Since the late twentieth century, “respite care” names temporary institutional or in-home care for a dependent person (elderly, chronically ill, or disabled) that gives family caregivers relief from an unremitting burden. This modern technical sense extends the ancient meaning of relief-from-burden into social-policy language, and it is today one of the commonest uses of the word in official documents.
  • Natural and meteorological usage. The word is idiomatic for weather and hostile nature giving pause: “the rain gave a brief respite,” “no respite from the wind.” The bridge is again the image of hostile forces intermittently ceasing — the elements, like a pursuing enemy, can grant or refuse a respite.
  • Figurative and literary usage. Respite is a favourite of formal prose and poetry for any lull in suffering, mourning, work, illness, or war: “a respite from grief,” “death grants no respite.” Its register is literary-formal; in conversation, “break,” “breather,” or “lull” usually displaces it, and using respite in speech deliberately raises the tone.
  • Grammar and pronunciation. The noun is both countable (“a short respite”) and, more normally, uncountable in phrases of duration (“three days’ respite”). British pronunciation is historically /ˈrɛspɪt/ — still heard in legal and formal contexts — with modern /ˈrɛspaɪt/ spreading in general and American usage; the spelling has kept the French-derived -spite with silent echoes of Latin respectus.

The semantic bridge. The word descends from Latin respectus, “a looking back, regard, consideration.” Late Latin legal and chancery practice used respectus for the consideration a court showed a party by granting extra time: the regard for a person’s circumstances became, by metonymy, the delay itself. That double heritage — compassionate consideration on one side, mechanical postponement on the other — explains why the word still covers both mercy (respite from punishment) and pure duration (a pause in the rain). Respect itself is the same Latin family, so that to “show respect” and to “grant respite” are, etymologically, one gesture: looking back at someone in consideration of their need.
