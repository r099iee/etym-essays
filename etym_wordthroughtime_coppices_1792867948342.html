- c. 1400 — the cut-over wood: Middle English copys names a wood newly cut for regrowth, a working asset of the manor, not scenery.
- 15th–17th c. — the fuel economy: coppices feed charcoal ironworks, bakehouses, and hop-poles; whole parishes are mapped as coppice panels on rotation.
- 18th–19th c. — enclosure and romance: poets discover the coppice as picturesque cover while coal and coke quietly kill its trade; the word grows sentimental as the craft declines.
- Early 20th c. — neglect: chestnut and hazel coppices fall silent, stools overgrow into high forest, and the word survives chiefly in place-names and novels.
- Late 20th c. — conservation revival: ecologists recognise coppicing as the engine of woodland biodiversity, and volunteers take up the billhook again for dormice and fritillaries.
- Today — craft and habitat together: coppices mean green woodwork, charcoal, and nature reserves at once, a working landscape remembered and restored.
