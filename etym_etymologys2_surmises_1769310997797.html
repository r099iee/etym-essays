1. PIE *meit- "to exchange, send, cast" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3500 BCE) — debated, see note. 2. Latin mittere "to send, let go, cast, put" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE). 3. Latin super- "above, over, upon" + mittere → supermittere "to send over, lay upon" (attested in classical Latin, though uncommon). 4. Old French sur- + mettre (from Latin mittere) → surmettre "to lay a charge upon, accuse, allege" (12th–13th c.). 5. Old French surmise — feminine past participle of surmettre used as a noun, "accusation, charge, allegation" (13th c.). 6. Middle English surmise "formal accusation, suspicion, allegation" (borrowed via Anglo-Norman; late 14th c.). 7. English surmise "conjecture, unproven guess" (noun; sense softened in the 16th–17th c.; the verb is a back-formation from the noun) → plural noun SURMISES. Debate / nuance notes: - The reconstruction of Latin mittere's PIE root as *meit- "to exchange, send" is traditional but not fully secure (de Vaan treats mittere as lacking a completely certain Indo-European equation); treat this stage as inferred. - The semantic drift from "formal legal charge" to "unproven guess" is documented across the 16th–17th centuries and is the single most important shift in the word's history. - English surmise was first a noun (borrowed from Old French); the verb to surmise was back-formed from it in English.
