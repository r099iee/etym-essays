The biography of stews is a study in how architecture, public hygiene, and morality can permanently warp a word. A perfectly innocent word for a heated room or a bath became, within a few centuries, the standard English term for the brothel district of a city.

The contested origin: two theories for a heated room.

  • The Romance forms (Old French estuve, Italian stufa, Spanish estufa) all point to a base meaning 'heated place for steaming.' The standard Graeco-Latin account derives it from Vulgar Latin *extūfare 'to heat in steam,' built on Greek tŷphos 'steam, smoke, vapour' — a word connected with the same Greek family that gives us typhoon (the whirlwind) and typhus (the fever once blamed on foul vapours).
  • An older rival theory derives the whole family from a Germanic root *stubō 'heated room,' which survives in German Stube 'room, parlour' and in English stove — which originally meant 'heated room' and only later 'the heating appliance.'
  • Whichever route is right, the semantics converge: in the cold climate of northern and central Europe, a heated room was a central amenity, and public bath-houses were common in medieval towns, heirs to the Roman habit of communal bathing.

Old French and the crossing into English.

  • Old French estuve named the bath-house or sweating-room; the verb estuver meant both 'to bathe in hot water or steam' and 'to stew (food)' — because slow cooking over moist heat is the culinary version of steaming.
  • The Normans brought the word to England after 1066. Middle English stewe appeared in the 13th century for a heated room or bath-house. From the same stem, in its cooking sense, came the verb 'to stew' and the noun 'stew' for the simmered dish — a branch that would later dominate the word entirely.

The stews of Southwark and the descent into infamy.

  • In medieval London the public bath-houses clustered in the district of Southwark, on the south bank of the Thames, outside the strict jurisdiction of the City. These were the famous 'stew-houses,' licensed by the Bishop of Winchester.
  • Because bath-houses offered privacy, warmth, and unclothed customers, they became natural fronts for prostitution. The bath and the brothel fused, and by the late 14th and 15th centuries the plural 'the stews' had become the standard name for a city's brothels and the district where they stood.
  • The metaphor was physical: steam, sweat, heat, and enclosed moist spaces — the very atmosphere of the bath-house — became the permanent connotation of the word. 'The stews' no longer described washing but the boiling of human beings in vice.

The word splits: dish, verb, and archaism.

  • As public bathing declined in the early modern period, the brothel sense lived on in literature and legal writing, and 'stew' in its cooking sense flourished: 'to stew' became the plain verb for slow simmering, and 'stew' the hearty dish.
  • In the 19th century the verb developed a vivid figurative sense: 'to stew' = to fret, worry, or brood, to be consumed by one's own heat of anxiety — and 'to stew in one's own juices' fixed the image of being cooked in one's own emotion.
  • Meanwhile 'the stews' hardened into a deliberately archaic, literary term for the depths of urban vice, used in historical fiction and moralizing prose. It appears, for example, in early-modern and Victorian writing about the old cities of Europe, and it survives today almost only in such consciously historical settings — precisely the register of this card's sentence about the 'delicate maid' falling into a 'life of shame in the stews of the old city.'

Linguistic notes:

  • The word's career shows a striking split: one stem, two destinies. The cooking branch ('stew' the dish) stayed innocent and became everyday; the bathing branch ('the stews') was criminalized by association and became an archaism.
  • Homonymy adds confusion: the fish-pond 'stew' (Old French estui 'enclosure') is a different word entirely, and the modern noun 'stew' (dish) is historically a different development from 'stew' (bath-house) — yet all are now the same spelling.
  • The plural is essential to the meaning: 'the stews' is a collective noun naming a district, not just several buildings, which is why the word evokes an entire underworld of heat, filth, and hidden vice.
