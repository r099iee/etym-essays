- “quickening”, 14th–16th c. - “restoration to life, animation”; strongly religious (the quickening Spirit) and literal (reviving a person, a plant, the world).
- “quickening”, 16th–18th c. medicine and law - the first felt fetal movements; a theological and legal milepost dividing the stages of pregnancy.
- “quickening”, 17th–19th c. general use - any increase of liveliness or vigor of mind, feelings, or nature; Wordsworth's “quickening virtue” belongs here.
- “quickening”, 19th c. onward - increasingly the acceleration sense as quick shifts from “alive” to “fast”: quickening of trade, pace, and life itself.
- “quickening”, modern - everyday sense = speeding up; poetic and religious sense = giving life; fantasy and tradition use (the Quickening) add mythic overtones of transmitted life-force.
