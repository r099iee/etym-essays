In the narrative, characters hear talk of their aunt's family in Nanking being 'involved in a case of manslaughter'—the killing associated with Xue Pan's men and Feng Yuan. Manslaughter here is the legal-moral label for a human killing that the system may treat as less than full murder: violence without (or without provable) malice aforethought, or a charge diluted by status, influence, and corrupt procedure. The word's tone is forensic and grave; it frames death as a case rather than pure melodrama, while still naming wrongful slaying. In a world of bribed magistrates and powerful clans, manslaughter can also signal the lighter charge through which wealth escapes the noose that murder would demand. At its core, manslaughter is the crime of killing a human being without malice aforethought—the unlawful killing that is neither murder (which requires malice or extreme recklessness) nor excusable or justifiable homicide. The word is a transparent English compound of man ('human being') and slaughter ('killing'): literally 'the killing of a person.' In common-law jurisdictions it is classically divided into two grades.
  • Voluntary manslaughter: a killing that would be murder (intent present) but is mitigated by recognized provocation, imperfect self-defence, or diminished responsibility—the law reduces the grade because the killer acted in the heat of the moment or under reduced mental culpability.
  • Involuntary manslaughter: an unlawful killing where there was no intent to kill at all, arising from criminal negligence, gross recklessness, or an unlawful act that foreseeably caused death (the 'unlawful act' doctrine)—e.g. death caused by grossly negligent medical care or by a reckless fist-fight that went wrong.
  • Vehicular manslaughter: the specialized modern statutory form—death caused by driving under the influence or with criminal negligence; often named separately in penal codes.
  • Corporate / gross-negligence manslaughter: a modern extension charging companies and senior managers for fatal failures of the duty of care in workplace and public safety law.
  • Historical and literal senses: before it was a refined legal term, manslaughter simply meant 'the slaughter of men' or mass killing of human beings (a sense alive in Middle English); in older legal usage it was sometimes used loosely for any homicide before the murder/manslaughter distinction crystallized.
Synonym boundaries: murder in common law and modern statutes usually requires malice aforethought or extreme recklessness with special stigma; manslaughter is the residual unlawful killing without that full intent package. Homicide is the neutral umbrella for any killing of a human by a human, lawful or not. Killing and slaying are general and moral-descriptive rather than technical charges. Assassination implies political or targeted killing for hire or ideology. Culpable homicide is a related statutory umbrella in some jurisdictions (e.g. Scots and South African law) under which murder and manslaughter sit. Manslaughter is specifically the offense-name that marks both gravity and a doctrinal gap below murder—exactly the legal 'gap' wealth can exploit. Collocations: charged with manslaughter, guilty of manslaughter, voluntary/involuntary manslaughter, vehicular manslaughter, manslaughter case, a verdict of manslaughter. Register is legal, journalistic, and formal narrative. In historical or translated Chinese settings, the English term stands in for local categories of killing that are serious yet negotiable under patronage. Keep the full compound MANSLAUGHTER as the headword; do not split or modernize it to a softer paraphrase like 'accidental death'—manslaughter is not accidental but criminal.
