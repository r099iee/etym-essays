The history of truce is one of the most instructive accidents in English: a plural noun became a singular one, and in doing so reshaped the vocabulary of war and peace. The starting point is the enormous Indo-European root *deru- "to be firm, solid" — the same root that gives English tree (the firm, standing thing) and true, Greek drŷs "oak" and Sanskrit dāru "wood". In the Germanic branch the root generated a family of words for faithfulness conceived as firmness: the adjective *triwwiz "true, trustworthy" and the noun *trewwō "faith, pledge, covenant". Gothic, the oldest attested Germanic language, shows the noun as triggwa, used in Wulfila's fourth-century Bible to translate Greek diathḗkē "covenant, testament"; Old High German triuwa is the ancestor of German Treue "fidelity"; Old Norse tryggð means both "faith, security" and, strikingly already, "truce". English inherited the same base twice over: true, troth (Old English trēowþ, "pledged faith", the word of betrothal and fealty) and truce all come from this one family.

Old English and the plural shift. Old English had the feminine noun trēow "faith, fidelity, pledge", and what appears to have happened — the details are visible across late Old English legal and historical prose — is that the plural trēowa/treowe "the pledges" came to mean "the (sworn) terms of an agreement", and specifically the terms of an armistice: enemy leaders exchange pledges, and the pledges constitute the truce. It is worth noting that Old English had native competitors — friþ "peace, protection" and griþ "truce, asylum" (the latter borrowed from Old Norse grið) — so the vocabulary for "cessation of war" was crowded; trewes won out for the specific sense of a time-limited, sworn suspension of arms.

Middle English trewes (13th–15th centuries). Middle English inherits the plural form as trewes, trewse "a truce", recorded from the thirteenth century in chronicles, romances and diplomatic records. In the chivalric world the truce was a sacred, sworn instrument: chronicles record with scandal any breach of "the trewes", because honour itself was pledged in the terms. The word appears in the formula "truce and parley", the two-step procedure of medieval negotiation — precisely the formula Tolkien revives in The Hobbit when Bard demands that the dwarves "call on your side for a truce and a parley", a collocation that would have sounded antique even in the fourteenth century, and deliberately more so in 1937.

The sixteenth-century re-analysis. In early Modern English the plural trewes was re-analysed as a singular noun with an ordinary plural ending, and the new singular truce was created by dropping the -s — the same process that produced pea from pease and cherry from cherise. From the sixteenth century onward truce is a fully singular noun: Shakespeare uses it of arranged cessations of hostility, and the phrase "flag of truce" enters the vocabulary of war, the white flag signalling willingness to negotiate.

Codification, 1600–1900. Early modern jurists took up the Roman term indutiae "truce" and folded the concept into the developing laws of war: Grotius's De jure belli ac pacis (1625) discusses the truce as a species of agreement distinct from peace, and English diplomatic practice of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries carefully distinguishes truces (limited, fixed-term) from treaties of peace (permanent). Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines truce plainly as "a temporary cessation of hostilities". The medieval institution was remembered in the "Truce of God" (Treuga Dei), the tenth–eleventh-century Church prohibition of fighting on Sundays, holy days and Lent — a truce imposed from above on the warrior aristocracy of western Europe.

The twentieth century and today. The twentieth century sharpened the legal taxonomy: an armistice (a general suspension negotiated toward peace, as in 1918), a ceasefire (a limited halt to firing) and a truce (a broad mutually agreed suspension) are now distinct instruments of international law, while the word's figurative life boomed: "a truce in the political war", "a fragile truce between factions". The single most famous truce in popular memory — the spontaneous Christmas truce of 1914, when opposing soldiers exchanged gifts and songs in no-man's-land — has given the word an undertone of poignant, fleeting humanity. In contemporary speech truce denotes any sworn or practical pause in a conflict, from war to a family quarrel, and it always carries the implicit warning that the restraint, however welcome, is temporary.
