Equivocation is the use of deliberately ambiguous, evasive, or double-meaning language to mislead, to avoid commitment, or to utter a statement that is technically defensible while intended to deceive. In the source sentence, John Ruskin in Praeterita recalling his sailor-grandfather who spoiled his daughters with all his heart when at home unless there appeared any tendency to equivocation, or imaginative statements, on the part of the children, which were always unforgiveable, the word names the child's sin the old seaman could not pardon: not outright lying so much as trimming, quibbling, and dressing up fancy as fact. The grandfather, a man of the quarterdeck where an order must mean one thing, demands plain truth; imaginative statements, Ruskin's tender euphemism for childish invention, belong to the same unforgiveable family.

Morally and legally, equivocation sits between lie and discretion. A lie asserts as true what the speaker believes false; equivocation asserts words that are true in one sense while the hearer takes them in another. Classic cases fill casuistry: the Jesuit doctrine of mental reservation (to swear I did not see him while silently adding in this room yesterday), the politician's non-denial denial (mistakes were made), the witness's literally true but misleading answer (I have never taken money for testimony, omitting gifts), the diplomat's constructive ambiguity (both sides read victory into one clause). English law treats deliberate equivocation in testimony as perjury in spirit; moral theology debates whether grave cause can excuse it; plain ethics usually condemns it as cowardly lying with an alibi.

Linguistically, equivocation exploits every resource of ambiguity: homonymy (bank, cleave), polysemy (fine, table), scope (all men do not lie), tense and aspect (I have not touched it today), presupposition (have you stopped cheating implies you cheated), and implicature (saying the library is closed to mean go away). Logicians name its misuse the fallacy of equivocation: using one word in two senses in one argument (man is a rational animal, no animal is rational in law, therefore man is not man). Rhetoricians distinguish it from vagueness (borderline cases), amphiboly (grammar with two parses), and euphemism (softening without deceiving).

  • Core moral sense (uncountable and countable): evasive double talk to mislead without stating a direct falsehood; for example guilty equivocation, Jesuitical equivocation, to answer without equivocation. This is the source sense and dictionary head sense, always pejorative except in casuistry defending it.
  • Logical fallacy sense: the informal fallacy of shifting word sense mid-argument; for example to convict an opponent of equivocation on law, right, or good. Textbooks list it with amphiboly and composition. The bridge is sameness of mechanism: one form, two meanings.
  • Legal and parliamentary senses: deliberately obscure testimony or ministerial answer; for example equivocation before the committee, to accuse a minister of equivocation. Hansard, courts, and inquiries treat it as contempt-adjacent. The bridge is institutional demand for plain yes or no.
  • Literary senses (Macbeth, Hamlet): the Porter's equivocator who could swear in both the scales against either scale (Macbeth 2.3, glancing at the Gunpowder Plot trial of Henry Garnet), Hamlet's words, words, words. Here equivocation is dramatic irony: the audience hears both senses, the victim one.
  • Neutral linguistic sense (equivocality): the property of having two equal voices or readings; for example the equivocation of bank, the equivocation of light. Philosophers of language use equivocation for lexical ambiguity itself, without moral blame. The bridge is etymology: equal voice equals two readings, good or bad.
  • Diplomatic euphemism (constructive ambiguity): deliberately equivocal drafting to secure agreement; for example the equivocation that saved the talks. Peacemakers sometimes praise what grandfathers punish: one man's unforgiveable quibble is another's peace formula.

Grammatically equivocation is an uncountable abstract noun (much equivocation) also used countably for instances (three equivocations in one answer), with adjective equivocal, adverb equivocally, verb equivocate. Pronounced in Received Pronunciation as ee-kwiv-oh-KAY-shun, with stress on KAY. It must not be confused with prevarication (straying from the point), tergiversation (changing sides), or Vatican-style mental reservation (its Catholic cousin). In Ruskin the word is quarterdeck plainness: a sailor's hatred of shore shuffle, passed to a moralist grandson who will make truth-telling the spine of his art criticism.
