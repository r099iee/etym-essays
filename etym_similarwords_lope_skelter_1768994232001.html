• Bound: Similar in covering ground with springing leaps; differs because "bound" is an energetic upward-and-forward jump, a leaping motion, while "lope" is the easy, rhythmic, long stride that covers distance without strain.
  • Gallop: Similar in describing a large animal's running gait; differs because "gallop" is the explosive four-beat full speed of a horse, brief and straining, while "lope" is power held in reserve at an easy pace.
  • Stride: Similar in being a long confident step; differs because "stride" is neutral and human, a walker's steady step, while "lope" carries the wild, effortless register of a wolf or fox.
  • Run: Similar in being the general word for fast motion; differs because "run" is faster, more urgent, and neutral, while "lope" is unhurried, rhythmic, and animal — distance covered without hurry.
  • Trot: Similar in being a regular, steady gait; differs because a "trot" is the short, brisk, four-beat pace of a horse, less ground-covering, while a "lope" is the longer, looser, easier stride beyond it.
  • Canter: Similar in being an easy three-beat horse gait; differs because a canter is the collected, light pace of riding school, while a lope is the extended, ground-eating, slightly wilder working pace of open country.
  • Scurry: Similar to skelter in quick, hurried motion; differs because "scurry" is the light, quick patter of small feet, while "skelter" adds the note of confusion, disorder, and headlong scatter.
  • Scamper: Similar in running hastily with short quick steps; differs because "scamper" is playful or energetic (a child, a squirrel), while "skelter" is panicky and disorderly — the scatter of fleeing things.
  • Scuttle: Similar in moving hurriedly away; differs because "scuttle" is a sidling, scuttling retreat (a crab, a mouse), while "skelter" is the headlong, legs-in-all-directions rush of a stampede or scatter.
  • Scramble: Similar in disorderly, urgent motion; differs because "scramble" implies climbing or struggling over obstacles, while "skelter" is a flat, horizontal, confused running.
  • Hurry: Similar in moving fast; differs because "hurry" is neutral urgency, purpose-driven, while "skelter" is disorderly panic — speed that has lost its direction.
  • Helter-skelter: Similar as the very compound that contains skelter; differs because "helter-skelter" is the full rhyming jingle for headlong confusion (and the fairground slide), while "skelter" alone is the back-formed verb.
  • Pell-mell: Similar in headlong, confused disorder; differs because "pell-mell" (from Old French pesle-mesle) is a fixed adverb of jumbled haste, while "skelter" is the English expressive verb of the same disorderly motion.
