- 12th to 14th c. — Originated as a Middle High German toponymic demonym (*Wertheim* + *-er*), designating an inhabitant or native of the Franconian town of Wertheim am Main, Germany.
- 17th to 18th c. — Adopted as a prominent hereditary surname by Ashkenazi Jewish families in the Holy Roman Empire, most famously Samson Wertheimer (1658–1724), court factor and chief rabbi of Vienna.
- 1912 — Max Wertheimer published his seminal monograph on the phi phenomenon in Frankfurt, transforming Wertheimer in European academic philosophy and psychology into the personal emblem of Gestalt psychology.
- 1920s to 1930s — Associated across international science with the revolutionary laws of perceptual grouping (Wertheimer's Gestalt laws) and the Berlin School of experimental psychology.
- 1933 to 1945 — Following Wertheimer's flight from the Nazi regime to New York, the name became deeply tied to the "University in Exile" at the New School for Social Research and the intellectual diaspora of European scholars.
- Post-war to 21st c. — Permanently enshrined in global psychology, cognitive science, philosophy of mind, and design pedagogy as the foundational pioneer of anti-reductionist cognitive holism.
