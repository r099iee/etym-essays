Single linear derivation chain for RAPTURES (Latin/Romance line, with the plural as the final form):
  • PIE *rep- (or *reup- / *rāp-), "to snatch, seize, tear away, carry off" (c. 4000 BCE) — a root of violent grasping; its reflexes are debated in exact form but its meaning is secure.
  • Proto-Italic *rap- "to seize, snatch" (c. 1500–1000 BCE).
  • Latin rapere, "to seize, snatch, drag off, carry off by force" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; Plautus uses it of abduction and pillage).
  • Latin raptus, past participle of rapere, "seized, carried off" (c. 1st c. BCE); also the noun raptus, -ūs "a seizing, an abduction."
  • Late / Medieval Latin raptura, "a seizing, a carrying away; (in Christian Latin) the rapture or transport of the soul" (c. 4th–12th c., built on the supine stem rapt- of rapere).
  • Middle French rapture, "violent seizure; ecstatic transport, rapture" (16th c.), borrowed into English.
  • English rapture, "a carrying off by force" (c. 1600, first in theological context), then "spiritual ecstasy" (17th c.), then any intense delight (17th–18th c.).
  • English plural raptures (17th c.) — "bursts of ecstatic delight," the form used in "in raptures"; our word.
NUANCE NOTES: (1) The exact PIE root shape is debated — Pokorny lists *rep-, and some scholars compare *reup- ("to break, tear"); the Italic and Latin line is secure, so the debate concerns only the deepest reconstructed node. (2) The spelling/pronunciation French rapture (with the Latin -t- restored) was borrowed into English; English rapture and French rapture are direct loans, not independent derivatives. (3) The modern capitalised theological "Rapture" (believers caught up at the end of time) is a 19th-century revival of the medieval mystical sense, directly continuing the Latin rapere image of being seized and carried off. (4) raptures is grammatically a plural but in "in raptures" behaves as an uncountable plural formula.
