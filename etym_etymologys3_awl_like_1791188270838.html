Part 1 — awl: a Stone-Age point that never changed. The noun awl is among the oldest tool-words in English, denoting a piercing spike that predates metal.

  • Prehistory and PIE: Piercing implements of bone and flint are Palaeolithic. Linguistically awl continues Proto-Germanic *ēlō (also *alō) "awl, piercing tool," attested in Old Norse alr, Middle Dutch aelse, aal, Old High German ala. Deeper PIE origin is debated: often referred to PIE *h₁el- / *ela- "to drive, pierce, pointed instrument" or *h₂eHl- "awl," cognate with Latin ala? No — Latin subula (awl, from suere to sew) is unrelated, showing independent innovation. Some connect Greek aulos (tube, flute) via hollow bone awls. The OED notes ultimate pre-Germanic origin uncertain; we state the Germanic stage as secure and the PIE link as *el- "sharp point" with debate flag.
  • Old English (c. 700-1100): æl, awel, eal "awl, bodkin" — a cobbler's and saddler's staple for boring stitch-holes in leather that needles cannot pierce. Spelling varied because initial æ- + w glide shifted. Glosses render Latin subula as æl.
  • Middle English (1100-1500): aul, alle, awl with loss of inflection; Chaucerian saddlers and shoemakers wield alles. The tool diversifies: brad-awl for wood, scratch-awl for marking, saddler's awl with interchangeable needles.
  • Early Modern stabilisation: Spelling awl fixes (homophonous with all in many accents, distinguished in RP by vowel length/quality). Shakespeare puns on awl/all ("I am but an ass, and thou an awl"?). Industrial catalogues list dozens of awl types; phonologically initial /ɔː/ from OE æ + w vocalisation.
  • Modern survival: Despite power drills, the hand awl persists in leatherwork, bookbinding (awl for sewing signatures), and Barbier's slate — an awl-like punch. Semantic core never drifted: always a hand-held piercing spike.

Part 2 — -like: from body to simile.

  • PIE body-root: Suffix -like continues Old English -lic, from Proto-Germanic *-lika- "having the form of," from *lika- "body, form, appearance" (cf. German -lich, Dutch -lijk), ultimately from PIE *leik- / *leyk- "form, likeness, image" (also source of English lich, lich-gate, lichas? and like adjective/verb). Originally gelic meant "having the same body/shape."
  • Old English adjective: gelic "like, similar" (with prefix ge- "with, together") — Christes lic = Christ-like. The prefix wore off: gelic → ilic → like (c.1200). As suffix -lic formed adjectives (eorþlic earthly, freondlic friendly).
  • Middle English split: Free word like (preposition, verb to like from "to be pleasing/suitable") diverges from bound -ly (as in friendly) and hyphenated -like (as in childlike, godlike). The hyphenated form retains literal resemblance sense, while -ly drifts to manner.
  • Modern productivity: -like is fully productive: any noun can take it (shell-like, wave-like, awl-like). Style guides prescribe hyphen after short stems or to avoid doubling l (shell-like not shelllike). Semantics is hedging simile: X-like = similar to X in relevant respects, not identical.
  • Compound awl-like (19th-21st c.): First frequent in natural-history floras and tool catalogues describing substitutes: awl-like leaves, awl-like stylus. No sound change beyond compounding stress (primary on awl). The form exemplifies Germanic compounding meeting Latinate subulate as learned synonym.

Together awl-like joins a Neolithic tool-root with a body-image suffix to make an instantly intelligible hedge: not an awl, but sharing its piercing point.

Deep dive — why awl-like matters in the Braille story and in science prose. Barbier's slate is a hinged metal frame holding paper over shallow cups; the poinçon (punch) is literally a short awl: turned wooden handle, 1-2 mm blunted steel point. Pressing from verso creates raised dots on recto. Describing it as awl-like rather than awl is precise — a cobbler's awl would tear, while the stylus is blunter, for embossing not piercing through. Nineteenth-century catalogues list poinçon, stylet, alène (awl) side by side, showing the family resemblance. In botany the same hedge-function operates: awl-like (subulate) leaves of Juniperus communis, awl-like spines of Echinocactus, awl-like teeth of Esox — the reader instantly visualises a cross-section (broad base, steadily tapering, rigid) without needing Latin. Corpus data show awl-like peaking in floras (1840-1920) and craft manuals, then stabilising as a low-frequency but persistent technical simile, unlike ephemeral poetic compounds.

  • Key attestations: Old English glosses (Ælfric, æl = subula); Chaucer's saddlers; Shakespearean pun awl/all; 19th-c. tool catalogues (Mathieson, Stanley); Hooker floras (awl-like leaves); Barbier/Braille slates (1820s-present).
  • Sound recap: OE æl /æːl/ → ME aul /au̯l/ → ModE /ɔːl/ (RP) with w-vocalisation and lengthening; gelic /jeˈliːtʃ/ → like /laɪk/ via palatal g loss and Great Vowel Shift; compound stress ÁWL-like with secondary on -like.

Thus the hyphen joins deep time (Neolithic piercing) with a universal hedging device (body-likeness) to yield a modest, durable, instantly intelligible adjective — the opposite of obscure jargon, yet fully at home in keys, dig reports, and disability histories.

Note on compounding: modern style prefers hyphenated awl-like attributively (an awl-like stylus) and predicatively (the stylus is awl-like), reserving closed awllike as a typo and spaced awl like for clausal simile (like an awl). The form remains fully productive and transparent to native readers.
