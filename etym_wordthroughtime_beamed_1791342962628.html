- Old English (c. 800–1000) “tree, cross, pillar of light” - *bēam* meant a living tree or squared timber; biblical translations of the pillar of fire (*fýrbēam*) and sun rays (*sunnebēam*) introduced the concept of light shafts.
- Middle English (c. 1200–1400) “rafters and radiant shining” - the noun solidified as an architectural timber beam (*beem*); the weak verb *bemen* developed, meaning to shine brightly or emit rays of light.
- 16th–18th c. “optical and solar rays” - poetry and drama firmly established beams as concentrated rays of sun, moon, or divine grace descending from the heavens upon mortal earth.
- Mid-19th c. (Victorian era) “the radiant human smile” - novelists like Charles Dickens popularized the metaphorical extension of beaming to human faces, describing benevolent gentlemen and proud mothers smiling with sunny radiance.
- Early 20th c. “drawing-room geniality and radio transmission” - Wodehouse and contemporaries adopted “beamed genially” as the hallmark of upper-class amiability; simultaneously, radio engineers began “beaming” directional wireless signals.
- Mid-20th c.–present “science fiction, lasers, and modern usage” - *Star Trek* popularized matter teleportation (“beam me up”); particle physics and laser technology adopted beam lines; while the emotional sense remains ubiquitous in everyday literature.
