The story of paradigm is the story of a Greek compound that rose, over two and a half millennia, from a schoolroom term for an example to one of the most powerful intellectual slogans of the modern age. Its oldest recoverable ancestor is the Indo-European root *deyk-, meaning 'to point out, show, indicate'. The root is attested across the family: Sanskrit disati 'points, shows', Avestan daes- 'to show, teach', Latin dicere 'to say, declare' (with index, digitus 'finger' — the pointer — and iudex 'judge', the one who points out the law), and Greek deiknumi 'I show'. The primitive image is the hand extended toward an object, the finger that singles out; from 'point at' the root slid to 'show, teach, declare', and the Latin branch specialised toward speech — dicere 'to say' — while the Greek branch kept the visual act of showing.

Greek built the compound paradeiknumi, 'to show side by side' — para, 'beside', plus deiknumi, 'I show' — and from it formed the noun paradeigma, 'that which is shown alongside'. The core image is pedagogical: the teacher holds one perfect specimen beside the learner's attempts so the comparison is visible. Aristotle uses paradeigma in the Rhetoric for an example adduced as evidence, a paradigm of reasoning by analogy; Plato uses it of the ideal Form of which earthly things are imperfect copies, and of the divine model from which the cosmos is made. For centuries of Greek and Roman intellectual life, the word meant exactly this: an example, a model, a pattern shown for imitation — in grammar, in rhetoric, in philosophy. Latin simply borrowed it as paradigma, a learned word of the schools, and the Romance and English traditions took it from there. In grammar the word found its most durable technical home: a paradigm is the complete set of inflected forms of a noun or verb displayed as a model — mensa, mensae, mensae, mensam — the pattern of endings held beside the learner's own forms.

Sound and transmission:
  • Greek paradeigma to Latin paradigma (a straight learned borrowing; Latin kept the Greek shape, respelling -eigma as -igma).
  • Latin to Old French paradigme to Middle English paradigme (1483). English initially spelled it paradigme; the final -e was dropped and the modern spelling paradigm settled in the 16th–17th centuries.
  • Pronunciation: Greek paradeigma (par-A-deig-ma) to English /'paeradaim/, with the stress pulled forward onto the first syllable and the Greek -deigm- reduced to -digm-.

For roughly four centuries in English, paradigm lived unobtrusively in three learned corners — rhetoric (an example adduced as proof), grammar (the declension model), and philosophy (Plato's ideal Form) — and ordinary speakers rarely met it. Its modern explosion began in 1962, when Thomas Kuhn published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. Kuhn gave the word a new and far larger job: a paradigm is the entire constellation of beliefs, values, methods, and shared exemplars that governs a scientific community, the framework within which normal science is done — and a 'paradigm shift' is the revolution in which one such constellation is abandoned for another. The book was one of the most influential academic works of the century, and 'paradigm shift' escaped the seminar room to become a headline phrase for any fundamental change — in politics, business, and popular culture.

Semantic drift and social factors:
  • Ancient to 19th century: a modest scholarly word for an example or model.
  • 1962 (Kuhn): relaunched as the worldview of a scientific community; 'paradigm shift' becomes a slogan.
  • 1960s onward: the word floods into management, technology, and journalism as a prestige term for any approach, framework, or school of thought — programming paradigms, business paradigms, a paradigm for living.
  • Modern register: ubiquitous and, in careless use, debased — critics call it overworked jargon, while the precise scientific sense and the old grammar sense both remain alive.

The final stage, PARADIGM, is thus a word both enriched and diluted by fame: born as a schoolroom pattern 'shown beside' for comparison, it became the emblem of whole worldviews, while its oldest sense — the convenient model, the premonitory paradigm that Quirk politely declines to offer — survives quietly in scholarly prose, waiting for a careful reader.
