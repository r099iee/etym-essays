The story of thee is bound up with the most stable word in the whole Indo-European family: the second-person singular pronoun, which goes back to Proto-Indo-European *túh₂, the "you" addressed to exactly one person. That ancient form survives with astonishing fidelity across the branches — Sanskrit tvám, Greek sý, Latin tū, Old Irish tú, Old Church Slavonic ty, Lithuanian tù, and Proto-Germanic *þū (the ancestor of English thou). Where the nominative proved nearly immortal, the objective case followed its own quieter path, and it is that path — plus an extraordinary social drama that unfolded in early modern England — that gives thee its modern flavour. Sound change from PIE to Old English.
  • From the oblique stem *twé ("thee") the Germanic branch built its accusative with the emphatic particle *-ke / *-ge, giving Proto-Germanic *þek; the same *-k mark appears in Gothic þuk, Old Norse þik, Old Saxon thīk, and Old English þēc, so the Germanic "thee" is recognizably marked with that emphatic consonant, unlike its Romance and Indo-Iranian cousins.
  • Grimm's Law shifted the initial PIE *t- to Germanic *þ-, so *túh₂ became *þū and the oblique *twé began as *þw-; the modern voiced pronunciation /ðiː/ reflects the regular voicing of initial consonants in unstressed function words during the Middle and Early Modern English period, so that today thee begins with voiced th rather than the voiceless one of thin.
  • Old English inherited both the long emphatic accusative þēc and the shorter þē, which served dative and, increasingly, accusative functions; by Middle English the two had fused into a single object pronoun spelled the(e) or þe, and that is the form that has come down to us.
The social drama: T/V and the retreat of thee. In Old and Middle English, thou and thee were the ordinary singular pronouns, while ye and you served the plural. From the Norman period onward, you also functioned as the polite singular modelled on French vous — the "T/V" distinction made famous by the sociolinguists Brown and Gilman. During the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the polite you systematically invaded the singular, so that thou/thee became marked: the pronoun of intimacy between lovers, of address to God in prayer, of affection toward children, of condescension from master to servant, and of deliberate contempt — a whole etiquette of pronouns. This is why Shakespeare's characters switch between you and thee to register anger, tenderness, or social distance, and why the King James Bible addresses God with thee. Literary attestations.
  • King James Bible (1611): "The Lord is my shepherd... he leadeth me"; "Woe unto thee, Chorazin!"; the language of prayer relies on thee as its reverent object form.
  • Shakespeare (late 16th–early 17th c.): Juliet's "Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" and the famous intimacy of "I do love thee" exploit the T/V charge of the pronouns.
  • John Milton, Paradise Lost (1667): the opening invocation addresses the heavenly Spirit — "Delight thee more, and Siloa's Brook that flow'd / Fast by the Oracle of God" — where thee carries both intimacy with the divine and the high seriousness of epic.
  • Romantic and later poetry: Wordsworth, Coleridge, and the Victorians revived thee as a deliberately archaic, tender, or sacred form, sometimes in artificial "poetic diction".
  • Quaker tradition: from the 1650s the Society of Friends (George Fox and successors) used thee as "plain speech" to express spiritual equality and resistance to flattering hierarchy; the practice survives in some Quaker communities.
  • Dialect: Yorkshire and Lancashire speech retained thee (and subject tha) as the everyday second-person singular into modern times, so that thee is simultaneously the most ancient and the most locally alive pronoun in English.
Semantic and sociolinguistic conclusion. The meaning of thee — "you, singular, as object" — never drifted; what changed is who may say it and to whom. Once the unmarked pronoun of all speech, it was displaced by the universal you and took refuge in four niches: worship, poetry, egalitarian religious practice, and regional dialect. In Milton's line the word still carries the full ancient weight of the direct address to God, so that "Delight thee more" is not merely archaic — it is theologically precise, using the singular object pronoun of the divine presence.
