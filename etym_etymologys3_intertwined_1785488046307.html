Part I - the prefix inter-: from "in" to "between". The first half of intertwined is the Latin prefix inter-, and its own history reaches back to the oldest layers of the Indo-European language family. The PIE base *h₁en. The story begins with the Proto-Indo-European preposition and adverb *h₁en, "in". This is the same root that gives English in, German in, Latin in, and Greek en, "in". In its simplest form it named being inside or within something - the basic, bodily relation of a thing inside a container, a person inside a house, an arrow in a wound. It is one of the most stable roots of the family, surviving recognisably in almost every Indo-European language. The comparative extension *h₁enter. From *h₁en, speakers built an extended form *h₁enter by adding the suffix *-ter, a contrastive or comparative element found across the family (the same element appears in *h₂ent-, "front", the source of Latin ante, "before", and in other locational formations). The result, *h₁enter, meant "between, among" - the space of being in the middle of things rather than merely inside one thing. The semantic step is natural and observable in many languages: "in" the midst of a group is to be "between" its members. The extension changed the simple containment of *h₁en into the relational, reciprocal sense of standing among. The reflexes of *h₁enter. The extended root left clear traces across the family. In Greek, while en (en) kept the simple sense "in", the derivative enteron (enteron), "intestine", literally means "what lies within" - the innards - and preserves the extended root with the "in" sense. In Indo-Iranian, Sanskrit antar (antar), "between, within", and its adjective antara (antara), "inner, interior", survive into modern Hindi अंतर (antar), "difference, interval" - the space between things - and into Odia ଅନ୍ତର (antara), "interval, distance". In Italic the form settled as Latin inter, "between, among", a preposition of enormous range and importance in the classical language. Inter in Latin literature. In Latin, inter ran through every register from the farm to the forum: inter vias, "between the roads"; inter se, "among themselves"; inter arma, "in the midst of arms". Its compounds - intercedere, "to come between", intercludere, "to shut off between", interponere, "to place between", interpretari, "to stand between and explain" - all carried the sense of placing oneself between, standing in the midst, or acting mutually. The root of the prefix in every case is the relational "between" of *h₁enter. Latin inter and English inter-. English borrowed inter wholesale from Latin as the living prefix inter-, which it has used for centuries to build new words: international, intervene, interlock, interact, interchange, interest, interrupt, intercede, interpret - and, our word, intertwine. The prefix contributes two related ideas: a locational one, "between, among", and a reciprocal one, "mutually, one with another". In intertwine it is the reciprocal force that matters: to intertwine is to twist between each other, each strand acting on the other. The prefix has no motion of its own; it only directs the action of the verb it attaches to. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the prefix remains fully productive - interface, internet, interactive, interdisciplinary - and in every new word the ancient "between" of *h₁enter is alive. Cultural note. Because inter- was borrowed wholesale from Latin into the learned vocabulary of English, it belongs to the international stratum of the language - the words of science, law, diplomacy and scholarship that English shares with French, Italian, Spanish and German. In intertwine it is grafted, significantly, onto a purely Germanic stem, and that graft - a Latin prefix on an English verb - is exactly the kind of mixed formation the English language produces with ease, and which marks the word's two-sided ancestry: one half international and learned, the other half native and homely. It is fitting that a word meaning "twisted together" should itself be a joining of two strands of the language. Part II - twine: from "two" to "twisted thread". The second half of intertwined, twine, is Germanic to the bone, and its history begins with the Indo-European word for the number two. The PIE root *dwóh₁. The story starts with the Proto-Indo-European cardinal *dwóh₁, "two". This is one of the most widespread roots in the entire family. In Hellenic it gave Greek duo (duo), "two"; in Indo-Iranian, Sanskrit dva (dva), "two", whence Hindi दो (do) and Odia ଦୁଇ (dui); in Italic, Latin duo, "two", ancestor of English dual, duet and double. In every branch the root means simply "two". From "two" to "double" to "thread". In the Germanic branch the root took on the meaning "double, twofold" in the combining form *twi-. Old English twa, "two", is the direct ancestor of English two; Old English twin, "double, twofold", gives English twin, a doubled child; and Old English twin, "twisted thread", is literally "a two-fold strand" - a thread made of threads, twisted from two strands. The logic is precise and tells us something about ancient technology: a twine is a thread that is double, because twisting requires at least two strands. The word family around twine is therefore an example of numeral-to-noun derivation: the name of the thing encodes how it is made. The verb and the noun in English. From the noun twin grew a verb, Old English twinan, "to twist, to twine", and both noun and verb came down into English: twine the noun, "a strong thread or cord made by twisting strands together", and twine the verb, "to twist, to wind about". The noun preserves the old two-strand technology - sailors, weavers and gardeners have always worked with twine - while the verb preserves the motion: to twine is to twist one thing about another. The material word is deeply embedded in the crafts of everyday medieval life, which is why it survived the Norman Conquest and the flood of French loanwords: twine was the ordinary word of the rope-maker, the weaver and the gardener. The compound intertwine (1640s). English began combining inter- with twine in the mid-seventeenth century: the first recorded use of the verb intertwine is from 1640, and the participle intertwined, "twisted together", followed at once. The new compound meant literally "to twist between each other", and it was used first of concrete things - threads, cords, vines, branches, hair - the physical world of twisting fibres and growing plants. Metaphorical ascent. From ropes and vines the word passed, by a steady metaphorical climb, to anything that stands in the relation of strands. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries intertwined was in common use for lives, fates, destinies, fortunes and families: two houses whose fortunes are intertwined cannot rise or fall separately. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word reached the intellectual and technical registers: intertwined arguments, intertwined theories, intertwined histories; in the twentieth century, intertwined strands of DNA, intertwined codebases, intertwined supply chains, intertwined geopolitical interests. In every case the word insists on a delicate balance: the strands are distinct, and yet they cannot be pulled apart without damage to both. The word in the present passage. Our passage uses the word with full analytical precision: the theories that informed critical approaches to poetics are "partly distinct, partly intertwined", and rhythm and metre are likewise "partly distinct and partly intertwined". The word is exactly right for a pair of concepts that are two things and one thing at once - each still itself, yet one cannot be moved without the other. It is the gentlest and most organic of the words of combination, and it is the perfect image for the mutual entanglement of rhythm and metre that the book sets out to explore. Conclusion. The two halves of intertwined could hardly be more different in origin: a Latin prefix of position, descended from the PIE word for "in", and a Germanic noun and verb of weaving, descended from the PIE word for "two". Their marriage in the 1640s joined the international and the native strata of English, and produced a word whose physical image - two strands twisted between each other - has carried it from the weaver's craft to the DNA double helix and the analysis of poetics. Both halves keep their ancient meanings alive: inter- the "between" of *h₁enter, and twine the "two-fold strand" of *dwóh₁.
