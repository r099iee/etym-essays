- 16th c. - English borrows candelabra as the Latin neuter plural of candelabrum (a branched candle-stand); it enters as a strictly plural, learned, ceremonial word of classical antiquity.
- 17th–18th c. - the word serves churches and grand houses as a plural for branched candle supports; it keeps its Latinate, ritual ring, far above the plain "candle-stand".
- 19th c. - the grammatical accident: because the form ends in -a like feminine singulars (umbrella, arena), speakers begin treating candelabra as a singular noun; a back-formation, candelabras, becomes its new plural.
- 20th c. - candelabra as a singular is standard; it descends from the altar into the domestic world, a silver table centrepiece for dinners and weddings, while keeping its faintly gothic, ceremonial glow.
- 20th–21st c. - electric light strips the candelabra of utility and leaves it pure atmosphere: candlelit rituals, film-noir menace, festive grandeur; the word now conjures an antique, ritual, slightly Byronic romance.
