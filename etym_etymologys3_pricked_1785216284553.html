Pricked is the preterite and past participle of prick, a verb recorded in Old English as prician — "to prick, sting, pierce" — beside the related noun prica, "a point, puncture, prickle, sting." Both descend from a Proto-Germanic base *prik- denoting a pointed object, attested across the West Germanic dialect area: Middle Dutch pricken and Dutch prikken both mean "to prick," as does Low German pricken. Beyond Germanic, however, the trail goes cold. The OED treats the family as of uncertain origin, and modern philologists generally regard it as an expressive — probably echoic — formation whose sharp pr- onset mimics the sudden jab of a point rather than a true Indo-European inheritance. Older attempts to attach the word to a reconstructed PIE root such as *prek- have not won acceptance, and no Romance or Indo-Aryan cognates can be demonstrated. The family is genuinely Germanic-only.

Within English the senses develop steadily outward from the physical act of piercing. Middle English prikken meant "to stab, sting, pierce," and by the sixteenth century the transferred senses had grown: to prick a horse was to urge it on with the spur, and from that spurring came the general sense "to incite, goad, spur to action" — a man pricked forward by ambition, a heart pricked by compassion. The moral sense arrived through the same image: a "prick of conscience" is a sudden, stabbing pang of guilt, the sharp point turned inward. In gardening the word took a gentle technical sense — to prick out seedlings is to lift them with a pointed dibber — and in botany the noun named the thorns and spines of plants, the prickles that defend a rose or a thistle.

The decisive moment for the modern idiom came in the 1590s. It was then that the phrase "to prick up one's ears" first appears in English, describing dogs, horses, and hares raising their ears to listen. The image is exquisitely physical: the animal is imagined "pricking" its ears upright exactly as a needle makes a point rise from cloth — the sharp ear stands like a tiny spike, alert and ready. The phrase belonged first to the hunting-lodge and the kennel, the language of sportsmen watching a hound catch a scent or a horse take alarm at a distant noise. From that hunting vocabulary it migrated into general English in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, so that by the nineteenth century any sudden, sharpened attention could be described as pricking up one's ears — a listener catching his own name, a gossip catching a secret, a miser catching the chink of gold.

Tolkien's use of pricked in The Hobbit leans precisely on this history. "Bilbo pricked up his ears": the idiom lends the comfortable, homely hobbit a hunter's reflexive alertness, and the sharp, pointed physics buried in the Old English verb resurfaces in the image — attention itself becomes a needle standing suddenly upright. The word has never lost its coarse physical shadow: the vulgar slang noun "prick" keeps the old English earthiness alive in the low register, while the verb continues in the literal sense of piercing, the spurring sense of inciting, the moral sense of a guilty pang, and the vivid idiomatic sense of sudden attention. From the expressive Germanic jab of *prik- to the hound's raised ear to the hobbit's sharpened instinct, the word has carried a single image for over a thousand years: the sudden, pointed, unmistakable sign.
