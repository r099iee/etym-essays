1. The prehistoric stem: *sleb- and *swep-. The English family of sleep has a purely Germanic core, *slēpan-, attested in Gothic slepan, Old Saxon slāpan, Old High German slāfan (modern German schlafen) and Old English slǣpan. Outside Germanic the stem has no certain relatives; the most-cited proposal is a PIE root *sleb- glossed 'to be weak, languid, exhausted' — sleep as the state of a body that has spent its strength — but the OED is explicit that the pre-Germanic history is uncertain, and the reconstruction must be flagged as debatable. A striking fact about the Germanic vocabulary is that North Germanic does not share the stem at all: Old Norse sofa (Danish sove, Swedish sova) descends from a quite different root, *swep- (cf. Sanskrit svápiti, Latin somnus, Greek hýpnos). Old English, strikingly, knew both families: ordinary prose used slǣpan, while the poets could reach for the archaic inherited swefan — a living echo of two prehistoric streams within one language.

2. Old English: the everyday verb and its endings. In Old English slǣpan ('to sleep') was an old strong verb of class VII (preterite singular slēp, plural slēpon), and its third-person singular present appeared in two shapes: the older, syncopated slǣpþ (like dēþ 'does', gǣþ 'goes') and the younger, unsyncopated slǣpeþ with the regular strong-verb ending -eþ. The ending itself descends through Proto-Germanic *-iþi from the PIE primary verbal ending *-eti (cf. Sanskrit -ti, Greek -ei, Latin -t). Dialectal play was already at work in Anglo-Saxon England; the ending that would become the famous '-eth' of the Bible was originally just the standard West Saxon and Anglian present ending of most verbs.

3. Middle English: the great split. After the Norman Conquest the third-person present ending diverged dialectally: the north and the East Midlands generalized -es/-s (the triumphant -s is itself of debated origin — it has been linked to Scandinavian contact and to the syncopated northern forms of the Old English ending — and its slow conquest of the south is one of the great unremarked revolutions of English grammar). London English of the fourteenth century, Chaucer's English, still used -eth routinely, and the print standard established by Caxton carried -eth into the sixteenth century. A medieval reader meeting 'slepeth' therefore heard nothing unusual — it was grammar, not style.

4. Early Modern English: scripture fixes the form. William Tyndale's New Testament (1526), the base of every later English Bible, chose the conservative -eth system, and the King James Version (1611) confirmed it. In that register SLEEPETH received its definitive cultural charge: John 11:11 — 'Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep' — and Psalm 127:2 — 'for so he giveth his beloved sleep' — made the verb's archaic shape the sound of Christian consolation. Shakespeare exploited the same resources, leaning on -eth wherever metre or solemnity demanded it, while Elizabethan normal speech shed the form: scholarship on Early Modern English morphology tracks the -s ending sweeping from the north and from colloquial prose into all spoken varieties during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, so that by 1700 'sleepeth' was already a literary relic.

5. Afterlife: from quiet usage to deliberate archaism. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries kept 'sleepeth' alive on tombstones ('here sleepeth…', a genuine epigraphic formula), in hymnals and in the pulpit; the Victorian historical novel revived it for period colour. In the twentieth century fantasy literature — above all Tolkien — deliberately rebuilt the KJV register for invented ancient peoples, and modern writers reaching for solemnity, irony or comedy still generate 'sleepeth' today. Pronunciation has fossilized along with the form: the -eth is ordinarily read /ɪθ/ or /əθ/, a reading preserved from Early Modern usage. The result is a form that travelled from everyday grammar to sacred diction to a living literary archaism — a full rotation of register in a thousand years.
