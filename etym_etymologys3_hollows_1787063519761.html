hollows belongs to one of the oldest and most ramified word-families in the Germanic languages. The noun comes down from Old English holh (also holg), "a cave, den, hollow place, or internal cavity", and the family also produced Old English hol, "a hollow, an opening", which is our modern hole. Both descend from the Proto-Germanic adjective and noun stem *hul-, "hollow", a form ultimately traced by most scholars to the Proto-Indo-European root *kel-, "to cover, conceal, save". The reasoning is that a hollow is, at bottom, a covered or enclosed space — the cave is a place the earth hides. The same Indo-European root lies behind hell (the hidden place), hall (a covered building), cell, cellar, and Latin occultus, "hidden".

The noun and the adjective grew together. In Old English, holh was a noun, while the adjective hol meant "hollow, concave". As Middle English developed, the noun holowe/holow and the adjective hollow converged, and by the fourteenth century the word meant a scooped-out or sunken place of any kind — a pit, a depression in the ground, a cavity in a tree, a gap in a hillside. From the earliest records the word carried both a protective connotation (a den, a refuge, a sheltered valley) and an emptiness connotation (a thing with nothing inside), and that double character has stayed with the family ever since.

  • Proto-Indo-European (*kel-): "to cover, conceal" — reconstructed; also the source of hell, hall, cell, and cellar.
  • Proto-Germanic (*hul-): "hollow; covered or hidden place" — the stem of both hole and hollow.
  • Old English (holh, 8th–11th c.): "cave, den, hollow place" — the noun sense of a sheltered, excavated place in the earth.
  • Middle English (holow, 13th–14th c.): "cavity, depression, hollowed-out place" — the sense generalises beyond caves to any sunken place.
  • Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): the noun solidifies its topographic sense — a low basin or small valley — while the adjective sense "empty inside" flourishes and takes on moral colouring (a hollow heart, a hollow promise).
  • Modern English: hollows is used for land depressions, bodily cavities, and interior spaces, and the adjectival and verb senses ("hollowed out", "to hollow") remain fully alive.

Phonology: The development holh > holow > hollow shows Middle English regularisation of the final cluster: the Old English -lh sequence was simplified, and the word settled into the shape hol-ow, with the modern disyllable hollow and plural hollows. The same root with different endings produced hole (with a lengthened vowel), while holly, the plant name, is an unrelated word of different origin.

Literary and cultural resonance: The word has always been at home in landscape and folklore. "Hollow hills" and hidden dales are staples of fairy-tale geography; the American "holler" — the secluded mountain valley — kept the word's most rural sense alive across the Atlantic; and Washington Irving's The Legend of Sleepy Hollow (1820) made one hollow famous as a place of drowsy, haunted seclusion. In the source text, Tolkien's dwarves hear Smaug's rage "echoing in the stony hollows far above": the noun's ancient sense of excavated, echoing, sheltering-and-menacing spaces is pressed into full service, showing how durable this small word has been across more than a thousand years of English.
