- 1660s — English borrows French gentil in jocular spellings such as jantee and janty, meaning “elegant, stylish”; the word is fashionable, Frenchified, and slightly affected.
- 1670s — the sense shifts from stylish appearance to “easy and sprightly in manner,” and the modern meaning takes hold.
- 18th c. — jaunty can still mean showy or finical, and it is often used of dress and bearing with a hint of mockery.
- 19th c. — the word settles into its modern positive sense of cheerful confidence, common in fiction and description.
- 20th c. — it becomes a favourite in comic and journalistic prose (Wodehouse’s narrators wave jaunty hands), signalling brisk, unforced high spirits.
- 21st c. — it remains a standard descriptive word, warm and slightly old-fashioned, with a faint permissible edge of cockiness.
