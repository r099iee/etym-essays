1. The Indo-European root and the meaning of "weather". Withered is the past participle of wither, and the whole family springs from a root that, remarkably, means "to blow" — the same root that produced weather. Proto-Indo-European had a root *we- / *h₂weh₁- meaning "to blow", which generated a derived noun for wind and atmospheric condition. Through Proto-Germanic this yielded *wedra- ("wind, weather"), the ancestor of Old English weder ("air, sky, wind, weather, storm"), Old Saxon wedar, Old High German wetar, and Old Norse veðr. Cognates across the family include Greek áēmi ("to blow"), Latin ventus ("wind"), and Sanskrit vāti ("it blows"). The semantic core is wind and the atmospheric condition produced by moving air — the agent that dries, or batters, whatever it meets.

2. From "weather" to "wither": the lexical split in Middle English. In Old English, alongside the noun weder, there was a verb wederian meaning "to expose to the weather" and, of wind, "to blow, shake". In Middle English this verb took the form wederen, "to expose to the weather". The crucial development — the birth of a distinct word — came when, by about 1300, the stem vowel changed from e to i, producing widren / witheren, "to dry up, shrivel". The semantic bridge is concrete and vivid: a thing exposed to the weather — grass, leaves, flesh, cloth — is dried out, battered, and shrivelled by wind, sun, and frost. The verb "to weather" and the new verb "to wither" thus split from a single source, each preserving a different facet: weather kept the general sense of exposure to atmospheric conditions and (eventually, as a verb) of surviving them; wither specialised in the drying, shrivelling, and decay that exposure brings about. This split is a textbook case of how a single inherited lexeme can diverge phonologically and semantically into two modern words.

3. The adjectival past participle and the scriptural register. From the later Middle English period the past participle, spelled widred and then withered, began to be used as a fully adjectival form meaning "dried up, shrivelled". The decisive literary fix came with the Bible. The King James Bible of 1611 uses "withered" in some of its most famous verses: "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: because the spirit of the LORD bloweth upon it" (Isaiah 40:7), and the healing of "a man which had his hand withered" (Matthew 12:10). Here "withered" denotes a hand paralysed, shrunken, and wasted — flesh that has dried and lost all strength. Through scripture, "withered" became the archetypal word for lifeless shrinkage, applied to both the botanical and the human realms, and steeped in the Bible's meditation on the frailty and transience of life.

4. Figurative drift in the literary centuries. From the seventeenth century onward the word underwent steady figurative extension. Because drying-up is a natural image for the loss of strength, vigour, and promise, "wither" and "withered" came to describe the decline and death of abstractions: withered hopes, withered affections, withered reputations, withered empires. The great poets seized on it — Milton's images of blasted strength, the Romantics' melancholy over faded beauty and decay, and the Victorian moralists' use of the word for decline and sterility. Tennyson and the Pre-Raphaelites alike employed "wither" for the fading of life and love. This figurative career made "withered" a standard member of the English vocabulary of mortality, alongside "fade", "falter", and "decay", though it keeps a distinctive physical sharpness: to "wither" is to shrink, not merely to decline.

5. Sounds and modern form. Phonetically, the word passed through predictable changes: the Old English long vowel in weder shortened and lowered in Middle English, the stem vowel shifted to the position that produced modern /ɪ/, and the final consonant cluster settled. Modern "wither" is pronounced /ˈwɪðə/ (with the voiced dental fricative), and "withered" /ˈwɪðəd/ — the spelling -er now silent in the modern form's final syllable (an ə). The th spelling continues the Old English þ/ð consonant, which English kept while most continental Germanic languages replaced it.

6. The modern residue. In contemporary English, "withered" has two distinct registers. Literally, it is the standard word in botany, horticulture, and medicine for dried, shrunken, lifeless tissue — withered leaves, withered crops, a withered limb. Figuratively, it remains a favourite in literary and journalistic prose for anything deprived of vitality: withering criticism (criticism that reduces its target to shrinkage), a withering glance, withered hopes, and the idiom "wither on the vine" (to fail before completion, like unharvested grapes drying on the branch). The connection to "weather" is no longer felt by most speakers, yet it remains the invisible thread that explains why the word for "dried up" should literally mean "weathered".
