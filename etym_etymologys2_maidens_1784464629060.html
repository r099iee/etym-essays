PIE *maghu- / *magʰu- "young person, child, unmarried youth" (reconstructed from Germanic and Celtic material; wider Indo-European connections uncertain) [DEBATED — some scholars treat the attestations with caution] → Proto-Germanic *maguz (young person, boy; cf. Gothic magus "boy, servant," Old English magu "son, boy, servant") and *magaþiz (young unmarried woman; cf. Gothic magaths, Old High German magad, Old Norse mær/mey), late 2nd–1st millennium BCE → West Germanic: Old English mægþ (maiden, virgin, young woman), attested from the 8th century CE → Old English mægden / mæden (a young girl, maiden; diminutive of mægþ with the -en/-in- suffix), attested from the 9th century CE → Middle English maiden / meiden (unmarried young woman; virgin), attested from the 12th century CE → Modern English maiden (an unmarried young woman; as adjective "first, unused"), hence the plural MAIDENS of the card. NOTE: the short form maid is a later aphetic variant that specialized toward "female servant"; maiden itself kept the literary young-woman/virgin line. German Magd shows the same West Germanic stem drifting toward "servant." The PIE root's wider connections (sometimes linked to a root meaning "ability, might") are uncertain.
