A word built twice over. Besiegers is a triple-layer word: a plural agent noun (-s, -er) formed on a verb that is itself a prefix-formation on a borrowed noun (be- + siege), which in turn descends from a Latin verb of “sitting”. Its history is therefore the history of three successive operations — Latin derivation, French compounding, English agent-noun morphology — each laying a distinct semantic or phonetic trace on the final form.

Layer one: the Latin sitting-verbs. PIE *sed- “to sit” gives Latin sedēre “to sit”. From it Latin derived the compound assidēre “to sit down by, to sit at or near” (ad- “to, at” + sedēre). The military application appears already in Latin: assidēre is used of armies camping beside and pressing a town — the Roman historians use it of a general “sitting” before a city, in effect besieging it. Late Latin extended the verb into the -āre conjugation as *assediāre, the direct ancestor of the Romance verbs: Old Provençal asetjar, Catalan assetjar, Spanish asediar, Portuguese assediar, Italian assediare, all meaning “to besiege”. The core image remained transparent in Latin and Romance alike: to besiege is to sit oneself down beside the enemy’s walls and stay there.

Layer two: French, with and without the prefix. Old French inherited the verb as assegier (modern assiéger), used in epic and chronicle — the Chanson de Roland and Villehardouin’s chronicle of the Fourth Crusade describe armies “assegier”-ing towns. Alongside the a- form, Anglo-Norman — the French of the English ruling class after 1066 — developed a variant with the Germanic-derived prefix be- (the same prefix as in beget, becalm, beseech): besieger “to besiege”, transparently “be- + sege”. Continental French never adopted this form, and the two dialects therefore went different ways: France said assiéger, England took besieger. This is a textbook case of the English lexicon being furnished from Anglo-Norman specifically — Old French material shaped by the particular dialect of the cross-Channel ruling class.

Layer three: the English verb and the agent noun. Middle English borrowed the Anglo-Norman verb as besegen/besiegen around 1300, meaning “to lay siege to, to surround and blockade”. English then did what English habitually does to borrowed verbs: it built the agent noun with the native suffix -er (“one who”), giving besieger, attested from the 16th century (OED), and the collective plural besiegers. The stress pattern — be-SIEG-ers — reflects the prominence of the French-derived core; the initial be- is unstressed and semantically inert to most speakers today. Phonologically the -ge- of the middle syllable gives /dʒ/, the standard English outcome for French -g- before a front vowel in loanwords (siege, college, refuge), and the plural -s is added in the ordinary way. The agent noun thus condenses in one word the whole chain: the sitters (sed-) who invested (assidēre) a city from France (assegier) for England (besieger).

The word in action: literary and historical career. As a collective plural, “the besiegers” is a staple of narrative history and epic. 14th–15th centuries: the English chronicles of the Hundred Years War — the sieges of Calais, Rouen and Orléans — report the doings of the English and French hosts, where the verbal forms dominate but the conception is already collective. 16th–17th centuries: Shakespeare’s histories dramatise the besiegers of Orléans (1 Henry VI, where Joan of Arc faces the English circumvallation) and of Harfleur (Henry V); the epic diction of Spenser and Milton takes the word up; pamphlet journalism covers the great sieges of the Dutch Revolt and the Thirty Years War. 18th–19th centuries: the great historians fix the word in grand narrative — Gibbon describes the besiegers of Constantinople in 1453, Mehmed’s hosts ringing the Theodosian walls; Scott’s Ivanhoe stages the besiegers of Torquilstone; Macaulay writes of the besiegers of Derry and Limerick; the siege of Sebastopol (1854–55) fills the first war-reportage with “the besiegers” besieging in the Crimea. 20th–21st centuries: world-history narratives (the besiegers of Leningrad, 1941–44; of Sarajevo, 1992–96) and modern journalism adopt the word as a grave, neutral way to name encircling armies; in transferred journalism the besiegers can be suitors, reporters, creditors or a cyber-army.

The Tolkien context. Tolkien’s use in The Hobbit — the host of men and elves encircling the Lonely Mountain, unable to ring the whole mountain because “for that they had not sufficient numbers” — is exactly the medieval-chronicle register the word has carried for seven centuries: the patient, encircling, numerically tested host. The word performs its ancient military duty: it names the armed sitting-down before a place, and the whole history of felt meaning — from Latin assidēre, through Anglo-Norman besieger, to the plural noun — is present in the single sentence.

Semantic summary. Across seven centuries of English the noun has never left the military semantic field: a besieger is defined by encircling pressure and the will to take a place. Its few figurative uses (besiegers of a heart, of privacy, of a reputation) all preserve the image of being surrounded on all sides by a persistent hostile or pressing force. The plural dominates because siegecraft is collective; the singular belongs mostly to romance and poetical metaphor. Register: grave, narrative, historical — never casual.
