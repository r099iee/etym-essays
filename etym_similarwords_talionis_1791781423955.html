• talion: Similar because it is the English noun for the very same principle of identical requital; differs by being the standalone borrowing, extremely rare, with none of the phrase's Roman-law authority.
  • lex talionis: Similar because it is the complete Latin phrase from which the genitive is taken; differs in that Talionis is only one component and cannot stand alone as a natural English noun.
  • retribution: Similar because both concern deserved punishment for a wrong; differs because retribution need not mirror the injury, being proportional rather than identical, and it belongs to moral and penal theory rather than to archaic statute.
  • retaliation: Similar because it is the vernacular translation of talionis; differs by naming the act of the injured party rather than a rule imposed by law, and by lacking the requirement of exact equivalence.
  • revenge: Similar as payback for a wrong; differs because revenge is personal and passionate, whereas the talion is an impersonal rule that limits what revenge may do.
  • vengeance: Similar in meaning and archaic dignity; differs by implying honour, kin loyalty and often unlimited violence, the very thing the talion was designed to cap.
  • reprisal: Similar as a return of harm; differs because reprisals are acts of states or armies against an enemy, not judicial punishments measured against a private injury.
  • requital: Similar because it means repayment of like for like; differs by being neutral as to good or evil, whereas the talion is strictly about harm.
  • reciprocity: Similar in its logic of like for like; differs because reciprocity covers mutual benefit and fair exchange, while the talion is confined to injury and punishment.
  • an eye for an eye: Similar because it is the biblical statement of the same rule; differs as a vernacular formula that emphasises the limit (“no more than an eye”) and is embedded in scriptural law rather than in a Latin maxim.
  • measure for measure: Similar as a phrase of exact correspondence; differs by coming from the Sermon on the Mount's warning against judging, and by being used more often of mercy and irony than of punishment.
