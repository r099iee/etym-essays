In this context. In the sentence “He won high acclaim – to give one outstanding example – for his book on Elizabethan classical verse, Well-weighed Syllables, which was published in 1974”, the word is a cultural-period label doing the work of locating a book in a whole epoch. The reference is Derek Attridge’s landmark study Well-weighed Syllables: Elizabethan Verse in Classical Metres (1974), which examines how English poets of the late sixteenth century tried to compose verse on the quantitative metrical principles of Greek and Latin, measuring syllables by length rather than by stress. Here Elizabethan invokes the reign of Queen Elizabeth I (1558–1603) and, specifically, its literary world — the age of Sidney, Spenser, Marlowe, Jonson and Shakespeare — together with the linguistic self-confidence that made English, in the hands of these writers, a self-conscious rival to the classical tongues. The tone of the surrounding sentence is scholarly and celebratory: “high acclaim” and “outstanding example” mark the book as a landmark of its field, and the adjective does the cultural work of naming an entire golden age in a single word.

Core meaning. At its centre Elizabethan means “of, belonging to, or characteristic of the reign of Queen Elizabeth I of England (1558–1603) and its culture and literature.” It is an adjective derived from the royal personal name Elizabeth by the suffix -an (ultimately Latin -ānus, “belonging to, connected with”). Because it derives from a proper name it is always capitalised. The same pattern produces the other royal-epoch adjectives of English history — Victorian, Edwardian, Jacobean (from James), Carolean or Caroline (from Charles), Georgian — a productive family in which a monarch’s name denominates an age.

Polysemy: the full range of senses. The word is far more than a bare date-stamp. It carries several distinct, historically layered senses:

  • Chronological period label. The years 1558–1603, the latter half of the sixteenth century: a reign that began after Mary I’s death, included the 1559 Elizabethan Religious Settlement that fixed the shape of the Church of England, the long struggle with Spain and the defeat of the Armada in 1588, and the overseas expeditions of Drake and Raleigh. In this sense it designates a political and religious era, not merely a literary one.
  • Literary and cultural label. The high Renaissance of English literature: the sonnet sequences of Sidney and Spenser, Spenser’s Faerie Queene, the flowering of the public and court theatres (Lyly, Kyd, Marlowe, early Jonson, and the first half of Shakespeare’s career), the prose of Hooker and Bacon, the poetry of Donne’s early years, and the music of Byrd, Dowland and Morley — the madrigal and the court consort.
  • Stylistic sense. “In the manner of the age”: applied approvingly or loosely to anything exuberant, ornate, rhetorically elaborate, expansive or word-rich, hence almost a synonym of “Shakespearean” in flavour — an Elizabethan speech, an Elizabethan exuberance, an Elizabethan turn of phrase.
  • Historical-technical sense (the sense in our context). “Elizabethan verse in classical metres” — the quantitative experiments by which poets like Sidney, Harvey, Spenser and Campion tried to scan English by syllable length, the very subject of Attridge’s book. Here the adjective anchors a specific technical debate in poetics.
  • Substantive noun. “An Elizabethan” (plural “the Elizabethans”) — a person who lived in that age, or the age’s people collectively as a cultural type (“the Elizabethans were born showmen”).
  • Modern jargon / metaphoric extension. The “Elizabethan collar” — the wide plastic or fabric cone fitted round a pet’s neck after surgery to stop it licking its wounds — named for the starched, pleated ruffs fashionable at the queen’s court, which the cone absurdly resembles. The historical dress sense survives in this veterinary joke; the semantic bridge is purely visual.
  • Applied senses in the arts and crafts. “Elizabethan” architecture (the timber-framed, gabled manor houses and the great prodigy houses of the gentry), “Elizabethan” furniture (oak, bulbous turned legs, strapwork carving), “Elizabethan” costume (the ruff, the farthingale, the doublet and hose), and “Elizabethan” stage conventions (the soliloquy, the chronicle play, the revenge tragedy, the boy actor).

Semantic bridge. The development from proper name to epoch label is a familiar eponymous shift: because Elizabeth I gave her name to the age, the adjective moved from “belonging to the queen herself” to “belonging to her reign” to “characteristic of that reign’s culture” to “suggestive of that culture’s style”, and finally to a free-floating marker of richness and flamboyance. Nineteenth-century scholarship sealed the process: the Romantic and Victorian rediscovery and canonisation of the period turned the word from a bare chronological term into a banner of golden-age glamour, and that glamour still colours the word today, whether it points at Sidney’s metres or at a bemused cat wearing a plastic ruff.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. Tudor covers the whole dynasty (1485–1603) and is the broader frame, embracing Henry VIII’s Reformation and the mid-century religious crises; Elizabethan names only Elizabeth’s reign and its literary flowering. Jacobean marks the successor reign of James I (1603–1625) — the later Shakespeare, the court masque, Webster and Donne — and is more sombre and ironic in tone. Renaissance spans all of Europe across two centuries, and early modern is the modern historian’s wider, emotionally neutral period label stretching well past 1603. For the world of Attridge’s study — high Elizabethan poetry and its metrical experiments — Elizabethan is the precise, irreplaceable term.
