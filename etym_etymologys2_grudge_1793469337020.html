PIE (reconstructed, c. 3500 BCE)
*gʰru- / *gʰreu-
"to grumble, grunt, make a low throat sound"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
grouchier / grochier
"to grumble, murmur, complain under one's breath"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th c.)
grucchen / grouchen
"to murmur secretly, complain discontentedly"
↓
Middle English (c. 15th c.)
grudgen / grodge
"to grumble at; to yield or give with reluctance"
↓
Modern English (current)
grudge
"to give reluctantly; persistent ill will"

Note: The word is fundamentally of onomatopoeic / sound-imitative origin, mimicking low guttural grumbling in the throat (cognate with Middle High German grogezen "to groan, howl" and Old Norse krutr "a murmur"). In Middle English, the original voiceless affricate ending in grucchen /tʃ/ underwent a phonological split: the voiceless form survived dialectally and evolved into modern grouch, while the voiced affricate variant /dʒ/ produced standard grudge. The semantic trajectory shifted from the physical sound of muttering under one's breath to the psychological attitude of begrudging a gift, and ultimately to the cherished memory of grievance.
