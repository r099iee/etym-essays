Cracked is the past participle and adjective of crack, and in its most everyday physical sense it means split or fractured by a sharp blow, pressure, or stress while still holding together in one piece — a cracked phone screen that still lights up, a cracked cup that still holds tea, cracked ice spreading white veins across a winter pavement, cracked earth gaping after a rainless summer.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses the great human sense of the word: Bilbo, who had always been rather cracked, is judged by his neighbours to have gone quite mad at last — not clinically ill but eccentric past the point of respectability, the village oddball who vanishes without explanation and runs off into the Blue. The neighbourhood verdict turns a hairline fracture of crockery into a hairline fracture of reputation, and the comedy lies in that very transfer from kitchen shelf to character.

Core meaning in detail: Physically, cracked sits precisely between whole and broken: the thing has failed but not fallen apart. A cracked beam still spans the gap, a cracked bell still rings (though off-key), cracked lips still smile. Engineers, doctors, and cooks all use the word for this in-between state — stress fractures in metal, cracked ribs, cracked wheat — where damage is visible but function continues, often painfully or imperfectly. The participle carries the history of a sudden event inside a lasting condition: something cracked (there was a snap, a blow, a frost), and now it is cracked (the line remains to tell the tale).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Split or fractured but still in one piece (primary physical sense, from the 14th c.): a cracked mirror, cracked glaze on pottery, cracked heels. The bridge is the verb crack itself — to break with a sharp snap — frozen by the -ed ending into the lasting state after the snap.
  • Chapped or fissured by dryness or cold (bodily and landscape sense): cracked lips, cracked hands, cracked riverbeds. The bridge runs from sudden breakage to slow splitting: frost and drought pull surfaces apart just as surely as a hammer, and the skin of hands and the skin of the earth crack alike.
  • Harsh, broken, or intermittently failing in sound (of voices and instruments): a cracked voice breaking at puberty, a cracked old bell, cracked notes from a tired singer. The bridge is acoustic: a flawless surface gives a pure tone, a cracked one wavers and splits, so the word for the flaw becomes the word for the faltering sound.
  • Mentally unsound, eccentric, or mad (colloquial, from the 17th c., Tolkien's sense): rather cracked, a cracked inventor, to go cracked. The bridge is the great early-modern metaphor of the mind as a vessel: a cracked pot leaks, a cracked bell jars, a cracked brain or cracked pate lets reason leak out — hence crack-brained, crackpot, and the asylum slang of earlier centuries.
  • Solved, decoded, or forced open by ingenuity (of codes, safes, and systems): a cracked cipher, a cracked password, a cracked safe. The bridge runs from breaking things to breaking into things: the safebreaker's snap and the codebreaker's insight are both openings achieved by force or wit, so detectives and hackers alike speak of cracking.
  • Told or uttered suddenly (of jokes and remarks): he cracked a joke, she cracked wise. The bridge is the snap of a whip: a joke lands like a crack — quick, sharp, startling — and the participle survives in reported speech, as in a joke already cracked at dinner.
  • Opened by splitting (of eggs, bottles, and gatherings): crack open a bottle, get the meeting cracked open, crack an egg into the pan. The bridge extends the fracture to deliberate beginnings: to start the feast you split the shell, so cracked open becomes celebration's overture.
  • Crushed or split into coarse pieces (of grain and foods): cracked wheat, cracked pepper, cracked corn. The bridge is the miller's art: grain deliberately broken between stones cooks faster and grinds easier, and the participle names the honest middle state between whole seed and flour.
  • Split by chemical breaking (petroleum refining, technical): cracked gasoline, catalytic cracking. The bridge carries the kitchen image into the refinery: long hydrocarbon chains are snapped into shorter, more useful ones by heat and catalysts, exactly as a log is cracked for kindling, only at the molecular scale.
  • Excellent or expert (the adjective crack, as in crack troops, a crack shot): strictly a cousin rather than a sense of the participle, but speakers constantly link them. The bridge is the snap itself — sharp, sudden, first-rate — so that what cracks does so with decisive excellence, and the echo colours the participle with admiration even amid breakage.

Register and connotation: cracked is warm, homely, faintly comic English — the kitchen and workshop rather than the laboratory. A cracked cup is loved, not discarded; a cracked old fellow is smiled at, not feared. Even the mad sense stays affectionate in Tolkien's Shire: Bilbo is rather cracked the way a favourite uncle is, and the neighbours' verdict of madness says as much about their narrowness as about his strangeness. Only the refinery and the cryptographer's office make the word cold and technical; everywhere else it keeps the sound of the snap and the sympathy for the thing that snapped yet still serves.
