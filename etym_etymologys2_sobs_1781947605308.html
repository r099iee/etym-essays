Derivation chain for SOBS (single root word, the plural noun formed by conversion from the verb sob): 1. Origin: the verb is not traced to a Proto-Indo-European root; it is generally held to be a Germanic echoic (onomatopoeic) formation, imitative of the convulsive, broken breathing it names. It was probably influenced by Old English seofian "to lament, groan, complain" (attested 9th c.) and is related to the Middle Low German family sabben / sabbeln "to slaver, to mumble" — words for wet, broken, choked sounds. 2. Middle English sobben "to sob" — echoic formation; attested c. 1300 (Cursor Mundi), with the derived sobbinge "sobbing" appearing in the same period. The noun sob is formed from the verb by conversion (no affix). 3. English sob, verb and noun, with the plural SOBS "convulsive gasps made while weeping" — attested from the 14th c. onward, in the plural used of a series of convulsions. Note on the debate: an old idea that sob descends from a Latin word (singultus, "a sob") is rejected; the word is native Germanic and echoic. A proposed Old English *sobb- is unattested, so the documentary record begins in Middle English — a gap that must be stated rather than filled with invention.
