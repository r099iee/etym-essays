• Persuading: Similar because both mean getting someone to do something; differs because persuading is the neutral, reasoned verb (argument and evidence), while coaxing is gentle, patient, and emotional rather than logical.
  • Wheedling: Similar because both use flattery and sweet talk; differs because wheedling implies sly, ingratiating, manipulative flattery for personal advantage, while coaxing is more affectionate and tender.
  • Cajoling: Similar because both persuade with flattery and softness; differs because cajoling often carries a hint of deceit or pressure (from Old French "to chatter like a jay"), while coaxing is gentler and more nurturing.
  • Enticing: Similar because both lure someone toward something; differs because enticing works through the appeal of the goal itself, while coaxing works through the speaker's soothing manner.
  • Luring: Similar because both draw someone in; differs because luring suggests temptation, often with bait and an element of entrapment, while coaxing is softer and more affectionate.
  • Urging: Similar because both encourage action; differs because urging is more forceful, pressing, and insistent, while coaxing is gentle and patient.
  • Inducing: Similar because both cause someone to do something; differs because inducing is neutral and causal, often impersonal (a drug induces sleep), while coaxing is personal and verbal.
  • Soothing: Similar because both calm and comfort; differs because soothing is primarily about calming distress without necessarily requesting action, while coaxing steers toward a desired behaviour.
  • Alluring: Similar because both attract someone toward something; differs because alluring works by the attractiveness of the person or thing itself, while coaxing is verbal persuasion.
  • Prevailing upon: Similar because both mean winning someone over; differs because prevailing upon is more formal and often involves repeated, earnest argument, while coaxing is tender and fondling.
