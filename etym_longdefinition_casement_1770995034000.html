casement is a window, or the frame of a window, that opens by swinging on hinges attached to one of its vertical sides, so that the whole glazed unit pivots outward or inward like a door. In strict architectural terminology, a casement is a window sash carried on side hinges, distinguished from the sliding sash, which moves vertically or horizontally in grooves, and from the fixed window, which does not open at all.

Contextual reading: in this card's passage — the Taoist's verse within Dream of the Red Chamber — 'The cottage casement with choice muslin gleams.' The casement is the old-fashioned window of a poor cottage, dressed in fine muslin curtains, catching the light while the poem contrasts the fleeting elegance of life with cobwebs, abandoned halls, and death. The word carries a nostalgic, domestic, and deliberately archaic resonance: it evokes an older style of building in which windows were hinged frames rather than sliding sashes.

Core senses:

  • Primary sense — the hinged window: a window attached by hinges along one side and swinging open, the standard type in most of Europe and the world; the term is still current, especially in British English, in building and joinery.
  • Historical sense — the frame or casing: in earlier use (15th–17th centuries) a casement was the frame, case, or enclosure of a window opening, whether or not the glass itself was hinged; the modern sense is a narrowing of this.
  • Technical extension — casement hardware: 'casement stay,' 'casement bolt,' 'casement window,' and 'casement curtain' are the standard collocations of the building and furnishing trades.
  • Military sense — casemate (related but distinct): in fortification, a casemate is a vaulted chamber sunk in a rampart from which guns fire through embrasures; the words share the idea of an enclosing case, and the forms are often confused, but they are separate formations.
  • Poetic-literary sense: since the 18th and especially the 19th century, 'casement' became a favourite poetic word for 'window,' evoking romance, old houses, Gothic atmosphere, and moonlight (Keats's 'The casement slowly grows a glimmering square,' and countless Victorian verses).
  • Figurative sense: rarely, anything that opens like a hinged frame or encloses like a case may be called a casement — an extended, literary use.

Semantic bridge:

  • The word derives from the idea of a 'case': a casement is literally a small case or encasement around an opening. The window frame was conceptualized as a box-like enclosure holding the precious panes of glass, and later the hinged opening itself came to be called the casement.
  • Loss of generic status: once sliding sash windows became fashionable in England (17th–18th centuries), 'casement' narrowed from 'any window frame' to specifically 'a side-hinged window,' and acquired its slightly old-fashioned, poetic flavour.

Register and collocations:

  • Common collocations: 'casement window,' 'open casement,' 'lattice casement,' 'casement curtain,' 'leaded casement.'
  • Literary: strongly associated with Romantic and Victorian verse and with descriptions of old, rustic, or ruinous buildings.
  • Technical: remains the standard trade term for a hinged window in British English ('casement window' versus 'sash window').
