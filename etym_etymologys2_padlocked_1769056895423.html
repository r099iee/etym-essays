CHAIN FOR THE ELEMENT “pad-” (first element of PADLOCKED):
The element pad- has NO secure etymology. It is first attested in Middle English padlok (c. 1275); the OED marks its origin “uncertain,” and no PIE, Germanic, Romance, or other etymon has been reliably established. It is an English-only element (with Scots cognates), so no attested chain of forms can be given — the stage between “pad-” and any ancestor is unknown, and this must be said explicitly rather than guessed.
Leading hypotheses (each a guess, not a proven etymology):
  • Old English *padd, “a basket, hamper, wicker vessel” — the sense would be “the lock of a portable hamper/chest”; supported by the fact that the earliest padlocks secured chests, hampers, and panniers, and by Scots pad “a pannier, a wicker basket.”
  • Dialectal pad, “a small bag, pouch, or wallet” — a travelling lock carried in a bag.
  • “Pad” as “a soft cushiony part or foot” (of a toad or bear) — a 19th-century suggestion based on a fancied resemblance of a padlock’s body to an animal’s paw; rejected by the OED as fanciful.
No stage chain is therefore possible for pad-; the only certain stage is the first recorded composite form padlok (c. 1275).

CHAIN FOR THE ELEMENT “lock” (second element of PADLOCKED):
1. Proto-Indo-European *leug-, “to bend, twist, turn” (reconstructed; pre-3rd millennium BCE) — a root of bending and pliant twisting; zero-grade *lug- underlies the Germanic verb. Greek reflexes include λυγίζω (lygízō) “to bend, make pliant”; Lithuanian lenkti “to bend.”
2. Proto-Germanic *lūkaną, “to close, shut” (strong verb) and noun *luką, “a bolt, fastening, lock” (c. 500 BCE–500 CE) — the idea is that a door is closed by bending a bar into place.
3. Old English lūcan (strong verb), “to lock, shut” and loc (noun), “a lock, fastening” (before c. 1000 CE) — the native Germanic stock.
4. Middle English lok, “a lock, fastening” (12th–15th c.) — the direct ancestor of modern “lock.”
5. Middle English padlok, “a padlock” (c. 1275) — the compound “pad-” + “lok,” a portable lock that can be attached to and removed from a hasp or staple.
6. English padlocked (past participle / adjective; 19th c. onward) — “secured with a padlock” ── OUR WORD.
Note on nuance: Old English loc “lock, fastening” is etymologically distinct from Old English locc “a lock of hair” (a different root); they are homophones, not cognates. The lock-mechanism line is the one that builds “padlock,” “locksmith,” “lockup,” and “deadlock.”
