The name Plutarch is an anglicized patronymic and personal name derived from the classical Greek Πλούταρχος (Ploútarkhos), a dithematic compound formed from two ancient Indo-European linguistic roots: πλοῦτος (ploûtos, meaning "wealth, riches, abundance") and ἀρχός / ἄρχω (arkhos / árkhō, meaning "leader, master, commander; to lead, rule, or begin"). Together, the compound personal name originally carried the auspicious aristocratic meaning of "master of riches," "ruler of wealth," or "he who commands abundance."

1. Indo-European Foundations & Greek Development:

  • The first element, ploûtos, derives from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *plew-, meaning "to flow, float, or swim." In early Hellenic society, the image of a flowing, overflowing abundance of agricultural produce, grain, wine, and livestock gradually evolved into the generalized substantive for material wealth and prosperous fortune. This same root gave the Greek god of wealth and the subterranean underworld, Ploutōn (Latinized as Pluto), as well as modern English derivatives like plutocracy (rule by the wealthy) and plutocrat.
  • The second element, arkhos / árkhō, descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂erǵ-, meaning "to begin, lead, command, or rule." In classical Greek political and administrative terminology, this root generated essential civic concepts such as arkhōn (archon, chief magistrate), arkhē (beginning, principle, sovereign power), monarkhia (monarchy), and hierarkhia (hierarchy).
  • Compounded in archaic Boeotian and Attic Greek onomastics, Πλούταρχος was bestowed upon sons of noble families as a wish for aristocratic prosperity, authority, and high civic station.

2. Roman Empire, Latinization, and Byzantine Transmission:

  • During the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, as the Greek author gained renown throughout the Roman Empire, his name was adapted into Classical Latin as Plūtarchus (preserving the long vowel ū representing Greek ου). He received Roman citizenship from his patron, the consul Lucius Mestrius Florus, taking the full tria nomina Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus.
  • Throughout late antiquity and the Byzantine Empire, Plutarch was celebrated as a stylistic model of refined Atticizing Koine Greek and a treasury of moral wisdom. Scholars like Maximus Planudes (13th c.) compiled comprehensive editions of his manuscripts, preserving them from the destruction that befell many other classical libraries.

3. Renaissance Translation and Western European Adoption:

  • Following the Council of Florence (1438–1439) and the fall of Constantinople (1453), Greek scholars carried codices of Plutarch to Italy, where Renaissance humanists like Leonardo Bruni, Guarino da Verona, and Desiderius Erasmus translated individual Lives and Moralia treatises into Latin.
  • In 1559, Jacques Amyot published his monumentally influential French translation, Les Vies des hommes illustres, rendering the name into French as Plutarque. Amyot's elegant, fluid vernacular prose transformed Plutarch into one of the most widely read classical authors in Western Europe.
  • In 1579, Sir Thomas North translated Amyot's French edition into Early Modern English as The Lives of the Noble Grecians and Romanes, establishing the standard anglicized form Plutarch. North's muscular, idiomatic English prose directly provided William Shakespeare with the plot, psychology, character dynamics, and dramatic language for his Roman plays.

4. Modern Scholarly and Cultural Status:

  • Between 1683 and 1686, John Dryden oversaw a celebrated collaborative English translation of the Lives (the Dryden Plutarch, later revised in the 19th century by Arthur Hugh Clough).
  • In contemporary academic and literary discourse, Plutarch stands as both the historical individual and an enduring archetype for comparative biography, psychological historiography, and classical ethical humanism.
