Impart looks like a plain synonym for give or tell, but its history preserves a Roman idea of sharing by division: to impart is literally to in-part — to give someone their part or share of something, whether wisdom, flavour, or motion. That partitive origin explains why the verb still prefers abstract, divisible goods (knowledge, courage, meaning) as objects.

Latin pars, partis (feminine) meant “part, piece, share, party, side.” From it came partire “to divide, distribute” and, with the prefix im- (assimilated in- “in, into, to”), impartire (also spelled impertire) “to give a share to, bestow, communicate.” Classical authors use it for both material and immaterial sharing: Cicero impartit salutem (shares greeting), Livy impartire honores (bestow honours), Seneca impartire scientiam (communicate knowledge). The prefix does not mean “not” here but direction — into another’s portion.

Key stages:

  • Classical and Late Latin impartire (1st c. BCE–5th c. CE): elevated diction for generous distribution. Connotation is civic and ethical: the good man imparts benefits, the teacher imparts doctrine. Distinguished from dare (simply give) by implying participation — the recipient now holds a share.
  • Old French empartir / impartir (12th–13th c.): carries both “to divide among” and “to grant.” Anglo-Norman administration uses it for assigning shares of land or goods. The participial sense lingers in English legal French (“party” to a deed is one who takes part).
  • Middle English imparten (14th–15th c.): borrowed via French with the full Latin range. Chaucer and Gower use it for bestowing grace or telling tidings: “to impart his woe.” The religious register is strong — priests impart blessing, God imparts wisdom. Spelling fluctuates (enparte, imparte) before settling on impart.
  • Early Modern broadening (16th–18th c.): Shakespeare, Bacon, and Locke make it the standard verb for communicating abstract goods: “impart knowledge,” “impart motion,” “impart a flavour,” “impart courage.” Science adopts it for physical transmission (heat imparts motion to molecules; oak imparts flavour to wine). The sense “to disclose information” (impart news, secrets) becomes dominant in polite prose, slightly more formal than tell.
  • Modern formal-register verb (19th c.–present): impart stabilizes as a formal, slightly literary alternative to give/convey/tell. Schools “impart knowledge and values”; coaches “impart wisdom”; materials “impart strength or colour.” Legal-administrative use survives (“powers imparted to the committee”). The source sentence — “the intuitive grasp of motive which analysis can impart to us” — uses the classic abstract-object construction: analysis does not hand us an object but gives us a share in an insight.

Phonologically the word is a straightforward learned borrowing: Latin impartire /imparˈtiːre/ → Old French /empartir/ → Middle English /imˈpart/ with stress on the second syllable, preserved in RP /ɪmˈpɑːt/ (no rhotic, long open back vowel). The prefix assimilates in- → im- before labial p, a regular Latin sandhi rule still visible in spelling. No Great Vowel Shift distortion affects the short a lengthened in RP.

Literary attestations show the persistence of the sharing metaphor: Sidney’s Arcadia (“impart thy grief”), Milton’s Areopagitica (“imparting light”), Austen’s letters (“impart any news”), and modern wine writing (“the barrel imparts vanilla notes”). Each keeps the idea that what is given is now jointly held — knowledge imparted is not lost by the giver, unlike a coin handed over. That non-zero-sum sharing is why prose analysis can “impart” motive-grasp: the critic still keeps it while the reader gains it.
