• vānara: Similar because both refer to monkeys or simian beings in Sanskrit epic literature; differs because vānara (often traditionally analyzed as vana-nara, "forest-man") specifically denotes the sentient, speaking, divine-descended monkey-warriors of the Rāmāyaṇa who form civilized alliances and display refined moral discernment, whereas kapi is the broader, more ancient zoological, physical, and cosmic designation for the animal species itself.
  • śākhāmṛga: Similar because both denote monkeys; differs because śākhāmṛga is a vivid descriptive compound meaning literally "branch-beast" or "tree-canopy deer" (from śākhā "branch" + mṛga "wild animal/deer"), highlighting arboreal agile locomotion and grazing across boughs, whereas kapi is an elemental, non-compound primary noun rooted in Vedic antiquity.
  • plavaga: Similar because both designate simian creatures in classical Sanskrit poetry; differs because plavaga literally means "going by leaps" or "bounding mover" (plava + ga), emphasizing rapid bounding leaps across trees, cliffs, or chasms, whereas kapi encompasses broader mythological, solar, anatomical, and botanical meanings.
  • plavaṅgama: Similar because both are common Sanskrit epic synonyms for monkeys; differs because plavaṅgama is an intensified morphological compound emphasizing the continuous, erratic leaping gait of simians through air and canopy, whereas kapi serves as a base nominal root for epic epithets like Kapīndra and Kapidhvaja.
  • markaṭa: Similar because both denote monkeys in Sanskrit and Prakrit; differs because markaṭa (likely an onomatopoeic or substrate loan) carries a more mundane, mischievous, or derogatory connotation ("ape, trickster, grimacer"), commonly used in fables (like the Pañcatantra) to depict foolish, greedy, or meddlesome monkeys, whereas kapi possesses sacred, epic, and cosmological dignity.
  • hari: Similar because both are used as epithets for monkeys in epic texts; differs because hari is primarily a broad chromatic adjective meaning "tawny, yellowish-golden, or fawn-colored," which transferred metonymically to monkeys, lions, and horses due to their pelt hue, whereas kapi is intrinsically an anatomical, zoological, and mythical noun.
  • vanaukas: Similar because both refer to monkeys in Sanskrit narrative verse; differs because vanaukas literally means "forest-dweller" (vana + okas), designating monkeys by their wild forest habitat alongside hermits and woodland beasts, whereas kapi designates the creature itself rather than its residence.
  • kiṃpuruṣa: Similar because both touch upon mythological ape-like or semi-human forest beings; differs because kiṃpuruṣa ("what sort of man?") denotes mythical half-human, half-beast celestial beings (often horse-headed or ape-like) associated with Kubera's heavenly realm, rather than real biological monkeys or epic simian allies.
  • gohima: Similar because both refer to primates in esoteric lexicons; differs because gohima specifically denotes large black-faced langurs or baboon-like apes in specialized vocabularies, whereas kapi is universal across all simian families.
