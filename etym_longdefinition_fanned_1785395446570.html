FANNED is the past tense and past participle of the verb fan, and it unites two closely related senses that descend from a single instrument. In its first and most concrete sense, fanned means blown upon with a directed current of air, most famously applied to fire and flame: to fan a fire is to feed it air so that it burns brighter, hotter, and more violently, and the participle therefore describes something that has been urged, enlarged, or intensified by moving air. In its second sense, fanned means opened, spread, or arranged into the shape of a fan: a semicircular, radiating array of lines or plumes, as when a peacock spreads its tail, a player spreads cards in a hand, or a lady's hand-fan is unfolded before a face. Both senses descend from the same object — the fan, originally a winnowing basket or shovel with which threshed grain was tossed into the wind so that the lighter chaff blew away and the heavier grain fell back to the floor. The word thus always carries an instrumental, deliberate quality: fanned air is directed air, and a fanned object always has the distinctive half-moon silhouette of an opened fan. In the goblins' taunting song from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit — "Fifteen birds in five fir-trees, their feathers were fanned in a fiery breeze!" — the participle is deliberately double-edged, and the line draws its sinister energy from the ambiguity. On one reading the feathers are spread out like a fan: opened into a wide semicircle, a half-moon of plumes, as a peacock's train opens or a hand-fan unfolds. On the other reading the fiery breeze blows upon them, exactly as air is blown upon a fire to make it blaze — so that the wind simultaneously fans the feathers open and fans the flames that consume the birds. The participle thus carries an active, escalating energy: air driven into fire to feed it, objects unfurled into radiating arrays, destruction itself given a dancing, rhythmic, almost festive shape. The tone is mocking and incantatory, the grammar of a jeer turned into a sing-song, and the word's double sense is the engine of the whole couplet: the birds are both spread out in the breeze and roasted by it. The full range of senses of fanned, with the historical bridges between them:
  • Fanned of fire and heat: blown upon to increase burning — "the wind fanned the embers", "fanned into a blaze". This is the oldest living fire sense, a direct continuation of winnowing: the winnower's sweep that scatters chaff is the same sweep that feeds a flame.
  • Fanned of abstract intensification: to stimulate, rouse, or inflame — "fanned public anger", "fanned the controversy", "fanned by rumour". A metaphoric transfer of feeding a fire: what is fanned grows, what is fanned is deliberately made worse.
  • Fanned out (spatial array): spread into a semicircular pattern — troops fanned out across a field, a search party fanned out, cards fanned out in a hand, feathers fanned in display. The geometric sense, now the most productive in modern English, and the sense Tolkien plays on.
  • Fanned of cooling: to move air to cool a face or body — "she fanned herself with a hat", "fanned by a summer breeze". The domestic instrumental sense, still fully current.
  • Fanned of winnowing: the original agricultural act of tossing grain so the wind separates chaff from seed; now archaic and confined to farming history, but preserved in the word's ancestry and in the phrase "the fanning-mill".
  • Fanned in sports slang: a batter is "fanned" when struck out by a swinging miss — the whiffing, air-cutting motion of the bat. Note that "fan" as a supporter (from fanatic) is a different, unrelated homonym and must never be merged with this word.
  • Fanned as a passive participle (with be or have): "the papers were fanned by the draught", "he has fanned himself all evening" — pure description of air moved over a surface by wind, a device, or a hand.
The boundaries with near-synonyms are precise:
  • Blown: describes mere air movement with no shape, instrument, or purpose; fanned implies a deliberate, rhythmic sweep and the fan's semicircular geometry.
  • Spread: static, general, and toneless — anything may be spread; fanned insists on radiating lines opening from a fixed point like a fan's ribs.
  • Unfurled: implies something rolled up being unrolled, and suits sails, flags, and banners; fanned implies opening in a half-circle, not along a roll.
  • Splayed: an awkward, uncontrolled spreading, especially of limbs or digits; fanned is deliberate, decorative, and instrumental.
  • Winnowed: the literal agricultural action of tossing grain so wind lifts the chaff; fanned can name the same act but extends far beyond the barn floor into fire, display, and abstraction.
  • Stirred: rousing without direction, shape, or instrument; fanned adds the directed blast of air and the fan's silhouette.
  • Wafted: carried gently on air, passive and directionless; fanned is active, applied, and purposeful.
  • Ventilated: technical, systemic air-exchange in buildings or lungs; fanned is personal, manual, and visual.
Collocations and register: the word falls into two clear families. With fire and heat: "fan the flames", "fanned embers", "fanned by the wind", "fanned into a blaze", "fanned the dying fire". With arrays: "fanned out", "a fanned tail", "fanned cards", "fanned in a semicircle", "fanned across the field". The register is standard to literary English: fanned is more deliberate and often more dramatic than blown, and its double allegiance — to fire on one side and to shape on the other — makes it a favourite of descriptive, lyric, and gothic prose. In the fire sense it is transitive with a subject supplying the air; in the array sense it is usually reflexive or takes "out". The participle is morphologically regular (fan + -ed) and keeps the monosyllabic base unchanged in all persons. Its twin poles, destruction and display, are exactly what Tolkien's line exploits: the feathers are simultaneously a fan unfurled and fuel for the fire that the breeze feeds, which is why the image lingers long after the song ends.
