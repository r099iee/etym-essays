- Ancient Greece — the spinning top: kônos names the pinecone, the apex of a helmet, and the child's whipping-top, one tapering shape under three uses.
- Rome — the borrowed solid: Latin cōnus keeps the pinecone and lends the shape to geometry, architecture, and the apex of helmets and tents.
- Middle Ages — the schoolroom solid: Euclid's conus teaches generations the tapering body, while herbalists and foresters keep the seed-case sense alive in the woods.
- 16th–18th c. — science adopts it: botanists fix cone as the strobilus of conifers, and geometers map cones, frustums, and conic sections across the new mathematics.
- 19th c. — the eye joins in: anatomists name the retinal cone cells, and the plural cones starts meaning both pinewood litter and the cells that let us see colour.
- 20th c. — streets and summers: traffic cones order the roads and ice-cream cones sweeten the summers, while campers still stoop for the oldest cones of all.
- Today — the humble and the technical side by side: children gather cones for fires and crafts while surgeons, opticians, and engineers speak of cones of vision, light, and force.
