Level is, in its oldest and plainest sense, the adjective of flatness: horizontal, even, without rise or fall — ground on which water does not run and a traveller does not climb. In the source sentence Cobbett uses exactly this road sense: most people bound for Chilworth would have taken the level road to Guildford and come round through Shawford under the hills, the easy valley highway, while he and his party chose instead to strike across Merrow Down and over the Surrey Hills for the sake of the prospect.

The sentence preserves a whole geography of choice: the level road is the sensible turnpike-parish route, winding with the river through the gap, while the hill route is the walker's extravagance — streets and turnpikes shunned for down turf, race-course, and view. Cobbett, who rode to see and to judge, takes the heights so that he can describe what the valley road hides.

Core meaning in detail: A level surface is one perpendicular to the plumb line at every point — the spirit-bubble centred, the table not rocking, the road neither climbing nor dropping. Builders test it with the instrument that shares its name: the mason's level, the surveyor's level, the laser line. Roads called level follow contours and watercourses, trading distance for ease; railways and canals, which can bear almost no gradient, hunt level ground with cuttings and embankments. In ordinary speech level also governs character and voice: a level head, a level tone, a level look — flatness carried from ground into temperament.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Flat, horizontal, without slope (primary, from 14th c., Cobbett's sense): the level road, level fields, a level crossing where road meets rail on the flat. The bridge from the instrument is direct: ground judged by the mason's level and found true.
  • At the same height or rank (extended): water finds its level; the scores are level; houses level with the church tower. The bridge runs through the surveyor's datum: things measured against one horizontal plane and found coincident, whether floodwater or football teams.
  • Even in score or contest (sporting): level pegging, all square at the interval. The bridge carries the height image into rivalry: neither side raised above the other, the match as flat as well-laid turf.
  • Calm and steady in temperament (figurative): a level-headed friend, to keep a level tone in anger. The bridge is the old body-as-landscape metaphor: just as level ground never pitches the traveller, so the level mind never pitches its owner into rashness.
  • To make flat, to demolish or equalise (verb, from 16th c.): to level a wall, to level the scores, to level up a character or a region. The bridge turns the adjective into action: making unequal things equal by cutting down, building up, or raising all together — from siege artillery levelling ramparts to modern levelling-up policy and the gamer's level-up.
  • A storey, rank, or tier (noun, modern): the ground level, sea level, A-levels, the reading level of a text. The bridge stacks flat planes vertically: each storey is a floor made level for feet, each grade a plane of attainment, and sea level the great datum plane against which all heights are measured.
  • An instrument for testing horizontality (noun, original tool sense): the carpenter's level, the surveyor's dumpy level. The bridge is naming backward: the tool that declares flatness takes the name of the flatness it declares, so that level means both the judge and the judgement.

Register and connotation: level is plain, honest, democratic English — the enemy of pretension and the friend of fairness. Level ground welcomes every traveller; a level deal cheats nobody; level-headed praise is the highest compliment to judgement. Against steep and lofty, which dazzle and exclude, level reassures: what is level can be built on, walked on, and trusted underfoot.
