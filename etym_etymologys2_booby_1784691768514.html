Non-Indo-European, expressive / baby-talk origin — no PIE ancestry can be claimed: 1. Late Latin / Vulgar Latin *baba, *bubb- — reduplicated childish syllables imitating stammering and babbling (cf. Latin balbus “stammering,” balbutiō “I stammer,” 1st c. BCE attested; the base itself is pre-literary and reconstructed) 2. Medieval Iberian Romance bobo (Spanish) and bobo (Portuguese) “foolish, stupid, naive” (noun “a fool, a jester”; from the baby-talk base, attested from the medieval period) 3. Spanish pájaro bobo “the foolish bird” — applied by Iberian mariners (16th c.) to the gannet-like seabirds of the genus Sula, so tame and clumsy they could be caught by hand 4. English booby “a dull, foolish person; the seabird” — borrowed in the 16th–17th century from Spanish/Portuguese nautical usage (first recorded c. 1600 in both senses) 5. Modern English booby (fool/simpleton; the seabird; base of booby-trap, 17th c., and booby prize, 19th c.) Debate note: some dictionaries connect the bird-name and the fool-name to native English baby-talk (bubby, “breast,” or babe-talk), while the standard account follows the Spanish bobo route via sailors; the word’s expressive, onomatopoeic nature makes a single point of origin hard to pin down. The OED and most handbooks favour the Iberian source.
