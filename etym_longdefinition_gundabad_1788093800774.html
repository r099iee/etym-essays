Gundabad (Mount Gundabad) is a place-name from the fictional world of J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings: a great mountain at the northern end of the Misty Mountains, the long range that runs from the far north of Middle-earth southward past Rivendell, Moria, and the Gap of Rohan. In the narrative geography it stands in the far North, at the northern extremity of the range where Eriador and Rhovanion meet, a landmark that dominates the passes and dales of the region.

1. The Dwarvish heritage: the awakening of Durin.

  • According to the lore of Durin's Folk (the Longbeards, the Dwarves of Khazad-dûm or Moria), Durin the Deathless, eldest of the Seven Fathers of the Dwarves, first awoke alone in the deeps beneath Mount Gundabad, and from there journeyed south until he came to the Mirrormere (Kheled-zâram) and founded the great subterranean city of Khazad-dûm. In Tolkien's later accounts Gundabad is thus the ancestral cradle and holy place of the Longbeards - the point where the first of their race stirred out of the stone of the earth.
  • This primeval sanctity gives the later history of the mountain its tragic cast: a place of origin that falls, age by age, into the hands of the very powers the Dwarves most hate.

2. The fall: capital of the Orcs of the North.

  • By the Third Age Gundabad has become the chief stronghold of the Orcs of the North, and in the sentence of this card - Gandalf's report at the Council of Elrond - it is explicitly "their capital": the Orcs "marched and gathered by hill and valley, going ever by tunnel or under dark, until around and beneath the great mountain Gundabad of the North ... a vast host was assembled ready to sweep down in time of storm unawares upon the South." The passage is a portrait of the enemy's war-machine: a shadow-army concentrating in the deep places of the world, out of sight of all watchers, preparing a sudden descent upon the free lands.
  • The context belongs to the build-up of the War of the Ring (c. T.A. 3018): Gandalf's intelligence had revealed that Sauron was stirring the northern Orcs into a great host beneath Gundabad. In the event the great descent never came as planned: the war ran its course, the Ring was unmade, and the fall of Sauron dissolved the power that drove the northern host.
  • Gundabad belongs to the same complex of northern goblin-and-orc power whose mountain strongholds Bilbo's company encountered in The Hobbit; the name recurs in Tolkien's narrative geography as the northern nerve-centre of that threat.

3. The name: an invented language, a philological puzzle.

  • Gundabad is a name in Khuzdul, Tolkien's invented language of the Dwarves - a deliberately harsh, guttural tongue which Tolkien himself described as Semitic in character, built on triliteral roots with heavy consonants. The first element is generally read as the Khuzdul stem *gund(u), glossed by Tolkien in his late philological notes and by modern reconstructions (e.g. Salo 2004) as "underground hall, delve" - a meaning perfectly apt for a mountain that is a hollowed citadel.
  • The second element, -abad (or -bad), has never been published with a gloss; its exact sense is unknown, and the whole is best treated as a fixed proper name whose visible parts invite but cannot confirm a composition.
  • An accidental resemblance worth noting: -abad is also a productive element in Persian place-names (Hyderabad, Islamabad, Ahmedabad, "settled place, town"). The likeness is coincidental - Tolkien modelled Khuzdul on Semitic patterns, not Persian - and it is a favourite example among students of the legendarium of how plausible-sounding invented etymology can be.

4. The name in the record and in culture.

  • The name first appears in The Hobbit (1937) and recurs in The Lord of the Rings (1954-55), where the Council of Elrond passage (this card's context) makes it the pivot of northern strategy; Appendix A of The Return of the King anchors it to the awakening of Durin.
  • Posthumous philological material (e.g. in The Peoples of Middle-earth, 1996) continues to discuss the Dwarf-names, and later Tolkien scholarship and derivative works - including the 2021 Gundabad expansion of The Lord of the Rings Online - keep the mountain alive as one of the iconic landmarks of Middle-earth's North.

5. Register and use.

  • As a proper noun, Gundabad is not translatable and carries no paraphrase; in reading it functions as a place-signature whose very sound - two hard syllables built on the guttural -g- and the cluster -d-b-d - evokes the underground and the monstrous. In the context sentence it is used appositionally ("the great mountain Gundabad of the North"), the narrator assuming, as Gandalf does, that every watcher knows the name.
