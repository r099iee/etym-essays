Core sense. Clad is the past tense and past participle of clothe — a shorter, contracted form of clothed — with the added force of a participle that usually names both the garment and the state: "clad in armour", "clad in white", "clad in furs". Because it is the clipped literary twin of clothed, the word carries a distinct stylistic charge: it is the form of epic, romance, chronicle and poetry, and of prose that wants a little age and dignity. In the source sentence, "Each one of his folk was clad in a hauberk of steel mail", the participle does what it does best: it dresses a whole army in a single stroke and leaves the eye on the mail, not on the grammar.

Grammatical status. Clad is a so-called irregular form of a regular verb: the modern verb clothe (from Old English clāþian) usually forms clothed/clothed, but the alternative clad has, since the fourteenth century, survived as the literary partner, exactly as lit competes with lighted and dreamt with dreamed. Both forms are correct in modern English; clad is the older, contracted form and clothed the transparent one. As a participial adjective it may stand alone ("the snow-clad hills", "a man clad in black") or combine with a material or colour: clad in steel, clad in ermine, clad in denim, clad in mist.

  • 1. Dressed in garments (the literal sense). A person "clad in" a specified costume: the knight clad in mail, the bishop clad in vestments, the swimmer clad in a wetsuit. The sense is exactly that of clothed but with a more formal or pictorial tilt.
  • 2. Covered, furnished, overlaid with a material (extended sense). From human garments the word extends to any covering that fits like clothing: mountains "clad in snow", slopes "clad in forest", the earth "clad in green", a ship "clad in ice". The bridge is functional — the covering clothes the surface.
  • 3. Compounds in -clad: ironclad, steel-clad, armour-clad, snow-clad, ivy-clad. The most productive modern territory. Ironclad (1850s) named the first armoured warships and then anything unbreakable ("an ironclad guarantee"); snow-clad, ivy-clad, tree-clad flourish in landscape description; armour-clad and mail-clad belong to the military and heroic vocabulary.
  • 4. Building technology: cladding, to clad (20th century). Since the mid-twentieth century, construction uses clad and cladding for the outer skin of a building: a timber-clad house, a building clad in glass panels, rain-screen cladding. Here the word has cycled from clothing to architecture — the building is "dressed" in its facade, and the verb is again fully technical.
  • 5. Figurative uses. Rarer but real: "clad in authority", "clad in innocence", "clad in mystery" — a person or institution presented as wrapped in an abstract covering. These are literary extensions of the same fitting-cover image.

Historical and stylistic note. Clad entered English as a natural contraction in Middle English and for centuries was the everyday past of clothe as much as clothed; in modern usage the balance has shifted, and clad now reads as poetic, archaic, or technical (armour, weather, architecture) while clothed is neutral. Writers of epic and fantasy — Tennyson, Morris, and Tolkien above all — use clad because it restores to arming-scenes their old heroic sonority: "clad in a hauberk of steel mail" is precisely a sentence in which the archaic participle and the archaic noun cooperate.

Register and connotation. Clad is never wrong, but it always means a little more than "dressed": it invests the covering with completeness and significance. A man in a coat is dressed; a knight is clad in steel. The word is the perfect participle for armour, snow, ivy, glass and legend.
