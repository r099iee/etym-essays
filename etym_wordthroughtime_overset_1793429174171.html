- Early 14th c. — Middle English origins: Formed as oversetten, originally carrying the physical and military sense of overcoming, overburdening, or oppressing an enemy through superior weight or force.
- 15th–16th c. — Mechanical and physical inversion: The sense specializes into the physical toppling over, capsizing, or inverting of containers, wagons, and small boats on rivers and roads.
- 17th–18th c. — Golden age of nautical and literary usage: Becomes the universal English nautical term for a vessel capsizing in a storm (as in Defoe’s Robinson Crusoe and Swift’s Gulliver’s Travels), while simultaneously developing the psychological sense of a mind overset by grief or joy.
- 19th c. — Competition with ‘capsize’ and ‘upset’: The loanword capsize ascends in naval usage and upset dominates domestic language, gradually pushing overset into more formal, literary, and historical registers.
- Late 19th–20th c. — Technical typographical specialization: Adopted into the printing industry to designate type or manuscript copy set in excess of the available column or page space.
- Today — Literary and archaic status: Preserved as a stately literary term evoking eighteenth-century maritime realism, period historical fiction, and formal emotional collapse.
