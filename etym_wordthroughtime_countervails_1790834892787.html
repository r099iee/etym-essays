- 14th Century: Entered Middle English via Anglo-French law as a technical term for matching equal financial value or compensating damages.
- 16th–17th Century: Adopted in dramatic and biblical literature (King James Bible) to denote moral compensation and balancing of fortunes.
- 18th–19th Century: Elevated in Enlightenment philosophy and essays (Addison) as the spiritual capacity of virtue to outweigh worldly hardship.
- 20th–21st Century: Institutionalized in international trade law (countervailing duties) and economics (Galbraith's countervailing power) to denote forces that check monopolies and subsidies.
