- 1895 (Coinage & Professional Aspiration) - Coined in Chicago in the pages of *The Embalmers' Monthly* by trade reformers seeking a Latinate, high-status professional label modeled on *physician* and *clinician*; designed to elevate funeral workers from perceived grim gravediggers into respected scientific practitioners of arterial embalming.
- Early 20th Century (Institutional Adoption & Legal Formalization) - Rapidly adopted by American trade associations, mortuary science colleges, and state licensing boards; perceived as a progressive, sanitary hallmark of modern municipal public health and chemical science.
- 1920s–1930s (Cultural Skepticism & Satirical Scrutiny) - Targeted by satirists and cultural critics, most notably H. L. Mencken in *The American Language*, who cited *mortician* as the supreme example of American commercial euphemism, status-seeking inflation, and linguistic pretense.
- Mid-20th Century (Commercial Retreat to "Funeral Director") - Following Jessica Mitford's scathing 1963 exposé *The American Way of Death*, funeral industry consultants noted that the Latin root *mort-* ("death") carried a macabre, clinical chill; commercial parlors shifted their public branding away from *mortician* to the gentler, hospitality-oriented *funeral director*.
- Late 20th Century (Bifurcation of Laboratory vs. Front Office) - Settled into industry parlance as an internal descriptor for laboratory embalming and restorative dermasurgery, while remaining in popular American vernacular as an evocative, slightly gothic synonym for undertakers.
- Contemporary 21st-Century Usage - Re-embraced in popular culture and the "death positive" movement (spearheaded by figures like Caitlin Doughty) as a proud, honest, and technically skilled vocational title, demystifying corpse preparation and funeral care in the modern era.
