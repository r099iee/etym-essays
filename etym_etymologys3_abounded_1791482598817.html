Abound is a water word that has forgotten its water. Its Latin ancestor abundare is built from ab-, "away, from", and undare, "to rise in waves, surge", which comes from unda, "a wave". The Proto-Indo-European root behind unda is *wed-, "water, wet", the same root that gives English water, Greek hydor and the second element of otter. In classical Latin abundare is vividly physical: a river abounds when it rises over its banks, a vessel abounds when it runs over. The notion of plenty is a consequence of the overflow, not its starting point.

Christian Latin and the language of grace. The verb's fortunes were transformed by the Vulgate. Latin Christian writers used abundare for the superabundance of grace, consolation, joy and suffering, and the metaphor of a fullness that spills over proved theologically fertile. Paul's argument in Romans turns on it: sin entered and "abounded", but grace "did much more abound". The Latin abundare thus acquired a strong association with moral and spiritual excess, a connotation that passed directly into English through the translators. When the word appears in religious prose today — "abounding grace", "abound in hope" — it is echoing this patristic and Pauline usage.

Old French and Middle English. Old French abonder simplified the Latin cluster and dropped the -d-, giving Middle English abounden around 1300. English also knew a variant with an inorganic h-, habounden, a common medieval spelling habit under French influence; the h- was eventually lost, while the -d- was restored by learned reference to the Latin. The early Middle English senses are still close to water: to overflow, to be full to the brim. But the quantitative sense — to be present in great quantity — is already visible in the fourteenth century, and it rapidly becomes the dominant one. The physical overflow survives in abundance and in the occasional literary phrase, but the verb itself becomes abstract.

  • Construction: intransitive; "X abounds" or "a place abounds in/with X".
  • Aspect: usually simple present or past, rarely progressive; a stative rather than an eventive verb.
  • Register: formal, bookish, faintly biblical; common in impersonal reporting.
  • Related words: abundance, abundant, abundantly, superabound, inundate (from the same unda).

The Reformation and the English Bible. William Tyndale and the translators of the King James Version made abound a familiar English word. The KJV's "where sin abounded, grace did much more abound" (Romans 5:20) is probably the single most influential sentence in the word's history: it fixes the verb in the register of sermon and hymn, and it establishes the pattern in which abound describes a surplus that overwhelms a deficit. The same period uses the word in controversy, in devotional prose and in the new natural philosophy, always with the sense of a fullness that exceeds expectation.

Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. As English prose became more polite and more abstract, the literal overflow disappeared from ordinary use and abound settled into the learned register. The prepositional patterns crystallised: abound in for qualities, resources and texts; abound with for animals, objects and concrete particulars. Eighteenth-century prose uses the verb freely in description and moralising, and the participle abounding becomes a favourite of hymn writers. At the same time the noun abundance and the adjective abundant take over the plain everyday work, leaving the verb to the more formal and generalising styles.

Nineteenth-century description. Travel writing, natural history and colonial reportage made "abounds in" a formula. A guidebook reports that the district abounds in game, the coast abounds with seals, the hills abound in minerals. The construction is useful because it generalises without counting, and it carries an implicit promise of richness. The card's own context — the Chibcha of Colombia exporting rock salt that abounded in their territory — belongs to exactly this descriptive, ethnographic register: a natural resource present in such quantity that it can be exported.

Twentieth and twenty-first centuries. The verb's modern life is overwhelmingly impersonal and journalistic: "rumours abounded", "speculation abounded", "evidence abounds", "opportunities abound", "examples abound". These formulas report a general condition while withholding agency, which makes them useful for cautious reporting and slightly evasive for the same reason. The word has also acquired a faintly archaic or formal shimmer, so that a writer who chooses abound over "there is plenty of" signals a certain elevation of tone. In technical and academic prose it survives in the phrase "abounds in", often in summaries of a literature or a data set.

Sound and inflection. RP /əˈbaʊnd/; the past tense abounded is /əˈbaʊndɪd/, with the -ed- syllabic because the stem ends in /d/. The spelling ou represents the diphthong /aʊ/, as in round and sound. The verb is regular throughout, and it has no passive: one cannot say "it was abounded". Its intransitivity is one of the clearest things about it, and it explains why the word feels like a description of a state rather than an action.
