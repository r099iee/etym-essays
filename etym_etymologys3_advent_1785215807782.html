**From a root that means "come" to the coming that changes eras: ADVENT** The history of ADVENT runs from the most basic verb of human movement to the most solemn claim of Christian theology, and then back down into the headlines. It begins with a Proto-Indo-European root that simply meant "to go, to come," and it ends, centuries later, as the standard word for the arrival of anything that changes an era. **The root *gʷem-: the coming-verb of the Indo-Europeans** The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷem-, "to go, come," one of the most widely attested roots in the entire family. Its reflexes span the Indo-European world: Sanskrit gacchati, "he goes," and gámanam, "going"; Greek baínō, "to go, step," the source of básis, "step, base," and hence English basis and base; the Germanic verb *kweman, attested in Gothic qiman, Old Norse koma, and Old English cuman, the ancestor of English come; and, in Italic, the verb that became Latin venīre, "to come." The Latin form is the regular outcome of the root's zero grade, *gʷm̥-, so that advent, come, and basis are all, in the last analysis, distant cousins. The sound change that carried the root from PIE to Latin — the initial gʷ being lost and the syllabic m becoming em — is one of the classic regular developments of Italic historical phonology, reconstructed in detail by comparative linguists even though no stage between the reconstructed root and the Latin verb is directly attested. **Adventus in the Roman world** From venīre, "to come," Latin formed advenīre, "to come to, arrive," by prefixing ad-, "to, toward," and from the perfect participle of that verb came the noun adventus, "an arrival, a coming, an approach." In secular Latin adventus was an ordinary word for the arrival of anyone or anything — of an army, of a fleet, of a traveller — and in the imperial period it acquired a ceremonial significance: the adventus of an emperor, his formal arrival in a city, was a state occasion, staged with processions, crowds, and public acclamation. The word already carried a grandeur that the plain "arrival" did not: an adventus was not the mere fact of arriving but the official, celebrated coming of someone of consequence. It is this Roman sense of the word as a solemn public arrival that the Christian use inherited. **The Christian transfer: Adventus Domini** The word's great fortune was ecclesiastical. In the New Testament and the writings of the early Church, the coming of Christ was named by the Greek word parousía, "presence, arrival," used both of his birth and of the expected Second Coming. Latin Christianity rendered parousía as Adventus — the coming of the Lord, Adventus Domini. From this theological usage, by the fourth century, Adventus (capitalized) came to designate the liturgical season of preparation before Christmas: the four weeks in which the Church commemorates the first coming of Christ and awaits his second. The season spread through Western Christendom in the early Middle Ages, acquiring its own liturgy, its own readings, its own discipline of waiting and penance, and eventually its popular apparatus of the Advent wreath and, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the Advent calendar. **Into English: from season to metaphor** English borrowed the word in the Middle Ages, through Old French advent, in the twelfth to fourteenth centuries, and it did so first in the strictly liturgical sense: Advent, the season before Christmas. For several centuries the word was a church word. Then, from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries onward, the sense broadened: anything that came — a king, an age, an institution — could have its "advent," and the grand, providential aura of the liturgical season travelled with the word into secular use. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries made "the advent of X" a standard formula of history and public commentary: the advent of printing, of the railways, of the steam engine, of democracy. It is in this register that Gladstone's tribute to Mill does its work. When Gladstone said, of Mill's brief parliamentary career, "I rejoiced in his advent and deplored his disappearance," he was doing something very English and very Victorian: borrowing the grandeur of the church's word and applying it to the arrival of a philosopher in the House of Commons, elevating a by-election result to the status of a providential event. **The twentieth and twenty-first centuries** In modern journalism and commentary the word is ubiquitous and largely secularized: the advent of television, of the internet, of artificial intelligence, of the smartphone. The formula "the advent of X" has become the standard way to mark an innovation that changed the way people live. Yet the word has never entirely shed its older meanings. The liturgical Advent still fills the weeks of December; Adventism, the nineteenth-century millenarian movement, hardened the theological sense into the name of a church; the Advent calendar has become a global commercial ritual. The word thus lives on three levels at once — as the name of a church season, as the theological word for the coming of Christ, and as the everyday metaphor for any era-changing arrival. It is a rare thing for a word to move so far, from the reconstructed coming-verb of a prehistoric people to the headline "the advent of AI," while keeping, at every step, its original meaning: something great is coming, and its coming changes everything.
