Old English (c. 8th–10th c.)
brōþor (plural brōþru / brēþer)
‘brother, fellow monk, kin’
↓
Early Middle English (c. 12th c.)
brether
‘brothers (umlaut plural)’
↓
Late Middle English (c. 13th–14th c.)
brethren
‘brothers, fellow guild or church members’
↓
Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)
brethren
‘associates in a faith, craft, or society’
↓
Modern English (current)
brethren
‘colleagues of a profession or faith’

Note: Descended from PIE *bʰréh₂tēr. In Old English, i-mutation (umlaut) shifted the root vowel from -ō- to -ē- in the dative singular and nominative/accusative plural (*brēþer*). In late Middle English, speakers added the southern weak noun plural suffix -en (as in oxen, children) onto the already-plural mutated stem brether, creating a cumulative double-plural form. By the 16th–17th century, a permanent semantic split occurred: brothers became standard for biological kinship, while brethren was reserved for spiritual, guild, and professional fellowship.
