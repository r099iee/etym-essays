I. The deep root: proclaiming (c. 4500 BCE – 500 CE). The family begins with the Indo-European root *bʰeh₂-, 'to speak, say', the source of Latin fārī 'to speak', fāma 'report, fame', Fābulae 'stories'; Greek phēmí 'I say', phōnḗ 'voice'; and Germanic *bannaną, 'to proclaim, to command, to summon'. The crucial Germanic development is legal: the Germanic word of public speech becomes the word of the public proclamation — the decree, the summons, the ban. In medieval law this is the bannum, the lord's or king's jurisdiction exercised by public announcement; a Bann in early German law is at once the proclamation and the territory it covers. To 'ban' a person was to proclaim them outside the law — the noun ban in English and the Italian bandito, 'a banned man', i.e. a bandit, preserve the same idea. The English word banns of marriage is the same root: the public proclamation of an intended wedding. This explains the paradox of the verb's history: the modern meaning 'exile' grows out of the ancient meaning 'speak out publicly'.

II. Frankish and French (5th–14th centuries). When the Franks overran Gaul, the Germanic verb passed into Gallo-Romance: Frankish *bannjan, 'to proclaim', yields Old French banir, bannir, whose present-participle stem baniss- is what English later borrowed. Within Old French the meaning advanced: banir first means 'to proclaim, to summon (by public ban)', then, by the twelfth century, 'to outlaw, to exile' — the shift is transparent: he who is publicly proclaimed an enemy of the community must leave it. Old French ban itself had already absorbed the sense 'exile, banishment' (compare the ban de l'évêque), and Latin bannum appears in Frankish legal texts such as the Lex Salica as the lord's jurisdiction and the penalty attached to it. The word was thus two centuries deep in legal-administrative use before it reached England.

III. Entry into English (14th century). Middle English banysshen is recorded from the early fourteenth century — in the Cursor Mundi (c. 1325) among the first citations — with the meaning 'to condemn to leave a country; to expel'. Early usage is heavily religious as well as legal: Adam and Eve banished from Paradise, sinners banished from the divine presence. The participle in particular lends itself to verdict-formulas: 'banysshed ut of the londe'. Chaucer and his contemporaries use the verb for formal expulsion, and by 1400 the word is a settled member of English administrative and literary vocabulary, standing alongside native 'outlaw' and borrowed 'exile'.

IV. Tudor and Stuart politics (1500–1700). The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries make banishment the drama of state: rival claimants, defeated factions and religious dissidents are banished from England, Scotland and Ireland; chronicles and ballads record the sentence-formula 'banished from the realm'. The Elizabethan stage took the theme up at once — Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet turns the whole plot on Romeo's banishment ('Hence, banished, is banished from the world'), Richard II banishes Bolingbroke and Mowbray, As You Like It exiles the good duke to Arden, and King Lear banishes Cordelia and Kent — banishment functions in this drama as a secular excommunication, the loss of identity itself. In the same period the word spreads figuratively in moral and religious writing: one banishes sin, banishes care, banishes the devil by prayer.

V. The turn inward (18th–19th centuries). The Enlightenment shifts the verb decisively inward: Johnson's Dictionary glosses banish as 'to drive away; to expel', and the century's prose banishes prejudice, melancholy, superstition and error from the republic of letters. Romanticism then poeticises the exiled condition — the banished poet, the banished lover, Banquo-like banished thoughts — and the participle acquires its modern melancholy: 'banished from happiness', 'banished from the world'. The penal meaning never dies, but after the rise of the modern deportation and extradition regime in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, literal banishment largely leaves Western law codes, surviving in popular imagination as the punishment of myth and romance.

VI. The twentieth century to today. In modern English 'banished' lives chiefly in formal, literary and fantasy registers: Tolkien's Gandalf wishes Sauron 'banished from the world', and fantasy fiction (banishing demons and dark lords) keeps the word in constant circulation. Political rhetoric still banishes ideas, parties and ideologies; sport banishes players; markets banish fear and doubt. The word has kept its full original grammar of authority — someone or something must do the banishing — and its archaic gravity is precisely what makes it right for verdicts, curses and elegies, from the Fall of Man to the fall of Sauron.
