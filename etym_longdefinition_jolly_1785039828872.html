An adjective meaning full of high spirits, festive merriment, and genial, hearty cheer — a state of carefree gladness at once warm and a little boisterous. In the Elves of Rivendell's song in The Hobbit, chapter III, "O! tril-lil-lil-lolly / The valley is jolly, ha! ha!" the word is the bright summit of a chain of nonsense syllables. After the bleak, dangerous crossing of the Misty Mountains, the Elves' lilting trill and exultant "ha! ha!" cast JOLLY as the very essence of Rivendell — sunlit, musical, enchanted, free of care. The tone is one of overflowing, almost childlike gaiety, the playful mockery of singers who have watched tired dwarves stumble down from the heights and now offer them a valley bathed in golden, soft, sweet air. The word's round, cheerful vowels carry the song's lightness: it is joy without anxiety, mirth without malice.

Set beside its near-synonyms, JOLLY differs from merry (an older native English word meaning "pleasant, joyful," more reserved and poetic), from cheerful (a steady, dispositional good humour), from happy (which originally meant "lucky," from hap "chance"), from jovial (hearty humour supposedly born of the planet Jupiter), and from gleeful (a lighter, more childish delight). JOLLY is the most festive and robust of the set — a large, smiling, convivial word.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings

  • Intensive adverb (British, from 1540s): "a jolly good fellow," "a jolly fine view" — an emphatic intensifier meaning "very, remarkably"; the related "jolly well" ("I'll jolly well do it") is a pugnacious British emphasis.
  • Noun — a pleasure trip: "a jolly" (British, 19th c.) is an outing or jaunt, especially a brief spree made at someone else's expense; from the same stem, "to jolly someone along" means to coax, flatter, or keep someone in good spirits.
  • Colloquial "somewhat drunk": from the 17th century "he was quite jolly" could mean pleasantly inebriated.
  • Jolly Roger: the name of the pirate flag (a skull and crossbones on black), attested from the early 18th century — "jolly" here in the old roistering, cheerful sense.
  • Jollies (Royal Navy slang): a "jolly" is a Royal Marine, so called from their red ("jolly") coats.
  • "Jolly hockey sticks": a British stereotype of hearty upper-class schoolgirl enthusiasm, popularised in 20th-century comic writing.
  • Historical senses (Middle English, c. 1300): the borrowed word could mean "vigorous, strong, youthful," "amorous, even lecherous," "handsome, well-dressed," "playful, frisky," and even "arrogant, overweening, foolish" — a far wider emotional range than today's.
  • Derivatives: jollity (festive merriment), jollification (a merrymaking party), jolliness, and jolly boat (a small ship's tender).

Every sense radiates from one core: holiday cheerfulness. Whether intensifying praise, naming a spree, or describing a festive drinker, JOLLY has always been the word of good-humoured celebration — the exact mood the Elves spin into the air above the valley.
