Origins: The Indo-European Root of Strength

The history of valiant begins with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *wal- (modern laryngealist notation *h₂welh₁-), which meant "to be strong, to have power, to be vigorous, to prevail." This root was extraordinarily widespread in ancient Eurasia. In Vedic Sanskrit it produced bala "strength, force" (and vala, a fortified cavern), surviving in modern Hindi bal "strength." In Proto-Germanic it produced *waldan "to rule, to wield power," the ancestor of English wield and of royal names like Oswald, Walden, and Waldemar. In the Balto-Slavic family it yielded Old Church Slavonic vlasti "to rule" and words behind the name Vladimir ("rule + peace/fame"). Everywhere the root carries the same double charge: power and worth.

Latin: valēre — To Be Strong, To Be Worth

  • In the Italic branch the root became the essential Latin verb valēre, "to be strong, to be healthy, to be influential, to have worth or value."
  • The Roman greeting valē ("be well!") and the farewell formula valeas preserve the verb's sense of wishing someone strength and health.
  • Latin valēre proved one of the most productive word generators in European history: valid, validate, value, equivalent, prevail, prevalent, avail, convalesce, valor, and valiant all descend from it.
  • Critically, Latin fused the ideas of strength and worth: to be strong was to be of value, and to be of value was to be strong. This fusion is the seed of everything the modern word means.

Old French: valoir and vaillant

  • As Latin passed into the Romance dialects of Gaul, valēre became Old French valoir, "to be worth, to be strong, to avail."
  • Its present participle was vaillant — literally "being strong, being worth."
  • During the high Middle Ages the participle hardened into an adjective meaning "strong, brave, worthy," and — crucially — the chivalric culture of feudal France reshaped its sense: physical strength was moralized. A vaillant knight was one whose bodily strength was placed in the service of honor, loyalty, and courage.

The Norman Crossing: vaillant → valiant

  • After the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman French flooded English with chivalric vocabulary: honor, chivalry, courage, valor, and valiant.
  • Middle English valiant is first attested around the 1330s, carrying the full knightly ideal of courage joined to honor.
  • In Middle English romance and chronicle, valiant knights, valiant deeds, and valiant hearts became fixed poetic formulas, cementing the word in the literary and historical register it still occupies.

Sound Changes

  • PIE *w- became Latin v- (pronounced /w/ in Classical Latin, later /v/), preserved in French and English v-.
  • Latin val- (short a) became Old French vail- through diphthongization of the stressed open syllable (a → ai), yielding the ai spelling that English retained.
  • The Latin present-participle stem valent- was remodeled in Old French as vaillant with a palatalized ll; English simplified this to a plain li-, giving modern valiant /ˈvæl.jənt/ (or /ˈvali.ənt/ in RP).

Semantic Development in English

  • 14th–15th centuries: valiant = brave in battle, the badge of the worthy knight; strongly martial.
  • 16th–18th centuries: widened from martial to moral courage — a valiant man could be valiant in faith, in justice, or in suffering.
  • 19th–20th centuries: extended to effort — a "valiant attempt" is a brave try that may fail; the word dignifies the striving rather than the result.
  • Contemporary: remains admiring, formal, and literary; applicable to any resolute courage of will, even in the small, the unarmed, or the defeated — which is precisely how it is used in the source passage about a small brave beast being honored.

Socio-Cultural Resonance and Literary Attestations

  • The word is inseparable from medieval romance: Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Chaucer, and the chanson de geste tradition all deploy valiant heroes.
  • In the Renaissance, Shakespeare uses valiant for both true courage and ironic or mocking bravery ("valiant flea"), showing the word's range.
  • Modern journalism reserves it for genuine acts of courage — "a valiant rescue," "a valiant effort" — and it remains a favorite of children's literature for honoring small heroes.

Summary

valiant runs from an Indo-European root of strength and power, through Latin valēre (strong and worth), Old French vaillant (chivalrically brave), and a Norman loan into Middle English, preserving across three millennia its double charge: courage that is not merely strong but worthy of admiration.
