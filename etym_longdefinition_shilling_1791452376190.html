Primary sense: a unit of British money. A shilling was the central silver coin and unit of account of the British currency system for more than four centuries. Until decimalisation on 15 February 1971 it was worth twelve old pence, or one twentieth of a pound sterling. Its abbreviation was s., from the Latin solidus, and it appeared in the familiar notation 1/6 (one shilling and sixpence) or 2/- (two shillings exactly). The word therefore names at once a coin, a value, and a place in a mental arithmetic that generations of British schoolchildren learned by heart.

The ladder of denominations. The shilling cannot be understood in isolation; it was one rung in a system whose internal ratios were memorised rather than calculated.

  • Penny (d.): twelve pence made one shilling; the abbreviation came from Latin denarius.
  • Threepenny piece: a quarter of a shilling, a small brass coin of the twentieth century.
  • Sixpence (slang tanner): half a shilling, a silver coin tied to weddings, Christmas puddings and lucky charms.
  • Florin: two shillings, introduced in 1849 as a tentative step towards decimal currency.
  • Half-crown: two shillings and sixpence, a substantial silver coin of the Victorian and Edwardian purse.
  • Crown: five shillings, struck mainly for commemorations rather than daily use.
  • Sovereign: twenty shillings, one pound in gold, the anchor of the gold standard.
  • Guinea: twenty-one shillings, a gentleman's unit of account for fees, horses, tailors and physicians long after the coin itself had vanished.

Secondary sense: the shilling abroad. The word travelled with British trade and imperial administration and proved remarkably durable. The East African shilling, issued under the East African Currency Board from 1921, circulated in Kenya, Uganda, Tanganyika and beyond until the successor states introduced national currencies in the 1960s. Today the shilling is the official currency of Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Somalia and South Sudan, each divided into one hundred cents. Austria used the Schilling from 1925 until the euro replaced it in 2002, and cognate forms such as skilling survive in Scandinavian monetary history. The shilling is thus a rare example of a medieval Germanic accounting unit that is still, in the twenty-first century, a working currency name on two continents.

Figurative and idiomatic senses. Because a shilling was small but real, it became a natural measure of a trifle, a token or a moral test.

  • To take the King's or Queen's shilling: to enlist in the army, from the recruiting practice of handing a coin to a new soldier as a binding token of service.
  • To cut someone off with a shilling: to disinherit them, from the old practice of leaving a single shilling so that a will could not be contested as accidental.
  • Not the full shilling: an Irish and British colloquialism for mentally deficient or not quite rational, implying that the coinage of the mind is short of its proper twelve pence.
  • Shilling shocker or shilling dreadful: a cheap, sensational novella of the late Victorian period, sold for a shilling and regarded as morally suspect.
  • A shilling in the slot: the coin-operated meter, gas meter or jukebox, a phrase that fixes the shilling in the material culture of the mid-twentieth century.
  • Worth a shilling / not worth a shilling: a blunt measure of value or contempt, still heard in historical fiction and period dialogue.

Technical and numismatic senses. In numismatics a shilling is defined by its issue, mintmark, metal and portrait; collectors distinguish the Elizabethan, Commonwealth, Hanoverian and Victorian series, and the last circulating British shilling bore the portrait of Elizabeth II and remained in use until decimalisation in 1971. In accountancy the shilling survived in bookkeeping notation long after the coin's withdrawal, and in Kenya and Tanzania the shilling is a fiat currency with a floating exchange rate, subdivided decimally rather than into twelve pence. In older legal and ecclesiastical documents a shilling often functions as a standard nominal penalty, a peppercorn rent or a token consideration, precisely because its smallness made it a convenient legal fiction.

The semantic bridge. The unifying idea behind these senses is division. A shilling was originally a partition of a larger unit of value, a slice of a pound or of a mark; from that arithmetic came the coin, from the coin came the idioms of smallness and tokenism, and from the imperial currency came the modern East African shilling. The word has moved from abstract reckoning to metal, from metal to metaphor, and from metaphor back to official currency, an unusually complete round trip in the life of a monetary term.
