- c. 1300 — little shore-stones: Middle English borrows Old French gravele for pebbles and grit, a mason's and gardener's word.
- 14th c. — the physician's metaphor: gritty bladder-stones become the gravel, and the word enters medicine for five centuries.
- 16th c. — verbs set sail and stick: to gravel a path, and to be gravelled like a ship stranded on a bank — perplexed, run aground for words.
- 18th–19th c. — the age of the road: Telford and McAdam lay broken stone across Britain, railways pack sleepers in ballast, and gravel becomes the foundation of the industrial landscape.
- Cobbett's 1820s — geology as politics: Thames terrace gravel under black loam becomes, in Rural Rides, the very image of thin hungry Middlesex feeding fat London.
- Today — paths and drives: the medical and perplexity senses are fossils, while tonnes of aggregate gravel pave the modern suburb — honourable, humble, and crunching.
