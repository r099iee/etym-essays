• Śūrpaṇakhā (Surpanakha): Similar because both are prominent female rakshasis in the Ramayana who confront Rama and initiate critical narrative turning points; differs because Tāṭakā is a primal monstrous brute slain outright for terrorizing holy sages in the northern wilderness, whereas Śūrpaṇakhā is Ravana's aristocratic sister whose sexual advances toward Rama and Lakshmana result in her mutilation, triggering the abduction of Sita and the grand war of Lanka.
  • Pūtanā (Putana): Similar because both are monstrous demonic women in Hindu epic and puranic lore who attempt to destroy an avatar in his youth; differs because Pūtanā targets the infant Krishna under the deceitful guise of an affectionate maternal wet-nurse using poisoned breast milk, whereas Tāṭakā confronts the adolescent Rama with overt, colossal physical violence and elemental sorcery in an open forest.
  • Hiraṇyākṣa (Hiranyaksha): Similar because both represent primal destructive entities that terrorize holy hermits and disrupt sacred cosmic equilibrium; differs because Hiraṇyākṣa is a cosmic Daitya king who submerges the entire Earth into the cosmic ocean requiring the Varaha avatar to rescue it, whereas Tāṭakā is an earthbound Yakshi-turned-rakshasi localized to the terrestrial forests of northern India.
  • Mārīca (Maricha): Similar because both are demonic kin (mother and son) possessing magical shape-shifting powers who cross paths with Rama in the forest; differs because Tāṭakā relies on terrifying brute force and localized devastation, whereas Mārīca relies on cunning illusion (maya), famously disguising himself as the golden deer to lure Rama away from Sita.
  • Rākṣasī (Rakshasi): Similar because Tāṭakā belongs to this generic ontological category of female carnivorous supernatural beings; differs because rākṣasī is a broad typological classification encompassing all female demons throughout Indic literature, whereas Tāṭakā is a specific historical-mythological individual with a distinct lineage, curse narrative, and moral role.
  • Yakṣiṇī (Yakshini): Similar because Tāṭakā was originally born into this class of nature spirits associated with fertility and earthly wealth; differs because typical Yakshinis remain benevolent, seductive tree-and-earth spirits of celestial beauty, whereas Tāṭakā was stripped of her Yaksha status and transformed into a horrifying cannibalistic monster by Agastya's curse.
  • Hārītī (Hariti): Similar because both are demonic mothers in Indic lore who initially devoured human children before being subdued by a divine figure; differs because Hārītī was converted by Gautama Buddha into a benevolent protector of children and childbirth in Buddhist tradition, whereas Tāṭakā was lethally dispatched by Rama's arrow.
  • Medusa: Similar in comparative mythology because both were originally beautiful women who suffered a catastrophic bodily curse transforming them into terrifying monstrous figures slain by a young hero-prince; differs because Medusa petrifies with her gaze in Greco-Roman myth and is decapitated by Perseus, whereas Tāṭakā uses colossal physical strength and weather sorcery and is pierced through the chest by Rama.
