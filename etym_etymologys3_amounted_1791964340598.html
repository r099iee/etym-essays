amounted reaches English through the French of the Norman and Plantagenet administrations, and its history begins not with counting but with climbing.

From mountain to verb

The ultimate source is the Latin phrase ad montem, “to the mountain”, “uphill”. Spoken Latin fused the preposition and the noun into admontem, which became Old French amont, an adverb meaning “up, upward, above” — a word still alive in modern French (en amont, “upstream”). From that adverb French formed the verb amonter, “to go up, rise, mount”, and Anglo-French carried amounten into English legal and commercial writing in the medieval period.

  • The Latin noun mons, montis is the same word that gives English mount, mountain, surmount, dismount and paramount — the last from French par amont, “by above”.
  • Tantamount is the closest cousin of all: Anglo-French tant amunter, “to amount to as much”, preserves the identical verb inside a single frozen adjective.
  • The pairing shows how thoroughly the metaphor of height organised medieval ideas of quantity: to reach a sum was to climb to it.

The literal sense in English

Middle English amounten could still mean simply “to go up, ascend”, and the sense survived into the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries in elevated and technical prose. A rider could amount a hill; smoke could amount into the air. This physical meaning is now entirely obsolete in standard English, but it explains why the word for a total is a word of rising rather than of adding.

The arithmetical turn

By the sixteenth century the dominant sense is financial and arithmetical: a set of figures, debts or receipts amounts to a stated sum. The image is that of a column of numbers in an account book, each entry adding to the height of the column until the final line gives the total. The noun amount — “the total, the quantity” — follows in the same period and soon supplies the everyday phrases a large amount, any amount, the amount due.

From sum to significance

The evaluative construction is a natural extension. If a collection of items “amounts to” a figure, then the figure is what the collection is worth; transfer the notion of worth from money to meaning and one arrives at “his silence amounted to consent”. The step is documented in English prose from the seventeenth century onward, and it turned a bookkeeping verb into one of the language’s most useful instruments of measured judgment.

  • Numerical: the bill amounted to £400.
  • Equivalence: the delay amounted to a refusal.
  • Outcome: the affair amounted to a scandal.
  • Deflation: his contribution amounted to very little.

The ending -ed

The form amounted is morphologically unremarkable but historically instructive. English builds the past tense of weak verbs with a dental suffix whose Old English shape was -ode in the singular and -od in the participle, from Proto-Germanic *-ōdē. A verb borrowed from French is simply fitted with that native suffix, which is why amount behaves like a native verb despite its foreign root. Phonetically the ending is syllabic after the final -t of the stem, giving the pronunciation /əˈmaʊn.tɪd/ with a distinct vowel in the last syllable.

Modern usage

In contemporary English the numerical sense has narrowed towards finance, statistics and formal reporting, while the evaluative sense has expanded into law, journalism and criticism. Legal drafting is especially fond of it: an act “amounts to” a waiver, a breach, a repudiation or a nuisance. The form is also a favourite hedge — what at times amounted to, as in the source, lets a writer claim an extraordinary effect while conceding that it was intermittent. The obsolete literal sense survives only in the metaphor that started it all: totals still mount, and a mounting total still amounts to something.
