• Barbarity: similar because both descend from the same Greek–Latin "barbarous" stock and denote uncivilized character or cruelty; differs because "barbarity" names the quality of acts or traits (savageness, cruelty) and is almost always moral, whereas "barbarism" primarily names a stage or state of society and can be used without reference to cruelty at all.
  • Savagery: similar because both denote a primitive, uncivilized condition; differs because "savagery" (Latin silva "forest," the wild-forest dweller) tilts toward bloodiness, ferocity, and the hunt, while "barbarism" may describe a merely backward or uncultivated condition with no wildness attached.
  • Primitiveness / primitivism: similar because both name an early or underdeveloped stage of culture; differs because "primitiveness" is comparatively neutral-descriptive, and "primitivism" is a cultural movement that values the primitive — the exact reverse of barbarism's disapproval.
  • Rudeness: similar because older English used "rudeness" where we now use "barbarism," for lack of cultivation; differs because modern "rudeness" has narrowed to mere bad manners between individuals, losing the historical and civilizational scope.
  • Brutality: similar because both can label cruel, violent conduct; differs because "brutality" (Latin brutus "heavy, dull, brute") is confined to force and violence: it has no stage-of-society sense and no linguistic sense.
  • Atrocity: similar because both can name a dreadful, uncivilized act; differs because "atrocity" is precise and legally heavy (crimes against humanity, war crimes), while "barbarism" in the act-sense stays rhetorical and diffuse.
  • Solecism: similar because both are errors of language; differs because "solecism" (from the "bad Greek" said to be spoken at Soloi) is strictly a breach of syntax or idiom, while a rhetorical barbarism is an error within a single word, including foreign or mis-formed words.
  • Vulgarity / vulgarism: similar because both mark speech or taste as unrefined; differs because "vulgarity" implicates the common crowd (vulgus "the people") and class judgments rather than foreignness or historical backwardness.
  • Philistinism: similar because both mean hostility or indifference to art and culture; differs because "philistinism" (via Matthew Arnold, from the biblical Philistines) denotes specifically bourgeois disdain for the finer things, not a whole social stage.
  • Incivility: similar because both are the lack of "civil" polish; differs because "incivility" means discourtesy or bad manners — a lightweight etiquette term — while "barbarism" implies the wholesale absence of culture.
