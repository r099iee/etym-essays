Ascot is a proper noun denoting the village and world-famous racecourse in Berkshire, England, and by metonymy the annual Royal Ascot horse-racing meeting held there in mid-June — the centrepiece of the English summer sporting and fashion calendar, attended by the monarch, famous for top hats, lavish millinery, and elite flat racing. In the source sentence Jeeves does not wish "to miss __________": the valet, a stickler for sartorial and social ritual, refuses to forgo the June meeting even for a Riviera holiday in Cannes. The joke depends on knowing that for Jeeves, Ascot outranks the seaside.

Core place sense: Ascot is a town on the edge of Windsor Great Park, site of Ascot Racecourse founded by Queen Anne in 1711. To say "at Ascot", "to Ascot", or "down at Ascot" means at that course. Example: "She stabled her filly near Ascot." Purely geographical, capitalized, pronounced ASK-ut.

  • Event sense — Royal Ascot: five days of Group 1 flat races (Gold Cup, Prince of Wales's Stakes, Diamond Jubilee) in the third week of June, with the Royal Procession, Royal Enclosure dress code, and global television coverage. Example: "They missed Ascot this year" means they missed the meeting, not the village. The bridge is standard metonymy: place for event held there, as with Wimbledon or Epsom.
  • Fashion and social sense: Ascot connotes morning dress, tailcoats, waistcoats, and extravagant hats; an "Ascot hat" is a wide, theatrical creation; an "ascot tie" (lower-case in American usage) is the loose cravat worn with morning dress, named from racegoers. Example: "That brim is pure Ascot." Here the word evokes class display, tradition, and press photography.
  • Racing and betting jargon: "Ascot form", "an Ascot winner", "Ascot ground" (the turf's going) use the name as a quality stamp: winning at Ascot proves a horse at the highest level. Example: "He is an Ascot horse" means top-class miler or stayer.
  • Global echoes: Ascot Racecourse in Perth, Ascot in Brisbane, and streets named Ascot borrow the prestige. The semantic bridge is honorific imitation, common for Epsom, Goodwood, and Henley.

Register, grammar, and boundaries: Always capitalized for place and meeting; pronounced /ˈæskət/, never "as-COT". It takes no article for the meeting ("to miss Ascot", "at Ascot") but "the Ascot meeting" or "the Ascot Gold Cup" for races. It does not mean any race-meeting generally, nor a hat generally: context must invoke Berkshire royalty. Its power in Wodehouse is characterological — Jeeves's loyalty to Ascot signals his devotion to English correctness over continental pleasure.

Wodehouse nuance and social comedy: Bertie narrates Ascot as a fixed point of the valet year: devotion to correct dress, correct horses, and correct company peaks at Ascot, so leaving Jeeves behind to guard against missing it is both considerate and comic. For modern readers the name also summons television images of royal carriages, bookmakers boards, photo-finishes, and rain-or-shine millinery, all condensed into two syllables. To miss Ascot is thus to miss England itself in miniature.
