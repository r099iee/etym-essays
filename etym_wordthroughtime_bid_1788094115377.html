- Two Old English verbs, bēodan “offer, proclaim, command” and biddan “ask, entreat, pray”, collide in Middle English into one verb bidden — the command and the prayer share a single form.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) — “bidden/bēden” covers commanding, asking, praying and offering; the prayer sense survives in “bidding prayer” and in “bead” (a rosary prayer, from OE gebed).
- 13th century — the auction sense is born: “to bid” = to offer a price for goods at market.
- 14th–17th centuries — the literary command sense flourishes in Bible and drama; the King James Version uses bid/bade/bidden for divine and royal commands; “bid farewell” and “bid welcome” crystallize as formulas.
- 18th–19th centuries — “bid” extends from auctions to ambition: “a bid for power”, “to bid for the crown”; the noun gains the sense “attempt”.
- 20th century — business tenders, takeover bids, electoral bids and the bridge table all use the word; the command sense recedes into archaism, so that “we bid him consider” now sounds medieval — exactly the register Tolkien wanted.
