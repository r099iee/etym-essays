1. Etymological Architecture: A Dual-Root Germanic Toponym. The compound proper name Weathertop represents a classic English geographical place-name created by J.R.R. Tolkien to serve as the vernacular Westron translation of the Sindarin Elvish name Amon Sûl. It brings together two deep-seated Proto-Indo-European roots: the meteorological root *h₂wéh₁-dʰro-m (yielding weather) and the topographic root *dewb- / *dʰewb- (yielding top).

2. Evolution of Root 1: Weather (PIE *h₂wéh₁- to Old English weder).

  • Proto-Indo-European Roots: The root originates in the verbal base *h₂wéh₁- ("to blow"), which also produced wind (PIE *h₂wéh₁-nt-) and Greek aēr ("air"). By appending the instrumental-nominal suffix *-dʰro-m, Proto-Indo-European formed *h₂wéh₁-dʰro-m, literally meaning "the instrument or manifestation of blowing—the wind and atmospheric condition." Cognates include Old Church Slavonic vedro ("clear weather") and Lithuanian vėtra ("tempest, storm").
  • Proto-Germanic *wedrą: Through regular Germanic sound shifts, the root developed into *wedrą, meaning "weather, wind, storm, or air." Notably, in ancient Germanic tongues, the word frequently carried an ominous connotation of "bad weather, storm, or gale," rather than a neutral meteorological state.
  • Old English weder: In Anglo-Saxon literature, weder was ubiquitous. It denoted both atmospheric seasons and violent tempests. In Beowulf, the Geats are repeatedly referred to as the Wederas or Weder-Gēatas ("Weather-Geats," those who brave storm-tossed seas), and compounds like weder-burg ("storm-city") and weder-wolcen ("storm-cloud") were commonplace.
  • Middle to Modern English: In Middle English, weder yielded modern weather, maintaining both its broad meteorological sense and its active verbal usage ("to weather a storm," meaning to endure harsh elements).

3. Evolution of Root 2: Top (PIE *dewb- to Old English topp).

  • Proto-Indo-European Origins: The root traces to the nominal base *dewb- or *dʰewb-, signifying a "tuft, tip, crest, or pointed apex."
  • Proto-Germanic *tuppaz: In Proto-Germanic, it appeared as *tuppaz ("crest, tuft of hair, summit"), cognate with Old Norse toppr ("tuft of hair, crest of a hill") and Old High German zopf ("braid, tuft of hair").
  • Old English topp: In Anglo-Saxon usage, topp primarily denoted the highest point or apex of an object, such as the crown of a human head, the pinnacle of a tower, or the summit of an isolated hill or knoll.
  • Toponymic Development in Middle English: During the Middle English period, top became a standard topographic suffix in English geographic nomenclature, applied to prominent, exposed knolls across the English Pennines and Lake District (e.g., Black Top, High Top).

4. Tolkien's Philological Synthesis: Amon Sûl and Weathertop. When constructing the geography of Eriador, Tolkien systematically aligned his Westron toponyms with the physical reality of the landscape and the underlying Elvish nomenclature:

  • The Sindarin Original: In Sindarin, the hill was named Amon Sûl. The element amon is a common Elvish noun for an isolated hill or mount (seen in Amon Amarth, Mount Doom; Amon Hen, Hill of Sight; Amon Lhaw, Hill of Hearing). The genitive element sûl is derived from the Elvish root SŪ / THŪ, denoting "wind, breath, blowing."
  • The Westron Translation: In Westron, the common folk did not employ high Elvish names, but instead translated them into plain descriptive terms. By compounding weather (carrying its archaic Anglo-Saxon sense of fierce, howling wind and exposed gales) with top (the lonely, bald peak), Tolkien captured the exact spirit of Amon Sûl.
  • Literary Resonance: The name Weathertop evokes an immediate sensory atmosphere of cold, desolate wind howling through abandoned ruins, perfectly preparing the reader for the vulnerability of the hobbits and the terrifying encounter with the wraiths beneath the darkening sky.
