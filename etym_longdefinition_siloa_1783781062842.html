SILOA (proper noun; coexisting form Siloam). A poetic, Vulgate-derived English name for the pool and conduit of Siloam in Jerusalem, used by Milton in Paradise Lost, Book I (1667): the gently flowing brook "fast by the Oracle of God" (the Temple Mount). Because it is a proper noun its polysemy is not the polysemy of a common noun but the layering of senses, functions, and associations that a single holy place accumulates over nearly three millennia of biblical, liturgical, literary, and archaeological use. A complete lexicographic account must register each of those layers. Senses and uses:
  • The waterwork and pool (primary, etymological and geographical sense). The name originally denotes a piece of ancient hydraulic engineering: the conduit, aqueduct, and rock-cut pool that "sent" the waters of the spring of Gihon, through Hezekiah's 533-meter tunnel (c. 701 BCE), from outside the city wall down into a pool inside the City of David at the mouth of the Tyropoeon Valley. This is the sense behind 2 Kings 20:20 and 2 Chronicles 32:30, where the word designates the watercourse Hezekiah constructed.
  • The soft-flowing sacred water (prophetic sense). Isaiah 8:6 speaks of "the waters of Shiloah that go softly," contrasting the gentle, trustful water of Jerusalem with the onrushing flood of Assyria; the image became proverbial for humble faith in God rather than in alliances and armies.
  • The healing pool (New Testament sense). In John 9:7 Jesus sends the man born blind to wash in the pool of Siloam, "which is by interpretation, Sent," and he returns seeing. This made Siloa/Siloam a standing emblem of divine healing, of baptism (the washing of the font), of spiritual illumination, and of Christ himself as the One "sent" by the Father.
  • The Miltonic symbol of inspiration (17th-century literary sense). In Paradise Lost, "Siloa's Brook" is placed deliberately beside Sion Hill and against the "Aonian Mount": the softly flowing water of Judeo-Christian revelation is Milton's chosen alternative to the pagan Muses' Helicon as the source of his epic song.
  • The hymnodic sense (18th–19th-century devotional). In the hymn tradition (e.g. "Siloam's shady rill" in the well-known children's hymn), the pool becomes an image of gentle grace, of the quiet flow of blessing, and of the soul's still waters.
  • The typological / patristic sense. Church fathers and liturgical commentary read the pool as a type of baptism and of the Church; the blind man's washing is a figure of the catechumen emerging from the font illumined.
  • The modern living toponym (contemporary geographical sense). The Arabic neighborhood Silwan (Silwan), built over the ancient village at the site in the Kidron Valley just outside the Old City walls, preserves the name in living geography, and archaeology at the biblical pool (excavated from the 2000s) keeps the name current in news and scholarship.
  • Metonymic / poetic use for Jerusalem's waters generally. In devotional poetry Siloa can stand loosely for the sacred streams of the holy city, just as Parnassus stands for Greek poetry.
Historical bridges and reasoning:
  • The whole polysemy grows from a single root metaphor, "sending." The spring of Gihon sends its water through the conduit; God sends His Son; the Son sends the blind man to the pool; the washing sends the darkness from his eyes; Christ is the Sent One. Every later sense re-reads the literal sending of water as a divine sending, so that a piece of waterworks becomes a theology.
  • Formal transmission: Hebrew Šīlōaḥ → Greek Σιλωάμ Silōám (Septuagint, Gospels) and the shorter Σιλωά Silōá → Latin Siloa (Vulgate-derived) → English Siloam (Tyndale 1530, King James 1611) beside the poetic Latin-form Siloa (Milton 1667). The double English form is itself part of the word's history and register split.
  • Register split today: Siloa is archaic, poetic, and Miltonic; Siloam is the biblical and scholarly form; Silwan is the living Arabic name. Revivals of Siloa occur in poetry and hymnody aiming at 17th-century diction.
Meaning today: outside biblical, liturgical, literary, archaeological, and tourist contexts the word is not part of ordinary vocabulary. It is a proper noun whose meaning is anchored in the Bible (Isaiah 8; John 9), in Milton's Paradise Lost, in the geography and archaeology of Jerusalem, and in the devotional hymnody that kept "Siloam's rill" alive in Sunday schools and churches.
