The compound snuffbox joins two histories that met in the pocket of late 17th-century Europe: a new colonial powder and a very old container. Each half of the word travelled for centuries before the hinge of a little silver lid brought them together.

First element: snuff, from sniff to powder. The verb footing is Middle Dutch snuffen, to sniff or snuffle — an imitative formation of the kind every language reinvents, built on the audible sn- cluster of nose-words that English also keeps in sniff, snort, sneeze, and snore. Middle English borrowed snuffen by the 1300s in the plain nasal sense of sniffing or snorting, including the snuffing of a candle — trimming its wick so it burns clear. When powdered tobacco arrived from the Americas and conquered the courts of France and Spain as an inhaled luxury, English already owned the exact verb for what users did with it, and the noun snuff narrowed from the act of sniffing to the powder sniffed. By the later 1600s snuff unambiguously means scented powdered tobacco, ground in mills, perfumed with flowers and oils, and graded like wine.

  • Tobacco's voyage: the plant is American; Columbus's men saw it smoked, Jean Nicot sent it to the French court in 1560, and by the mid-1600s the French aristocracy preferred it powdered and inhaled. Catherine de' Medici's headaches, treated with tobacco powder, gave snuff its first fashionable testimonial, and from Paris the habit radiated to London, where Charles II's exiled court had acquired the taste.
  • Sound and spelling: Dutch snuffen entered a Middle English ear already full of native sn- words, so it naturalized without friction; the noun snuff kept its short vowel and final double consonant while the candle-trimming sense branched off into snuffers, the scissors for wicks — a reminder that one sniffing root served fire as well as tobacco.
  • Socio-cultural moment: taking snuff was cleaner than smoking in an age of wigs and lace, kinder to company, and endlessly ritualizable — the tap, the offer, the pinch, the sneeze — which is why moralists fretted about it exactly as later generations fretted about cigarettes.

Second element: box, from boxwood to container. The container half is ancient. Greek pyxos named the dense evergreen boxwood prized by carvers; from the tree came pyxis, a small lidded casket of boxwood for ointments and jewels, carried by Greek women to the bath. Latin borrowed it as buxis, keeping both the tree and the casket senses, and the word passed into Germanic as Old English box — already generalized beyond wood to any small case. Note the consonant journey: Greek p to Latin b, the y vowel settling toward short o, the ending worn down — a textbook borrowing chain across three civilizations, with the meaning broadening at each step from a species of tree to any handy receptacle.

  • From wood to world: by Middle English box could be made of anything — ivory, horn, metal — and could hold anything; the compound snuffbox, first recorded in the 1680s, simply names the newest and most fashionable thing a box was asked to hold.
  • Stress and spelling: the closed compound takes single initial stress, SNUFF-box, and has kept its plain spelling against the French rival tabatière, which English borrowed alongside it but reserved for connoisseur talk.

The golden age and after. The 1700s made the snuffbox the jewel it remains in museums: English goldsmiths, French émailleurs, and Russian court workshops produced portrait boxes, patriotic boxes, and snuffboxes that were really miniature paintings with lids. Snuffboxes served as diplomatic currency — a prince's portrait set in diamonds, presented to an ambassador, was a pension in portable form. Satirists from Pope to Hogarth used the box as shorthand for foppery and court polish. Then, across the 1800s, cigars and cigarettes displaced the pinch; the boxes that remained became presentation pieces, masonic jewels, and grandmotherly relics. Medicine preserved the word in flesh: the wrist hollow where the pinch was laid became the anatomical snuffbox, still examined for scaphoid fractures today. So the modern word points three ways at once — to the antique in the cabinet, to the hollow on the hand, and, as in the source sentence, to the vanished etiquette of leaving a shining thing behind so that friendship outlasts absence.
