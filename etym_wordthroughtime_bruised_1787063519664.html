- Old English brȳsan 'to crush' and Old French bruisier 'to break, shatter' merge in Middle English (c. 1200-1300) as brisen/bruisen - a broad sense of crushing by force
- 14th-16th centuries - the sense narrows: bruise specialises from 'crush, shatter' to 'injure by a blow without breaking the skin'; the black-and-blue mark becomes the everyday picture
- 17th-18th centuries - extends to fruit and produce damaged by rough handling ('bruised apples'); the 'crush' senses survive only in cooking (bruised herbs, bruised mint)
- 19th century - the figurative extension ripens: 'bruised pride', 'bruised feelings' - invisible wounds spoken of with the language of visible ones; religious and literary prose had long used 'a bruised reed' for fragility
- 20th-21st centuries - bruised is ubiquitous in sport, medicine, and emotional language; 'bruised and battered' pairs it with buffeted; the healing bruise remains the image of hurt that does not break
