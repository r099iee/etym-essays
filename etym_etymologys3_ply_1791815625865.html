The ancestry of ply is straightforward where it matters and obscure only at the very bottom. Latin plicāre meant “to fold, bend, lay one thing on another”, and it was extraordinarily productive: applicāre (to fold toward, hence apply), implicāre (to enfold, hence involve), replicāre (to fold back, hence reply), multiplicāre (to fold many times). The root behind it is reconstructed as PIE *pleḱ- “to plait, fold, weave”, which also yields Greek plékein “to plait” and Latin plexus “plaited”. The Latin verb passed into Gallo-Romance as Old French plier (also pleier), “to fold, bend, give way”, and it is from that verb that English took both its verb and, along a separate channel, its noun.

Middle English: bending and applying

Middle English plien, recorded from the fourteenth century, first meant to bend or fold something, and then, reflexively, to bend oneself to a task — to apply oneself. The second sense is the hinge on which the modern word turns: once “bend oneself to” is available, “ply one’s trade” follows naturally, and it is recorded by about 1400. The old physical sense then faded, leaving only its relatives: pliable, pliant, supple and the ballet term plié, all preserving the image of a body folding.

Four branches of the modern verb

  • Work at a calling (c. 1400): the craftsman’s sense, and the one in the source. It implies continuity and assiduity, and it is now slightly elevated in register — a phrase for obituaries and biographies.
  • Wield, use vigorously (late 14th c.): “ply the oars”, “ply the whip”. Here the original notion of repeated strokes survives most clearly.
  • Press with offers (1620s): “ply with drink”, “ply with questions”. The construction generalises the idea of repeated application from a tool to a person, who becomes the surface worked upon.
  • Traverse a route regularly (1803, originally of ships): a commercial and timetable sense, later extended to coaches, buses, taxis and, in journalism, to anyone moving steadily through a place.

Nautical and technical life

The nautical ply, “to beat to windward”, is recorded in the sixteenth century and belongs with the older sense of working against resistance: the ship plies, tack upon tack, to make ground. Alongside it grew the noun ply, “a fold, layer, strand”, borrowed from Old French pli and first recorded in the late fourteenth century. That noun is the source of the modern measurement words: two-ply and three-ply for yarn and timber, plywood (an American coinage of the early twentieth century) for boards glued layer upon layer, and multi-ply for tyres and tissue. The same noun supplies the heraldic and textile senses of a fold or pleat.

Register and modern distribution

  • Literary and formal: “ply a trade”, “ply one’s craft” — the word lends dignity to manual work and is common in profiles of artisans.
  • Journalistic: “ply with questions”, “taxis plying the streets”, “boats plying the crossing” — neutral, descriptive, faintly old-fashioned but never archaic.
  • Legal and regulatory: “ply for hire” appears in licensing law and local bylaws, where it names the offence of soliciting passengers without a licence.
  • Commercial: the noun compounds are entirely unmarked — nobody hears a Latin verb in “three-ply”.

What the word is not

Ply is not a shortening of apply, though the two are cognates and the sense “apply oneself” probably reinforced the English verb. Nor is the noun ply a development of the verb: it is a parallel borrowing, which is why the same spelling can mean both “to work at” and “a layer”. The homography is an accident of French phonology — Old French plier and pli are simply the verb and its deverbal noun — and English inherited both.

Carlyle’s “ply their business” is therefore a small act of lexical invention: he takes the artisanal collocation and applies it to commerce, making the city’s whole economy sound like a craft handed down and practised daily, indifferent to the fall of kings.
