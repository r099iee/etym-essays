firma is the feminine singular of the Latin adjective firmus “firm, solid, steadfast”. It is not an English word in its own right: it survives in English inside the phrase terra firma, where it agrees with the feminine noun terra and means “solid, firm”. In the source sentence — “the great terra firma of custom” — it supplies the note of stability: custom is the firm ground on which the restless phenomena of society stand.

Latin adjectives of the first and second declension take -a in the feminine, so firmus (masculine), firma (feminine), firmum (neuter) are one paradigm. English readers meet the feminine form because terra is feminine; the phrase is a miniature Latin noun-adjective pair, not an English compound.

The Latin senses of firmus and firma

  • Physically solid. Of ground, stone, timber or a building: firmum solum “firm ground”, firma terra “solid earth”. This is the sense at the root of terra firma.
  • Steadfast in character. Of a person or a friendship: reliable, constant, not easily moved — firmus amicus, firma fides “steadfast faith”.
  • Secure in law and politics. Of peace, alliance, treaty or possession: settled, guaranteed, not liable to be upset — firma pax, firmum imperium.
  • Strong in argument. Of evidence, proof or testimony: sound, weighty, convincing — the sense that passes into English firm as “definite, not tentative”.

English life of the form

  • terra firma — dry land, the mainland, solid ground; figuratively any secure footing or dependable foundation. The phrase is the only context in which most English speakers ever meet firma.
  • “Firma” alone — in English dictionaries it appears as a Latin citation or as a combining element in Latin-derived names; it is not used as a common noun.
  • The farm branch — Medieval Latin firma meant a fixed payment or rent, and through Old French ferme it gave English farm, originally “land held at a fixed rent”. This is a genuine descendant of the same Latin adjective, but it belongs to a different semantic line from terra firma.
  • The signature branch — in Italian and Spanish, firma means “signature”, from firmare “to fix, confirm (a document)”. English speakers who know those languages may expect this sense, but English does not borrow it.

Related English words from the same root

  • firm (adjective) — through Old French ferme, the direct French descendant of firmus: solid, unyielding, decided.
  • firmament — Latin firmamentum, the vault of heaven, literally a “support” or “strengthening”, used in the Vulgate for the expanse of Genesis.
  • confirm, affirm, infirm, firmness, firmly — all built on firmus or firmare.

How the sense developed

The physical sense is primary: something firm is something that holds together and can be leaned on. From ground that does not give way, Latin moved easily to promises that do not give way, then to payments fixed by agreement — a rent that is “firm” because it cannot be varied. The modern English cluster therefore contains three branches from one adjective: solidity (firm, terra firma), confirmation (confirm, affirm), and fixed tenure (farm).

Register and connotation

  • In English, firma is learned and slightly ornamental; it signals classical culture and is normally italicised.
  • Its connotation is reassuring: firmness implies safety, reliability and the absence of slippage.
  • In the source passage the phrase is used with deliberate gravity: custom is not merely old but foundational, the dry land on which law’s waves break.
