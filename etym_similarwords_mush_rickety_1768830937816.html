• Mash: Similar because both name soft, crushed, pulpy masses of food or matter, and mush is historically a dialectal variant of mash; differs because mash is crushed solid matter (mashed potato, brewing mash) while mush is a boiled soft porridge and, figuratively, cloying sentiment.
  • Porridge: Similar because both are soft cereal foods cooked to a thick pulp; differs because porridge is the general British term for oatmeal gruel, while mush specifically means boiled cornmeal in America and carries the figurative "sentiment" sense that porridge lacks.
  • Gruel: Similar because both are soft, semi-liquid cereal foods; differs because gruel is thinner (a watered-down soup, the workhouse fare of Oliver Twist) while mush is thicker and more substantial.
  • Pap: Similar because both are soft semi-liquid foods and both metaphorically mean bland, undemanding material; differs because pap is infant and invalid food with a contemptuous intellectual sense, while mush is a general porridge whose figurative sense is cloying sentiment rather than intellectual banality.
  • Pulp: Similar because both name soft, wet, formless matter; differs because pulp is produced by crushing (wood pulp, fruit pulp) and is neutral or industrial, while mush is a food-based soft mass with a sentimental figurative life.
  • Slush: Similar because both are soft, wet, semi-solid masses (snow slush, emotional slush); differs because slush is watery and melting, while mush is thick and porridge-like.
  • Sludge: Similar because both are thick, soft, semi-fluid masses; differs because sludge is foul, industrial, or sewage-like, while mush is innocuous or even nourishing.
  • Glop: Similar because both name soft, shapeless, unappetizing food or material; differs because glop is a dismissive coinage for gooey mass, while mush has a respectable porridge sense.
  • Shaky: Similar because both mean unstable and liable to fail; differs because shaky describes any trembling instability (a shaky voice, shaky hands) while rickety specifically names a structure or body on the verge of collapse.
  • Wobbly: Similar because both describe loose, unsteady motion; differs because wobbly emphasizes rocking, side-to-side instability, while rickety emphasizes unsoundness and imminent collapse.
  • Ramshackle: Similar because both describe structures in poor, fragile condition; differs because ramshackle (from "ransack") implies a neglected, tumble-down building, while rickety emphasizes the loose, tottering jointedness of any weak frame.
  • Dilapidated: Similar because both describe decayed, falling structures; differs because dilapidated (from Latin lapis, stone) implies stonework falling to ruin through neglect, while rickety is about weak joints and imminent wobble.
  • Decrepit: Similar because both describe something old and worn down; differs because decrepit (from Latin crepare, to creak) emphasizes age and feebleness of persons or things, while rickety emphasizes structural looseness.
  • Unsteady: Similar because both mean not firm or stable; differs because unsteady is general and neutral, while rickety is vivid and specifically about jointed frames about to give way.
  • Tottering: Similar because both describe something about to fall; differs because tottering emphasizes the trembling, swaying motion just before a fall, while rickety names the static unsoundness that causes it.
