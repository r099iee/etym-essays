Shuffling is the present participle and gerund of shuffle, used both as a verb form and as an adjective and noun. Its meanings cluster around two connected ideas: a repeated, dragging, scraping motion of the feet (a shuffling walk), and the repeated mixing or moving about of objects — above all playing cards. From the physical base grow the figurative senses of evasiveness, hesitation, and clumsy or shifty handling.

Primary senses

  • Dragging gait: walking without lifting the feet, sliding them along the ground — "a shuffling step", "the shuffling of feet in the corridor". Typical of the aged, the infirm, the exhausted, the barefoot, or the cautious, and often used of crowds and queues.
  • Mixing cards: interleaving and rearranging a deck of playing cards so that their order is randomised — "shuffling the pack", "a quick shuffle before dealing". This is the dominant modern verb use.
  • Moving or rearranging objects: sliding things about rather than lifting them — "shuffling papers on his desk", "shuffling the chairs", "shuffling files" — including, in computing, the reordering of data.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Evasiveness and dishonesty: "to shuffle" can mean to dodge a question, equivocate, or act shiftily — "stop shuffling and answer me"; as an adjective it describes evasive, prevaricating behaviour ("a shuffling answer").
  • "Shuffle off": to get rid of, evade, or cast aside — made famous by Shakespeare's Hamlet: "shuffle off this mortal coil" (1602), i.e. escape from life; modern "shuffled off" means shirked or passed on (responsibility, blame).
  • Dance and music: the shuffle is a sliding, syncopated dance step of the jazz and blues era (the shuffle step, ancestor of the Lindy hop), and "shuffle" names the syncopated "swing" rhythm of blues, jazz, and rock drumming.
  • Random playback: in digital music and media, "shuffle" is the mode that plays songs in random order — "put it on shuffle", "shuffle all" — extending the card-mixing sense into software.
  • Computing: "shuffle" describes reordering operations (the Fisher–Yates shuffle, deck-shuffling algorithms); "shuffling" data between processes is routine in distributed systems.
  • Zoological and mechanical: penguins and hermit crabs are said to shuffle; there are card-shuffling machines; "shuffleboard" is a game of sliding discs — the word keeps generating sliding-motion compounds.

Polysemy: the semantic bridge

The verb "shuffle" is a frequentative of "shove": to shove again and again, with the -le suffix (as in "wriggle", "sparkle"). Every sense is the same repeated pushing motion transferred to a different surface or context: the feet pushed repeatedly along the floor give the dragging gait; cards pushed repeatedly across the table give the mixing; a person who "shuffles" through a question is metaphorically pushing words around rather than facing them; and the random-play "shuffle" is the card-mixing sense digitised. The common thread is repetition without lifting — motion that is continuous, sliding, and slightly uncontrolled, which is why "shuffling" so often carries undertones of awkwardness, weariness, or evasiveness.

Register and usage

  • Everyday and informal-to-neutral; "shuffling" as a noun ("the shuffling of feet") and as an adjective ("a shuffling old man", "a shuffling gait") are both common in fiction.
  • The evasive sense is somewhat dated and literary, but survives in criticism of legal and bureaucratic conduct ("shuffling the blame").
  • In technology and music the word is thoroughly modern, showing an old verb happily adapting to algorithms and playlists.
