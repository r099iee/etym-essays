Chain for the single root of SCALLOPED (one root: PIE *skel- “to cut, split”). Variable stages = 7. 1. PIE *skel- “to cut, split, separate” — a root of division; reconstructed from Sanskrit śakala “a fragment, chip, splinter” (probable), Greek skállō “to hoe, dig, scrape” (probable), and the Germanic family (c. 4500–3000 BCE). 2. → Proto-Germanic *skaljō “shell, husk, scale” — the thing split off or peeled away; the covering that protects. 3. → Frankish *skalja “shell, husk” — the West Germanic form carried into Gaul by the Franks. 4. → Old French escalope “shell, husk; a slice” (9th–12th c.) — borrowed from Frankish; the sense “slice” reflects the same idea of something cut off. 5. → Middle English scalop / scaloppe “the scallop (the fan-shelled bivalve)” (15th c.) — the shellfish named for its fluted, fan-shaped shell. 6. → English scallop (noun) “the edible bivalve with a fluted shell” (16th c. onward). 7. → English scalloped (adjective) “cut or ornamented with shell-like semicircular curves” (16th–17th c.) — Final node: English SCALLOPED (rootWord), formed from the noun by the regular participial -ed; the cookery sense “baked in a scallop shell or shell-shaped dish” develops in the 18th–19th c. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The PIE root *skel- is secure, but the precise Sanskrit connection (śakala) is debated by some scholars, who would derive it from a parallel root *śal- “to split”; it is probable rather than certain. (2) The route from PIE *skel- to Proto-Germanic *skaljō is standard; the Frankish → Old French borrowing (stages 3–4) is well attested for the word family (cf. escale > English scale). (3) The modern French doublet escalope “a thin slice of meat” (English escalope) descends from the same Old French word and illustrates the “slice = cut off” sense; it is a sibling, not an ancestor, of the English scallop.
