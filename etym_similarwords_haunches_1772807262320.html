• Hips: Similar because both name the lower side region of the body where the leg joins the trunk; differs because hips names the joint and the bony area only and is the neutral everyday word, whereas haunches adds the buttock-and-thigh mass and a coarser, more animal register.
  • Buttocks: Similar because both denote the rear sitting area of the body; differs because buttocks is purely gluteal and can sound crude or clinical, while haunches covers the hip and the upper thigh as well.
  • Hindquarters: Similar because both denote the rear body zone, especially of animals; differs because hindquarters is strongly veterinary (horses, dogs, game) and is rarely used of a seated person.
  • Thighs: Similar because both cover part of the upper leg; differs because thighs names only the leg segment and misses the hip-and-buttock region that haunches combines.
  • Rump: Similar because both name the hind part of a body; differs because rump is chiefly the posterior and back part (of animals or people) and does not normally imply the hip-and-thigh mass or a squatting posture.
  • Bum / backside / behind: Similar because these informal words name the rear; differs because they are purely gluteal slang with no hip-thigh zone and no animal or culinary sense.
  • Hams: Similar because a dated sense of hams denoted the back of the thigh and buttock; differs because hams is archaic in that anatomical sense and today mainly means the cured pork cut.
  • Loins: Similar because both lie in the lower trunk region; differs because loins names the sides of the lower back and hip area (and by extension the reproductive region), not the sitting mass, and in butchery denotes a different cut.
  • Saddle: Similar because in butchery saddle (of venison, mutton) denotes a hind-region cut; differs because a saddle is the loin together with both hindquarters, a jointing term rather than an anatomical body-part word.
  • Haunch (singular): Similar because it is the same lexeme; differs because the singular is used for one side or for the meat joint, while the plural haunches is the standard anatomical form.
