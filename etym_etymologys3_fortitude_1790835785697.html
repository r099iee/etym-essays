The English noun Fortitude descends through Anglo-Norman and Old French from the classical Latin noun fortitudo (genitive fortitudinis), an abstract quality noun constructed from the adjective fortis ("strong, firm, steadfast, brave"). In comparative linguistics, fortis is derived from the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰerǵʰ-, which fundamentally signified "high, elevated, fortified, rising to a height".

From Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Italic:

  • The root *bʰerǵʰ- is the ancestor of an immensely influential network of European terms associated with elevation and defensive strength, including Germanic *burgz (whence Old English burg / burh $\rightarrow$ modern borough, burg, burgher) and Celtic *brig- (as in the Celtic tribal name Brigantes, "the high/noble ones").
  • In Proto-Italic, the aspirated stop *bʰ- shifted to the voiceless labial fricative *f-, producing *forxtis / *forctis (attested in Old Latin inscriptions as forctis). In early Latin phonology, the cluster -rct- simplified to -rt-, yielding standard classical Latin fortis.

Classical Latin and Stoic Moral Philosophy:

  • In the late Roman Republic, Latin formed abstract nouns of quality from adjectives using the suffix -tūdo (genitive -tūdinis), creating fortitudo alongside magnitudo, altitudo, and pulchritudo.
  • While fortitudo occasionally denoted raw physical strength or bodily vigor (fortitudo corporis), its primary development in Latin literature occurred within ethical philosophy. Roman authors—principally Cicero in De Officiis and Tusculanae Disputationes, and Seneca in his moral epistles—utilized fortitudo to translate the Greek Stoic concept of $\alpha\nu\delta\rho\epsilon\acute{\iota}\alpha$ (andreia).
  • Cicero defined fortitudo as "animi affectio cum in patiendo et perferendo, tum in agendo et faciendo" ("an affection of the mind both in suffering and enduring, and in acting and executing"), firmly cementing the duality between patient endurance and active courage.

Medieval Scholastic Theology and Patristic Adoption:

With the Christianization of the Roman Empire, fortitudo was integrated into the core of medieval moral theology as one of the Four Cardinal Virtues (along with prudentia, iustitia, and temperantia).

  • In the 13th century, St. Thomas Aquinas devoted extensive treatises in the Summa Theologiae (Secunda Secundae, Questions 123–140) to fortitudo, famously identifying its principal act not as daring aggression (aggredi), but as standing firm and enduring (sustinere) under the threat of death or mortal agony for the sake of divine good.
  • This theological framing decisively shifted the Western concept of fortitude away from pagan martial aggression toward inward spiritual steadfastness.

Transmission into French and Middle English:

  • In Gallo-Romance, the word survived as a learned borrowing in Old French as fortitude (c. 12th century), functioning in devotional literature and chivalric codes.
  • The word entered Middle English during the late 14th century (c. 1380–1390), appearing in John Trevisa's translations, the works of Geoffrey Chaucer (such as The Tale of Melibee and Boece), and John Lydgate's poems.
  • During this period, it retained its dual senses of noble strength of character and physical capacity, though the moral and philosophical sense dominated English literature.

Early Modern to Contemporary Development:

In the 16th and 17th centuries, humanist writers such as Sir Thomas Elyot in The Boke Named the Governour (1531) and William Shakespeare celebrated fortitude as the crowning jewel of the noble mind. In the 18th century, Joseph Addison popularized the concept in The Spectator, drawing the vital philosophical distinction between animal courage (which stems from mere bodily constitution or instinct) and true fortitude (which is founded upon reasoned principle and moral reverence).

  • Phonetically, the word was naturalized into standard Received Pronunciation as /ˈfɔː.tɪ.tjuːd/.
  • In contemporary English, fortitude stands as an enduring emblem of the human capacity to endure suffering with serene, unbroken dignity.
