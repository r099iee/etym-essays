- Borrowed into English in the late 16th century from Latin praeceps, "headlong," the word's first English life is about the falling body — the head pitched forward toward the drop.
- In the 17th–18th centuries it hardened into the noun for a very steep rock face, a place where the ground keeps its promise until it abruptly ends.
- By the 19th century it leapt from geology into morals: to stand "on the edge of a precipice" meant imminent ruin, crisis, catastrophe.
- Today it runs both tracks at once — a literal sheer cliff and the figurative brink we totter on — still carrying its old headfirst magnetism toward the abyss.
