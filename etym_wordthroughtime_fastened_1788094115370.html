- Old English (c. 725): "fæstnian" = to make fast, fix, secure; the word is born in the concrete world of binding, tying, and making firm; perception: physical, practical.
- Middle English (c. 1200): "fastnen" = to attach, bind, fix; also to become fixed; ordinary domestic register.
- 14th–16th centuries: "to fasten on/upon" = to seize upon, to attach oneself to (a person, an idea, a blame); the figurative clamp is fully alive: "the bats fastened on the stricken" is this ancient sense in modern literary dress.
- 17th–18th centuries: "to fasten one's eyes/gaze upon" = to look fixedly; "fastened" = locked, bolted, secured; nautical "make fast" = tie down; perception: secure, immovable.
- 19th century: fastenings multiply with industrial clothing: buttons, hooks, zips; "fastener" becomes a technical word.
- 20th–21st centuries: "fasten your seatbelt" is a global airline and road-safety formula; "fastened" is everyday and neutral, but keeps its core sense of firm, secure attachment — clinging, as Tolkien's bats cling.
