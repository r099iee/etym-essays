The biography of MARTYRED runs from a root about memory to a word about death, and every stage of that journey is visible in its derivation. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *smer-, "to remember, to be mindful of," a root whose whole genius was to connect remembering with the act of speaking up. In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit smarati "he remembers, he recollects," with the noun smṛti "remembrance, sacred tradition," and the affectionate smara "remembrance, love" — a word that in Sanskrit poetry doubles as the name of the god of love, since love itself was thought of as the memory of a beloved. From Sanskrit the word family flows directly into the modern Indian languages: Hindi smaraṇa (स्मरण) and Odia ସ୍ମରଣ (smaraṇa), both "memory, remembrance," are tatsamas taken wholesale from the classical stock. In Avestan the same root appears as mar- "to remember, to think of," used of the liturgical memory of the hymns. The meaning carried into Greek, where an *smer-tu- formation produced mártys (μάρτυς) "witness": the witness is etymologically the one who remembers what happened and can testify to it in court. Around this core Greek grew a whole family — martyréō "to bear witness," martýrion "testimony" — and a striking side-branch, mérimna "care, anxiety," the troubled remembering that gnaws at the mind.

In classical Athens the mártys was the courtroom witness, the man whose memory made the case; in the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures (the Septuagint) and then in the New Testament the word was taken up and elevated: the apostles were mártys, witnesses to Christ's resurrection, and their testimony was itself a kind of remembering. The great semantic turn came in the second century AD, in the persecutions of the early Church. Christians who died for their faith were called martyrs because their deaths were the supreme form of witness — testimony sealed in blood — and the first confident uses cluster around the martyrs of Polycarp's circle and the writings of Tertullian, from whose Latin (ecclesiastical martyr) the whole of Christian Europe took the word.

From the Latin of the Church the word entered every European vernacular, and English took it twice. The Old English form martyr, martir (10th century) was borrowed directly from ecclesiastical Latin and used for the saints of the Roman calendar; the modern spelling was reinforced in the 14th century by a second borrowing through Old French martir and Anglo-Norman. The word therefore carries both the Greek sacred register and the Latin liturgical register into English. From the beginning its connotation was heroic and religious: the martyr was the supreme Christian honour, and the Middle Ages cultivated the martyr's cult — relics, shrines, feasts, and the martyr's crown in the hymns and hagiographies of the Church.

Through the Reformation the word acquired a polemical energy: both Catholics and Protestants claimed martyrs, and the great martyrologies of the sixteenth century — Foxe's Book of Martyrs in England being the most famous — used the word to vindicate a cause by its dead. Here the modern secular extension begins: to be martyred is to die for a truth that the world has rejected, and the vindication belongs to the cause as much as to the person. From the religious wars onward the word broadened steadily, until in modern English "martyred" can describe anyone killed for their beliefs, religious or political or moral — a martyred president, a martyred reformer, a martyred journalist.

The final, ironic extension belongs to the twentieth century. Because the martyr is defined by self-sacrificing endurance, "martyred" came to be applied, at first humorously and then clinically, to the person who endures or parades small sufferings — the "martyred expression," the "martyr complex" of popular psychology. This is a genuine metaphoric shift: the physical death for belief becomes a metaphorical social or emotional self-punishment, and the sacred word acquires a satirical edge. It is a sign of the word's power that even in its ironic use it still borrows the language of heroic sacrifice to mock the petty.

Socio-culturally, the word has never been neutral. To call someone "martyred" is to take sides: it asserts that the death was unjust, that the victim was in the right, and that the killing condemns the killer. This is why the word is so carefully chosen in Hong lou meng: applied to Bi Gan, the Shang minister whose heart was cut out for daring to protest, "martyred" imports the whole Judaeo-Christian theology of sacrificial witness into a Chinese legend, making Dai-yu's grief a quasi-sacred testimony. The literary record bears this out — from Chaucer's martyr-saints and Shakespeare's martyred victims to the martyred heroes of Victorian fiction and the ironically martyred characters of the modern novel, the word has always been a moral verdict as much as a description. Its etymology — remembering, witnessing, testifying — is still alive in it: the martyred person is, at bottom, the one who remembers the truth and speaks it to the point of death.
