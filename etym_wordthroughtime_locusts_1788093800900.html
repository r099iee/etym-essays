- (Antiquity) - In the ancient world the word (Hebrew אַרְבֶּה, Greek ἀκρίς) already named a divine scourge: the eighth plague of Egypt and Joel's “day of the locust” made the insect an instrument of judgment.
- (Early Church–Middle Ages) - John the Baptist's diet of “locusts and wild honey” (Matthew 3:4) is increasingly explained by medieval exegetes as carob pods, so the word begins to attach to trees and fruit — the start of its botanical career.
- (14th–17th centuries) - In English “locust” stays chiefly the devouring insect of Scripture and natural history; plague and famine reports keep its terror vivid.
- (17th–18th centuries) - In colonial America settlers transfer “locust” to the loud periodical cicadas and to the black locust and honey locust trees, multiplying the word's referents and loosening its tie to the true insect.
- (19th–20th centuries) - The figurative sense hardens: “locusts” becomes the standard metaphor for exploitative multitudes — invading armies, absentee landlords, and finally finance capital, culminating in Franz Müntefering's 2005 German coinage “Heuschrecken” (locusts) for private-equity firms.
- (Today) - The word oscillates between science and symbol: desert-locust swarms are tracked by satellite in the Horn of Africa, while Tolkien's “sea of locusts” remains instantly readable as numberless, devouring destruction.
