The history of inquisition is the history of a neutral Latin legal term that became the name of one of the most feared institutions in European history and then was domesticated again into a vivid metaphor for hostile questioning. Few English words travel quite this road — from courtroom to torture chamber to family argument.

The chain of descent is clear and Latin in every stage. The base verb is Latin quaerere, "to seek, to look for, to ask, to demand," a word of classical antiquity attested from the second century BCE. Its own origin, however, is unknown: although many attempts have been made to supply quaerere with an Indo-European ancestor (some scholars have connected it with roots for "acquire" or "search," others have proposed a loanword), the OED and modern comparative dictionaries such as de Vaan's treat its prehistory as obscure. The honest etymological record therefore begins with Latin, not with Proto-Indo-European. From quaerere the Romans built the compound inquirere, "to search into, to examine" (from in- "into" + quaerere), and from the participle of that compound they formed the abstract noun inquisitio, "a searching into, a judicial investigation, a legal inquiry."

In Roman civil procedure the inquisitio had a precise technical sense: it was the mode of investigation in which the magistrate himself — the inquisitor — actively gathered the evidence, as opposed to the older accusatorial procedure in which a private citizen brought charges and conducted the prosecution. Roman law thus already contained the seed of everything that followed: the figure of the investigating judge, and the state taking the initiative in the pursuit of crime and error.

The word crossed into the church with the canon law. Latin remained the language of the church, and the church's courts adopted the inquisitorial procedure: the bishop's court investigated, summoned witnesses, and sought out offenders on its own authority. In the thirteenth century the papacy turned this procedure into an institution. In 1231 Pope Gregory IX issued the bull that entrusted the Dominicans and Franciscans with the office of inquiring into heresy, and the Medieval Inquisition was born — created to combat Catharism and Waldensianism in southern France, Italy, and Germany. The institution's name, inquisitio haereticae pravitatis, "inquisition into heretical depravity," was taken directly from the Latin word. Middle English had borrowed the term a little earlier: inquisicioun is attested in English from the fourteenth century, via Old French inquisition, meaning "a judicial inquiry, an official investigation."

The sixteenth century transformed the word's emotional meaning. The foundation of the Spanish Inquisition in 1478 under Ferdinand and Isabella, the creation of the Roman Inquisition (the Holy Office) in 1542, the use of torture to extract confessions, the secret proceedings, and the public burnings of the auto-da-fé turned "the Inquisition" into the byword of Europe for religious terror. Protestant polemicists seized on the word and loaded it with every association of cruelty and fanaticism; English writers of the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries used the Inquisition as the standing example of tyranny. The word capitalized names the institution; the word uncapitalized inherits the fear.

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, with the historical tribunals in decline, the lower-case word re-entered ordinary speech as a metaphor. To be "subjected to an inquisition" is to be questioned with relentless, hostile thoroughness — the emotional memory of the tribunal transferred to a job interview or a parent's interrogation. The metaphor works precisely because the history is still alive in the word: no synonym can summon the same mixture of judge, torture, confession, and helplessness. Modern legal English also retains the adjective inquisitorial for the continental system of procedure that descends from the Roman inquisitio, and inquisitor names both the historical officer and, by extension, any relentless questioner. The word family is extensive: inquire, inquiry, inquest, inquisitive, inquisitor, require, requisite, acquire, exquisite, and query all descend from the same Latin quaerere. Inquisition thus stands as a complete case study in how an institution's reputation can flood a neutral word with meaning — and how that meaning can then be re-exported, centuries later, as everyday metaphor.
