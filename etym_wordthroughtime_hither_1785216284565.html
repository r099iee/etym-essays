- [Old English period: the everyday adverb] - hither (Old English hider) is the ordinary, workaday word for "to this place" — the standard directional counterpart of "here", used by everyone from chroniclers to servants; it carries no archaism and no ornament, merely motion toward the speaker.
- [Middle English: the deictic family intact] - the word survives the spelling shake-up of the 1500s (the -der to -ther shift shared with father and gather) with its full role intact: hither, thither, whither, hence, thence still form a complete, living system for saying where motion points.
- [17th–18th century: retreat to the written page] - as colloquial speech simplifies, "hither" withdraws into formal, literary, and Biblical use; "hither and thither" and "hither and yon" freeze into stock phrases of chronicle-style narrative, and the word begins to sound deliberately old-fashioned.
- [19th century: the fossilizing pair] - the binomial "hither and thither" hardens into the word's principal survival; ordinary prose now says "over here" or "this way", while "hither" becomes the marker of antique gravity — the word of sermons, romances, and mock-medieval story.
- [20th century: Tolkien's medieval robe] - Tolkien deploys it deliberately for high archaic texture in The Hobbit (1937) — goblins "ran ________ and thither" — proving that a dead adverb can still generate living kinetic chaos when worn as period costume.
- [Contemporary sense] - hither survives almost only in the fossilized pair "hither and thither", in the flirtatious "come-hither" glance, and in jocular or literary play; its register is comic-archaic, and it is prized precisely because modern English has no equally sharp single word for "toward this place".
