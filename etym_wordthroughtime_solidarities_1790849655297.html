- Roman Law (2nd c. BCE–6th c. CE): Existed purely as a technical civil law concept (in solidum), denoting joint and several liability where each debtor is individually responsible for an entire undivided obligation.
- 1790s–1840s: Transformed in post-Revolutionary France (solidarité) into a vibrant secular, political, and socialist concept of universal brotherhood, mutual interdependence, and collective civic responsibility (Pierre Leroux, Auguste Comte).
- 1850s–1910s: Borrowed into English (solidarity), gaining sociological rigor through Émile Durkheim's distinction between mechanical and organic solidarity, alongside widespread adoption by trade unionism and working-class labor movements.
- 1920s–1980s: Pluralized into solidarities in political philosophy, anthropology, and literary theory to analyze complex, intersecting networks of allegiance, anti-colonial struggles, and intellectual factions (e.g., the Polish Solidarność movement, Sartre, Barthes).
- 1990s–Present: Widely utilized in feminist theory, intersectionality, international human rights, and environmental justice to describe decentralized, horizontal networks of mutual aid and transnational coalition-building.
