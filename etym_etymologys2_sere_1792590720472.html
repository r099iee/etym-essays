Note on phonology and orthography: The phonological descent from Proto-Germanic *sauzas to Old English sēar illustrates fundamental historical sound shifts: the Proto-Germanic diphthong *au regularly developed into the Old English long diphthong ēa, while the voiced alveolar sibilant *z underwent rhotacism to *r via Verner's law before being lost word-finally. In Early Modern English, the adjective was spelled interchangeably as sear or sere. During the 17th and 18th centuries, orthographic differentiation gradually assigned the spelling sear to the causative verb meaning "to scorch or cauterize" (cognate with Old English sēarian), while reserving the spelling sere for the evocative poetic adjective describing dry autumnal leaves.
