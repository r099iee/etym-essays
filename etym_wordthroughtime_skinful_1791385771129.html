- 1400s–1500s: Literal volumetric measure - the full liquid capacity contained within an animal hide wineskin, waterskin, or pelt container.
- 1600s–1700s: Jacobean and Georgian tavern slang - humorously transferred to the human body, denoting as much wine or ale as a person's stomach could hold; a state of heavy drunkenness.
- 1800s–1910s: Victorian working-class and sporting idiom - associated with tavern revelry, fair days, and temperance broadsheets warning against excessive public intoxication.
- 1920s–1960s: Ironic upper-class euphemism - adopted by humorists and gentlemen (most famously in P.G. Wodehouse) to speak whimsically of becoming pleasantly or strategically intoxicated.
- 1970s–present: Colloquial British and Commonwealth vernacular - standard informal idiom for having consumed too much alcohol during a night out, or rarely for receiving a severe scolding.
