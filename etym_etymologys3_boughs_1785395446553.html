The biography of boughs begins in the body. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰeh₂ǵʰu-, meaning 'arm' or 'forearm', an anatomical word whose reflexes span the ancient family: Sanskrit bāhu- (बाहु), Avestan bāzu-, Armenian bazuk (բազուկ), and Tocharian poke, all meaning 'arm', with the Iranian line continuing into New Persian bāzū (بازو) and the Indic line into Hindi bāh (बाँह) and Odia bāhu (ବାହୁ). The root is thus among the most stable of the family's body-words, its 'arm' sense essentially unchanged from Anatolia to Bengal across more than five thousand years. Greek pêkhus (πῆχυς) 'forearm, cubit' is sometimes loosely cited as a cognate, but it descends from a different root, *peh₂ǵʰu-, and must be excluded from this family; the careful etymologist records it as a separate sister rather than merging it, exactly as the anti-merging guardrail demands.

In the Germanic branch the root produced *bōguz, 'arm, shoulder', a word used of the human arm and of the foreleg or shoulder of an animal. Early Germanic speakers then made an analogy that has never been lost: the great limbs of a tree resemble arms, and so the same word came to mean 'branch'. Old English bōg (or bōh) carries both senses — 'arm, shoulder' and 'bough of a tree' — and the anatomical sense survives among the Germanic kin: Old Norse bógr, modern Icelandic bógur, 'the shoulder of an animal'; and, transferred to shipbuilding, Old Saxon bōg and Old High German buog, which yield Dutch boeg and German Bug, 'the bow of a ship', the shoulder-like curve of the hull. Meanwhile Old English bēag, 'ring, crown', looks temptingly similar but descends from a wholly different root, *bʰewgʰ-, 'to bend', and must not be merged into this family — the arm → branch transfer is a Germanic development, not a PIE one.

Middle English inherited the tree-word as bough or bow, and in the fifteenth century the spelling was deliberately fixed: the -ough form was adopted to set the tree-word apart from the archer's bow, with which it was homographous and nearly homophonous. The pronunciations later diverged as well (tree-bough kept the Old English diphthong to rhyme with cow, while the weapon-bow kept a long vowel to rhyme with go), so the two words, identical in writing for a time, were permanently separated in both spelling and sound. This spelling split is one of the rare deliberate acts of English orthography, and it shows how important the tree-word had become in everyday speech.

Thereafter the history of boughs is a history of register. In the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries the word entered its literary golden age: it throve in poetry, in carols, and in biblical language — the dove returning to Noah with 'an olive leaf plucked off' gives the olive bough its emblematic status as a sign of peace; Shakespeare and the ballad tradition use boughs of sheltering oak and 'under the greenwood tree'; and the Christmas carol 'deck the halls with boughs of holly' fixes the festive evergreen bough in the language forever. The word's dignity made it the natural name for the great, sheltering limbs of ancient trees, and that dignity is exactly what Tolkien exploits when he places Dwalin and Balin 'in the greenery of the topmost boughs' — the storybook word confers a touch of archaic majesty on a scene that is, in fact, scanty and precarious.

Key stages and attestations:

  • PIE (reconstructed) — *bʰeh₂ǵʰu- 'arm, forearm'; reflexes across Sanskrit, Avestan, Armenian, Tocharian, Persian, Hindi, Odia; Greek pêkhus excluded as from a different root.
  • Proto-Germanic (reconstructed) — *bōguz 'arm, shoulder'; the arm → branch metaphor is forged in Germanic.
  • Old English (pre-1000) — bōg/bōh 'arm, shoulder; bough'; bēag 'ring' excluded as from *bʰewgʰ-.
  • Middle English (12th-15th c.) — bough/bow; the 15th-century spelling split fixes -ough for the tree-word.
  • 16th-19th c. — literary golden age: carols, Shakespeare, the Bible; the olive bough as emblem of peace; 'deck the halls with boughs of holly'.
  • Modern register — bough yields to neutral branch in ordinary speech, surviving in poetry, folk song, and storybook prose.

Socio-culturally, boughs has become a word for the language of trees when they matter — in ceremony, in song, in refuge. Etymologically an arm, the bough has kept its body-metaphor for millennia, and in every generation it has been the word for the tree's great limbs when those limbs are asked to shelter, to decorate, or to dignify. In Tolkien's fir, the boughs do all three at once, and the ancient body-word of the Indo-Europeans reaches down to give two fleeing dwarves a seat in the storybook canopy.
