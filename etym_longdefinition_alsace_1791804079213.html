Alsace is the name of a region of north-eastern France, stretching along the west bank of the Rhine between the Vosges mountains and the river, with Strasbourg as its capital and Colmar and Mulhouse as its other principal cities. In this reading it is a military objective: Louis XV, having “suspended his conquests in Flanders”, is hastening “to the assistance of Alsace” in 1744, when an Austro-Saxon army under Prince Charles of Lorraine threatened the province and the king's sudden illness at Metz interrupted the relief.

The name is old, Germanic and slightly mysterious. It descends from an Old High German compound usually analysed as ali- “other, foreign” plus sāzzo “sitter, dweller”, hence “the foreign settlers” or “those who sit on the other side”. Whether the “other side” meant the far bank of the Rhine, the far side of the Vosges, or simply the non-Frankish population of the conquered borderland is not settled; what is clear is that the name describes people rather than terrain, unlike most French regional names.

Historical development

  • Alemannic settlement. From the third century CE the Roman frontier on the upper Rhine collapsed and Alemannic groups settled on both banks; the region became Germanic-speaking while remaining under Roman cultural influence.
  • Frankish and imperial period. After the Frankish conquest the area was organised as a duchy of the Merovingian and Carolingian world; by the Treaty of Verdun in 843 it belonged to East Francia, and from 962 to the Holy Roman Empire.
  • The Decapole. In 1354 ten imperial cities of Alsace, including Colmar, formed a league (Décapole) that lasted until the French annexation dissolved it in 1679, a sign of the region's fragmented, city-centred political order.
  • French annexation. The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 transferred the Habsburg rights in Alsace to France, and Louis XIV occupied Strasbourg in 1681; the region became French in law and gradually in language and administration.
  • German and French alternation. Annexed by the German Empire in 1871 as the Reichsland Elsaß-Lothringen, returned to France in 1919, annexed again by Nazi Germany in 1940 and liberated in 1944–45, Alsace has changed sovereignty four times in seventy-five years.

Cultural associations

  • Language. The traditional dialects are Alemannic in the south and Franconian in the north, collectively called Alsatian; French is the official language, and the dialect is now spoken mainly by older speakers.
  • Wine. Alsace is one of France's most distinctive wine regions, famous for dry Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat, sold under the regional appellation Alsace.
  • Architecture and art. Timber-framed villages, the Strasbourg cathedral, the Isenheim Altarpiece and the Unterlinden museum in Colmar are standard reference points; Goethe studied in Strasbourg in 1770–71.
  • Storks. The white stork, a protected symbol of the region, appears on postcards and rooftops and is inseparable from the tourist image of Alsace.

Extended English uses

  • Alsatia. The Latin name of Alsace was borrowed in seventeenth-century London as a cant term for the debtors' sanctuary of Whitefriars, and from there for any disreputable refuge; Thomas Shadwell's comedy The Squire of Alsatia (1688) fixed the usage.
  • Alsatian. In British English the word is the traditional name of the German Shepherd dog, a usage adopted during the First World War and still current.
  • Alsace-Lorraine. The hyphenated phrase denotes the imperial territory of 1871–1918 and, more loosely, the whole Franco-German border question.

Register and connotation

“Alsace” is a neutral geographical name, but it carries a heavy historical freight: in French it evokes the lost provinces of 1871 and the patriotic literature of revanche; in German it evokes the Reichsland and the ambiguity of 1940; in English it usually signals wine, storks and Christmas markets rather than war. Carlyle's use is purely strategic — Alsace is the endangered frontier that pulls the king away from Flanders.

Why it matters in this reading

The clause “to fly to the assistance of Alsace” marks the turn of the 1744 campaign. The French had been successful in the Austrian Netherlands; the Austrian advance on the Rhine forced a rapid redeployment, and the king's illness at Metz turned a strategic manoeuvre into a public drama. Alsace, Flanders and Metz are thus three points on one itinerary, and the three cards belong together.
