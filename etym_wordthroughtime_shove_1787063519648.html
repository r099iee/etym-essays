- Old English scūfan — "to push, thrust" — a plain strong verb for physical pushing, used of boats, men, and loads; also of divine thrusting in the Psalms (God shoving the wicked).
- Middle English shoven — the broad physical sense continues; by the 15th century "shove off" develops as the nautical act of pushing a boat away from the shore.
- 16th–17th c. — "shove off" migrates from boats to people: "shove off!" = "get going, leave!" — the nautical origin gives it a salty, impatient, working-class register.
- 17th–19th c. — "push and shove" describes crowd jostling; shove becomes the word for rough, impolite physical contact in queues and markets.
- 19th c. — "shove-ha'penny", the pub game of shoving coins, shows the word settling into everyday domestic slang.
- 20th c. — "shove it!" as an aggressive dismissal; "shove up / shove over" = make room grudgingly — the register hardens: shove is what rude people do, while polite people push.
- 21st c. — shove keeps its blunt, abrupt flavour in "shove it down someone's throat" and "if push comes to shove" — the moment of last resort; the strong past (scēaf) is long dead and the regular past shoved rules everywhere.
