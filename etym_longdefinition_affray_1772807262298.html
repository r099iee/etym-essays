In this legal-narrative moment, affray denotes the sudden public clash that erupts when neither party will yield — an ensuing brawl whose chaos produces wounding and later death. The word's tone is formal and slightly archaic-legal: it frames the fight not as private revenge romance but as a breach of the peace, a disorderly struggle that society (and the law) can name and judge. In the sentence, "the ensuing affray" compresses cause and consequence: stubborn confrontation → violent free-for-all → fatal injury. The literary effect is cool and judicial rather than sensational; violence is recorded as public disturbance with mortal result.

In its core sense, an affray is a fight or brawl in a public place that disturbs the peace and alarms onlookers. The essence is the public breach: it is not the private settling of a quarrel but a disorder that breaks the community's peace and terrifies bystanders. In British law the word survives as a technical public-order offence — fighting (or threatening unlawful violence) in such a way that another person fears for their safety; the legal sense requires a real fight or its threat, two or more parties, and the frightening of the public.

Polysemy — range of senses.
  • Public fight/brawl (modern, dominant): "charged with affray," "killed in the affray"; the legal and ordinary sense of a disturbance of the peace by fighting.
  • Alarm, fright, terror (historical): the word's earliest English sense, borrowed from Old French effroi "dread"; a person could be "in an affray" — in a state of fear. Now obsolete, but historically central.
  • Disturbance, commotion (historical): any noisy public disorder, not necessarily a fight; the bridge sense between "fright" and "fight."
  • Verb to affray — to frighten, to disturb (archaic): "affrayed" = frightened; this verb is the parent of the common adjective afraid.
  • Legal affray (technical): a statutory/public-order offence in English law (Public Order Act 1986, s.3): the use or threat of unlawful violence causing another to fear for their safety.

The bridge runs from the Germanic idea of peace (friþu — the protected, inviolable social order) → the Romance construction "to take out of peace" (exfridare — to disturb, to startle) → the fright thus caused (effroi, affrai — alarm, terror) → the public commotion that causes such fright → the fight that breaks the peace (modern affray). The word has moved from fear to fight while keeping both: the affray is precisely the fight that frightens the public.

A brawl is a noisy, often drunken fight and is colloquial; it need not carry legal colouring. A fracas stresses noisy disturbance and can be almost comic; it is weaker on bloodshed. A melee highlights confused multi-person combat. A riot implies larger-scale public disorder, often group or political. An assault is typically one-sided unlawful attack in modern legal English; affray traditionally involves fighting that terrifies the public. A duel suggests regulated single combat with rules — the opposite of the messy "ensuing" scramble. An altercation may be verbal only. A scuffle is minor, physical, and brief. Affray sits at the intersection of fight + public peace + fright.

Collocations: public affray, charged with affray, killed in the affray, the ensuing affray, affray outside a tavern. Register is formal, legal, historical, or literary; everyday speech more often says "fight" or "brawl." In narrative prose, using affray elevates the event into the realm of recorded disturbance and consequence, which matches a storyline of wounding, death, and accountability.
