Introduction. Flagons is the plural of flagon, a word English borrowed from Old French flacon in the Middle Ages, but whose ultimate ancestry is Germanic — a loanword that travelled from the north into Latin and back out again through French. The word’s journey shows how vessels themselves travelled: the object (a large bottle, later a handled drinking vessel) and the word for it both crossed Europe between Germanic, Romance, and Latin speakers.

1. The Germanic source. The ancestor is the Proto-Germanic noun *flaskōn, “a bottle, a flask, a vessel”. This word is securely attested through its many descendants — Old English flasce / flaxe (flask), Old Norse flaska, Old High German flasca, Middle Dutch flessce, Dutch fles, German Flasche — all meaning “bottle”. The Germanic word itself is usually derived, with a suffix, either from a root meaning “to plait, to weave” (older vessels were often covered with plaited basketry) or from a root meaning “to fill” (a vessel being that which is filled). Both derivations have scholarly support, and the question is debated.

2. Into Latin and back. The Germanic word was borrowed into the Latin of the late Empire and early Middle Ages as flasco (genitive flasconis), “a bottle, a wine vessel”. The form is recorded in Medieval Latin and was most likely transmitted through the Frankish court language into the Latin of Gaul. From Latin the word passed into Old French, where flacon (and flasche) meant “a bottle, a flask” — typically a small container for liquids, with the diminutive nuance of the stem form.

3. The English borrowing and semantic growth. English took the word into Middle English in the fourteenth century, in forms such as flacon, flagoun, and flaggan, meaning “a large bottle or vessel for drink”. By the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the sense settled and specialised: a flagon was a large, usually handled vessel for ale or wine, used at taverns and banquets, and — crucially — it was a vessel from which cups were filled, not a cup itself. The English spelling “flagon” reflects the Old French stem form flacon- and was reinforced by the association with larger serving sizes.

4. Early-modern diversification. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries flagons appear constantly in inventories, wills, and church records. Pewter flagons were standard tavern ware; silver flagons appear among church plate, where a flagon of communion wine became a standard liturgical object in the Anglican church. The word remained fully alive in ordinary usage, with no archaic flavour whatsoever — a flagon was simply the everyday large vessel of the table and bar.

5. The nineteenth-century shift to an archaic flavour. As industrial manufacturing changed tableware — cheap glass bottles and moulded jugs — the distinctive handled metal flagon became less everyday. Romantic and Victorian historical fiction (Scott, the Gothic novel, Victorian medievalism) and the ballad revival began to use “flagon” for antique colour, and the word took on the romantic-register tone it still has. Twentieth-century fantasy, above all Tolkien, cemented this: flagons belong to old halls, feasts, and ruin — the precise atmosphere of the source sentence, where flagons lie among skulls on the floor of the dragon’s lair, in The Hobbit.

6. Modern resurgence in a new register. In the late twentieth century the word returned to shop shelves in a humbler form: the large glass or plastic handled bottle of wine or cider sold cheaply and casually. The medieval serving vessel and the modern bulk bottle are the same word, separated by six centuries and connected by the constant human need to hold, pour, and share drink.

Conclusion. The history of flagons is a compact loop: a Germanic word for a bottle, Latinised, Frenchified, and re-Englised, which then narrowed to a handled feasting-vessel and broadened again into a modern bulk bottle — a word that has been a bottle all along, only changing the register of the occasions on which it is used.
