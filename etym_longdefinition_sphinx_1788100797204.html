Greek myth. In Greek legend the Sphinx (Greek Σφίγξ, Sphinx, from the verb sphíngō, “to squeeze tight, bind, strangle” — her name declares her: “the Strangler”) was a winged creature with the head and breast of a woman, the body of a lion and a serpent’s tail. Sent to punish Thebes, she perched on a rock above the road and stopped every passer-by with her riddle: “What goes on four legs in the morning, two legs at noon, and three legs in the evening?” She strangled and devoured all who could not answer, until Oedipus answered: “Man — who crawls on all fours in infancy, walks upright in maturity, and leans on a staff in old age.” Defeated, the Sphinx cast herself from the citadel and died. The canonical wording of the riddle — “what is that which has one voice, and is four-footed, two-footed and three-footed?” — is preserved in Apollodorus’ Library (3.5.8); the verse form of the riddle survives in Athenaeus (Deipnosophistae 10.455). Hesiod already knows the monster in the Theogony (c. 700 BCE), where she is called Φίξ (Phix), the Boeotian spelling, “ruin to the Cadmeans”; the full story was dramatised and alluded to by Aeschylus (Seven Against Thebes), Sophocles (Oedipus Tyrannus) and Euripides (Phoenissae).

  • Greek iconography. On archaic vases and grave monuments the sphinx appears as a winged female feline: devourer of youths on early Corinthian and Attic pots, guardian of graves (a watcher at the door of the dead), door-warden of temple treasuries, and guardian figures flank Etruscan tombs. The “Sphinx of Naxos” column at Delphi (c. 560 BCE) lifts a limestone sphinx above the oracle’s approach — the monster already tamed into an omen-keeper.
  • The Egyptian Sphinx. In Egypt the “sphinx” is a wholly different being: a recumbent lion with the head of a man — normally the king — guarding temples, tombs, and processional ways. The Egyptians called such figures šsp-ꜥnḫ, “living image”. The Great Sphinx of Giza (c. 2500 BCE, carved from the bedrock of the pyramid plateau, generally associated with the pharaoh Khafre) is the colossal prototype — 73 metres long and 20 metres high — and New Kingdom texts call it “Horus-of-the-Horizon” (Heru-em-akhet, Harmachis). The Dream Stela of Thutmose IV (c. 1400 BCE) records how the sphinx spoke to the sleeping prince and promised him kingship if he cleared away the sand. Pliny (Natural History 36.77) describes the Giza colossus in the first century CE, and it is in this period that the Greek word “sphinx” crossed over to name the Egyptian monuments.
  • Figurative sense. Since the 19th century “a sphinx” is any person — or expression, face, silence — that is unreadable, mysterious, impassive: “he sat like a sphinx”; “her face was sphinx-like”. The bridge is the monument’s stillness: a stone lion-body with a human face that “knows everything” yet answers nothing — precisely the Sphinx of Wilde’s passage, “who is as old as the world itself, and lives in the desert, and knows everything”. “Sphinx-like” is the corresponding adjective for deliberate or instinctive inscrutability.
  • Modern offshoots. The riddle, the monument and the mystery breed new senses: the “sphinx moth” (family Sphingidae) is named for the sphinx-like posture of its larva; the “Sphynx” cat — a deliberately Egyptianising respelling, bred in Toronto in 1966 — is the hairless breed that flatters itself with the name; and the word appears in brands, games and puzzles wherever a mystery is marketed. Wilde himself wrote the poem “The Sphinx” (1894), dense with Egyptian imagery — the same mirage that floats through the story’s sentence.

One word, two monsters. The Greek strangler and the Egyptian guardian share only lion bodies and human heads — but over two millennia the two images have fused in the Western imagination into a single idea of the Sphinx: the ancient, impassive, all-knowing questioner of fate, the being that guards the answers nobody asks. Every sense — monster, monument, metaphor, mascot — descends from that fusion, and the capital letter covers them all.
