The adverb afield places an action, state, or position in or to the field — the open, unforested land beyond the settlement — and from that concrete footing it reaches its far more common modern meaning: at or to a distance from home, one's accustomed place, or the matter under discussion. The word is essentially relational. It always measures a position against an unspoken centre: the hearth, the base of operations, the topic at hand. To be afield is to have moved outward from that centre, whether the movement is literal travel across countryside or figurative drift away from the point. In the reading context supplied with this card — 'He traveled far ______ in search of the rare herbs needed for his experiments' — the blank is precisely the idiom 'far afield,' and it communicates both the physical remoteness of the search and the single-mindedness of a collector who has deliberately pushed the limits of his usual range. Because afield is so tightly bound to the idea of a home base, its tone is quietly dramatic: it converts mere geography into a contrast between safety and exposure, the known and the unknown. The word nudges the reader to imagine a journey that breaks with routine, so it appears naturally in narratives of exploration, war, hunting, scholarship, and error. Its register today is formal, literary, and faintly archaic; outside the set phrase 'far afield' it survives mostly in journalism and considered prose, where an elevated or old-fashioned flavour is wanted. The full polysemy of afield can be set out exhaustively:
  • Sense 1 — literally 'on or to the field': in the open countryside, on the pasture, the hunting ground, or the battlefield, as opposed to being indoors or within the settlement. This is the historically oldest sense, now largely archaic, yet it remains the mental image that underwrites every other meaning.
  • Sense 2 — 'away from home, at or to a distance': the central modern sense. It denotes departure from one's accustomed place or base of operations without specifying national borders or exact mileage; the removal can be great or small, so long as it is real.
  • Sense 3 — figurative 'far from the point, topic, or aim': in expressions such as 'far afield,' the word detaches itself from physical geography altogether and signals a wandering of thought, discussion, or research away from the central subject — a research question can lead 'far afield' of the original hypothesis.
  • Sense 4 — professional and military 'in the field of activity': in journalism, medicine, archaeology, and the armed forces, being 'in the field' (and, by extension, going afield) contrasts hands-on work at the actual scene of events with desk-bound or theoretical work.
  • Sense 5 — adjectival/predicative 'astray, at fault' (rare, by connotation): occasionally the distance carries a hint of waywardness — one can be 'wide of the mark' or 'far afield' in an argument — though, unlike astray, afield itself imposes no moral verdict.
The historical bridge between these senses is transparent. A field, in Old English feld, was the open, unwooded, uncultivated land beyond the settlement — a place both alluring and exposed, where grazing, hunting, battle, and exploration took place. To be 'on the field' (Old English on felde) was already to be outside the protected centre. A single semantic step widened the phrase so that 'afield' meant simply 'out there, away, at a distance,' and a further figurative step carried it into the realm of argument, research, and inquiry. The result is a word whose modern register is literary and slightly archaic, surviving chiefly in formal prose, news commentary, and poetry, where it lends an air of dignified, deliberate distance rather than the neutral flatness of plain 'away.'
