BELCHED is the past tense and past participle of the verb belch. At its literal core, it describes the sudden, noisy expulsion of gas from the stomach through the mouth — the sound and force of an eructation. By a very old and still-living figurative extension, it also describes the violent outward discharge of fire, smoke, steam, flame, ash, or even curses, as if the emitting thing (a volcano, an engine, a dragon) were itself vomiting them up. In the source sentence — "His fire belched forth, the hall smoked, he shook the mountain-roots" — the word dramatises the dragon Smaug's eruption from his lair: his flame does not simply flow or shoot, it belches, a thick, furnace-like, spasmodic discharge that is at once gaseous and terrifying, as though the fire itself were a great eructation from the mountain's gut.

Primary senses

  • Physiological (literal): to eructate — to bring up gas from the stomach through the mouth, usually audibly and with force. Register: everyday, mildly informal, often comic.
  • Violent emission (figurative): to hurl out fire, smoke, flames, steam, or clouds of matter from a furnace-like source. Register: dramatic, literary, descriptive; used of volcanoes, chimneys, engines, cannons, and monsters.
  • Abusive emission (metaphorical): to pour out words — anger, oaths, curses — as if spat up violently: "he belched forth abuse". Register: literary, emphatic.

Secondary and peripheral senses

  • As noun: a belch — a single such expulsion of gas, or a burst of smoke/flame from a vent or chimney ("a belch of grey smoke").
  • In technical prose: internal-combustion engines "belch" exhaust; industrial stacks "belch" particulates; the register is journalistic or engineering-descriptive rather than vulgar.
  • In meteorology/geology: volcanoes "belch" ash columns; geysers and fissures "belch" steam — the verb supplies violent, rhythmic, choked discharge rather than smooth flow.

Semantic bridge between senses: the physiological burp is an involuntary, bursting, noisy escape of gas; metaphorically the same sound-and-force profile is transferred to any vent that "gasps out" contents — hence fire, smoke, and curses can all belch. The word thus carries an inherent sense of pressure released in a burst, distinguishing it from smooth, continuous words like emit or flow.

Connotations and register

  • Colloquial and slightly coarse in the bodily sense; in older etiquette literature the word was considered vulgar to use at table.
  • Grand and dramatic in the figurative sense; favoured by epic, fantasy, and action prose precisely because of its crude physical force.
  • Neutral-technical in engineering and environmental journalism.

In the source text the past tense is key: belched compresses the whole dragonish event into one choked, thunderous discharge — the fire bursts out before the hall smokes and the mountain trembles, giving a sequence of cause (eruption), effect (smoke), and aftermath (quake). The choice of belched rather than burst or shot keeps the fire organic and gutsy, as if the mountain-palace itself were retching it forth.
