- Old English - trēow 'faith, pledge'; the plural treowa comes to mean the sworn terms of an armistice — rivals exchange pledges, and the pledges are the truce.
- 13th–14th c. - trewes: a binding, sacred suspension of war for a fixed term; breaching the trewes is mortal dishonour in the chivalric chronicles; perception: sworn, honourable, sacral.
- 16th c. - the -s is dropped: truce is re-analysed as a singular noun; the word becomes the standard everyday term for a temporary stoppage of fighting.
- 17th–19th c. - diplomatic codification: flags of truce, truce negotiations, and the legal distinction from peace in the laws of war; perception: formal, contractual, official.
- 20th c. - international law separates truce, armistice and ceasefire; the Christmas Truce of 1914 gives the word a poignant folk-heroic glow.
- 21st c. - fully figurative use: a truce in the tariff war, a fragile truce in domestic disputes; perception: temporary, precarious, hopeful.
