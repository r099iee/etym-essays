• Everyman: Similar because both represent the universal human being whose experiences, flaws, and mortal fate reflect the condition of all mankind; differs because Everyman is an abstract, allegorical figure originating in medieval Christian morality plays without historical specificity or psychological depth, whereas Ilyich is a fully realized, flesh-and-blood 19th-century bourgeois bureaucrat whose existential crisis arises from detailed social and domestic realities.
  • Babbitt: Similar because both embody the complacent, materialistic bourgeois professional immersed in petty conformity and commercial status; differs because Sinclair Lewis's George F. Babbitt is a satirical caricature of American middle-class boosterism, consumerism, and superficial optimism, whereas Tolstoy's Ivan Ilyich is a tragic existential figure whose spiritual agony and physical death force a profound metaphysical awakening.
  • Akaky (Akakievich): Similar because both are iconic 19th-century Russian civil service functionaries whose lives revolve around bureaucratic routine; differs because Gogol's Akaky Akakievich Bashmachkin (*The Overcoat*) is a timid, downtrodden, impoverished copyist who becomes the victim of cosmic and bureaucratic cruelty, whereas Ivan Ilyich is a wealthy, polished, successful magistrate who has enjoyed social privilege and must confront his own moral complicity.
  • Oblomov: Similar because both represent classic archetypes of the Russian 19th-century soul in crisis; differs because Ivan Goncharov's Ilya Ilyich Oblomov represents fatal apathy, inertia, and romantic daydreaming while lying in bed, whereas Ivan Ilyich Golovin is energetic, socially ambitious, and highly functional until physical mortality strikes him down.
  • Patronymic: Similar because Ilyich is linguistically a patronymic; differs because patronymic is the broad, neutral linguistic classification for any name derived from a father's given name, whereas Ilyich is an individual, highly specific name laden with immense literary, existential, and Soviet historical connotations.
  • Raskolnikov: Similar because both are monumental literary protagonists created by Russian realist titans who undergo intense psychological and moral trials; differs because Dostoevsky's Rodion Raskolnikov (*Crime and Punishment*) is a feverish, impoverished student who commits murder to test a philosophical theory of exceptionalism, whereas Tolstoy's Ivan Ilyich is a conventional, law-abiding judge who never broke a social rule yet realizes his entire life was spiritually vacant.
