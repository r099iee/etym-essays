1. A name before a capital. The cuneiform name Ninua/Ninuwa is older than the imperial city the word now calls to mind. Nineveh’s settlement on Kuyunjik reaches back to prehistoric times, and a temple of Ištar, the city’s great goddess, is attested there from the third millennium BCE; the Sumerian divine name Ninâ (‘Lady’, perhaps ‘Lady of the Waters’) lies behind the toponym according to the most common modern interpretation, though Hurrian or substrate alternatives keep the question open. Through the Middle Assyrian period the city was a royal residence and religious center while the empire’s capitals lay at Assur and then Kalhu.

2. Sennacherib’s city (704–681 BCE). The transformation came with Sennacherib, who made Nineveh the single seat of empire. The king boasts in his own inscriptions of the ‘palace without rival’, of the wall — about twelve kilometers of it with fifteen gates — of parks and gardens, and above all of water: an aqueduct at Jerwan and a canal network that turned the rain-fed Tigris plain into an irrigated capital. His reliefs (the Lachish series, the campaigns, the quarrying of winged bulls) are palace art as imperial propaganda. His successors, especially Ashurbanipal, kept the city grand and added the scholarly crown: the library of cuneiform tablets, assembled from the whole kingdom, which preserved the Epic of Gilgamesh, omen literature, medicine, mathematics, and royal correspondence. This archive is why Nineveh, alone among destroyed capitals, speaks to us with its own words.

3. The fall (612 BCE). The end was sudden and total. In alliance with Cyaxares the Mede, Nabopolassar of Babylon pressed Assyria back and, in 612, took Nineveh; the Babylonian Chronicle records plunder and the leveling of the city. The Assyrian state dissolved. Nineveh was not gradually depopulated but annihilated, and the mound it left was never rebuilt as a city. The event became the ancient world’s model of imperial catastrophe.

4. The biblical and classical afterlife. The Hebrew scriptures carried the name through the ages. Jonah, disobedient toward the ‘great city’, preaches its overthrow and instead provokes its repentance — Nineveh as the type of mercy; Nahum and Zephaniah voice its doom — Nineveh as the type of judgment. In the Septuagint the name became Nineuē, in the Vulgate Ninive. Greek historiography, above all Ctesias as transmitted by Diodorus, dressed the ruins in romance: a legendary founder Ninus and his queen Semiramis, walls said to be of staggering height and circuit — folklore that fixed the name’s fabulous character for a millennium.

5. From pious error to archaeology (Middle Ages–19th c.). Medieval geographers and pilgrims sought the biblical city at one wrong site after another; the mounds at Mosul were only guessed at. Modern recovery began with Claudius Rich (1820), continued with Botta at Khorsabad (1843), and exploded with Austen Henry Layard at Kuyunjik (1845–51). Layard’s winged bulls, his best-selling Nineveh and its Remains (1849), and the tablets shipped to London re-established both Assyria and Nineveh in the European imagination; decipherment followed (— Rawlinson, Hincks), and the library tablets began yielding the literature of Mesopotamia. In 1897 Kipling’s ‘Recessional’ turned the name into the measure of empire’s impermanence for the whole English-speaking world.

6. Nineveh in the 21st century. The site is a protected archaeological zone, its suburb names ('Nineveh Governorate', 'Ninawa') anchoring the ancient name in daily Iraqi geography. In 2015 ISIL destroyed or damaged the site’s gates and walls along with nearby Nimrud, renewing Nineveh’s oldest symbolic job: the name of a city whose destruction is itself a measure of civilization’s fragility. Conservation, reconstruction of the Mashki and Adad gates, and museum work keep the memory alive — the city that died in 612 BCE and keeps being rediscovered.
