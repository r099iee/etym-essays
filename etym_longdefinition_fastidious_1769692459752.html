fastidious: (1) very attentive to and concerned about accuracy and detail; meticulous; (2) hard to please; excessively particular, exacting, or squeamish in one's standards. Primary sense — meticulous care for detail. In modern usage a fastidious person demands high standards and pays scrupulous attention to accuracy and detail: "a fastidious editor," "she is fastidious about her appearance," "fastidious record-keeping." In this sense the word is now largely positive — it names carefulness, precision, and a refusal to accept sloppiness. This is the sense that has come to dominate in professional and descriptive contexts. Secondary sense — hard to please, overly particular. The older and still-live core of the word is negative: someone fastidious is hard to please, easily disgusted, fussily exacting. A "fastidious eater" is a picky eater; a fastidious taste rejects anything coarse or imperfect. Here the word's Latin root fastidium — loathing, squeamishness, disdain — is fully visible: the fastidious person is repelled by what falls below their standard. In Austen's description of Darcy as "haughty, reserved, and fastidious," the word carries exactly this disdainful, hard-to-please edge — Darcy's fastidiousness makes his manners "not inviting" and leads him to "continually giving offense." Technical sense — microbiology. In laboratory science, a "fastidious organism" is a microorganism that has complex, exacting nutritional requirements and grows only on specially enriched media (fastidious bacteria such as Neisseria or Streptococcus). This is a modern technical transfer: the organism, like a fussy diner, refuses anything but precisely the right fare. It is a striking example of a word of aesthetic distaste becoming a term of precise science. Full polysemy map (historical and modern):
  • Meticulous, exacting attention to accuracy and detail — modern, positive.
  • Hard to please; excessively particular or picky — modern, mildly negative.
  • Squeamish, easily disgusted; revolted by coarseness — 17th–19th-century sense.
  • Disdainful, haughty, contemptuous of what is beneath one — 15th–16th-century original English sense.
  • Technical: fastidious organism (microbiology) requiring special growth conditions.
  • Of taste or standards: over-refined, finicky, scrupulous to the point of rigidity.
The semantic bridge. Latin fastus "haughtiness, disdain" + taedium "weariness, disgust" yielded fastidium "loathing, squeamishness" and the adjective fastidiosus. English took the word in the 15th century in the "haughty" sense, softened it to "squeamish, easily disgusted," then in the 19th century began to bend it toward "scrupulously exact." The modern positive sense is the result of a long semantic rehabilitation: what once meant contemptuous disdain now often means admirable precision — the same fastidiousness of taste re-read as a virtue of character. Register and collocation:
  • "Fastidious about …" — meticulous care (positive) or fussiness (negative), context decides.
  • "Fastidious eater/dresser" — hard to please, particular.
  • "Fastidious in his habits/work" — precise, careful, scrupulous.
  • Register: formal; far stronger than "fussy," more careful than "particular," less neutral than "meticulous" (which carries no squeamishness).
  • Related family: the Romance cognates diverged — French fastidieux "tiresome, boring," Spanish fastidiar "to annoy," Italian fastidioso "annoying, tiresome." Only English preserved and developed the "hard to please" direction into its positive "meticulous" sense.
