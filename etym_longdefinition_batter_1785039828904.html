**BATTER (verb) — at its plainest core** At its most basic level, **batter** means to strike repeatedly with violent, heavy blows so as to bruise, break, or destroy. It is the verb of relentless, cumulative violence: not one clean blow but an unending rain of heavy strikes that bruises, crushes, and finally destroys the victim. In the goblins' gloating chorus "Batter and beat!" the word captures the tormentors' vision of annihilating violence against their captives — menacing, gluttonous, and dehumanizing, perfectly suited to creatures who sing of roasting and boiling their prisoners far underground. The word's frequentative character is built into its very form: it encodes beating again and again. **Primary sense: the destructive verb** As a verb, *batter* describes the action of striking something repeatedly and heavily so as to deform, damage, or break it — storm winds battering a fortress wall, hail battering a window, waves battering a shore, soldiers battering down a door. Its object is typically something resistant that is progressively worn down or broken; the word insists on both repetition and destructive force, welding the idea of insistent recurrence to the notion of crushing or deforming what is struck. **Polysemy and secondary senses** The word's history has produced a remarkable set of related but distinct forms, most of them traceable to the same Latin root *battuere*, "to beat":
  • The cooking noun: *batter* (from Old French *bateure*, "a beating") is the thin, pourable mixture of flour, egg, and liquid that is beaten until smooth — pancake batter, cake batter. The semantic bridge is the act of beating: the mixture is literally beaten into being.
  • The sports noun: a *batter* is the player who wields the bat in baseball or cricket — an agent noun from *bat*, which itself descends from the cudgel sense of the same striking family.
  • The battering ram (1610s): a heavy siege beam swung repeatedly to breach a wall — the pure military embodiment of the verb.
  • Domestic abuse: from 1962 the word describes the physical abuse of a partner — a "battered spouse," "battered woman syndrome" — the modern social sense of the verb's oldest force.
  • Battery: from Old French *baterie* ("a beating"), the word passed from a physical thrashing to the legal "assault and battery," to an artillery unit, to the electrical storage device — each step preserving the notion of stored or delivered force.
  • Architecture: a *batter wall* has a backward-sloping face, built to withstand impact; the batter is the inward lean of a wall or embankment, derived from the same beating/strengthening semantics.
**Distinctions from near-synonyms**
  • Beat: similar because both mean to strike; differs because beat is the general term for any striking, with no insistence on crushing or deforming.
  • Pound: similar because both mean repeated striking; differs because pound stresses rhythmic, driving impact, as of pistons or waves.
  • Pummel: similar because both mean repeated blows; differs because pummel implies fists or blunt blows, not tool-like strokes.
  • Thump: similar in blunt heavy striking; differs because thump is a single heavy blow or its sound, not an extended beating.
  • Hammer: similar because both suggest tool-like repeated strokes; differs because hammer implies the tool and its rhythm, while batter emphasizes destructive outcome.
  • Thrash: similar because both are violent hitting; differs because thrash suggests wilder, more chaotic, flailing force, often of a beating administered by a whip or stick.
  • Wallop: similar in heavy hitting; differs because wallop is a single resounding blow, colloquial and sudden, without batter's relentless repetition.
**Semantic reasoning and historical bridge** The polysemy of *batter* is a study in how one Latin verb of beating ("to strike") dispersed into cooking, sport, siege-warfare, law, electricity, and domestic abuse across eight centuries of borrowing. Old French *batre* (to beat) gave the cooking sense through *bateure* (a beating), the legal *baterie*, and the military *battering-ram*; the bat of sport is a separate but kindred word from the cudgel; the architectural batter (the slope of a wall) and the modern sense of spouse-abuse both draw on the same ancient image of repeated, destructive blows. Every modern sense of the word still carries the fingerprint of its root: something that beats, is beaten, or is built to withstand beating.
