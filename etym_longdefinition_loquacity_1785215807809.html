The noun loquacity names the quality or habit of talking a great deal — abundant, fluent, ceaseless speech. It is the abstract counterpart of the adjective loquacious, and it describes not merely "talking a lot" but the ready, copious, welling forth of words, often with a mildly critical or ironic flavour attached to the excess. In the Carlyle sentence that anchors this card, the word is used with exquisite irony: Mill, come to confess the burning of the manuscript of The French Revolution, "detained Carlyle with desperate attempts at loquacity for two hours." Here loquacity is not sociable chatter but talk pressed into service as a shield — nervous, compulsive, excessive speech produced from the terror of a pause, the wife speechless and the husband drowning the dreadful news in a torrent of words. The nuance is of talk as a defensive flood, and Carlyle's dry wit measures both Mill's anguish and the sheer physical effort of keeping a conversation alive without ever saying what he had come to say. Core senses and shades of meaning:
  • Primary sense — the disposition to talk much: a personal quality of fluency and abundance in speech; the habitual pouring out of words with ease and without pause, as in "her natural loquacity made her the life of every dinner party."
  • Temperamental trait: when applied to a person's character, loquacity is the temperament of the tireless talker, opposed to reticence, taciturnity, and reserve.
  • Mild censure or irony: in social and moral usage the word usually implies that the abundance overshoots the occasion — that speech is flowing faster and fuller than discretion or relevance requires, shading toward garrulity, gossip, or idle gushing.
  • Neutral or even admiring energy: in other contexts loquacity can simply describe a lively, spirited ease of talk without blame, especially where the fluency is charming rather than wearying.
  • Defensive or pathological speech: as in the card's context, loquacity can name speech produced to fill a silence that threatens the speaker — talk as avoidance, as a desperate bulwark against an unbearable truth; clinically, pressured or excessive talk (logorrhea) is a symptom of certain psychiatric and neurological states.
  • Rhetorical and literary usage: in criticism, "loquacity" is used of a style, a pen, or a passage that goes on at copious length, often with the implication that economy has been sacrificed to fluency.
Register, grammar, and collocations:
  • Register: formal and literary. Loquacity is a Latinate, learned word; in everyday speech it is largely displaced by "talkativeness." It retains a faintly classical, almost essayistic flavour.
  • Grammar: an uncountable abstract noun; it takes no ordinary plural (*loquacities is rare and jocular). Typical constructions: "the loquacity of the after-dinner speaker," "a torrent of loquacity," "his natural loquacity."
  • Common partners: "excessive loquacity," "ceaseless loquacity," "bouts of loquacity," "loquacity gave way to silence," "a gift for loquacity."
  • Family words in English: loquacious (adjective), loquaciously (adverb), loquaciousness (noun), and the whole loqu- family of learned borrowings: loquacious, eloquent, colloquial, colloquy, soliloquy, obloquy, allocution, elocution — all built on Latin loquī, "to speak."
Historical and etymological bridge:
  • English loquacity is a learned borrowing, entering the language in the early seventeenth century (c. 1600s), probably through French loquacité or directly from Latin loquācitās.
  • Latin loquācitās is the abstract noun built on the adjective loquāx, "talkative," formed from the deponent verb loquī, "to speak," with the suffix -āx, which marks an inclination or tendency (the same suffix as in audāx, "bold," and tenāx, "tenacious").
  • The deep Indo-European root is uncertain: some scholars propose *tolkw- "to speak, talk," but the family is attested almost solely in Latin, so the reconstruction is fragile and widely treated as weak or speculative.
  • Because the entire family is a Latin scholarly import, loquacity has never developed folk, everyday descendants; it is a word of the essay, the lecture, and the literary biography, which is exactly why Carlyle could use it with such deliberate precision.
Synonyms in brief: garrulity (the closest, but stressing rambling, trivial, wearisome chatter), volubility (smoothness and rapidity of delivery, a compliment), verbosity and prolixity (excess of words in writing), talkativeness (the plain everyday word). Loquacity uniquely stresses the fluent, copious, unending flow of speech itself.
