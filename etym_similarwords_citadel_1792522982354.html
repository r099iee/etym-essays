• fortress: Similar because both denote large, heavily fortified defensive military structures built to withstand siege warfare; differs because a fortress is an independent, self-contained military stronghold built anywhere in the countryside or frontier, whereas a citadel specifically commands, overlooks, or sits nestled within a wider municipality or city.
  • stronghold: Similar because both signify secure places of defensive retreat and armed protection; differs because stronghold is a broad, non-technical term encompassing natural geographic hideouts or figurative centers of ideological power, whereas citadel conveys engineered urban fortification and architectural prominence.
  • bastion: Similar because both represent formidable defensive works and are used figuratively for bulwarks of principles; differs because in architecture a bastion is a specific projecting angled work projecting outward from a curtain wall to provide flanking artillery fire, whereas a citadel is a complete multi-bastioned inner fortress complex.
  • acropolis: Similar because both denote elevated, commanding fortified settlements raised above a surrounding city; differs because an acropolis (literally 'high city' in Greek) historically served as the sacred religious, civic, and spiritual heart of an ancient Greek polis (housing major temples), whereas a citadel primarily fulfills a strictly martial, defensive, and garrisoning function.
  • redoubt: Similar because both provide fortified positions of refuge and military resistance; differs because a redoubt is a small, temporary or enclosed outwork lacking flanking defenses, often built rapidly during field campaigns, whereas a citadel is a permanent, massive stone or masonry citadel dominating an urban center.
  • keep: Similar because both represent the ultimate, innermost line of defense when exterior walls fall; differs because a keep (or donjon) is a single, towering central fortified tower within a medieval castle, whereas a citadel is an expansive urban military enclosure containing barracks, arsenals, and multiple bastions.
  • castle: Similar because both are fortified stone compounds erected for military security; differs because a castle functions simultaneously as the private, fortified aristocratic residence of a lord or sovereign and the seat of feudal administration, whereas a citadel is purely an urban municipal fortress garrisoned by professional troops.
  • bulwark: Similar because both convey formidable protection against external assault or catastrophic collapse; differs because a bulwark (originally a rampart constructed of tree trunks) denotes an earthwork wall or seawall providing physical obstruction, whereas a citadel is an entire commanding military complex.
  • fastness: Similar because both describe places of secure, unassailable shelter; differs because a fastness derives its defensive invulnerability almost entirely from inaccessible natural terrain (such as deep mountain ranges, trackless bogs, or dense forest gorges), whereas a citadel is an intensely engineered urban masonry work.
  • donjon: Similar because both are innermost defensive refuges; differs because a donjon is an archaic term specifically designating the fortified inner master tower of a Norman or continental castle, whereas a citadel encompasses an entire city-commanding fortress.
  • rampart: Similar because both are structural fortifications constructed to resist cannonades and assaults; differs because a rampart is a broad earthen or stone defensive barrier or elevated wall upon which soldiers mount artillery, whereas a citadel is the comprehensive fortified enclosure itself.
  • garrison: Similar because both are tied to armed municipal defense; differs because a garrison designates the body of troops stationed within a defensive post rather than the physical stone walls and architecture of the citadel itself.
