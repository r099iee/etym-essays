- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - *Bita* meant a mouthful, small bite of food, or fragment; *bite* was the metal mouthpiece of a bridle that the horse 'bites'.
- 12th–15th c. (Middle English) - Form collapsed into *bite* / *bit*, expanding in equestrian culture across medieval chivalry while solidifying general idiomatic uses ('a bit of bread', 'not a bit').
- 16th–18th c. (Early Modern English) - Proliferated into idioms of equine control ('take the bit between one's teeth' = to become unruly; 'champ at the bit' = to show restless impatience) and mechanical tools (drill bit).
- 1948 (Claude Shannon & Information Theory) - Coined as a portmanteau of *binary digit* by John Tukey and popularized by Shannon, becoming the foundational unit of computing and digital information.
- 21st c. (Contemporary English) - Thrives across equestrian sports, mechanical engineering, everyday conversational quantification ('wait a bit'), and computer science.
