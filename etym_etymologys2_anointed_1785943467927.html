LINEAR CHAIN (single root word ANOINTED — the past participle of ANOINT, a loanword through French from Latin inunguere) Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *ongʷ- "to salve, smear, anoint with oil or grease" (c. 4000 BCE); a root of the practical smearing of fats and oils, attested also in Greek *enkh- (aliphein is the usual Greek verb for anointing, but the root survives in the Old Irish and Germanic spheres) and reflected in Latin unguen "fat, grease" and unguentum "ointment". Stage 2 — Latin unguere "to smear, rub with oil or grease, anoint" (attested from the 2nd century BCE in Plautus); a verb of ordinary, everyday oiling — the athlete's body, the guest's head at a banquet, the corpse for burial, the wound for healing. Its participle and derivatives gave unguentum "ointment, perfume". Stage 3 — Latin inunguere "to smear on, to anoint" (attested from the 1st century BCE–CE); formed with the prefix in- "on, upon, into" (from PIE *en "in") + unguere. The compound specialised the action: not merely to oil oneself in the baths, but to apply oil to a person or object as a solemn, marking, consecrating act. Late Latin inunctio "anointing" (the source of unction) belongs to the same verb. Stage 4 — Old French enoindre "to anoint" (attested from the 10th–11th century CE); inherited from Latin inungere through Vulgar Latin, with the expected palatalisation of the velar before the front vowel producing the sequence [nj], and the past participle enoint. The verb carried both the religious sense (consecration, last rites) and the everyday medicinal sense. Stage 5 — Anglo-Norman and Middle English anoynten/anointen "to anoint" (first recorded in English c. 1300, in the writings of the period's religious and legal texts); the Old French enoindre entered English through the Anglo-Norman channel with the initial vowel shifted to a-, giving the forms anoint, anoynt. The verb was from the start dominated by the religious use: the anointing of kings, priests, and the sick. Stage 6 — Middle English anointed (past participle and adjective) "consecrated with holy oil, set apart" (recorded from the 14th century); used in the biblical phrase "the Lord's anointed" for the sacral king of Israel, and for the English monarch anointed at coronation. Final node — Modern English ANOINTED "consecrated or chosen by ritual smearing of oil" (standard since the 16th century); ANOINTED. NOTE ON THE TWO ELEMENTS: The word is a single rootWord, but its Latin ancestor is a compound. Element 1: PIE *en "in, into" → Latin in- "on, upon". Element 2: PIE *ongʷ- "to smear, anoint" → Latin unguere "to smear with oil". The chain above follows the unguere element, which carries the core meaning. NOTE ON DEBATED ETYMOLOGY: The line PIE *ongʷ- → Latin unguere is the standard and well-attested reconstruction (the root appears in Hittite and in Greek *onkh-, and Latin unguere, unguen, unguentum are all securely formed from it). The only nuance is that English anoint, although ultimately from Latin inunguere, reached English through the French/ Anglo-Norman channel, so its direct parent is Old French enoindre rather than Latin itself; the "in-" prefix is therefore inherited via French and not added in English. Related by the same root: ointment (Old French oignement, from Latin unguentum) and unguent (direct Latin loan).
