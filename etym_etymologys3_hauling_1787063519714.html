1. A word with a circular history: Germanic to French to English

Haul is an unusual English verb because it arrived through French — but the French word itself was borrowed from Germanic. Old French haler, “to pull, drag, haul” (a sailor’s and boatman’s word), goes back to the Frankish world, either Frankish *halōn “to fetch, summon, pull” or Old Norse hala “to haul (a boat)”. Both descend from Proto-Germanic *halōną, “to fetch, summon, call”, ultimately from an Indo-European root meaning “to call out” — the striking idea being that to “fetch” is to “summon to come”, and pulling something toward you is a physical calling of it. The word thus crossed from Germanic into French and then came back into English a century or two later: a circular journey, Germanic to Romance to Germanic.

2. Into Middle English (14th century)

English took haler in the 14th century as halen (and the by-form haulen), meaning “to drag, pull, draw”. From the start it was a word of heavy work: Middle English texts haul nets, ropes, ships, and great weights. Notably, English already had native synonyms — draw, drag, pull, tug — but haul earned its place precisely because it meant the heavy, sustained, pulling-with-effort kind of drag: the specialist’s pull. The word quickly became standard for rope-work and watercraft, the two domains where pulling is at once skilled, heavy, and constant.

3. The nautical century (16th–18th c.)

From the 16th century haul and its participle hauling are everywhere in English seafaring language: hauling on sheets and halyards, hauling up a boat, hauling in a line, hauling the wind (sailing closer into the wind). Sea-dogs’ English, logbooks, and the great age of sail all made hauling a defining maritime verb — the physical poetry of ropes and wind. It is no accident that the source context here (“hauling up what they needed from below with their ropes”) is a cliffside operation using exactly this nautical machinery: ropes, weight, and a vertical haul.

4. Industrialisation: from sea to land (19th c.)

The 19th century widened hauling beyond the water. Canals, railways, mines, and factories all needed heavy things moved, and hauling named the job: horses hauling barges and wagons, steam engines hauling trains, winding gear hauling ore to the surface. The nouns haulage and haulage-way, and the agent nouns hauler and haulier, date from this industrial moment, and “haul road”, “haulage firm”, and “long-distance hauling” enter the professional vocabulary of transport. Meanwhile the colloquial, financial, and police senses flower: “hauling in” profit (as one hauls in a net) and “hauling off” a wrongdoer are informal coinages of this period that survive today.

5. The twentieth century and today

  • The literal physical sense remains fully alive: hauling ropes, boats, sledges, furniture, and nets.
  • The dominant modern professional sense is goods transport by road — the haulage industry — where hauler and haulier are the standard agent nouns.
  • Colloquial senses persist in finance (“hauling in cash”) and law enforcement (“hauled in for questioning”).
  • The participle hauling, because it is a verb form, keeps all of these senses available in a single word; in the source text it is doing its most ancient job — pulling loads upward with ropes.

Summary: a Germanic verb of fetching and summoning, loaned into Old French, reborrowed into English in the 14th century as the heavy-pull specialist; it spent three centuries as a nautical word, gained industrial and road-transport empire in the 19th century, and today carries both the physical effort of the rope and the mass logistics of the motorway in the same lively verb-form.
