**YAMMER: a lament that soured into a nag** YAMMER has no secure Indo-European pedigree; etymologists class it as an imitative or expressive formation, a word that grew out of the very sound it describes. Its earliest recoverable Germanic ancestor is the reconstructed *jēmaraz, "sorrowful, miserable," which surfaces in Old Saxon jamar "sad," Old High German jāmar (modern German Jammer, "lamentation, misery"), and Old English geōmor, "sorrowful, distressed." From the Old English adjective arose the verb geōmrian, "to lament, mourn, complain, groan," which survived into Middle English as yeomeren or yemeren, still purely mournful in tone. The word thus began life as a genuine word of grief — the sound of a person or people lamenting real sorrow, with the heavy, drawn-out quality of mourning ritual. **The Continental stream and the blending in English** Meanwhile the Continental Germanic dialects had developed their own parallel verbs — Middle Low German jammeren and Middle Dutch jammeren, "to wail, grieve," ancestors of modern Dutch jammeren and German jammern. In the late fifteenth century (in Caxton's prints) English absorbed the Continental form as yameren, and native and imported streams mingled. The two forms were so close in sound and sense that they fused in the single modern verb: the inherited geōmrian line and the borrowed jammeren line both fed the same word, reinforcing its vocalic pattern and its meaning of wailing, sorrowful noise. **Semantic pejoration: from mourning to nagging** Almost at once the meaning began to shift — a process linguists call pejoration. By the 1510s yammer could mean "to make a loud, annoying noise": the mournful lament had degraded into mere clamorous complaint, and finally into noisy chatter and nagging. The sound of genuine grief became the sound of an irritating, attention-seeking whinge. The Old English geōmor "sorrowful" lost its dignity; what was once the cry of the bereaved became the yap of the querulous. This trajectory — a word of mourning sliding toward annoyance — is a miniature case study in how emotional registers flatten over time, as everyday use drains the gravity from grief-vocabulary. **The German offshoot and literary life** The German branch spun off the expressive compound Katzenjammer, literally "wailing of cats," borrowed into English in 1821 for a hangover and for discordant noise generally — a vivid, onomatopoeic image of a racket nobody wants. In English literature and journalism, yammer appears as the sound of crowds, wolves, machines, and complainers — always loud, persistent, and slightly ridiculous. It is the noise of the mob, the howl of the pack, the drone of the nag. In Modern English YAMMER names a loud, persistent, irritating outcry — whether of animals, machines, or complaining people — exactly the kind of helpless, ugly racket the goblins gleefully anticipate from their victims in "Yammer and bleat!" The word has come full circle from lament to mockery: the sound of grief that once deserved pity is now the sound of noise that deserves ridicule, and the goblins' jeer draws its cruelty precisely from that ancient downgrade.
