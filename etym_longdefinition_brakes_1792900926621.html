Brakes, the plural of the thicket-brake, are patches of dense, tangled undergrowth — bracken, bramble, hazel scrub, thorn — massed so thick that neither eye nor foot passes easily. The word belongs to the lane, the hillside, and the wood-margin: those in-between places where the managed land gives up and the wild takes back over. Tolkien’s ‘deep brakes of hazel’ are exactly this: not a forest and not a field, but the shaggy slopes between, where young thicket presses close on either hand.

In the source sentence the depth matters as much as the density: ‘many deep ______ of hazel on the rising slopes.’ Deep here means thick through and through, layered, hiding-places deep — the lane runs like a cutting between walls of green. The walkers pass through a corridor of scrub, overlooked by nothing, overlooked by everything that might lurk. The brake is landscape as enclosure, the opposite of the open hilltop.

Core meaning in detail: A brake is a clump or stretch of bushes, briars, ferns, or low tangled trees occupying waste or woodland-edge ground. Bracken-brakes glow copper in autumn; bramble-brakes bar the way with thorn; hazel-brakes, like Tolkien’s, are coppice-like thickets of slender poles and broad leaves. Game lives in brakes — pheasants, rabbits, deer — and so the word carries a sportsman’s ear: to beat the brakes is to drive covert game into the open.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A thicket of brushwood, bracken, or brambles (primary, from late 14th c.): brakes of hazel, ferny brakes. The bridge from the fern-sense is metonymy: ground thick with bracken becomes the name for any ground thick with tangled growth.
  • The bracken or fern plant itself (older, related): a brake of fern, brake-fern. This is likely the parent sense — the plant that defines the place — before the place swallowed the plant.
  • Covert for game, hunting ground (sporting extension): to draw the brakes for a fox. The bridge is use: thickets shelter animals, so the brake becomes, to the hunter, a container of quarry.
  • A clump of reeds or canes (riverine variant): cane-brakes of the American South. The bridge crosses the Atlantic with settlers, who found a new tangle — giant cane — and gave it the old name.
  • The stopping-device brake (homonym, different origin): carriage brakes, bicycle brakes. This is a separate word — from the crushing-machine brake of Dutch origin — that converged in spelling only; the thicket-brake stops nobody, though Tolkien’s lanes may feel otherwise.

Register and connotation: The thicket-brake is rural, old-world, and faintly poetic — Shakespeare’s ‘bank where the wild thyme blows’ sleeps in a brake, and Bottom is translated there. It smells of leaf-mould, fox-earth, and autumn bracken, and it always implies cover: something may be hidden inside. Modern ears must keep it strictly apart from the mechanical brake of cars and bicycles, its noisy homonym; the thicket is silent, green, and patient, and it slows travellers the way beauty slows them — by being worth stopping for.
