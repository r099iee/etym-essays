- 16th c. — borrowed as a learned adjective for the medieval theologians and their method; in humanist and Protestant polemic it is usually hostile, meaning barbarous, quibbling, unchristian in style.
- 17th–18th c. — the pejorative dominates English philosophical prose; Bacon, Locke and the Enlightenment use “scholastic” to dismiss an argument as verbal trickery.
- 19th c. — a neutral educational sense grows in American usage (scholastic year, scholastic records), while Catholic revivalism makes the medieval sense respectable again under the name neo-scholasticism.
- 20th c. — scholastic aptitude and standardized testing fix the institutional sense, and the word becomes a familiar classifier in school publishing and administration.
- 21st c. — the medieval sense is confined to intellectual history, the educational sense sounds formal and slightly dated, and the pejorative remains alive whenever a scholar is accused of preferring technique to thought.
