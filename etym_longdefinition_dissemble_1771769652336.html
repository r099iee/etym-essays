In the famous close of his memoirs, Edward Gibbon declares that he "will not dissemble the first emotions of joy on the recovery of [his] freedom, and perhaps, the establishment of [his] fame." To dissemble there would be to hide elation under a false face of modesty — the courtly or scholarly pose that pretends a finished masterpiece was no great relief. By refusing to dissemble, Gibbon stages transparency: the long bondage of composition is over, and joy is admitted without cosmetic humility. The verb's tone is ethical and slightly theatrical; it implies that concealment of feeling is always available as a social strategy, and that candor is a deliberate, praiseworthy choice.

Core meaning: to conceal one's true motives, feelings, or beliefs by presenting a misleading outward appearance — in short, to make the truth look unlike itself. The word comes from Latin dissimulāre, "to make unlike, to disguise," a compound of dis- "apart, un-" and simulāre "to make like." Etymologically, to dissemble is to "make [the real thing] unlike" — to deform the face of the true while wearing a plausible mask.

Senses and uses:
  • Conceal true feelings behind a false appearance (core sense) — hiding anger, joy, grief, or fear behind calm, cheer, or indifference, as in "he dissembled his disappointment."
  • To feign a contrary state of mind — not merely to hide but to actively put on an opposite face: to dissemble innocence while knowing guilt, to dissemble friendship while plotting.
  • Intransitive: to practice dissimulation; to be a hypocrite — "she was too honest to dissemble." Here the verb means habitual false seeming, not a single act.
  • Rare/archaic: to disguise or conceal oneself as another — to "dissemble oneself" as a beggar or pilgrim, playing a part physically; attested in older prose, now obsolete.
  • The rhetorical negative formula — "I will not dissemble…," "I shall not dissemble…": a flourish of emphatic frankness in which a writer renounces the available mask before making a confession or claim. Gibbon's use is the classic instance; the pattern runs through 18th-century memoir and essay prose.
  • As a participle/adjective, dissembled = false, assumed, counterfeit — "dissembled grief," "dissembled loyalty," where the participle modifies the feigned thing rather than the agent.
Polysemy in modern registers:
  • Psychology and self-presentation — in the modern jargon of impression management, "dissembling" overlaps with "masking" and "strategic self-presentation": a person may dissemble to manage others' perceptions, not out of malice but out of self-protection.
  • Historiography and biography — historians use "dissemble" to describe courtly dissimulation, diplomatic feints, and political hypocrisy in pre-modern and early modern courts; it is the idiomatic everyday verb for "dissimulation."
  • Legal and rhetorical contexts — in older legal and clerical prose, dissembling is a species of fraud or hypocrisy (a witness "dissembling" the truth); in rhetoric, dissimulatio is the classic figure of irony by understatement or pretended ignorance.
  • Theatrical and literary metaphor — "mask," "cloak," and "veil" metaphors cluster around the word; a character in fiction "dissembles" when he hides the true self behind a performed role, linking the verb to the ancient trope of life as theater.
Boundary contrasts:
  • Dissimulate — near doublet, more learned and technical; dissemble is the more idiomatic literary verb for hiding feelings.
  • Conceal / hide — broader and morally neutral; one can conceal without any false face.
  • Feign / simulate — they invent a fiction (feign illness, simulate a signal); dissemble disguises what truly exists.
  • Pretend — wider, often playful or childish; dissemble carries the deliberate ethical charge of hypocrisy.
  • Mask / cloak — figurative and less precise about motive; dissemble implies a sustained strategy of false seeming.
Collocations: dissemble one's feelings/emotions/anger/joy/fear; not to dissemble the fact that…; to dissemble friendship/ignorance/innocence; an ability (or gift) for dissembling; a dissembled countenance. Register: literary, formal, slightly archaic-elevated; rare in everyday speech, where "hide" and "pretend" dominate. The verb continues to thrive in historiography, moral and political essays, and period drama and fiction.
