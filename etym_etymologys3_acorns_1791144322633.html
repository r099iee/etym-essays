The story of acorn, whose plural acorns appears here, is a classic case of folk-etymology obscuring a once-transparent Germanic word. Old English æcern did not mean oak-nut specifically but mast in general: the nuts, beechmast, and forest fruit that fed swine and, in famine, men. It derives from Proto-Germanic *akrana, fruit, crop, produce, itself from *akraz, field, harvest, acre, ultimately from PIE *h₂eǵros, field or pasture driving-place. The semantic path was field to fruit-of-field to tree-nut, narrowing as woodland economy specialized. Cognates include Old Norse akarn, Gothic akran fruit, Old High German ackaran, confirming a common Germanic agricultural term, not an oak-specific one.

In Middle English the word appears as akern, acharn, akorn with metathesis and vowel variation, still meaning any mast. From the 15th-16th centuries speakers reanalysed the opaque first syllable a- as oak and the second -kern as corn in its old sense of grain, seed, kernel. Spelling acorn and oak-corn appears, pronunciation shifts to /ˈeɪkɔːn/ under that analogy, and meaning narrows decisively to the fruit of the oak. This is folk-etymology: a familiar compound replaces an unfamiliar simplex without changing the referent for most speakers, but permanently altering spelling and perceived morphology. Botanists still note the irony that acorns are not corn and oaks are not grain.

  • Old English mast economy: Æcern-feeding was a legal right: pannage payments for turning swine into woods to eat mast. Charters measure woods by swine capacity, not acreage. Æcern included beech and oak alike, a vital autumn resource.
  • Middle English narrowing: As beechmast keeps its own name and oak-mast dominates English woods, akern gravitates to oak. Herbals and husbandry texts list akernes for hogs, deer, and medicinal astringency. Famine bread from acorn meal is recorded in dearth years.
  • Early Modern remodelling: Printed herbals fix acorn spelling. Gerard, Parkinson, and Evelyn discuss oak-apples, acorn-cups, and acorn-meal. The oak + corn analysis is taught as if etymological, entrenching the error in dictionaries until 19th-century comparative philology restores æcern to *akrana.
  • Natural-history and symbolic career: Woodpeckers storing acorns, jays caching them, squirrels burying them become textbook examples of animal foresight and forest regeneration, exactly the image in the source sentence. Proverbs grow: great oaks from little acorns grow, the acorn as emblem of latent power, frugality, and American frontier sustenance. Acorn woodpeckers with granary trees and oak savannas give the plural acorns ecological salience.
  • Plural morphology: Old English æcern was neuter with plural æcern; Middle English generalizes -s, giving acorns. Modern singular acorn, plural acorns is fully regular, with RP /ˈeɪkɔːn/ to /ˈeɪkɔːnz/ voicing assimilation.

Today acorns denote strictly the ovoid nut of Quercus species seated in a woody cupule, green to brown, bitter with tannins, edible after leaching, crucial for wildlife and oak reproduction. The plural in woodpecker contexts stresses multitude and storage: granaries drilled with thousands of acorns. The long drift from field-fruit to oak-nut to symbol of thrift and potential shows how economy, analogy, and natural observation jointly remake a word while leaving its consonantal skeleton /k-r-n/ almost untouched for two millennia.
