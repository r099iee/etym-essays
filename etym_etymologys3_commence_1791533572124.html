The historical evolution of commence is a quintessential case study in the sociolinguistic stratification of post-Conquest English. It illustrates how the collision between Old English and Anglo-Norman established a permanent bilingual doublet system, assigning native Germanic words to ordinary, domestic life and Romance borrowings to legal, administrative, and ceremonial registers.

1. Classical Latin Foundations and Vulgar Latin Re-formation

In Classical Latin, the standard verbs for beginning were incipere ("to take in hand, begin") and the defective verb coepisse ("to have begun"). However, the noun initium ("an entrance, beginning," from the verb inire, "to enter, go into," composed of in- "in" + ire "to go") also gave rise to the denominative verb initiare. In the classical and imperial eras, initiare meant specifically to initiate someone into religious rites, sacred mysteries, or specialized disciplines.

During the transition from Imperial Latin to Late and Vulgar Latin (circa 4th to 6th century CE), popular spoken Latin across Western Europe systematically favored prefixed, phonetically heavier, and more emphatic verb compounds over older, phonetically reduced Classical forms. In spoken Gaul, the collective and intensive prefix com- (from PIE *kom-, "together, with") was joined to initiare, producing the unattested but firmly reconstructed Proto-Romance compound verb *cominitiare, meaning "to begin together, to set in motion, to undertake an enterprise." This innovative compound completely displaced Classical incipere and coepisse in Western Romance speech, evolving into Old French comencier, Old Occitan comensar, Italian cominciare, and Spanish comenzar.

2. Old French and the Anglo-Norman Conquest

In Old French, comencier (attested from the late 11th century, notably in the Chanson de Roland) became the universal primary verb for beginning. Following the Norman Conquest of England in 1066, Anglo-Norman French was installed as the language of the royal court, the baronial aristocracy, the judiciary, and monastic scriptoria. When bilingual Norman clerks and legal scribes drafted administrative scrolls, court pleadings, and chivalric romances in England, comencier was the natural verb of governance and courtly narrative.

3. Middle English Naturalisation and Semantic Stratification

By the late 13th century (circa 1280), as English reasserted itself as a written vernacular, Anglo-Norman administrative vocabulary flooded into Middle English. The verb was adopted as commencen (also spelled comensen or comounsen). Crucially, English did not lose its native Old English verb beginnan (modern begin), nor the related starten (originally meaning "to leap, flinch, jump"). Instead, a rich semantic division of labor emerged:

  • Begin remained the universal, emotionally neutral, intimate native word spoken at home, in fields, and in everyday hearthside conversation.
  • Commence was retained as a deliberate marker of stylistic elevation, rhetorical grandeur, and official dignity. It appeared in Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales, Wycliffite texts, and the Rolls of Parliament to signal the outset of legal treatises, university disputes, and solemn festivities.

4. The Medieval University and "Commencement"

A profound development occurred in late medieval university culture. At the universities of Paris, Oxford, and Cambridge, the formal conferral of the Master of Arts or Doctor of Theology degree was called the inceptio or commenciatio. The candidate "commenced" as a full member of the faculty, entering into the rights, robes, and privileges of the teaching guild. The culminating university ceremony became known as Commencement. While in Britain the terminology eventually yielded to "congregation" or "graduation," North American colonial colleges (beginning with Harvard in 1642) preserved Commencement as the official name for graduation day, directly perpetuating this 14th-century scholastic usage.

5. Early Modern to Contemporary Stylistic History

During the Renaissance and 18th century, the expansion of English statutory law solidified commence as an indispensable technical term of Chancery and common-law pleading. An action at law was formally "commenced" when the initial writ was issued. In the 19th century, excessive Latinate diction in Victorian civil service documents led stylistic reformers (such as Henry Fowler and later George Orwell) to warn against overusing commence as a pompous synonym where plain begin would suffice. Despite these purist critiques, commence remains an indispensable element of contemporary English, retaining its specific functional roles in legal enactments, military directives, corporate contracts, and formal public inaugurations.
