NOUMENON (noun, plural NOUMENA) is the term of Kantian philosophy for a thing as it is in itself, independent of all possible perception and of the forms of intuition (space and time) that structure every appearance. It stands opposed to the PHENOMENON, the thing as it appears to a perceiving mind. In Empson's sentence — "A poem is a noumenon rather than a phenomenon" — the word is wielded with deliberate philosophical precision: a poem's true being lies not in its sensory surface (the printed letters, the heard rhythms) but in an ideal, mind-constructed object that the intellect alone can grasp. The tone is elevated, ironic, and insistently anti-empiricist; Empson harnesses Kantian metaphysics to insist that meaning is not perceived but thought, and that the critic's task is to recover a thing-in-itself that never appears directly to the senses.

Polysemy and the full range of uses.
  • Kantian technical sense (positive): the object of a purely intellectual intuition — a thing that would be known by the understanding alone if such an intuition existed. Kant holds that human beings possess no such intuition, so this sense names a limiting idea rather than a known object.
  • Kantian technical sense (negative): the thing insofar as it is NOT an object of our sensible intuition — the unknowable "thing-in-itself" (Ding an sich), the "x" that lies beyond every appearance and can be thought but never known.
  • General philosophical / post-Kantian: any object of thought that transcends experience; ultimate or unconditioned reality; that which reason posits beyond the bounds of possible experience. In German Idealism the "intelligible world" of noumena becomes the ground of freedom and morality.
  • Literary and aesthetic theory: the ideal, mind-constructed being of a work of art as opposed to its material or sensory embodiment — Empson's sense. A poem as noumenon is the meaning-thought, not the text.
  • Looser vernacular (rare, often loose): "the hidden reality behind appearances," or anything known only by the intellect rather than the senses. In loose speech the word is often conflated with "essence" or "abstraction."

Semantic bridge and derivations. The word is the Latinized spelling of the Greek participle nooumenon, "a thing being thought of," from the verb noein, "to perceive with the mind." Kant chose the Greek form deliberately to invoke the ancient vocabulary of nous, "mind, intellect." From the noun come the adjective NOUMENAL ("pertaining to noumena"; the noumenal world), the adverb NOUMENALLY, and the plural NOUMENA. The word is essentially confined to philosophy, theology, and literary theory — it is a high-register, rarified term that signals technical competence and, outside Kant scholarship, often carries a quasi-mystical aura of ultimate hidden reality. Collocations: "noumenal reality," "noumenal self," "the noumenal/phenomenal distinction," "the unknowable noumenon," "the noumenon behind the phenomenon." The opposite of a noumenon is always a phenomenon; the two terms are used as a paired distinction and should never be collapsed. In a Kantian framework, the noumenon is thinkable but not knowable: reason can postulate it as the ground of appearances, but no empirical evidence can ever confirm its character.
