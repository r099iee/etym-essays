- Old English to Middle English (10th–14th c.) - The verb *fīlian* and noun *fīle* (the toothed abrasive tool) were standard metallurgical terms. *Filings* emerged in late Middle English (c. 1380s) specifically in alchemy and metalcraft for the fine powdered metallic residue scraped off when polishing or shaping iron, gold, and silver.
- 17th Century (Scientific Revolution) - Became a central experimental demonstration object in natural philosophy; William Gilbert (*De Magnete*, 1600) and later Robert Boyle popularized scattering "iron filings" over paper to reveal invisible magnetic flux lines and field contours.
- 18th–19th Century (Industrial Revolution) - Multiplied across manufacturing, metallurgy, and lock-making as workshop residue; simultaneously adopted by Romantic and Victorian essayists (Carlyle, Emerson) as a standard metaphor for scattered, atomized ideas assembling under an invisible organizing force or ideological magnet.
- 20th Century - Deepened in literary theory and modern philosophy (T.S. Eliot's catalytic analogies, Roland Barthes' textual fragments) to describe how diverse textual details align magnetically into coherent patterns.
- 21st Century - Continues in secondary school physics classrooms and engineering workshops, while retaining its evocative metaphorical resonance in cultural and critical prose.
