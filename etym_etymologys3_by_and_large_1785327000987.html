BY AND LARGE was born at sea. Its two halves are ordinary English words — by, the ancient Germanic preposition of nearness, and large, the Romance adjective of breadth and generosity — but the phrase itself was coined in the seventeenth-century sailing navy as a technical verdict on a ship, and only gradually drifted ashore to become the modern idiom of hedged generalisation. Its history shows how a precise professional observation, repeated until it became shorthand, can crystallise into a piece of everyday grammar.

The two words at sea. In seventeenth-century seamanship, a ship “sailed by” when she was close-hauled — lying as near to the wind as her rig would allow, her sails sheeted in hard and her bow pointed almost into the wind, the course a vessel must take when beating to windward. She “sailed large” when the wind was free, coming from abaft the beam, so that the sails were eased out and the vessel ran before or across it. Every point between these two extremes — close-hauled, close-reaching, beam-reaching, broad-reaching, running — was a course in the sailor’s table, but the phrase “by and large” named the whole range of sailing conditions at a stroke: a ship that handled “by and large” was one that behaved creditably in every condition of wind and sea, whether fighting into the wind or running before it.

The first records. The earliest known English attestation is in the diary of Samuel Pepys, who in 1669 recorded the phrase in its technical nautical sense as a description of how a ship performs “by and large” — that is, throughout the entire gamut of sailing conditions. From the navy the phrase spread quickly through the language of the sea, and the seaman’s all-round verdict on a vessel was a natural candidate for metaphor. A ship that could be trusted in all conditions could be trusted without qualification; the transfer to people, plans, and situations was only a matter of time.

The shift ashore. By the early eighteenth century the phrase had left the deck for the desk. Writers such as Daniel Defoe and Jonathan Swift used “by and large” to mean “in all respects, everywhere, taking everything together” — a comprehensive summary, not yet the hedged generalisation it later became. This interim sense is the semantic hinge of the word’s history: the nautical meaning had been “in every condition of the wind”; the metaphorical step generalised it to “in every condition whatsoever”; and from there it was a short further step to “on the whole, everything considered,” the sense that carries a broad truth while leaving the door open for exceptions. By the nineteenth century the modern value was firmly established, and “by and large” had settled into the polite formula by which a careful speaker asserts a general claim without pretending to completeness.

Sound changes and forms. Neither element underwent any further sound change within the phrase; English by and large are simply the ordinary modern forms of Old English bī and Middle English large, whose pronunciations have evolved in the ordinary course of English sound change (the Great Vowel Shift reshaping the vowel of by, and the medieval and early modern development of the a in large giving its modern ɑː-type vowel). The real interest is syntactic and morphological. “By and large” is a frozen, irreversible binomial: the order of its two elements cannot be swapped, no element can be modified or expanded, and the phrase behaves as a single unit in the sentence. Linguists class it among English’s irreversible binomials — alongside “cup and saucer,” “salt and pepper,” and “to and fro” — and its fixedness is the proof that it is now an idiom, not a freely assembled prepositional phrase. Its register today is neutral-to-informal: equally at home in conversation, journalism, and the cautious prose of scholarship, where it lets an author make a responsible generalisation.

The word in the card. In the card’s sentence — a scholar explaining that he has had to limit his scope to the main tradition of accentual-syllabic verse in Middle and Modern English — the phrase performs exactly this modern office. “By and large, therefore, I have had to limit my scope”: the author concedes, in the idiom’s built-in way, that other verse traditions exist and have been set aside, while affirming that, exceptions apart, his chosen corpus is the relevant field. The idiom’s nautical origins are invisible in everyday use — few speakers think of close-hauled ships when they say “by and large” — but the deep structure of the phrase still carries its old logic: a verdict delivered with the whole range of conditions in view, a judgement passed after sailing the whole sea. From the forecastle to the monograph, the phrase has never lost its original confidence that the general view, honestly taken, is the trustworthy one.
