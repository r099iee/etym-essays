*mregh-u- (Proto-Indo-European root; prehistoric, c. 4000–2500 BCE) — “short, brief” → Proto-Germanic *murgijaz (reconstructed; Germanic, 1st millennium BCE) — “short-lasting; pleasing, delightful” (the semantic bridge “brief → pleasant”, the idea that pleasant time passes quickly, is reconstructed and debated) → Old English myrge (also merg(e); attested from the earliest Old English texts, 8th–9th c.) — “pleasing, agreeable, pleasant, sweet” (e.g. of a melody or a gift) → Middle English mirie, mery, merry (12th–15th c.) — “pleasant, delightful, joyful, festive, lively” → Early Modern English merry (16th c.) — “cheerful, jovial, full of good spirits; (of a season) festive” → English merriment (merry + suffix -ment, denoting a state or condition; first attested mid-16th c., e.g. in the Book of Common Prayer and in Shakespeare) — “the state of being merry; festivity, gaiety, laughter” → Modern English merriment (present-day) — “gaiety and fun; cheerful high spirits, especially social and audible” NOTE (debated/nuanced): The change of sense from “short” to “pleasant/delightful” within Germanic is one of the most discussed semantic puzzles in English etymology; the usual explanation — that time spent agreeably feels short — is plausible but unverifiable, and a minority of scholars argue that the two senses developed independently from the same root or that the Germanic form is contaminated with a separate root.
