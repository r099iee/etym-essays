The word jiggered begins in motion. Its family is the imitative cluster built on the base *gig-, a sound-symbolic Germanic element that evokes quick, jerky, bouncing movement—the same imitation of rapid oscillation that underlies the English verbs jiggle, jitter, and jog, and the noun gig, a light carriage named, as contemporaries felt, for its bouncing ride. From this base, in the sixteenth century, English produced jig, the lively, jerky dance (recorded from about 1560), a word probably supported by the Middle French gigue, "a dance and its fiddle-music," itself an offshoot of the same Germanic sound-word. German shows the same complex in Geige, "fiddle, violin," so the whole family is recognisably a set of coinages meant to sound like quick, repeated, bouncing motion rather than a set of words inherited from Proto-Indo-European; no PIE ancestor can be reconstructed for it. 1. The imitative birth and the dance
  • Imitative base *gig- / *jig- (Germanic): quick, jerky, bouncing motion or sound. The Middle English gigge (14th century) meant a giddy girl and, more revealingly, a top spun by a whip—an object whose whole character is the rapid, shaking spin; the 16th-century gig, a light two-wheeled carriage, was named, contemporaries said, for its jerky, bouncing ride.
  • English jig (c. 1560): the lively, jerky dance, from the same base, with the probable reinforcement of Middle French gigue, "a dance and its fiddle-music"; the German Geige, "fiddle," shows the same imitative complex, so that the dance, the music, and the instrument all share one sound-picture.
  • Derived verbs: to jig, "to dance a jig; to move with quick jerky motions"; jiggle (19th century, with the frequentative suffix -le), "to move with a rapid, slight, jerky motion."
2. The 19th-century slang expansion
  • From jig, English formed jigger with the agentive suffix -er (19th century). The word meant "a person who jigs," then, in the great Victorian habit of calling any little thing a jigger, "a small device, gadget, or thing"—hence the American bartender's jigger, the small glass that measures a shot of spirits.
  • The verb to jigger meant "to jerk or shake; to fiddle with; to tamper with, to spoil"—"to jigger something up." From it came the chiefly British adjective jiggered, "broken, ruined, spoilt."
  • From the measure of spirits came the chiefly American sense jiggered, "slightly intoxicated," i.e. having had a jigger or two.
3. The exclamatory sense (the sense of this card)
  • In the 1830s the phrase "I'll be jiggered!" appeared: a genteel, nonsense minced oath, read by most lexicographers as a euphemistic softening of "I'll be buggered!"—i.e. "I'll be damned." The speaker turns the strong word into a harmless near-twin, and the very absurdity of the substitute increases its expressive force, so jiggered became the gentle, half-jocular exclamation of astonishment and consternation.
  • It joined a whole family of self-directed oaths—I'll be blowed!, I'll be blessed!, I'll be damned!—as the gentlest and most whimsical member, mild enough for children's literature yet vivid enough for real mental rattling, which is exactly how C.S. Lewis uses it when Peter, confounded by the discovery, says "Well, I'm—I'm jiggered," and his voice "also sounded queer."
4. The three paths and the modern date
  • The three senses of jiggered—astonished, broken, intoxicated—are three paths out of the one verb to jigger, "to jolt, to shake": a man who is jiggered has been jolted, whether by a shock, by a mechanism shaken to pieces, or by a dram of spirits. Peter's use shows the exclamatory path at work: the jolt is to the composure, and the word both names it and softens it.
  • In the 20th and 21st centuries the word aged into a dated, half-jocular schoolboy exclamation; a modern speaker would say gobsmacked or dumbfounded, so jiggered now works as deliberate period flavour—the sound of a gentleman being jolted, his voice gone queer, a fossil of Edwardian politeness preserved in the amber of the page.
