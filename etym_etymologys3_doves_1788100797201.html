1. The Germanic bird-name and the PIE question. English “dove” descends from Old English dūfe (a weak feminine noun), attested from about the 8th century in glosses and continued into Middle English as douve, duve, dove. The word belongs to a compact West and North Germanic family: Old Saxon dūba, Old Frisian dūve, Old High German tūba (German Taube), Middle Dutch duve (Dutch duif), Old Norse dúfa (Scandinavian duva) — all pointing to a Proto-Germanic *dūbǭ. Traditional etymology connects this word with the Germanic verb “to dive, plunge” and, behind it, a PIE root *dʰewbʰ- “deep”, making the dove “the diving bird” — perhaps from its sudden plummeting flight or its habit of plunging head-first into water. This is the standard account but it is not proven: a rival view holds that the name is simply imitative of the dove’s deep “dū…ū” coo, and some scholars suspect both ideas reinforced each other. Crucially, English must not be traced through Latin: the Romance bird-words (French colombe, pigeon) descend from Latin columba “dove” and from *pīpiō “young chirping bird”, entirely separate lines that also gave English “columbine”, “pigeon” and “pigeonhole”.

2. Old English: from bird-name to holy bird. In the oldest English records the dūfe is simply the bird: glosses render Latin columba and turtur as dūfe and turtle-dūfe. Christianity then loaded the word. The Gospel scene of the Spirit descending “like a dove” at Christ’s baptism, and the Psalmist’s “wings of a dove”, made the dūfe the symbol of the Holy Ghost, of purity and of peace in homilies (Ælfric’s prose homilies at the turn of the 11th century treat the dove of the flood as a type of the Church), in illuminated manuscripts, and in the vocabulary of devotion. The plain bird-word thus left the farmyard carrying the full weight of biblical typology on its back.

  • 3. Middle English: the chaste and the amorous. Middle English keeps both registers. The douve is the Holy Ghost’s bird in religious lyrics and mystery plays; the turtel (turtle dove) becomes the medieval emblem of faithful wedded love — Chaucer’s Parliament of Fowls (c. 1380) sets the “wedded turtil” above the wanton birds and ends on a dove’s note of fidelity. Meanwhile the vocative “dove” slides into love-language: lovers address each other as “my dove” in lyric and letters, and the word acquires its tender second life.
  • 4. Early Modern English: Bible, Shakespeare and the shortened vowel. Tyndale’s Bible and the King James Version (1611) engrave “dove” into the sacred vocabulary — the Spirit “descending like a dove”, Noah’s dove and the olive leaf. Shakespeare uses dove of sweethearts (“so loving to my mother that he might not beteem the winds of heaven visit her face too roughly” is Lear; the dove-endearments are everywhere in the comedies), of innocence, and of the gentle heart, so that by 1600 the word carried the whole chain bird → purity → peace → beloved. Sound meanwhile reshaped it: Old English long ū (dūfe) passed through Middle English douve with a long rounded vowel, but 15th-century spellings dove, doove show an early shortening before /v/ — the same development as in love, glove, above (and shove) — giving modern /dʌv/; the older long pronunciation survives only in dialect verse-rhymes. Separately, from the 19th century, American English formed the past tense “dove” of the verb dive by analogy with drive–drove — a homograph that still confuses: the bird DOVES /dʌvz/ and the diver DOVE /doʊv/ are unrelated words wearing the same spelling.
  • 5. The 19th century: sentiment, science and “dove-grey”. Victorian taste made the dove the bird of chaste affection — valentines, wedding hymns, and the “dove and olive branch” of peace societies. Natural history added its species names (mourning dove, rock dove, stock dove, ring dove, turtle dove); “dove-grey” entered fashion as a soft bluish grey; and Darwin’s demonstration that all domestic pigeon breeds descend from the rock dove made the dove a showpiece of artificial selection in On the Origin of Species (1859).
  • 6. 1962: the political dove. In “In Time of Crisis” (the Saturday Evening Post, November 1962), Stewart Alsop and Charles Bartlett sorted the divided advisers of the Cuban Missile Crisis into “hawks” and “doves”; the Vietnam era then made the pairing standard political vocabulary. The ancient symbol of peace became a faction label: a “dove” is now a policy stance as much as a bird, and the plural “doves” names a party.
  • 7. The later 20th century: icon and brand. Picasso’s 1949 dove lithograph (drawn for the World Peace Congress) fixed the “peace dove” as the century’s emblem; white doves became the ceremonial release of weddings, funerals and Olympic openings; and “Dove” became a soap brand (1957) and a common nickname. The plural DOVES today holds all of this at once — a flock, a species, a peace congress, a missile-crisis faction, a wedding release — one short word with the whole history of gentleness inside it.
