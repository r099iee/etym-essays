hewers (plural noun; the agent noun of the verb to hew). The verb hew means “to cut, chop, fell, or shape with a blade — typically an axe — and, by extension, to strike and bring down a standing tree.” A hewer is therefore, in the most literal sense, a person who hews: one who cuts down trees, splits logs, squares timber, or dresses stone with a bladed tool. The plural hewers designates such workers as a class or group. In the passage at hand the word does not occur alone but in its most famous collocation: “His folk are known for hewers of wood and drawers of water but in truth his father has been a schoolmaster.”

The biblical phrase and its burden. The expression “hewers of wood and drawers of water” is a fixed phrase taken from the King James Version of the Bible, Joshua 9:21 and 9:23, where the Gibeonites, having deceived Israel into a treaty, are condemned to perpetual servitude as “hewers of wood and drawers of water.” It became the scriptural archetype of the lowliest manual labourer — a people reduced to the two most menial tasks, woodcutting and water-carrying. When the passage applies the phrase to the boy's family it does so with deliberate double edge: on one side the collocation is archaic and dignifying, granting the family an Old-Testament pedigree of honest toil; on the other it is a class label, and the sentence confesses that the attempt to escape that class has failed — the schoolmaster “lies in drink,” so the heredity of the axe reclaims him. A hewer, in this context, is a small emblem of inherited station: bodies bound to physical labour, and the irony that the one who tried to leave that station did not.

Polysemy and related senses of the verb. The semantic field of hew branches in several directions. (1) To cut with an axe: the primitive, physical sense — to chop wood. (2) To shape or square timber: to hew a beam is to cut it to a shape, to dress it, the sense preserved in rough-hewn (“cut without finishing,” now also “lacking polish, uncouth”). (3) To fell: to hew down a tree. (4) Figurative adherence: to “hew to the line” means to adhere strictly to a rule or standard, an image drawn from the carpenter squaring timber against a chalk line. (5) To strike or smite: the archaic sense “to deliver a blow,” still alive in battle language (to hew an enemy down). Each of these gives the agent noun a corresponding colour: a coal-hewer is a miner who cuts coal from the seam; a stone-hewer dresses masonry; a timber-hewer squares logs.

Modern and technical register. Today hewer is largely archaic and literary outside the biblical phrase and a few surviving trades. Industrial senses persist in mining (coal-hewer), stonemasonry (stone-hewer), and forestry history, and the word carries a working-class heroism in labour-history writing. In general use, however, to call someone a hewer is deliberately to invoke the Old-Testament class epithet — an emblem of inherited manual station — not to describe an actual modern woodcutter. The word's double inheritance, then, is the semantic bridge: a plain craft-name that the King James Bible froze into a label for the lowest worker, and that then grew industrial and figurative progeny without ever losing its scriptural ring.

  • Primary sense: one who cuts or fells wood or timber with an axe.
  • Secondary sense: one who shapes or dresses timber or stone to a form (hewn beams, rough-hewn masonry).
  • Tertiary sense (biblical/class): the “hewer of wood” as the archetypal menial labourer, bound to servitude — from Joshua 9, “hewers of wood and drawers of water.”
  • Metaphoric drift: “hew to the line” = strict adherence; “rough-hewn” = unpolished, unrefined; “hewn from/out of” = formed or made, as “hewn from stone.”
  • Technical jargon: coal-hewer (mining), stone-hewer (masonry), timber-hewer (squaring logs).
  • Historical bridge: the craft sense of shaping timber (Old English hēawan) persisted for centuries as an everyday word; the 1611 King James Bible froze the plural into a class label, and industrial and figurative senses then grew out of that double inheritance.
