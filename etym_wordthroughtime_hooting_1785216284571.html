- [c. 1200: the bare shout] - Middle English houten first means simply “to shout, call out” — a plain, hollow, carried cry of the human throat, with no colour of mockery yet and no animal echo.
- [mid-15th century: the owl's cry] - the word is first applied to bird cries, above all the owl's deep, wavering call; from a shout it becomes a sound that names a sound, and the 1826 compound hoot owl later sets the hooting owl apart from the screech owl.
- [c. 1600: the public jeer] - “to shout in disapproval or scorn”: to hoot at a bad performer, to hoot a politician from the hustings — the cry becomes the crowd's weapon, derision given an open voice.
- [early 19th century: the trifle] - “not worth a hoot,” “I don't give a hoot”: the cry shrinks to the smallest unit of value, a damn — hooting's sound dwindles into an indifferent shrug.
- [1926: the laugh] - something hilarious is “a hoot”: derision softens into laughter, the crowd's contempt turning into the crowd's amusement, the least hostile descendant of the jeer.
- [Contemporary: the animal jeer] - hooting keeps both strands fused — the owl's hollow tu-whit and the mob's jeer at once, comic and menacing; in Tolkien's trees the goblins hooting and hallooing are sound reduced to a repeated hollow call, a noise with no words in it, only pursuit.
