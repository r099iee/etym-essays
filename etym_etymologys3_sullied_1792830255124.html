The biography of sullied begins, humbly and unmistakably, in a pigsty. Few English words of such dignity have so undignified a cradle: the literary stain on honour descends from the Latin word for a pig, through the French word for the mud the pig wallows in, into English as the verb for dirtying white things. To trace sullied is to watch filth climb the social ladder from farmyard to epic without ever washing off its origin.

The pig and the sty: PIE *su- named the pig across the whole Indo-European world — Latin sus, Greek hŷs, Sanskrit sūkara, Old English sugu, German Sau. Latin derived suile for the pig's enclosure, the sty: a muddy, trampled, stinking place by definition. In the collapse of Latin into Romance, northern French turned suile into souil, meaning the wallow itself — the muddy hollow where boars roll — and built the verb souillier, to plunge into that mire, to soil. The image is intensely physical: white things dragged through black mud. When Middle English borrowed it around 1350 as sulien, it named exactly that — soiling cloth, armour, and skin with mire.

From mud to morals: the great elevation happened in the sixteenth century, when Tudor and Elizabethan writers discovered that honour behaves like linen. If a fair name is a white garment, then scandal is mud, flattery is grease, and bribery is filth — and all of them sully. Shakespeare uses the verb across the plays for stained beds, smeared reputations, and compromised triumphs, fixing the moral sense in the literary language forever. The pulpit followed: divines preached of hands sullied with innocent blood and consciences sullied with secret sin, giving the barnyard verb the gravity of judgment day. By 1700 the physical and moral senses stood as equals, and the moral one was winning.

  • Sound shifts to notice: Latin u before a vowel diphthongised in Old French to ou (suile to souil); English borrowed the ou as short u (sully rhymes with fully); the verb's -y palatalised the stem; the past participle adds regular -ed, pronounced as a separate syllable -id after the y-to-i change.
  • Semantic drift, stage by stage: pig (PIE) → pigsty and wallow (Latin to French) → to plunge in mire (Old French verb) → to soil cloth and skin (Middle English) → to stain honour and reputation (Tudor moral metaphor) → to defile the sacred (pulpit and epic) → the formal literary stain of modern use, with unsullied as its honoured opposite.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer's age soils garments; Shakespeare sullies beds, honours, and triumphs; Milton's devils fear Heaven sullied by their presence; Victorian poets sully snow, veils, and moons under industrial smoke; Tolkien crowns the tradition by defining the Three Rings negatively — great precisely because never sullied — purity proved by absence of stain.

Why the pigsty still matters: every synonym of sullied reaches for the same farmyard physics — besmirched, bemired, mud-slinging, whitewash — because human judgment thinks in laundry. A stain is guilt made visible: it contrasts with what was, spreads beyond its first touch, and resists washing. Sullied outlived its barnyard cousins because it kept both halves of that truth — the physical mark and the moral verdict — in a single grave syllable pair. When Elrond's council says the Three were never sullied, every listener understands at once: no mud, no touch, no guilt, forever white.
