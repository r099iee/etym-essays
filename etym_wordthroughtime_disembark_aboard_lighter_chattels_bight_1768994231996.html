disembark:
- Coined in the logbook (1590s) - English forms disembark from dis- + embark (embark borrowed from French embarquer in the 16th c.), first in naval and shipping records; the whole family is a loanword chain with no Indo-European ancestry
- Four centuries of stability - the meaning has barely shifted: to step out of a boat onto land, formal, agent-focused, and people-centered; it gains disembarkation and disembarkation point, and crosses from sea into aviation
- The bureaucratic register (20th c.) - 'passengers will disembark' becomes the official idiom of ports and planes, keeping its documentary dryness - which is why, of settlers slipping down into a lighter, it gives the scene the gravity of a matter of record

aboard:
- 'On the board' (14th c.) - 'on bord' fuses into 'aboard': to step onto the ship's planked deck; the older, more nautical form of 'on board'
- Any conveyance (19th-20th c.) - 'aboard the train', 'aboard the plane', and the genial office idiom 'welcome aboard' - the maritime core extended to every vehicle

lighter:
- The lightening boat (16th-17th c.) - a lighter is the flat-bottomed barge that 'lightens' a deep-draft ship of part of its load, worked by lightermen at 'lighterage' within harbors
- The archaic harbor trade (19th c. onward) - receding from working use, the word now carries the physicality of low water, ropes, and oars in historical prose

chattels:
- Movable wealth (13th-14th c.) - from Norman French, the same word as 'cattle': the earliest chattels were literally the livestock one drove before one
- Legal precision (17th c. onward) - chattel becomes the juristic term for personal, movable property in 'goods and chattels', and darkens in 'chattel slavery', a person reduced to a movable possession

bight:
- A bend (Old English) - from the native word for a bend, 'bight' names a wide, gentle curve of a coastline
- Rope and chart (nautical use) - 'the bight of a rope' is the loop in a line, and geography keeps the word in the Great Australian Bight and the Bight of Benin - the coastline rendered as a curving line in haze and sand
