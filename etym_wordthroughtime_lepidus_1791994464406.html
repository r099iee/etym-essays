- 3rd–2nd c. BCE — The Latin adjective lepidus flourished in Roman comedy (Plautus, Terence) meaning "pleasant, witty, charming, neat"; concurrently adopted as an honorific cognomen by the patrician gens Aemilia.
- 1st c. BCE — Marcus Aemilius Lepidus rose to consul, Caesar's Master of the Horse, Pontifex Maximus, and triumvir in the Second Triumvirate (43 BCE), before being stripped of military power by Octavian in 36 BCE.
- 1st c. CE–16th c. — Chronicled in classical histories (Appian, Plutarch, Suetonius, Cassius Dio) as a tragic, wavering Roman magnate caught between the towering ambitions of Antony and Octavian.
- 1599–1606 — Immortally caricatured by William Shakespeare in Julius Caesar and Antony and Cleopatra as an errand boy and lightweight, establishing the English literary trope of "a Lepidus" as an overshadowed partner.
- 17th–21st c. — Serves in classical historiography and political theory as the definitive archetype of a factional ally utilized for his resources and legitimacy, then unceremoniously cast aside when no longer needed.
