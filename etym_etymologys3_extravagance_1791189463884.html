Extravagance begins literally as wandering outside. It is a compound of Latin extra meaning outside, beyond (from PIE *eghs out) + vagari meaning to wander, roam (from PIE *wagʰ- to wander, also yielding vague, vagabond, evagation). In Late Latin extravagari meant to wander beyond limits, stray, digress — used of both physical straying and texts or decrees that fell outside the regular corpus (the Extravagantes of canon law were papal decrees wandering outside the standard collection).

Medieval Latin extravagantia (13th-14th c.) meant divergence, irregularity, going beyond rule. When the word entered French as extravagance in the 16th-17th centuries it still meant wildness, irregularity, eccentricity of conduct or language — extravagance de style meant bombast. English borrowed both adjective extravagant (c.1600, originally wandering, irregular, as in extravagant and erring spirit in Hamlet) and noun extravagance (c.1640-1700).

  • 1600s — irregularity: Extravagant opinions, extravagant actions meant straying from norm, immoderate, unreasonable. The noun meant excess generally, not yet specifically money.
  • 1700s — spending beyond bounds: In a commercializing, consumer society, the most salient way to wander beyond bounds was to spend beyond income. By Addison, Pope, and Johnson, extravagance settles as lavish, wasteful expenditure. Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines extravagant as wasteful, prodigal, expensive.
  • 1800s — moralized luxury: Victorian political economy moralizes extravagance as private vice and public danger — national extravagance, household extravagance. Simultaneously, Romantic aesthetics reclaims a positive sense: extravagance of fancy, poetic extravagance meaning rich inventiveness.

Phonetically the word is almost unchanged since Latin: extra- + -vag- + -ance. The stress pattern /ɪkˈstrævəɡəns/ preserves French accent on the second syllable. The spelling -vag- sometimes tempts false link to vague in the sense of unclear, but the true link is to wandering — an extravagant person wanders off the budget path.

Socio-cultural context explains the pejoration. Protestant thrift, classical economics, and bourgeois domestic manuals all needed a vice-word for spending that was not criminal but imprudent. Extravagance filled it, covering dress, funerals, weddings, furniture — ritual expenditures where display exceeds function. Hence your context sentence: from one rationalist point of view funerals seem an unnecessary extravagance — money wandering beyond use into show.

  • Modern split: Today extravagance retains two poles — (1) disapproving: wasteful lavishness (an extravagance we cannot afford), and (2) admiring: imaginative abundance (an extravagance of colour, an operatic extravagance). Context decides whether wandering outside is waste or genius.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare's extravagant and erring spirit; Pope on the extravagance of Grub Street; Austen on the Crawfords' London extravagance; Thackeray's Vanity Fair as a panorama of social extravagance; modern journalism on fiscal extravagance.
  • Debate note: No serious dispute over the extra+vagari compound, but scholars note the canon-law detour (Extravagantes) helped fix the -ance noun in learned use before the financial sense took over in vernacular French and English.

To call a funeral an extravagance is thus, etymologically, to say grief has wandered off the map — beyond necessary expense into symbolic display.
