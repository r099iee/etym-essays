Aphorism begins not as literature but as science — as definition. The Greek verb aphorizein joins apo- off, away + horizein to bound, from horos boundary. To aphorize is literally to mark off by a boundary, to delimit, to define. The noun aphorismos thus first means definition, distinction, and only secondarily the short sentence that embodies a definition. That double sense — to draw a line and to draw it sharply — never leaves the word.

The founding text is the Hippocratic Aphorisms, some 400 sentences compiled around 400 BCE, opening with the most famous aphorism of all: Life is short, art long, opportunity fleeting, experience treacherous, judgment difficult. In Greek medicine an aphorism was a bedside rule: compact enough to memorise, sharp enough to separate right practice from wrong. The form suited an oral, itinerant profession. Latin physicians preserved the collection as Aphorismi, and medieval universities lectured on it alongside Galen. Through the Middle Ages aphorismus in Latin means almost exclusively a medical maxim.

  • Greek philosophy and rhetoric: Parallel to medicine, Presocratics, Isocrates, and later Stoics cultivated gnomai — pointed moral sentences. Aristotle in the Rhetoric analyses the gnome as a statement of general truth useful in oratory. Hellenistic scribes sometimes label such sentences aphorismoi, blurring definition and maxim. The semantic bridge is clear: a good definition, like a good maxim, marks off truth from error in few words.
  • Latin transmission: Latin borrows aphorismus by the 1st century CE for a short, detached sentence. Seneca's letters and Tacitus' epigrams provide models, though Romans more often say sententia. The word survives in monastic medicine and in canon law collections.
  • French mediation: Old and Middle French aphorisme (14th–15th century) still denotes a medical rule. By the 16th century, with Erasmus' Adagia (1500) and the humanist fashion for commonplacing, French moralists extend it to any pithy maxim. The spelling with -ph- betrays learned Greek transmission, not popular sound change.
  • English borrowing 1528: Early English attestations are medical: treatises translate Hippocrates Aphorisms. Within decades, statesmen and divines apply the term to statescraft and theology. Francis Bacon deliberately writes his Novum Organum (1620) in numbered aphorisms to signal break with scholastic system and return to Hippocratic piecemeal observation. Harvey, Boyle, and later Blackstone all use the prestige of the aphoristic method.

Phonetic evolution is conservative because the word travelled by book, not by mouth. Greek ph [pʰ] becomes Latin ph [pʰ], French ph [f], English ph /f/. Stress moves from GreekaphorismÓs to French aphorIsme to English Aphorism /ˈæfərɪzəm/. The -ism suffix, originally Greek -ismos denoting act or result, is reanalysed in English as doctrine or saying, parallel to witticism, criticism.

The great literary flowering comes with the French moralists. La Rochefoucauld's Maximes (1665), Pascal's Pensées (1670, posthumous fragments), Chamfort, Vauvenargues, then Lichtenberg in German, Blake's Proverbs of Hell (1790), and Schopenhauer turn the medical rule into worldly irony. The aphorism becomes the genre of disillusionment: short because truth is short-lived, sharp because society is self-deceiving. By the 19th century English critics use aphorism, maxim, and epigram almost interchangeably for this salon art, though purists reserve aphorism for truth and epigram for wit.

Nietzsche revolutionises the form a second time by making fragmentation philosophical method. Human, All Too Human, Daybreak, The Gay Science, and Beyond Good and Evil proceed in numbered aphorisms of varying length, from one line to several pages. After Nietzsche, to write aphorisms is to refuse system — a stance adopted by Wittgenstein, Adorno, Cioran, and feminist and postcolonial fragmentists. The medical sense recedes; the anti-system sense dominates high culture, while popular culture simultaneously trivialises the aphorism into inspirational quote.
