startled — past tense and past participle of the verb startle: to cause (a person or animal) to move or jump suddenly and involuntarily, as from surprise, alarm, or pain; or, intransitively, to make such a sudden reflexive movement oneself. In the card's context, 'she drew back startled' describes the woman's sharp, involuntary recoil after the drop of blood: her body leaps backward before her mind has decided anything — a physical spasm of alarm registering the sting of the sword-point. The word is built on the verb start ('to leap up, to jump suddenly') with the frequentative suffix -le, and it retains that iterative, reflexive sense: a startle is a jump, a flinch, a bodily betrayal of shock. In this context the word does double work: it marks the moment the duel turns — the attacker's surprise is the defender's opening — and it humanizes the woman for an instant, her ferocity broken by a physiological reflex that no discipline can govern.
  • Sense 1 — transitive: 'the noise startled the cat' — to cause a sudden involuntary jump or start in someone or something.
  • Sense 2 — intransitive: 'she startled at the sound' — to jump, flinch, or start oneself.
  • Sense 3 — extended (mild): to surprise or alarm without motion — 'I was startled to hear the news' — the sense in which the physical jump is metaphorized as any sudden shock to the mind.
  • Sense 4 — past participle adjective: 'a startled look,' 'startled eyes' — describing a person or expression in the act of being suddenly alarmed.
The semantic bridge from body to mind is built into the word's form. 'Startle' is a frequentative of 'start': the -le suffix marks repeated or reflexive action, so a startle is literally a repeated starting — the body's recurrent jump under shock. From the physical jump of a startled horse or deer, the word extended in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to the mind: to be startled is to be suddenly surprised or alarmed, and the involuntary core is kept — a startle is a leap the body makes before the mind decides. In the card's context, the woman 'up from her saddle snatched a bow' — the saddle is the fixed point from which all her mounted action is launched, the rider's seat and seat of command.
  • Sense 1 — riding: the rider's seat on a horse, mule, or other animal; the equipment (tack) by which it is strapped in place.
  • Sense 2 — vehicles: the seat of a bicycle, motorcycle, or tractor; any saddle-shaped seat.
  • Sense 3 — engineering / shape: a saddle joint, a saddle roof, a saddle of a hill — any structure concave between two high points, resembling a saddle.
  • Sense 4 — cuisine: a saddle of lamb or venison — a cut of meat taken from the back.
  • Sense 5 — verb, to burden: 'to saddle someone with a task' — to load a burden or obligation onto a person, from loading a saddle onto an animal.
The figurative senses grow from the riding sense by two bridges: the shape of the seat (concave between high points) gives the engineering and culinary uses, and the act of loading the saddle onto an animal gives the verb 'to saddle (with),' meaning to burden. The idiom 'in the saddle' — in command, in office — extends the rider's seated posture into the image of authority: the person in the saddle is the person in control, and to 'unseat' a rival is to deprive them of power. From the stable to the boardroom, the saddle has carried its core idea for over a thousand years: a seat, and the control that comes with sitting on it.
