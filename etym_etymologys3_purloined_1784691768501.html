The ancestry of purloined reaches back to one of the most productive roots of the Indo-European family, *delh₁gʰ-, "long." The root is securely attested across the daughter languages: Greek dolikhós "long," Sanskrit dīrghá- "long," Avestan darəga- "long." It is one of those roots whose primary reference is spatial extension, and it entered Latin by way of Proto-Italic *dlongos, which became longus, "long; far, distant." The Latin adjective produced a vast family — longitudo "length," longitudo "longitude," longaevus "long-lived," and the adverb longē, "far off, at a distance." The Romance languages kept the adjective alive as French long, Italian lungo, Spanish largo/luengo, and English borrowed learned derivatives such as longitude, longevity, and prolong.

It is the adverb longē, "far off," that matters for our word. In Gallo-Romance, longē yielded the Old French adverb and adjective loign (later loing), "far, distant." Old French then built a new verb by prefixing por- or pur-, the popular form of Latin pro-, "forth, forward, away," to a stem derived from loign: the result was porloignier / purloignier, whose literal sense was "to put far off, to remove to a distance." A secondary sense of "to postpone, to delay" arose naturally, since deferring something is a way of pushing it far away in time. This is a beautiful example of how concrete spatial language becomes abstract temporal language — a theme that runs throughout the history of European vocabulary.

When the Normans carried their insular French, Anglo-Norman, across the Channel, they brought the verb purluigner with them. English took it up in the fourteenth century as purloinen, and for a time it meant simply "to put aside, to remove, to set far away," with no necessary implication of crime. The crucial semantic shift then happened on English soil. In a society where removing someone else's property quietly was the very model of theft, the neutral "to put far away" narrowed by euphemism to "to remove (another's property)," and from there to "to steal," with the specific nuance of stealing secretly and cleverly. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries purloin was a settled literary word for theft, always carrying that hint of abstraction — the image of carrying the thing off to a distance — which is exactly why it suits a thief who works without violence, and why it has never lost its faintly theatrical dignity.

The word's literary life was fixed by Edgar Allan Poe in 1844 with "The Purloined Letter," in which a minister steals a compromising letter from the queen's boudoir, not by hiding it but by leaving it in plain sight. Poe's story made purloined the connoisseur's name for artful theft and gave the word a permanent home in detective fiction. In Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the narrator's comic sentence about a first-class and legendary burglar who would have picked the trolls' pockets, pinched the mutton off the spits, and purloined the beer plays precisely on this register: the sentence climbs from the slang of the streets to the mock-elegant purloined, dignifying the theft of beer with the most refined word available. It is a philologist's joke about levels of style, and it shows how a word's entire history — from PIE "long," through Latin, Old French, and Anglo-Norman, to a narrowed euphemism for theft — can be compressed into a single comic effect.

  • Sound changes: PIE *delh₁gʰ- > Proto-Italic *dlongos > Latin longus shows the loss of the initial cluster and vowel reduction; Latin longē > Old French loign shows the diphthongization and velarization of final consonants typical of Gallo-Romance.
  • Morphology: the Latin adverb suffix -ē and the Old French infinitive suffix -ier are stripped away in English; the past participle suffix -ed (purloin + -ed) marks the word's use as adjective and perfect form.
  • Semantic drift: "long" (space) > "far off" (adverb) > "to put far away" (verb) > "to postpone" > "to remove secretly" > "to steal" > "stolen, especially artfully."
  • Socio-cultural context: the euphemistic narrowing reflects medieval and early modern attitudes in which property crime was punished brutally, so a word for theft that stressed removal rather than aggression was socially convenient.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer's era first records the verb; Shakespeare uses purloin in Hamlet and Othello; Milton uses it in Paradise Lost; Poe's "The Purloined Letter" (1844) and Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) are the two modern pillars of its literary register.
