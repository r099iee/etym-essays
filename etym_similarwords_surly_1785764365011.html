• Churlish: Similar because both label rude, ungracious behaviour and both judge a person’s manners; differs because churlish foregrounds boorish lack of breeding and the refusal of courtesy (with a class-coded history, the churl as rustic commoner), while surly foregrounds dark, resentful ill-humour and an unapproachable, almost threatening manner rather than mere vulgarity — the guard of the card’s sentence is surly in temper, not simply uncouth.
  • Sullen: Similar because both involve gloomy unfriendliness and both describe an unresponsive mood; differs because sullen is typically passive, silent, and withdrawn — sulking and brooding — while surly more often includes active rudeness: gruff speech, curt refusals, an outward edge of hostility aimed at others.
  • Gruff: Similar because both can describe rough, abrupt address; differs because gruff may be only surface roughness — even affectionate (the gruff but kindly uncle) — while surly implies real bad temper or contempt, not a harmless rough voice.
  • Morose: Similar because both are negatively valenced mood words describing an unhappy disposition; differs because morose stresses lasting gloomy bitterness and inward introspection, while surly stresses interactive unfriendliness — how one treats others in a social encounter, as when the guard refuses our greetings.
  • Irascible: Similar because both relate to bad temper; differs because irascible means easily angered, quick to flare up at provocation, while surly describes the already-present, brooding hostility of manner that needs no trigger and may never explode.
  • Uncouth: Similar because both judge a person as socially unpleasant; differs because uncouth means awkward, clumsy, or unrefined — a lack of polish — while surly means deliberately and resentfully hostile, an active refusal of courtesy rather than mere ignorance of it.
  • Testy: Similar because both describe an irritable manner; differs because testy implies impatience and quick irritation at small provocations, while surly implies a settled, sullen hostility of bearing that is present even before anything provokes it.
  • Hostile: Similar because both carry active antagonism; differs because hostile is broader and stronger — open opposition or enmity toward someone — while surly is narrower, a matter of manner and temper, the sullen ill-humour of the guard rather than declared enmity.
