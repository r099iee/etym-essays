• Vest: Similar because in American English it is the same garment; differs because in British English “vest” means an undershirt, and American “vest” also covers casual, sleeveless outer garments that a British tailor would not call a waistcoat.
  • Doublet: Similar because it is a historical close-fitting upper garment; differs because it belongs to the 15th–17th centuries, had sleeves, and was worn as the principal jacket, whereas the waistcoat replaced it as a sleeveless under-layer.
  • Jerkin: Similar because it is a historical sleeveless outer garment; differs because it was heavier, worn over the doublet, and was a working or casual garment rather than part of formal tailoring.
  • Jacket: Similar because both are upper-body garments worn over the shirt; differs because a jacket has sleeves, is worn as the outer layer, and is looser and more varied in use.
  • Cardigan: Similar because it is a buttoned garment worn over a shirt; differs because it is knitted, has sleeves, and is an outer layer rather than a formal under-jacket piece.
  • Gilet: Similar because it is a sleeveless garment; differs because it is typically casual, padded, or quilted, worn as outerwear, and comes from French (gilet is French for waistcoat but has taken a looser English meaning).
  • Bodice: Similar because it covers the upper body; differs because it is part of a woman's dress, often fitted and laced, not a separate male tailoring piece.
  • Cummerbund: Similar because both are connected with men's formal waist area; differs because it is a wide sash worn around the waist over the shirt, not a garment covering the torso.
  • Weskit: Similar because it is the traditional colloquial pronunciation of “waistcoat” itself; differs only in formality and class register — “weskit” sounds rustic or old-fashioned today.
