Pork is a Norman French food-word standing on a very old Indo-European animal-name, and its history is the history of English eating after 1066. The story begins with the reconstructed Indo-European *porḱos, "young pig, piglet," a base attested across the western branches of the family — Italic, Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic — which tells us that the pig was an animal of the Indo-European farm from an early period. From this base Latin took porcus, the ordinary classical word for the pig (in contrast with the older generic sus), and porcus has a richly attested Roman life: it appears in Plautus and in the farm manuals, and in the gastronomic literature of the empire, where pork was one of the most prized meats. The cognates across the family confirm the root's antiquity: Old English fearh "young pig" survives in English farrow, "a litter of pigs"; German Ferkel means "piglet"; and the Baltic and Slavic branches show their own pig-words from the same base. These are sister forms, not ancestors: the English word pork comes to us by a different, southern road — through Latin, French, and the Norman kitchen.

From the Latin farm to the Norman kitchen. Latin porcus passed into the Gallo-Romance vernaculars and emerged as Old French porc, a word that meant both the living animal and its flesh. The decisive historical event came in 1066: with the Norman Conquest, French became the language of the ruling class of England, and the Anglo-Norman kitchen vocabulary flooded into Middle English. The famous English split settled in at this moment — the Germanic words pig, swine, and hog remained the farmers' words for the living animals, while the French-derived names pork, beef (from Old French buef), mutton (mouton), and veal (veau) named the meat on the platter. The contrast is a perfect sociolinguistic mirror of the conquest: the Saxon peasant, who saw the animal alive, kept the animal word; the Norman lord, who ate the dressed meat, supplied the table-word. This two-register system is so systematic that it is taught as a textbook fact of English social history, and it explains why pork has never had a plural in the culinary sense: it arrived as a mass noun of the table, not as a countable name of creatures.

  • Middle English (c. 1300 and after): the word is recorded from about 1300 as pork or porc, meaning pig-flesh served as food, in cookery, butchery, and satire alike. Chaucer uses pork in the Canterbury Tales; the medieval kitchen accounts show pork among the great meats of the household; and the word quickly produced its dish-compounds — pork, pork-pie, pork sausages — establishing the pattern that survives today.
  • Early modern English (16th–17th century): pork is firmly the culinary noun. It appears in the great cookery books (Gervase Markham, Hannah Woolley), in the household accounts of the gentry, and in proverbial and satirical use. Shakespeare plays on the word's fleshly associations; the religious resonance is old and present, since for the observant of the Hebrew and Islamic traditions pork is forbidden flesh, and the English word inherits that cultural and theological weight.
  • The pork pie and the tradition (18th–19th century): the raised pork pie becomes a classic of English cold cookery, above all the Melton Mowbray pie of the East Midlands, protected as a traditional speciality. The phrase in this card — "a traditional pork-pie and a bottle of cider" — belongs to this long, hearty, festive tradition of portable cold fare, and the word pork as the first element of the compound carries the full weight of that tradition: a dish of crust and seasoned meat made for picnics, hunts, and harvests.
  • "Pork barrel" and the American political metaphor (mid-19th century): in the United States the word made its most striking leap. From the image of the household pork barrel — the cask of salted pork kept on hand and doled out — came the political sense: "pork" as government money and favours distributed to local districts to win votes. The idiom "pork-barrel spending" is recorded from the 1860s and 1870s and remains a fixed term of political reproach. The bridge is the concrete image of the shared-out cask: the congressman hands out the public pork to his constituents as the farmer handed out the salt meat to his workers. Both senses now live in the one word, and the example sentences of this card deliberately set the kitchen sense beside the political one.

Modern senses, sound, and form. Modern English keeps pork as a neutral mass noun of the kitchen and as the head of a large family of dish-compounds — pork pie, pork chop, pork belly, pulled pork, roast pork, pork sausage. The sound has been stable for centuries: the Old French porc gave Middle English pork, and the modern pronunciation /pɔːk/ is the direct continuation; the word is a single closed syllable, the shortest possible shape for so old a word. The word carries dietary weight in Jewish, Islamic, and Hindu contexts, where pork is variously forbidden or avoided, and it carries cultural colour in English culinary identity, where the pork pie is as British as the cider with which it is packed. In the sentence preserved in this card, the word performs its most ordinary and most characteristically English task: naming the savoury filling of a portable pie, the food-word that the Norman kitchen left in the language of hampers and afternoon walks.
