- 16th–17th c. verb “to seduce from duty or allegiance” - neutral-military and moral sense; to debauch soldiers meant to entice them to desert, with censure but no necessary sensuality.
- Mid-17th c. noun “a bout of sensual excess” - the Restoration rake era fixes the word to wine, women, and riot; connotation openly censorious yet fascinated.
- 18th–19th c. “habitual dissolution and its episodes” - moralists thunder against debauchery; the noun debauch narrows to a single disgraceful episode, fully pejorative.
- Early 20th c. literary-historical use - “the debauches of the Regency”; the word becomes period costume, used by historians and novelists for colour rather than live condemnation.
- Late 20th c.–present playful bleaching - “a chocolate debauch, a reading debauch”; guilt becomes a joke, though the word still frames pleasure as naughty excess.
