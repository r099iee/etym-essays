The history of affectation is a history of hypocrisy's softer cousin - the studied performance of a self one does not possess. The word travels from the humble act of making, through the eager pursuit of a goal, to the theatrical mimicry of virtue, and it does so by a chain of nearly two thousand years of Latin and French usage before it reaches English in the sixteenth century. The root of doing. The chain begins in the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *dhe- "to put, place, set, make, do" - one of the great "action" roots of the family. It surfaces in Greek tithemi "I set, place" (whence thesis, theme, theke "case"), in Sanskrit dadhati "he places" (whence dhatu "element", dhaman "dwelling"), and in Germanic *donan "to do" (Old English don, English do and deed). In Italic, *dhe- underwent a characteristic sound change: the aspirated stop became f-, so that the Latin verb "to make, do" is facere (perfect feci "I made"), the ancestor of English fact, factory, factor, and manufacture. Doing something to someone. The crucial compound is ad-ficere, later afficere: ad- "to, toward" + facere "to do". Literally it means "to do something to someone", and from this it came to mean "to act upon, influence, affect, move". This is the verb that gives English affect "to influence" - and its noun affect (the emotion) and affection "a feeling toward". The performance sense, however, comes from a different formation. Striving after. Latin built a frequentative verb on afficere: affectare, which means "to do repeatedly, hence to strive after, to aim at, to pursue eagerly, to aspire to". A Roman who "affected" eloquence was one who strove after eloquence - and therein lay the seed of the modern word. The moral psychology of the Latin world noticed that the man who eagerly pursues a quality he does not naturally possess is apt to overdo it: his striving is visible, his imitation exaggerated, his performance strained. Cicero and Quintilian warn against precisely this, the affectatio of style - a straining after ornament that betrays the absence of the genuine thing. The abstract noun affectatio, attested in Imperial Latin, therefore already means both "an eager pursuit" and, by extension, "a studied, artificial manner". Through France to England. Latin affectatio passed into Old and Middle French as affectation, and English borrowed it in the 1540s. In early English it kept both strands: the older, neutral "eager pursuit, inclination" (an affectation of learning = a leaning toward learning, now obsolete), and the newer, critical "studied artificiality". The critical sense won. In the courtly society of Renaissance and Restoration England, where self-presentation was an art form, affectation became a favorite term of satirical mockery: the affected gentleman, the affected lady, the affected style. To be "affected" was to be visibly performing one's refinement - exactly the failure classical rhetoric had condemned fifteen hundred years earlier. Austen and the affectation of candour. The word reaches its most perfect social usage in Jane Austen. In Pride and Prejudice (1813), Chapter 4, Elizabeth Bennet says: "Affectation of candour is common enough - one meets with it everywhere. But to be candid without affectation or design... belongs to you alone." Here affectation is the counterfeit of a moral virtue: pretending to judge people generously, performing universal good opinion, in order to be admired. Austen's genius is to apply the ancient word for stylistic striving to the Regency drawing-room's deadliest social coin - the performance of virtue. The "affectation of candour" is candour as costume: the actress who plays at sweetness is everywhere; the genuinely candid soul, Jane, is rare. The word here carries the full weight of its Latin history: affectation is virtue that is "striven after" rather than possessed. The modern word. In contemporary English, affectation covers the whole range of studied self-presentation: a fake accent, a staged interest in obscure art, an assumed air of indifference, a writer's mannerisms. It is critical but usually mild - it mocks show rather than denouncing deep deceit. The key modern distinction: affectation is the manner; pretension is the claim; hypocrisy is the concealed vice; ostentation is the flaunted wealth. Affectation is the costume of the self, worn to impress. Bullet summary of the trajectory:
  • PIE *dhe- "to put, place, do" - the root of making and doing.
  • Latin facere "to make, do" - the doer's verb (English fact, factory).
  • Latin afficere "to do to, influence" - doing something to someone.
  • Latin affectare "to strive after, aim at" - the eager pursuit.
  • Latin affectatio "a striving after; a studied manner" - Imperial Latin; Cicero and Quintilian condemn stylistic affectation.
  • French affectation - English affectation (1540s) - first neutral "inclination", then critical "artificiality".
  • Austen (1813) - "affectation of candour": virtue performed, candour as costume.
  • Modern - artificial behavior, speech, or taste designed to impress; mild mockery, not moral condemnation.
Affectation is thus the rare word whose history is a lesson in psychology: the more eagerly a quality is pursued, the more visible the pursuit, and the more surely the pursuer is caught in the act of not possessing it. To affect a virtue is to announce its absence.
