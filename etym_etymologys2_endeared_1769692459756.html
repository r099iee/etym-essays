The word endeared is a compound formed in English: the prefix en- + the adjective dear, plus the past-participle suffix -ed. Each root word is traced separately. ROOT 1 — the prefix en- "in, to make": PIE *en "in, into" (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE; source of Greek en, Latin in, Germanic *in) → Proto-Germanic *in "in, into" (reconstructed, c. 1000 BCE) → Old English in / en- "in, into" (attested 8th c. CE), used as a verbal prefix with causative sense "to put into, make" (as in enclose, enlighten) → English en- prefix "to make, to put into" — productive causative prefix in endear (16th c. CE) ROOT 2 — dear "precious, beloved": Proto-Germanic *deurjaz "costly, precious, dear" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE; ultimate PIE origin uncertain — see debate note) → Old English dēore / dīore "dear, precious, costly, loved" (attested 9th–11th c. CE, as in Beowulf; also source of dearth, darling) → Middle English dere / deere "dear, costly, beloved" (attested 12th–15th c. CE) → English dear "loved, cherished; costly" (Modern English) → English endear "to make dear, to win affection for" — en- + dear, formed c. 1580s (attested 16th c. CE); earliest sense also "to make costly" → English endeared "made beloved, made dear" — past participle of endear ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The ultimate origin of Proto-Germanic *deurjaz is uncertain. It has been compared with a possible PIE root *dʰew- "to run, flow" and with Celtic forms, but no widely accepted PIE etymon exists; most dictionaries mark the deep ancestry as unknown or disputed. What is securely attested is the Germanic chain (Proto-Germanic → Old English → Middle English → modern dear) and the English coinage endear (en- + dear) in the late 16th century. The semantic coexistence of "costly" and "beloved" in Old English dēore is historically crucial: it produced both the obsolete economic sense of endear ("to make expensive") and the surviving emotional sense ("to make loved").
