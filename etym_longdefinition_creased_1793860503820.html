creased (adjective & verb participle): Characterized by having one or more visible linear ridges, indentations, grooves, or folds pressed, bent, or crushed into its surface through physical pressure, compression, habitual movement, or aging. Colloquially in British, Australian, and Commonwealth English, it describes a person doubled over with uncontrollable amusement (creased up with laughter) or completely exhausted and depleted of physical stamina.

The semantic versatility of creased spans the physical properties of matter, formal textile craftsmanship, human emotional expression, competitive sport boundaries, and slang metaphor:

  • Textiles, Garments, & Tailoring: In the world of fabrics, creased operates across a dual aesthetic spectrum. On one hand, it describes the unwanted, rumpled wrinkles that plague garments made of linen, cotton, or silk when folded, sat upon, or crammed into luggage (e.g., a rumpled, creased jacket that desperately requires pressing). On the other hand, in formal menswear, a pressed crease (such as the razor-sharp vertical lines running down the center front and back of trouser legs) is a pinnacle of sartorial precision and discipline, achieved through heavy steaming and mechanical pressing.
  • Paper, Parchment, & Cartography: Describes sheets of paper, documents, maps, or banknotes that have been folded or scored. In archival preservation, heavy creasing damages the cellulose fibers of historical manuscripts, leading to structural weakening and micro-tears along the fold line.
  • Physiognomy & Facial Semiotics: Frequently employed in literature to depict faces marked by life experience, deep thought, or joyous temperament. Unlike wrinkled (which can carry connotations of frail decrepitude) or furrowed (which suggests dark brooding or anxiety), creased often conveys warmth, robust vitality, and expressive character—such as laughter lines around the eyes (crow's feet), laugh lines framing the mouth, or a forehead creased in keen curiosity. This is vividly captured in literary portraits such as J.R.R. Tolkien's description of Tom Bombadil, whose face was 'red as a ripe apple, but creased into a hundred wrinkles of laughter'.
  • Sports Terminology (Cricket, Hockey, Lacrosse): In cricket, the crease refers to any of the white boundary lines marked on the pitch (the bowling crease, popping crease, and return crease) that define the legal operational zone for the bowler and the batsman's safe territory. To be creased historically meant being stationed at or confined within this zone. Similarly, in ice hockey and lacrosse, the goal crease is the designated semi-circular painted area in front of the net reserved for the goaltender.
  • Ballistics & Frontier Horsemanship ('Creasing'): In 19th-century North American frontier lore and hunting, creasing was a legendary, delicate marksmanship technique used to capture wild mustangs. A marksman aimed a rifle ball to graze the top of the horse's neck ligament (the crest), temporarily stunning the spinal nerves and dropping the animal without shattering bone or causing permanent injury, allowing it to be haltered before it regained consciousness.
  • Colloquial British Slang (Humor & Exhaustion): In modern British and Commonwealth vernacular, creased (or creased up) functions as a vivid figurative idiom for laughing so violently that one's body physically doubles over at the waist, resembling a pair of trousers folded in half (e.g., that stand-up comedian had the entire room creased). A secondary slang extension means thoroughly worn out, drained, or flattened by fatigue (after a twelve-hour shift, I am absolutely creased).

The underlying conceptual bridge of creased traces back to its surprising etymological parent, the word crest (Latin crista). A crease was originally the prominent ridge formed along the spine of a fold. Over time, the English language shifted attention from the raised ridge itself to the visible line, groove, and indentation left behind on any surface that bends beneath force.
