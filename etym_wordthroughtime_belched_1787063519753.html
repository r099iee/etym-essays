- [c. 1000] 'bealcan' — a plain bodily function recorded in Old English glosses and medical texts; socially neutral, an unremarkable fact of digestion
- [13th–14th c.] 'belchen' — extended to 'discharge violently'; smoke, fire, and guns now belch, and the word gains dramatic, violent force in narrative verse
- [16th–19th c.] Etiquette age: open belching at table becomes taboo; the word itself is branded coarse and vulgar in 'polite' society, while its figurative 'belching fire/smoke' use thrives in epic and journalism
- [19th–20th c.] Industrial century: engines, furnaces, and factory stacks 'belch' smoke and soot — the word becomes a staple of environmental and engineering prose
- [21st c.] Double life continues: comic bodily humour ('he belched loudly') alongside vivid fantasy and news imagery ('the mountain belched fire'); register swings between slapstick and sublime depending on context
