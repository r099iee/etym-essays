Abaft is chiefly a nautical preposition and adverb meaning behind, to the rear of, or toward the stern (the back end of a ship) relative to a stated reference point. To be abaft the mast is to be positioned between the mast and the stern, further back toward the tail of the vessel. As an adverb, to stand abaft is to stand further aft, closer to the stern.

In its strict sea sense, abaft is positional and directional at once. As a preposition it takes an object: abaft the funnel, abaft the beam, abaft the bridge. As an adverb it stands alone: to look abaft, to move abaft. It contrasts carefully with near-neighbours: aft means generally at or toward the stern, astern means behind the ship altogether in the water, after as an adjective means rear (afterdeck), while abaft always means behind something aboard and toward the stern from it.

The source phrase "nestling snugly abaft the shoulder blades" uses abaft playfully outside its normal domain, applying shipboard language to human anatomy. It imagines the body as a vessel facing forward, so that the spot just behind or to the rear of the shoulder blades is abaft them. The effect is faintly comic and literary: a cozy nestling described in salty navigational terms.

  • Nautical preposition - behind and toward the stern from (a shipboard point): the primary technical sense. Example: cargo stowed abaft the mainmast, cabins abaft the engine room. The semantic bridge from Old English general behind is specialization: sailors needed an absolute fore-aft axis independent of observer orientation.
  • Nautical adverb - further aft, toward the stern: to go abaft, to look abaft. Example: "The wind came from abaft the beam." Here no object is named; the meaning is simply rearward.
  • General / archaic - behind, in the rear (obsolete on land): Middle English baft could mean behind a house or person. This sense is now archaic except in deliberate archaism or poetry, having been replaced by behind.
  • Humorous / literary extension - behind (a body part or landmark treated as a ship): as in abaft the shoulders, abaft the ears. The extension works because readers recognize the incongruity of precise sea language applied to soft anatomy; it makes coziness sound like navigation.
  • Adjectival edge - (rare) situated further aft: some manuals speak of an abaft gun or abaft compartment, though after is more standard as an adjective. This shows the word straining toward adjective use without fully achieving it.

Connotation is crisp, traditional, and masculine-nautical: abaft smells of tar, canvas, and orders shouted on deck. Unlike neutral behind, it signals insider knowledge and discipline. Used on land it can sound affected or jocular, deliberately importing that discipline into soft contexts for ironic contrast, exactly as the shoulder-blades phrase does.

In short, abaft means behind something toward the rear, in strict use toward a ship's stern, and by playful extension behind any landmark imagined as facing forward.
