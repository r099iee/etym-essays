wriggle: (1) verb: to twist and turn the body or a part of the body with quick, small, sinuous movements, as a worm or eel does, or as a person does when confined or trying to free himself; (2) verb figurative: to move out of, into, or through a difficulty, obligation, or accusation by cunning, evasive, or sly means (to wriggle out of something); (3) noun: an act or instance of wriggling. Sense in this literary context. In the passage "...he tried to bark and struggle and wriggle in the pocket," the word describes the dog Rover's attempts to move and free himself inside the cramped, confined space of a trouser pocket. The verb sits in a small chain of escalating physical efforts — bark, struggle, wriggle — and the last word captures the special kind of movement possible in a tight space: not a whole-body jump but a twisting, squirming, snake-like contortion of the body against the enclosing fabric. The passage then adds the crucial detail: "Try how he would, he could only move a very little," because the pocket confines him. The word's comical, slightly piteous flavour — a dog doing his best to make progress by serpentine wriggling, and succeeding only a little — is exactly right for the whimsical, toy-like atmosphere of the story. Rover's wriggling is persistent, determined, and only partly effective, and that combination is the emotional heart of the scene. Primary sense — to twist and turn with quick, sinuous movements. At its core, wriggle describes movement made by twisting and turning the body or limbs in quick, small, side-to-side motions. It is the characteristic motion of worms, eels, snakes, and other limbless creatures moving over a surface; of fish held in the hand; of a child squirming on a hard seat; and of anyone trying to move through a tight gap. The movement is typically serpentine — the body travels along a wavy path — and it is usually small in scale, quick, and slightly frantic. Secondary figurative sense — to evade or extricate oneself cunningly. From the 17th century, wriggle acquired a metaphorical life: to "wriggle out of" a difficulty, promise, obligation, or accusation is to escape it by twisting and turning like an eel — by evasion, excuses, or sly manoeuvring rather than direct confrontation. The image is of someone slipping out of a tight grasp or a locked position by contortion, exactly as a caught eel or worm slips free. This sense carries a slightly negative, mocking flavour: a person who wriggles out of a duty is dodging it cleverly but not honourably. Tertiary sense — to make one's way by wriggling. Related to the primary sense, wriggle can mean to move or make progress by wriggling: to wriggle through a gap, to wriggle under a fence, to wriggle free of a trap. The direction can be out of, into, through, or under, and the word always implies a tight, twisting passage. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • To twist and turn the body with quick, sinuous movements (worms, eels, restless people) — primary modern sense.
  • To move or make one's way by such movements ("wriggle through the gap", "wriggle free").
  • Figurative: to escape or extricate oneself from a difficulty, duty, or accusation by cunning or evasive means ("wriggle out of it").
  • Noun: an act or instance of wriggling ("a little wriggle of delight").
  • In phrases: "wriggle room" (informal, from the 20th c.) — the freedom or margin for manoeuvre and flexibility in a decision or negotiation.
  • Of the body in emotion: to wriggle with pleasure, embarrassment, or discomfort — an involuntary, expressive squirming.
  • Historical: to move quickly, turn, or twist (the base verb's oldest, less restricted sense, from Middle English).
The semantic bridge. The word descends from a Germanic base meaning "to turn, twist, or move," reinforced by a frequentative suffix that makes the action repeated and small. The core image is always the same: turning this way and that in quick succession. From the concrete movement of a worm came the concrete movement of a person in a tight space, and from that, by a perfectly natural metaphor, came the idea of evading — twisting one's way out of a grasp, a duty, or an accusation as an eel twists out of a hand. In every sense, wriggling is the movement of something determined to go somewhere it is not quite supposed to go. Register, collocation, and confusables.
  • "The worm wriggled on the hook / wriggle out of the dress" — physical, standard, slightly comic.
  • "Wriggle out of a promise / wriggle out of paying" — figurative, informal-to-mocking.
  • "Wriggle room" — informal, 20th-century, about negotiating margins.
  • Confusables: wiggle (same general twisting but faster, lighter, more playful, and usually with visible side-to-side motion of a whole body or a part), writhe (slower, more violent, often in pain), squirm (uneasy, embarrassed twisting).
In the test sentence wriggle is used in its primary physical sense: Rover, confined in the pocket, tries to free himself by quick serpentine twisting of his body — and the reader is invited to picture the determined, comical contortions of a small dog doing his best in a very tight space.
