The story of “omnibus” begins with an Indo-European root for work and plenty. The reconstructed *h₃ep- meant “to work, to produce, to be abundant,” and its descendants cluster in the Italic branch: Latin ops, “power, resources, wealth,” opus, “work” (our opus, operation), and opulentus, “wealthy” (our opulent). From the same source came Latin cōpia, literally “co-opsia,” abundance (our copious) and — through the medieval habit of calling a transcript an “abundance” of copies — our copy and copyright. The key word for us is omnis, “all, every.” The traditional etymology reads it as *op-ni-, “that is in full supply, that is complete,” the point at which abundance becomes totality. Modern scholars, notably Michiel de Vaan, warn that the chain is not securely established — de Vaan marks omnis as having “no reliable etymology” — but the traditional link to ops remains the standard textbook account, and it is the one followed here. However the root is read, what is certain is that omnis, “all, every,” was a workhorse of Latin, the base of a vast family of compounds — omnipotens “all-powerful,” omnisciens “all-knowing,” omnipraesens “everywhere present” — and the source of the English prefix omni- in words like omnipotent, omniscient, and omnivorous. From omnis Latin formed the ordinary plural omnibus — the dative/ablative plural, meaning literally “for all, to all,” carrying the third-declension plural ending -ibus. For over a thousand years the word was nothing more than an ordinary Latin grammatical form, appearing in phrases like omnibus rebus, “in all things,” and pro omnibus, “for all.” Its leap into the modern world came in early nineteenth-century France. Around 1826–29, public horse-drawn carriages began to run on fixed routes, first in Nantes and then in Paris, carrying any passenger for a small fare, and the entrepreneur Stanislas Baudry is traditionally credited with the name — by one famous anecdote, after a Nantais hatter named Omnès whose shop sign read Omnes Omnibus, “Omnès for all.” However the name arose, the French voiture omnibus, “a carriage for all,” passed into English as omnibus in 1829, the year John Shillibeer introduced the first London omnibus service — a vehicle with a flat fare, a fixed route, and a sign that promised to carry everyone. The word’s career then split in two directions. On the transport side, the English language performed a remarkable act of amputation: by 1832, the clipped form bus was in slang use, and by the twentieth century the full word omnibus was the formal, historical name while bus had become the everyday word for the motor vehicle — and the clipped bus itself gave rise to autobus, and in Latin America to Portuguese (Brazilian) ônibus, a further adaptation of the Latin plural. On the book-trade side, the early twentieth century extended omnibus to publishing: an omnibus volume was a single book reprinting several works previously published separately, and the Modern Library — the American imprint whose fine print appears in this card — issued exactly such omnibuses, bundling titles published separately into one all-inclusive volume. In between, the adjective spread into legislation and broadcasting — an omnibus bill gathered many subjects into one act, an omnibus edition compiled episodes previously aired — and the word settled as the general term for any comprehensive gathering. The socio-cultural dynamics of the word’s career are worth spelling out:
  • The democratic idea: the original omnibus was the first genuinely public transport — a carriage “for all” that anyone with a small fare could board — and the Latin “for all” became a motto of urban modernity and egalitarian mobility.
  • The book-trade economy: the omnibus edition answered the economics of the publisher — gathering separately published titles into one cheaper volume for the mass market — and the word came to signal comprehensiveness and convenience.
  • The clipping habit: the rapid reduction of omnibus to bus shows the pressure of everyday speech on a long Latinism, and the two forms now coexist as formal and informal registers of the same word.
  • The legislative and broadcast extensions: the same all-inclusive logic was applied to bills and programmes, giving the word a second life in law and media after its transport origin.
Literary and cultural attestations mark the stages. The London omnibus of the 1820s appears in the memoirs and sketches of the age — Dickens wrote affectionately of the omnibus as a little democracy on wheels; the Oxford English Dictionary records omnibus from 1829 and bus from 1832; the book-trade omnibus is standard in twentieth-century catalogues and fine print, exactly as in this card’s Modern Library sentence. The word’s biography is thus a study in how a Latin dative plural became, in the space of two centuries, a carriage, a bus, a bill, a broadcast, and a book — every stage carrying the original idea of “for all.” In the sentence on Gibbon, the word performs that whole history in miniature: the Modern Library omnibus gathers the separately published titles of the Decline and Fall into one comprehensive volume, a book that is, in the oldest Latin sense of the word, for all — every reader, every title, everything under one cover.
