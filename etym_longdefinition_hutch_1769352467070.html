hutch (noun) — a wooden cage or pen for keeping small domesticated animals, especially rabbits; also, historically and today, a chest, bin, or piece of furniture for storage. The word is a quiet little cabinet of meanings, all descended from a single root idea: a box. The primary modern sense is the animal enclosure — a "rabbit hutch" is a roofed wooden box with a wire-mesh or slatted front, raised off the ground so that the animal is protected, ventilated, and safe from predators — and that is the sense in the source sentence: "The rabbits were kept in a sturdy wooden hutch in the yard." Primary senses:
  • A box or cage for keeping small domestic animals, especially rabbits, guinea pigs, ferrets, or poultry — "the rabbit hutch," "a hutch for the guinea pigs."
  • A chest, bin, or storage container for grain, flour, bread, or valuables — the original meaning, still alive in older rural usage ("a bread hutch," "a meal hutch").
  • A piece of furniture with storage below and open display shelves above — the "china hutch" or "Welsh hutch," a sideboard or cabinet used to display china, glassware, and ornaments.
Secondary and technical senses — the polysemy in full:
  • Mining: a hutch is a small wagon, tub, or trough used in coal and ore mining to carry or wash the mineral; a "hutch" is also a box used in ore-washing, and the miners' use of the word for a tub or hopper is a direct descendant of the storage-chest sense.
  • Agriculture and milling: historically a hutch was the bin or trough where flour was stored and bread dough set to rise in medieval kitchens and bakeries; in some dialects it is still a storage chest for corn.
  • Fishing and wildlife management: a hutch can be a box or enclosure used for trapping or holding small game and fish alive.
  • Baseball slang (obsolete): in early American baseball slang a "hutch" was the dugout or bench shelter for players — a small covered box, in the same spirit as the rabbit hutch.
  • Verb use: "to hutch" means to store something in a hutch, or to hoard or coop up — "he hutched the grain for winter," "to hutch oneself up" in older dialect meaning to crouch or cower like a creature in its box.
  • Metaphoric extension: by extension a hutch is any cramped, box-like dwelling or enclosure — the word can describe a cramped little room with affectionate or deprecating humour, bridging the box, the hut, and the cage.
Historical semantic bridge. Every sense of the word is a different door into the same wooden box. The medieval hutch was a chest for storing grain, bread, linen, and plate — the secure box of the medieval household. When rural farmers needed a predator-proof pen for breeding rabbits, they adapted the chest, adding a wire front, and the box became a cage. When cabinetmakers built a piece of dining furniture with a chest below and shelves above, they kept the same word, and the box became a sideboard. When miners needed a tub to carry ore and a box to wash it, they used the old chest-word again. The unity of the word is the unity of the box: a hutch is always a wooden container, whether it holds rabbits, flour, china, or coal — and the word has travelled from the pantry to the farmyard to the mine and to the sitting room without ever losing its box-like heart.
