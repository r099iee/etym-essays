Bickering belongs to a small family of English words built on an old Germanic and Romance sound-base meaning “to strike or peck,” and its history shows a word moving from physical violence to verbal noise. The Middle English verb bikkeren appears in the late fourteenth century as a frequentative — the suffix -eren marking repeated or continuous action — formed on a root *bik- or *bikken, “to strike, thrust, or peck,” which has relatives in Middle Dutch bikken (“to hack, peck, cut”) and Middle Low German bicken (“to cut, strike”), and which answers in the Romance world to Old French bequer, “to peck with the beak,” from bec, “beak” (modern French becqueter). The earliest English sense was therefore military and physical: to bicker was to engage in skirmishing, to exchange repeated light, sharp blows in the small encounters of border warfare or archery duels, and the word is so used in fourteenth- and fifteenth-century chronicles and verse, where armies “bicker” across a field before the main battle is joined. The frequentative form itself deserves attention, because it builds the word’s character into its very grammar. A frequentative verb expresses action done again and again, lightly and repeatedly, rather than once and decisively; bikkeren was thus from its birth a word of the repeated small blow, the running skirmish, the desultory exchange — the opposite of a single shattering blow or a pitched battle. That grammatical habit of repetition is the deep source of the word’s modern meaning: bickering is by definition the quarrel that goes on and on in small, snapping exchanges, the dispute that never resolves because it never accumulates into a single decisive argument. The turn from weapons to words happened in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when the constant striking of blows was understood as a metaphor for the exchange of sharp retorts: a quarrel conducted in quick, stinging exchanges was itself a kind of skirmish, and the verb took on its modern sense of squabbling over trifles. At the same period an independent imitative strand of the word grew up to describe quick, light, repeated sounds and movements — a flame flickering, water rippling over pebbles, rain pattering on leaves — senses later taken up by the Romantic and Victorian poets (Southey, Tennyson, Dickens), so that by the nineteenth century bicker carried both “to squabble pettily” and “to move or sound with light, rapid repetition.” The two strands are one: the repeated light striking of blades or beaks, transferred to the repeated light striking of tongues on the one hand and of flame and water on the other. In The Hobbit (1937), Tolkien uses the word at its most precise. Gandalf’s ventriloquised voices keep the trolls “bickering and quarrelling, until the light came and made an end of them.” The word names the whole mechanism of the scene: the trolls’ dispute is petty, repetitive, and incapable of resolution — exactly the frequentative quality of bikkeren — and it is that endless, self-renewing squabble that keeps them arguing in the open until dawn, when the sun turns them to stone. Their bickering is not comic noise but doom: the habit of mind that cannot combine against a common enemy, that always turns on its own companions over trifles, is the trolls’ fatal weakness, and Tolkien makes the word do the work of the whole moral of the scene. The career of bickering thus shows a small word moving through the whole range of English word-formation. It begins as a frequentative of a medieval sound-base shared with Dutch and Low German and reaching toward French bec, “beak”; it moves from the battlefield to the kitchen-table quarrel by metaphor; it sprouts an imitative branch for flickering flame and rippling water; and it settles, in modern English, as the standard word for the petty, noisy, repetitive quarrel — a word whose very grammar (the -eren of repeated action) still carries the memory of skirmishes and peckings from seven centuries ago.
