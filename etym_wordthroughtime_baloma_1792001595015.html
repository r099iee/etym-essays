- Pre-colonial antiquity — Preserved for millennia in oral Kilivila culture across the Massim archipelago as the supreme spiritual entity governing post-mortem existence, the afterlife paradise of Tuma, and matrilineal reincarnation.
- 1916 — Documented and introduced into Western academic nomenclature by Bronisław Malinowski in his landmark monograph Baloma; the Spirits of the Dead in the Trobriand Islands, published in the Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute.
- 1920s–1930s — Sparked intense international debates among social anthropologists, psychoanalysts (Ernest Jones, Sigmund Freud), and Christian theologians regarding "primitive ignorance of paternity" and animistic soul-pluralism.
- 1960s–1980s — Re-evaluated in symbolic and structural anthropology by scholars such as Edmund Leach, Melford Spiro, and Annette Weiner, who demonstrated that the baloma doctrine was a sophisticated ideological charter defending matrilineal clan ownership against male patrilateral encroachment.
- 21st c. — Cites perennially in comparative religion, cognitive anthropology, and ethnopsychology as the classical global paradigm of ancestral procreation and cyclical spirit rebirth.
