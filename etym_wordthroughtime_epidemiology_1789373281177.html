- 1850s - Coined in Victorian Britain during the sanitary revolution with the establishment of the Epidemiological Society of London (1850), initially restricted to investigating virulent acute infectious contagions and atmospheric scourges (such as Asiatic cholera, typhus, and yellow fever) decimating urban working classes.
- Late 19th Century - Gains international prestige and institutional formalization during the dawn of bacteriology; champion doctors like Adrien Proust (father of Marcel Proust) travel across the Mediterranean and Middle East to establish international sanitary cordons, quarantine treaties, and global hygiene standards to halt continental cholera epidemics.
- Mid-20th Century - Undergoes a monumental paradigm shift often termed "the second epidemiological revolution" or the birth of "chronic disease epidemiology"; expanding beyond infectious pathogens to investigate non-communicable lifestyle and environmental conditions, famously validated by the Doll and Hill studies linking cigarette smoking to lung cancer (1950) and the Framingham Heart Study.
- Late 20th Century - Diversifies into an array of specialized subfields including molecular epidemiology, genetic epidemiology, social epidemiology, and pharmacoepidemiology, utilizing advanced multivariate regression models and longitudinal cohort methodologies to disentangle complex gene-environment interactions.
- 21st Century - Thrust into the absolute center of global politics, economics, and media coverage during modern pandemics (SARS, MERS, Ebola, COVID-19); today integrates real-time genomic pathogen sequencing, big-data human mobility tracking, machine learning transmission forecasting, and syndromic surveillance to steer global health policy.
