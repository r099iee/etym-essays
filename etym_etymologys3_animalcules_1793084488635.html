Every syllable of animalcules breathes — literally, for the word begins with breath itself. Its deepest root is PIE *h₂enh₁-, to breathe, the ancient verb behind Greek anemos (wind), Old Irish anál (breath), and the whole Latin family of anima (breath, soul, life-principle) and animus (spirit, mind). To the Romans, breath was life: the anima animated the body exactly as wind animates the world, and from anima came the adjective animalis (having breath, living) and the neuter noun animal — a living being, anything that breathes.

Through Latin literature: animal in classical use covered every living creature including man — Lucretius peoples his universe with animalia, and Pliny catalogues them. The diminutive animalculum, a little animal, existed but modestly: Roman authors used it for small creatures of no great account, with none of the microscopic resonance it would later acquire, since no Roman lens could show what swims in a raindrop.

  • Sound change: the Latin cluster survived intact into English as a learned borrowing — animal (14th c., via Old French) kept its vowels, and animalcule (1590s, direct from Latin) preserved the diminutive suffix -culum anglicised to -cule, as in molecule and minuscule.
  • Semantic drift: from breathing soul to living being to little animal — each step shedding grandeur: anima the immortal soul becomes animal the beast (man distinguishing himself from the rest), becomes animalculum the negligible small fry.
  • Socio-cultural context: medieval schoolmen inherited animal as a category of creation between angels and plants; Renaissance naturalists used animalcule for insects and other small fry without any sense of peering deeper.

The microscope revolution (1670s): Antony van Leeuwenhoek of Delft, a draper with a gift for grinding lenses that magnified two hundred times, turned the bookish diminutive into front-page news. His letters to the Royal Society — on pepper-water teeming with eels, on the white matter of his own teeth alive with darting animalcules, on the spermatic animals of 1677 — were read aloud in London to disbelieving Fellows who confirmed them with English lenses. Within a decade animalcules named a newly discovered universe, and popular lecturers hawked the wonders to paying crowds.

  • Sound change: none in this period — the word was fixed in print, its four syllables pronounced as spelled, the plural alternating between learned animalcula and English animalcules, with the English form winning.
  • Semantic drift: explosive widening — the word stretched to cover bacteria, protozoa, rotifers, spermatozoa, and anything else motile and minute, becoming less a species-name than a cry of astonishment.
  • Literary attestations: the Royal Society's Philosophical Transactions (1670s–1700s), Addison and Pope's playful references, and eventually Emerson's limestone sentence, which assumes readers already know that continents are animalcule-architecture.

Geology and after: the nineteenth century gave animalcules their grandest role when Christian Ehrenberg proved chalk, limestone, and coral rock to be compacted shells of microscopic life — continents as animalcule masonry, the very image the source passage immortalises. Then bacteriology (Pasteur, Koch, 1860s–1880s) dissolved the old grab-bag into precise kingdoms, and the laboratory dropped animalcule for microorganism, bacterium, protist. The word survives where wonder survives: in Emerson, in histories of science, and in every writer who prefers little animals to sterile Latin.
