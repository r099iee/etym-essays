Primary sense. Cowered is the past tense and past participle of the verb to cower: to crouch or bend low to the ground, drawing the body in, under the influence of fear, weakness, or submission. The motion is unmistakable — knees bent, spine bowed, shoulders hunched, head lowered — the body making itself as small as possible, as if to be less visible and less in the strike path of a threat. In the source sentence the dwarves, having just boasted, "cowered down in fright": the word collapses their earlier confidence into a single image of hunched, terrified dwarves pressing low in the dark.

Full range of senses.

  • To crouch in fear (the modern core). Applied to animals and people: a dog "cowers" beneath a table during a thunderstorm, a beaten child "cowers" in a corner, troops "cower" behind walls under shellfire. The word always implies real or extreme fear, and usually a threat that has already made itself felt; it is rarely used of mere anticipation.
  • To crouch in submission. A close variant: the lowering of the body before a stronger power — an abject posture of surrender or appeasement, as of a whipped dog before its master. Here the fear is of punishment, and the posture is an apology made with the body.
  • To shrink, recoil, or draw back. An extension in which the physical crouch is lighter or absent: "she cowered from his anger", "he cowered at the scream" — the sense of cringing away from something terrible. This use shades into "shrink", "quail", and "wince".
  • The older hunting sense (obsolete). The word's original English meaning, from its 13th-century borrowing, was "to crouch, stoop, or lie in wait" — the purposeful, poised crouch of a hunter or predator hiding from its quarry, with no fear at all. This sense is now obsolete, but it is the historical bridge: a crouch is a crouch, and the modern word simply attaches terror to the posture.
  • Figurative and metaphorical. Nations, institutions, and even abstractions may "cower": "the press cowered before the regime", "democracy cowered as the tanks rolled". Here the crouch is wholly metaphorical, meaning to abandon resistance or dignity under pressure.

Register and tone. "Cower" is always pejorative or pitying. It describes fear that degrades — the cowering creature is humiliated by its own posture, small, low, and helpless — so the word often carries a faint contempt or a strong appeal to sympathy. It is stronger than "wince" (a brief twitch), stronger and more sustained than "flinch", more specific than "tremble", and more passive than "grovel", which adds crawling and self-abasement.

Collocations and idiom. The standard patterns are "cower in fright/terror/fear", "cower down", "cower before", "cower from", "cower in a corner", "cowering behind". The participle "cowering" is a stock adjective ("cowering children", "a cowering dog"), and "cowering" is also a noun for the posture itself. Note the spelling: cower + ed, giving cowered; the word is not "coward" (a noun meaning a timid person, which is a different word, from Old French couard) — though the two are near neighbours and are easily confused.

Grammar note. Cower is a regular, usually intransitive verb: cower / cowers / cowered / cowering. "Cowered" functions as past tense ("they cowered down in fright") and past participle ("the dog, cowered and shaking, crept out"), and the participle can stand adjectivally ("cowered forms"). It may be followed by adverbials of direction ("cowered down", "cowered back") and by "before/from/at" naming the source of fear.
