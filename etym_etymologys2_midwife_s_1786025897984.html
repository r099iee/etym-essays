MULTIPLE ROOT WORDS: "MID" + "WIFE" — two independent chains, then the compound.

ROOT 1 OF 2: MID ("with")

PIE *medhi- / *me-
"with, among, in the middle of"
(reconstructed; cf. Greek meta "with, among";
the link to the "middle" root *medhyo- is debated)

↓

Proto-Germanic *midi
"with, together with"
(Gothic miþ, Old Norse með,
Old Saxon mid, Old High German mit)

↓

Old English mid
"with, in company of"
(attested c. 8th–9th c. in glosses and prose)

↓

Middle English mid
"with"
(common until c. 13th–14th c., then displaced
by with; survives mainly inside compounds)

↓

MID (bound combining form)
"with" — frozen as the first element
of midwife (and midland, midriff)


ROOT 2 OF 2: WIFE ("woman")

No secure PIE ancestor
"woman" — Proto-Germanic *wībą
has no certain Indo-European etymology;
older proposals (e.g. *gʯībʰ- "shame") are
speculative — origin to be treated as unknown

↓

Proto-Germanic *wībą
"woman, female person"
(Old Norse víf, Old Saxon wīf,
Old High German wīb, modern German Weib)

↓

Old English wīf
"woman; wife; female person"
(attested from c. 8th c.; Beowulf, the laws;
"married woman" already appears as a narrowing)

↓

Middle English wīf / wif
"woman; wife, married woman"
(the "woman" sense gradually narrows
to "married woman")

↓

Modern English wife
"married woman, spouse"


COMPOUND SYNTHESIS

Middle English midwif / midewif
"midwife — literally the woman who is
with [the mother]"
(mid "with" + wif "woman"; attested 14th c.)

↓

Early Modern English midwife
"a woman who assists at childbirth"
(the wife element keeps its old sense "woman")

↓

MIDWIFE'S
"possessive of midwife; in the idiom 'a midwife's tale',
an unscientific folk belief passed down orally"

Note on debate: the origin of Germanic *wībą is unknown; earlier handbooks derived it from PIE *gʯībʰ- "shame, pudenda", but modern scholarship treats this as speculative, and de Vaan/OED mark it obscure. The connection of the "with" preposition mid to the PIE "middle" root *medhyo- is probable but not universally accepted.
