The story of abstinence begins deep in Proto-Indo-European with the enormously productive root *ten-, meaning to stretch, extend or hold. This root is one of the great workhorses of Indo-European vocabulary, giving Latin tenere (to hold), tendere (to stretch), tenor, tent, thin, and dozens of other words. The crucial Latin development was the prefixing of abs- (away from) to tenere, yielding abstinere: literally to hold oneself away from something. Classical Latin used abstinere for soldiers holding back from plunder, magistrates refraining from judgment, and individuals declining food or drink. The abstract noun abstinentia therefore meant a principled holding-back, a self-imposed distance between desire and act.

In late Republican and Imperial Rome, abstinentia acquired a strong moral and civic charge. Cicero praises the abstinentia of generals who refuse bribes and spare provinces from extortion; it becomes a cardinal virtue of public administration alongside iustitia and continentia. Early Christian writers, translating Greek enkrateia, seized upon abstinentia to describe fasting, chastity and renunciation of the flesh. Jerome, Augustine and Tertullian use abstinentia carnis and abstinentia vini to describe Lenten fasting and clerical continence. Thus by Late Latin the word had already travelled from a neutral act of holding back to a spiritually meritorious discipline of the body.

Old French inherited the word almost unchanged as abstinence by the 12th century, in a culture saturated with monastic fasting rules, Lenten observance and chivalric vows of continence. Anglo-Norman clerics carried it into Middle English in the 14th century, where it appears in Chaucer, Langland and devotional prose with the sense of fasting from meat and drink and refraining from lechery. The Ayenbite of Inwyt and the Ancrene Wisse treat abstinence as one of the remedies for gluttony and lust. At this stage the word is overwhelmingly religious and bodily: it means going without.

  • Phonetic path: PIE *ten- → Latin tenere → abstinere → abstinentia shows regular Latin prefixation and abstract-noun formation in -ntia; Old French preserves the Latin spelling with loss of quantitative vowel distinctions; Middle English borrows the French form wholesale with stress shift to the first syllable and reduction of the final -ence to /əns/.
  • Semantic drift: from physical holding-back (Latin) to civic virtue (Ciceronian self-restraint in office) to ascetic Christian discipline (fasting, chastity) to modern general sense of voluntary non-indulgence in anything (drink, drugs, voting, speech).
  • Socio-cultural context: Roman administrative ethics, early Christian asceticism, medieval monastic rules and Lent, Reformation debates over fasting versus faith, Victorian temperance movements, and modern clinical language of substance-use recovery.
  • Literary attestations: Cicero De Officiis on abstinentia; Vulgate and patristic writings; Chaucer's Parson's Tale listing abstinence among remedies; Shakespeare on abstemious behaviour; 19th-century temperance tracts on total abstinence; modern public-health slogans on abstinence-only education.

The Reformation and early modern period broadened and secularised the word. Protestant moralists retained fasting but emphasised temperance in drink, while Catholic theology kept abstinence days (abstaining from meat on Fridays) as canon law. By the 18th century Samuel Johnson defines abstinence simply as forbearance from any indulgence, and total abstinence becomes the rallying cry of the temperance movement in the 19th century, giving us teetotal abstainers and abstinence pledges. The Wodehouse context — a comic reference to abstinence from indigestion — plays on this long moral history by applying a lofty ascetic term to a trivially physical complaint.

In contemporary English abstinence covers sexual abstinence, alcohol and drug abstinence, fasting and dietary abstinence, and even electoral abstention by metaphorical extension. Public-health discourse speaks of abstinence-based recovery, abstinence-only sex education, and nicotine abstinence, while religious discourse retains Lenten abstinence and abstinence from meat. The word has thus kept its Latin core — holding oneself away — while its objects have multiplied enormously, from food and flesh to ballots, screens and speech. Its prestige remains mildly formal and moralised: one practises abstinence rather than merely going without.
