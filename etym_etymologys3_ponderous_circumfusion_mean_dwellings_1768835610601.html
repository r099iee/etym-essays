PONDEROUS — The story of ponderous begins at the ancient act of stretching and pulling. The PIE root *(s)pen- (c. 4000–3000 BCE) described drawing something out, stretching a fibre, spinning a thread — and from that act of stretching came Latin pendere, whose meaning split into two: 'to hang' (a thing stretched out and suspended) and 'to weigh' (because a balance hangs a weight against a counterweight). Out of pendere grew pondus, 'weight', and from pondus Latin built ponderōsus, 'weighty', an adjective that passed into Old French as pondereus and into Middle English as ponderous in the fifteenth century. In early English it simply meant 'heavy, weighty', and the figurative sense of 'dull, laborious' developed by the physics of experience: anything very heavy is slow, and anything slow and heavy seems solemn or tedious.

Latin's own branch went on to plant a whole family in English: the pendulum that hangs and swings, the dependent who hangs upon another, the spendthrift who weighs out (expendere) his money, and the pensive man who weighs his thoughts — even our verb spend is literally 'to weigh out'. On the Germanic side, the same root of stretching became the spinning of thread: Proto-Germanic *spinnaną, Old English spinnan, our spin, and German spinnen, the stretched fibre of the distaff. No Sanskrit or other non-Italic/Germanic branch of this root is securely attested, and none is forced here: the family is Italic and Germanic from the root downward. In the sentence, ponderous does exactly this double work: it gives Gormenghast the physical gravity of enormous weight and the slow, solemn dignity of a thing that cannot be hurried. The word's career — from hanging to weighing to weighty to slow to dull — is a model of how concrete physical experience (the weight on the balance) becomes the vocabulary of character and style (the ponderous book, the ponderous gait).

Literary register and attestations. In English literary history ponderous has been the favourite adjective of the solemn and the oppressive. Milton applies it to the slow-moving and the vast; the eighteenth-century essayists made 'a ponderous style' a standing criticism of the heavy-handed and the self-important; and the Romantics, recoiling from the ponderous machinery of the preceding age, sharpened the word's hint of dullness. Dickens and the Victorians apply it to furniture and officials alike, and the word retains in modern use its double charge: ponderous prose is heavy in the wrong way, while a ponderous silence is heavy in a serious and dignified one. The adjective is also the exact word for the immovable in gothic and fantasy writing — the castle that cannot hurry, the giant whose tread makes the ground complain — and Gormenghast, the great stone massing of the original building, is one of its most celebrated modern applications. The family the word carries with it is a lexicon of weighing: ponder (to weigh in the mind, from Latin ponderāre, a verb formed directly from pondus), pendulum (the hanging weight), depend (to hang from), expend and spend (to weigh out payment), pensive (weighing one's thoughts), and appendix (the part that hangs on) all descend from the same ancient act of stretching and hanging that produced the ponderous stone.

CIRCUMFUSION — This word is a rare and learned English survival of a very ancient verb of pouring. PIE *gheu- meant 'to pour', and the root poured itself across the Indo-European world with remarkable fidelity. In India it became the sacred verb of sacrifice: Sanskrit juhóti, 'he pours' — the pouring of the oblation into the fire — whence homa and Hindi havan, the fire-sacrifice; the priest's act of pouring clarified butter upward into the flames is the earliest liturgical act to which this root is tied. In Greece it produced khéō, 'I pour', and the khymós 'juice' that English science later borrowed as chyme, the semifluid mass in the stomach. In the Germanic north it yielded Old English ġēotan, 'to pour' — our dialectal yeten — and German gießen, Dutch gieten. In Italy it became Latin fundere, 'to pour', a verb of enormous fertility: fundere gave English fuse, diffuse (to pour apart), confuse (to pour together), profuse (to pour forth), and even the casting of molten metal, our found and foundry.

The compound circumfundere — circum 'around' plus fundere — is attested in Pliny the Elder (first century CE) and describes the pouring of a fluid about a central point, and its noun circumfūsiō, 'a pouring around', was taken up by Late Latin and by Renaissance scholarship. English borrowed circumfusion in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as a dignified, quasi-technical term of learned prose, alchemy, and medicine — the circumfusion of humours, of light, of an aura. Its career in English has always been bookish and rare: it never entered ordinary speech, and that rarity is exactly its power. In Peake's sentence, the circumfusion of mean dwellings 'that swarmed like an epidemic around its outer walls' lets an epidemic of architecture behave as a fluid poured around a fixed point — the castle — and the word's strangeness makes the image of the swarming dwellings stranger still. The semantic bridge is direct: fundere is pouring, circum- is around, and the noun freezes the act into a state — the condition of being surrounded by something that spreads and flows.

Attestations and register. The word entered English through the learned translations of the sixteenth century, where it rendered the classical and alchemical idea of an enveloping medium — the circumfusion of the humours in the body, of the elements in the cosmos, of light in a medium. The seventeenth-century poets, and above all the scientific writers of the Royal Society, prized it for its precision: the circumfusion of a fluid about a body is exactly described, and no plainer word could match it. By the eighteenth century it had become a word of scientific and poetic diction, and in modern English it survives as a rarity — a word that startles precisely because it makes the abstract concrete. When Peake writes of the circumfusion of mean dwellings, he forces the vocabulary of chemistry and light onto the sprawl of the poor, and the word's strangeness is the point: the hovels are not a collection of houses but a fluid, an atmosphere, poured around the castle. It is the same transformation the sentence performs on the whole scene — architecture behaving as liquid, and the stronghold standing at the still centre of the flood. Rare words repay such risks: circumfusion has waited four centuries for a sentence worthy of it, and the swarming epidemic of Gormenghast is that sentence.

MEAN — The adjective mean, in the sense 'poor, shabby, inferior' that this sentence requires, is the degraded descendant of one of the oldest communal words in the Germanic languages. Its ancestor is PIE *mei-, 'to change, to exchange' — the root of Latin mūtāre, 'to change', and of Greek ameíbein, 'to exchange' — and its zero-grade formation *moinos meant 'exchanged, shared'. From *moinos the Germanic tribes built *gamainiz, 'shared by all, common', with the collective prefix *ga-; Gothic gamains shows the word in the fourth century with the meaning 'common, defiled'. Old English gemǣne, 'common, shared by all', descends directly from this, and in the oldest texts it is a neutral, even positive word: what is common belongs to everybody, the community's own.

Then the great social descent begins. In Middle English mene kept the sense 'common, shared', but the social hierarchy of the age was already converting the common into the vulgar: what belongs to everyone belongs to no one in particular, and the shared becomes the ordinary, the ordinary slides into the low, and the low into the contemptible. By the early modern period 'mean' described poor quality and low rank, smallness of scale, the visible signature of low estate — the aristocratic judgment, the exact opposite of noble. It is this fully-degraded sense that Peake deploys: the mean dwellings are the shabby houses of the poor, judged from the castle's height. In the nineteenth century a further American development extended 'mean' from 'low, unprincipled' to 'cruel, unkind', the sense in which a mean person is a petty bully. Throughout, the word has been shadowed by its two homonyms — mean 'to intend' from Old English mǣnan (PIE *men- 'to think') and mean 'average' from Latin medianus 'middle' — which are entirely different words that happen to share the spelling. The history of mean is thus a three-part story of convergence in form and divergence in origin, with the 'common' word carrying a social judgment that its ancestors never intended.

Attestations and register. The degraded sense is everywhere in the literature of social judgment. Shakespeare uses mean freely of low birth, low station, and poor quality — the meanest slave, the meanest house — and the King James Bible gives the word its scriptural weight in the same register: the mean of the earth, the meanest of men. By the nineteenth century the word had hardened into the vocabulary of class: a mean street, a mean lodging, a mean spirit; and the American extension to 'cruel, unkind' completed the moral degradation of the term. In Peake's sentence the mean dwellings are judged from above — the word is the castle's verdict on the swarm — and that aristocratic point of view is built into the word's history: mean is the adjective of the low, seen by the high. No neutral term could carry the sentence's contempt, and none is intended. The word's power lies precisely in its double sense at once: the dwellings are mean in the sense of squalid, and mean in the older sense of common — shared, ordinary, belonging to everybody and to no one, pressed together at the base of the wall.

The sound history is regular: PIE *ei- became Germanic *ai-, Old English ǣ, and Middle English ē, later monophthongised in the modern vowel, so that the old 'shared' word and the old 'intend' word came to be pronounced alike by the fourteenth century — homophones by accident of sound change, not by origin.

DWELLINGS — The word dwelling begins in mist and smoke. PIE *dheu- described clouds and haze — the world becoming dim, obscured, unclear — and its extended form *dhwel- produced the idea of being confused, misled, or benighted: across Indo-European, the words for error and delusion are descendants of the word for clouding. In Sanskrit the root became dhūma, 'smoke', and dhūli, 'dust', surviving in Hindi as dhuan and dhool; in Greek it became thýō, 'to offer sacrifice', because the smoke of the burning incense was the act of worship. In the Germanic north the root took the form *dwaljan, 'to cause to wander, to lead astray, to delay, to deceive'. Old English dwellan meant 'to hinder, to mislead, to cause to linger'; its noun dwola meant 'error, heresy' — the heretic is the one who has been led astray. The Old Norse cognate dvelja meant 'to delay, tarry', and Dutch dwalen means 'to wander, to err' to this day.

Then the crucial reversal occurred. In Middle English, dwellen — which had meant 'to be delayed, to tarry, to linger' — shifted its sense from the temporary to the permanent: if you linger long enough in one place, you are no longer wandering but staying. By the thirteenth century 'to dwell' meant 'to remain, to abide', and by the fourteenth it meant 'to reside, to make one's home'. The dwelling is thus, etymologically, the place where you stopped wandering — the point at which the fog of the journey lifted and delay became settlement. The noun dwelling is the gerund substantivised: first the act of dwelling, then, by metonymy, the place of it. Its register never recovered from its administrative origins: a dwelling is the word of statute, survey, and chronicle — the dwelling-house of the law-courts — and it is precisely that flat, legal neutrality that Peake exploits when the dwellings of Gormenghast's shadow 'by ancient law, were granted this chill intimacy with the stronghold'. In the sentence, the word's buried history of errantry works quietly: these are places where generations have lingered so long that lingering became a fixed, granted, and servile estate, pressed against the walls of the castle. From the smoke of sacrifice to the mean dwelling under the castle wall, the word has travelled from the fog to the hearth, and kept the coldness of both.

Literary attestations and register. The noun dwelling has been the workhorse of official and biblical prose for centuries: the Psalms speak of the dwelling of the Most High, and the statute books of the dwelling-house of the subject. Shakespeare uses it of the body — the soul's frail dwelling-house — and the poets of the heart as the dwelling of love or grief. Its dry neutrality makes it the perfect instrument for what Peake calls 'chill intimacy': a dwelling is granted, permitted, endured; it is not loved. The word also holds a whole family of compounds — dwelling-house, dwelling-place, dwelling — and its gerund form keeps the older sense of lingering alive, so that even 'dwelling on the past' echoes the original meaning of being delayed in one place. The word thus spans the whole history from the smoke-clouded root of wandering and error to the fixed, cold, legal unit of residence, and in a single sentence of Gormenghast it carries the burden of that long descent: the dwellings of the poor, granted their chill intimacy by ancient law, are the places where wandering stopped — and stayed, and was held, and pressed against the stronghold that loomed above them.
