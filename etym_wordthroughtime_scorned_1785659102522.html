- [Middle English (c. 1200s)] - Borrowed from Old French escarnir, "to mock, jeer at, hold in contempt" — an active, mocking contempt born of the medieval court and battlefield, with the violent image of public derision.
- [14th–16th century] - Becomes the word for proud, decisive rejection — to scorn advice, to scorn one's enemies; in the Authorized Version, "scorners of wisdom" are the proud who mock what is good, making the word carry moral weight.
- [17th–19th century] - The participle scorned solidifies as a badge of public humiliation: the "scorned woman," the scorned lover, prophet, or statesman whose dignity has been cut away; it becomes the engine of revenge tragedy and romance.
- [Modern register] - Slightly archaic in casual speech but still powerful in narrative, oratory, and poetry; keeps its Old French image of mocking derision, yet in heroic contexts ("he scorned all offers of compromise") it reads as proud self-respect rather than spite.
