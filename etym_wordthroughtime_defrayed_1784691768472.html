- 14th century - borrowed from Old French "desfrayer" in the language of accounts and contracts, "defray" meant to pay out and discharge the costs of an undertaking — money "broken off" from a purse, spent to settle a stated expense.
- Early Modern English - it stayed a grave, bookkeeping word, appearing in wills, charters, and official correspondence: one pays a bill, but one defrays an expense, in documents.
- Modern English - "defray" remains formal, legal, and commercial, implying an obligation assumed in advance and discharged in full rather than a casual payment — which is why it sounds so solemnly comic in a dwarven contract itemising funeral expenses.
