The term "calico" primarily refers to a type of plain-woven cotton textile, distinguished by its unbleached and often undyed state, giving it a natural, somewhat coarse appearance. Historically, it was one of the earliest cotton fabrics imported into Europe from India, specifically from the city of Calicut (modern-day Kozhikode) on the Malabar Coast. This original form of calico was a simple, inexpensive fabric, often used for items like undergarments, linings, and household purposes due to its durability and affordability. Its natural color ranges from off-white to a light tan, reflecting the raw cotton fibers.

Beyond its initial definition as a plain, unbleached fabric, "calico" also came to denote a printed cotton fabric. As European textile manufacturers sought to compete with Indian imports, they began to produce their own cotton goods, often printing them with colorful patterns. These printed calicoes became immensely popular, leading to a significant shift in fashion and textile production. The printing process involved applying dyes to create intricate designs, often floral or geometric, making the fabric more decorative and suitable for clothing, draperies, and upholstery. This distinction is crucial: while the base fabric is still plain-woven cotton, the addition of printing transforms its aesthetic and market appeal. The vibrant patterns and relative affordability of printed calico made it a staple in homes across various social strata, challenging the dominance of more expensive silks and wools.

A significant secondary meaning of "calico" refers to a specific color pattern, particularly seen in cats and horses. A "calico cat" is characterized by a coat with three distinct colors, typically patches of orange (or red), black, and white. This tri-color pattern is almost exclusively found in female cats due to the genetic mechanism of X-chromosome inactivation, where different X chromosomes are randomly deactivated in different cells, leading to varied pigment expression. Male calico cats are exceedingly rare and usually result from a chromosomal abnormality (XXY syndrome), rendering them sterile. Similarly, a "calico horse" exhibits a mottled or patchy coat with multiple colors, though this term is less common than for cats. The semantic bridge here is the visual resemblance of these mottled animal coats to the variegated, patchy patterns often found on printed calico fabrics.

In a more specialized and somewhat archaic or regional context, "calico" can also refer to a type of firearm, specifically a rifle or carbine. This usage is less common in modern parlance but historically referred to certain models, particularly those produced by the Calico Light Weapons Systems company, known for their helical-feed magazines. This usage is a direct proper noun derivation, rather than a semantic shift based on appearance or material. The company's name itself is likely a reference to the fabric's association with common, everyday utility, or perhaps a more abstract connection to variety or pattern, though this is speculative.

The historical impact of calico cannot be overstated. Its importation into Europe in the 17th and 18th centuries sparked a textile revolution and, controversially, led to protectionist measures like the Calico Acts in Britain (1700 and 1721). These acts aimed to protect the domestic wool and silk industries by banning the import and later the wearing of printed calicoes, inadvertently stimulating the British cotton manufacturing industry to produce its own plain cottons for printing. This period marked a pivotal moment in global trade and industrial development, with calico at its heart. The fabric's versatility meant it could be used for everything from robust sails and tents to delicate dresses and quilts. Its ability to take on vibrant prints made it a canvas for artistic expression and a symbol of changing tastes. Even today, plain calico remains a fundamental fabric in crafts, pattern making, and as a base for various textile projects, valued for its natural feel and adaptability. The term, therefore, encapsulates a rich history of trade, industry, fashion, and even genetics, demonstrating a remarkable semantic journey from a geographical place name to a multifaceted descriptor of material, pattern, and even biological phenomena.
