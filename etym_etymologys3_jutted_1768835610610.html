From a thrown thing to a jutting stone

Every etymology of jut begins with a gesture: a hand opening, a stone leaving it. At the root of the whole lineage stands the Proto-Indo-European verb *h₁yeh₁- (traditionally written *yē-), 'to throw, to cast' — a root whose children scattered across the ancient world.

  • The Greek branch: the root produced Greek ἵημι (híēmi), 'I send forth, I throw,' and from its compound ἀνίημι, 'to let go,' came the noun ἄνεσις, 'a letting go, a relaxing' — still used in English medical prose as anesis, the subsiding of a fever or symptom.
  • The Latin trunk: Latin built iacere, 'to throw, to hurl,' a verb of war and casting. Its noun iaculum, 'a thrown thing,' was the javelin — and from iaculum, through Old French javeline, comes English javelin. Latin then derived the intensive frequentative iactāre, 'to throw again and again, to toss about,' the everyday verb of pitching and bandying.
  • The Romance voyage: iactāre carried the root into the Romance world: Old French jeter, 'to throw, to cast,' became one of the most productive verbs of the language. From jeter French built the pier 'thrown out' into the water — jetée, English jetty — and from Latin iactātiō came the cargo 'thrown overboard' in a storm: Old French getaison, English jettison.
The semantic leap happened on French soil. To 'throw oneself forward' (se jeter en avant) came to describe anything that thrusts itself out from a line, and the participle jeté could mean both 'thrown' and 'jutting.' Middle English borrowed the verb as jetten, 'to stick out, to protrude,' and by about 1560 English jut appears as a variant of jet in exactly this sense of protruding — the spelling with u hardening the word, perhaps under the blunt pressure of project itself. The past form jutted simply fixes the throwing-image in a finished action: the stones of the Outer Wall jutted forward — the throw is over, the stone arrested, the mass holds its lunge.

  • Sound changes: PIE *h₁yeh₁- → Latin iac- (the root in the zero grade with *-k- extension); Latin iactāre → Old French jeter (loss of internal -ct-, fronting of initial *ja- to Old French j-); Old French jeter → Middle English jetten (borrowing with the French infinitive ending); Middle English jetten → jut (the vowel shift and spelling change of c. 1560).
  • Semantic drift: 'to throw' (physical, transitive) → 'to throw oneself forward' (reflexive, pronominal) → 'to protrude, to stick out' (intransitive, of surfaces) → 'a protrusion' (noun). The modern word has lost the transitive throwing entirely and keeps only the intransitive standing-out — with the kinetic fossil of the throw preserved in the sense of abrupt, angular, violent emergence.
  • Literary and cultural career: in the 17th–18th centuries the word was a plain, muscular verb of topographical and architectural description — crags jut, towers jut, cliff-edges jut; in the 19th century the participle jutting became a stock ornament of romantic prose and sailing journals, loved for its abrupt, shelf-like force; in the 20th century the word remained literary and physical, never absorbed into neutral scientific speech. In Mervyn Peake's Gormenghast (the source of the passage) the word is perfectly at home: the wall's stones jut forward as a two-to-three-hundred-foot shelf, painted white, and on it the annual judgement of the carvings takes place — a jut of stone that is also a jut of fate.
