- 16th century (c. 1590s) - Borrowed from French *avenue* (fem. past participle of *avenir* "to arrive") into English as a military and architectural term for an approach, entrance, or access route to a fortress or estate.
- 17th–18th centuries - Expanded in landscape design to describe a long, straight driveway or path lined with rows of trees leading to a country house or monument.
- Late 18th–19th centuries - Adopted into urban planning (especially in the United States and France) to denote wide, prestigious grid thoroughfares; simultaneously developed the figurative sense of a "means of access, opportunity, or path toward an objective."
- 20th–21st centuries - Universal in both literal urban geography (e.g. Fifth Avenue, Madison Avenue as metonyms for wealth and advertising) and idiomatic metaphorical usage ("exploring all avenues of negotiation").
