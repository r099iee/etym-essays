The word dogma is a late Greek noun with a remarkably consequential history, one that shows a single root moving from simple perception to institutional power. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *dek-, whose central meaning is "to take, to accept, to perceive as fitting." The same root produced Latin docēre, "to teach," and through it the whole English family of doctor, doctrine, discipline, and didactic; the underlying idea is of making someone "take in" or "accept" something. In Greek the root yielded the verb dékhomai, "to take, accept, receive," already attested in Homer. From the o-grade form *dok- Greek built dokéō, "to think, to suppose, to seem," whose logic is subtle: to think something is to "take it as so," to accept a proposition into the mind. From dokéō, with the noun-forming suffix -ma (which makes result nouns), came dógma: literally "that which one takes as so," hence "an opinion, a belief." At its earliest attested stage, in the philosophy of the 5th and 4th centuries BCE, dógma was a modest, psychological word. Plato uses it of beliefs and opinions — the things a person holds to be true — and it was entirely personal and private, close to our modern "opinion" or "view." There was nothing authoritarian about it. The great transformation happened in two steps. First, in Hellenistic Greek, the word moved from the private mind to the public decree: the Septuagint and the New Testament use dógma for an edict or ordinance issued by a ruler or assembly. The Gospel of Luke records that Caesar Augustus decreed (dógma) a census of the whole Roman world, and the Acts of the Apostles speaks of the "dogmas" — the decrees — issued by the council at Jerusalem. Here the psychological sense "opinion" is absorbed into the administrative sense "official decision": a dogma becomes what an authority lays down. Second, the Christian church, once it became an institutional power in the 4th century CE and began to define its faith against heresies, seized on this official word. The great ecumenical councils — Nicaea in 325, Constantinople, Ephesus, Chalcedon — produced dógmata in the administrative sense, but the content was theological: doctrines about the nature of Christ, the Trinity, and salvation. So dogma acquired its distinctively religious meaning: a doctrine revealed by God and formally defined by the Church, demanding the assent of the faithful. Church Latin carried the word over unchanged as dogma, and from there it passed into the European vernaculars. English borrowed it in the 1540s, first in theological writing, where it sat alongside doctrine and creed. Once installed in English, dogma spread far beyond the church:
  • Philosophy and science. From the 17th century, the Enlightenment and the scientific revolution treated dogma as the enemy of free inquiry. Kant's dogmatism, in the Critique of Pure Reason, is the fault of asserting principles without first examining the powers of reason itself. The "central dogma" of molecular biology — Crick's 1958 statement that information flows from DNA to RNA to protein — is a deliberate, slightly ironic appropriation of the religious word for an assumption then treated as beyond challenge.
  • Politics and ideology. Political parties and revolutionary movements produced their own dogmas — party dogma, Marxist dogma — and the word became a general term of abuse for any belief imposed rather than argued.
  • Ordinary usage. To call a person dogmatic is to say they assert their views as if they were church doctrine, refusing evidence and argument. This is exactly the charge the Gibbon passage disclaims: his history is praised precisely because it contains so small a dogmatic ingredient, because it argues rather than decrees.
The socio-cultural arc of the word is thus a widening of authority: from personal opinion (Greek philosophy) to political decree (Hellenistic administration and the Bible) to church doctrine (the councils) to a general term for unexamined authority in science, politics, and daily life (modern English). Its register has steadily soured: few people today call their own views dogmas; they call other people's views dogmas. The Gibbon sentence sits at the modern end of this arc, using dogmatic and doctrinaire as unambiguous criticisms, and celebrating their absence.
