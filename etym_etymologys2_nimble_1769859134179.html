PIE *nem- "to distribute, allot, take, grasp, take hold of" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; also the source of Greek nemein "to distribute" → nomos "law, custom", nemesis "distribution of justice"; and Sanskrit nam- "to bow" → namas "reverence", namaste "homage to you") → Proto-Germanic *nemaną "to take, seize, accept, lay hold of" (reconstructed; cf. Old English niman, Old Norse nema, Gothic niman, Old High German neman → modern German nehmen "to take", Dutch nemen) → Proto-Germanic *numalaz / *numolaz "quick at taking, apt to grasp, receptive" (reconstructed adjective formed on the past participle *numenaz "taken, seized") → Old English numol / nymel "quick to catch, receptive, grasping, holding fast" (attested, pre-11th c. CE) → Middle English nymel / nimel "quick, agile, active, swift to seize" (attested 14th c. CE) → Early Modern English nimble "quick and light in movement; agile, quick-witted" — with an intrusive, unetymological -b- inserted after m (16th c. CE) → nimble ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The insertion of the -b- in nimble is unexplained in the historical record; it appears in the 16th century and is usually read as an ease-of-articulation or dissimilation change (cf. the parallel -b- in thimble, from Old English þȳmel, and in crumble, humble), possibly reinforced by association with words like nimble-fingered nim... nim. The intermediate Proto-Germanic adjective *numalaz / *numol is reconstructed on the strength of the attested Old English numol and its Germanic cognates; the exact prehistoric form is inferred rather than directly attested. The connection to numb (Old English numen, past participle of niman, "taken, seized" → "benumbed") is secure and shows the passive side of the same root.
