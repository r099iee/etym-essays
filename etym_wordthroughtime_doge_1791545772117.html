- 16th c. — Introduced into English travel narratives (such as William Thomas's The Historie of Italie) and diplomatic dispatches; perceived as an exotic, opulent symbol of Venetian republican statecraft and Mediterranean maritime dominion.
- Late 16th – 17th c. — Familiarized on the London stage through Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice and Othello); viewed as a grave, impartial civic magistrate balancing strict law against commercial necessity.
- 18th c. — Viewed by Grand Tour travelers as a magnificent, antiquated relic of ceremonial splendor, famously depicted in the luminous canal paintings of Canaletto and Guardi during the twilight of the Venetian Republic.
- 19th c. — Romanticized in English literature (Lord Byron's Marino Faliero, John Ruskin's The Stones of Venice) as a tragic, poetic symbol of fallen republican majesty following Napoleon's abolition of the office in 1797.
- 20th c. — Primarily restricted to historical, architectural, and art-historical contexts, closely identified with the Gothic architecture of the Doge's Palace (Palazzo Ducale) on St. Mark's Square.
- 2010s–2020s — Underwent a dramatic, dual-track semantic explosion in global digital culture; homographically popularized as the ubiquitous Shiba Inu "Doge" Internet meme and the major cryptocurrency Dogecoin, existing in surreal juxtaposition with its six-century-old Venetian heritage.
