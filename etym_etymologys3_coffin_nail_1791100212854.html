COFFIN — from basket to burial box. The first element begins in Ancient Greek kophinos, a wicker basket or hamper for figs, dung, or baggage. Latin borrowed it as cophinus, still a basket. In Late Latin and early Romance the sense widened to any large chest or coffer for valuables. Old French cofin by the twelfth century meant a small chest, jewel box, or case, and by extension the chest in which a corpse is laid. Middle English borrowed cofin around 1300 in both senses: Chaucer can use coffin for a pie-crust case and for a burial chest. Over the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries English narrowed it: coffer kept the generic box sense while coffin specialised to the burial box, aided by funeral practice and church vocabulary. By Shakespeare and the King James Bible the funerary sense dominates, and the basket origin is forgotten by ordinary speakers.

  • Greek to Latin: borrowing of a farm word into Roman household vocabulary without sound violence, kophinos to cophinus.
  • Latin to Old French: semantic widening from basket to lockable chest, reflecting furniture change from wicker to wood.
  • Old French to Middle English: post-Conquest borrowing with double meaning, pastry coffin versus corpse coffin.
  • Early Modern narrowing: coffer versus coffin split, funerary specialisation complete by c. 1600.
  • Literary fixing: Shakespeare, Donne, and the funeral liturgy entrench coffin as solemn and final.

The compound coffin-nail itself is a transparent Modern English compound of the nineteenth century, needing no sound change: coffin plus nail joined by hyphen. Its novelty is semantic, not phonetic. Dickens in A Christmas Carol (1843) exploits the literal sense for comic logic: if Marley is dead as a door-nail, a coffin-nail, belonging to the coffin trade itself, must be deader. That joke presupposes readers know both compounds. Within decades the same compound jumps to tobacco slang. The OED traces coffin-nail for cigarette to the 1880s–1890s, paralleling coffin-varnish for bad whisky: Victorian gallows humour naming vices after undertakers' supplies. Twentieth-century anti-tobacco campaigns literalised the joke with images of cigarettes as nails, cementing it for non-smokers. Thus the first element travelled from Greek baskets to English graves in two millennia, then in fifty years acquired a smoky second life.

NAIL — from claw to spike. The second element descends from PIE *h3nogh-, meaning nail, claw, or hoof, a root naming hard keratinous tips. The same root yields Greek onyx (claw, nail, onyx stone), Latin unguis (fingernail), and Sanskrit nakha. In Germanic the form became Proto-Germanic *naglaz, preserving both senses: the nail on the finger and the iron nail, linked by shape — long, hard, pointed. Old English nægel meant both, as in Beowulf-era carpentry and body vocabulary. Old Norse nagl, Old High German nagal, and Gothic cognates confirm the common inheritance. The phonetic path is regular: PIE palatal and laryngeal developments to Germanic g, vowel stability through Old and Middle English, final -el to -ail spelling after the Great Vowel Shift without major consonant change.

  • PIE *h3nogh-: keratin tip, claw and hoof, extended by analogy to metal spikes.
  • Proto-Germanic *naglaz: unified finger-nail and iron-nail polysemy, everyday carpentry and body term.
  • Old English nægel: attested in glosses and laws for both anatomy and building.
  • Middle English nail: spelling simplified, senses stable, compounds proliferate: door-nail, head-nail, coffin-nail.
  • Modern technical split: anatomy keeps nail while hardware diversifies into brads, spikes, tacks, and screws, leaving nail as generic.

When Victorian English joined coffin to nail, it revived the ancient Germanic hardware sense in a funerary frame, then immediately lent that frame to cigarettes by visual metaphor. No borrowing or sound law is involved at the compound stage, only cultural history: industrial coffin-making, cheap cigarettes, and a moralising press. Debated nuance: some dictionaries derive the cigarette sense purely from shape and death-joke, others stress American frontier slang where coffin-nail competed with grave-dust and death-mist; both agree the mechanism is jocular metaphor rather than regular semantic drift, so the etymology is secure but the exact first coiner is unknown, as is normal for slang.

For coffin specifically, the Early Modern funeral trade fixed the modern form. Joiners offered coffins in elm, oak, and lead, with furniture of nails, handles, and breastplates priced by the dozen. Trade cards list coffin-nails by length and blacking, alongside shroud pins and burial gloves. Sermons and elegies from Donne to Blair made the coffin a pulpit commonplace, so that by Dickens every reader pictured the nailed lid without description. The hyphenated compound thus carried workshop specificity and pulpit solemnity at once, letting one word do commercial and eschatological work. Anti-smoking moralists after 1880 simply seized the eschatological half: if life is a coffin being built, tobacco is the nail-bag.

For nail specifically, industrial history matters as much as phonology. Hand-forged nails gave way after 1790 to cut nails and after 1860 to wire nails, cheapening coffin-nails and cigarettes together: both became mass-produced sticks, one of steel wire and one of paper and tobacco. Slang loves such parallel cheapening, and the American West, where coffin-nails held together pine boxes on the frontier, supplied the first recorded cigarette senses in mining camps and barracks. British soldiers brought the joke home with Woodbines. Lexicographers from Farmer and Henley to Partridge record coffin-nail alongside coffin-dodger and coffin-varnish as a coherent undertaker slang for vice, confirming that the second element nail kept its full hardware tang even while naming smoke.
