atavistic describes a throwback: the reappearance in a person, behaviour, custom, or institution of traits characteristic of remote ancestors rather than of the present stage of development. To call worry about Greek rhetorical terms atavistic is to call it primitive, backward-looking, a survival from an earlier evolutionary or cultural stage — an indulgence out of step with a communications revolution. The word always looks backward; it diagnoses the present as haunted by the past.

In biology and anthropology, its home disciplines, atavistic means exhibiting atavism: the reappearance of an ancestral character after generations of absence. Classic textbook examples include human infants born with vestigial tails, horses with extra toes, or plants reverting to wild-type flowers. The mechanism — dormant genes re-expressed, developmental pathways reactivated — matters less to general usage than the image: evolution running briefly in reverse, the remote past surfacing uninvited in the modern body.

  • Biological sense: pertaining to atavism; showing a trait of a distant ancestor. Atavistic musculature, atavistic throwback. Neutral and technical.
  • Psychological / behavioural sense: reverting to primitive impulses — violence, tribalism, superstition. Atavistic fears, atavistic rage, atavistic loyalties. Here the word is pejorative: reason has been overrun by the old brain.
  • Cultural / institutional sense (this card): clinging to outmoded forms, rituals, or vocabularies. Atavistic snobbery, atavistic ritual, atavistic grammar-worship. The charge is not violence but irrelevance: fussing over rhetorical nomenclature while the world reinvents communication feels like ancestor-worship.
  • Literary sense: evoking a primitive, mythic past. Writers speak of atavistic memories, atavistic landscapes — moors, jungles, deserts that awaken something pre-civilised in the visitor. Here the connotation can be romantic rather than critical.

Connotation depends entirely on the speaker's attitude to the past. For the Darwinian anthropologist, atavistic is descriptive; for the moderniser, it is dismissive (backward, outmoded, regressive); for the poet, it can be reverent (primordial, elemental, rooted). On this card the author uses it self-deprecatingly and ironically: it seems almost atavistic to worry about... — conceding that such worry looks old-fashioned while half-defending it. The almost is doing argumentative work, softening the self-accusation.

Collocation and grammar follow suit. Atavistic is an adjective only; the noun is atavism, the adverb atavistically. Typical partners are atavistic urge, fear, instinct, impulse, return, revival, throwback, trait, tendency. It collocates with verbs of surfacing: awaken, stir, evoke, revert to, betray. Register is educated and formal — at home in essays, reviews, and science writing, rare in conversation, where primitive, throwback, or old-fashioned do the work instead.

  • Atavistic vs primitive: primitive describes an early stage itself; atavistic describes its reappearance much later. A Stone Age tool is primitive; a modern cult using one is atavistic.
  • Atavistic vs retrograde / regressive: those imply backward movement; atavistic implies backward survival or resurgence, often involuntary.
  • Atavistic vs nostalgic: nostalgia loves the past consciously; atavism resurrects it unconsciously, bodily, or institutionally.

In short, atavistic is the learned word for the past that will not stay past — whether as gene, impulse, or, as here, a stubborn love of old rhetorical patterns in a new media age.
