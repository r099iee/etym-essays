Enumerate means to name, mention, or count things one by one — to run through a set of items individually, giving each its own place in the list. The verb carries the full weight of its Latin ancestry: e- (ex-) "out" + numerus "number," hence literally "to number out," to call the items out of the count one by one. In the passage from the novel — "besides other furnishings which it would be superfluous to enumerate" — the word is deployed with deliberate, urbane irony: the narrator has been cataloguing a room's opulent furnishings and now draws the line, not because the list is complete, but because the abundance is so vast that continuing would be tedious, even a breach of taste. The word's formal, faintly bookish cadence performs the very gesture it names — it closes the list courteously — and the decision to stop enumerating becomes a quiet mark of the speaker's own worldliness: the room is so richly furnished that listing everything would itself be superfluous.

  • Core sense — to list one by one. To enumerate is to mention each item of a set individually and in order: to enumerate the reasons for a decision, the risks of a venture, the clauses of a contract, the stars of a constellation. Unlike "count," which stresses reaching a total, "enumerate" stresses the act of naming each member of the series; the two may coincide but the emphasis differs.
  • Administrative and official sense. In bureaucratic and statistical language, to enumerate is to count systematically and completely — a census enumerates a population, an inventory enumerates goods, a survey enumerates households. Here the word names the official act of complete, item-by-item counting, the foundation of modern administration and statistics.
  • Legal and formal sense. In law and formal documents, enumerated rights, powers, or offences are those explicitly listed one by one in a statute or charter, in contrast to implied or residual ones. "The enumerated powers of Congress" are those expressly named in the Constitution — a technical, semi-legal sense that presumes exhaustiveness.
  • Rhetorical sense — the enumeratio. In classical rhetoric, enumeratio (Latin) is the figure that piles up items one by one, often for accumulation or emphasis; in English, "enumeration" names this technique. The narrator's "superfluous to enumerate" inverts the figure: the enumeration is declined precisely to signal that it could be endless.
  • Figurative and ironic sense. As in the passage, enumerate is used with deliberate over-formality to make the point that an abundance defies listing. "It would be superfluous to enumerate" is the urbane speaker's way of saying "there are too many to mention" while proving, by its very courtesy, that the speaker is above such bookkeeping.
  • Register. The word is formal and slightly elevated — the register of law, scholarship, official records, and careful prose. It is chosen where plain "list" would be too flat, "count" too numerical, "mention" too casual.

Word-boundary discriminations. Against "count," enumerate emphasizes naming each item rather than reaching a total. Against "list," it adds a hint of systematic completeness and formal occasion. Against "itemize," which is commercial and bill-oriented, enumerate is broader and more learned. Against "detail," which gives full particulars of each thing, enumerate is satisfied with naming each thing once. Against "recite" and "recount," which imply telling a connected story or listing from memory, enumerate is neutral and systematic. The word is the vocabulary of the ledger, the census, and the catalogue — which is why, in the passage, refusing to enumerate is a witty refusal to behave like a clerk.
