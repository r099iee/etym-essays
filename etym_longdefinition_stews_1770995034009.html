stews (plural noun, almost always 'the stews') denotes, in historical usage, a place of prostitution — a brothel or, collectively, the brothel district of a city — and, more broadly, any place of ill repute. The singular stew originally meant a heated room or a public bath-house, and the connection is direct: in medieval European cities, bath-houses — where heat, steam, and unclothed bathers came together — became notorious as cover for prostitution, so 'the stews' became the standard euphemism for a city's brothel quarter.

Contextual reading: 'The delicate maid has fallen into a life of shame in the stews of the old city.' The word names the underworld of the brothel district — the nadir of the maiden's descent from delicacy and innocence into ruin. Its steam, heat, and squalor make it the perfect antonym of her former 'delicacy.'

Core senses:

  • Primary historical sense — brothel / brothel district: 'the stews' = the houses of prostitution of a city, especially the licensed bath-house brothels of medieval London's Southwark.
  • Extended sense — place of ill repute generally: loosely, any squalid or disreputable quarter, establishment, or den of vice.
  • Original literal sense — heated bath-house: the singular 'stew' meant a hot-air or vapour bath, a sweating room, or a public bath-house (from Old French estuve); this is the sense from which the brothel meaning grew.
  • Culinary sense (same stem, separate development): the verb to stew (to simmer slowly in liquid) and the noun stew (the slow-cooked dish) come from the same French root via the idea of slow, moist heat in an enclosed vessel — but they are a parallel branch, not a metaphor of the brothel sense.
  • Figurative verb sense: 'to stew' = to fret, worry, or brood (19th c.), literally 'to be cooked in one's own heat or emotion'; 'to stew in one's own juices' and 'to let someone stew' are the fixed phrases.
  • Historical fishing sense: in older English, 'a stew' also meant a fish-pond (from Old French estui 'enclosure, fish-pond') — a homonymic near-twin that adds to the word's tangled history but is unrelated to the bath-house and brothel senses.

Semantic bridge:

  • Heat and shame: bath-houses offered privacy, warmth, and steaming water; when they doubled as brothels, the association of steam, sweat, and bodily heat with illicit pleasure hardened into a permanent metaphor — 'the stews' sounds like a place where people are 'boiled' or 'stewed' in vice.
  • The plural is the key: the plural 'stews' came to mean 'the collective houses of prostitution,' then the district itself; the singular 'stew' retained the bath-house and cooking senses.

Register and collocations:

  • Almost exclusively historical, archaic, or deliberately literary today; collocations include 'the stews of Southwark,' 'the city's stews,' 'to fall into the stews.'
  • The culinary noun 'stew' and the verb 'to stew' are now the living words; the brothel sense survives in historical fiction, early-modern drama, and etymological discussion.
