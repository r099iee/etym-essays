antiquity denotes both a vast span of ancient time and a tangible survivor of that time. In its uncountable sense it means the distant past before the Middle Ages, especially the classical Greco-Roman world; in its countable sense it means an ancient object, work of art, or monument. The word therefore bridges history and material culture: it names the age and what the age left behind.

In Walter Pater's sentence — the revival of classical antiquity — the first, uncountable sense is active: the whole body of ancient literature, art, philosophy, and civic ideals rediscovered in the fifteenth century. Pater deliberately extends the word beyond mere oldness to a complex civilisation taken as a model for renewal.

  • Ancient times in general (uncountable): the remote past, especially before c. 500 CE. Since antiquity, olive oil has been traded across the Mediterranean. The semantic bridge is straightforward: that which is antique belongs to antiquity as a period.
  • Classical antiquity (proper, capitalised): specifically ancient Greece and Rome. Universities still have departments of Classics devoted to Classical Antiquity. This narrowing reflects Renaissance humanism, which privileged Greece and Rome above other ancient cultures.
  • An ancient object or monument (countable, usually plural antiquities): a statue, coin, vase, inscription, or building surviving from ancient times. The museum's Egyptian antiquities draw large crowds. The bridge is metonymy: the age gives its name to things left by the age.
  • Old age or great age (archaic/literary): the quality of being very old. A castle of great antiquity. This preserves the Latin antiquitas sense of oldness, still heard in phrases like of high antiquity.
  • The people of ancient times (collective, rare): the ancients themselves. Antiquity believed the earth the centre of the universe. Here the period stands for its inhabitants, a common personification in historiography.

Domain usages: historians contrast antiquity with the Middle Ages and modernity; archaeologists catalogue antiquities under strict provenance and antiquities law; art dealers distinguish antiquities (ancient, generally pre-medieval) from antiques (typically 100+ years old but not necessarily ancient); theologians speak of Christian antiquity for the patristic era; legal language protects national antiquities.

Literary nuance: writers use antiquity to confer dignity, authority, or remoteness. To call something hallowed by antiquity is to claim continuity and prestige; to call an idea a relic of antiquity can praise its endurance or dismiss it as obsolete. Pater's use belongs to the first mode: antiquity as a living spring of sweetness and light to be recovered, not a dead curiosity.

Grammar note: uncountable for the period (in antiquity, from antiquity); countable, usually plural, for objects (an antiquity, priceless antiquities). Adjectival form is antique or ancient; the study of antiquities is antiquarianism, its student an antiquary or antiquarian.
