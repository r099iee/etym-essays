Thy shares its ancestry with thou and thee: all three descend from the Proto-Indo-European second-person singular pronoun *túh₂, and the possessive is built on the same ancient stem. But while the nominative and objective survived nearly everywhere, the possessive took a distinct route — a separate stem and a separate suffix — and then underwent a purely phonetic split in Middle English that produced the modern thy/thine pair. Building the possessive: the *-no- adjective. The possessive of the second-person singular was formed, in several branches of Indo-European, by taking the genitive stem of the pronoun and adding an adjectival suffix. The PIE stem is reconstructed as *tew- / *tey- ("of thee"), and with the *-no- suffix it produced a form that appears as Proto-Germanic *þīnaz, "thine." The Germanic family is remarkably uniform here: Gothic þeins, Old Norse þinn (modern Icelandic þinn, Danish din), Old Saxon thīn, and Old High German dīn (modern German dein, "your") all continue the same *-no- adjective. Old English þīn came down this same line, serving both as the possessive adjective ("þīn fæder," thy father) and as the possessive pronoun ("þæt is þīn," that is thine). The phonetic split: thy versus thine. In Middle English the form underwent a change that is exactly parallel to my/mine. In the weak, pre-consonantal position the final -n dropped, so mīn became mī (my) and þīn became þī (thy); before vowels, before silent h, and in free-standing position the -n was retained (mine, thine). Thus "thy father" but "thine eyes," "thine honour," "it is thine." The King James Bible and the English Book of Common Prayer printed both forms by precisely this rule, and the pattern survives intact in modern my/mine. What had been one word now stood in two phonological shapes, but never with two meanings. Cousins beyond Germanic. The same possessive idea was built differently in the other branches, all from the *tu- pronoun:
  • Latin built tuus ("thy") with a possessive *-wos suffix — an archaic form tovos is attested — giving the Romance possessives French ton/ta/tes, Italian tuo, and Spanish tu.
  • Greek built sós from *twos, so that "thy" in Greek is sós.
  • Indo-Iranian produced Sanskrit táva, the genitive of tvám, which lives on in the modern languages of the subcontinent: Hindi terā (तेरा) and Odia tora (ତୋର) descend from an extended Sanskrit *tavaka-, the everyday "thy/your" of ordinary speech.
  • Slavic built Old Church Slavonic tvojь (modern Russian tvoj, Polish twój), and Celtic shows Old Irish do/to (modern Irish do, Welsh dy).
The social career of the word. The meaning of thy — "of thee" — never changed at all; what changed was its place in society. In Old and Middle English it was the ordinary singular possessive. As the you-forms captured the polite register in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, thy retreated into the pronouns of intimacy, devotion, and address to God. In the King James Bible and the Prayer Book it became the voice of prayer ("thy kingdom come, thy will be done"); in Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) it opens the epic — "I thence / Invoke thy aid to my adventrous Song" — addressing the heavenly Muse with the possessive of reverence. Quakers carried it into their "plain language" of spiritual equality, and northern English dialect kept it as a living everyday form. Conclusion. Thy is thus a word with a two-fold biography: a deep phonological history, in which a final -n was dropped before consonants to create thy beside thine; and a social history, in which the whole archaic set was pushed from ordinary speech into the sacred and poetic registers. It survives today precisely because it is the possessive of prayer and verse — the sound of reverence in the second person singular.
