- Ancient–Medieval Equine Lore - Equine ear positions recognized by horsemasters (Xenophon, medieval farriers) as the primary indicator of temper, willingness, and physical comfort.
- 18th–19th c. (Agricultural & Sporting Literature) - Idiomatically fixed in English equestrian prose and translation literature (notably Leo Tolstoy's *Kholstomer / Strider: The Story of a Horse*) to depict equine emotional interiority and silent rebellion against harsh masters.
- 19th c. (Victorian Animal Novels) - Prominently featured in Victorian narratives (e.g., Anna Sewell's *Black Beauty*) to protest animal mistreatment and teach readers to interpret subtle nonverbal cues of distress.
- 20th–21st c. (Modern Ethology & Horsemanship) - Scientifically codified in animal behavior studies (equine facial grimace scales) alongside ongoing use in pastoral and literary writing.
