- [Ancient Greece] - [the Strangler: a killing daemon of Thebes whose name literally says what she does; feared, ensnaring, unanswerable — a threat, not a wonder]
- [Classical Athens] - [the tragedians turn the monster into theatre; her riddle becomes the riddle, her defeat the proof of wisdom — the Sphinx is on her way to being a puzzle rather than a plain terror]
- [Greco-Roman Egypt] - [the word crosses over to the colossal Egyptian statues; on the Nile the sphinx is a silent guardian — ancient, impassive, ‘as old as the world itself’]
- [Medieval Europe] - [through Latin learning the sphinx survives mainly in encyclopaedias and the Oedipus tradition: a marvel of old books, reduced to a curiosity]
- [18th–19th century Egyptomania] - [Denon’s engravings after Napoleon’s expedition put the Great Sphinx before Europe; the word shifts to a monument — the desert giant with the missing nose, sand-drifts and stored mystery]
- [Victorian era] - [journalistic metaphor: ‘a sphinx’ is an unreadable, closed person; ‘sphinx-like’ enters ordinary prose; Wilde writes his dense Egyptian poem ‘The Sphinx’ (1894)]
- [20th century] - [Freud (1900) fixes the sphinx and her riddle inside the Oedipus complex; the creature becomes the stock emblem of the uncanny and of riddles themselves]
- [Today] - [the Great Sphinx is a world monument and the word a cliché for any silent enigma — from ‘political sphinxes’ to the hairless Sphynx cat — the strangler has been tamed into a mask]
