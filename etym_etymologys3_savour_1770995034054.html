ETYMOLOGY ESSAY: SAVOUR 1. THE PIE ROOT *sep-: TASTING AS KNOWING Savour descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *sep- (sometimes reconstructed with a laryngeal as *sepH-), whose core meaning is reconstructed as "to taste, to perceive, to try, to discern." For early Indo-European speakers the mouth was an organ of truth: tasting a plant, a fruit, or water told you whether it nourished or poisoned, and the act of "tasting" became the ancient metaphor for direct, trustworthy knowledge. This conceptual fusion — taste and understanding as one — is the deepest layer of the word's history. (The same root is also sometimes adduced for Sanskrit sapati "he serves, honours," but this connection is debated: many linguists treat "taste/perceive" and "serve/honour" as separate roots, so the Indo-Aryan link must be marked as uncertain.) 2. GERMANIC REFLEXES: SAP AND JUICE In the Germanic branch the root produced the noun *safją, "sap, juice," the vital liquid of a plant, preserved in Old English sæp and modern English sap (and German Saft, "juice"). The connection is meaningful: the sap is the "taste" of a tree, the fluid that gives it life and flavour. This Germanic line does not feed directly into English savour (which is a Romance borrowing), but it shows how widespread and fundamental the root's "juice/taste" semantics were. The relationship between Germanic *safją and Latin sapere is accepted by some scholars and doubted by others; the debate should be acknowledged rather than hidden. 3. LATIN sapere: THE VERB OF WISDOM The decisive branch is Italic. Latin sapere meant literally "to taste of, to have a taste," and — in one of the most famous semantic leaps in the language — also "to be sensible, to know, to be wise." A Roman sapiēns was a "sage," literally "one who has tasted," and the word survives in modern English as Homo sapiens, "wise man," and sage (via Old French). From sapere Latin formed the noun sapor, "taste, flavour," the adjective sapidus "having a taste" (source of English sapid and its negative insipid, "tasteless"), and the Late Latin verb saporāre, "to give flavour to, to season, to taste." 4. OLD FRENCH saveur AND savourer In Gallo-Romance, Latin sapor became Old French saveur, "taste, flavour, smell," and saporāre became savourer, "to taste, to appreciate at leisure." The French line enriched the word: where Latin stressed the fact of tasting, French stressed the appreciation, and savourer developed its signature sense of lingering, discriminating enjoyment. The noun entered Middle English in the 13th century (from Anglo-Norman and Old French) and the verb followed in the same period. 5. ENTRY INTO MIDDLE AND MODERN ENGLISH In Middle English, savour (noun) meant taste, smell, or flavour, and the verb savouren meant to taste, smell, or give flavour to. Both literal senses long dominated. The figurative extension — to "savour" an idea, honour, or prospect by mentally tasting it — appears in the 16th century and grows steadily. The phrase "to savour of" (to have the flavour of, i.e., a trace of) is likewise early and durable. By the 18th-19th centuries the word is increasingly tied to refined, deliberate, slow enjoyment, and in modern usage the sensory and the intellectual senses operate together: to savour a meal and to savour a moment are the same act applied to different objects. 6. SOUND AND SPELLING Latin sapor → French saveur: the short open -a- of sapor was raised and diphthongised in Gallo-Romance, giving the French -au-, and the final -or became -eur. English kept the French spelling savour, and the -our remains the standard British form, while 19th-century American usage (under the influence of spelling reformers such as Noah Webster) simplified it to savor. The pronunciation settled at modern /ˈseɪvə/ (British) and /ˈseɪvər/ (American). 7. LITERARY ATTESTATIONS AND MODERN CAREER - 13th century: literal taste/smell in Middle English verse and cookery. - 14th-16th centuries: the noun extends to "characteristic quality"; Chaucer uses it for reputation and flavour. - 16th-17th centuries: the verb takes the figurative sense of mental enjoyment; "savours of" is established. - 18th-19th centuries: refined, slow appreciation becomes the dominant verb sense in literary prose. - 20th-21st centuries: food-writing, travel writing, and memoir have made "savour the moment" a widely used exhortation; the word retains its elegant, sensuous, unhurried register. 8. THE SOURCE SENTENCE In the card's context — "a desire to savour country sights and sounds led him outside the city walls" — the word calls up its entire ancestry: the Latin fusion of tasting and knowing, the French refinement of appreciative leisure, and the English habit of applying a mouth's vocabulary to the mind's experience. To savour country sights is to taste them slowly, and to know them by that slow tasting.
