Rustle names a soft, light, continuous sound of many small contacts — dry leaves moving, paper or silk stirred, corn in the wind — and the verb that produces or moves with such a sound. It is strongly onomatopoeic: saying the word approximates the sound. In American English a second, independent sense developed in the 19th century: "to rustle" also means "to steal livestock" (especially cattle) by quietly driving them away — hence "cattle rustler." A third, colloquial sense, "rustle up," means to gather or prepare quickly ("to rustle up some dinner").

  • Primary sense — the sound: a "rustle" is a soft crackling or whispering sound, as of leaves, grass, paper, silk, or wings: "the rustle of autumn leaves," "the rustle of a gown," "the rustle of turning pages." As a verb: "the wind rustled the leaves," "leaves rustled in the breeze."
  • Echoic / onomatopoeic quality: the word imitates the sound it names; related echoic verbs in other Germanic languages (e.g. German rauschen, Dutch ruishen) show the same sound-symbolism, each independently built from the same noise.
  • Sense — to move with a rustling sound: "she rustled down the corridor" (moved briskly, with silk or paper audibly stirring). Also "to move energetically or busily" (16th-17th centuries): the bustling sense underlies later American developments.
  • American sense — to rustle up: to gather, prepare, or contrive quickly and resourcefully, chiefly of food or supplies: "to rustle up a meal," "to rustle up volunteers." Colloquial, warm register.
  • American sense — to steal livestock: (c. 1880s, western US) "to rustle cattle or horses" = to steal them, originally by driving them off quietly at night; "rustling" (noun) = cattle theft; "rustler" = a cattle thief, romanticised in western films and fiction as a daring outlaw of the range.
  • Secondary noun uses: "a rustle of interest" (a stir of excitement passing through a crowd, from the sound of movement or murmurs); "rustle" in cookery and nature writing for the sound of dry or moving material.
  • Register: the sound-sense is universal and everyday; "rustle up" is informal; the theft-sense is now largely historical or regional but still used in rural and judicial contexts ("rustling charges," "cattle rustling").

Semantic bridge: the theft-sense is a classic example of semantic specialisation: 19th-century western American speech used "rustle" for brisk, energetic movement ("to rustle about," "to rustle in"), then for gathering things quickly ("rustle up"), and finally for rounding up cattle — legitimately at first (herding), then illegitimately (stealing). The word's two faces — the soft dry sound and the bold cattle thief — seem far apart but are joined by the idea of quiet, purposeful movement. In the Tolkien sentence, "hear the rustle of the whispering voices of the others just outside," the word carries its oldest sense: the faint, dry, almost inaudible sound of moving and speaking, heard through stone.
