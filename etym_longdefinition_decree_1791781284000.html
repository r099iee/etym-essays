decree is both a noun and a verb, borrowed in the 14th century from Old French and ultimately from Latin dēcrētum, “a decision, ordinance.” As a noun it names an authoritative order issued by a sovereign, government, court or other competent authority and having the force of law. In the source sentence — “law is usually a decree of the master” — the word is used in its oldest and strongest sense: a command imposed from above, by contrast with custom, which “rises out of the people.” The antithesis is between the artificial, vertical command and the organic, horizontal growth of habitual practice.

The noun: primary senses

  • Sovereign or executive order. An edict issued by a monarch, president or ruling council, often without parliamentary consent: a royal decree, a presidential decree, a decree of the junta. “Rule by decree” is the standard phrase for government that bypasses the legislature.
  • Judicial decision. In courts of equity and in canon law, a decree was the final order determining a cause; in matrimonial law it survives in the phrase decree nisi (an order that takes effect unless cause is shown) and decree absolute (the final order). In Scotland, a decree is still the ordinary term for a court's judgment, including a decree of divorce and a decree arbitral.
  • Ecclesiastical and conciliar decree. The decrees of a council or synod are its binding rulings on doctrine or discipline; the Decretum of Gratian (c. 1140) is the classic medieval collection, and a papal decretal is a decree in letter form.
  • US consent decree. A negotiated settlement approved and entered by a court, enforceable as an order; widely used in antitrust, environmental and civil-rights cases.

Figurative and extended senses

  • Fate, providence, destiny. “The decrees of heaven,” “the decree of fate”: an irreversible ordinance beyond appeal. The metaphor treats the universe as a sovereign whose word cannot be questioned.
  • Any unquestionable pronouncement. A critic's decree, fashion's decree — the word lends mock-solemn weight to merely human preferences.
  • In sports and games, a ruling by a governing authority; in computing, rare informal use for a setting enforced from above rather than chosen by the user.

The verb

To decree is to order, ordain or decide by decree: “The king decreed that the tax be lifted.” It takes a that-clause or a direct object (“decree an amnesty”), and it is mostly formal or literary; in ordinary speech one would order or rule. The participle decreed is common in passive and adjectival use: “a decreed fate,” “as decreed by law.” The verb implies no deliberation on the part of those affected, only authority on the part of the speaker.

Grammar and spelling

Plural decrees; possessive decree's and decrees'; past tense decreed; present participle decreeing. The double e is retained before -ing, while the past tense adds only -d. Pronunciation is /dɪˈkriː/, with stress on the second syllable, and the plural is /dɪˈkriːz/. The word is not related to degree, which descends from Latin gradus, “step,” despite the similar look.

Register and rhetoric

“Decree” is formal, slightly archaic and weighty. It implies hierarchy: someone decrees, someone else obeys. That is why it is the preferred word of polemic against arbitrary power — “government by decree,” “a decree from Brussels” — and why democratic bodies usually prefer act, statute or regulation. In the source, the word's connotations of imposition, mastery and unanswerability do most of the argumentative work: custom is described as a slow natural selection, law as a command that arrives already finished.

Decree, edict, statute: the boundaries

An edict is a public proclamation, historically the word of a Roman magistrate or a monarch; a statute is a law passed by a legislature; a decree may be either, but always emphasises the will of the issuing authority rather than the procedure that produced it. A regulation is subordinate legislation made under statutory power, and an order in council is the British executive instrument that most closely resembles a decree. The distinctions matter in political argument, because calling an instrument a decree denies it the legitimacy of debate.

Historical weight

The word carries the memory of Roman imperial legislation, of papal and conciliar rulings, of absolutist monarchy and of revolutionary governments legislating without assemblies. It also carries the counter-memory of equity: the Chancellor's decree was a remedy against the rigidity of the common law, a use in which the word stood for justice rather than tyranny. Both associations are available to modern speakers, which is why “decree” can be used admiringly of a decisive court order and disapprovingly of a president's whim.
