savour (US spelling: savor): noun and verb. As a noun, the distinctive taste or smell of something — its flavour, zest, or characteristic quality; by extension, a particular quality that gives something its distinguishing character, or a slight trace of something. As a verb, to taste or smell with appreciation and lingering attention, or to enjoy an experience slowly and completely, drawing out every moment of pleasure.

PRIMARY SENSE (noun: taste, flavour, relish). The noun's core is the sensory one: the savour of a dish is its flavour or relish, and, by easy extension, the appetising or distinctive smell of food ("the savour of roasting meat"). From "taste" the noun moves naturally to "characteristic quality": a thing's savour is the peculiar element that gives it flavour or interest, whether pleasant or not ("the conversation had a savour of scandal," "a political career with a savour of corruption"). Finally, "a savour of" something can mean simply a hint or trace of it — a flavour of the thing in question.

PRIMARY SENSE (verb: to taste or enjoy at length). The verb is the more active of the two. To savour food is to taste it slowly, deliberately, appreciatively — to hold each mouthful, roll it over the tongue, and extract the fullest possible pleasure. By extension the verb is used of any experience or moment: to "savour the moment," "savour the victory," "savour the silence," "savour every page." In the card's source sentence ("a desire to savour country sights and sounds led him outside the city walls"), the word applies the whole slow, appreciative machinery of tasting to the act of looking and listening: the traveller does not merely see the countryside but tastes it, lingering over each sight and sound.

SECONDARY SENSES AND EXTENSIONS.

  • savour of (verb phrase) — to have a trace or suggestion of a quality, often an undesirable one: "his generosity savoured of ostentation," "the plan savours of desperation." This is a direct extension of "to have the flavour of."
  • Culinary jargon — in cookery writing, savoury (the adjective, "salty or spicy rather than sweet," and the noun "a savoury dish served before or after the main course") belongs to the same word-family; "to savour" is standard in food criticism.
  • Figurative and journalistic use — "savour of success," "savour of the exotic" — used of any quality that gives an experience its distinctive, lingering character.
  • Sense of anticipation — to savour can also imply relishing something before it happens, as one savours the prospect of a holiday.

GRAMMAR, COLLOCATION, AND REGISTER. The noun is countable or uncountable ("a savour," "a certain savour," "no savour at all") and collocates with sweet, bitter, stale, exotic, of. The verb is transitive ("savour the dish," "savour the moment") and is frequently used with objects naming moments, victories, silences, successes, and flavours. The register is literary, elevated, and mildly formal; savour is far more deliberate and sensuous than plain enjoy, and it is beloved of food writers, memoirists, and poets. The British spelling -our (retained from French saveur) is standard in UK, Australian, and New Zealand English; American English prefers savor.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES. Enjoy is broad and passive; relish is zestful, eager, and often anticipates pleasure; appreciate is intellectual, recognising value; bask in suggests passively soaking up warmth or approval; cherish is protective and long-term; delight in is lively and expressive. Savour alone insists on slowness, attention, and sensory depth — the mental act of "tasting" an experience, born of the same root that gave Latin its verb "to be wise" (sapere). Its historical bridge runs from the mouth to the mind: in ancient thought, to taste was to know.
