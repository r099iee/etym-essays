a stone’s throw is an idiom of distance meaning a very short way, roughly the distance a small stone travels when tossed by hand — in modern feeling anything from a few dozen to a couple of hundred metres, always near enough to walk to in a minute or two and near enough to see clearly.

In the source sentence the hobbits keep a stone’s throw to the left of the road, staying parallel to it but hidden: the phrase fixes their position with travelling precision, close enough to follow the road’s line yet far enough into cover to stay unseen, and it carries the wary, footsore logic of people measuring safety in paces rather than miles.

Core meaning in detail: the idiom never names an exact length; it names a human-scaled nearness judged by the body, not the map, and its vagueness is its point, since a stone flies farther in open ground than in thickets, farther for a strong arm than a tired one, yet every hearer instantly pictures the same short arc and landing thud. Dictionaries gloss it as a short distance or a stone’s cast, and usage bears that out, with writers reaching for it whenever characters hover just offstage from a road, a village, a river, or danger itself.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A very short physical distance (primary modern sense): the cottage stood a stone’s throw from the church, meaning a minute’s walk at most, and the bridge from throwing to distance is direct, since the stone’s flight is the measuring stick, an ancient body-based unit like a bowshot, a spear’s cast, or an arrow’s flight.
  • Close proximity with separation kept (Tolkien’s nuance): keeping a stone’s throw to the left of the road means near yet deliberately apart, and the bridge is pragmatic wayfinding, since travellers shadow a road for guidance while avoiding its exposure, a usage common in walking narratives and military scouting.
  • Near miss or narrow margin (figurative extension): they came within a stone’s throw of disaster or victory, and the bridge runs from space to outcome, since a stone landing just short of its mark pictures any near achievement or escape, the same image that gives us within spitting distance and within shouting distance.
  • A trivial, easily covered interval (hyperbolic): the shops are a stone’s throw away, said even where no stone is ever thrown, and the bridge is affectionate exaggeration, since the speaker borrows the throw’s brevity to promise convenience, much as a five-minute walk rarely lasts exactly five minutes.
  • Literary archaism with biblical gravity (stylistic): he withdrew about a stone’s cast from them, echoing older scripture and chronicle phrasing, and the bridge is register memory, since stone’s cast and stone’s throw preserve a pre-metric world where distances were cast, shot, and thrown rather than numbered.

Register and connotation: the phrase is warm, rural, and slightly old-fashioned, at home in novels, travel writing, and estate-agent prose, and it flatters nearness without promising adjacency, so that a hotel a stone’s throw from the beach may still sit across a road and a dune. It implies visibility and reachability rather than touch, and it keeps a faintly playful violence, since stones are also thrown in anger, though the idiom itself never threatens, and its charm lies in measuring the modern world with the oldest missile, the hand and the pebble.
