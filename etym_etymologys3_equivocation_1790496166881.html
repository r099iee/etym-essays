Equivocation fuses two Latin certainties, equal and voice, into one moral suspicion, and its history runs from school logic through scaffold treason to nursery discipline.

The first tributary is Latin aequus, level, even, equal, just, fair (aequor, level sea; aequalis, equal; aequitas, equity; iniquus, unjust). Its deeper Indo-European source iskisputed: de Vaan and Walde-Hofmann mark aequus as without secure PIE etymology, with a provisional link to h2eyk- (sharp? even?) offered only as conjecture. Responsible lexicography therefore starts at Latin: aequus is certain from the Twelve Tables onward as the Roman word for fairness as flatness. Its combining form aequi- (equi-) means equal or same: equidistant, equilateral, equivalent.

The second tributary is secure Indo-European: PIE wokw-s, voice, speech, giving Sanskrit vac- (to speak, Veda as knowledge spoken), Greek epos (word, epic), Latin vox, vocis (voice, word), vocare (to call, summon, name), vocabulum (name-word). Phonologically PIE w- gives Latin v-, -kw- gives -qu- before back vowels in vocare but -c- in vox by regular labiovelar treatment. From vocare come advocate, revoke, invoke, vocabulary, and vocal itself.

  • Late Latin union (3rd-6th century): grammarians and logicians need a term for one word-form with two senses (canis as dog and dogfish, acies as edge and battle-line). They coin aequivocus (called by the same name, of equal voice) and aequivocatio (same-naming, ambiguity). Boethius and Martianus Capella use it as a neutral logical category beside univocatio (one sense) and analogia (proportional sense). No moral blame yet attaches; equivocation is a property of language.
  • Scholastic fallacy (12th-14th century): Peter of Spain and the Summulae make fallacia aequivocationis one of six linguistic fallacies. Schoolboys learn to detect shifting middle terms. Old French borrows equivocacion; Chaucer's England borrows equivocacioun c. 1380 in Boethius translations, still purely logical.
  • Gunpowder moralisation (1598-1606): Jesuit Henry Garnet writes A Treatise of Equivocation defending mental reservation under persecution; after the Gunpowder Plot his trial makes equivocator mean popish traitor who swears innocence with fingers crossed. Shakespeare's Porter in Macbeth jokes that drink provokes nose-painting, sleep, and urine, and that an equivocator could swear in both the scales against either scale. From then on equivocation in English is never merely logical; it is evasive, priestly, and hanging-matter.
  • Polite and nursery afterlife (18th-19th century): Chesterfield warns against equivocation in letters; Parliament hears equivocal answers; Ruskin's sailor-grandfather, who lived by plain orders, makes it the unforgiveable nursery sin beside imaginative statements. The quarterdeck and the pulpit agree: a child who trims truth will trim charts.
  • Modern logic and spin (20th century to present): textbooks keep the fallacy (man shifts sense), courts punish misleading literal truth as perjury, diplomats rehabilitate it as constructive ambiguity that lets enemies sign one sentence. The equal voice that once named a dictionary fact now names a character flaw, except when it saves peace.

Together the evolution shows how a technical compound for same-naming acquired a soul: equal plus voice began as description, became accusation on a scaffold, then a grandfather's rule, which is why Ruskin remembers it with salt severity rather than school dryness.
