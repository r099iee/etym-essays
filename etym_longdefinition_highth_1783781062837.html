highth (n.) — an archaic Early Modern English spelling of height, meaning: (1) the vertical measurement of something from its base to its top; (2) the summit, peak, or topmost point of a physical object; (3) the utmost degree, acme, or culminating stage of an abstract quality or process; (4) in literary usage, the sublime pinnacle of a great theme or argument.

1. Concrete sense — vertical dimension. In its most concrete sense highth names the measurable distance by which something stands above a reference plane: the altitude of a mountain, the elevation of a tower, the stature of a person, or the rise of flood-water. This is the everyday sense of "the height of a building" or "height above sea level." Crucially, the word is built with the suffix -th, the abstract-noun suffix that English inherited directly from Germanic (compare length, breadth, width, depth, strength, warmth), so that highth literally means "the state or degree of being high."

2. Concrete sense — the summit itself. From the measurement the word shifts metonymically to the thing measured at its maximum: the peak, crest, apex, or crown of a hill, tower, or other elevated object. Here highth equals summit or pinnacle, referring to the visible top of something, as in "the highth of the mountain." The endpoint of the measurement comes to stand for the whole elevated thing at its extreme point.

3. Abstract sense — utmost degree. By a natural spatial metaphor the vertical scale is transferred to intensity and degree: highth names the highest point of an emotion, condition, career, excellence, or reputation — "the highth of his fame," "at the highth of summer," "in the highth of battle." This is the sense nearest to acme, climax, or peak in modern usage, and it is the sense in which a career or an argument has a "highth."

4. Miltonic sense — the sublime top of a theme. In Paradise Lost (Book I, line 24), Milton invokes the Holy Spirit so that he may rise "to the highth of this great Argument" — the word names the supreme elevation of his subject, the highest reach of epic theme, above the classical "Aonian Mount" of pagan poetry. The deliberately archaic spelling highth (re-formed directly from high + -th) gives the verse an elevated, dignified register and marks the poet's consciously solemn diction.

Polysemy & semantic reasoning.

  • Measurement → summit: the move from "amount of vertical distance" to "the topmost point" is metonymy; the terminal point of a scale stands for the whole thing at its maximum. The same bridge yields modern heights "a piece of high ground" and depth for the bottom.
  • Summit → utmost degree: the transfer from physical peak to abstract acme is a spatial metaphor that English uses constantly (peak, climax from Greek "ladder," summit, top); the scale of height becomes the scale of intensity, and "to reach the highth of X" means to reach the greatest degree of X.
  • Standard height vs. analogical highth: both descend ultimately from Old English hēahþu "height." The modern standard height is the regular phonological descendant through Middle English heihte/heighte; highth is a re-formation that attaches -th directly to high, following the pattern of breadth and width. The two forms competed in print through the 17th century; highth lost the standard-language contest but survived as a poetic variant.
  • Register shift: once a neutral spelling used by Shakespeare and Milton, highth is today a deliberate archaism signalling 17th-century epic diction; it has no modern technical sense but recurs in Milton scholarship and in allusive poetic titles.

Current status. In present-day English highth survives only as a historical spelling; dictionaries list it as an archaic variant of height. It carries no distinct lexical sense of its own beyond those of height, but its function is indexical: it flags a particular literary-historical moment and a particular solemn, elevated register, and its suffix -th visibly ties the word to the native Germanic abstract-noun system.
