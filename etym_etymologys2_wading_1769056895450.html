Chain for "wade" — the ancestry of wading: 1. Proto-Indo-European *weh₂dʰ- (also written *wādʰ-, *wadh-) — "to go, to walk, to advance, to pass through". A root of motion; its best-known cognate is Latin vadere "to go, to walk" (source of English "invade", "evade", "pervade"). 2. Proto-Germanic *wadaną (reconstructed; pre-Common-Era Germanic) — "to go, to walk, to wade". The inherited Germanic verb of motion, with the sense of "going forward" already able to include crossing water. 3. Old English wadan (attested from the 8th century onward) — "to go, to advance, to travel; to cross water, to ford". The general sense "to go" is well attested in Anglo-Saxon prose and verse (e.g. in Beowulf), alongside the water-crossing sense. Its strong-verb forms: wadan, wōd, wodon, waden. 4. Middle English waden (12th–15th century) — "to go, to walk, to advance; to walk through water". The general "go" senses begin to yield to the water sense as the verb "go" takes over ordinary motion. 5. English wade (16th century onward; modern form) — "to walk through water too deep to run in but shallow enough to keep the feet on the bottom", and, figuratively, "to make slow, effortful progress" ("wade through a book") and "to enter boldly" ("wade in"). The earlier general "go" senses have been entirely taken over by the water sense. 6. English wading (present participle) — "walking through shallow water; making slow effortful progress". wading IS OUR WORD. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: The chain from PIE to Old English is well attested and uncontroversial. The one nuance is semantic: the earliest Old English sense is broad ("to go"), and the narrowing onto water happened gradually across Middle English — there is no single date at which "wade" stopped meaning "go". Modern "wading" thus preserves the ancient "go" sense only in frozen compounds and the figurative uses noted above.
