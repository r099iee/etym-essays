- [Biblical Hebrew & Ancient Judaism (c. 10th c. BCE–1st c. CE)] — Rooted in the Covenant of Sinai (*Shabbat*), observed as a joyful yet strictly commanded cessation from all agricultural, craft, and commercial labor — [perception: celebrated as the holy hallmark of the Jewish people, a sacred covenantal sign, and a living memorial of God's seventh-day rest at Creation]
- [Early & Medieval Christianity (c. 4th–14th c.)] — Adopted into ecclesiastical Latin (*sabbatum*) and vernacular Old/Middle English (*sabat*); theological debates arose over whether the Sabbath remained the Saturday seventh day or transferred to Sunday (*Dies Dominica*) — [perception: regarded primarily as a liturgical calendar designation and solemn church holiday, often contrasted polemically with Jewish rites]
- [Puritan & Protestant Reformation (c. 16th–17th c.)] — Under strict English Sabbatarianism, the spelling stabilized as *Sabbath* with a restored Hebrew terminal *-th*; Puritans imposed severe legal prohibitions against all Sunday commerce, games, theatricals, and frivolous sports — [perception: feared and respected as a stern, unbending moral discipline, enforcing solemn churchgoing, introspection, and austere domestic sobriety]
- [Romantic & Poetic Era (Late 18th–19th c.)] — Poets like Wordsworth, Coleridge, and Keats secularized and internalised the term, applying *Sabbath* metaphorically to pristine natural landscapes, artistic contemplation, and quietude free from industrial wage-slavery — [perception: romanticized as a blessed sanctuary of mental peace, creative freedom, and pastoral communion with the divine in nature]
- [Modern & Contemporary Era (20th–21st c.)] — Retains traditional Jewish (*Shabbat*) and Christian Sunday worship usages while expanding in secular discourse into concepts like the "digital Sabbath", academic sabbaticals, and ecological resting periods — [perception: revered as an essential psychological antidote to 24/7 hyper-connectivity, relentless capitalist productivity, and cognitive burnout]
