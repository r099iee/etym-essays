- [1530s] - penetrate is borrowed into English from Latin penetratus: bullets, blades, light, and water penetrate bodies - [perception: technical, physical, slightly learned]
- [17th–18th c.] - philosophy and science extend it: rays penetrate the atmosphere, arguments penetrate the understanding - [perception: the crossing from body to mind becomes the decisive metaphor]
- [19th c.] - criticism uses it of style and influence: a principle penetrates a poet’s verse - [perception: penetration tests depth and sincerity] (the sense in the reading context)
- [20th c.] - marketing, security, and physics standardise it: market penetration, penetration testing, penetration depth - [perception: the word supplies the imagery of measured intrusion]
- [Today] - still physical and precise; in some registers a clinical term for sexual entry - [perception: ordinary word, freighted wherever it touches the body]
