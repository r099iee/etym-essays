Single linear derivation chain for WHISKED (one inherited Germanic base; the modern form is the past participle of the verb "to whisk"):
1. PIE *weis- (also reconstructed *weisk-) — "to turn, twist, bend; a pliant twig" (reconstructed; per Pokorny; debated — see note; also the base of Latin vītis "vine" and Greek itéa "willow").
2. Proto-Germanic *wiskaz — "a bundle of twigs or straw (used as a brush); a wisp" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE – 200 CE; cf. Middle Dutch wisch, Old High German wisc "bundle of straw", German Wisch "wisp, rag").
3. Old Norse visk — "a wisp of straw or hay; a small brush" (attested, Viking Age).
4. Middle English wisk / wisken (noun and verb) — "a sweep, a brush; to sweep, to move quickly, to brush away" (attested c. 1300; the verb wisken "to move quickly" and the noun wisk are recorded from the 13th–14th c.).
5. Early Modern English whisk — verb "to sweep or move quickly, to brush away", noun "a small brush or broom" (attested 15th–16th c.; the spelling with wh- appears from the late 15th c.; the culinary utensil sense is from the 18th c.).
6. Modern English whisked — past tense and past participle of "to whisk": "swept, moved, or carried off quickly" (attested from the 16th c. onward).
Notes on debated / nuanced stages: (a) The OED marks the origin of "whisk" as uncertain, "probably" from a Scandinavian base (Old Norse visk); the traditional PIE *weis- assignment (Pokorny) is plausible but not proven. (b) The initial wh- spelling reflects the historical aspirated /hw/ onset of "whisk"; by the 18th c. the word was pronounced with a simple /w/ in most of England. (c) "Whisker" (facial hair, 1600s) and the culinary "whisk" (18th c.) are derivatives of the same word, not separate etymologies.
