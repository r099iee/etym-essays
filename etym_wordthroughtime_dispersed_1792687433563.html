- [Late 14th c. Middle English] — Entered English via Old French disperser and Latin dispersus, used in theological translations (Wycliffe) to describe the biblical diaspora of nations and the divine scattering of prideful builders at Babel — [perception: divine judgment scattering mankind across the earth]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English] — Adopted into military manuals and Tudor riot proclamations (such as the Riot Act ancestors) to command unlawful assemblies to break apart and return home — [perception: restoration of civil and sovereign order over disorderly crowds]
- [17th c. Scientific Revolution] — Adopted into natural philosophy; Isaac Newton utilized dispersed to describe the refractive separation of white light into spectral rays by a prism (1704 Opticks) — [perception: an objective physical mechanism governing waves and optics]
- [Early 19th c. Romantic Era] — Adopted by Wordsworth and Shelley to depict the melancholic, ethereal dissolution of sound, clouds, and human assemblies into nature — [perception: poetic entropy, transience, and atmospheric beauty]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Era] — Standardized in colloidal physical chemistry, statistical analysis (dispersion metrics), computer network architectures (dispersed data storage), and global diaspora studies — [perception: an essential structural, mathematical, and materials-science concept]
