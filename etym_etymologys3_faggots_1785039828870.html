The history of FAGGOTS is the history of a humble object — the bound bundle of firewood — told through a word that travelled the length of Europe and accumulated layer after layer of meaning. Its recorded life begins in the Mediterranean, where classical Greek possessed phákelos, "a bundle, a faggot," a word whose strange shape (the clustering of labial and velar consonants, the -el-os ending) has led modern scholars such as Robert Beekes to classify it as a Pre-Greek substrate term — that is, a word inherited from the older, non-Indo-European population of the Aegean rather than formed from an Indo-European root. If that is right, the word is a fossil of the deep pre-history of Europe, adopted into Greek, then passed on into the Romance speech of the Italian peninsula, where the diminutive fagotto, "a small bundle of sticks," gave the modern word its shape. From Italian the term entered Old French as fagot (13th c.), and from Old French it crossed the Channel into Middle English in the late 13th century, recorded in the spellings fagot, fagald, and faggald. Inside English the word was immediately at home, because the object was everywhere: in a wood-poor medieval economy, coppiced woodland was cut on a rotation and the small brushwood bound into bundles was the standard fuel unit of the hearth, the bake-oven, and the forge. The meaning was precise — a faggot was not a log but a bundle — and the word passed unchanged into the daily round of the household. The doubled spelling faggott (and then faggot) consolidated in the 16th century, at exactly the moment the word's history darkened. From the 1550s "fire and faggot" meant the supreme penalty of the heretic: to be burned at the stake, the faggots heaped at the feet of the condemned. The word also grew sideways into other trades. In ironworking a faggot was a bundle of bars or scrap heated and welded into a single bloom; in the kitchen of the English Midlands, from about 1851, faggots named the butcher's dish of spiced offal balls wrapped in caul fat — a "bundle" of minced meat. In 1733 the confectioners' faggot was a roll of candied orange peel, and in sewing fagoting described the bundled, bound threads of an openwork stitch. Even the bassoon owes its name to the same source: Italian fagotto, "little bundle," because the instrument's wooden sections were bundled together. The word's most infamous development, however, belongs entirely to the 20th century. In American slang from 1914, faggot became a brutal pejorative for a homosexual man. Etymologically it is a pure homonym — a chance sound-alike with no meaningful connection to the bundle of sticks — although folk explanation has tried to manufacture links. Its presence in the language has made the old, innocent firewood word uncomfortable for many modern readers, which is precisely why careful texts must read by context and date. In J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), written half a lifetime before the slur gained its later currency, the plural FAGGOTS in the Elves' song — "The faggots are reeking, / The bannocks are baking!" — is unambiguously the immemorial hearth-word: the smoke-blessed bundles crackling on the fire of Rivendell, tokens of warmth, shelter, and welcome to travellers who have crossed the cold of the Misty Mountains. Tolkien, a philologist in full command of his word-stock, reached for the oldest and most homely layer of English; the reeking faggots belong to the world of coppice, hearthstone, and the Last Homely House, not to any later, separate homonym. A word that began as a bundle of sticks in the Pre-Greek Aegean thus ends its journey as a token of hospitality in an invented Elvish valley — the same small bundle, bound and rebound across three thousand years.
