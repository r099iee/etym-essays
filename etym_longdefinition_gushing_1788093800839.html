Gushing is the present participle and gerund of the verb gush, with two great families of meaning: literally, pouring or flowing out rapidly and copiously (of liquids, and by extension of smoke, wind and light); and figuratively, expressing speech or emotion in an effusive, exaggerated, overflowing manner. In the source sentence — the death of Smaug in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit — "a hiss, a gushing whirl, and then silence" describes the sudden violent outrush of the lake water as the great body crashed into it, and the participle fuses sound, speed and quantity into a single image of tremendous, unstoppable motion.

1. Literal senses.

  • Of liquids: to flow out rapidly, copiously and with force — "blood gushing from a wound", "a gushing spring", "water gushed from the broken pipe". The emphasis is on quantity, speed and pressure: a gush is a flood under pressure, not a trickle.
  • Of substances and phenomena: smoke gushes from a chimney, wind gushes through a gap, lava gushes from a fissure, tears gush from the eyes; the participle names the torrent in mid-act — a gushing wound, a gushing flow, the "gushing whirl" of the Tolkien passage.
  • As a noun: "a gush" — the sudden outpour itself ("a gush of blood", "a gush of wind", "a gush of water"), and by the same extension "a gush of words", "a gush of sympathy".

2. Figurative and emotional senses.

  • Effusive speech and emotion: to express praise, admiration or affection with exaggerated, overflowing feeling — "she was gushing about the wedding", "gushing reviews", "gushing fans". The semantic bridge is the torrent: emotion and words pour out of a person as liquid pours from a burst pipe — abundant, visible, hard to stop.
  • Register shift: curiously, the figurative sense is usually mildly critical. To call someone "gushing" is to say their praise is excessive, unmeasured, possibly insincere — the register leans toward the saccharine and the embarrassing, though it can also be affectionate ("she gushed with delight"). The same phenomenon produced the American slur of the era of sentiment: gushing was what one did when one had more feeling than taste.
  • Derived forms: "gushy" (adjective, 19th century) — effusively sentimental, as in "gushy love letters"; "gusher" — in petroleum engineering (1886) an oil well that flows freely without pumping, from the literal sense, and colloquially a person who gushes; "gushingly" (adverb).

3. Jargon and technical domains.

  • Petroleum geology: a "gusher" is the dramatic, uncontrolled flow of an oil well — the image of 19th-century boom folklore; "gushing oil" remains standard in industry prose.
  • Medicine: "gushing" is used of copious bleeding, especially arterial haemorrhage, in clinical and first-aid description.
  • Hydrology and civil engineering: "gushing" springs and flows describe high-yield water sources; a burst main "gushes".
  • Everyday domestic speech: a "gushing tap" or "gushing pipe" — the word is as much at home in kitchens and repairman English as in epic prose.

4. Grammar. Regular verb: gush, gushed, gushing. As a participle it builds continuous tenses ("the wound was gushing"), participial adjectives ("a gushing stream", "gushing praise"), and the gerund-noun ("all this gushing makes me uneasy"). The form takes prepositions — "gushing from", "gushing out of", "gushing over (a person or topic)" — and the emotional sense favours "gushing over".

5. Summary. To be gushing is to pour out suddenly and abundantly — literally of liquid, smoke, wind and flame, and figuratively of words and emotion; the same torrent underlies geysers and oil wells, weeping wounds and effusive fans: a word that has always described what cannot be held back, from a dragon's death-whirl to a fan's praise.
