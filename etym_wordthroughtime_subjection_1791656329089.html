- Late 14th c. (Middle English) — Borrowed from Old French into Middle English legal, theological, and chivalric prose; denoted both feudal obedience to an overlord and spiritual submission to God.
- 15th–16th c. — Prominent in Tudor constitutional doctrine and biblical translations (Tyndale, Great Bible); framed obedience to the monarch as divinely ordained subjection to lawful authority.
- 17th c. (English Civil War & Political Thought) — Central to debates between absolutism (Thomas Hobbes's *Leviathan*, arguing that social order requires absolute subjection to the sovereign) and emerging liberal philosophy (John Locke, contesting patriarchal subjection in *Two Treatises of Government*).
- 18th–19th c. — Applied extensively to colonial empires conquering overseas peoples, and famously crystallized in feminist literature, most notably John Stuart Mill's landmark 1869 treatise *The Subjection of Women*.
- 20th–21st c. — Expanded into post-structuralist philosophy (Michel Foucault, Judith Butler) as *assujettissement* (the paradox of becoming a subject through subordination to power structures), while retaining standard scientific usages for material stress tests.
