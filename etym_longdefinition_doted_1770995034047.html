doted: showed excessive, uncritical fondness or affection toward someone; the past tense and past participle of the verb dote (usually "dote on/upon"). The word describes love that is so warm, so complete, and so uncritical that it blurs the line between devotion and foolishness. Core sense - to be blindly and excessively fond. To dote on someone is to shower them with affection and attention to the point of excess - to treat them as precious, to overlook their faults, to center one's happiness on them. The verb is almost always used with "on" or "upon". In the context sentence, both parents "doted on her, seeing in her their only hope and the sole source of their future happiness" - the doting is explicitly linked to a loss of proportion: the child has become the entire emotional universe of the parents, their "only hope", the "sole source" of their future joy. The word thus carries, in its core, a mild warning: doting love is tender but blind, warm but unmeasured. The historical core - mental clouding. The verb's older sense, preserved in the noun dotage, was "to be feeble-minded, to behave foolishly, to be senile." When the verb attached to love in the late medieval period, this history stayed inside the word: to dote on someone was to be foolish with love, to have one's judgment clouded by affection, to love with a mind "fogged" by tenderness. Modern usage has softened this enormously - "doting parents" is now an image of warm, adorable devotion - but the old idea of love as a form of sweet foolishness is what gives the word its distinctive colour and its faint, affectionate excess. Full polysemy map:
  • To show excessive and uncritical fondness toward someone (with "on"/"upon") - modern dominant sense.
  • Archaic: to be weak-minded, senile, or in one's dotage - the original sense, surviving in the noun dotage and in older literary uses of dote.
  • Archaic: to behave foolishly or absurdly; to talk or act like one whose wits are clouded - 14th-17th-century sense.
  • As participle/adjective: doting - "excessively and uncritically fond" ("a doting mother", "doting parents"); doted as verb past - "they doted on her".
  • Rare transitive (now obsolete): to make someone foolish or infatuated.
  • In compounds of the family: dotage (senile decay; excessive fondness), doter (one who dotes), dotingly (adverb).
The semantic bridge. Middle English doten (c. 1200) meant "to behave foolishly, to be out of one's wits", inherited from Middle Dutch and Middle Low German doten. By the 14th century English had begun to apply the verb to love: a person "dotes" when love makes them as irrational as one whose wits are clouded. The shift from "foolish" to "fond" is a beautiful case of a word of infirmity becoming a word of tenderness - the irrationality was reinterpreted as devotion, the clouding of the mind as a heart so full it has no room for judgment. The two senses ran side by side for centuries, and the older one still glimmers faintly in "dotage". Register and comparison:
  • Doted on vs adored: adored implies reverent, worshipful love; doted on implies fussy, pampering, uncritical attention with a hint of excess.
  • Doted on vs pampered: pampered focuses on comfort and spoiling; doted on focuses on the emotional fondness itself.
  • Doted on vs idolized: idolized suggests reverent idealization; doted on suggests affectionate, often fussy over-indulgence.
  • Register: warm and slightly old-fashioned; "dote on" is literary and carries more tenderness - and more excess - than plain "love".
