In the English countryside the raspberry was for centuries a wild hedgerow and woodland fruit, gathered freely as a summer treat and known as a food of field and cottage before it was of the gentry table.
“Raspberry” by the 18th–19th century became a star of English preserves — raspberry jam, syrups, and cordials — a homely symbol of country-house and cottage pantry comfort.
“Raspberry” with 19th–20th-century commercial cultivation turned into a standard market berry and dessert ingredient, while the word stayed the everyday name for the little red drupelet fruit.
“Raspberry” gained a cheeky second life in the 1890s when Cockney rhyming slang (“raspberry tart” for a rude noise) gave English “to blow a raspberry” — the harmless fruit-name now also the name of a rude tongue-noise.
