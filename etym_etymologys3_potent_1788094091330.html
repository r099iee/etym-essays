I. The Indo-European root (c. 4500–2500 BCE). Potent descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *poti- "powerful; master, lord". Its reflexes are widespread: Sanskrit pátiḥ "master, lord, husband", Avestan paiti- "lord", Greek pósis "husband, master" (Homeric pótnia "lady, mistress"; despótēs "master of the house" from *dems-pot-), Lithuanian pàts "self, husband", and the Germanic -faths element in Gothic brūþ-faþs "bridegroom". The root denotes power exercised over something or someone — the authority of a lord over his household, a husband over a wife, a master over servants. It is a root of social power, not of physical force; it is what parents, masters, and gods hold.

II. Latin: potis, posse, potens (archaic period – 2nd c. BCE). In archaic Latin the adjective potis meant "able, capable, powerful, master of" (with the variant form pote). The phrase potis esse "to be able, be master of" contracted into the verb posse "to be able, have power, be capable" — one of the most important verbs in the language (source of French pouvoir, Italian potere, Spanish poder). The present participle of posse was potēns (genitive potentis) "able, powerful, mighty", attested from the 2nd century BCE (Plautus, Terence). From potēns Latin built potentia "power, might, capacity" (whence English potency, potential) and potentātus "dominion, sovereignty" (whence potentate). The participial form gave the language a ready-made adjective for rulers: rex potentissimus, "the most powerful king", is a stock phrase of Roman historiography.

III. Medieval transmission: Latin to English via two routes (5th c.–15th c.). The Romance languages developed their own continuants of Latin potēns: Old French puissant (from Vulgar Latin *potentem, with irregular shortening of the first syllable — the pot- → pui- shift is still imperfectly explained), which English borrowed in the 15th century as puissant. English also took potent itself directly from Latin potēns around 1400, attested first in the sense "influential, powerful". The word arrived as a learned, dignified adjective for rulers and powers: "potent prince", "potent king" are typical early collocations, and the noun potentate entered English at the same period for "a sovereign, a ruler". Early Modern English thus possessed a three-tier vocabulary of power: native mighty (colloquial-to-literary), French puissant (poetic, elevated), and Latin potent (formal, scholarly).

IV. From persons to things (16th–18th c.). In the 16th and 17th centuries potent broadened from rulers to effective things of every kind: potent arguments, potent medicines, potent liquors, potent charms. The shift is a generalisation: if a king is potent because his will produces results, then a drug or argument is potent because it too produces results — persuasion, healing, intoxication. Shakespeare uses the word of magic — "potent art" (The Tempest, 1611) — and of strong feeling. By the 18th century the quantitative sense was firmly established: a potent dose is one that acts strongly in small quantity; medical writers praised or warned against "potent remedies" and "potent poisons". The heraldic and crutch senses of the noun potent (from Old French potence "crutch") belong to this same era — a wholly separate borrowing from the same Latin family.

V. Science, physiology, and the modern era (19th–21st c.). The 19th century gave the word its scientific home: pharmacology measures the potency of a drug per unit dose; potentiation names the amplification of one drug’s effect by another; reproductive medicine speaks of male sexual potency and of the potency of semen, with impotence as the corresponding pathology. Meanwhile potential — from Latin potentia "capacity" — developed its own scientific career (electric potential, potential energy, the potential mood in grammar) and became a ubiquitous noun meaning "possibility", so that the family now spans the actual (potent) and the possible (potential). The 20th century added journalistic collocations: "a potent mix", "potent imagery", "potent symbolism", always with the sense of concentrated, effective force. Tolkien’s "It must be potent wine to make a wood-elf drowsy" is the modern quantitative sense at its crispest: small draught, great effect.

VI. Summary of the semantic path. The history of potent is a movement from social mastery to impersonal efficacy: Indo-European *poti- names the lord’s authority; Latin potens names the able, powerful person; English potent first names mighty rulers, then powerful arguments, then concentrated drugs, drinks, and forces. What has not changed is the root idea — power that shows itself in effects.

Key literary attestations

  • Plautus (3rd–2nd c. BCE): early attestations of potēns "able, powerful".
  • Cicero and Livy (1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE): rex potentissimus, "the most powerful king", a stock phrase of Roman power.
  • Shakespeare, The Tempest (1611): Prospero’s "potent art" — magic as the paradigm of concentrated power.
  • 18th- and 19th-century pharmacopoeias: "potent remedies" and "potent poisons" as standard usage.
  • Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937): "It must be potent wine to make a wood-elf drowsy" — the modern quantitative sense.
