The root: a voice that flows with tears. At the foundation of deplore lies water. The Indo-European root *pleu- / *plew-, "to flow, to run, to wash," is one of the great roots of liquid motion, and its descendants confirm the image with striking consistency: Greek pléō, "to sail," and plýnō, "to wash"; Germanic flow and flood; a family that runs through the meaning of running water itself. From this root of flowing, Latin built the verb plōrāre, "to cry out, to weep aloud, to lament" — the voice imagined as a stream of tears, or the face as running with them. In the ancient Mediterranean world, weeping was public and ritualised: the Roman funeral demanded loud lamentation, the mourning women of Greek tragedy beat their breasts and wailed on stage, and the literature of grief — from the laments of the Iliad to the elegies of Catullus — is full of the language of flowing tears. Plōrāre belonged to this loud, visible grief, and its derivatives multiplied the image: to plōrāre over the dead was to give grief a voice that anyone could hear.

Latin then strengthened the verb with the prefix dē-, meaning "thoroughly, utterly, completely." The compound dēplōrāre, attested from the first century BCE, meant to weep bitterly over something, to bewail it with the full force of grief — "to cry one's eyes out over," as it were. Where plōrāre was the general word for weeping, dēplōrāre was the intensified word for pouring grief out over a specific object: a dead son, a ruined city, a shattered hope. Roman usage shows it applied to persons, losses, and misfortunes alike, and it never carried the modern sense of moral censure; a Roman who deplorabat something grieved for it, he did not judge it.

The journey through French into English. From Latin the word passed into the Romance vernaculars, and Old French adopted it as deplorer, "to lament, to bewail, to express grief." Middle English borrowed the verb in the fifteenth century as deploren, and for the next two centuries — the age of Shakespeare, of the Book of Common Prayer, of the great moral essayists — it remained essentially a verb of weeping and mourning. In Shakespeare's English one could deplore a death or deplore a loss, and the word sat naturally beside lament, bewail, and bemoan, all of them verbs of grief. The emotion is vividly attested in the religious prose of the period, where deploring sin meant grieving over it in tears rather than pronouncing sentence upon it.

The great semantic shift: from weeping to censure. The decisive change came gradually across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Because deploring was so often directed at misfortunes that were also blameworthy — at wars, cruelties, follies, and sins — the grief in the word slowly gathered a moral judgment. Writers who deplored something began, by a nearly invisible drift, to mean not merely "I mourn it" but "I mourn it and I condemn it." By the nineteenth century the balance had tipped: the modern sense of strong disapproval had become primary, and the old sense of pure lamentation had retreated into the poetic and the archaic. The word itself, however, retained its emotional interior — modern English "deplore" is not a cold cognitive verdict like "disapprove" but a warm, sorrowful censure, and the etymology explains why: the judgment is still carried on a current of tears.

Literary and cultural attestations. The transitional blending of the two senses is well attested in Victorian biography and criticism — precisely the register of the source sentence about Mill's references to his wife, where "however much we may deplore the exaggeration" mixes regret, censure, and charity in one concessive gesture. In twentieth- and twenty-first-century public language the word became institutionalised in the official register: United Nations resolutions, government statements, corporate apologies, and editorials all "deplore" acts of violence, violations of rights, and breaches of standards, while the adjective deplorable ("deserving strong censure") followed the same path from "worthy of tears" through "pitiable" to "shamefully bad."

Sound and form. Phonologically the word has changed little: Latin dēplōrāre became Old French deplorer, Middle English deploren, and modern [dɪˈplɔː], with the English stress pattern and vowel reductions typical of Romance loans. The morphology remains transparent: de- (intensive) + plor- (weep) + verbal ending, the same plor- that gives us implore ("to call upon with tears, to entreat") and, by a debated route, explore. The word thus preserves, in its very skeleton, the ancient picture of grief pouring out like water — a picture that the modern sense of formal disapproval has almost, but not quite, washed away.
