PIE *ǵʰel- 'to shine, glow, shimmer' (reconstructed; the exact shape of the initial consonant is debated, and *gʰel- is sometimes preferred, but the root consistently denotes light) [note: see debate below] → Proto-Germanic *glasą 'glass' (reconstructed; the shiny, translucent material, so called because it glistens; the derivational route from the shine-root is broadly accepted but its precise morphology is opaque) → Old English glæs 'glass' (attested from 8th–9th c. glosses; a neuter a-stem) → Middle English glas 'glass' (attested from the 12th c.) → Middle English glasen (verb) 'to furnish with glass, to make glassy' (attested late 14th c., c. 1374; a denominal verb built on glas with the infinitive suffix -en) → Early Modern English glaze (verb) 'to set or cover with glass; to make smooth and glossy' (attested 15th c.; the medial -s- was lost in pronunciation and respelling, and the noun glaze 'a glassy coating' appears from the 15th c.) → English glaze 'to cover with a vitreous coating' (pottery and culinary senses, 16th–17th c.), then intransitive 'of the eyes, to become fixed and glassy' (to glaze over, 19th c.) → GLAZE DEBATE / NUANCE: The Germanic word for glass, *glasą, is usually derived from the PIE shine-root *ǵʰel-, the same root that gives glow, gleam, glimmer, and glare. The difficulty is formal: the expected treatment of a PIE palatal *ǵ before l is not always regular in Germanic, so some scholars prefer to compare the word with a Germanic-internal formation from *ǵʰel- 'to shine' via a suffix, while a minority treat the root of glass as independent and unanalysable beyond Germanic. The overall lineage 'shine → glass → glasen → glaze' is secure from the 14th century onward and is not in dispute.
