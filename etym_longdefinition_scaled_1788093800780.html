Scaled, in the sense of the source context, is the past tense and past participle of the verb scale: to climb up, over, or across a height — a wall, cliff, or mountainside — by direct ascent, often with ladders, footholds, or ropes ("Goblins had scaled the Mountain from the other side"). The verb's core image is of ascending as if by a ladder or a series of steps; it is the formal and rhetorical partner of "climbed" whenever the climb is steep, deliberate, dangerous, or impressive.

Polysemy — the three faces of "scaled": English scale is a notorious triple, and a complete definition must untangle it:

  • (1) To climb (the source sense): from Latin scala "ladder, flight of steps" — hence "scaling ladders" in siege warfare, "scaling the heights" of ambition or achievement, and mountaineering's "scaling Everest".
  • (2) To adjust proportionally ("scale up/down"): from the "scale" of a graduated ruler or balance — a ladder-like row of marks — so "scaled" in modern business, computing, and engineering means "resized in proportion" ("scaled infrastructure", "scaled-down production").
  • (3) Concerning the scales of fish and reptiles (the adjective "scaled" = "covered with scales"; the verb "to scale a fish" = to scrape its scales off): an entirely different word, from the Germanic root meaning "shell, husk, split" — English scealu and Old French escale.

The semantic bridges in detail: each homonym has its own history and its own bridge. Sense (1) crosses from Greek and Latin climbing verbs into the military ladder, then from fortifications to mountains, and finally upward into metaphor — "scaling the heights" of office, fame, or excellence, an ambition image with classical pedigree that Romantic culture made into a moral commonplace. Sense (2) grows from the physical object called a "scale" on instruments — the graduated row of marks that looks like a ladder — to the act of reading it, then to adjusting sizes by its ratios; by the twentieth century "scaling" is a standard operation of engineering, statistics, and economics. Sense (3) is unrelated: the fish-scale descends from shell and husk imagery, and "scaled" as "covered with scales" or "descaled" answers to a different word family altogether.

Grammar and register: "scaled" is the regular weak past and participle; used transitively it takes the height as direct object ("scaled the Mountain"), and it can stand passively ("the wall was scaled"). In register it is more formal and more heroic than "climbed": it suggests a serious, extended, or dangerous ascent, and it is the verb of record for siege ladders and for the first ascents of famous peaks. In the modern sentence it must be disambiguated from the homonymous "scaled" of fish and of proportions — a three-way ambiguity native speakers resolve from context in milliseconds, and a favourite subject of dictionaries and word-nerd commentary.
