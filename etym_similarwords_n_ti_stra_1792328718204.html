• Arthaśāstra: Similar because both represent classical Indian treatises on governance, administration, and political realism; differs because arthaśāstra focuses extensively on state wealth, economic infrastructure, taxation, and military mechanics, whereas nītiśāstra emphasizes the broader moral, pedagogical, diplomatic, and gnomic conduct of rulers and subjects.
  • Dharmaśāstra: Similar because both are foundational Sanskrit genres prescribing codes of human behavior and legal standards; differs because dharmaśāstra is fundamentally religious, ritualistic, and deontological (governing sacred law, caste duties, and cosmic righteousness), whereas nītiśāstra is secular, pragmatic, consequentialist, and political.
  • Daṇḍanīti: Similar because both pertain to statecraft and the enforcement of order; differs because daṇḍanīti specifically denotes the restrictive "policy of the rod/punishment"—the coercive punitive mechanism of sovereign law enforcement—whereas nītiśāstra encompasses the complete philosophy of governance, diplomacy, and wisdom.
  • Rājanīti: Similar because both concern politics and the administration of the realm; differs because rājanīti specifically designates the active practice or contemporary doctrine of royal politics and statecraft, while nītiśāstra denotes the formal textual canon and scientific literature expounding those principles.
  • Nītisāra: Similar because both designate texts expounding prudent governance and political policy; differs because nītisāra refers to a specific classical compendium (literally the "essence of polity", famously authored by Kāmandaka), whereas nītiśāstra is the broad, overarching category and genre designation.
  • Statecraft: Similar because both describe the art and skill of governing a state and conducting foreign affairs; differs because statecraft is a broad, universal English concept describing practical governing skill, whereas nītiśāstra is an ancient, codified Sanskrit textual genre embedded with distinctive concepts like the mandala and upāyas.
  • Realpolitik: Similar because both prioritize practical, pragmatic power politics and tangible outcomes over moral idealism; differs because Realpolitik is a nineteenth-century European concept rooted in secular nationalism and power balances, while nītiśāstra operates within a classical Hindu cosmological framework that ultimately seeks the preservation of social order (lokasaṅgraha).
  • Mirror for Princes: Similar because both denote a medieval and classical genre of advisory literature written to instruct future monarchs in virtous and effective rule; differs because mirror for princes is an umbrella term encompassing Western and Islamic advisory texts (such as Machiavelli's Il Principe or Nizam al-Mulk's Siyasatnama), whereas nītiśāstra represents the specific Indian Sanskrit textual lineage.
