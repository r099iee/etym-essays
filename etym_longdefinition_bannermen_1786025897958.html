BANNERMEN is the plural of bannerman — the armed followers, retainers, or vassal knights of a feudal lord who are bound to follow his banner into war and, by extension, any loyal band of supporters rallying to a cause or a leader. It is a transparent compound of banner (“a flag, standard, or ensign”) and men (“people, soldiers, followers”). In the sentence “The Tyrells must be taken in hand. Their bannermen are wavering,” the word denotes the sworn military followings of the great houses of the fantasy world of Westeros: the knights and lords sworn to House Tyrell whose loyalty is faltering — a classic use of the word in modern epic-fantasy fiction, popularized above all by A Song of Ice and Fire.

Primary senses.

  • Feudal military sense: vassals, knights, and men-at-arms who owe military service and rally under a lord’s banner; the force a lord raises when he “calls his banners”.
  • Collective body: “the bannermen of a house” — the aggregated armed following of a great lord, as a single body of men and of loyalty.
  • Historical standard-bearer: a bannerman as the man who carries the banner — an ensign or prominent officer bearing the lord’s colours into battle.

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Fantasy-literature jargon: sworn vassal houses; “the Tyrell bannermen”, “the bannermen of the North”, where the word names the houses and their armies bound by oath to a paramount lord.
  • Political / organizational metaphor: committed loyalists and faithful partisans of a party, faction, or corporation, rallying under its standard; a modern figurative extension of the feudal sense.
  • Manchu and Qing historical usage: the bannermen of the “Eight Banners” were the hereditary Manchu and allied troops of the Qing dynasty — a distinct historical sense arising from a different military organization of the same name.
  • Related but distinct form — banneret: a “knight banneret” was a knight entitled to carry a banner in the field; the word banneret is a different formation from bannerman, though both involve banners.

Nuances of usage. The word is effectively plural-oriented: English-speakers say “bannermen” (the body of followers) far more often than “a bannerman”. It stresses military loyalty under a specific flag rather than the land-tenure obligations of a vassal, and in modern English it has acquired a distinctly epic, medieval-fantasy flavour while remaining a perfectly legitimate, transparent compound.

Historical semantic bridge. Banner descends from Late Latin bandum (itself of Germanic origin), via Old French baniere, while men descends from the Germanic root behind Old English mann. The compound “men of the banner” forms naturally and was institutionalized by genre fiction, which revived and widened a feudal vocabulary that had largely passed out of daily use.
