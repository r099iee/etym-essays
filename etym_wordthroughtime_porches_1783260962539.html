- [Old French 'covered entrance'] - In its current form since Middle English (from Old French porche, from Latin porticus), 'porch' has meant a covered entrance to a building, at first often a grand, columned one.
- [Classical portico association] - Through the Renaissance and Augustan age the word carried the memory of the classical portico - the columned entryways of temples and fora - dignity and public architecture.
- [Domestic narrowing] - In modern usage the porch became chiefly domestic: the covered stoop, veranda or entry of a house - the place of rocking chairs and summer evenings, modest and homely.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'porches' are the homely, attached covered entrances of houses, while the grand architectural sense survives in 'portico', the learned descendant of the same Latin word.
