gravely (adverb) — in a serious, solemn, and dignified manner ("he spoke gravely"); and, in a distinct and important secondary use, to a severe, critical, or dangerous degree ("gravely ill," "gravely wounded," "gravely concerned"). The adverb is derived from the adjective grave ("serious, weighty, solemn") with the native English suffix -ly, and its two senses map exactly onto the adjective's own two great semantic strands: a manner sense describing how something is done, and a degree sense describing how serious or life-threatening something is. CORE SENSES
  • Manner sense: with serious dignity. The primary sense: doing or saying something in a grave manner — solemnly, earnestly, without levity. "He gravely shook his head"; "she gravely assured them." Here gravely describes the demeanour of the agent, the weighty seriousness of the moment.
  • Degree sense: to a critical degree. The secondary sense: to a severe, critical, or life-threatening extent. "Gravely ill," "gravely wounded," "gravely at risk" — the standard collocations of medicine, law, and journalism. Here gravely modifies the degree of a condition, not the manner of a person.
  • The unifying bridge: weight. Both senses rest on the core image of heaviness: from Latin gravis, "heavy, weighty," the word moved from physical weight to metaphorical weightiness — what is heavy presses down, demands attention, and is therefore serious; what is seriously heavy is critically dangerous. The adverb inherits this bridge intact.
SECONDARY AND MODERN MEANINGS AND USES
  • Collocational families. In modern English gravely has hardened into two recognizable collocational sets: the medical/emergency set (gravely ill, gravely injured, gravely wounded) and the moral/journalistic set (gravely concerned, gravely mistaken, gravely serious, gravely wrong). The degree sense dominates the first set, the manner sense the second.
  • Formal register. Gravely is a formal, literary adverb. In speech it is rarer than seriously; it appears in news reports, legal writing, obituaries, and high literary narrative, where it carries a weight of dignity that seriously has worn away.
  • Ironic or ironic-sounding use. Because the word is so formal, it can be used with comic or ironic gravity — a speaker gravely pronouncing something trivial — where the dignity of the word is deliberately disproportionate to the content. The source context (Macaulay) exploits exactly this: the able historian "gravely related" supernatural marvels, the word's formal earnestness clashing with the credulity of what is related.
  • Archaic-literal residue. The literal sense "heavily, weightily, in weight" survives only in archaic or poetic use; in ordinary English the physical sense has been entirely taken over by the metaphorical one.
  • Homograph caution. Gravely must not be confused with grave as a noun meaning "a burial pit" — that grave is a different word (Old English graef), and gravely meaning "seriously" has no connection with graves, being buried, or the graveyard; the visual and phonic similarity is a coincidence of the English spelling system.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE The adverb entered English in the sixteenth century as the regular -ly formation on the newly borrowed adjective grave, itself from Middle French grave, continuing Latin gravis "heavy, weighty." The semantic arc runs from physical heaviness through the moral metaphor (heavy matters are weighty matters) to degree-severity (a weighty matter is a dangerous one). Both modern senses of gravely — solemn demeanour and critical degree — are thus the same root metaphor viewed from two angles, and the word's formal dignity today still carries the memory of the Latin weight it once named.
