lassitude enters English as a learned borrowing that preserves an almost unbroken Latin shape, and its history illustrates how a physical word for tired muscles became a psychological and moral word for tired will. The ultimate root is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European base meaning to let go, slacken, release, which also yields words for late, let, lenient, and leisure. The semantic logic is transparent: what is slack is not taut, not braced, not ready; what is released droops. From this broad notion of looseness a specialized suffixed extension developed denoting slowness and weariness, the state of an organism whose tension has gone slack.

In Latin this became lassus, faint, tired, weary, one of the central fatigue adjectives alongside fessus and fatigatus. Lassus is not merely sleepy; in Caesar, Cicero, Vergil and Livy it describes soldiers whose strength fails after marching, hands that can no longer hold weapons, eyes that grow dim. From lassus Latin regularly formed an abstract noun in -tudo, as in magnitudo from magnus and solitudo from solus: lassitudo, lassitudinis, faintness, weariness, heaviness. Lewis and Short gloss it as the condition of being lassus, and citations show military and medical use: vires lassitudine deficiebant, strength failed through lassitude; nulla lassitudo impedire officium debet, no weariness ought to hinder duty. The suffix gives it a statesmanlike, almost clinical dignity; it is not a momentary yawn but a condition.

  • Transmission through French: Old and Middle French took lassitude directly from Latin as a bookish medical and moral term in the 14th century. Cotgrave and early French dictionaries define it as weariness and faintness. English borrowed it in the early 15th century in the same learned register, first in medical and devotional prose where bodily faintness and spiritual listlessness were closely linked.
  • Phonetic stability: Because it was borrowed through writing rather than popular speech, lassitude underwent almost no sound change. Latin lassitudo with stress on -tu- became French lassitude with final stress and silent e, then English lassitude with antepenultimate stress. The double -ss- remained voiceless, the -tude syllable retained its full vowel in careful speech, giving modern Received Pronunciation with a palatal glide. There was no popular vowel reduction or folk-etymological reshaping, which preserves its Latinate, clinical aura.
  • Semantic drift from body to mind: In 15th-17th century English lassitude is overwhelmingly physical: agues, fevers, wounds, marches produce lassitude. By the 18th century, sensibility literature extends it to nervous and mental exhaustion: the lassitude of grief, of boredom, of satiety. Victorian medicine then makes it a symptom: tropical lassitude, neurasthenic lassitude, the lassitude of anaemia. In the 20th century psychology and political commentary complete the metaphor: moral lassitude, cultural lassitude, voter lassitude, a society too sated or too disillusioned to act.
  • Literary attestations: Burton in The Anatomy of Melancholy lists lassitude among signs of melancholy. 18th-century travel writers describe the lassitude produced by heat in Bengal and the West Indies. Keats, Coleridge and Tennyson use it for poetic languor that is half pleasant, half ominous. Modern clinicians still use lassitude to distinguish a subjective lack of energy and motivation from objective muscular fatigue or somnolence, preserving its Latin breadth covering both body and mind.
  • Debate and nuance: The deeper Indo-European connection of lassus to the base meaning slacken is widely accepted following Pokorny and de Vaan, linking it to lenis mild, gentle and to Germanic late and let, but de Vaan notes the exact suffixal path through supposed led-to- remains reconstructed rather than directly attested. The Latin stages onward, however, are fully attested and undisputed.

Thus lassitude today carries a double charge absent from plain tiredness: medically it suggests systemic depletion rather than local muscle fatigue, and morally it suggests a failure of drive rather than a need for sleep. That is why the source sentence opposes excited activity to exhausted lassitude of satiety or peace: not sleep after labour but slackness after fulfilment, the organism released and therefore drooping, exactly as its Indo-European root predicted.
