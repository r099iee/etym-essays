Origins: the PIE root and its Germanic legacy. The ultimate source generally accepted by historical linguists is the PIE root *swer- "to whirl, swirl, turn round", a root that also lies behind Germanic verbs meaning "to rub, file, scour" (Old English sweorfan, Old Norse sverfa), where the abrasive action was evidently conceived as a rotary, grinding motion. The same root family produced a cluster of "whirl" words across the Germanic dialects, and it is from this Germanic stock — not from Latin or Greek — that English swirl descends.

The Scandinavian question. The OED and standard references treat the Middle English verb as probably of Scandinavian origin, since no Old English verb *swirwan is recorded and the closest attested relatives are Scandinavian and continental dialect forms: Norwegian svirla "to whirl around", Dutch zwirrelen, German dialect schwirlen. The precise route of borrowing is unknown; the word may have entered through Norse speech in northern England or through Dutch and continental trade contacts. This stage is therefore inferred from modern dialect cognates rather than from a written ancestor — an honest gap in the record, flagged rather than filled.

Middle English: first attestation. The verb appears in the early 15th century (c. 1400–1425) as swirlen / swyrlen "to whirl, eddy", and the noun swirl is recorded from the 1530s. In these first centuries the meaning was strictly physical: water swirling in an eddy, wind swirling dust and leaves. There is no trace yet of psychological or social uses.

Sixteenth to eighteenth centuries: consolidation. During the Early Modern period the verb acquires regular transitive force — "to cause to whirl" — and appears in accounts of navigation, weather and domestic life. It remains a plain, descriptive word: swirling tides, swirling smoke, swirling flour. Its register is neutral and demotic, at home in diaries, travel books and early natural history.

Romantic and Victorian expansion. The 18th- and 19th-century literary imagination seizes on swirl as a word for sublime, uncontrollable nature: swirling storms, floods and fires in Gothic and Romantic fiction, swirling mists in Victorian ghost stories, swirling crowds in urban novels. The participle swirled becomes a favourite of descriptive prose because it compresses motion, force and visual pattern into a single word.

The twentieth century: figurative leap and adjectival life.

  • Psychology and emotion: from the 1920s onwards, memories, fears, doubts and ideas "swirl" in the mind; the vortex becomes the standard image of mental turmoil.
  • Media and politics: rumours and speculation "swirl" around public figures; journalism adopts the verb for circulating but unverified information.
  • Design and craft: "swirled" patterns — marbled paper, swirled glass, marbled cake, latte art — enter everyday aesthetics; the participle now names a decorative result.

Modern profile. Today swirled is fully polysemous: physical fluid motion, meteorological and aquatic force, emotional and cognitive turbulence, circulating social talk, and ornamental pattern. Its tone has softened from menacing to often beautiful — the swirl of a dancer's skirt, the swirl of cream in coffee — while retaining an undertone of being caught in something larger than oneself. The word's journey, in short, is from plain physics to the psychology of chaos, and from the whirlpool to the whirl of modern life.
