- 14th c. - Middle English takes mail(e) from Old French maille: armor of interlocked rings; the chain-armorer took the open mesh of a net and forged it into links of metal.
- 14th-17th c. - mail is the standard word for ring-armor, the flexible fabric of knight and of war that moves and yields while still turning a blade.
- 18th c. - chain mail is coined as a pleonasm when "mail" alone had grown opaque to everyday speakers; the phrase is kept because it sounds specific.
- 19th-20th c. - mail broadens to mean any armor - overlapping plates, scales - and by metaphor any natural protective covering; a creature's scaly hide becomes its "mail," the armorer's craft borrowed into natural history.
- Contemporary - the armor-word now collides in spelling with mail the postal system (a Germanic word for a bag or wallet); the two are unrelated yet are felt as one word, which makes "mail" the armor sound faintly antique and medieval - a relic kept alive in period prose and metaphor.
