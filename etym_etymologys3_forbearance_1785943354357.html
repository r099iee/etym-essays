1. The Indo-European root: carrying
Forbearance descends from one of the most securely reconstructed roots of the entire Indo-European family: *bher-, "to carry, to bear." The root's reflexes stretch across the ancient world — Sanskrit bhárati "he carries," Avestan baraiti, Greek phérō "I carry" (whence English phosphorus, "the light-bearer," and metaphor, "carrying over"), Latin ferō "I carry, bear" (whence fertile, transfer, confer, conifer), and the Germanic verb *beraną that lies behind English bear, birth, burden, and bier. In the Germanic branch the root meant both "to carry" and "to give birth to," so that to bear covered everything from carrying a load to bearing a child, and this double life of the root is present beneath the later moral meanings of our word: bearing is, in the oldest sense, physical endurance of a weight.

2. Germanic and Old English: holding back
The Germanic peoples built a rich set of compounds on *beraną with the prefix for- (Proto-Germanic *fra-), which meant "away, off, refraining" — the same element in forgive, forbid, forsake. The compound *farbaraną, "to hold oneself back, to abstain, to endure," passed into Old English as forberan, attested from the earliest English records (c. 9th–10th century): to forberan was to refrain from an action, to abstain from food or pleasure, to endure hardship without giving way. The prefix carried the crucial nuance of voluntary non-action: forberan was not merely suffering passively but deliberately holding back from doing something one might do — fasting, forgiving, forbearing revenge. Old English writers used it of self-denial in the religious life and of the restraint a man shows when he does not strike or answer back.

3. The noun: from abstinence to patience
Middle English continued the verb as forberen, and in the 16th century English formed the abstract noun forbearance by adding the French-derived suffix -ance (as in endurance, appearance). Its earliest senses were "abstinence, the act of holding back," and "patient endurance" — the same two senses as the verb. In the moral and religious writing of the Early Modern period, forbearance became a fixed virtue-term: the quality of enduring provocation without retaliation, of leaving punishment to God, of long-suffering toward one's neighbors. The language of the sermon and the devotional treatise made forbearance a household word of Christian ethics, paired constantly with patience, mercy, and long-suffering; Milton uses it, Bunyan uses it, and the word sounds to modern ears, as it did then, of the New Testament's great exhortations to restraint.

4. Law and commerce: the meaning specializes
From the 17th century the word took on a second, specialized life in the law. Because forbearance meant "refraining from doing what one has a right to do," the legal draftsmen seized on it: in contract law, a creditor's forbearance to sue a debtor became itself a form of valuable consideration — the promise not to enforce a right, given in exchange for something in return. "Forbearance to sue" is today a technical term of contracts, and a creditor who agrees to wait is said to have granted forbearance. In the 19th and 20th centuries, with the rise of consumer finance and mortgages, the sense migrated into banking: a lender's "forbearance" is now the temporary postponement or reduction of payments granted to a borrower in distress, a formal relief program under which the lender agrees not to foreclose or collect for a defined period. This modern financial sense is a straight line from the Old English verb: to forbear is to hold back from collecting what you are owed.

5. The full arc
The word has therefore kept a single core meaning — voluntary restraint — while its register has moved from the physical ("holding back a load") through the moral ("patient self-control," 16th c.) to the institutional ("legal and financial forbearance," 17th–21st c.). It is a rare word in that its modern technical senses are not drifted away from the old sense but are the old sense, institutionalized: the bank that grants forbearance, the creditor who forbears to sue, and the saint who forbears revenge all do the same thing — they hold themselves back. The noun remains formal and dignified, at home in court documents, financial news, and sermons, and its quiet power lies in the image of the hand that is raised and then, deliberately, not struck.
