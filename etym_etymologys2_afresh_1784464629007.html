AFRESH is a late Middle English fused adverb built from two native English elements; the linear chains for each element follow in isolation. Chain 1 — the prefix a-: Old English on 'on, in' (also of 'of, from') → reduced Middle English prefix a- 'on, in, to' (as in asleep, aside, aloft; the same reduction in ago from Old English āgān) → the phrase of fresh / on fresh 'anew' (Middle English) → fused adverb afresh (c. 1440). Chain 2 — the adjective fresh: PIE origin uncertain and debated (no secure reconstruction; see note) → Proto-Germanic *friskaz 'fresh, lively, unspoiled' (secure at the Germanic stage, attested through cognates) → Old English fersc 'fresh; of water, not salt' (attested) → (reinforced after 1066 by Old French freis/fres 'fresh, cool, new') → Middle English fresh/fressh 'fresh, new' → phrase of fresh 'anew' → fused adverb AFRESH (c. 1440). Note on the debate: the PIE ancestry of Proto-Germanic *friskaz is genuinely uncertain. Some handbooks connect it to a root meaning 'to cut, divide' (with 'fresh' understood as 'newly cut'), others reconstruct a root for liveliness or moving air, and a minority deny any secure Indo-European cognates, treating the word as Germanic-internal or expressive. The Germanic and Old English stages, by contrast, are solidly attested. The a- prefix is a native English reduction of the preposition 'on/of'; no Romance whole-word borrowing is involved in the fusion, though Old French reinforced the form and currency of the adjective fresh itself.
