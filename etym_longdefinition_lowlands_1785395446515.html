LOWLANDS is the plural of LOWLAND, which denotes areas of country lying low in relation to the surrounding region — land that is flat or gently undulating, near sea level or in river basins, as opposed to the uplands and mountains that enclose it. The word is fundamentally comparative: a lowland is not low absolutely but low in relation to the surrounding country, so that the term is meaningless without its opposite, the highland. In The Hobbit, Bilbo, looking forward from the heights after the company leaves the Misty Mountains, "could see before him only ridges and slopes falling towards LOWLANDS and plains glimpsed occasionally between the trees": the lowlands are the terminal horizon of the descent — open, level, habitable country seen only in flashes between the trees, the absolute contrast to the mountains behind. The tone is distance and promise: the lowlands are what the travellers cannot yet reach, glimpsed and lost again behind the ridges, the first intimation that the journey will end in gentler country. The plural pictures broad expanses of sunken ground spreading toward a horizon held out of reach, a whole category of landscape glimpsed rather than entered, and the word's very calmness makes the mountains seem all the more harsh.

The word belongs to a systematic pair — highland and lowland — that maps whole regions rather than single features. Its most famous application is to Scotland, where the Lowlands (the anglicised, arable, commercially connected south and east) stand against the Highlands (the Gaelic, mountainous north and west); the plural form is therefore also a proper-name usage with a long social history. In geography and ecology the singular adjective and noun do systematic work: lowland rain forest, lowland rivers, lowland plains — land defined by low elevation, opposed to upland and mountain.

  • Sense 1 — low-lying country in general (primary topographical sense): areas of land lying low in relation to the surrounding region, typically flat or gently undulating, near sea level or in river basins — coastal lowlands, river lowlands, the lowland plains. The plural collects such regions into a single broad category of landscape.
  • Sense 2 — the Lowlands of Scotland (proper-name sense): with a capital, the Lowlands is the traditional name for central and southern Scotland, the low-lying, anglicised part of the country as against the Highlands. The bridge is the same elevation-comparison institutionalised in a national geography; the Lowlands vs. Highlands contrast also carries social, linguistic, and historical weight (the anglicised lowlands against the Gaelic highlands).
  • Sense 3 — the Low Countries (historical allied usage): the plural in the old geographic phrase "the Low Countries" (the Netherlands and Belgium) belongs to the same family — low-lying land of the North Sea coast; "lowland" and "low countries" are close cousins, though the phrase is now historical.
  • Sense 4 — ecological and scientific usage (modern technical sense): in ecology, biogeography, and soil science, "lowland" is a technical classifier: lowland rain forest, lowland forest, lowland river, lowland species — habitats defined by low altitude, warm and often humid, as opposed to montane and alpine zones. The bridge is the elevation-comparison given a systematic technical life.
  • Sense 5 — the adjectival modifier (grammatical extension): lowland functions both as a noun (the lowlands) and as an adjective (lowland areas, lowland rain forest); the adjective is the more common modern use, and the noun plural — as in The Hobbit — is the literary and geographic way of collecting all such country into a single horizon.

The semantic bridge in one paragraph: the word is a compound — low + land — and its meaning is the meaning of the two parts composed: land that lies low. English borrowed the adjective low in the twelfth century from Old Norse lágr (English having no native adjective of the root), and land is native from the beginnings of English; the compound lowland was formed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, chiefly in Scots, where it named the low country of Scotland against the Highlands. Every later sense — the national Lowlands, the ecological lowland, the literary plural — is the same elevation-comparison applied at larger and larger scale, until the plural comes to mean not one region but a whole category of habitable, sunken country seen from the heights.

Register, grammar, and collocations: lowland is a standard geographic term, used in science, travel, journalism, and literary prose without archaism; it belongs to the systematic pair highland / lowland, and it maps regions rather than single features. As a noun it is countable (a lowland, the lowlands) and the plural is regular; as an adjective it is invariable (lowland areas). Common collocations include lowland rain forest, lowland area, lowland rivers, coastal lowlands, lowland plains, the Lowlands of Scotland, and, historically, the Low Countries. In The Hobbit the plural participates in a descending landscape inventory — ridges, slopes, lowlands, plains — each term carrying the eye one stage further down; the lowlands are the last and gentlest stage, glimpsed between the trees, the level horizon the travellers are moving toward but cannot yet reach.
