- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Ancestor noun *gebed* strictly designates a spoken religious prayer, petition, or supplication to God, completely devoid of any physical or spherical meaning.
- Middle English (c. 13th–14th c.) - Through the Roman Catholic devotional practice of reciting the Rosary (*saying one's beads*), the physical pierced spheres on the counting cord absorb the name *bede*.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - The noun *bead* broadens to encompass any miniature decorative sphere of glass, amber, or jet, as well as droplets of dew or sweat; the derived adjective *beady* appears in the late 1590s meaning "covered with beads or resembling beads".
- 18th to 19th Century (Victorian Narrative Fiction) - The idiomatic collocation *beady eyes* explodes across Victorian literature (Charles Dickens, Arthur Conan Doyle, Oscar Wilde), cementing its role as an iconic literary trope for sharp, cunning, rodent-like, or observant gaze.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains universal status in literary prose, zoological field guides, and clinical descriptions of sweat, while expanding in artisanal beverage culture to describe the bubbly foam ring (*beading*) on fine liquors.
