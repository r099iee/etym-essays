The little word mayest carries one of the longest continuous histories in English: five thousand years from a PIE verb of power to a wonder-tale horse's permission slip. Its stem has done nothing but soften — from strength to leave to mere possibility — while its ending has done nothing but age — from everyday grammar to story-time costume.

Power at the root. PIE magh-, to be able, to have power, is among the best-attested of roots, and its children are giants: English might and main (strength itself), Latin magis and magnus (greatness — whence majesty, magnify, master), Greek mēkhanē (a contrivance — whence machine), Persian magu- (the priest — whence magi and magic), and Slavic moga (I can). The Germanic branch built a preterite-present verb, maganan: a verb whose present tense looks like other verbs' past, a quirk may shares with can, shall, must, and ought — the whole modal family being, grammatically, old pasts doing present duty. Old English magan still meant straightforward ability: ic mæg is I can, I have the strength for it.

  • Sound shifts: PIE voiced aspirate gh became Germanic g by Grimm's law, giving mag-; Old English magan lost its infinitive ending, its vowel levelled toward a, and Middle English mowen shows the rounded vowel before the modern spelling may fixed the diphthong.
  • The can-may split: Old and Middle English used magan where modern English uses can; across the 1400s–1600s can (from cunnan, to know) captured ability while may specialized in permission and possibility — a division of modal labour still taught, and still broken, in classrooms today.

Permission, possibility, prayer. The slide from I am strong enough to go to I am allowed to go to it is possible that I go is one of language's easiest paths: social power becomes social leave becomes logical openness. By Chaucer's century may granted permission freely; by Shakespeare's it also blessed (may you prosper), purposed (that you may learn), and conceded (you may well say so). The King James translators used it thousands of times, and the prayer book's collects — that we may obtain, grant that we may — made it the very sound of asking heaven. The horse's speech in the source sentence stands squarely in this tradition: thou mayest sow is permission with the force of ordinance, the donor-figure's leave that is also the quest's law.

  • Literary peaks: from may it please your majesty to Keats's odes, the modal lent English its most graceful machinery of wishing; proverbs like may the best man win keep the optative alive in the most modern mouths.
  • Dialect and survival: Scots kept thou may and ye may longer than southern English kept thou mayest, and liturgy preserves the whole -est choir — mayest, mightest, wouldst, shouldst — in readings millions still hear weekly.

The tail that tells the time. The -est descends from the Old English second-person singular ending, worn through Middle English into the Early Modern thou hast, thou mayest, thou mightest system. As you replaced thou across the 1600s — politeness swallowing intimacy — the ending lost its only subject and became, within two generations, a deliberate antique. Translators of the Russian wonder tales seized it for exactly that patina: the talking horse must sound older than any living master, so he speaks the grammar of the English Bible, and thou mayest sow a measure of wheat lands with the weight of commandment. From a PIE strongman's boast to a fairy-tale horse's errand-list, the word has travelled from power to permission to poetry — and the journey, like the sowing and reaping it commands, is the whole of English modal history in six letters and a tail.
