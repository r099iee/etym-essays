The word is confined to North and West Germanic; the Proto-Germanic base *snag- "projecting point, knob" is of unclear pre-Germanic origin, likely an expressive (sound-symbolic) formation. → Old Norse snagi "projecting point; point of land" (also a projecting rock or headland) → Middle Low German snagge "branch, broken tooth" (parallel West Germanic form) → English SNAG (noun) "tree-stump or branch projecting from water; sharp projecting point" (borrowed c. 1570s, from the Scandinavian and Low German forms together) → within English: "sharp projecting point; obstruction" (17th c.); snag (verb) "run aground on a snag" (1807, American); "catch or tear on a snag" (1850s, snagged stocking); "hit a snag" idiom (19th c.); slang "seize, obtain" (1890s) Note on debated/nuanced points: The exact route of borrowing into English is uncertain — both Old Norse snagi and Middle Low German snagge are plausible donors, and the OED leaves the immediate source open. No PIE cognate has been demonstrated; the family is treated by the OED as Germanic-only, and speculative links (e.g., to English "snake" or to a supposed sn- "nose" formation) are rejected.
