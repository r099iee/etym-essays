sawmill — "He works in a sawmill," the passage says, and the noun sets the child's world among mechanical labour. A sawmill is a factory where logs are sawn into boards — a mill driven by water, steam, or engine power — and in the frontier economy it was the town's engine: the building where forest became lumber, where saws screamed, dust hung in the air, and tired hands were fed into blades. The tone here is blunt and unromantic: "sawmill" is not a picturesque word but a working one, the name of a place that processes trees into timber, and its flatness is the point — the child's labour is presented as raw industrial fact, one factory among others in the landscape.

Primary sense. A sawmill is an establishment in which logs are sawed into planks, boards, or timber, using power-driven saws — historically water-, steam-, or engine-driven, and today electric. The word emphasizes the process (the sawing) rather than the product, and it names both the building and the machinery within it. In the frontier and colonial economy the sawmill was often the first industrial building of a settlement, converting the surrounding forest into the lumber that built the town.

Secondary senses and the mill family. "Sawmill" belongs to the great family of mills: gristmill (grain), cotton mill, paper mill, flour mill, steel mill — a "mill" being any powered factory that processes material, from the Latin and Old English word for the grain-grinding machine. The compound "sawmill" fuses the tool (saw) with the factory (mill): the place where the saw is powered. Near-synonyms and neighbours include lumber mill and timber mill (emphasizing the product), a lumberyard or timber yard (the open storage ground, no sawing), and the older sawpit (the pre-industrial hand-sawing pit with two men, one above and one below). In some modern industrial usage "sawmill" is also loosely used for the sawing workshop of a timber complex, and the word extends figuratively to any place or process that "cuts things to size" — a school of hard knocks, a rigorous workplace.

  • Register: industrial and matter-of-fact — the vocabulary of work itself; collocations "sawmill town," "sawmill district," "sawmill hands," "a day in the sawmill," "work in a sawmill."
  • Pairing in the passage: the sawmill is deliberately paired with the "pesthouse" — the boy moves between the place that cuts living wood and the place that holds the dying, two engines of destruction, one mechanical, one biological, both named with the same flat factuality.

diphtheria — The "diphtheria pesthouse" is a place of quarantine and death. Diphtheria is a highly contagious bacterial infection caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, characterized by a sore throat, fever, and — classically — a thick, grey, leathery membrane that forms over the back of the throat and can obstruct the airway; the bacterial toxin can also damage the heart and nerves. Before antitoxin and vaccination, diphtheria was one of the most feared killers of children.

Primary sense (the disease). In medicine, "diphtheria" names the infection itself, its membrane, and its complications: the grey "false membrane" of dead tissue, the obstruction of breathing ("croup-like"), the toxin's effects on myocardium and nerves. It is a word of quarantine, isolation, and epidemic control — the "diphtheria pesthouse" of the passage is the isolation ward where the sick were confined, and where, before antitoxin, many died.

Secondary senses and extensions. The word's etymology — from Greek diphthéra "leather, hide" — names the disease by its leathery throat-membrane, and that image carries into all its uses: "diphtheritic membrane," "diphtheria antitoxin" (developed in the 1890s by Behring and Kitasato), "diphtheria vaccine" (the toxoid of the 1920s), and the modern "DPT" (diphtheria–pertussis–tetanus) immunization. Historically the word evokes the great pre-vaccine scares — the plagues of children's throats, the pesthouses, the sudden suffocation — and in modern texts it is largely a historical word, kept alive by the old fear and by the continuing need for vaccination campaigns.

  • Register: medical, technical, and historical; collocations "diphtheria membrane," "diphtheria antitoxin," "diphtheria vaccine," "a diphtheria outbreak," "the diphtheria pesthouse."
  • Contrast in the passage: sawmill (the mechanical destruction of wood) and diphtheria (the biological destruction of children) are the two poles of the child's world — work and sickness, labour and death.

Summary of polysemy. SAWMILL: (1) a factory where logs are sawn into boards; (2) the machinery itself; (3) loosely, any sawing workshop or "cutting-to-size" process. DIPHTHERIA: (1) the contagious bacterial disease; (2) its leathery membrane, which gives the disease its name; (3) the historical terror, the pesthouse, the quarantine; (4) the vaccine-era memory of a preventable child-killer. The bridge in each word runs through the same raw image — a blade for the sawmill, a leathery membrane for diphtheria — both named with a single, flat, terrible fact.
