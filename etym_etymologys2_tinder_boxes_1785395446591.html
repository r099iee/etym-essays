Derivation chain for tinder-boxes (a compound of two roots, tinder + box, presented in isolation): CHAIN FOR THE FIRST ROOT, tinder: 1. Proto-Germanic *tundr- 'tinder, combustible material' (reconstructed from Old English tynder, Old Norse tundr, Old High German zuntra; the noun is built on the Germanic verb-base *tund- 'to kindle, set fire to', seen in Gothic tundnan 'to be kindled' and Old Norse tenda 'to kindle'; no secure PIE etymon is agreed - the ultimate origin of the base *tund- is uncertain). 2. Old English tynder / tinder 'tinder, fuel, flammable material for catching a spark' (8th-11th c.; attested in the Old English corpus as the word for the dry material that catches a spark). 3. Middle English tinder / tynder 'tinder' (12th-15th c.; unchanged in sense). 4. Modern English tinder (16th c. onward) - the first element of the compound; also the modern app Tinder, a trade-name built on the ancient word, whose 'matches' metaphor keeps the old fire imagery alive. CHAIN FOR THE SECOND ROOT, box: 1. Greek pyxos 'box-tree, Buxus sempervirens' (attested from the 5th c. BCE; of uncertain, possibly non-Indo-European origin - no secure PIE etymology is agreed, and a Mediterranean or other loanword source is suspected). 2. Greek pyxis (genitive pyxidos) 'a box, a case, originally one of boxwood' (attested from the 5th c. BCE; used of cosmetic, perfume, and ballot boxes; the source of Latin and English words). 3. Latin buxis / pyxis 'a box' (borrowed from Greek in the Republican period; the spelling buxis with the Italic b- is the source of the Western forms), with the accusative buxida 'box' in Late Latin. 4. Old English box 'a box, a receptacle' (borrowed in the Anglo-Saxon period directly from Late Latin buxis; attested from the 8th-9th c.). 5. Middle English box 'a box, chest, case' (12th-15th c.). 6. Modern English box, plural boxes (regular -es plural) - the second element of the compound. THE COMPOUND: 7. The compound tinder-box, plural tinder-boxes (hyphenated form attested from the late 17th c.; the solid spelling tinderbox from the 19th c.; the metaphorical 'volatile situation' sense recorded from the 19th c.). Note on the debate: the Germanic base *tund- 'to kindle' has no securely reconstructed PIE ancestor; various connections have been proposed, but none commands general assent, and the OED treats the origin as uncertain. The Greek pyxos is likewise of uncertain, possibly substrate origin. Neither uncertainty affects the attested chains above, which rest on well-documented Germanic, Greek, Latin, and English forms.
