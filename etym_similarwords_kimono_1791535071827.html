• robe: Similar because both denote a long, loose, flowing outer garment that wraps around the body; differs because 'robe' is a broad, generic Western term encompassing academic gowns, bathrobes, judge's vestments, and ceremonial dress, lacking the kimono's rigid T-shaped geometric construction, flat-folded seams, and cultural requirements.
  • yukata: Similar because both are traditional Japanese T-shaped wrapped robes cut to the same fundamental silhouette; differs because a yukata is specifically an unlined, casual summer garment made of breathable cotton, traditionally worn after bathing or during summer street festivals, whereas a formal kimono is crafted from layered silk, lined, and worn for formal ceremonial occasions.
  • dressing gown: Similar because both are loose, wrapped garments worn casually for warmth and comfort; differs because a dressing gown is exclusively Western loungewear worn over nightclothes inside the domestic sphere, whereas a traditional kimono is formal outdoor attire and high textile art.
  • haori: Similar because both are traditional Japanese silk garments worn as part of formal attire; differs because a haori is a thigh-length or hip-length jacket worn open over the kimono like a coat, fastened loosely at the chest with small braided cords (haori himo) rather than a wide wrapped obi sash.
  • caftan: Similar because both are long, loose-fitting, flowing robes with wide sleeves originating in non-Western traditions; differs because a caftan (kaftan) originates in ancient Mesopotamia and the Middle East, typically pulls over the head or buttons down the front center, and is cut with flared side gores rather than straight-line Japanese panels.
  • tunic: Similar because both are traditional garments hanging loosely from the shoulders down to the thighs or ankles; differs because a tunic is an ancient Mediterranean and European garment, usually sleeveless or short-sleeved, pulled over the head and belted at the waist, lacking overlapping front panels and wide rectangular sleeves.
  • housecoat: Similar because both are floor-length, comfortable wrap garments designed for relaxed wear; differs because a housecoat is utilitarian Western everyday domestic loungewear fastened with snaps, zippers, or a simple cloth belt, designed for household chores rather than ceremonial presentation.
  • pelisse: Similar because both are long, outer garments worn over other clothing; differs because a pelisse is a historic European fur-trimmed cloak or long-sleeved fitted winter coat fashionable in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, featuring tailored European shoulders and armholes.
