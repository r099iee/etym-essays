The present participle of to stray: wandering away from a proper, intended, or familiar path, place, or group — and, in its oldest application, an animal leaving an enclosure, herd, or pasture without an owner's guidance. In the Elves of Rivendell's song in The Hobbit, chapter III, "Your ponies are straying!" turns the word to gentle, singsong mockery. The company's ponies, exhausted and untethered after the descent from the Misty Mountains, have begun drifting off into the soft meadows in search of grass, and the Elves announce the fact with mischievous delight. The nuance is crucial: STRAYING here is not a grim disaster but a light, chiding warning — the animals are slipping away almost absent-mindedly, and so, the song implies, are the weary dwarves themselves, in danger of losing the road, their mounts, and their dignity at once. The word's tone is teasing, affectionate, and faintly archaic, perfectly fitted to Elvish song and to the homespun practical care beneath it.

Set beside its near-synonyms, STRAYING differs from wandering (aimless movement without any sense of being lost or out of bounds), from roaming and roving (wide, deliberate movement), from drifting (passive, directionless motion, often figurative), and from errant (a formal word meaning "wandering, wayward," from Latin errare). STRAYING keeps the specific sense of crossing a boundary: a stray is a beast that has wandered from its lawful place, and to be astray is to be lost from the right road.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings

  • Moral and religious deviation: since the Middle English period "to stray" could mean to depart from the path of justice, truth, or faith; "straying" is still a conventional euphemism for marital infidelity, and "straying from the truth" for error or dishonesty.
  • Stray as noun: a homeless or lost domestic animal — "a stray" (dog, cat), impounded as a "stray" in the pound; in medieval law an estray was an ownerless beast that had wandered from its lawful pasture and reverted to the crown or lord.
  • Stray as adjective: isolated, separated from the main body — a stray bullet, a stray hair, a stray thought, a stray current or stray capacitance (an unwanted electrical effect in electronics), stray light (optics), stray radiation.
  • Navigation and tracking: straying off course, strays from a route, radar blips that wander from the expected pattern.
  • Astray: the frozen compound (originally "o strai," literally "on stray") survives in "to go astray" and "to lead astray," keeping both the lost-cattle and the moral senses.
  • Figurative drift: of attention, thoughts, or memory — "her mind was straying" — passive, gentle movement away from focus, very common in modern prose.

All senses share one geometry: movement beyond a limit. Whether the boundary is a pasture fence, the right road, the truth, or the focus of attention, to stray is to cross it unintentionally — which is why the Elves' warning is at once physical and gently moral.
