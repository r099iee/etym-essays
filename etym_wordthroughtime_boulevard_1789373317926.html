- 15th to 16th Century - Borrowed into Middle French from Middle Dutch *bolwerc*; strictly designated a military fortification, artillery bastion, or timber rampart defending fortified towns.
- Late 17th Century - King Louis XIV ordered the demolition of Paris's defensive fortifications in 1670, replacing the ramparts with wide, tree-lined promenades called *boulevards*; societal perception shifted dramatically from martial defense to aristocratic leisure and carriage promenading.
- 18th Century - The *Grands Boulevards* became the dynamic social, commercial, and theatrical epicenter of Paris; the Boulevard du Temple gave rise to *théâtre de boulevard* (popular farces and crime melodramas).
- Mid-19th Century (Haussmannization) - Baron Haussmann revolutionized Paris by driving massive, straight, tree-canopied boulevards (such as Boulevard Malesherbes) through congested medieval neighborhoods, establishing the global archetype of modern urban grandeur.
- Late 19th to Early 20th Century - Coined the social archetype of the *boulevardier* (the cosmopolitan gentleman-flâneur); English borrowed the word extensively to designate grand, prestigious avenues in booming metropolitan centers across the globe.
- 21st Century - Retains formal classification in transportation planning as a multi-way landscaped arterial street, while continuing to evoke European charm, outdoor dining, and architectural majesty.
