- Latin — evadere means to go out or escape, feet finding the way out of danger, physical and neutral.
- Late Latin and French — evasio and évasion name the escape itself, still mostly of bodies from prisons and battles.
- 1720s — English coins evasive for answers that escape like prisoners, the dodge moves from legs to language.
- 1800s — evasively spreads through novels and newspapers for glances, smiles, and replies that will not meet the eye.
- 1900s — politics and war adopt it, evasive action for pilots and evasive answers for ministers, respectability and suspicion in one word.
- Today — Frodo’s use keeps the humane shade alive, evasion as loyalty under pressure, while headlines keep the harsher shade of stonewalling.
