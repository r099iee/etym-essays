- [15th century: "one who does good deeds"] - Borrowed from Late Latin; a moral, religious register — doing good for its own sake, the good deed itself being the point.
- [16th–18th centuries: the civic benefactor] - Moves into institutional life: founders and endowers of schools, colleges and almshouses; "benefactors' rolls" turn the plural into a public honorific category of civic memory.
- [19th century: the great philanthropist] - Industrial magnates saluted as "benefactors of mankind"; the word gains grandeur — and, among the skeptical, a whiff of self-congratulation.
- [20th century: irony and institutions] - Anonymous benefactors and fundraising tiers institutionalize the term; literature discovers its ironic edge — Tolkien's mock "roll of the benefactors" names a hero whose deed brought only dragon-fire and ruin.
- [Contemporary: dignified and old-fashioned] - Fundraising prefers "donor" and "patron"; "benefactor" survives for large, remembered, often posthumous generosity.
