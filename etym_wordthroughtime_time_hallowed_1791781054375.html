- 18th–19th c. — Enters literary English as a compound of reverence: customs and buildings are said to be hallowed by time, a favourite note of antiquarian and Romantic prose.
- 19th c. — Victorian heritage writing and nationalist rhetoric make it a badge of continuity; abbeys, charters and ceremonies are routinely “time-hallowed”.
- 20th c. — Political speeches use it to defend institutions, while skeptical journalism turns it against them: “time-hallowed inefficiency”, “time-hallowed excuses”.
- 21st c. — Chiefly a heritage and editorial word, often gently ironic; it now signals attitude toward the past rather than any specific belief.
