Mayhaps is a small masterpiece of English word-building: a whole sentence squeezed into an adverb. Its history is the history of three elements — the modal may, the noun hap, and the adverbial -s — each with its own ancient pedigree, joined by the ordinary processes of phrase-contraction and analogy. And its story is a reminder that the most archaic-sounding English words are often the most transparently constructed. Element one: may — a thousand-year-old modal The first syllable descends from Old English magan, "to be able, to have power, to avail", whose present tense mæg is the direct ancestor of modern may. The verb reaches back to Proto-Germanic *maganą and beyond that to the Proto-Indo-European root *magʰ-, "to be able, to have power" — the same root that appears, through Greek mēkhanē and Latin machina, in English machine. From its earliest attestations magan/mæg carries both ability ("I can") and possibility ("it may be"), and the possibility sense is the one at work in mayhaps: "it may happen". The modal is thus not merely a grammatical auxiliary but the bearer of the word's whole meaning of uncertainty. Element two: hap — a Viking gift The second element, hap, is a Scandinavian loan. Middle English took hap, "chance, fortune, luck", from Old Norse happ, which in the North meant both "chance" and "good luck" — the Norse sense preserved in the derived English words hap (chance), hapless (luckless), haphazard (governed by chance), and, most famously, happen (to come to pass) and perhaps (by chance). The deeper etymology of happ is debated: it is traditionally connected with the Proto-Indo-European root *kob-, "to suit, fit, succeed", the idea being that "luck" is what happens fittingly or successfully — the reconstruction is repeated in the handbooks but rests on slender Germanic evidence, and some authorities treat the word's ultimate origin as uncertain. What is not in doubt is that hap was a living, everyday English noun from the thirteenth century to the sixteenth, the era in which mayhaps was formed. Element three: the contraction and the suffix The phrase "it may hap", "it may happen", is exactly the kind of colloquial formula that languages turn into single words. By the sixteenth century the phrase had contracted into the adverb mayhap: the first attestations date from the 1500s, in exactly the rustic, proverbial register in which such contractions thrive. A generation or two later the language added the finishing touch. English had long formed adverbs by appending an -s — needs, unawares, sometimes, and above all perhaps itself, which is "by haps" with an adverbial -s hardened into one word. On the model of perhaps, the late-sixteenth and seventeenth centuries produced mayhaps, and the doublet mayhap/mayhaps entered the language side by side. Social and literary afterlife The word never entered the mainstream of educated prose, and that is precisely what preserved it. While perhaps marched forward as the standard adverb, mayhap and mayhaps stayed in the country dialects of England and Scotland and in the literature that copied them. The nineteenth-century novelists of provincial life — the writers of dialect dialogue from the Brontës to Thomas Hardy — kept the word alive on the page, and the antiquarian taste of the age gave it a rustic, nostalgic charm. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word was adopted with enthusiasm by fantasy and historical fiction, where archaic diction is a tool of world-building: mayhaps instantly places a speaker in a pre-modern, folk-tale, or courtly register, and it has become one of the signature archaic adverbs of the genre. Its use in modern speech, when it occurs, is playful or mock-archaic — a wink at the old world. Linguistic notes
  • Mayhaps is a compound of a modal verb, a noun, and a suffix — a phrase, not a root, made into a word.
  • The final -s is analogical (modelled on perhaps), not grammatical.
  • Mayhap (without -s) remains a fully acceptable variant; mayhaps is the more rustic-sounding form.
  • The word is often grouped with peradventure and perchance; both are Latinate, while mayhaps is purely Germanic and Scandinavian — the most "native" of all the archaic adverbs of possibility.
  • Historically the word competed with the older happenly and the regional happily (in its now archaic sense "perhaps"); only the may-forms survived.
