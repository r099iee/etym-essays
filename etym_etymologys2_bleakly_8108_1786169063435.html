PIE *bʰel- (to shine, gleam, burn; to be bright, white) — earliest known stage
├── Extended PIE base *bʰleik- (*bʰel- with a -k- extension: to shine white, to become pale) — the stage behind the Germanic color words; a minority reconstruction writes the extension as *bʰelg-
│ └── Proto-Germanic: *blaikaz (pale, white, shining, bleached)
│ ├── Old Norse: bleikr (pale, white, wan) — the direct source of English bleak, borrowed in the 16th century
│ │ └── Middle English: bleik / bleke (pale)
│ │ └── English: bleak (16th c., originally a colour word: pale, wan, bloodless)
│ │ └── English: bleak + -ly → bleakly (adverb) ◄ TARGET
│ └── Old English: blāc (pale, shining) and blǣcan (to make pale) — native Germanic cognates, the source of English bleach and bleakness' native twin
├── Possible distant cognate: Sanskrit bhrājate (it shines) — sometimes cited, but Sanskrit bhrāj- is usually assigned to the different root *bʰreg- (to gleam), so the connection is debated
└── Note: The chain in English is a borrowing, not a direct inheritance: bleak entered the language from Old Norse bleikr in the 16th century, while the native English reflex of the same Germanic root survives in blāc / bleach. The pre-Germanic extension (whether *bʰleik- or *bʰelg-) and the Sanskrit relationship are points of scholarly debate; the Germanic stage *blaikaz and its color meaning are secure.
