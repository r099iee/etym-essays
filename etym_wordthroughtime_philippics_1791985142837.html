- 1590s — English borrows philippic from French, first as a name for the speeches of Demosthenes and Cicero.
- 17th c. — the common noun is established for any fierce public denunciation; the plural denotes a set of such speeches.
- 18th c. — parliamentary and pamphlet warfare make the word a favourite for sustained political attack.
- 19th c. — Victorian reviewers use it for long moralising assaults on books, manners and ministers; the classical reference fades.
- 20th c. — the word becomes rarer and more bookish, often displaced by diatribe and tirade.
- 21st c. — survives as an elevated or ironic term for a furious speech, with capitalised Philippics reserved for the Ciceronian corpus.
