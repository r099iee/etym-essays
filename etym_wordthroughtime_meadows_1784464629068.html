“Meadow” entered English from its Old English ancestor meaning mown or low grassland — land kept for hay, land that is mown — the word itself tied to the cutting of grass.
“Meadow” through the centuries settled as the standard word for low-lying, lush grassland by streams or below arable slopes, kept for hay or pasture rather than ploughing.
“Meadow” carried the pastoral image through English poetry — the meadow of green peace, flowers, and grazing — a word of settled rural comfort.
“Meadows” today still means that green belt of hay and pasture between garden and river — soft, open, low-lying grassland, the very middle distance of a hobbit’s view from his round windows.
