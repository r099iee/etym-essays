WEARY is an adjective and a verb describing the deepest form of tiredness: exhaustion of the body, the mind, or the spirit that comes from prolonged exertion, hardship, strain, or repetition. As an adjective it describes both the person who is exhausted (a weary traveller) and the thing that exhausts (a weary climb). As a verb it means to exhaust someone or grow exhausted (the long wait wearied them). Crucially, weariness is more than tiredness: it is a heavy, lingering, cumulative depletion that a nap cannot cure, and it often carries an emotional or moral overtone — weariness of the world, of oneself, of endless struggle. In the source passage the bare sentence "He was weary" gathers all this weight into three words: the man is worn down to the bone, past the point of ordinary tiredness.

The word is native to English, descending from Old English wērig "weary, exhausted, dispirited," attested from the earliest Anglo-Saxon poetry — in Beowulf and The Wanderer weary warriors, exiles, and seafarers are the archetypes of the word. Its deep Germanic ancestor is the reconstructed adjective *wōrigaz "tired, worn out," whose ultimate Indo-European root is debated but often connected to a root meaning "to tread down, exhaust, or injure." The word has stayed at the emotional centre of English for over a thousand years without any break in its line of descent.

Primary and Secondary Senses

  • 1. Physically exhausted (adjective, primary sense). Worn out by labour, travel, sleeplessness, or bodily strain: "the weary soldiers limped home." This is the earliest and most literal sense.
  • 2. Mentally and emotionally depleted (adjective, secondary sense). Exhausted in spirit by grief, anxiety, care, or chronic stress: "a weary heart." From Old English poetry onward the word has carried this melancholy, spiritual weight.
  • 3. Impatient, bored, or sick of something (adjective + of). "Weary of X" means tired of X through over-exposure or repetition: "the public grew weary of empty promises." This sense shades from exhaustion into irritation and disillusionment.
  • 4. Causing tiredness (adjective, attributive). Applied to tasks, journeys, and waits that produce weariness in those who endure them: "the weary miles."
  • 5. To exhaust (transitive verb). "the long argument wearied everyone."
  • 6. To become exhausted (intransitive verb). "her patience wearied." The verb has grown rarer in modern English but survives in literary use and in the participle wearying.

Metaphoric Shifts and Semantic Bridges

The history of weary is a steady inward migration of meaning. In Old English the word described bodies beaten down by the harsh northern world — sea-voyages, battle, exile, travel on foot. By Middle English it had extended to the mind and heart, and in religious and courtly texts the weary soul burdened by sin and the weary lover sick with longing became standard images. The Early Modern period added the transitive verb and the "weary of X" construction, turning exhaustion from a state into a judgement — one can now be weary of something, tired through of a person, a cause, a fashion. In the modern language the psychological sense has overtaken the purely physical: "weary" is far more likely to describe a jaded public or a heartsick idealist than a tired body, and the word has become a staple of existential and political vocabulary, as in world-weary and battle-weary.

Register and Tone

  • Register: Literary and poetic but not archaic; the word is perfectly at home in ordinary speech and has been for a millennium.
  • Tone: Melancholy and grave. Weariness implies suffering, burden, or disillusionment in a way that tired and fatigued do not.
  • Compound forms: world-weary, battle-weary, war-weary, weary-hearted, and the Old English mōd-wērig, "weary in spirit."
  • Nominal and adverbial forms: weariness (the state), wearily (the adverb), and the participial adjectives wearying and wearisome (causing weariness).
