malignant:
- 16th c. - English takes malignant from Middle French: 'ill-disposed, spiteful', of persons and dispositions - evil by nature rather than by act, born bad rather than choosing bad.
- 16th–17th c. - the medical turn: a 'malignant fever' resists treatment and spreads; the word begins its long career as the opposite of benign.
- Late 17th c. onward - the tumor word: malignant growths invade surrounding tissue and recur; the sense becomes clinical, precise, and technical.
- Contemporary - it lives split between the medical opposite of benign and the general term for inborn, organic, spreading evil - Burroughs's 'malignant column' is a tumor of the air, willing evil that grows as it pursues.

swelling:
- Old English onward - swellan, of rivers rising, wounds inflaming, sails filling with wind; from the first an internal, intransitive growth of substance from within under pressure.
- Middle English - the participle swelling becomes an adjective for anything in the act of inflating; the rising body of water, flesh, or sail.
- 19th c. - the noun takes a social turn: a 'swell' is a person puffed up with fashion and importance; the word's oldest logic shows through - to swell is to become more than one was.
- Contemporary - used of tides, floods, tumors, and storms; in the passage it catches the zoorth mid-inflation, a boil of the sky filling itself as it rolls across the plain.

discerned:
- 14th c. - English takes discern from Old French and Latin discernere, 'to sift apart'; the original force is active separation by close examination.
- 15th–17th c. - it settles into the language of perception: to distinguish one thing from another, to separate the true from the false by judgment.
- 18th–19th c. - it becomes the refined word for perceptive effort: to discern a faint thing through distance, clutter, or darkness.
- Contemporary - the word of active sifting, separating signal from noise and motion from stillness - precisely the labor of the earthmen scanning a lifeless land for the first sign of animation.
