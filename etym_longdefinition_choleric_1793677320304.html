choleric (adjective) describes a person, temperament, or emotional state marked by an extraordinary susceptibility to anger, irascibility, and sudden, fierce outbursts of hot-tempered wrath. A choleric individual is quick to take offense, vehement in asserting authority, and prone to volatile indignation, often exhibiting an energetic, domineering, and combative demeanor that makes peaceful deliberation difficult. Historically, the word is not merely a synonym for bad-tempered; it is a foundational clinical and philosophical concept deriving directly from ancient and medieval humoral medicine, where it designated an individual whose physical constitution, emotional dispositions, and bodily health were governed by a predominance of yellow bile (choler).

In classical humoral pathology, formulated by Hippocrates and systematically codified by Galen of Pergamon, the human body was maintained by the dynamic equilibrium of four cardinal humors: blood (the sanguine humor), phlegm (the phlegmatic humor), black bile (the melancholic humor), and yellow bile (the choleric humor). The choleric humor was defined by two primary elementary qualities: heat and dryness. Because heat corresponds to activity and dryness to sharpness and division, the choleric complexion was intimately associated with the element of fire, the scorching season of summer, and the astrological patronage of the warlike planet Mars. Individuals of a choleric temperament were believed to be physically lean, wiry, and dark-haired, with a sallow or yellowish skin tone, rapid pulse, and sharp, piercing eyes. Mentally and behaviorally, they were recognized as courageous, ambitious, incisive, and natural leaders, yet tragically burdened with a critical lack of patience, a short temper, and an alarming propensity for vindictive rage when crossed.

The term displays significant polysemy across medical, literary, psychological, and figurative domains, unfolding into several distinct nuances and specialized applications:

  • Primary Characterological & Psychological Sense (Hot-Tempered and Irascible): The predominant contemporary usage designating someone who has a hair-trigger temper and flashes into immediate, aggressive fury. Unlike the sullen, brooding resentment of the melancholic or the cold, bitter malice of the splenetic, the choleric person's anger is acute, active, vocal, and explosive.
  • Historical Humoral & Physiological Sense: Pertaining to, generated by, or characterized by the cardinal humor yellow bile (choler). In historical medical treatises, physical ailments such as burning fevers, dry coughs, ulcers, jaundice, and violent internal heat were diagnosed as "choleric distempers" caused by an excess or burning (adustion) of yellow bile.
  • Archaic Medical Pathology (Relating to Cholera): Historically used to describe the acute diarrheal disease cholera, or gastrointestinal afflictions marked by severe vomiting, dehydration, and bilious discharge (frequently termed choleric flux or choleric diarrhea). This medical usage preserves the ancient connection between bilious evacuation and intestinal disorder.
  • Figurative, Sensory & Metaphoric Sense (Fiery, Pungent, or Inflammatory): Extended metaphorically to inanimate objects, environments, foodstuffs, and discourse that possess sharp, burning, or irritating characteristics. In early culinary and dietetic medicine, foods heavily seasoned with mustard, pepper, garlic, and hot spices were classified as "choleric meats" because they were believed to excite yellow bile and inflame the blood. In rhetoric, a "choleric style" denotes polemical, stinging, vitriolic prose designed to incite indignation.
  • Dramatic & Literary Archetype: In classical, Elizabethan, and Jacobean drama, the choleric man serves as a quintessential stock character and tragic foil—such as William Shakespeare's depictions of the impetuous Hotspur in Henry IV, the fiery Tybalt in Romeo and Juliet, or King Lear's uncontrolled, storm-like tempers—representing the catastrophic dangers of unbridled passion unmoderated by reason.

The semantic bridge connecting the physical secretion of bile to the psychological state of fury lies in ancient psychosomatic observation. When human beings experience sudden, violent indignation or stress, physiological responses occur—including facial flushing, accelerated heartbeat, gastrointestinal churning, and the release of bitter digestive secretions. Classical physicians, observing these visceral phenomena without modern biochemical tools, inferred that yellow bile was the causal agent of wrath rather than a collateral symptom. Consequently, an excess of yellow bile was held to ignite the passions, giving rise to the semantic shift from a concrete bodily fluid to an ingrained personality type, which persisted in language long after cellular biology replaced humoralism.
