Gnawed is the past tense and past participle of the verb to gnaw: to bite at something repeatedly and persistently, scraping or wearing it away with the teeth, or to corrode and erode something gradually by any biting, eating action. The word applies chiefly to animals and vermin — a dog gnawing a bone, a rat gnawing through a sack, a squirrel gnawing bark, beavers gnawing down trees — but it extends, by a deeply natural metaphor, to intangible processes: gnawing hunger, gnawing doubt, a gnawing pain that eats at the sufferer from within, a guilt or regret that gnaws at the conscience. Unlike a single decisive bite, to gnaw implies sustained, repetitive, patient attrition: the teeth working at a surface until matter is actually removed, leaving grooves, splinters and hollows behind. It is a word of destruction by degrees rather than by a single blow, and that slow, corrosive quality is precisely what the metaphor of mental distress seizes on. The participle gnawed (as in "gnawed mutton") reports the finished result of such attrition — a surface visibly tooth-scored, worn, and partly eaten away. The sense in Tolkien's The Hobbit, Chapter 2. The phrase "the spilled jugs, and the gnawed mutton" is the detail through which Bilbo, clinging in the tree above the trolls' camp, first grasps the full depravity of what has happened. The trolls have not simply cooked their stolen mutton — they have torn and worried it like beasts while drinking, so that the remains of the sheep lie half-eaten, tooth-scored and abandoned in the dirt. The participle gnawed therefore does double duty in Tolkien's prose: it records, factually, that the mutton has been partly eaten, but it also quietly characterises the eaters. Gnawing is what animals do, not what cooks do, and the word implants the trolls' bestiality in the reader's mind a full paragraph before the sacks begin to descend over the dwarves' heads. The spilled jugs and the gnawed mutton are the trolls' fingerprints — the evidence of a violent, slovenly, greedy presence. Primary senses of gnawed / gnaw
  • Past tense and past participle of gnaw (the card's core sense) — bitten, chewed, or scraped away persistently with the teeth; worn down by repeated biting.
  • Physical, of animals — to bite at a hard surface (bone, wood, bark, rope, a sack) repeatedly until matter is removed; also of beavers and rodents felling or damaging trees.
  • Of erosion and corrosion — to eat into, wear away, or consume gradually by any biting or eating action, including rust, acid, or water eating at a surface.
  • Figurative, of emotions and physical distress — to torment, distress, or afflict persistently: hunger, doubt, fear, guilt, remorse, or pain "gnaw" at a person, as if teeth were eating from within; the participial adjective gnawing is fixed in this role (gnawing hunger, gnawing pain).
  • Of conscience and anxiety — to trouble or plague unremittingly, the same corrosion applied to the mind: a gnawing sense of wrong.
Polysemy: how the senses developed
  • The physical sense is native and very old: Old English gnagan meant to bite gradually, to fret, to eat into — a word of everyday animal life from the earliest records.
  • The corrosive sense is a natural extension of the same image: anything that eats away at a surface — rust, acid, water, time — came to be said to gnaw, because the physical process is identical to the tooth-scraping of a rodent.
  • The figurative sense is the word's richest modern resource. From the sixteenth century onward hunger, care, doubt, fear, guilt and pain have been said to gnaw at a person from within: the emotional suffering is pictured as a persistent, inward tooth, and gnawing became the stock adjective of that inner erosion. The bridge is precisely the patient, unrelenting quality of the physical act.
Distinctions from near-synonyms Gnawed should be distinguished from its near neighbours. Chewed implies deliberate mastication with the back teeth; nibbled is small, neat, tentative biting; munched is noisy, comfortable eating; bitten is a single decisive snap; worried (of an animal) is restless tearing and shaking of prey; scraped and grazed are abrasion without teeth; eroded and corroded are the passive, impersonal forms of the wearing-away sense. Gnawing alone combines persistent repetition with actual removal of matter, and only gnaw carries the modern metaphor of inner, mental corrosion.
