No secure PIE root; the word is imitative / expressive in origin. - Imitative base *jēmar- / *jam- "to lament, wail, make clamorous noise" (expressive; no reliable PIE ancestor) → Proto-Germanic *jēmaraz "sorrowful, miserable, lamentable" → Old English geōmor "sorrowful, distressed, miserable" → Old English geōmrian "to lament, mourn, complain, groan" (attested) → Middle English yemeren / yeomeren "to mourn, complain" (15th c.) → (converges with the borrowed Continental form) Middle Low German jammeren "to wail, grieve, lament" → Middle English yameren "to lament, wail, shriek" (borrowed late 15th c., as in Caxton) → Modern English YAMMER "to wail, then to cry out noisily; to chatter on complainingly" (sense "loud, annoying noise" from the 1510s). Note on debate: the word has two converging streams — the native Old English geōmrian line and the borrowed Continental jammeren line (Middle Low German / Middle Dutch jammeren, Old High German jāmar "misery", modern German Jammer "lamentation" and jammern "to wail"). The two forms blended in late Middle English. The ultimate root is imitative/expressive rather than inherited: no PIE reconstruction is accepted, and the repeated *m* and open vowels are widely judged to mimic the drawn-out, wailing sound. The German compound Katzenjammer "wailing of cats" was borrowed into English in 1821.
