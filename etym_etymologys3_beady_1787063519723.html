An adjective born of the prayer bead

Beady is a straightforward English formation — the noun bead plus the adjectival suffix -y — first recorded in the 16th century with the meaning covered with beads or resembling beads. Its own history is short and regular; the entire interest of the word lies in the spectacular history of the noun it is built on.

Bead was once a prayer

Old English gebed (with the West Germanic prefix ga-) meant prayer, supplication. It descends from Proto-Germanic *gabedą, built on *bedą prayer, request, a root shared with the verb *bidjaną to ask, entreat, pray — the same root that survives in modern English bid and beg. The pre-Germanic origin is debated. The standard guess links *bedą to a PIE base *bʰedʰ- to bend, on the theory that prayer was accompanied by bowing the body; but the equation is not universally accepted, and it must not be confused with the homophonous PIE root *bʰedʰ- to dig, pierce (the source of Latin fodio to dig). The safer statement is that the Germanic prayer word is solidly attested while its Indo-European ancestry is uncertain.

The medieval metonymy

In the later Middle Ages prayer was counted with strings of small balls — the rosary. Each ball stood for one prayer, and the Old English word for prayer was transferred to the object by metonymy. To tell one's beads (14th century) meant to count one's prayers on the string; because each ball represented a single prayer, the ball itself became a bead. Once the transfer was complete, bead simply meant a small round perforated object, and the spiritual sense quietly withdrew from the common word. The shift is one of the most famous metonymies in English — an object named from its function, then named for its shape.

Beady arrives, and finds eyes

Given a noun meaning small round object, the adjective beady emerged naturally in the 16th century meaning bead-like or covered with beads. For roughly three centuries it kept near its literal senses: beaded ornament, bead-shaped drops of liquid, globular fruit or berries. The decisive development came in the late 19th century, when beady eyes was first applied to small, round, glittering eyes and, through them, to sharp, greedy, watchful people. The collocation was an instant success and has dominated the word's life ever since — Dickensian misers, hawk-eyed spinsters, and watchful thrushes all have beady eyes. In the reading context the old thrush is exactly this: a small bird with tiny, bright, alert eyes, head cocked to scan the ground.

Modern register

Today almost all use of beady is the collocation beady eyes (with its derivative beady-eyed). The phrase implies alertness touched with cunning, greed, or suspicion when applied to people, and simple accurate description when applied to birds and small mammals. The literal covered-with-beads sense survives mainly in historical costume writing and beadwork craft talk. The whole career of the word — from a devout prayer counted on a string, to a little ball, to a greedy glittering stare — is a compact case study in how an ordinary suffixation rides on the back of a spectacular noun history.
