THE EVOLUTION ESSAY OF PARABLES 1. THE ROOT OF THROWING 'Parable' is a Greek word born of a Greek habit of thinking by comparison, and its deep source is the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷelH-, 'to throw, strike, hurl,' the same root that stands behind Greek ballō, 'I throw.' The root itself did not mean 'to teach' or 'to speak'; it was a word of physical motion, of casting, hurling, and striking. From ballō Greek formed the noun bolē, 'a throw, a casting,' and it was this noun that served as the second element of an enormous family of compounds — each one a word that pictures something being 'thrown' in a particular direction. To throw beyond was hyperbolē, our 'hyperbole,' an exaggeration that overshoots the truth; to throw across or over was metabolē, 'a change,' giving our 'metabolism'; to throw together was symbolon, 'a token, a sign,' giving our 'symbol'; to throw forward was problēma, 'a thing thrown before one, a task,' giving our 'problem'; to throw across maliciously, 'to slander,' was diaballein, whose agent noun diabolos, 'the slanderer,' became our 'devil.' The whole family is a monument to the Greek genius for metaphor — for picturing abstract relations as acts of throwing. 2. THROWING BESIDE: PARABALLEIN AND THE RHETORICAL PARABOLĒ The particular compound that concerns us is paraballein, 'to throw beside, to set alongside, to compare,' formed with the preposition para-, 'beside.' Its noun, parabolē, 'a placing beside,' became in Greek rhetoric a technical term for an illustration that sets a familiar scene alongside a truth in order to illuminate it — an extended simile or likeness used in argument and teaching. Aristotle used the word for an illustrative parallel; the Greek orators and philosophers used parabolic comparisons to make abstract points concrete. The rhetorical parabolē is not yet a story in our sense but a comparison drawn out at length — the seed that falls on stony ground, the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to seek the one — a familiar experience set beside an unfamiliar truth so that the one sheds light on the other. This was the rhetorical tool ready to hand when the Greek-speaking world needed a word for a revolutionary new kind of teaching. 3. THE NEW TESTAMENT: THE PARABLE AS TEACHING STORY The Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures (the Septuagint) had used parabolē to render the Hebrew mashal — a word covering proverb, riddle, and wisdom comparison — and this prepared the word for its decisive development in the New Testament. The Gospels use parabolē for the teaching stories of Jesus: the sower and the seed, the mustard seed, the lost sheep, the prodigal son, the good Samaritan, the talents. These are realistic, human, everyday scenes — a farmer sowing, a woman sweeping, a father welcoming a son — set beside a spiritual truth about the Kingdom of God. The Gospels explicitly mark the form: Jesus 'spake many things unto them in parables,' and the evangelists comment that without a parable he spoke not unto them, so that the word became the name of a recognizable genre of teaching, a story whose meaning lies hidden in a likeness and demands interpretation. It is from this scriptural use that the modern sense of 'parable' — a short instructive story — is directly fixed: the rhetorical comparison of the Greeks became, in the Gospels, a narrative form, and that narrative form carried the word into every language of Christendom. 4. THE LATIN SIDE-STREAM: PARABOLA AS 'WORD' While the Greek religious sense was entering Latin as parabola — a term of rhetoric for a comparison, then a proverb or saying — Latin also pushed the word in a startling new direction. In Vulgar and Late Latin, parabola broadened from 'a saying' to 'speech, word, talk' generally, and it is this form that produced the Romance words for 'word': Italian parola, French parole, Spanish palabra, Portuguese palavra. Through Old French, English received parole, 'word of honour,' and its modern legal sense of conditional release. This side-stream is a reminder of how unpredictably words develop: the same Greek noun for 'a placing beside' that gave us the sacred story also gave half of Europe its ordinary word for 'a word.' It does not lie on the direct ancestor line of English 'parable' — the English word preserves the religious sense through Old French parabolé — but it shows the full breadth of the family's fortune. 5. THE MATHEMATICAL SIDE-STREAM: PARABOLA THE CONIC Nor was geometry exempt. The Greek geometer Apollonius of Perga (third century BC), in his treatise on conic sections, applied parabolē to one of the curves — the section whose curve stands, as it were, 'thrown beside' its diameter, with the property that each point is equally distant from a focus and a directrix. The learned Latin parabola and its English descendant parabola (attested in English from the seventeenth century) preserve this mathematical sense, a collateral member of the same family as 'parable.' The two words are near-doublets: 'parable' and 'parabola' are the same Greek word travelling to English by different routes — one through the sacred and rhetorical tradition, the other through the mathematical — and their coexistence in English is a striking accident of word history. 6. THE WORD IN ENGLISH: THIRTEEN CENTURIES OF THE INSTRUCTIVE STORY English borrowed 'parable' from Old French in the thirteenth century, and the religious sense has dominated from the first attestations: the Middle English homilies and the Wycliffite Bible use it for the Gospel stories. In the centuries that followed, the word served the pulpit, the commentary, and the devotional life as the standard term for Jesus' teaching stories and for any story told to point a moral. By the early modern period it had also broadened into general literary and moral use: a parable could be any instructive tale, and 'to speak in parables' meant to speak cryptically or allusively, hinting at a meaning the hearer must supply. The word's register remained dignified and instructive — the language of the sermon, the morality, and the essay. 7. THE MODERN WORD: LESSON, OBJECT-LESSON, AND GIBBON'S USE In the eighteenth century and since, 'parable' has continued in both its strict biblical sense and its broad instructive sense, and it has taken on the looser modern meaning of an object-lesson: a person's life, a historical episode, a natural event can be 'a parable of' ambition, vanity, decay, or redemption. It is in this broad sense that Gibbon, in the sentence at issue, 'managed with deceptive ease to translate the catastrophes of nature into parables of human nature': the historian treats earthquakes, plagues, and storms not merely as physical events but as instructive tales about human conduct, each catastrophe a little moral text, together forming a scripture of human nature written in the language of natural disaster. The word carries into that sentence its whole history — the Greek rhetoric of setting a likeness beside a truth, the Gospel genre of the story that hides a lesson, the pulpit's two thousand years of telling 'a parable' — and Gibbon's use shows the form fully secularized, the religious genre turned into an instrument of historical irony. Across two and a half thousand years, from the throw of the Greek ballō to the divine catastrophes of the Roman Empire, the parable has remained exactly what its name says: something thrown beside, a story laid alongside a truth, so that the two, running parallel, illuminate each other.
