disinterested is a formal, bookish adjective meaning “free from self-interest, bias, or personal gain; impartial, unprejudiced, and directed by principle rather than by profit, ambition, or private advantage.” It is a strongly moral word: it praises not merely fairness of judgment but the absence of any personal stake. In the present passage, describing a scholar-philosopher who retired to Avignon — “the pleasant little house where the happiest years of his life had been spent in the companionship of his wife” — and “continued his disinterested labours,” the adjective functions as a quiet encomium on intellectual integrity. The labours are disinterested because they serve truth and the public good rather than the scholar's own wealth, fame, or advancement. The nuance is one of noble, selfless devotion, the exact quality a biographer would wish to attribute to a figure he admires.

Precisely because it belongs to elevated, moral discourse, disinterested is the centre of one of the most famous prescriptive battles in English usage. The strict, traditional sense is “impartial, free from bias” — the judge who has no stake in either party. The looser modern sense, “uninterested, bored, lacking curiosity,” is widely condemned by usage guides as a corruption. The distinction is genuinely functional: a person may be intensely curious about a subject (far from uninterested) and yet wholly disinterested in the outcome of a dispute because no personal advantage is at stake. The two words are not interchangeable.

  • Sense 1 (judicial / impartial): “free from bias or prejudice in weighing evidence or judging between parties”; the default sense in legal and formal contexts — a disinterested witness, disinterested arbitrator, disinterested tribunal.
  • Sense 2 (selfless / unselfish): “acting without thought of personal gain; not motivated by advantage” — disinterested service, disinterested kindness, and the “disinterested labours” of the scholar in the present context. This sense shades into generosity, altruism, and magnanimity.
  • Sense 3 (contested “bored” sense): from the early 20th century, “having no interest in, indifferent, uninterested” (“the audience remained disinterested”). Usage authorities (Fowler, the OED's usage notes, Garner) label this sense nonstandard or a confusion, because speakers re-parse the prefix dis- as a simple negation of “interested.”
  • Historical bridge: the word descends from Latin interest “it matters, it concerns, it is of importance” (third person of interesse, “to be between, to differ, to matter”). Because interest acquired the financial sense “money paid for the use of capital,” to be “interested” came to mean “having a stake or share in something.” The prefix dis- negates that stake: the dis-interested person is the one with no share, no stake, no axe to grind — hence impartial.
  • Derivatives: disinterestedly (adverb), disinterestedness (the abstract virtue of acting without regard to self-advantage, a key term of 18th–19th-century moral philosophy and of Victorian critical thought), and the back-formation disinterest (noun, 19th c.) “impartiality” — itself later blurred into “lack of interest, boredom.”
  • Domain jargon: in law, “disinterested” is technical (a disinterested trustee or disinterested director has no conflicting financial interest); in literary criticism, Matthew Arnold's ideal of critical disinterestedness meant seeing a work apart from political, religious, and personal bias; in science, the “disinterested pursuit of truth” names the ideal of objectivity free of funding or fame bias.
  • Register and connotation: formal, evaluative, largely written; in speech it is often replaced by impartial or unbiased to avoid ambiguity. Its connotation is consistently approving: it celebrates integrity, justice, detachment, and generosity, and its chief risk is the widespread confusion with uninterested.

Antonyms: interested (in the sense “having a stake”), biased, partial, prejudiced, self-interested, mercenary, venal, self-serving. A full treatment of disinterested therefore requires both the positive portrait — the judge, the trustee, the scholar labouring selflessly at Avignon — and the warning that the word must never be reduced to mere boredom.
