MARTYRED is the past participle and adjective of the verb "to martyr" — "killed because of their religious or other beliefs." In its primary, historical sense it describes a person put to death for refusing to renounce a faith, and by extension for refusing to abandon any conviction, cause or principle. The word occurs in the verse couplet about Lin Dai-yu in David Hawkes's translation of Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng (The Story of the Stone): "She had more chambers in her heart than the martyred Bi Gan; And suffered a tithe more pain in it than the beautiful Xi Shi." "Martyred" is the translator's adjective for Bi Gan, the legendary Shang-dynasty minister whose heart the tyrant King Zhou ordered cut out because Bi Gan alone dared to protest his excesses. In English the word carries the full weight of its Christian history: to be martyred is to die as a witness to the truth, killed for one's convictions, and to be vindicated by the very act of dying. The tone is tragic, sacred, and deliberately hyperbolic: the comparison claims that Dai-yu's inner life outdoes the greatest exemplars of suffering in Chinese legend — more "chambers" (the Chinese original speaks of "apertures," xin qiao, the channels of feeling) than the martyred minister's heart, and more pain than the beautiful Xi Shi suffered. By choosing "martyred" rather than "executed" or "slain," Hawkes imports a Judaeo-Christian aura of sacrificial witness into the Chinese allusion, making Dai-yu's grief not merely pitiable but quasi-holy — the suffering of a soul that testifies to its own truth.

The senses of MARTYRED, primary and secondary, must be enumerated exhaustively:
  • Religious sense (the oldest and core sense): killed because of one's religious faith — a saint, an apostle, a confessor who dies for Christ; historically the martyr dies as a witness to a faith, so the death is itself testimony.
  • Secular sense: killed because of one's political or moral beliefs — a martyred leader, a martyred reformer, a martyred journalist; the word extends to anyone put to death for a cause, whether or not the cause is religious.
  • Witness sense (etymological and theological): "martyr" literally means "witness" in Greek, so "martyred" retains the notion of dying as public testimony to a truth — the death declares what the life believed.
  • Figurative or hyperbolically emotional sense: "martyred" applied to someone who suffers greatly, self-sacrificingly or with an air of complaint — a "martyred expression," a "martyred sigh," the "martyr complex" of someone who exaggerates their suffering to win sympathy; this modern, often ironic use is a metaphoric shift from physical death to social or emotional self-punishment.
  • Passive-verb sense: "to be martyred" = to be put to death for one's beliefs; the adjective is also used predicatively of the manner of death ("he was martyred in 303 AD").
  • Extended/collective sense: "the martyred" as a class — the martyred faithful, a martyred nation, a martyred people; the word lends solemnity and moral vindication to any group that dies for a cause.

The semantic bridge from the ancient to the modern senses is the idea of dying for belief. The earliest Christian use of "martyr" meant only "witness" to the resurrection; in the persecutions of the second century AD the word fused witness with death — the martyr is the witness whose testimony is sealed in blood — and from there the concept widened across religions and causes until, in modern English, it can describe anyone killed for what they believe in, and finally anyone who suffers self-sacrificingly. The register ranges from the sacred (the martyred saint) through the tragic (the martyred reformer) to the ironic (the martyred look of a complaining spouse), and the context in Hong lou meng deliberately occupies the tragic-sacred register, comparing a fictional heroine's grief to a legendary minister's sacrificial death.

Register boundaries against the near-synonyms sharpen the word:
  • "Killed" and "put to death" are factual and neutral; they record the event without any of the meaning that martyrdom supplies.
  • "Executed" is legal and judicial — Bi Gan was indeed executed by royal decree — but it emphasises the criminal-justice frame and strips the death of witness and honour.
  • "Slaughtered" evokes animals or a battlefield, brutal and dehumanising, quite wrong for a minister's sacrificial death.
  • "Sacrificed" is the nearest competitor, for both words involve death for a higher cause; but "sacrifice" emphasises the offering and its utility — something given up for a purpose — while "martyrdom" emphasises testimony, the death as a public bearing of witness that outlives the victim.
  • "Persecuted" describes the suffering, not the death; one may be persecuted and survive, while "martyred" entails death.

In short, MARTYRED is a word of sacred testimony: it never merely reports a death, it interprets it — the death becomes a statement, and the victim is vindicated by the very act of dying. That is the full weight the translator loads onto a single adjective in the couplet about Dai-yu's heart.
