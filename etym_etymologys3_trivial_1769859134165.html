The evolution of TRIVIAL (two component roots)

Because trivial is a Latin compound, its full history must be told as two separate root narratives that meet at the Roman crossroad, together with the joint story of how the compound became a modern English adjective.

Component 1 — the numeral TRI-, from PIE *trei- "three"

The first component descends from the Proto-Indo-European numeral *trei-, "three." Numerals are among the most stable words in any language family, and *trei- survives recognizably in every branch of Indo-European: Vedic Sanskrit tri, Greek treis, Latin trēs, Gothic threis, Old English þrēo (modern three), and Old Church Slavonic trije. In Latin the combining form tri- was extraordinarily productive, generating words for things conceived of in threes: triplex "threefold," tribus "a third of the people" (hence "tribe"), triangulum "triangle," and — crucially for our word — trivium. The numeral component is therefore the simplest and oldest layer of the story, and it contributes only the idea of "three."

Component 2 — the road VIA, from PIE *wegh- "to convey, travel"

The second component descends from Proto-Indo-European *wegh-, "to convey, carry, travel, go." This is one of the most widespread roots in the family. In Germanic it produced Proto-Germanic *wegaz "way, path" — the source of English way — as well as *wagnaz "wagon" (Old English wægn) and the verb wegan "to carry, move, weigh," source of English weigh and weight. In Sanskrit it produced vahati, "he carries, conveys, drives," and vāhana, "vehicle" (modern Hindi vahan "vehicle"). In Greek it produced ochos "carriage" and ocheomai "to ride." But the branch that matters for trivial is Italic: Latin via, "way, road, path," from the feminine of an old participle meaning "being conveyed" — the road is literally "that over which one is carried." Latin via gave English a rich family of its own: viaduct, devious (off the road), obvious (in the way), previous (going before), and trivial itself.

The meeting at the crossroads: Latin trivium

At some point in early Latin, tri- "three" and via "road" combined to form trivium, literally "a place where three roads meet": a public crossroad or street corner. In the Roman world the crossroads was the natural gathering place of common people — travelers, market vendors, gossips, and idlers. Anything said at the crossroads was the cheapest and most public of information. From this noun Romans formed the adjective triviālis, "of the crossroads": common, ordinary, vulgar, belonging to the street. Quintilian uses it in the first century CE to dismiss what is trite and common. The sense development is exactly parallel to modern "street-level" knowledge or "watering-hole gossip" — the crossroads was where the undifferentiated public met, and what they said there was by definition unrefined.

A false friend with a real effect: the medieval trivium

In the Middle Ages the word acquired a second, unrelated life. When the universities organized the seven liberal arts, they divided them into the introductory trivium — grammar, rhetoric, and logic — and the advanced quadrivium — arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. This trivium (from tres "three" + via "way," "a threefold path of learning") is technically a different word from the "crossroads" trivium, but the two were constantly confused. The medieval curriculum word meant the elementary, foundational subjects every student passed through first. Because both words meant something low, common, or introductory, Latin scholarly Latin and then the vernaculars began to blur them: what was "of the trivium" was elementary, and what was "of the crossroads" was common — and both fed the emerging idea of "trivial" as minor, basic, and unremarkable.

Into English: sound and sense change

The adjective triviālis passed through Middle French trivial (15th century) into English at the end of the 16th century, first attested around 1589. English initial tri- kept its t and r, and the word was anglicized as /ˈtrɪvɪəl/, later /tɹˈɪvɪəl/ in RP, with the stress on the first syllable. Its early English meaning was "commonplace, ordinary, of the common sort" — the dictionary of the period glosses it as "vulgar, common." During the 17th and 18th centuries the sense drifted from "commonplace" to "of little value or importance": once a thing was no better than street-corner talk, it was easy to dismiss it as beneath serious attention. The 18th-century prose of the essayists and novelists fixed the modern pejorative sense, and by the 19th century "trivial" was a standard word for the minor, the lightweight, and the inconsequential.

Modern extensions

The 20th century added two notable technical and cultural extensions. In mathematics, logic, and computer science, "trivial" became a precise term for a solution or proof that is self-evident or degenerate — a "trivial solution" needs no effort to find, and its opposite is "non-trivial." In popular culture, the noun "trivia" was revived in the early 20th century from Latin trivia "crossroads" (literally "things found at the crossroads," i.e. bits of common knowledge), and the game "Trivial Pursuit" (1981) fixed "trivia" as the worldwide word for miscellaneous quiz facts. The word in Austen's predecessor texts and in the motorcar passage above — "a new thing is always interesting, in its trivial fashion" — stands exactly at the modern sense: charmingly unimportant, a small matter in a vast world, which is precisely what a creature who has lived 132 years would think of a brand-new motorcar. From the Roman crossroads to the trivia quiz, trivial has never stopped meaning, at bottom, "the sort of thing common folk talk about at the corner of three roads."

Summary

  • *trei- "three" → Latin tri-: supplies the "three" of the compound.
  • *wegh- "convey, travel" → Latin via "road": supplies the "road."
  • tri- + via = trivium "crossroads" → triviālis "of the crossroads, common."
  • Confusion with the academic trivium added the sense "elementary, basic."
  • French trivial → English trivial (c. 1589) "commonplace" → "of little importance."
  • Modern extensions: math/CS "trivial solution," and the noun "trivia."
