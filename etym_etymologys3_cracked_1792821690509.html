The history of cracked begins with a noise. Long before anyone wrote the word down, Germanic speakers were imitating the snap of breaking wood, bursting ice, and splitting stone with a syllable built around the hard consonants k-r-k, and that piece of sound symbolism became one of the most durable stems in the language family.

Germanic thunder: The reconstructed Proto-Germanic stem *krak- named the sharp report itself, and its daughters kept the sound faithfully: Middle Dutch kraken, Old High German krahhon, Old Norse kraka all crackle with the same consonants. Old English cracian (9th c.) meant to resound or crack, and it already carried a boastful overtone — to crack was to make a noise about things, whence the later sense of boasting and the Scots survival crack meaning chat or news. The participle cracked is simply that noise frozen into a state: the snap is over, the line remains.

Middle English breakage: By the 14th century Middle English craken had widened from noise to fracture — to break with a snap — and cracked described timber, ice, pottery, and masonwork split but standing. Cookery and husbandry adopted it early: cracked grain, cracked hides, cracked hooves. The great semantic engine was the vessel metaphor already forming: a cracked pot leaks, a cracked bell cannot ring true, and by analogy whatever is flawed is cracked, whether wall, voice, or wit.

  • Voices and bells (15th–16th c.): writers describe cracked voices at puberty and in age, cracked bells in half-ruined towers; Shakespeare's age is full of them, and the acoustic sense — purity spoiled by a flaw — becomes the template for every later moral and mental extension.
  • Skulls and sense (17th c.): crack-brained, cracked pate, and crackpot pour the metaphor into the head; a 1670s dictionary already glosses a cracked fellow as half-mad. The image is the leaking vessel: reason, like water or sound, escapes through the fissure, and Tolkien's rather cracked Bilbo is the direct, softened heir of this slang.
  • Safes, jokes, and bottles (18th–19th c.): the criminal underworld cracks safes and cribs while polite society cracks jokes and bottles; both extend the snap — forced entry and sudden wit — and industrial milling gives the world cracked wheat and cracked corn as honest coarse-ground staples.
  • Petroleum and ciphers (20th c.): thermal cracking of oil (1913, Burton process; catalytic cracking in the 1930s) industrialises the snap at the molecular scale, while two world wars make codebreakers of scholars: cracked ciphers at Bletchley Park turn the word into the emblem of Allied ingenuity, later inherited by hackers cracking software.

Sound symbolism triumphant: scholars of phonosemantics point to crack as a textbook example of a word that never needed a respectable PIE pedigree because its meaning lives in its sound; children invent it independently, and every Germanic language kept it without borrowing. That is why the etymology handbooks mark the deeper origin as imitative and leave it there — the k-r snap is the etymology, renewed every time ice breaks underfoot.

Literary crowning: from Shakespeare's cracked voices through Dickensian cracked old clerks to Tolkien's rather cracked Bilbo and the modern cracked screen glowing in every pocket, the participle has stayed intimate and slightly comic. Things that are cracked still serve — the cup still pours, the bell still rings, the hobbit still returns with pockets full of stories — and the word's long history is really the biography of damage survived with character intact.
