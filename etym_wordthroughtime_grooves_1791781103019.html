- 14th c. — “grove” names a furrow or ditch; the word is agricultural and topographic, with no hint of machinery.
- 16th–17th c. — the spelling settles as “groove” and the sense narrows to a channel cut in wood, stone or metal to receive another part.
- 19th c. — industrial precision multiplies applications (shafts, pulleys, rails), and the first figurative “groove of habit” appears.
- 1877–1900 — the phonograph turns the groove into a carrier of sound, making “the grooves” a household metonym for recorded music.
- 1930s — jazz adopts “in the groove” for a performance running freely; “groovy” follows as a term of approval.
- 1960s–70s — funk and soul criticism establish “the groove” as a technical-aesthetic term for cyclic rhythmic feel.
- 1980s–present — the older metaphor of entrenched routine revives (“stuck in a groove,” “the grooves of thought”), so the word now carries both warm musical praise and mild contempt for repetition.
