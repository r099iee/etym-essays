- Old English 'ford' - an ancient Germanic noun for the shallow place where a stream could be crossed on foot; in a land without bridges, the ford was a daily feature of roads and travel, fixed into place-names like Oxford, Stratford, and Hereford.
- Middle English - the verb 'to ford' is formed from the noun and stays a workaday verb: armies, traders, and pilgrims ford rivers; the ford is a recognized point of passage on every route.
- 18th-19th century - as turnpikes, bridges, and ferries multiply, fording recedes from ordinary travel, and the verb begins to sound rustic, historical, or colonial — the term of pioneers and wilderness narrative rather than of the modern road.
- Contemporary - 'forded' is archaic and consciously evocative: it belongs to adventure, saga, and historical prose, its lingering weight carried by the old names in the landscape that keep the memory of the shallow crossing alive.
