finches is the plural of finch, the English name for a family of small, seed-eating songbirds (the Fringillidae), characterised by stout conical beaks — powerful little seed-crackers — and, in most species, bright, cheerful song. The core English finches are the birds whose names carry the word itself: the chaffinch (Fringilla coelebs), goldfinch (Carduelis carduelis), greenfinch (Chloris chloris), bullfinch (Pyrrhula pyrrhula), and hawfinch (Coccothraustes coccothraustes), together with the wide clan of cardueline finches: linnets, siskins, redpolls, crossbills, and canaries. In looser usage the name extends to any small finch-like seed-eater, from the house finches and purple finches of North America to the famous “Darwin's finches” of the Galápagos — which, to the delight of taxonomists, turned out not to be finches at all.

Primary sense — the family and its look. Ornithologically, the “true finches” are the Fringillidae: small to medium passerines with deep, conical bills specialised for husking seeds, and — in many European species — strong wing-bars and red or yellow patterning. The chaffinch is the archetype: one of the commonest and most beloved birds of Britain, named in the very earliest English glossaries. The goldfinch, with its crimson face and golden wing-bar, has been kept as a cage-bird since antiquity and appears in Renaissance religious painting as a symbol of the Passion. The greenfinch is the sturdy olive-green garden finch; the bullfinch, with its plush pink breast and black cap, is named for its thick-set, “bull-necked” head; and the hawfinch, England's largest finch, crushes cherry stones and hawthorn kernels in a bill of extraordinary power. The plural form finches is the natural collective of these birds: flocks of finches working a stubble field, a finch table at a bird feeder, a whole winter finch irruption from the north.

  • Sense 2 — loose colloquial extension. In everyday speech “finch” extends to many small seed-eating birds outside the family: the American house finch and purple finch are in fact cardueline kin of the European finches, while sparrows and buntings were loosely grouped with finches by country people for centuries. The boundary only became sharp when modern taxonomy moved the buntings into their own family (the Emberizidae) and, in the late twentieth century, revealed via DNA analysis that “Darwin's finches” are tanagers — a scientifically explosive misnomer that the world has declined to correct.
  • Sense 3 — “Darwin's finches”. Since the term was coined in 1936 for the Galápagos birds Darwin collected in 1835, “Darwin's finches” has been a fixed collocation and the classic textbook example of adaptive radiation: fourteen species descended from a single colonising ancestor, their beaks specialised for seeds, insects, cactus, and even blood (the sharp-beaked ground finch drinks the blood of seabirds). The finch that cannot be told apart from a finch — the icon of evolution itself — is the word's most famous modern career.
  • Sense 4 — the word in name-history and idiom. The English finch names preserve a small museum of medieval observation: chaffinch is the “chaff-finch”, the bird that picks chaff at the threshing floor; goldfinch from its golden wing-bars; greenfinch from its olive plumage; hawfinch from the “haws”, the hedge fruits whose stones it cracks; bullfinch from its bull-headed stoutness; linnet from the flax (lin) whose seeds it eats. And “finch” itself is almost certainly echoic — formed from the bird's sharp “fink” call — making it one of the oldest bird words in the language, a name given straight from ear to mouth.

Usage. “Finches” is everyday, standard English in birdwatching, farming, and poetry alike; it names both the birds themselves and, in finch counts and finch tables, the guild of small dry-seed eaters. Its tone is warm, homely, and precise — a rarity among bird names in being at once scientifically exact and rooted in village speech.
