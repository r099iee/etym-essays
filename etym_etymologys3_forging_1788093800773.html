1. Latin foundations. The chain begins with Latin faber, "workman, artisan, smith", attested from the third century BCE in Plautus's comedies, where the faber appears among the ordinary tradesmen of Roman life. Its deeper ancestry is genuinely uncertain: the standard Latin etymological dictionaries (e.g., de Vaan 2008) report "no good etymology", and proposals to connect it with a PIE root meaning "to fit together" or "to do, make" remain unproven - one of those cases where honest scholarship stops at the oldest attested form rather than inventing a root. Whatever its prehistory, faber anchored a productive word-family: the noun fabrica ("workshop; craft; a product of skill"), and the denominative verb fabricāre / fabricārī ("to make, build, construct"), which in later Latin also carried the smith's sense "to forge". The specialist title faber ferrarius, "iron-worker, smith", shows how naturally the craftsman's word attached to metalwork, and Roman writers from Cicero to the elder Pliny treat the faber as the type of the useful maker.

2. Gallo-Romance and Old French. In the spoken Latin of Gaul, fabricāre underwent the regular softening and reshaping of medial clusters: the heavy group -bric- (in the stem fabric-) was reduced, voiced, and fricated, producing the Old French verb forgier ("to work metal; to build; to falsify") and the noun forge ("furnace, smithy"). Old French already shows the semantic spectrum that would reach English whole: honest metalwork, general construction, and - notably - falsification, since the same act of "making" could produce false documents and coins. The word travelled with the aristocratic and military culture of medieval France: forges, armour, and weapon-smithing were central to the feudal war economy, and the market for fine blades kept the smith's vocabulary alive in courtly romance and chanson de geste.

3. Middle English and the counterfeit turn. English borrowed the verb around 1300 as forgen. Middle English usage confirms the full three-way spread: forgen "to shape iron with fire and hammer" (the literal smith-sense), forgen "to fashion, compose, create" (applied to speeches, verses, and great works), and forgen "to invent, fabricate, devise" - first of stories and lies, then of writings and seals. The crucial bridge is mental rather than material: the same hand that shapes iron "shapes" a narrative, and by the early modern period forging came to mean tampering with the very instruments of proof - documents, coin dies, signet rings. By Shakespeare's time the participle forged was an established epithet for what is counterfeit and false, and the noun forgery (1570s) named the fabricated thing; a generation later it named the crime. The forger entered English law, literature, and folklore as the false artisan: the man who makes, with exquisite craft, what has no right to exist.

4. Industrial and metallurgical revolution. The nineteenth century transformed the physical sense. Village anvils gave way to foundry-scale hammers: the steam hammer patented by James Nasmyth (1839) made it possible to shape steel masses of many tons, and drop-forging and press-forging turned out railway axles, crankshafts, and boiler plates in quantity. Forging became a precise industrial category opposed to casting (pouring) and machining (cutting), prized because hot working refines the metal's internal grain flow, closes internal voids, and improves toughness and fatigue life. Modern metallurgy quantifies those benefits: the crankshafts, connecting rods, turbine blades, railway wheels, and aircraft parts of today are still overwhelmingly "forgings", and presses of tens of thousands of tons shape superalloys for jet engines. The old smith's act survives, magnified by science.

5. Figurative glory and fraud in modernity. Meanwhile the figurative sense reached its peak in political and literary rhetoric: nations are "forged" in wars and revolutions, constitutions and identities are "forged" in long deliberation; the image of the smith as primeval maker (Wayland, Hephaestus/Vulcan) keeps the metaphor warm, and Tolkien's own myth - Aulë the Smith, the forging of the Rings and of swords - echoes in the very sentence of this card ("a forging and an arming"). At the opposite pole, art forgery became a modern scandal-industry: forged canvases, signatures, manuscripts, and passports are exposed by spectroscopy, dendrochronology, and provenance research, and the "forger" has been romanticised in film and fiction as a tragic virtuoso. The word now spans the full arc from the honest hammer to the false signature, and to "forge ahead" (18th century, of obscure origin, perhaps from "force") - a fourth, unrelated homonym that speakers today feel as part of the same family of effortful forward motion.

6. Phonetic outline. Latin /ˈfa.ber/ → Vulgar Latin /fabriˈkaː.re/ → Gallo-Romance /fɔrˈdʒje/ (Old French forgier; medial -bric- → -rg-) → Middle English /ˈfɔr.dʒən/ (forgen) → Modern English /fɔːdʒ/, with the gerund/participle forging /ˈfɔː.dʒɪŋ/ built by the productive -ing suffix, which in English conflates the Old English participial -ende and gerundial -ung(e). The spelling forg- preserves the Old French voiced affricate to this day.
