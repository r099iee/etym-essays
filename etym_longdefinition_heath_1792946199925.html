Heath names, in its primary British sense, a broad tract of open, uncultivated land — sandy or peaty ground grown over with low shrubs, above all heather and gorse, with coarse grasses between: the wild margin between farmland and forest, grazed, burned, and walked but never ploughed.

In the source sentence the traveller crosses exactly such country at dusk: on a dark heath, with a strange salt smell in the air — open windswept ground, purple-brown under a failing light, lonely and exposed, the classic threshold landscape of fairy tale and ghost story, where being lost is always one wrong turn away.

Core meaning in detail: A heath is defined as much by what it lacks as by what it holds: no crops, no fences worth the name, thin acid soils that generations of grazing and periodic burning have kept treeless. Its signature plants are ling and bell heather, gorse and broom, bracken and wiry grass; its signature fauna the nightjar, the Dartford warbler, the sand lizard, and the adder basking on bare sand. In southern England great heaths — Hampstead, Blackheath, the Surrey heaths, the Dorset heaths — survive as commons and nature reserves, islands of wilderness inside suburbia, while in the north the word shades into moor and fell.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Open uncultivated shrubland (primary sense, from Old English): the tract itself, as in Hounslow Heath or the heath at night. The bridge is direct inheritance: Old English hǣþ already meant both the land and what grows on it, and modern conservation keeps the word as a technical habitat term.
  • The low evergreen shrubs themselves, especially heather (botanical, transferred): gardeners plant heaths, meaning heather species of Erica and Calluna. The bridge runs from ground to garment: the land named for its covering, then the covering named apart from the land, until a potted heath can sit on a patio that never was a heath.
  • Wild, lonely, exposed country in literature (poetic, from Shakespeare onward): Lear rages on the heath, Hardy's Egdon Heath broods across a whole novel, and every windswept no-man's-land of fiction borrows the gloom. The bridge is atmospheric: real heaths are genuinely bleak after dark, and writers from the Jacobeans to the Victorians made that bleakness the scenery of madness, exile, and the supernatural.
  • A place-name and surname element (onomastic): Heath as surname, and in names like Hampstead Heath or Heathfield, marks families or spots tied to such ground. The bridge is settlement history: cottages on the waste took the waste's name, and the name then travelled with emigrants to every continent.
  • Kin to heathen (historical association): the heathen is, by one old account, the dweller on the heath — the pagan countryman beyond the Christian town — though scholars debate whether the link is true kinship or folk etymology. The bridge, real or imagined, paints the heath as the land beyond conversion, wild in faith as in vegetation.
  • A suburban open space or former common (modern urban): London's heaths are now parks with ponds, pubs, and kite-flyers. The bridge is preservation: enclosure and building spared these particular wastes, so the ancient word now labels recreation ground, and heath can connote affluent villagey charm rather than desolation.

Register and connotation: Heath is a word of weather and weathered feeling — crisp, Anglo-Saxon, faintly melancholy. It carries purple late-summer colour, woodsmoke, and a chill after sunset; it suggests freedom to roam and danger in roaming. Ecologists use it precisely, poets use it portentously, and estate agents use it profitably, but all three mean the same undrained, unploughed, unhurried ground.
