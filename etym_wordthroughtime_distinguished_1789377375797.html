- Late 16th Century (Tudor Era) - Emerges as the past participial adjective of *distinguish* (derived from Latin *distinguere*), originally meaning strictly "separated into parts, divided by boundaries, or marked off by differentiating qualities."
- Early to Mid-17th Century - Expands from concrete physical separation into sensory and mental perception, describing objects, sounds, or ideas that are sharp, distinct, and easily discerned from their surrounding background.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment & Augustan Era) - Undergoes social and intellectual elevation; writers (Samuel Johnson, Alexander Pope) begin applying *distinguished* to men marked out from the common multitude by extraordinary virtue, genius, or public service.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Cemented in aristocratic society and literature (Jane Austen, Charles Dickens, Oscar Wilde) as the ultimate hallmark of high social eminence, venerable dignity, and refined aesthetic appearance; also institutionalized in British military gallantry orders (*Distinguished Service Order*, 1886).
- 20th Century - Formalized in academia, civil service, and diplomacy (*distinguished professorships*, *distinguished delegates*), while retaining its widespread cultural use for mature, silver-templed elegance.
- 21st Century - Continues to serve internationally as the premier tribute for lifetime intellectual achievement, statesmanlike gravitas, and enduring cultural impact.
