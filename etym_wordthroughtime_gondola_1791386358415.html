- Late 11th c. "Venetian canal vessel" - First recorded in a Latin charter of Doge Vitale Falier in 1094 (*gondulam*); establishes itself as the primary mode of private transport and trade navigation throughout the Venetian archipelago.
- 16th c. "sumptuary standardization and foreign travel accounts" - In 1562, the Venetian Senate mandates uniform black lacquer to halt aristocratic excess; English travel writers (Thomas Coryat) and playwrights (Shakespeare) introduce the word to Tudor and Stuart England as the supreme symbol of Venetian exoticism.
- 18th–19th c. "Romantic poetry and the aeronautical leap" - The Grand Tour and Romantic poets (Byron, Shelley) romanticize the moonlit gondola; early French and English aeronauts (Montgolfier, Pilâtre de Rozier) borrow *gondola* for the passenger basket swinging beneath gas balloons.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c. "railroad freight and alpine transport" - North American railroad companies adopt *gondola* for open-topped bulk freight cars; European alpine engineers apply it to enclosed aerial cabins on mountain cableways (ski gondolas); humorists like Wodehouse parody the clichéd romanticism of moonlit canal rides.
- Mid-to-Late 20th c.–Present "retail merchandising and modern survival" - Supermarket architects adopt *gondola* for freestanding double-sided aisle shelving; the word maintains a vibrant dual identity as a romantic cultural emblem of Venice and a technical standard across global transit, skiing, and retail planning.
