- Late 11th–13th c. — Known in Ecclesiastical Latin as crucesignātī ("the cross-signed") and in Old French as croisés; participants enjoyed supreme religious veneration, chivalric glory, and papal indulgences.
- 14th–16th c. — As holy land expeditions ceased, Romance languages formalized the collective nouns croisade (French) and cruzada (Spanish); Renaissance scholars evaluated the wars with historical detachment.
- 17th–18th c. — Borrowed into English as crusade and agent noun crusader; Enlightenment historians (Edward Gibbon, David Hume, Voltaire) critically condemned the crusaders as violent, superstitious fanatics.
- 19th c. (Romantic & Victorian eras) — Romanticized in literature (Sir Walter Scott's Ivanhoe and The Talisman) and academic painting (Eugène Delacroix), while the term secularized into a positive metaphor for moral fighters (e.g. abolitionist crusaders).
- Mid-20th c. — Embraced in Western popular culture as the ultimate title of righteous heroism (Dwight Eisenhower's Crusade in Europe, Batman the "Caped Crusader").
- 21st c. — Retains strong dual status: celebrated in civic discourse for investigative and moral integrity, yet deeply sensitive and politically fraught in Middle Eastern geopolitical contexts.
