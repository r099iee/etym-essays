Utterly is a degree adverb meaning "completely, totally, absolutely — to the fullest possible extent, without remainder, qualification, or exception." In "Truly songs and tales fall utterly short of the reality," Bilbo tells Smaug that every song and tale fails completely — not partly, not nearly, but entirely — to capture the dragon's true magnificence. The word is an intensifier of totality: it denies degrees and admits no exception, which is why it pairs so naturally with absolutes ("utterly impossible"), with destruction ("utterly destroyed"), and with states of isolation or despair ("utterly alone").

Core sense and grammar.

  • Totality: utterly modifies adjectives, past participles, verbs, and adverbs: utterly silent, utterly exhausted, utterly fails, utterly and completely. It asserts that the quality or action holds without residue.
  • Negative polarity and absolutes: it is at home with words that already exclude degree — utterly impossible, utterly useless, utterly different — where it functions as an emphatic seal rather than a measure.
  • Register: standard but formal-leaning; utterly is the most literary of the common totality adverbs (beside totally, completely, entirely). In everyday speech it has been largely displaced by totally and completely, so uttering "utterly" today marks a slightly elevated, emphatic, or old-fashioned tone — precisely the register Tolkien gives to Bilbo's flattery of Smaug.
  • Related forms: the adjective utter "complete, total" (utter nonsense, utter ruin) is the base; the rare noun utterness is attested but literary.

Polysemy and homography — the two "utter" words.

  • utter (adjective) "complete": from the comparative of out — "outer, exterior" (Old English ūtera), shifted through "extreme, thoroughgoing" to "complete, total." Utterly is its adverb.
  • utter (verb) "to speak": a different word, from Middle English utteren, borrowed from Middle Dutch ūteren "to make known, show, speak," built on uit "out" — literally "to put out (words)." It is unrelated to the adjective except that both ultimately descend from the same PIE root for "out" (*ūd) by separate routes; the identical spelling is coincidence-by-descent, not identity. "He uttered a cry" and "utter nonsense" share no grammar or meaning.
  • This accidental identity is worth knowing because both words are common: the adjective utter is a totality word; the verb utter is a speaking word; and utterly belongs only to the first.

Collocations and usage notes.

  • Utterly favours the catastrophic and the absolute: utterly destroyed, utterly defeated, utterly lost, utterly devastated, utterly alone, utterly hopeless — a semantic gravity towards ruin and extremity that totally lacks.
  • In legal and formal prose it marks voidness and nullity: "the contract is utterly void"; the phrase is a fossil of centuries of statute language.
  • In negative constructions it doubles the denial: "I am utterly at a loss," "utterly unlike anything I have seen" — the adverb says even comparison fails.
  • Historically it intensified religious and moral statements (the King James Bible: "utterly destroyed," "utterly consumed"); the moral weight clings faintly to the word today, which is why it suits Bilbo's solemn, flattering compliment to a dragon.

Why the word means this. The semantic bridge is spatial-turned-absolute. Old English ūt "out" formed a comparative ūtera "outer" — the farther out. "Outer" slid to "extreme" (the utter end of the earth = the farthest end), and "extreme in position" became "extreme in degree," then simply "complete, total" by the 14th–15th centuries. Utterly — "in an utter manner" — therefore means "to the outermost degree," and its force is that of a superlative of space applied to degree: nothing lies beyond it, nothing is left outside it.
