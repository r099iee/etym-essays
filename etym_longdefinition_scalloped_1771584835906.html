Scalloped describes an edge, border, hem, or outline that is cut or ornamented with a series of rounded, semicircular (or shallow-arc) curves, so that it looks like the fluted, fan-shaped rim of a scallop shell. In The Story of the Stone, a smiling maid enters “wearing a dress of red damask and a black silk sleeveless jacket which had scalloped borders of some coloured material.” The adjective here does precise sartorial work: the scalloped border is not a plain trim but a decorative one, and the curve of each scallop gives the garment a rhythmic, feminine softness that the straight, severe lines of black silk would otherwise lack. That a mere servant's jacket should carry such ornament is itself a statement about the Jia household — a domestic economy so dedicated to aesthetic harmony that even a maid's sleeve-hem is finished with shell-like curves and bright “coloured material.” The word thus contributes to the passage's depiction of a world where rank is regulated but beauty is lavished everywhere, and where even the edge of a garment repeats the gentle, wavy geometry of the natural world. Primary senses.
  • Of an edge or border — cut or ornamented with a series of rounded semicircular curves, as on hems, collars, frills, wallpaper borders, pie crusts, and mouldings; the standard adjective sense since the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
  • Cookery (scalloped dishes) — of food, baked in a scallop shell or in a shell-shaped dish, or (by extension) baked with a creamy sauce and a crumb topping: scalloped potatoes, scalloped oysters, scalloped corn. The cookery sense rests historically on the practice of baking oysters in their own scallop shells.
  • Of the shellfish — belonging to or shaped like the scallop, the marine bivalve with the characteristic radiating, fluted shell; “scalloped” can thus mean “armed or decorated with scallop shells”, the older, literal sense.
  • Verb: to scallop — to cut or shape an edge into scallops, or (in cookery) to bake in a shell-shaped dish.
The craft sense. In sewing and the needle arts, scalloping is a specific finishing technique: the edge is cut or hemmed in a series of curves, each scallop being an arc of a circle, and the curved edge is often finished with a tight buttonhole or blanket stitch, so that the fabric does not fray. Scalloped collars, hems, and handkerchief edges were a mark of fine domestic needlework in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; the scalloped pie crust is the cookery mirror of the same aesthetic — the edge of the pastry given a wavy, shell-like finish before baking. Because the scallop is a repeated curve rather than a single wave, the effect is rhythmic and ornamental rather than merely undulating. How scalloped differs from its decorative neighbours. A scalloped edge is a row of little half-circles, each arch bulging outward. A serrated edge is a saw-tooth line of sharp, pointed teeth with a cutting function — scallops are rounded and purely ornamental. A crenellated edge (crenellation) is the square, battlement-like notching of castle walls, made of straight-topped rectangular merlons and crenels — the exact opposite of the continuous curve. A notched edge is the generic term for any cut or nick, with no implication of decorative regularity; a notched edge may be accidental, a scalloped edge is deliberately patterned. A wavy edge undulates in a single smooth line, without the repeated, discrete, self-contained arches of scalloping. A fluted surface is grooved with parallel rounded channels (as on a classical column or a pie-crust rim) — a set of curved grooves rather than curved edge-cuts. To be festooned is to be hung with garlands — decoration laid on the surface, not cut into the edge. The distinguishing feature of scalloping is therefore the series of repeated, self-contained semicircles along a margin. Polysemy and the historical bridge. The adjective's whole family hangs from the noun scallop, the fan-shelled bivalve. The shellfish was named in Middle English from Old French escalope, “shell, husk”, itself from the Frankish word for a shell or husk, and the shell's radiating ribs and scalloped rim supplied the image for the edge. From “shaped like a scallop shell's rim” the word moved in the sixteenth century to any border cut in shell-like curves, and in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, by way of the oyster shells used as baking dishes, it moved into the kitchen: scalloped oysters were literally baked in scallop shells, and from that concrete practice the word spread to any dish baked with cream and crumbs in a shell-shaped or shallow baking dish. The same shell that marked the pilgrim of Saint James on the roads to Compostela thus left its wavy trace on our hems, our pie crusts, and our casseroles — one of the more charming journeys of an English adjective from the seashore to the sewing box and the oven. Modern usage. Today scalloped is used most often in three registers: fashion and soft furnishing (scalloped hems, collars, cushions, lampshade trims), cookery (scalloped potatoes and other baked dishes), and crafts (scalloped edgings, scalloped frames). In architecture and design a scalloped moulding or a scalloped arch repeats the same curve on a larger scale. The word has never lost its shell: whether it is a hem, a pastry crust, or an architectural border, a scalloped edge still says, faintly, “like the rim of a scallop shell”.
