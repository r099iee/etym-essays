- [Middle English: poetic measure] - Inherited from Greek metron ("measure") via Latin and French, metre is from the start the word for the measured rhythm of verse, an old, standing term in English poetry.
- [16th-17th c.: the named classical system] - The Renaissance imports the named feet (iamb, trochee, dactyl, spondee), turning metre into a taught, rule-governed system of scansion carried with scholarly prestige.
- [late 18th c.: the measure of length] - After French metric reform, metre is also adopted as an SI unit of length; British English keeps the -re spelling for both senses, so the word now splits into two unrelated meanings.
- [Modern: studied pattern vs. felt rhythm] - In criticism, metre is the countable, nameable arrangement of feet, deliberate and artificial, contrasted with the natural, felt pulse of rhythm, and here held up with amused mockery as recherche.
