Late 13th c. — The word appears in surnames; the earliest sense is a large tub or cask, a coopered vessel for holding liquid.
Early 14th c. — Tankard is recorded in English for a tub or cask, often wooden and iron-hooped.
Late 15th c. — The sense shifts to a large drinking vessel, the ancestor of the modern handled mug.
16th–17th c. — Tankards can be very large, including water carriers of two gallons or more; pewter and silver examples become common in taverns and households.
18th–19th c. — The lidded pewter or silver quart pot becomes the standard image of the tankard; glass and ceramic versions appear.
20th c. — Tankards become collectible antiques and nostalgic pub ornaments; the word acquires a warm, old-fashioned, country-house flavour.
21st c. — Used for antique silver and pewter, personalised gifts, craft-beer glassware and fantasy props; the word remains a byword for hearty traditional drinking.
