Linear derivation chain for RUEFUL (root word one of two): R — PIE *kreu- (to strike, dash against, cause blood to flow) — the root of physical blows and raw flesh. → E — Proto-Germanic *hrewwaną (to grieve, distress, feel sorrow; lit. "to be struck within the heart") — the physical blow internalised as emotion; noun *hrewwō (grief, regret, sorrow). → D — Old English hrēow (noun: sorrow, grief, regret, repentance) and hrēowian (verb: to feel sorrow, to repent). → C — Middle English rewe / reuwen (verb: to regret, be sorry for; noun: sorrow). → B — English rue (verb: to regret; noun: sorrow, archaic) + the suffix -ful (full of). → A — rueful (coined 14th century: full of regret or sorrow) ◄ TARGET. Note on the debate: The connection of *kreu- to the Germanic "grief" family is broadly accepted, but the intermediate chain showing exactly how a "strike" root produced *hrewwaną is reconstructed rather than attested; some scholars posit contamination with a separate root. No solid Sanskrit cognate for the "strike" sense is agreed upon, so that branch is omitted. The Germanic-to-English chain (E → A) is secure.
Linear derivation chain for VEXATION (root word two of two): R — PIE *weǵh- (to carry, convey, travel in a vehicle) — one of the widest-spread Indo-European roots of motion. → E — Latin vehere (to carry, convey); its intensive formation vexāre (to shake, jolt, toss about, harass) — repeated, violent carrying, the image of a rough cart-ride. → D — Latin vexātiō, vexātiōnis (a shaking, a jolting, a harassing, an annoyance) — the abstract noun of vexāre. → C — Old French vexacion (torment, harassment, annoyance) — borrowed from the Latin noun. → B — Middle English vexacioun / vexation (14th century: physical torment or affliction, later mental annoyance). → A — vexation (modern English: the state of being annoyed or worried; the cause of such a state) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain *weǵh- → vehere → vexāre → vexātiō → vexacion → vexation is fully secure and uncontroversial. The intensive sense of vexāre — "to carry roughly, to jounce, to shake" — is the semantic hinge on which the whole modern meaning turns.
