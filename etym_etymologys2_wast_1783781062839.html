PIE: *wes- "to remain, stay, dwell, pass the night" — the root of the Germanic past of "be" (the present forms of "be" come from a different root, *h₁es-; the verb is suppletive).
Proto-Germanic: *wesaną "to be, remain" — from *wes- with the thematic verb formation; supplies the past (and the infinitive) of the suppletive "be."
Old English (c. 700–1100): wesan "to be"; past singular wæs "was, was present." The inherited second-person singular past was wǣre (from the *es- conjugation, *wer-?), alongside which the -st pattern was already spreading.
Middle English (13th c.): wast — new second-person singular past "was + -st," formed analogically on the pattern of satst and didst; coexists with wert (subjunctive).
Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700): wast "thou was" — the standard indicative thou-form in the Authorized Version (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton (indicative wast vs. subjunctive wert).
Modern English (archaic): wast — survives only in liturgy, scripture, and archaizing verse; standard English uses were/was for all persons.
NOTE ON SUPPletion AND DEBATE: The past of "be" is built on *wes-, but the present paradigm (am, is, are) is built on the unrelated *h₁es- and *er-, and the infinitive and future on *bʰuH- (*bēon). Wast itself is a Middle English analogical creation (was + -st); the older, phonologically regular second-person singular past form was wǣre/wert, which survived as the subjunctive. No single "etymon" of wast exists apart from this combination, so the chain is the standard suppletive history of be.
