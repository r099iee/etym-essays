Evolution of "presentment." The biography of PRESENTMENT begins with two Proto-Indo-European roots joined in Latin: *per-, "forward, through, before," and *es-, "to be." The second of these, the "being" root, is among the most widely attested of all roots in the family: it appears as Sanskrit ásti and Hindi hai, as Greek estí, as Latin est and esse, and in the Germanic is of English — the very verb "to be" itself. From prae ("before") and esse ("to be"), Latin formed praesens, "being before," that is, "present, at hand," and from praesens came the verb praesentare, "to place before, to set in front of the eyes or the mind, to present." The core idea — that to present something is to make it be before one — is already fully present at this Latin stage, and it is this image of a thing placed before a perceiver that every later sense of the word preserves.

1. From Latin to medieval English. In Late Latin and Old French, the verb presenter and the nominal formation presentement ("the act of presenting") developed naturally, and Anglo-Norman carried presentement into Middle English in the thirteenth century. At first the word meant simply "the act of presenting," and it entered English in the official and administrative registers where French-derived terms of government, church, and law were being absorbed after the Norman Conquest. It is significant that the word's first recorded career in English was administrative and legal, not aesthetic: English society in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries needed words for the formal acts by which persons and things were solemnly laid before authorities.

2. The legal career (grand-jury presentment). The word's most durable semantic development was legal. In English law, a presentment came to mean the formal statement of an offense made by a grand jury on its own knowledge, without a bill of indictment being preferred — a solemn laying-before of facts before a court or authority by the jurors themselves. The distinction from an indictment is precise and ancient: an indictment is framed and presented at the prosecutor's instance, whereas a presentment is the jury's own declaration of a crime of which it has direct knowledge. This technical sense gave the word a gravity and formality that it never entirely lost, and it survives to this day in Anglo-American legal usage: the presentment of a bill of exchange, meaning the formal offering of it for payment or acceptance, and the grand jury's presentment as a formal charge. Even outside law, the word's bookish weight is a legacy of these solemn, institutional uses — a word used by juries and courts does not easily become casual.

3. The aesthetic sense and Mill. Alongside this legal career, however, the word maintained its general sense — the act of setting something before the mind or the eyes, a representation, depiction, or rendering. It is this general, vivid sense that Mill invokes in the sentence preserved in this card, where "the lively and vivid PRESENTMENT of concrete fact" comes as a "delightful and agreeable shock" to a mind wearied by abstraction. For Mill, presentment names the capacity of concrete reality to present itself to thought with the force of immediate presence — not merely described or summarized, but set before the inner eye with the freshness of an actual encounter. The word is well chosen for a philosopher who repeatedly insisted on the priority of concrete fact over abstract speculation: the presentment of fact is the moment when the real world reasserts itself before the speculating mind, and the "delightful and agreeable shock" is the pleasure of rediscovering the senses after too long in the realm of ideas. The word's legal gravity here serves the prose: the fact is laid before the mind with the solemnity and definitiveness of a formal instrument.

4. The modern balance. In contemporary English the word is largely technical again — the presentment of a bill, the grand jury's presentment — while in general prose it is bookish and slightly archaic. It has also settled into a stable relationship with its near-doublet presentation, which has absorbed the neutral, everyday work of the family, leaving presentment to the formal, the legal, and the vividly aesthetic. This division of labor is a late refinement of a word that has always been able to do double duty: a grand jury presents, a merchant presents a bill, and a writer presents a scene — and in all three cases the underlying image is the same ancient one of a thing made present before its beholder.

  • PIE *per- "forward, before" + *es- "to be" → Latin praesens, praesent- "being before, present, at hand."
  • Latin praesentare "to place before, to present" → Late Latin/Old French presentement "act of presenting" → Middle English presentment (13th c.).
  • English law: presentment = the grand jury's formal statement of an offense on its own knowledge (distinct from indictment); commercial law: presentment of a bill or note for acceptance/payment.
  • General/aesthetic sense: representation, depiction, vivid setting-forth before the mind — the sense Mill uses in the present passage.
  • Contemporary: largely technical in law and commerce, bookish and slightly archaic in general prose; distinguished from the neutral presentation and from the unrelated presentiment ("foreboding").
