Hammurabi’s name is Amorite, not Akkadian, and that fact is the key to its form. The Amorites were a West Semitic people who supplied the ruling dynasties of several Mesopotamian cities in the early second millennium; their language survives mainly in personal names recorded by Akkadian scribes. The first element, ḫammu, belongs to a Semitic set of kinship words — Arabic ḥam “father-in-law”, Hebrew ḥām — and is generally taken to mean “kinsman” or “paternal uncle”. The second element, written -ra-pí, is usually connected with the Akkadian root rpʾ “to heal”, giving a sentence-name of the common ancient type: “Hammu heals” or “the kinsman is a healer”.

  • Alternative parses: a few scholars have preferred “the kinsman is great” (linking the element to rabû) or have treated Ḫammu as a divine name rather than a kinship term. The syllabic spelling ḫa-am-mu-ra-pí favours a reading with p, which tells against rabû, but the debate is not closed.
  • Transmission: the name is known from the stele itself, from date-formulae of his reign, from letters and administrative documents, and from later Babylonian copies of the laws. It never entered Greek or Latin literature; European knowledge of it dates only from the decipherment of cuneiform.
  • Spelling in English: Hammurabi reflects the conventional transliteration of ḫ as h; scholarly texts often print Ḫammurabi or Ḫammurapi. The variant Ammurapi belongs to a different, later name — the last king of Ugarit — and should not be confused with it.

The rediscovery

Cuneiform was deciphered in the middle decades of the nineteenth century, and the name of Hammurabi began to appear in Assyriological literature well before the stele was found. The decisive discovery came in December 1901, when a French expedition at Susa, led by Jacques de Morgan, unearthed the basalt pillar in three pieces. Vincent Scheil published the text in 1902, and within a decade the code had been translated into the major European languages. Its arrival transformed the study of ancient law: here was a complete, numbered, monumental statute book older than any Greek or Roman code, and its similarities to biblical legislation were immediately obvious.

The biblical debate

  • Similarities: both the Mesopotamian and the Pentateuchal traditions use casuistic formulation (“if a man…, then…”), both regulate the goring ox, the treatment of slaves and the talion principle, and both present law as divinely warranted.
  • Nineteenth-century speculation: some scholars identified Hammurabi with Amraphel, the king of Shinar in Genesis 14, and argued for direct borrowing by the biblical writers. The identification is now generally rejected on chronological and philological grounds.
  • Modern view: most historians explain the parallels by a shared ancient Near Eastern legal culture, transmitted through scribal training across centuries, rather than by direct literary dependence in either direction.

Modern reference

In contemporary English the name functions as a cultural shorthand. “The Code of Hammurabi” appears in legal-history syllabi, in journalism about harsh sentencing, and in popular writing about the origins of justice; the adjective Hammurabic is rare but intelligible. The stele itself, with its relief of the king before Shamash, has become one of the standard images of Mesopotamian civilisation, reproduced wherever the history of law is taught. In the source passage, the name is used in exactly this shorthand sense: not a man but a landmark, the earliest written witness to the principle of retaliation.
