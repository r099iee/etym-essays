- 13th–14th c. — “piping” is the sound of a pipe instrument (verbal noun of pipe): music, and by extension any thin reedy sound — neutral, pastoral register
- 14th–16th c. — extended to voices and birds: a “piping voice” is high and thin; perception shifts toward delicacy, fragility, even mockery (piping = childish, weak)
- 1560s — “piping hot” appears: food so hot it hisses and sizzles like a pipe's note — the word becomes an intensifier of heat
- 16th–17th c. — “piping” names the tube of cloth trimming edges of clothes and cushions — the pipe's shape, not its sound, now drives the meaning
- c. 1592 — Shakespeare's “weak piping time of peace” (Richard III): the sound-sense carries pastoral innocence and contemptible softness
- 19th–20th c. — the conduit sense dominates: “piping” = the network of water, gas and heating pipes — an everyday technical noun
- 20th c. — cooking: piping icing through a piping bag; computing: piping data between programs (Unix) — the tube-image renews itself in kitchen and code
- Today — the sound-sense survives in literary and birding prose; the everyday senses are infrastructure, sewing, gastronomy and software
