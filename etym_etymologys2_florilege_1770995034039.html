The word florilege is a compound of two roots; the chain for each element is given in isolation, then the compound formation. ROOT 1 - the "flower" element (flos, floris): PIE *bʰleh₃- "to bloom, blossom, flower, flourish" (reconstructed root; c. 4000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *flōs "flower" (reconstructed; c. 1000-500 BCE) → Latin flos, floris "flower, blossom; the best of its kind, the flower of something" (attested; c. 3rd century BCE onward) → (with legere) Latin florilegus "flower-gathering" → Latin florilegium "a gathering of flowers; an anthology of choice extracts" (attested in medieval Latin; book-genre sense from c. 12th-13th centuries CE) ROOT 2 - the "gather" element (legere): PIE *leǵ- "to gather, collect; to pick out words, to speak" (reconstructed root; c. 4000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *legō "to gather, collect" (reconstructed; c. 1000-500 BCE) → Latin legere "to gather, collect; to choose, select; to read" (attested; c. 3rd century BCE onward; the "read" sense arises because reading is "picking out" letters) → (with flos) Latin florilegus / florilegium (medieval Latin; c. 12th-13th centuries CE) COMPOUND CHAIN (both elements together): Latin flos "flower" + legere "to gather" → Late Latin florilegus (adjective) "flower-gathering" (attested) → Medieval Latin florilegium (noun) "a gathering of flowers; a collection of choice literary extracts" - calque of Greek anthologia (attested as a book-genre name from the 12th-13th centuries CE) → Middle French florilège "anthology" (attested 18th century CE; French retains it as a living word) → English florilege "a collection of choice extracts; an anthology" (rare learned borrowing, 19th century) → florilege TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The two roots are secure and independently well attested. The only nuance concerns the exact route into English: "florilege" is an anglicized spelling of the Latin florilegium / French florilège, and the word has never been common in English, where the calque-sibling "anthology" (from Greek anthos "flower" + legein "to gather") long ago won the field. The medieval florilegium genre is well documented in manuscripts, where it functioned as a scholarly compendium of patristic and classical sentences.
