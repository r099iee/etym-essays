Core sense. Benefactors (singular benefactor) are persons who confer a benefit — in modern usage, above all people who give money, property, or other material support to an individual, an institution, a charity, a school, a hospital, a museum, or a cause. The plural form benefactors is the collective form: it names such people as a group or class, often a remembered and honored one — "the benefactors of our town", "the benefactors of the university". The word arrives with an implied monument: benefactors are people whose good is inscribed in rolls, plaques, and endowed buildings.

Primary sense: financial patronage. In contemporary English the dominant sense is monetary. A benefactor is a donor — typically a wealthy one — who endows a scholarship, funds a new wing, underwrites a theatre season, or leaves a substantial bequest. Institutions keep "rolls of benefactors"; universities name libraries, chairs, and colleges after theirs; museums print donor lists that place "Benefactor" above "Patron" and "Friend". The word implies generosity that is public, significant, and remembered: a benefactor is not a casual giver but a notable one, whose gift changes what an institution can do.

Secondary senses. The word reaches well beyond money:

  • Helper, rescuer, doer of good — the older and wider sense: anyone who does good to others, saves them from harm, or improves their lot, with no financial element. A passer-by who pulls a family from a burning house is a benefactor; this is the sense nearest the Latin source benefacere, "to do good", and it survives in elevated and rhetorical use.
  • Institutional and honorific category — "Benefactor" functions as a formal tier in fundraising ("Friend, Donor, Patron, Benefactor"), as a title ("Benefactor Fellow"), and as an event ("Benefactors' Day"). In these uses it denotes a documented, often large, cumulative gift.
  • Ironical and sarcastic use — the word is ripe for irony, because its splendor sits so far from its reality. The context here is a classic case: the Master of Lake-town mockingly enrolls the dragon-slayer among the "benefactors" of the town even though dragon-fire has just ruined it. The gap between the fine title and the ruinous deed is exactly what the irony exploits; "so-called benefactor" is a standing phrase of complaint.
  • Grammatical notes — the feminine benefactress (18th c.) is now rare and often avoided; benefactor is used of both sexes. The plural benefactors is the only form in the source context, where it means "those who have done this town good".

Semantic bridge. Latin benefactor means "one who does good", from benefacere "to do well, do good". In antiquity and the Middle Ages the sense was general: any doer of good deeds, any protector. From the Renaissance onward, as European society built institutional charity — colleges, hospitals, almshouses, chantries, libraries — the typical "good deed" of the powerful became financial endowment. The word therefore narrowed from "one who does good" to "one who funds good". The bridge is social history: doing good increasingly meant paying for good.

Register, collocations and word-family. Formal, respectful, slightly old-fashioned; common collocations include generous benefactor, anonymous benefactor, great benefactor of the arts, the benefactors of the hospital. In plain speech "donor", "sponsor", or "patron" are often preferred; "benefactor" adds gratitude and historical weight, which is why the Master of Lake-town reaches for it. The word-family includes benefit ("a good done"), benevolent ("wishing well"), beneficent ("doing good"), benefice (a church living), and benefaction (the act or gift itself).
