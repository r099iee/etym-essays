- 14th c. [Old French *à droit* ('in a straight line, properly, rightfully, skillfully')] - A prepositional phrase denoting correctness, rectitude, and upright posture according to customary law and physical alignment.
- Late 16th c. [Middle French *adroit* as a fused adjective ('dexterous, clever, nimble')] - Crystallized as a single adjectival modifier praising physical skill, swordsmanship, and courtly manners.
- 17th c. [Borrowed into Early Modern English during the Restoration] - Adopted into English courtly literature and diplomacy as an aristocratic Gallicism denoting cleverness, wit, and social finesse.
- 18th–19th c. [Expansion across politics, legal rhetoric, and narrative prose] - Applied widely to describe strategic statesmen, skilled artisans, and masters of rhetoric maneuvering through controversy.
- 20th–21st c. [Contemporary global usage across journalism, sports, and psychology] - Universal high-register descriptor for mental dexterity, media maneuvering, technical finesse, and crisis management.
