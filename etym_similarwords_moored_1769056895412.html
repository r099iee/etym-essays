• anchored: Similar because both hold a vessel fixed in place; differs because anchoring is the stricter, more technical act of letting down an anchor to the seabed, held by weight and grip, while mooring fastens a rope or cable to a fixed point — a tree, a bollard, a buoy, or the shore.
  • docked: Similar because both bring a vessel to rest; differs because docking (and berthing) implies port infrastructure — coming alongside a pier into an assigned station — whereas a boat can be moored anywhere there is something solid to tie to, even a tree in a creek.
  • berthed: Similar because both secure a vessel at a place of rest; differs because berthing means assigning a vessel a specific place in a harbour or on board ship (a berth), while mooring is the more general act of tying to any fixed object and does not require harbour infrastructure.
  • tethered: Similar because both secure something with a rope; differs because tethering suggests a rope restraining an animal or, figuratively, a person, while mooring is the specifically nautical act of securing a vessel, unambiguously at home on the water.
  • tied up: Similar because both make a boat fast; differs because "tied up" is the informal, landside equivalent with no nautical flavour, while moored is the seaman's word that anticipates unmooring — the vessel is secured, not imprisoned.
  • hitched: Similar because both describe fastening something to a fixed point; differs because hitching implies a quick, temporary fastening (as of a horse to a post), while mooring is the deliberate, nautical act of securing a vessel for a stay.
  • fastened: Similar because both mean made secure; differs because fastened is the general, colourless verb for any kind of fixing, while moored is specific to vessels and carries the whole maritime context of ropes, tides, and casting off.
  • secured: Similar because both mean made safe from movement; differs because secured is broader (a window, a deal, a perimeter) while moored names the particular nautical procedure of tying a vessel fast, with its attendant image of a voyage safely ended.
