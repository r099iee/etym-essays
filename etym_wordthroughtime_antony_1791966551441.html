- 3rd–2nd c. BCE — the name is a plebeian Roman family name of obscure origin, borne by a house rising toward the consulship; it is neutral and purely identifying.
- 1st c. BCE — the family’s great orator makes the name a byword for forensic power; his grandson the triumvir gives it a second, political and military charge that eventually overshadows the first.
- 12th–14th c. — the name establishes itself in English chiefly as a baptismal name honouring saints rather than as a classical reference.
- 16th c. — the spelling with an added h gains ground and gradually becomes the everyday English form, leaving the classical spelling as the learned variant.
- 17th c. — Shakespeare’s Antony and Cleopatra fixes Antony in literary memory as the triumvir, while scholarly writing keeps the same spelling for the orator.
- 20th–21st c. — Antony survives as a given name and as the classical spelling; in Roman history it means the orator only when qualified, since popular culture knows almost exclusively Mark Antony.
