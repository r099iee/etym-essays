Wayside names the strip of ground beside a road or path: the verge, bank, or ditch-edge where travellers rest, beggars sit, flowers seed themselves, and the journey’s litter gathers, and by the oldest metonymy in English it also names whatever belongs to that margin, from shrines and inns to casualties and afterthoughts.

In the source sentence the hobbits sat in shadow by the wayside, and the word does quiet narrative work: they are off the road yet defined by it, resting in its shadow-zone, hidden from passers-by but still oriented to its line, and the shadow they sit in is both literal dusk and the political shadow of the road itself, the watched highway they dare not walk openly.

Core meaning in detail: physically the wayside is the uncultivated margin between metalled track and field wall, hedge, or ditch, narrow enough to step across yet wide enough to sit, sleep, graze a pony, or hide a fugitive: it holds milestones, fingerposts, shrines, crosses, and stalls precisely because it is public without being passage, the road’s waiting room, and its flora — plantain, yarrow, poppy, vetch — are the classic ruderals, plants that follow disturbance and feet.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Roadside verge or margin (primary, from Old English): flowers of the wayside, a cottage by the wayside, and the bridge is compositional, since way plus side literally means the side of the way, the same formation that gives hillside and riverside.
  • A place of rest or concealment beside the road (Tolkien’s nuance): sat in shadow by the wayside, and the bridge is situational, since the margin offers what the road denies: stillness, cover, and a vantage from which to watch traffic pass.
  • Chapels, crosses, shrines, and inns (historic compounds): wayside cross, wayside shrine, wayside inn, and the bridge is institutional, since piety and hospitality plant themselves where travellers must pass, blessing or feeding the margin’s stream.
  • Fallen by the wayside (idiom for failure or abandonment): resolutions, recruits, and comrades fall by the wayside, and the bridge runs through the gospel parable of seed sown by the wayside, devoured by birds because it never rooted, extended to every endeavour abandoned mid-journey.
  • Marginal, incidental, overlooked (adjectival extension): wayside remarks, wayside observations, and the bridge carries the margin’s low status into discourse, since what grows or is said by the wayside is pleasant but peripheral, not the main crop or the main argument.
  • Commerce of the margin (modern): wayside stalls, wayside garages, wayside attractions, and the bridge is economic, since the verge’s passing trade tempts sellers of fruit, fuel, and curiosities to pitch exactly where wheels slow.

Register and connotation: wayside is gentle, slightly archaic, and pastoral in its literal use, smelling of dust, hawthorn, and rain, yet its idiom is sternly moral: to fall by the wayside is to fail the march, and preachers, sergeants, and coaches all exploit the image, while Tolkien holds both tones at once, since the hobbits’ shadowed rest is both idyll and peril, a pause that could become a falling if they linger.
