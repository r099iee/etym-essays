The history of hetaerae is less a story of sound change than of social history. The word began as a perfectly innocent Greek term for "companion"—someone of one's own group, one's own people—and was progressively narrowed, specialized, and finally fossilized in English as a scholarly label for one of the most celebrated and paradoxical institutions of classical antiquity: the educated courtesan. Tracking the word means tracking the changing position of women in Greek society, the dynamics of the symposium, and the way Victorian and modern scholarship packaged the classical world for later audiences.

Stage 1: The root "one's own" and the Greek comrade. The deepest layer is the Proto-Indo-European base *swe- "self, one's own", a root that appears across the family: Latin suus "his/her/its own", Sanskrit sva- "own", Gothic swes. From this "own-ness" base, Greek developed a family of words for the ingroup: one's own people, one's own clan, one's comrades. The masculine noun hetaîros, "comrade, companion, mate", is already attested in Homer (8th century BCE), where it denotes the sworn companions and comrades-in-arms of a warrior—men bound to one another as "one's own people" rather than by blood. In the classical period hetairoi also named the members of political clubs (hetaireiai), the aristocratic factions that dominated Athenian politics—associations of "companions" in the fullest sense. The root's semantic field was thus male, political, and fraternal.

Stage 2: The feminine narrowing—from "companion" to "courtesan" (5th century BCE). The feminine hetaíra performed the same derivation but landed in a different social reality. In classical Athens, citizen women were largely secluded in the household (oikos); the respectable wife was confined to domestic, reproductive, and religious duties and did not appear at the male symposium. The only women who moved freely in elite male society—who drank, talked, sang, danced, and philosophized alongside men—were the professional companions: the hetairai. The word "companion" therefore became a euphemism for "courtesan", and the semantic narrowing encodes the whole social structure of Athens: because respectable women could not be companions, the companions could not be respectable. Yet the hetaira was emphatically not the same as the pornē (brothel prostitute). She was educated—often in music, poetry, and rhetoric—and could choose her patrons, accumulate wealth, and in some cases exercise genuine political influence. The most famous was Aspasia of Miletus, whose relationship with Pericles scandalized and fascinated Athens; Plutarch reports that she was so celebrated for her eloquence that Socrates brought his pupils to hear her. Phryne, another legendary hetaera, was famed for her beauty and her lavish lifestyle and served as the model for Praxiteles' Cnidian Aphrodite. These women are the historical reality behind the word's later glamour.

Stage 3: Transmission to Latin and survival as a learned word. The Romans knew the Greek institution and borrowed the word as hetaera, but it never became a native Latin term of living currency; it remained a Greek import used in descriptions of Greek mores (e.g., in Cornelius Nepos' biography of Alcibiades, where hetaerae figure among the corruptions of Greek city life). With the collapse of the classical world, the word effectively died in the European vernaculars, surviving only in manuscripts and lexica.

Stage 4: Revival in modern English scholarship (18th–19th centuries). The word re-entered English as part of the 18th- and 19th-century revival of classical learning. It appears in translations, histories of Greece, and discussions of Greek prostitution and women's history. The plural hetaerae (Latinized) became the standard learned form, and the word acquired its modern flavor: archaic, scholarly, faintly exotic. Writers of the Victorian era were simultaneously fascinated and scandalized by the hetaerae, and the word allowed them to discuss a delicate subject with classical decorum.

Stage 5: The literary and cultural afterlife. In modern usage, hetaerae functions as a high-register allusion. To compare a woman to the hetaerae of ancient Greece is to credit her with intellectual brilliance, social magnetism, and cultivated charm—the qualities of Aspasia and Phryne—while politely acknowledging a romantic or sexual freedom that sets her apart from conventional domesticity. In the card's context, the comparison is entirely flattering: it places the modern woman among the celebrated women of antiquity, known for "wit and beauty" and for commanding the attention of the most powerful men of their age.

Conclusion. The word hetaerae is a linguistic museum of ancient Greek social structure. Its root speaks of kinship and comradeship; its feminine derivation records the exclusion of respectable women from male society; its euphemistic narrowing names the profession that filled the gap; and its modern revival preserves all of this as a term of scholarly prestige and literary glamour. To use the word is to invoke an entire world.
