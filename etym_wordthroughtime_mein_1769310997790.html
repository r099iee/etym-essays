- Air and bearing (mid-16th c.) - Enters English from French with the sense of 'appearance, air, look,' and is spelled freely: myen, mein and mien all circulate for the same word.
- The character-reading look (16th-17th c.) - Widens from mere appearance to the whole outward self as testimony of character - face, posture and carriage fused - and flourishes in the elevated prose and poetry of the age.
- The noble bearing (18th-19th c.) - Becomes the bodily advertisement of rank in social observation; Austen's 'fine, tall person, handsome features, noble mien' tells the reader of Darcy's consequence before he speaks, the word almost always armed with a qualitative adjective: grave, haughty, majestic, stately.
- Modern usage - Rare in conversation, where bearing, demeanor and expression have taken over; it survives in literary and historical prose and in dignified fixed phrases, and is now easily confused with the German mein ('my') or the verb mean - a confusion no seventeenth-century writer would have felt.
