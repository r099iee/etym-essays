Octavianus is a Latin masculine proper name (genitive Octaviānī), not a common noun: it has no plural, no generic sense and no life outside historical writing, and in English it is always capitalised. It denotes Gaius Octavius Thurinus (63 BCE – 14 CE), the great-nephew and posthumously adopted son of Julius Caesar, for the period between Caesar's assassination in March 44 BCE and the senate's grant of the title Augustus in January 27 BCE. The source passage uses the name in exactly this scholarly way: “it was Octavianus, a young man very well known to the great orator, who became the first emperor under the title Augustus”.

The name is a small fossil of Roman adoption law. A Roman aristocrat carried three names — praenomen, nomen, cognomen — and a man adopted into another family kept a trace of his birth family in an extra -ianus adjective. Caesar's will therefore turned Gaius Octavius Thurinus into Gaius Iulius Caesar Octavianus: “Gaius Julius Caesar, formerly of the Octavii”.

The components of the name

  • Gaius — the praenomen, one of the small stock of inherited personal names (Gaius, Lucius, Marcus, Publius, Quintus, Sextus) that a Roman son normally received; it was abbreviated C. in inscriptions, because the letter G was a later offshoot of C and the old spelling Caius survived in the abbreviation.
  • Octavius — the nomen gentilicium, the clan name of the plebeian gens Octavia, an old equestrian family of Velitrae in Latium. The name is traditionally connected with Latin octāvus “eighth”, as a birth-order name of the same type as Quintus, Sextus and Decimus.
  • Thurinus — the original cognomen, which Suetonius derives from Thurii, either because an ancestor had won a victory there or because the boy was born there; Mark Antony later used it as a jeer, and the heir quietly dropped it.
  • -ānus — the productive adjective suffix “belonging to, of the family of” (as in Rōmānus, Albānus, Montānus). Attached to an adoptive name it did not mean “little Augustus” but “the man from the Octavii”, preserving the memory of the family he had left.

Who the name denotes

Octavian was born on 23 September 63 BCE, the year of Cicero's consulship and of the Catilinarian conspiracy; his grandmother Julia was Caesar's sister, which made him Caesar's closest male relative once the dictator had no legitimate son. At Apollonia in Illyria, where he was completing his education, he learned in March 44 BCE that the will had adopted him and named him heir. He returned to a Rome already convulsed, raised troops on his own authority, and in 43 BCE formed the Second Triumvirate with Mark Antony and Lepidus — a compact sealed by the proscription in which Cicero, the “great orator” of the source, was killed. He defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BCE, took Alexandria in 30 BCE, and in 27 BCE received the name Augustus, which became the title of every later emperor.

Why the name matters in this reading

The passage sets Octavianus against Cicero as a contemporary and rival: the two men were indeed shaped by the same generation of crisis, but they were never equals. Cicero hoped to use the young heir against Antony and was destroyed by the bargain the heir struck with Antony. Historians' use of “Octavianus” keeps that early, unfinished, ruthless politician separate from the mature Augustus of the Res Gestae and the Ara Pacis, and that separation is the name's main function today.

Register, grammar and modern use

  • Strictly a proper noun; the plural “Octaviani” exists only in learned jest, and there is no abstract noun formed from it in English.
  • English offers three forms: the Latinising Octavianus, the usual English Octavian (17th century onward), and Octavius, properly his birth nomen but often used loosely for the same man.
  • “Augustus” is a title, not a name, and “Caesar” after 44 BCE is a dynastic claim rather than a description; the three words are not interchangeable, although popular writing treats them so.
  • The feminine Octavia, borne by his sister and by Antony's wife, and the adjective Octavian (“of Octavian”; “Octavian propaganda”) belong to the same name-family.
  • In modern prose the name carries a faintly technical, classicist colour; it signals that the writer is distinguishing the heir from the emperor, and it appears in titles such as “Octavian's rise” rather than in neutral journalism.
  • Because the name is Latin, it is invariant in English except for the possessive “Octavianus's”, and it is never preceded by “the” unless the writer means the type—“a second Octavianus” is a rhetorical, not a lexical, usage.
