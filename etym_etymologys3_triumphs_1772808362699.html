TRIUMPHS: AN EVOLUTION ESSAY FROM A BACCHIC HYMN TO A ROMAN PROCESSION The remarkable story of "triumph" begins not in Rome but in Greece, in the cult of Dionysus. The Greek word "thriambos" named a hymn, a processional song, or the cry raised in honour of the god of wine, ecstasy, and theatre. In the classical period the word appears in the context of the dithyramb - the choral hymn sung in Dionysus's honour - and in comedy; "thriambos" was also a name or epithet applied to Dionysus himself. The word's origin is obscure: it has no secure Indo-European etymology, and scholars suspect a loan from a Pre-Greek or Anatolian source, or from the culture of Phrygia, which had its own ecstatic religious processions. What matters for the later history is the shape of the original thing: a noisy, singing, dancing procession in honour of a god, whose cry became a shout of celebration. THE BORROWING INTO LATIN At some point in the early history of Rome, Latin adopted the Greek word as "triumphus", giving it a Latin declension and, almost certainly, the mediation of Etruscan - the Etruscan inscriptions contain the form "triumpe", and Etruscan society contributed many ceremonial forms to early Rome. The Latin treatment of the Greek aspirate ("th" becoming "t") is regular, and the word settled as "triumphus", "triumphi" - a second-declension masculine noun. The semantic transfer was bold and culturally revealing: the Romans took a word for a god's festival and applied it to the greatest honour of the Roman state, the procession of a conquering general. The triumph as Romans practised it was indeed quasi-religious: the general wore the garments of Jupiter, his face painted red, his army, captives, and spoils paraded behind him, through the streets up to the Capitol, where a bull was sacrificed to Jupiter Optimus Maximus. The religious processional frame - song, costume, divine association - was inherited, but the god honoured was now the supreme god of the Roman state, and the celebration was of human conquest rather than divine ecstasy. THE ROMAN INSTITUTION In the Republic the triumph was a constitutional institution, not a mere parade. The Senate voted it, and strict rules governed who could receive one: the general had to be of consular or praetorian rank, to have won a major war against a foreign enemy, and to have brought his army home. The triumph was the visible summit of a senatorial career, and the competition for it drove Roman expansion - generals campaigned to earn the right to be carried in triumph through the city. The Fasti Triumphales, the inscribed list of triumphs, record centuries of these processions, and it is precisely this record that Gibbon evokes with "a rapid succession of triumphs". The word also acquired a technical companion, the "ovatio" or lesser ovation, granted for smaller victories; and the triumphing general ("triumphator") could be honoured again only under strict conditions. Under the emperors the triumph was reserved to the imperial house, and the triumphant ceremony was transformed into an imperial monopoly - another layer of meaning the word carried into late antiquity. THE WORD IN MEDIEVAL AND RENAISSANCE EUROPE Latin "triumphus" passed into the Romance languages: Old French "triumphe" or "triomphe" carried the sense of victory and of festive entry. English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century - "triumphe", "tryumph(e)" - and used it both of the ancient Roman rite and of contemporary royal entries and victory celebrations. The Renaissance gave the word a new artistic life: Petrarch's "I Trionfi" ("The Triumphs") was a set of allegorical poems in which personified figures - Love, Chastity, Death, Fame, Time, Eternity - ride in symbolic processions, and this literary conceit, together with the Roman iconography of the triumph (the "triumphal arch", the "triumphal chariot"), flooded European art and literature. A great ruler's formal entry into a city was routinely described as a triumph, and "triumphal arches" were built in the style of the Roman monuments. The word thus kept its concrete processional meaning while adding a rich allegorical and artistic one. ENGLISH EXPANSION AND MODERN SENSE In English the figurative broadening was complete by the seventeenth century: "triumph" came to mean any great victory or signal success, and by extension the exultant joy that accompanies success. The verb "to triumph" and the phrase "triumph over" became standard for overcoming obstacles, enemies, or difficulties. Gibbon, writing in 1776, uses the plural "triumphs" in its full historical sense - the Roman institution - while his readers would also hear the extended sense of "great victories". Today the institutional meaning survives in classical-historical prose and in "triumphal" collocations ("triumphal arch", "triumphal procession"), while the everyday sense is entirely general: a triumph is any brilliant, celebrated success. The word's journey from a Bacchic cry to Roman ceremony to Renaissance allegory to modern success is one of the most spectacular semantic developments in the history of the language.
  • 5th c. BCE: Greek "thriambos" names a Bacchic hymn and processional cry.
  • 3rd-2nd c. BCE: Latin "triumphus" names the Roman victory procession (Plautus).
  • Fasti Triumphales record centuries of Roman triumphs.
  • 12th c.: Old French "triumphe" carries the senses of victory and festive entry.
  • 14th c.: Middle English "tryumph(e)" appears (Chaucer, Gower).
  • Renaissance: Petrarch's "Trionfi" and Roman iconography enrich the word artistically.
  • 17th c.: the figurative sense "any great success" is established in English.
  • Modern: "triumph" is the default word for brilliant, celebrated success.
CONCLUSION The plural "triumphs" in Gibbon's sentence is a word with nearly two and a half thousand years of history compressed into it: a Greek religious cry for a wine-god; a Roman state procession of divine costume and human conquest; a medieval and Renaissance symbol of allegorical victory; and a modern synonym for brilliant success. Every layer is still faintly audible in the word, which is why "a rapid succession of triumphs" conveys not just winning but ceremonial glory, divine sanction, and the relentless rhythm of an empire built on celebration.
