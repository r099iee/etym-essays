In the same 1982 preface, Randolph Quirk writes that his volume 'permits of no such convenient premonitory paradigm.' Here paradigm is the plain object: it is the ready-made model that would describe the book's ideal reader in advance — a convenient pattern against which each prospective reader could be matched. The author concedes, with gentle self-deprecation, that his grammar cannot be advertised by any neat, pre-packaged mold of the audience it seeks; there is no such convenient premonitory paradigm. In this context paradigm keeps its oldest core sense, 'a pattern, model, or example held up for comparison or imitation,' and the sentence trades on the word's ability to name both a concrete template and an abstract scheme.

Polysemy: the senses of PARADIGM
  • Sense 1 — a typical example or model (primary): A paradigm is an outstanding or typical instance, a pattern that serves as a model or example to be copied: 'Her essay became a paradigm for the whole seminar.' This sense descends directly from the Greek root meaning 'that which is shown alongside.'
  • Sense 2 — grammar (technical): In linguistics, a paradigm is the complete set of inflected forms of a word, displayed as a model — the paradigm of a declension or conjugation: 'In Latin class the noun mensa serves as the standard paradigm for first-declension nouns.' The bridge: just as a pattern is held beside copies for comparison, the set of forms is held beside the learner's attempts.
  • Sense 3 — philosophy (Platonic): In Plato, a paradeigma is an ideal Form or exemplar, the perfect model of which earthly things are imperfect copies. The bridge: from 'shown alongside' to 'the ideal shown alongside its shadows.'
  • Sense 4 — science (Kuhnian, post-1962): In Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, a paradigm is the whole constellation of assumptions, methods, values, and exemplars that governs a scientific community — and 'paradigm shift' names the revolution in which one such constellation replaces another. The bridge: from a single model to the entire framework in which science is done.
  • Sense 5 — computing/technology: A programming paradigm is a style or school of programming — imperative, object-oriented, functional, logic-based. The bridge is the modern habit of treating a methodology as a model to be adopted wholesale.
  • Sense 6 — business and general usage (loosened modern sense): In management and everyday speech, paradigm has been stretched to mean any prevailing approach, worldview, or business model — 'a new paradigm for retail' — often disparaged as buzzword. The bridge: the word's prestige as a marker of deep structure made it attractive for anything claimed to be fundamental.

The semantic boundary: Paradigm is often confused with its near-neighbours, but each has its own shape. Model is the broadest term, any representation or person to be imitated; paradigm is a model with the authority of an exemplar. Archetype is the original, primal image from which copies descend — older, mythic, Jungian — while a paradigm is contemporary and functional. Prototype is the first working specimen of a thing, concrete and technical, not an abstract scheme. Template is a mechanical pattern for shaping copies, with no intellectual content. Exemplar is an individual case or person worthy of imitation — a paradigm realised in a single instance. Schema and framework name the structural skeleton of an idea without the prestige of paradigm; benchmark and yardstick are standards of comparison, not models to be imitated. Only paradigm carries the full weight of 'a pattern shown alongside for imitation and comparison' — which is precisely why Kuhn chose it for the worldviews of science.
