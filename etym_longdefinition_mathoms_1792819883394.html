Mathoms, plural of Tolkien's revived noun mathom, are objects of forgotten use that their owners cannot bring themselves to discard: birthday presents kept but never used, tools whose purpose has lapsed, ornaments nobody loves but nobody will burn. They are the Shire's affectionate name for clutter with a conscience, things preserved not because they are useful but because throwing them away feels wasteful.

In the source sentence the narrator explains that Bilbo's birthday presents were not always new, since one or two old mathoms of forgotten uses had circulated all around the district. The joke lands because every reader recognises the circulation: the unwanted vase, the duplicate kettle, the ornament that tours the neighbourhood from mantelpiece to mantelpiece, each giver quietly passing on what they never wanted. Bilbo, generous as ever, usually gave new presents and kept the recycled ones himself, which tells us as much about his character as about Hobbit economics.

Core meaning in detail: A mathom is defined by three traits together: first, it has lost its original function or never had one for its current owner; second, it retains enough imagined worth, sentiment, or sheer Hobbit thrift that destruction feels wrong; third, it therefore accumulates, displayed in the Mathom-house museum at Michel Delving or wrapped up again for the next birthday. The word is almost always plural because mathoms travel in drifts, never singly, like odd socks or spare buttons.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Unwanted but undiscardable objects in Tolkien's Shire (primary modern sense, from 1937): the circulating birthday presents of the source sentence. This is the sense every reader meets first, and the only sense recognised by most dictionaries that list the word at all.
  • Museum pieces in the Mathom-house at Michel Delving (Tolkien lore sense): arms, trophies, and curios the Shire-hoards as a communal memory, from old weapons to souvenir spoons. The bridge is institutionalisation: when private mathoms grow historic enough, the community gives them a building, turning clutter into heritage.
  • The Old English ancestor sense — treasure, jewel, precious gift (historical, 8th–11th c.): māþum in Beowulf means costly swords, rings, and war-gear given by lords to retainers. The bridge is Tolkien's learned irony: what was once a king's gold becomes a Hobbit's spare umbrella stand, high treasure sunk into comic bathos through thirteen centuries of inflation and peace.
  • Collector and fandom slang — any shelf of kept-but-useless curios (extended modern use): wargamers, readers, and archivists call their own drawers of spare dice, duplicate paperbacks, and convention freebies their mathoms. The bridge is self-recognition: Tolkien gave hoarders everywhere a dignified native word for the drawer nobody opens.
  • Gaming loot and inventory clutter (tabletop and video-game sense): quest rewards too weak to equip but too storied to sell, kept in the bank vault or bottomless bag. The bridge is functional: role-playing inventories reproduce the Mathom-house exactly, a museum of forgotten uses carried on one's back.
  • Metaphorical sense — stale ideas and inherited opinions (figurative): critics speak of intellectual mathoms, doctrines repeated because no one will throw them out. The bridge carries the image from mantelpiece to mind: beliefs too can circulate all around the district long after their use is forgotten.

Register and connotation: mathoms is warm, comic, and deeply English, smelling of attics, jumble sales, and village halls. It never sneers: unlike junk or rubbish, it grants the objects a shabby dignity and their keepers a thrifty virtue. Outside Tolkien it marks the speaker instantly as a reader, a philologist's wink across the table, and using it of one's own possessions is a small confession of love for things that do not work.
