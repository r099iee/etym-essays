Upturned is a compound with a split personality: its first half is pure Germanic hearth-words, its second half a Mediterranean craftsman's tool that travelled from a Greek workshop to an English farm. Few everyday adjectives unite the two great streams of English — Anglo-Saxon and Latinate — so neatly in a single syllable-pair.

First element — up:

  • PIE *upo (‘from below, up, over’): one of the oldest spatial words reconstructible, giving Sanskrit úpa (‘toward’), Greek hypó (‘under’ — the same root viewed from underneath!), and Latin sub (‘under’).
  • Proto-Germanic *upp: the Germanic branch keeps the ‘aloft’ sense — Gothic iup, Old Norse upp, German auf.
  • Old English upp, up: fully at home by the 9th century in compounds (up-astah, ‘up-rose’) and commands; a thousand years later it has barely changed its shape or sense.

Second element — turn:

  • Greek tórnos (5th c. BCE): the carpenter's compass, the lathe — the tool that draws perfect circles. Sophocles uses it metaphorically for a rule or standard.
  • Latin tornus → tornāre: Rome borrows the tool and verbs it: tornāre, ‘to turn on a lathe, round off, polish’. Horace boasts of verses ‘turned’ on the lathe of revision.
  • Old English tyrnan, turnian (9th c.): borrowed early — probably via Frankish craftsmen — the verb generalises from lathe-work to all rotation: to turn the body, turn back, turn aside.
  • Middle English turnen: by Chaucer's day it is among the commonest verbs in the language, keeps its Latinate ancestry invisible, and spins off turn the noun (a bend, a shift, a performance).

The marriage (16th c.): Upturn the verb — ‘to turn up, invert, expose’ — first appears in Tudor texts, and its past participle upturned becomes the standing adjective for faces, furrows, and keels. The compound is transparent enough that no speaker pauses over it, yet it quietly weds a word older than farming (up) to a word born in a bronze-age joiner's shop (turn).

Tolkien's faces: When the hobbits lift ‘upturned faces’ to the glowing flowers, the philologist in Tolkien may well have heard both halves sing: good Germanic up doing what it has done for five thousand years, and well-travelled turn completing a journey from Athenian lathe to Shire garden party. Afterlife: the noun upturn — ‘an improvement, a rise in fortune’ — grows from the same verb in the 19th century, Wall Street's favourite euphemism for recovery. So the compound that describes capsized boats also names economic rescues: what turns up may be a keel or a market, ruin or revival, and English happily uses one word for both. The adjective itself stays humbler, close to faces and furrows, leaving high finance to its noun.
