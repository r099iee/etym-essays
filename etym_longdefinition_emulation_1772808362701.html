In Edward Gibbon's opening chapter of The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the phrase "the active emulation of the consuls" is not decorative: it is one of three political engines — alongside "the policy of the senate" and "the martial enthusiasm of the people" — that together explain how the Roman Republic "achieved" its principal conquests. Emulation here names a precise psychological mechanism institutionalized in the Roman magistracy. Because the consulship was a dual, annual office, each year's two consuls competed with each other and with the memory of all their predecessors for military glory, spoils, and the triumph. Emulation is the interpersonal heat that turned a constitutional structure into a continuous engine of expansion. Core definition. Emulation is the effort to equal or surpass a person or achievement, typically by modelling one's own conduct on an admired example. It differs from mere copying in that it is competitive: the emulator does not simply want to reproduce the model but to measure up to it and, if possible, to outdo it. The word therefore occupies a precise semantic slot between imitation (which reproduces form) and rivalry (which may be hostile or indifferent to excellence). Polysemy and modern senses. The word has a remarkably wide current range, and nearly every sense is a refraction of the original Latin idea of competitive modelling:
  • Classical and rhetorical "noble emulation": the virtuous desire to equal the achievements of great men, praised by Roman moralists (Latin aemulatio) and by Renaissance humanists as the spur to glory; defined against envy (invidia), which wishes to pull the other down.
  • Ordinary modern sense: competitive imitation in work, sport, and study — "students working in emulation of a high-achieving peer" — retaining the admiring colour plus the edge of competition.
  • Computer science and technology: emulation as the reproduction by one system (software or hardware) of the functions of another, typically older, system — e.g., running a video game console's operating environment on a personal computer. The semantic bridge is the ancient idea of a copy that performs exactly like the original.
  • Software "emulator": the program or device that performs that reproduction; a sibling of emulation (from the same verb emulate), not a separate root.
  • Economics and market analysis (jargon): "emulation" for the competitive imitation of firms — one firm copying the successful practices of a rival to stay competitive.
  • Sociology and social psychology: emulation as the mechanism by which lower-status groups adopt the consumption or behaviour patterns of higher-status groups — a driver of social mobility and of "keeping up with the Joneses".
  • Psychology and child development: emulation as the tendency to copy and equal others, contrasted with envy and jealousy; infants and young children learn largely by emulating caregivers.
  • Literary and translation studies: emulation as the art of imitating the style of an admired author with the aim of matching or surpassing it — the Renaissance "imitation versus emulation" debate.
  • Historical political usage: "emulation" as a term of praise for civic competitiveness, common in classical-republican and eighteenth-century constitutional thought (Gibbon, and the admirers of Montesquieu).
  • Obsolete senses: in early modern English "emulation" could also mean jealous rivalry or factious strife (e.g., "emulations" among the "works of the flesh" in the Authorized Version of the Bible, Galatians 5:20) — senses now lost.
Historical bridges between senses. The bridge from "noble rivalry" to "technical reproduction" is the notion of a copy that equals its model in function. A computer emulator "matches or surpasses" the original machine's behaviour in exactly the way an emulating student matches the admired peer: the later thing is a functional double of the standard, produced deliberately so that it can perform as well. The bridge from "imitation" to "competition" was already complete in classical Latin, where aemulatio meant rivalry pursued by copying; English inherited that double charge and has never lost it. The obsolete negative senses ("jealousy", "faction") faded because the moralizing tradition that praised noble emulation, from Cicero through the humanists, gradually won the register war. Register and collocation. Emulation is formal-to-neutral; it sits comfortably in moral philosophy, history, technology writing, and journalism, and is rare in everyday casual speech, where "competition" or "copying" would be used. Characteristic collocations: "active emulation", "noble emulation", "emulation of the great", "in emulation of", "console emulation", "a healthy emulation". The adjective "emulous" (Latin aemulus) is the archaic literary twin; "emulate" is the verb; "emulator" the agent noun. (Full synonym boundary analysis appears in similarWords.)
