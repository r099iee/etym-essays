From open space to ransacked hold. The unlikely ancestor of rummaged is a word of empty, available space. The Proto-Indo-European root *reuh₁- meant "open space, room" — the sense of land or space opened out and made usable — and its Indo-Iranian relative, Avestan ravah-, "open space, open field," shows how ancient and how wide the idea was. In the Germanic branch the root formed *rūmaz, "space, room, open place," the same word that lies behind English room, Old Norse rūm, and Old High German rūm. For the Germanic peoples, rūm was the everyday noun for a clearing in the forest, an open area in a settlement, or the space within a house where things could be put — and, crucially, the space in the hold of a ship where cargo could be stowed. The idea of "room" as capacity-for-stowing is already present in the oldest Germanic usage, and it was this sense that the word carried into the sea-going world of the North Sea and the Channel.

The Frankish passage into French. The decisive event in the word's life came when the Franks carried the Germanic stem into Gaul. Frankish *rūm entered Gallo-Romance as rimer, "to put in place, to stow," and the prefixed verb arrimer meant specifically to arrange cargo in a ship's hold — to use the space of the vessel efficiently, to "make room" for the bales and chests below deck. From arrimer came the noun arrimage, "the stowage of cargo," and it was in this nautical costume that the word crossed the Channel in the early sixteenth century. English rummage, first recorded about 1520, meant the arranging or stowing of a ship's cargo in its hold — a word of neat, professional, orderly work, the exact opposite of everything the word means today. It is worth pausing on this first sense, because the entire later history of the word is a fall from this initial orderliness.

The great swerve: from stowage to search. Ships had to be searched for smuggled goods. From the 1560s and 1570s the customs examination of a vessel — the breaking-open of chests, the turning-out of bales in the confined darkness of the hold — was itself called a rummage, and the verb to rummage came to mean "to search a ship for contraband." The semantic mechanism is beautifully economical: the hold was the place of stowage; the customs officer searched the hold; therefore the "rummage" — the cargo-space, and by extension the cargo stowed there — became the object, and then the act, of the search. Within fifty years the word's orderliness had turned into its opposite: a rummage was now a violent, disordered, intrusive turning-over of goods in search of what was hidden among them.

The broadening into everyday disorder. From the sea, the word came ashore. In the eighteenth century the meaning broadened from the specific customs search of a ship to any messy, hasty search — to rummage a drawer, a chest, a pile of baggage, to turn everything upside down careless of how it lies afterward. The concrete image of the customs officer plunging through a cargo hold supplied the tone of the modern verb: quick, tactile, disrespectful of order. In 1808 the phrase rummage sale appears, originally a charity sale of odds and ends (the word carried an early association with goods of doubtful origin, sold after a ship's forfeited cargo), turning the same disorder to charitable good use. By the nineteenth century the verb had settled into its modern informal place, and it is this settled modern sense — rough, hasty, tactile, goal-less searching — that Tolkien picks up in 1937 when the goblins rummage the dwarves' broken packages. The ancient sea-going memory is still faintly alive in the word: a rummage is, etymologically, a ransacking of the hold, and when the goblins plunge their hands into the bags, the oldest cargo-space of the North Sea voyages is present just beneath the surface of the prose.

Sound, form, and meaning through time. Phonologically the word shows the ordinary fate of a French loan in English: Old French arrimage was reshaped to English rummage by dropping the initial a- and re-stressing, and the disyllable [ˈrʌmɪdʒ] with its medial double -mm- has been stable for four centuries. Morphologically the word is a plain English weak verb — rummage, rummaged, rummaging — and the noun survives in the same form. Semantically the history is a single long ironic arc: from "open space," to "room for cargo," to "the orderly stowage of cargo," to "the customs search of the cargo," to "any messy search." The word has travelled, in five centuries, from neatness to disorder, and in doing so it has carried the whole history of the sea-trade in its wake — a history that is invisible in ordinary use but entirely recoverable, and one that Tolkien, with his scholar's ear for the archaeology of words, chose with perfect precision.
