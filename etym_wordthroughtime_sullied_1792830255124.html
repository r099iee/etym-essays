- 12th century — the pigsty verb: Old French souillier means to roll or wallow in the souil, the muddy wallow and pigsty; barnyard filth, literal and low.
- Mid-14th century — English takes the stain: sulien enters Middle English for dirtying cloth and bodies, still physical mud and mire.
- 16th century — honour learns to stain: Shakespeare's age extends sullying to names, beds, and triumphs, so reputations can be dirtied exactly like linen.
- 17th–18th century — the pulpit polishes it: sermons warn of hands sullied with blood and souls sullied with sin; the word grows grave, sacred, judicial.
- 19th century — the poet's favourite fall: sullied snow, sullied veils, sullied moons fill verse as industry smokes the skies; physical and moral staining merge.
- Today — the guardian of whiteness: mostly moral and formal — sullied reputations, victories, memories — with unsullied praised as the rare exception, exactly as Tolkien uses it for the Three Rings.
