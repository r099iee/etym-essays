1. Indo-European roots of bestowal: the base *deh₃-. The etymological ancestry of rendition originates with the fundamental Proto-Indo-European verbal root *deh₃-, meaning "to give, grant, or bestow." This root is among the most prolific in the Indo-European lexicon, yielding Sanskrit dā- (दा, "to give"), Greek dídōmi (δίδωμι, "I give"), Russian dat' (дать, "to give"), and Latin dare ("to give"). In the Italic branch, dare formed compounds through prefixation to express nuanced spatial and relational transactions of giving, exchanging, and restoring.

2. Classical Latin prefixation: the formation of reddere. Within Latin, prefixing the iterative and reversive prefix re- / red- ("back, again, in return") to the verb dare produced the highly versatile compound reddere ("to give back, return, restore, deliver, replace, or render"). Roman authors employed reddere across administrative, economic, and intellectual spheres: one could reddere rationem (render an account), reddere epistulam (deliver a letter), or reddere verbum verbo (render word for word in translation, as advised by Cicero and Horace). In this classical phase, reddere already united two fundamental concepts that would endure into modern times: the physical act of returning a tangible object, and the intellectual act of translating or reflecting an original concept into a new form.

3. Vulgar Latin nasalization and Romance emergence. During the transition from Classical to Late and Vulgar Latin (circa 4th–6th century CE), a well-documented phonological development altered the root. Under the strong analogical influence of the rhyming, highly frequent verb prendere (contracted from prehendere, "to seize, take"), the standard Latin reddere underwent nasalization, yielding the Vulgar Latin reconstructed form *rendere ("to give back, yield, surrender"). This nasalized stem passed into Old French as rendre during the 10th and 11th centuries, developing a wide array of transitive and reflexive meanings, including "to yield, produce profit, vomit, surrender, or submit" (whence the reflexive se rendre, "to give oneself up, capitulate").

4. Middle French military siege warfare and the coinage of rendition. In 16th-century Middle French (circa 1530s–1540s), during the violent upheaval of the Italian Wars and the French Wars of Religion, military commanders and legal chroniclers required a precise nominalization for the act of surrendering a besieged fortress, fortified town, or garrison. By appending the Latinate action suffix -ition (from Latin -itiō) to the verbal stem of rendre, they coined the noun rendition ("the act of surrendering, yielding up, or capitulating"). In diplomatic capitulation treaties, the "articles of rendition" set forth the formal terms under which a conquered citadel was delivered into the hands of the victor.

5. Elizabethan English adoption and semantic expansion. English borrowed the noun rendition from Middle French during the late Elizabethan era (circa 1590s–1600). Writers such as Francis Bacon and contemporary chroniclers initially used it in its strict military sense: the surrender of towns and castles in the Low Countries or Ireland. However, Renaissance scholars quickly recognized its affinity with the related verb render (which had entered Middle English centuries earlier). Consequently, by the early 17th century, rendition expanded into literary and scholastic usage to denote the "translation, explanation, or paraphrase" of a classical or biblical passage—delivering the meaning of an ancient text back to the reader in the vernacular tongue.

6. Nineteenth-century aesthetic specialization and twentieth-century geopolitical return. During the 19th-century Victorian era, the rapid growth of public concert halls, theatrical companies, and art criticism catalyzed another profound semantic expansion. Critics began employing rendition to describe an actor's or musician's personal artistic performance or interpretation of a work. A pianist's performance of a Chopin nocturne was not merely a mechanical reading, but an interpretive "rendition" that delivered the composer's soul through the artist's personal execution. Concurrently, in American constitutional law, interstate rendition denoted the legal return of fugitive slaves or criminals between states. Finally, in the post-Cold War and post-9/11 eras, the term underwent a dark geopolitical reinvigoration through the phrase "extraordinary rendition," bringing the word's ancient 16th-century heritage of involuntary bodily surrender into modern international counterterrorism and human rights law.
