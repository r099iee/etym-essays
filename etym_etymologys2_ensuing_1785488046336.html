LINEAR CHAIN (single root word ENSUING) Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *sekʷ- “to follow, accompany, pursue” (c. 4000 BCE), the root of walking in another’s tracks; attested through Greek hépomai, Latin sequor, Sanskrit sácate, and Old Irish sechid. Stage 2 — Latin insequī “to follow upon, to pursue” (Classical Latin), the compound ancestor of the English verb: in- “upon, after” + sequī “to follow,” already carrying the “follow hard upon” force that survives in the modern word. Stage 3 — Old French ensuivre / ensuir “to follow after, to result” (from Latin insequī, via Vulgar Latin *insequere; recorded in medieval French by the 11th–12th c.), the sense already beginning to shift from physical pursuit to logical consequence. Stage 4 — Anglo-French ensuen / ensuivre “to follow, to result” (the legal and literary French of England, 13th–14th c.), the immediate channel by which the verb entered English. Stage 5 — Middle English ensuen “to follow, to pursue, to result” (first recorded 14th c.; used by Chaucer for cause and effect, e.g. of results that “ensuen” from actions). Stage 6 — Modern English ensue “to follow as a consequence or result” (the pursuit sense dies out by the 17th c.); the present participle ENSUING (“following immediately after; consequential”) crystallises as an attributive adjective, as on this card. NOTES - The Greek reflex ἕπομαι (hépomai) “I follow” is the true cognate of Latin sequor; the root also appears in Sanskrit sácate “he follows, accompanies,” whence Hindi sakhā “friend” and sahacar “companion,” and in Old Irish sechid “he pursues.” - The prefix in- in insequī supplies the “upon, immediately after” force; English ensue has always carried a sense of close, consequential succession rather than mere order. - The modern participle-adjective ensuing is a late crystallisation; the OED records the adjectival use chiefly from the 17th century onward, and its frequency in modern narrative prose greatly exceeds that of the bare verb. - The Latin family of sequī is enormous (sequence, subsequent, consequence, pursue, execute, sect, society, second, suitor); only the insequī line leads directly to ENSUE.
