1. The two stock-roots. "Onset" is a compound of two of the oldest Germanic building blocks. The first, on, descends from PIE *h₂en- "on, at, up", through Proto-Germanic *ana to Old English on — a preposition so basic that it has never stopped being one of the hundred most-used words of English. The second, set, descends from the PIE root *sed- "to sit" (Sanskrit sádati "sits", Latin sedēre "to sit", Greek hézomai "I sit"), which in Proto-Germanic produced the intransitive *sitjaną "to sit" and, by the ancient causative mechanism (the umlaut vowel-shift), *satjaną "to cause to sit, to set, place" — the source of Old English settan, "to set, put, appoint". The sit/set pair (like fall/fell and rise/raise) is one of the oldest grammatical contrasts in Germanic — intransitive vs causative — and from that single twig grows the enormous modern verb "set": set a table, set a trap, set in, set upon, set off, set out.

2. The compound verb. Old English onsett(an), "to set upon, impose, assail", pictures force being placed upon someone — the attacker lays his attack on his victim. The verb is recorded in Old English and continues as Middle English onsetten, attested by the 13th century in the sense "to attack". From the 16th century the noun detaches: "onset" (c. 1530) means an assault, a setting-on, and the same construction recurs across the Germanic languages (German ansetzen "to set on, to begin"; Dutch aanzetten), which shows how natural the figure was to the whole family. The noun is thus not a borrowing but a native conversion of an ancient English verb, and "onset" sits in the native word-stock alongside outset, offset and upset.

3. The great semantic drift. The decisive change happens within a generation: by the 1560s "onset" also means "the beginning of a period or condition" — the onset of winter, of age, of night. The bridge is the grammar of the verb phrase: what "sets on" you attacks you; but what merely "comes on" (winter coming on) also has an onset. The agency moves from the attacker to the event: a season or a sickness "comes on" a person exactly as an enemy sets on him, so the same word names both. In the 17th and 18th centuries the two senses flourish side by side — armies make onsets, winters have onsets — and the medical tradition begins to borrow the word: "the onset of the fever" is a clinical phrase by the 19th century, and the case-writing of the 20th fixed "onset of symptoms", "age at onset" and "acute versus insidious onset" as standard timestamps of disease. Meanwhile the aggressive sense retreats before "attack" and "assault", leaving "onset" with a curious double life: fully alive in medicine, climatology and phonology, archaic in battle narrative.

4. The modern profile. Four highways carry the word today. Medicine: onset of disease, early-onset and late-onset conditions, the timestamp of every case report. Meteorological and geological narration: the onset of the monsoon, of the ice age — the word marks a boundary moment when a state changes. Linguistics: the syllable onset, a coinage of 20th-century structural linguistics, with the triad onset–nucleus–coda taught to every phonology student. Computing and music technology: onset detection, the instant a note or sound event begins. In each domain the semantic constant is the same — a beginning treated as an arrival, an event that starts by "coming on" — and "at the onset" remains the prepositional formula of both ordinary and technical prose. Sound shape: Old English on /on/ → Middle English /ɔn/ → Received Pronunciation /ɒn/, the compound taking noun-stress /ˈɒn.set/, distinct from the phrase "set on" /ˌsɛt ˈɒn/.

5. Literary resonance. The older sense never quite died; it is kept alive by historians and by writers who want archaic bite. Tolkien's "his onset grew ever slower" (The Hobbit, 1937) is precisely such a resuscitation: a modern novel of the 1930s using the 16th-century military sense to describe a goblin charge losing speed as the valley widens. The word in that sentence does double work — it is at once a charge and the beginning of its own failure — which is exactly why the lexicographer's history of "onset" is, in the end, a history of beginnings: how an English word for "laying on" became the English word for things that come on you, from winter to war to the first symptom of a fever.
