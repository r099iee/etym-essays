ferried — past tense and past participle of the verb ferry: transported, conveyed, or carried across a body of water (a river, strait, estuary, channel, or sea) by boat, ship, or other watercraft, and by extension conveyed across any distance or repeated shuttle between two fixed points. 1. Primary sense: conveyance across water by boat.
  • To carry people, animals, goods, or vehicles across water in a boat or ship, especially on a short, regular crossing: passengers ferried from one bank to the other; a ferryman ferrying a boat across a river.
  • To act as the vehicle of such a crossing: the boat ferried supplies to the island throughout the day; the rescue team ferried stranded residents to safety.
  • In historical and literary prose the verb frequently carries mythic weight: to be ferried across the water of death, as Charon ferried the souls of the departed across the Styx — precisely the resonance of Gibbon's sentence about the spirits of the departed being ferried over from the land of the Franks at midnight.
2. Extended and secondary modern senses (polysemy).
  • Aviation and military logistics: to fly an aircraft from its place of manufacture, purchase, or storage to its operating base or to a theater of operations, usually without revenue cargo or passengers — a "ferry flight." Aircraft are routinely ferried across oceans or to forward bases; a "ferry pilot" flies such delivery missions. Here the water is replaced by air, but the core idea of conveying something from one fixed point to another under its own power remains intact.
  • Colloquial routine shuttling: to transport people or things back and forth repeatedly between two locations, usually by car or on foot: "ferrying the kids to soccer practice," "ferrying documents between offices." The boat is gone; what remains is the notion of short, repeated, bounded conveyance between two points.
  • Logistics and goods movement: in transport planning, ferrying means moving goods stage by stage or on a scheduled shuttle service between terminals, depots, or staging areas.
  • Data networking: "data ferrying" or "store-and-forward ferrying" — physically carrying data (e.g., by a mobile node or removable medium) between disconnected network segments that cannot communicate directly; the term borrows the water-crossing image wholesale.
  • Figurative and poetic: to carry across a boundary, threshold, or between two states — an idea ferried between cultures, a memory ferried across time, a soul ferried to the afterlife. This figurative use preserves the oldest semantic ingredient: crossing over from one domain to another.
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges. The whole family descends from the Proto-Germanic causative *farjanan "to make go, to cause to travel, to convey," built on the root of fare "to go, to travel" (ultimately PIE *per- "to go through, to cross, to pass over"). Because the most frequent kind of "making something go across" in the Germanic maritime world was crossing a body of water, the verb specialized to mean "to convey across water," and the noun ferry (the vessel itself) followed by the late medieval period. The historical bridge to aviation is purely technological: when aircraft had to be moved from factory to squadron in the twentieth century, the same word for "convey across" was reapplied, since a ferry flight still carries a plane between two fixed points. The bridge to routine shuttling is semantic bleaching: repeated short conveyance between two points became the dominant modern image, so that ferried today often simply means "chauffeured around in a vehicle," with the nautical origin faded or half-forgotten. 4. Grammar and form.
  • ferried is the regular weak past tense and past participle of ferry (ferry ~ ferried ~ ferried), formed with the suffix -ed, respelled -ied after a final consonant + y.
  • It functions both as a finite past tense ("They ferried the wounded across the river") and as a passive participle / participial adjective ("the wounded were ferried across"; "ferried goods").
  • In the source sentence the passive construction "were ferried over" shows the participle in passive use, the preposition over emphasizing the crossing of the water itself.
5. Related forms and cognates.
  • Derived nouns and compounds: ferry, ferryboat, ferryman, ferriage (the fee or act of ferrying), ferry flight, ferry service.
  • Cognates: Old Norse ferja "to ferry, to convey by boat"; German Fähre "ferry"; Dutch veer; all from the Germanic root of fare "to travel, to go."
6. Register and connotation.
  • In ordinary speech ferried is a neutral, common verb; in the "aviation logistics" sense it is more formal and technical.
  • In literary prose the verb retains an archaic, solemn shimmer because of the Charon association; a writer who says "ferried" rather than "carried" deliberately evokes a crossing, often with mythic, ironic, or elegiac colour — exactly Gibbon's register.
