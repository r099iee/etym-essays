In the portrait of Aroma (Xiren) in Honglou meng, "Aroma had a certain dogged streak in her nature" — a single-minded devotion that makes her utterly faithful to Grandmother Jia while she is Grandmother Jia's servant, and just as exclusively devoted to Bao-yu once her services are transferred. The adjective dogged is chosen precisely because it captures a loyalty that is not merely affectionate but grimly persistent: it names an unswerving, almost mechanical single-mindedness of attachment and duty. The tone is observational and slightly double-edged — fidelity that is admirable in its constancy but absolute, transferable, and closed to competing claims. Core definition. As an adjective, dogged means having or showing tenacity and grim persistence; refusing to yield or abandon a course of action in the face of difficulty, opposition, or fatigue. The word draws its force from an old animal metaphor: to be dogged is to hold on the way a dog holds on — refusing to release its grip, refusing to abandon the scent or the pursuit. It typically describes sustained effort or attachment carried out stubbornly but steadily over time, often when success is uncertain. Polysemy and senses:
  • Primary modern sense: tenacious, stubbornly persistent — dogged determination, dogged pursuit, dogged resistance.
  • Animal-derived sense (historical): "like a dog", especially in the dog's negative stereotype — surly, currish, snappish, malicious. This sense is now rare or archaic, surviving mainly in older literature (e.g. "a dogged look" meaning sullen).
  • Applied to persons: describing a character trait of grim persistence, as in the novel's "dogged streak".
  • Applied to effort, pursuit, or investigation: dogged detective work, dogged persistence — the admired modern sense, praising endurance against obstacles.
  • As past tense of the verb to dog: "to follow closely, to track persistently" (e.g. "she dogged his footsteps", "dogged by misfortune"). This is a different word from the adjective though spelled the same; careful pronunciation differs.
  • Figurative passive use: "dogged by bad luck" — plagued or pursued by something, using the verb's sense of relentless following.
The historical bridge: the adjective develops from the noun dog through the association of dogs with pursuit and tenacity. Early uses stressed the dog's negative traits (surliness, cruelty); as hunting and working dogs came to be valued, the prized traits — endurance, refusal to abandon a trail — moved to the centre of the word's meaning, and by the eighteenth century dogged settled into the sense of unflagging persistence, usually with reluctant or outright admiration. Adjective versus verb form. Because the same spelling serves the adjective and the past tense of to dog, careful English distinguishes them in speech: the adjective is disyllabic /ˈdɒɡɪd/ with a clearly sounded second syllable, while the past tense is commonly pronounced /dɒɡd/. In the novel, "dogged streak" is the adjective: a characteristic of tenacious devotion rather than an action of following. Collocations and register. Common collocations: dogged determination, dogged persistence, dogged pursuit, dogged resistance, dogged loyalty, a dogged streak, dogged perseverance, dogged detective work. Register is standard literary and journalistic; the word is more elevated than plain stubborn and is often admiring when paired with determination or loyalty, though context can still tilt it toward inflexibility. In the novel, the collocation "dogged streak" fixes loyalty as an ingrained trait rather than a passing mood. Synonym boundaries:
  • tenacious: similar in denoting firm holding-on; differs because tenacious stresses attachment to a goal or opinion, while dogged emphasizes wearying, grim follow-through against difficulty.
  • stubborn: similar in refusing to yield; differs because stubborn is usually pejorative and implies irrational obstinacy, while dogged carries more admiring or neutral persistence.
  • obstinate: similar in unyielding refusal; differs because obstinate is strongly pejorative and wilful, while dogged stresses sustained endurance more than wilfulness.
  • persistent: similar in continuing despite obstacles; differs because persistent is neutral and broad, lacking the grim, animal-like tenacity and effort implied by dogged.
  • relentless: similar in not giving up; differs because relentless sounds harsher and more aggressive, often of external pressure or pursuit, while dogged can be quiet and dutiful.
  • resolute: similar in firm commitment; differs because resolute is more dignified and will-based, while dogged implies slogging endurance rather than intelligent resolution.
  • steadfast: similar in loyal constancy; differs because steadfast is positive and noble, without the grim or wearying overtones of dogged.
