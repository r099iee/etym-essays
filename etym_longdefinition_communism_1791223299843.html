Communism denotes both a political theory advocating common ownership of the means of production and the abolition of class and private property, and the historical state systems claiming to implement that theory, most famously the Soviet Union, Maoist China and their allies. In Marxist theory it is the final stateless, classless stage after socialism, summarized as "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs." In Cold War popular usage it means one-party authoritarian rule with central planning. In anthropology it can also mean small-scale communal sharing, as in "communism in food," where resources are pooled and distributed by need without market exchange.

Principal senses today:

  • Marxist-Leninist theory: The doctrine developed by Marx, Engels, Lenin and successors holding that capitalism will be overthrown by proletarian revolution leading through dictatorship of the proletariat to a classless society. Includes concepts of historical materialism, surplus value, alienation.
  • Actually existing Communist states / parties: Capital-C Communism as political regime: USSR (1917-1991), Eastern Bloc, PRC, Vietnam, Cuba, North Korea, Laos. Characterized historically by vanguard party monopoly, planned economy, collectivization, censorship. Example: "He lived under communism in Poland."
  • Colloquial Western pejorative for state intervention: Loose, often inaccurate use to label welfare policies, nationalization or cultural progressivism as "communism." Example: "They called public healthcare communism." Linguists note this is semantic bleaching driven by Cold War rhetoric.
  • Primitive / communal sharing (anthropological): Pre-state or band-level pooling of food, tools, childcare. Lewis Henry Morgan, Peter Kropotkin and Marshall Sahlins use "primitive communism" to describe hunter-gatherer demand-sharing where hoarding is socially prohibited. This is the sense in "communism in food" — not factories nationalized, but meat shared camp-wide.
  • Utopian / religious communalism: Intentional communities, monasteries, kibbutzim sometimes described as practicing communism or communistic living: common purse, common table, no private wages. Example: early Christian Acts 2:44-45 community of goods.
  • Adjectival extension communistic: Describing behaviour evoking sharing or levelling: "communistic generosity," "a communistic lunch where everyone dipped into the same pot."

Polysemy bridges: all senses share the core of common (Latin communis) ownership versus private ownership. The bridge from small-band food-sharing to global revolution is Engels' argument in The Origin of the Family that primordial gens-communism was destroyed by private property and must be restored at a higher technological level. Hence anthropological and Marxist senses are historically linked, not homonyms.

Usage notes: communism is usually uncountable ("communism failed") but countable in plural communisms when comparing variants (Soviet vs Chinese communisms). Capitalization distinguishes doctrine (lowercase) from party/state (often capitalized: Communist Party, Communist China). Connotation varies sharply by speaker: positive liberation for adherents, negative oppression for victims and Cold War liberals, neutral analytic for scholars. Register is formal political; in casual speech often hyperbolic.
