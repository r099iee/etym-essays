Hoard is a stored mass of valuables — especially treasure hidden, guarded, or accumulated — and, by extension, any secret stock carefully piled away. In older heroic and Old English poetry the hoard is the treasure of a king, a lord, or a dragon; in modern use it can be a glittering cache of gold or, darker, the compulsive stockpile of a hoarder.

Core senses.

  • Noun, treasure (primary, older sense): a hidden or guarded accumulation of precious things — gold, gems, ancient treasure — especially the dragon-guarded heap of Germanic legend.
  • Noun, general store (extended sense): any carefully kept secret supply, from a squirrel's hoard of nuts to a hoard of documents, coins, or food; secrecy and deliberate accumulation are the defining features.
  • Verb, to amass and keep (often pejorative): to gather and retain in quantity, usually beyond need and without sharing — "to hoard money," "to hoard supplies."

Register and colour. Hoard keeps a double life. On one side it is archaic-epic and glittering: the gleaming hoard of a dragon, the lord's gift-hoard that binds retainer to ring-giver in Old English poetry (in Beowulf the dragon guards its goldhord in a barrow). On the other side it is modern and faintly clinical: hoarding is now a recognized psychological term (compulsive hoarding disorder), and a hoarder is a figure of disorder rather than of wealth. The same word covers legendary gold and obsessive junk.

How it differs from its neighbours.

  • treasure — the broad cover term for valued riches; lacks the insistence on hiding and guarding.
  • cache — hidden storage, often temporary or tactical (a weapons cache); less about wealth accumulation.
  • trove / treasure-trove — a found hidden deposit, legally and romantically; a hoard is what the owner keeps hidden.
  • stockpile — a large stored amount, modern, strategic, or military; no treasure-romance.
  • stash — informal and secretive, often small or illicit; casual where hoard is weighty.
  • wealth — abstract total riches; not a store in a place.
  • reserve / stock — supplies kept for future need; neutral, where hoard suggests excess or greed.

Polysemy: full range of modern uses.

  • Economics / personal finance: "hoarding" describes accumulating cash or goods rather than spending or circulating them — historically condemned in times of scarcity, as "hoarding and profiteering" were during wartime rationing.
  • Psychology / medicine: hoarding disorder (DSM-5) is the compulsive acquisition of possessions and failure to discard them, regardless of value; "hoarding" names the behaviour, and a "hoarder" the person.
  • Technology: "data hoarding" — building large personal archives of files, media, or information; the metaphor carries the secrecy and excess of the old word.
  • Archaeology: a "hoard" is a standard technical term for a buried deposit of valuables — coin hoards, gold hoards, votive hoards — one of the most important categories of archaeological find.
  • Gaming / fantasy: dragon hoards, loot hoards, and "hoarding" items in role-playing games directly continue the epic sense.
  • Ecology / animal behaviour: "hoarding" (scatter-hoarding, larder-hoarding) describes how animals — squirrels, jays, nuthatches, hamsters — store food for later.

Historical bridge of the senses. The noun descends from Old English hord "treasure; a hidden store," itself from a Germanic root meaning "hidden treasure" built on a Proto-Indo-European root *ḱeudh-, "to hide, conceal." The core image — treasure kept hidden — has never left the word; what changed is who does the hiding and what is hidden. In Anglo-Saxon society the hoard was honourable: a king's wealth, redistributed as gifts, bound warriors to their lord. By modern times the same keep-and-don't-share action had acquired moral disapproval, and the hoard shifted from the shared gold of a ring-giver to the selfish, even pathological, accumulation of the individual. The psychology, archaeology, economics, and data uses are all that ancient "hidden store" seen through different lenses.

In the card's context. In the dwarves' song — "There many a gleaming golden hoard / They shaped and wrought" — the dwarves' craft produces gleaming stores of gold that become the moral and narrative fuel of dragon-sickness and quest. The singular in the formula still feels plural in effect ("many a ... hoard"), and the word's Germanic past of guarded treasure foreshadows the whole plot of gold under a dragon. Do not confuse with horde ("a swarming crowd"), a near-homophone from a different (ultimately Turkic) path.
