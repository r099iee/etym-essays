EXALT — single root word; the chain from PIE to the present verb.

1. PIE *h₂el- “to grow, to nourish, to raise” (reconstructed root of organic increase — the source of Latin alere, Germanic *aldaz, Old Irish alim; Pokorny ‘al-’).
2. Latin alō, alere “to nourish, feed, rear, raise” (classical Latin verb, attested from the 2nd c. BCE).
3. Latin altus “grown, raised; high (and ‘deep’)” (past participle of alere — ‘grown’, hence ‘high’, the dimension of growth; also ‘deep’, as in altum mare, ‘the deep sea’).
4. Latin exaltāre “to raise up high, to lift up” (ex- ‘out, up’ + altus; attested in classical Latin with the literal sense).
5. Christian Latin exaltāre “to glorify, to magnify, to raise in honour” (Vulgate Latin: exaltabo te, Domine, ‘I will exalt thee, O Lord’, Psalm 30:1; the devotional sense developed in the biblical Latin tradition).
6. Old French exalter “to raise up, to exalt, to glorify” (attested from the 12th century; borrowed from Latin exaltāre).
7. Middle English exalten “to exalt, raise high, glorify” (attested from the 14th century, c. 1374 in Chaucer and contemporary texts).
8. English EXALT “to raise to a higher rank, position, or degree of glory; to praise intensely; to glorify” (modern form and senses; adjective exalted ‘elevated, lofty’; noun exaltation).

Note on debated points: the exact PIE root shape is written *h₂el- (some handbooks *al-); its collateral ‘beyond, other’ and ‘to burn’ homophones (Latin alius, ‘other’, and adolēre, ‘to burn up’ — the source of altar) are distinct roots and must not be merged. The Latin altus is famously ambiguous between ‘high’ and ‘deep’ — growth measured in either direction — and the ‘deep’ sense survives in words like altitude (from altitūdō) used of depth in some contexts; this ambiguity does not affect the chain of exalt itself, where the ‘raise up’ sense is clear.
