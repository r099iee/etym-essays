girding — the present participle and gerund of the verb gird, "to encircle or bind with a belt or band, to surround, or to prepare for action". As a gerund it names the act itself: the girding of a garment, the girding of armour, and — in the immortal biblical idiom — the girding of the loins, i.e. the disciplined bracing of oneself for work or struggle. In Walter Pater's sentence the word bears its full old force: the "charm of ascesis" belongs to youth's "austere and serious girding of the loins", the self-binding, self-preparing energy of the beginning of life, which Pater likens to the earliest, freshest phase of the Renaissance.

Primary sense — the physical act of encircling. To gird is to bind something around the waist, hips, or body with a belt, sash, cord, or band, or to fasten something onto the body thereby: to gird on a sword, to gird one's dress. The -ing form names this action as it happens or as a custom, and in this concrete sense it is as old as the verb itself: clothes had to be girt up before labour in every pre-modern society. Old English gyrdan, Old Norse gyrða, Gothic gairdan all show the same belt-centred core meaning.

Second sense — surrounding and encompassing. From binding the waist, the verb broadened to "encircle, surround, enclose" anything: a town girded with walls, a lake girding an island. The participle follows this path — "girding promontories", "girding the horizon". This spatial sense is the bridge to the modern technical noun girder, a beam that "girds" or binds a structure, and to the verb's relatives girdle (a belt; also the anatomical pelvic ring) and girth (the band around a horse's belly; hence a measure of circumference).

Third sense — the idiom "gird (up) the loins". In the ancient world the long tunic or robe had to be hitched up and tucked into a belt before running, fighting, or working; the Authorized Version fixes the image in "gird up thy loins" (Job 38:3; 2 Kings 4:29; Ephesians 6:14) and "with your loins girded, your shoes on your feet" (Exodus 12:11). From that literal gesture the phrase became the supreme figure for readiness, resolution, and self-mastery; "girding of the loins" is thus the name for arduous preparation, especially at life's outset — exactly Pater's point.

Grammar and morphology. The verb has two past participles: regular girded and archaic irregular girt, which also serves as a participial adjective ("girt about with strength"), recalling the class of weak verbs with preterite in -te/-de (/t/), where the final consonant cluster hardened (gird → girt). The -ing form accordingly does double duty — participle ("girding his loins, he set out") and verbal noun ("the girding of the loins").

Register and modern use. In contemporary English girding is predominantly literary, scriptural, or mock-heroic; ordinary speech prefers "bracing", "steeling", "getting ready", "barricading". Its prestige in English letters comes almost entirely from the Bible and from epic diction. When a modern sports writer says a team is "girding itself", the reference is consciously heroic — the word imports antique seriousness.

Polysemy summary.

  • binding/encircling with a belt — the oldest, fully concrete sense;
  • surrounding, encompassing — transferred to landscape, architecture, the body;
  • preparing, steeling, bracing oneself — figurative, chiefly via the "loins" idiom;
  • investing — ceremonial "girding on the sword" in knighting or consecration;
  • constricting/encircling pressure — "girding pressure" in medicine (a girdle-feeling, sensation of constriction), a minor technical offshoot.

The semantic bridge. Every sense passes through the single image of the belt: the belt binds the garment; the bound garment frees the legs for action; therefore binding and preparing are one motion. Girding never developed a purely abstract moral sense of its own — the idiom of the loins always carries it.
