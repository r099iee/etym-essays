Primary sense. "Bellowing" is the present participle of the verb to bellow and also its gerund (verbal noun). In its root, core meaning it denotes the deep, resonant, prolonged cry of a large animal — above all a bull, ox, stag, or cow — forced up from the throat and chest in a full-bodied surge of sound. When a bull bellows, the note is low, heavy, and sustained, swelling in volume before it dies away, and it can be heard across a surprising distance; it is this specific timbre — deep, sonorous, and breath-driven — that separates a bellow from any other cry.

The verb and its forms. As a participle, "bellowing" does the work of an adjective ("a bellowing bull", "a bellowing furnace") and of the continuous verb ("the herds were bellowing all night"). As a gerund it names the action or its sound: "the bellowing of the cattle", "a dreadful bellowing rose from the depths". A plural "bellowings" is also found for repeated cries. All these uses share one unvarying core: a loud, deep, resonant sound produced at full throat.

Full range of senses (polysemy). Modern usage applies the word across four broad domains:

  • Of animals. The oldest and primary use: the roaring cry of cattle (bulls, oxen, cows), stags and other deer during the rut, and by extension the deep vocalizations of whales, elephants, lions, and other large beasts. In zoological writing it describes any low-pitched, long-drawn, resonant animal call.
  • Of human voices. Transferred to people who cry out at the same depth and force: a man "bellowing" orders, insults, or pain. The word always implies anger, urgency, drunkenness, or distress, and it flattens the human voice toward the animal — a bellowing man sounds like a beast, and the word carries that bestial edge.
  • Of inanimate sound. Extended, from the 17th century, to anything that produces a deep, swelling, resonant noise: thunder, cannon, the sea, a gale, a bell, and later railway engines, factory machinery, loudspeakers, and bass-heavy audio. Here the shared feature is the depth and the surge — the sound seems to swell like a chest taking breath.
  • Figurative and metaphorical. By extension it describes anything loud, booming, and hard to ignore — a "bellowing" crowd, "bellowing" propaganda, a "bellowing" loudspeaker van — and, in literary writing, a narrator's or character's theatrical rage. The metaphor always trades on the bull: brute force, bulk, and barely controlled power.

Register and tone. "Bellowing" is never a polite, quiet, or gentle word. Even where it describes machinery rather than cattle, it keeps a note of menace, excess, or crudeness: the bellowing noise is too loud, too deep, too animal. In the source sentence, from The Hobbit, the word is chosen precisely for this dread: the "bellowing" that echoes up the tunnel is an inhuman, monstrous cry, one that makes the ground tremble — a bellow is the only animal sound big enough to fit a dragon.

Collocations and idioms. Common partners include "bellowing with rage", "bellowing orders", "bellowing at the top of his voice", "a bellowing bull", "bellowing thunder". The bare noun "bellow" (a single utterance) is in use, and note that the identical-looking word "bellows" (the tool for pumping air, and its verb) is a separate word of different origin and must not be confused with it.

Grammar note. "Bellowing" is fully regular: verb bellow / bellows / bellowed / bellowing. Because the present participle can also serve as an adjective and a noun, the single form carries all three functions, which is why a dictionary entry for the inflected form must describe it from all three angles.
