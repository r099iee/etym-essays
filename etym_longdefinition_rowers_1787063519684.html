Rowers (plural noun; singular: rower) are people who propel a boat or other small watercraft through the water by pulling on oars — long shafts of wood, metal or carbon fibre with a flattened blade at one end. The word is an agent noun built from the English verb to row ("to move a boat by means of oars") plus the suffix -er, which marks "a person or thing that performs the action"; a rower is therefore literally "one who rows". In the passage above, taken from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the townspeople of Esgaroth (Lake-town) put boats and rowers at the service of Thorin and his company of exiled dwarves; the plural form signals that whole crews of boatmen, not a single oarsman, are being lent so that the dwarves can be ferried about on the Long Lake.

Primary meaning. In everyday modern English a rower is simply any person who rows a boat with oars, whether for transport, for work, for fishing, for sport, or for recreation. The heart of the sense is the physical technique: the rower sits facing the stern (the rear) of the vessel, grasps one or two oars, dips the blade into the water, and pulls back against it so that the reaction force drives the boat forward. Two principal styles are recognised: sweep rowing, in which each rower pulls a single oar with both hands (raced typically in pairs, fours and eights), and sculling, in which each rower works two oars, one per hand.

  • Competitive sport. In modern athletics, rowers are the athletes of the sport of rowing (called "crew" in North America), racing in slender craft known as shells or eights. Rowing has been an Olympic sport since 1900, with events such as the eight, the four, the double scull and the single scull. In this register a rower is a disciplined competitor who executes the four phases of the stroke — catch, drive, finish, recovery — in perfect synchrony with the rest of the crew.
  • Working and professional oarsmen. Historically the rower was a labourer: ferrymen, fishermen, ship's-boat crews and harbour men who moved goods and passengers. Before steam, rivers and harbours depended on rowers, which is why Tolkien's Lake-town can lend them as casually as it lends boats — rowers were ordinary, workaday providers of transport.
  • Galley and forced-labour rowers. A darker historical sense is the oarsman of a galley — the warship of antiquity and the Mediterranean Middle Ages whose benches were often manned by slaves, convicts or pressed men. This connotation of brutal, chained labour once coloured the word heavily and survives today mainly in historical fiction and naval history.
  • Recreational rowers. In the present day the majority of rowers row for pleasure and fitness on lakes, rivers and canals, in dinghies, skiffs, gigs and recreational shells; this gives the word its current mild, riverside, wholesome flavour.
  • Figurative and extended uses. Rowing is teamwork in which everyone must pull in the same direction, so the wider vocabulary feeds idioms such as "to pull one's weight", "to be in the same boat" and "to row in the same direction". The agent noun rower itself, however, stays stubbornly literal; figurative weight is carried by the verb to row, which in British English also means "to quarrel" (a row, rhyming with "cow"), yet someone who rows in an argument is never called a rower.

Grammar and usage. Rower is a regular countable noun — singular rower, plural rowers — used of both sexes. The older gender-marked terms oarsman and oarswoman are increasingly replaced by the neutral rower, and in sporting contexts rowers is the standard inclusive word for a whole crew. The related abstract noun is rowing ("the sport or act of rowing"). Stylistically, rower is the plain, everyday word; oarsman sounds slightly literary or old-fashioned.

Secondary meanings and semantic bridges. The noun has essentially one family of senses, all flowing from the verb to row. The bridge from labour to sport is historical: the same strong, skilled bodies that once drove merchant and war vessels began, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, to race one another on rivers — the Oxford–Cambridge Boat Race was first rowed in 1829 and Henley Regatta began in 1839 — turning the worker into the athlete. As a result, rowers today appears far more often in sports reporting than on the dockside. The word has no other notable homonyms and no pejorative drift; it remains transparent, stable and positive, a small monument to an activity humans have performed for at least four thousand years.

In the reading context. Tolkien's phrase "boats and rowers were put at their service" is deliberately plain and old-fashioned. It belongs to a world in which water transport is human-powered and hospitality is measured in practical gifts — men and boats to move one's guests. The word anchors the reader in the everyday realism of Lake-town, in deliberate contrast to the magic of the Misty Mountains and the Lonely Mountain.
