- Old English — “gicel” (a lump of ice) joins with “īs” (ice) as “īsġicel”: even then the compound means the hanging spike, while the cognate Old Norse “jǫkull” drifts toward “glacier, mass of ice”.
- Middle English — “isikel / ikkel” narrows to the pendent spike; winter carols, proverbs, and seasonal complaint-literature take it up as the sharp ornament of cold.
- c. 1590s — Shakespeare sets the word in the winter song that closes Love’s Labour’s Lost (“When icicles hang by the wall”); the spelling settles on “icicle”.
- 18th–19th c. — naturalists study icicle growth as part of the new science of cold, while Romantic and Victorian writers aestheticise icicles as “crystal daggers” and “diamond fringes”; children’s fiction makes them winter toys.
- 20th–21st c. — the everyday plural “icicles” names roof ice anywhere; the word stretches to any long thin pointed thing — glass ornaments, knitting patterns, pendant lights — while keeping its crisp, cold, glittering image.
