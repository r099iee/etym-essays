heretofore is a formal compound temporal adverb meaning "before this time; until now; previously." Literally it decomposes into here + to + fore, "up to here-before," that is, "up to this point in time."

Primary sense and the passage

In the passage it fixes the absolute temporal frontier of the scene: up to this instant the Martian sky has been "silent and stirless," and nothing has moved on the river-bottom, whose stones are bare even of dead lichen. The entire weight of the sentence falls on the fact that all of this is about to change. Heretofore does not merely date the stillness; it seals it, presenting the past as a finished book — a condition so complete and uniform that it can be summed up in one word and then overthrown by what follows. The word is a raised curtain: everything behind it is one tableau, and the sentence that follows opens onto the storm.

Morphology and how the meaning works

The word is built by fusing three spatial deictics — here ("this place"), to ("up to"), fore ("before") — into a single time-pointer. The past is conceived as a place lying before the speaker, and the adverb says "up to this place in time." This spatial-to-temporal mapping is one of the most ancient and universal semantic strategies in language: what lies before us in space is what lies before us in time. The word's internal architecture is itself an argument about time as a path along which we move, with the present as the point we have just reached.

Polysemy and secondary senses

Heretofore is essentially univocal — it has one meaning, "until this time," with two shades:
  • the inclusive reading — up to and including the present moment, the past regarded as closed and summed up;
  • the narrative/legal reading — up to a specified point in time under discussion, often the moment immediately before a reversal, a judgment, or an exception ("all obligations heretofore incurred").
Its most important extension is registerial rather than semantic: it is pre-eminently the word of legal and formal prose. In statutes, contracts, and judgments it seals a closed past, where precision about the boundary of time is a matter of money and rights and where a plain spoken synonym would sound out of place. In Victorian adventure prose it performs the same act of sealing for literary effect.

Differentiating terms

  • previously: plain, neutral; no legal or ceremonial flavor.
  • formerly: points to an older state of affairs that no longer holds; heretofore points up to the present moment, inclusive of it.
  • hitherto: nearest twin, identical meaning, built on hither ("to this place") rather than here; slightly more literary and self-conscious.
  • before / earlier: informal and unemphatic.
  • thus far / so far: imply a measured stretch of time with an eye toward continuation.
  • until now: conversational and spoken.
Heretofore alone carries the finality of a closed ledger: it shuts the past whole, then opens the scene to what follows.

Register and usage notes

Rare in speech; confined to legal, statutory, and elevated literary registers. The spelling is closed (heretofore), with here-to-fore and here to fore as historical variants. It has no plural, no comparison, and no living derivatives. It is a fossilized three-part pointing gesture that still works with complete precision, and its meaning is unchanged since the fourteenth century.
