- Early 17th Century (1610s) - First attested as an adjective, 'gnarled' primarily described wood or trees full of 'gnarls' (knots or protuberances). It conveyed a sense of natural ruggedness and irregularity.
- 18th-19th Century - The usage expanded to describe human body parts, particularly hands and fingers, that had become twisted or stiff due to age, hard labor, or disease. This usage began to imbue the word with connotations of resilience, experience, and the marks of a life lived.
- Late 19th-20th Century - 'Gnarled' gained a more metaphorical and evocative power, extending to describe abstract concepts like problems, personalities, or narratives that were complex, difficult, or intricately twisted. It often carried a sense of deep-seated character, strength, and a history of endurance, sometimes with a hint of forbidding or unrefined beauty.
- Contemporary Usage - Today, 'gnarled' retains all these senses, being widely used in descriptive writing for nature, human features, and complex situations. It consistently evokes imagery of age, resilience, and a distinctive, rugged character, often admired for its authenticity and the stories it implies.
