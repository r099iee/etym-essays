THE EVOLUTION OF PROCOPIUS 1. A name, not a coinage: what "Procopius" is. Procopius is a proper noun, and its evolution is that of a Greek personal name — a transparently meaningful compound that gradually hardened into the label of one famous man. The name Greek Prokópios (Προκόπιος) is built from two elements: the prefix pro- "before, forward" and the noun kopē "a cutting, a blow," hence literally "a cutting forward," i.e., "making one's way ahead, progressing, thriving." Such auspicious names were typical of late-antique Greek Christianity, which favoured names expressing progress, victory, and divine favour. The man who made the name immortal, Procopius of Caesarea (c. 490–507 – c. 565), was a lawyer and secretary to the great general Belisarius, and his three works turned a common "progress" name into the archetype of the double-faced court historian. 2. The element pro-: the PIE notion of "forward." The prefix pro- is the Greek form of the Proto-Indo-European particle *pro "before, in front, forward," which appears across the family: Sanskrit prá "forward," Latin prō "before, on behalf of," and Greek pró. In composition, pro- regularly means "forward, onward, in advance," and it is the base of a large Greek vocabulary of progress and precedence. The notion carried by the prefix is direction and priority: moving ahead, standing before. In prokopē the prefix fuses with the "cutting" image to produce "cutting ahead" — the idea of advancing by forcing a way forward, exactly the semantic of "progress" (a Latin word with the same pro- prefix, though with a different second element, gradī "to step"). 3. The element kopē: a verb without a sure past. The second element, Greek kopē "a cutting, a blow," derives from the verb kóptō "to cut, strike, beat, knock," attested from Homer onward. The deep etymology of kóptō is a genuine problem: modern comparative linguists (notably Beekes) find no secure Indo-European etymon for it and leave open the possibility of a Pre-Greek (substrate) origin. The chain therefore runs from attested Greek back no further with certainty — an honest stopping point. What is certain is the internal derivation: kóptō → kopē, a standard Greek formation of action nouns in -ē from verbal roots, and then pro + kopē → prokopē "a cutting forward, progress," a word well attested in Classical prose (Xenophon uses prokopē for the progress of learning and improvement). 4. The compound: prokopē "progress" and the personal name. The compound prokopē (προκοπή) "progress, advancement, success" is first attested in the classical period and flourished in later Greek as the ordinary word for progress — it is, in fact, the etymon of the English theological and philosophical term prokope, "progress in virtue or knowledge," a borrowing via Latin from the patristic tradition. From prokopē the personal name Prokópios was formed in the standard Greek way (the -ios ending marking an adjective used as a masculine name), meaning "he who progresses, the thriving one." The name was worn by several historically attested men: the Roman usurper Procopius (d. 366 CE), the rhetorician Procopius of Gaza (c. 465–528), the 3rd-century martyr Procopius of Scythopolis, and — most famously — the historian of Justinian's wars. 5. Latinization and the route into English. As the Eastern Roman Empire communicated with the Latin West, Greek Prokópios was transliterated as Latin Procopius (Greek omicron-o retained, Greek kappa becoming Latin c, the -ios ending latinized as -ius). In this form the name entered medieval Latin historiography and church tradition, and from the sixteenth century onward it reached English in the shape Procopius — indeclinable, learned, and always capitalized. English histories of Byzantium (Gibbon above all, whose Decline and Fall is the immediate source of the sentence on this card) made Procopius the standard English form, and it remains so in Byzantine studies today. 6. The semantic turn: from "thriving" to "double-faced." The drift of the name's connotation is the most interesting part of its evolution:
  • Originally: an auspicious name meaning "progress, thriving" — a man destined to advance.
  • In the sixth century: the historian's public career as Belisarius's secretary and the author of the Wars, a sober, classicizing narrative of Justinian's reconquests, and of the Buildings, a court panegyric.
  • After 1623: the printing of the Secret History (Anecdota), composed about 550 but surviving in a single 14th-century manuscript, revealed a Procopius who savagely attacked Justinian, Theodora, and Belisarius, presenting the emperor as a demon and the empress as a whore. The name's meaning flipped: the "thriving" historian became the byword for the double chronicler, the man who wrote one history for the public and another for posterity.
  • Modern sense: "Procopius" and the adjective "Procopian" now denote the dual historical record — official panegyric versus covert invective — and serve as a standing warning about the reliability of court history.
7. Socio-cultural factors and literary attestations. The name's evolution tracks the survival of classical historiography into a Christian empire. Procopius wrote in an archaizing, Atticizing Greek that self-consciously imitated Thucydides, even while describing a Christian state; the Secret History, with its scandalous portraits, reads like the gossip of a disenchanted insider. Gibbon, in the eighteenth century, found in Procopius both a reliable witness to wonders ("as Procopius had been told") and, elsewhere in the Decline and Fall, material for the most sardonic passages on Byzantine decadence. In modern scholarship the "Procopian question" — how to reconcile the Wars, the Buildings, and the Anecdota — remains one of the liveliest debates in Byzantine studies. 8. Summary.
  • Origin: Greek pro- "forward" + kopē "a cutting" → prokopē "progress" → Prokópios "the thriving one."
  • Medium: Greek personal name → Latin Procopius → English Procopius, a straight line of transliteration.
  • Sense: from a name of good omen ("progress") to the emblem of the double-faced historian — the connotation reversed by the posthumous publication of the Secret History.
  • Linguistic honesty: the second element kóptō/kopē has no secure PIE etymology; the chain is fixed at the attested Greek verb.
The name Procopius therefore illustrates how a personal name can be simultaneously transparent in its etymology and opaque in its fame: built from two plain words for "cutting forward," it now stands for the most double-edged historian of the ancient world.
