- c. 1150–1350: Formed in early Middle English by attaching the diminutive suffix *-ling* to the Germanic verb *souken* (*suck*), denoting an unweaned infant human or young animal.
- c. 1380–1500: Widely disseminated in religious texts and early English Bible translations (such as Wycliffe's Bible), often paired poetically with *babes* ("babes and sucklings") to represent absolute innocence, purity, and radical dependency.
- c. 1500–1700: Frequently applied in agricultural and culinary treatises to young livestock destined for meat or specialized rearing (e.g., *suckling pig*, *suckling calf*).
- c. 1700–1900: Maintained steady use in zoological, natural history, and romantic poetry, evoking themes of maternal tenderness, mammalian biology, and vulnerable youth.
- c. 1900–Present: Retained in standard modern English both as a precise biological adjective/noun for unweaned mammalian offspring and in culinary terminology (*suckling pig*), as well as an occasional literary metaphor for nascent, developing ideas or organizations.
