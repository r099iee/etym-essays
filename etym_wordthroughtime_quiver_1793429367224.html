- 4th–6th c. — Eurasian steppe warfare: Proto-Germanic *kukur borrows an arrow-container term from Hunnic or Sarmatian equestrian archers, spreading across Germanic tribes as an archer's vessel.
- 12th–13th c. — Chivalric romance & Anglo-Norman arrival: imported into Middle English via Anglo-Norman quiveir and Old French quivre, replacing native Old English cocur in descriptions of archery.
- 14th c. — Yeoman longbowmen & Chaucerian verse: Geoffrey Chaucer immortalizes the yeoman archer bearing peacock arrows in a quiver, while English longbow victories at Crécy and Agincourt cement the quiver in folklore.
- Late 15th–16th c. — Homophonic union: Old English cwifer (nimble, lively) evolves into the verb quiver (to tremble), creating a permanent homophonic resonance between the arrow-case and the vibrating arrow.
- 17th–18th c. — Biblical metaphor & Swiftian satire: the King James Bible popularizes the "full quiver" of children (Psalm 127), while Swift parodies military majesty with the Lilliputian's miniature quiver.
- 20th–21st c. — Surfing culture & higher mathematics: adopted by 1960s surfers for surfboard collections, and coined in 1972 by Peter Gabriel for directed multigraphs in abstract algebra.
