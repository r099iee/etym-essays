I. The Latin base

FEVERED is the participial adjective formed in English from the noun fever, which in turn is a very early Latin loan. Old English had fēfor / fēfer, borrowed directly from Latin febris ("fever") in the Anglo-Saxon period (recorded in the Leechbook and other Old English medical texts). The Latin word febris itself is of debated etymology. The most widely accepted view connects it with the Latin verb ferveō ("to be hot, to boil, to glow") and thus with a PIE root meaning "to boil, to seethe" - the fever being understood as the body boiling. Some scholars, however (notably the etymologist de Vaan), prefer a derivation from PIE *dʰegʷh- ("to burn"), which would make febris literally "the burning", a distant relative of Sanskrit dahati ("it burns") and of English "day" (through the idea of heat). The two theories cannot both be right, and the debate should be noted rather than resolved; both agree that the ancients conceived of fever as an inner burning or boiling heat.

II. The Old English and Middle English noun

Old English fēfor appears in the medical tradition of the Anglo-Saxons, where Latin learning and native practice mix (for instance in the remedies against fevers in the Leechbook). In Middle English the word settles as fever (also fevour, feover), and by the later Middle Ages it is a fixed part of the medical vocabulary, used for the disease itself and for the hot, shivering state of the sick person. The noun's central image - heat - never wavers through this period.

III. The verb and the participle

English verbs are made from nouns with great freedom, and "to fever" ("to affect with fever, to heat to excess") is attested from the 16th century. From this verb English formed the participle fevered, attested from the mid-17th century with the literal meaning "affected with fever, having a fever". The earliest uses are medical and homely. In the same period the participial adjective begins to migrate: a "fevered brain", a "fevered imagination" - the mind heated beyond control, usually in devotional and later in Gothic contexts. Milton uses the noun's heat imagery; the novels and periodicals of the 18th century push "fevered" toward the emotional and the mental.

IV. The figurative flowering in the 19th century

The 19th century makes "fevered" a signature adjective of Romantic and Victorian prose. The feverish imagination is a Romantic motif (the visionary overheated by inspiration); the Gothic novel thrives on "fevered dreams" and "fevered fancies"; the new popular press speaks of "fevered excitement", "fevered speculation" in the stock market, and "fevered haste" in the new industrial cities. The OED's citations show "fevered" attaching to ambition, greed, haste, joy, crowds, and markets - always with the sense of heat raised beyond a healthy pitch. In the 20th and 21st centuries this figurative branch dominates: "fevered pitch" (the point of maximum intensity), "fevered anticipation", "fevered bidding" at auctions, "fevered speculation" in journalism. The literal medical sense recedes to literary use, since everyday medicine prefers "feverish" and "pyrexial".

V. "Fevered" versus "feverish"

The pair repays comparison. "Feverish" (from "fever" + "-ish", attested 1660s) describes a person or state as being in a fever-like condition - it is the standard adjective, neutral and everyday. "Fevered" (the participle) has two extra notes: (a) it implies cause and result - something has actively been heated, stirred, or excited to this state; and (b) it is markedly more literary and emphatic. A "feverish haste" is haste in the manner of a feverish person; a "fevered haste" suggests haste that has been worked up into a burning, uncontrollable pitch. Tolkien's "a more fevered shaking" chooses the emphatic form: the shaking of Bilbo's legs is not merely fever-like but has been raised to a fever's pitch by terror and effort - the participle reaches back to the root image of the body boiling.
