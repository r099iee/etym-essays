Repented is the past tense and past participle of the verb repent: to feel and show sorrow or regret for something one has done — above all for a sin or moral wrong — and, in the strongest religious sense, to turn away from it. The word entered English from Old French with the full theological weight of Christian penance, but it has long served ordinary regret as well.

1. Primary sense — remorse and amendment

  • Religious sense. To repent of sin is to feel genuine sorrow for it, to intend amendment of life, and (in Catholic tradition) to submit to confession and penance. Greek metanoia — literally “a change of mind” — is the New Testament concept that repent translates; repentance is a turning, a conversion of the will, not a mere pang of conscience.
  • Moral and personal sense. Plainly: “he repented of his decision” = he regretted it, wished it undone, and felt the sting of self-blame. The object can be a deed, a word, a choice, a purchase — anything one wishes to retract. It is stronger and more inward than mere “regret”: it implies acknowledgment of fault, not just disappointment with outcomes.

2. Secondary and older senses

  • The impersonal construction. Older English, mirroring Latin’s impersonal paenitet mē “it grieves me”, said “it repented me of my deed” or “it repenteth him of his words”. The King James Bible keeps this: “it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth” (Genesis 6:6); “the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people” (Exodus 32:14). Modern English has wholly replaced this with the personal construction “he repented”.
  • Change of mind (the etymological sense). The oldest French meaning of repentir was simply “to reconsider, to change one’s mind” — sorrow and second thoughts together. This survives in the phrase repent at leisure (as in the proverb “marry in haste, repent at leisure”, proverbial from the sixteenth century) and in the idea that repentance always includes something like a revised opinion of one’s own past conduct.
  • Obsolete nautical idiom. Older dictionaries record sailors’ English in which the sea or wind was said to “repent” — to relent, to fall calm after a storm. The usage is obsolete but shows how far the verb could wander from its religious core.

3. Register and modern use

  • Formal, literary, moral. In ordinary speech “regretted” has almost entirely displaced “repented”; repented now signals a moral dimension, an old-fashioned solemnity, or a consciously literary style. It is the favourite word of sermons, memoirs, period drama and fantasy prose — the review line from J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit has Dáin speak of “the words of Thorin, of which he repented”, the dying dwarf-king’s retracted harshness toward Bilbo.
  • Grammar. Regular weak verb: repent — repented — repented; the object is often introduced by “of”: “he repented of his bargain”.

4. The semantic bridge

Latin paenitēre meant “to cause regret”; the impersonal paenitet put the sorrow outside the self — “it sorrows me”. Old French repentir (re- “again, back” + pentir “to feel regret”) added the idea of turning one’s feeling back upon oneself, and Christian doctrine fused that emotion with the duty to change. The word thus bridges public theology and private self-reproach: today’s “repented” can mean anything from the sinner’s conversion to a shopkeeper’s rueful second thoughts about a bad deal.
