Moats, the plural of moat, are in their primary and oldest sense deep, broad ditches dug around a castle, fortress, or walled town as a line of defence — very often filled with water, so that any attacker must struggle across both the ditch and the water while exposed to missiles from the walls above.

In the source sentence the word appears as a landscape simile rather than a piece of fortification: unexpected deep folds in the ground lie before the travellers like the ruts of great giant-wheels or wide moats, with sunken roads long disused and choked with brambles alongside them. Here the comparison borrows the moat's great scale, its steep-sided hollowness, and its air of abandonment — these are moats with no castle, ditches whose garrisons marched away centuries ago, now filled with weeds instead of water.

Core meaning in detail: A classic wet moat is an engineered water barrier, typically several metres deep and ten or more metres wide, created by damming or diverting a stream so that it rings the outer wall. Crossing it demanded boats, rafts, or a single guarded crossing — the drawbridge — which could be raised or burned in moments of danger. Dry moats work on the same principle without water: their steep scarped sides trap attackers in a killing zone swept by archers and later by gunfire, as at the great fortresses of the crusader east and at the later star forts of early modern Europe. Famous survivals include the broad still waters around Bodiam Castle in Sussex, the great ditch of the Tower of London — its water drained in 1843 and now a grassy hollow — and the vast water defences of Dutch and French fortress towns, where whole landscapes of inundation could be summoned by opening sluices.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Defensive ditch around a fortified place (primary sense, from c. 1300): the water-filled or dry trench ringing castle, town, or fortress; the sense every dictionary lists first, and the one the source sentence borrows for its simile of folds in the ground.
  • Any broad encircling barrier or gap (figurative extension): as in a moat of lawn around a country house, or a great moat of parkland ringing a city. The bridge is visual and functional: whatever lies between the valuable centre and the outside world, keeping it apart, earns the name.
  • Economic moat (business jargon, popularised in the 1990s): a company's durable competitive advantage — brand, patents, network effects, low costs — that protects its profits from rivals, in Warren Buffett's famous image of a wonderful castle surrounded by a deep moat. The bridge is the military function carried into markets: just as water stops siege towers, so the moat stops competitors, and investors constantly ask whether a firm's moat is widening or being filled in.
  • The verb to moat (from the noun): to surround with a moat, literally or figuratively — a moated grange, a moated management team. Tennyson's moated grange fixed the adjective moated in the literary imagination as an emblem of lonely seclusion.
  • A natural trench likened to a moat (descriptive, as in the source): deep natural folds, ravines, or sunken lanes seen as moats without castles. The bridge runs from shape alone: the land itself digs the ditch, and the eye supplies the fortress.

Register and connotation: moats carries romance and menace together — childhood castles with drawbridges on one side, grim siege warfare on the other. In history and travel writing it is concrete and picturesque; in business writing it is the most successful military metaphor of modern finance, so familiar that analysts speak of moat erosion and moat expansion without a second thought. The source sentence reaches for exactly the word's lonely grandeur: wide moats with no castle left to defend, earthworks swallowing the empty land.
