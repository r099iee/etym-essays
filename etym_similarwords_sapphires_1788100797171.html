• Rubies: Similar because ruby is the other precious corundum gem — identical chemistry and hardness, traded in the same gemmological networks; differs because rubies are red (coloured by chromium) while sapphires are normally blue: colour twins of one mineral.
  • Emeralds: Similar because emeralds are a classic precious stone prized for a single colour (green beryl) and set in the same royal and ecclesiastical settings; differs because emerald is a beryllium mineral with hardness 7.5–8, noticeably softer and chemically unrelated to corundum.
  • Diamonds: Similar because diamonds are the premier precious gem, graded by cut, colour, clarity and carat, and mounted in the same fine jewellery; differs because diamond is pure carbon at hardness 10 — the only stone harder than sapphire — and is prized for fire and brilliance rather than for colour.
  • Lapis lazuli: Similar because in antiquity the name 'sapphire' almost certainly referred to this deep-blue stone from Afghanistan, so the two words share a piece of semantic history; differs because lapis lazuli is a soft, opaque rock (hardness 5–5.5) of lazurite, while modern sapphire is a hard transparent crystal.
  • Blue topaz: Similar because blue topaz is a blue precious stone marketed as a more affordable sapphire look-alike; differs because topaz is a silicate (hardness 8) and most blue topaz is colour-enhanced by irradiation, whereas sapphire's blue is natural and its hardness is 9.
  • Aquamarine: Similar because aquamarine offers a pale blue-green gemstone in the same 'cold colour' family, cut into the same jewellery shapes; differs because aquamarine is beryl (a cousin of emerald), lighter and cooler in tone, far more abundant, and hence far less costly.
  • Tanzanite: Similar because tanzanite is a blue-violet precious stone discovered in the 20th century and marketed against sapphire; differs because tanzanite is a calcium aluminium silicate found in one location (Tanzania), is softer (6.5–7), and has no corundum hardness.
  • Amethyst: Similar because amethyst is a purple gemstone historically ranked among the 'cardinal gems' and worn in bishops' rings like sapphire; differs because amethyst is quartz (hardness 7), famous for abundance, and coloured by iron — not by the corundum lattice.
  • Corundum: Similar because corundum is the mineral name of the whole family that includes sapphire and ruby; differs because 'corundum' is a mineralogical term covering opaque industrial abrasive grades too, while 'sapphire' names only the transparent (usually blue) gem variety.
  • Gems / jewels: Similar because sapphires are a subset of gems and jewels — hard, rare, cut stones used in ornament; differs because 'gem' and 'jewel' are generic terms for any precious stone or ornament, carrying no specific chemistry, colour or hardness.
