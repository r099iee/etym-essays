- [Late 15th c. Middle French] — Coined as rempart from the verb remparer ("to fortify in advance against assault"), designating the new thick earthen banks built to absorb artillery balls — [perception: innovative military engineering against gunpowder bombardment]
- [Late 16th c. Elizabethan English, c. 1580s] — Borrowed into English during the European Wars of Religion and the fortification of Tudor coastal forts against the Spanish Armada — [perception: modern, state-of-the-art bastion defense]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Age of Sieges] — Standardized in classical military architecture by Vauban; elevated in literature into a synonym for impregnable civic bastions and mountain heights — [perception: grand monument of sovereign security and architectural majesty]
- [1814 Early 19th c. War of 1812] — Immortalized in Francis Scott Key's lyrics for "The Star-Spangled Banner" ("o'er the ramparts we watch'd"), embedding the word forever in the American national mythos — [perception: sacred symbol of patriotic resilience and heroic national defense]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Widely deployed across political discourse, judicial constitutionalism (the "ramparts of democracy"), geomorphology, and epic literature — [perception: timeless metaphor for invincible physical or moral protection]
