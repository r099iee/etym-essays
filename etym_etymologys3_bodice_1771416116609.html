Bodice is a grammatical fossil: a plural that was reborn as a singular, and behind that, a garment named for the body itself. Its story begins not in tailoring but in being — in the Proto-Indo-European root *bhew-, "to be, exist, grow, become," one of the most fundamental roots in the entire family. From it descend Sanskrit bhū- "to be" (whence Hindi honā, "to be," and the noun bhava, "existence"), Greek phýō "to grow, become" (whence physis, "nature," and English physics, physical, physique), Latin fuī and fierī, the "was" and "became" of the Romance perfect tenses, and — most remarkably — the very b- forms of the English verb to be (I be, been, being), which continue a Proto-Germanic *beuną through Old English bēon. Somewhere in the Germanic west, this root generated a noun for the living trunk. Proto-Germanic *budagaz, "body, trunk," appears to mean "that which is, that which has grown," though the word is attested only in West Germanic and its full prehistory is debated. Old English bodig carried it down, attested by around 1000 in the sense "body, trunk, torso," and Middle English bodi (13th century) gave modern body — at first the entire physical frame, and later also the torso, the trunk of the body. It is from this second, narrower sense of body as "the torso" that the garment name was eventually cut. The garment enters in the sixteenth century. In Tudor England the close-fitting, often stiffened upper garment worn by women was constructed and laced as two halves, joined across the front, and tailors therefore called it a pair of bodies — the "bodies," meaning the torso-coverings, a phrase parallel to "a pair of bodices," "a pair of stays," or "a pair of sleeves," all used of garments made in matched halves. By the 1560s and 1570s the plural phrase began to contract: bodies was increasingly felt as a single word for a single garment, and its spelling drifted to bodice, the final -ice (fixed by the eighteenth century) arresting a pronunciation, /ˈbɒdɪs/, that no longer said "bodies" aloud. The semantic shift is subtle but complete: from "two laced halves covering the body" the word came to mean simply "the upper part of a dress that covers the body," and the ancient root — "to be, to exist" — was quietly preserved in the idea of a garment that defines a woman's bodily being. Across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the bodice was the structural core of women's dress. Frequently stiffened with buckram, stiffened linen, or whalebone, laced at the back or front, it was the fixed centre around which sleeves, skirt, and stomacher — the decorative triangular panel worn over its front — were built. The vocabulary of the period shows how central the garment was: "a well-bodied gown" meant one with a firmly structured bodice, and bodies and stays blurred into the ancestor of the corset. By the nineteenth century, as the separate stiffened bodice merged into the dress's own fitted waist and the fashion for corsets moved under the gown, bodice settled into its modern sense: the fitted upper part of a woman's dress, whatever its stiffening. It is this sense that a modern translator naturally reaches for when describing Xi-feng's fitted bodice of dark red silk damask, a garment whose raised gold-thread flowers and butterflies would have been entirely at home in the tailoring vocabulary of both European and Chinese luxury dress. In the twentieth century the word took on a new cultural life. The "bodice-ripper" novels of the 1970s and 1980s — historical romances set in the Regency or Victorian eras — made the garment a metonym for an entire genre, and the phrase is now as well known as the word itself. The pronunciation remained stable through all this (the odd spelling reflects the frozen plural, not the sound), and the word has never lost its sartorial, historical register: no one wears a "bodice" casually, and no modern fast-fashion label would use the word without intending the weight of the past. From a Proto-Indo-European verb for being itself, through a West Germanic noun for the living trunk, to a Tudor tailor's phrase for a laced torso covering, and so to the fitted heart of a gown — the history of bodice is the history of a word that refused to stop being a body.
