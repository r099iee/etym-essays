Sprouted is the past tense and past participle of the verb sprout: put forth shoots; began to grow rapidly; sprang up as new growth. Literally it belongs to plants — buds and shoots breaking from seed, root, or stem — but English freely extends it to anything that appears suddenly in abundance, from rumors and buildings to stories. In the prologue of The Hobbit, the narrator summarizes Gandalf's effect on the world: "Tales and adventures sprouted up all over the place wherever he went, in the most extraordinary fashion." The verb is metaphorical gardening: narrative and exploit grow like weeds or spring barley in the wizard's wake. The tone is playful, lightly magical-realist, botanical humor applied to legend-making. The particle up intensifies emergence from ground level into visibility, and "all over the place" multiplies the germination. Preserve the past form: it records the completed pattern of the past age of Gandalf's wanderings, not a present habit alone. The word's deep structure is that of emergence from a hidden or small beginning. A sprout is new growth pushing out of seed, root, or stem — the very act of a plant beginning to grow — and everything the word names, literal or figurative, shares that image of small vigorous beginnings multiplying at once. Sprout is both verb and noun: the verb for the growing, the noun for the shoot itself, and in the modern kitchen the noun specialized to the edible young shoot (bean sprouts, Brussels sprouts, alfalfa sprouts).
  • Primary sense (botany): to put forth shoots or buds; to begin to grow, as a seed germinates ("the seeds sprouted," "the potatoes have sprouted").
  • Sudden-appearance sense: to spring up rapidly and in abundance, as if by germination ("shops sprouted up all over the city," "tales sprouted wherever he went").
  • Noun sense: a young shoot or bud ("bean sprouts," "Brussels sprout," "a sprout of new growth").
  • Morphological uses: sprouted is the regular weak past and past participle of the verb (sprout / sprouted / sprouted), used in both the simple past ("stories sprouted") and the perfect ("have sprouted").
  • Jargon / technical senses: in agriculture and horticulture, sprouting denotes germination and the forcing of seed growth (sprouting seeds, sprouted grain); in nutrition, "sprouted" describes grains and legumes germinated for food (sprouted bread, sprouted flour).
  • Figurative register: the word is lively and positive, carrying growth, vitality, and multiplication; it is warmer than "erupted," less abstract than "proliferated."
Polysemy and the historical bridge: the botanical sense is the source of everything else. To sprout is literally to push new growth from seed, root, or stem, and the moment of sprouting is sudden, small, and multiple — a seed does not sprout one shoot and stop; it sends up growth "all over." English transfers that whole bundle of properties — suddenness, smallness, multiplicity — to anything that appears as if the ground of circumstance were fertile. The urban sense ("buildings sprouted") and the narrative sense ("tales sprouted") are both the same metaphor: rapid, unplanned, abundant emergence. The noun's specialization to edible shoots (bean sprouts, Brussels sprouts) is a twentieth-century kitchen fact that has made the word familiar in a wholly new context, but it is a transparent extension of the shoot-meaning. The morphological history is simple: sprout is a native Germanic verb, its weak past sprouted is a regular modern formation, and the figurative extension is recorded from the early modern period onward. Register and boundaries: sprouted is distinct from grew, which is general and slower-feeling; from sprang up, which is a close synonym of sudden appearance but lacks the germination image; from blossomed, which stresses flowering and success; from erupted, which is more violent and involuntary; and from proliferated, which is scientific and cold. Sprouted keeps the shoot-from-below image — small vigorous beginnings multiplying at once — and in the text it is exactly that image that makes Gandalf's passing like rain on dry ground: where he walks, the world grows stories.
