pecus is a Classical Latin noun meaning cattle, livestock, or a herd or flock of domestic animals held as property, and by extension the earliest Roman conception of wealth itself.

In its core Latin sense, pecus (neuter, genitive pecoris) denotes grazing livestock collectively — above all cattle, but also sheep, goats, pigs and other herd animals kept for food, milk, wool, traction and sacrifice. It is distinct from the feminine pecus, pecudis which more often means a single head of cattle or a beast, though Roman authors use the two interchangeably. The word never means wild animals; it always implies domesticated, owned, countable animals under human control.

Because cattle were money before coined money existed, pecus rapidly developed a transferred economic sense: livestock as capital, substance, fortune. This is the bridge to pecunia (money), peculium (private property, savings), peculiaris (belonging to private property, hence English peculiar), and peculari (to embezzle public cattle-money, hence English peculate). To a Roman, counting your pecus was counting your bank account.

  • Core collective sense — cattle/livestock as wealth: Pecus properly means the herd as a unit of value. Livy, Varro and Columella use it for droves driven to market, pastured on common land, or taken as booty. Example: pecus pascere — to graze cattle; pecori signum imprimere — to brand the herd. The semantic logic is pastoral economics: animals reproduce themselves, give milk and offspring, and can be exchanged, so they are self-increasing capital.
  • Extended pastoral sense — flock, herd of small stock: By analogy pecus covers sheep and goats (pecus lanigerum — wool-bearing flock), and even bees or poultry in agricultural writers. The bridge is management: any group of useful animals herded, folded and counted together.
  • Single-beast and collective-individual oscillation: In poetry (Virgil, Ovid) pecus can denote one animal, especially a sheep or sacrificial victim (mactare pecus), or a dumb beast generally. Hence Late Latin pecus = head of cattle, brute, and in Christian writers a contemptuous term for an unreasoning person or a docile congregation — the flock.
  • Figurative and contemptuous sense — crowd, mob, herd of people: Just as English says herd for people, Latin uses pecus for a mindless multitude easily led. Cicero calls venal jurors pecus. This metaphor survives in English derivatives only indirectly, but explains the pejorative colour of treating humans as livestock.
  • Juridical and religious senses: In early Roman law pecus is a formal category of res mancipi — property transferable only by solemn mancipation. Fines were assessed in oxen and sheep (multa pecore). In religion, pecus is the sacrificial animal par excellence: pecudes offered to purify fields, armies and cities. The animal is simultaneously economic unit, legal person-property, and sacred offering.
  • Modern learned survival: Pecus does not survive as an everyday English word; it lives inside pecuniary, peculate, peculation, peculiar, peculium and zoological Latin. When English writers use pecus untranslated, they deliberately invoke Roman cattle-money and pastoral origins of capital, as in the source sentence pairing pecus with pecunia.

The historical bridge for every secondary sense is the same: Rome began as a pastoral society where the visible herd on the hillside was the visible bank balance. From that identity — cattle = goods = money = property-law = sacrifice — all extensions radiate. To understand pecus is to understand why English still says capital, cattle, chattel and pecuniary from two different roots for the same barnyard reality.
