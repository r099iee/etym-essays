- [Old French & bodily origin (12th–14th c.)] - Talon originally meant the heel — from Latin talus, the ankle-bone — an everyday word for a part of the foot with no predatory force at all.
- [Falconry transfer (14th–16th c.)] - In the language of hawking, talon shifted from heel to the grasping claws of a bird of prey — the hooked, seizing weapon of the raptor, its massive hind claw (hallux) delivering the killing grip.
- [Mythic & metaphoric extension (17th–19th c.)] - Talon spread to dragons and mythic beasts, then to cruel human hands — a tyrant's talons, to fall into someone's talons — always implying grasping and seizing rather than mere scratching.
- [Modern register] - Today talon is almost exclusively raptorial and literary; it keeps its ominous predatory menace even in rescue, so that being caught in talons stays terrifying even when it saves your life.
