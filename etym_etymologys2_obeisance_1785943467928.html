LINEAR CHAIN (single root word OBEISANCE — a loanword through Old French from Latin oboedire, "to obey", literally "to give ear toward") Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *au- (also reconstructed *h₂ew-) "to perceive, notice, hear, give ear to" (c. 4000 BCE); a root of sensory attention, attested across the family in Greek *auō "to perceive", Latin audiō "I hear", and the Germanic and Slavic words for "ear". Stage 2 — Latin audīre "to hear, listen, pay attention to" (attested from the 3rd century BCE in Plautus); the standard Latin verb of hearing and, by extension, of attending to speech. Its noun audītus "hearing" and the derivative audīre family gave English audience, audible, auditorium, and audio. Stage 3 — Latin oboedīre "to give ear to, listen to, obey" (attested from the 2nd century BCE; the earlier form obaudīre is recorded by grammarians); formed from the prefix ob- "toward, facing, before" (from PIE *h₁epi/*opi "at, upon, toward") + audīre "to hear". The Latin image is physical: the obedient person turns the ear toward the speaker and attends to the command — obedience is, etymologically, attentive listening. Stage 4 — Late Latin and Vulgar Latin obēdīre → Old French obeir "to obey" (attested from the 9th century CE in the Strasbourg Oaths era); the Latin compound was simplified in Romance phonology, and Old French obeir became the ordinary verb for obedience to a lord, a law, a parent, or God. Stage 5 — Old French obeissance "obedience, submission, service" (attested from the 12th century CE); formed from obeissant, the present participle of obeir, with the abstract suffix -ance (Latin -antia). In the feudal world the word named the obedience a vassal owed a lord and the homage that enacted it. Stage 6 — Middle English obeisaunce/obeissaunce "obedience, homage; act of bowing or reverence" (first recorded in English c. 1374 in the poetry of Geoffrey Chaucer); borrowed from Old French through the Anglo-Norman channel (Anglo-Norman obeisaunce). In Middle English the word was already moving from the abstract "obedience" toward the physical "gesture of homage". Stage 7 — Early Modern English obeisance "a bow, curtsy, or other gesture of deference; reverent submission" (spelling stabilised as obeisance by the 16th century); the word was specialised in English to the performed gesture while French kept obeissance for abstract obedience. Final node — Modern English OBEISANCE "a gesture of respect or deferential respect itself"; OBEISANCE. NOTE ON DEBATED ETYMOLOGY: The root *au- for "to hear, perceive" is the standard reconstruction for Latin audire (cf. Greek aïein "to perceive") and is not seriously disputed. The interesting nuance is semantic rather than formal: the shift from "obedience" (the French abstract sense) to "bowing" (the modern English concrete sense) happened largely within English during the 15th–16th centuries, and the modern French descendant obéissance still means "obedience", not "bow" — the two senses have diverged in the two languages. Also note the common confusion with obedience in English spelling; the -ei- form of obeisance reflects a 16th-century re-spelling influenced by French, and the pronunciation /əʊˈbeɪ.səns/ preserves the old trisyllabic count.
