- 13th to 14th Century - Entered Middle English from Old French *chant*, designating primarily ecclesiastical plainsong, monastic choral singing, and Latin liturgical recitation in medieval Catholic worship.
- 15th to 16th Century - Expanded into secular music and formal poetics under French and Italian influence, acquiring the specialized literary sense of a canto or major division of an epic poem reflecting oral performance traditions.
- 17th to 18th Century - Deepened connections with magical incantations and poetic sorcery (closely linked to *enchantment*), while continuing to serve as the standard English term for church liturgical intonation (Anglican chant).
- 19th Century - Began to be applied to repetitive secular slogans, crowd outcries, and monotonous political speech; in literary criticism, used metaphorically to describe the undulating, hypnotic musicality of an author's sentence structure.
- 20th Century to Present - Universally recognized across stadium supporter culture, mass political demonstrations, and spiritual mindfulness practices (mantra chanting), while retaining its high-register ecclesiastical and literary connotations.
