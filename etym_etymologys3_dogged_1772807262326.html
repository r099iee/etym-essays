The adjective dogged is transparently built on the English noun dog, but it sits at the edge of two very different etymological stories: one of them is clear and English-internal, the other is one of the celebrated unsolved problems of the field. The opaque noun. Old English docga is attested only rarely and late, and it seems to have designated a powerful or mastiff-like animal. The ordinary Old English word for the common dog was hund "hound", a direct descendant of the ancient Indo-European word for "dog" — the PIE root *ḱwṓn-, cognate with Latin canis, Greek kyōn, Sanskrit śvan-, and German Hund. In Middle English, dogge emerges and gradually becomes the ordinary word for the animal, displacing hund / hound in general use (hound survives in the specialised sense of a hunting dog). Where docga / dogge came from is unknown: the proposals range over a Germanic diminutive or nursery formation, an obscure native coinage, and an unidentified loan, but no proposal is generally accepted and none can be traced to a PIE root. Scholars therefore treat dog as a native English word of unknown origin. All senses of dogged consequently hang on this opaque noun rather than on the old *ḱwṓn- family — which is why dogged has no PIE derivation chain of its own, and why the derivation shown for it runs English-internal only. The English-internal derivation. From the noun, English formed a verb, to dog: to follow someone the way a dog follows, closely and persistently. The adjective dogged arises from the noun (or the verb) with the adjectival suffix -ed, meaning "having the character of a dog; dog-like". Its earliest recorded senses in the fourteenth century reflect the negative canine stereotype of the period — surly, currish, malicious, cruel — because medieval and early modern culture associated dogs with scavenging, snapping, and a servile and threatening manner. The semantic centre of gravity then migrated. From the sixteenth century onward, the hunting and working dog's prized traits — refusal to abandon a scent, endurance, a grip that will not release — are increasingly what dogged points to. By the eighteenth century the adjective had crystallised around "tenacious, grimly persistent, unflagging", a sense that could still carry a hint of dogged obstinacy but was increasingly used with admiration: dogged determination, dogged pursuit, dogged resistance. Pronunciation as a semantic marker. Because the same spelling serves the adjective and the past tense of to dog, careful English preserves a distinction in speech: the adjective dogged is pronounced disyllabically /ˈdɒɡɪd/, with a clearly articulated second syllable, whereas the past-tense form dogged is normally /dɒɡd/ (monosyllabic). This phonological distinction lets the two words coexist in print without confusion, and it is a feature that careful readers and formal speakers of English maintain — a rare case where a sound difference exists specifically to keep two homographs apart. Sociocultural history. The word's changing evaluation tracks the changing status of dogs in English society. When dogs were viewed with suspicion — as scavengers, curs, and dangerous strays — dogged meant snappish and surly. As dogs became valued as hunters, guardians, and companions, and as the Victorian period celebrated grit, persistence, and the refusal to be beaten, dogged became, in effect, a praise-word for endurance. The collocation dogged determination is now a stock phrase of admiring biography, sports writing, and journalism. Yet the double edge never entirely disappears: dogged effort is admired, but a dogged person can also be seen as inflexible, unable to let go — a trace of the old stubborn animal image that has never fully faded. Literary attestation and register. In Honglou meng, Aroma's "dogged streak" exploits exactly this double edge: her tenacious devotion is presented as both a virtue (utter loyalty) and a limitation (the same persistence, transferred wholesale from Grandmother Jia to Bao-yu, looks like mechanical absoluteness). The word belongs to standard literary and journalistic register, sits above plain stubborn, and is at home in character sketches where persistence of character is being appraised. Its biography thus runs from a noun of unknown origin, through a period of negative dog-stereotyping, to a firmly admiring modern adjective of unflagging persistence — while always keeping the faint, ancient image of a dog refusing to let go. That image, older than the word itself, is what makes "dogged streak" so exact for Aroma: loyalty that holds on like a grip, regardless of who it is transferred to.
