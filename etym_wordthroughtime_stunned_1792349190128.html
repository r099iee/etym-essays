- [c. 1300–1450] — Evolved in Middle English (stonen / astonen / stunien) from the conflation of Old French estoner (to strike like thunder) and Old English stunian (to crash, make a loud din), describing being knocked senseless or physically dazed by a violent kinetic blow.
- [16th–17th c.] — Expanded in Early Modern English and Elizabethan drama to depict profound psychological shock, bereavement, and astonishment that arrests speech and reason.
- [18th–19th c.] — Cemented in boxing, sporting journalism, and military reports to describe a concussive blow that leaves an opponent groggy but still standing; simultaneously developed the derived aesthetic participle stunning (astonishingly attractive or splendid).
- [20th c.] — Codified in agricultural, veterinary, and humane slaughter legislation (the stunning of livestock prior to slaughter to eliminate pain); widely used in psychological literature on trauma.
- [Late 20th–21st c.] — Established as a universal tactical mechanic in tabletop and video game design (a "stun" condition temporarily paralyzing an avatar or monster); used universally in media for breaking news of national or global shock.
