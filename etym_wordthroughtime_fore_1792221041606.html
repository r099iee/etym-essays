- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) — Attested as the adverb and preposition fore, meaning 'in front of, before in place, ahead in time, or because of', functioning extensively as a compounding prefix to designate priority, spatial orientation, or temporal antecedence.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) — Retained as an adverb and adjective denoting the front part; increasingly coalesced with maritime vocabulary to distinguish the forward quarters of naval vessels from the stern quarters.
- Early Modern English (c. 16th–17th c.) — Firmly established as an independent substantive noun in naval terminology ('at the fore', 'fore and aft') and specialized sporting commands; expanded through robust compounding in civic, legal, and theological prose (e.g. forethought, forefather, foreknowledge).
- Late 18th–19th c. — Coined in Scottish and Northern English vernacular as the fixed idiom 'to the fore' (originally denoting 'still surviving, alive, at hand, or readily available in cash'), before expanding into standard British literary usage meaning 'into prominent display, active notice, or public leadership'.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains high-register idiomatic currency in journalism, literary criticism, and political analysis ('come to the fore', 'bring to the fore'); universally recognized on golf courses as a mandatory interjection warning spectators of incoming shots.
