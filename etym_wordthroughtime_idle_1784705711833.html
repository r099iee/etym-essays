- Old English 'idel' - meant 'empty, void, hollow' — used of things and persons vacant of worth or occupation; in a morally alert society the idle man was the empty man, void of substance.
- Middle English - the word splits into two strands: 'vain, futile, groundless' (idle speech, idle threats — talk with nothing inside it) and 'unoccupied, doing no work' (idle hands); both were read as a kind of absence.
- Renaissance & early modern - 'idle' sharpens into a social judgment — the idle poor, idle thoughts — still carrying emptiness as much as laziness.
- 18th-19th century - the Industrial age turns 'idle' into an economic term: idle machines, idle capital, idle time, where unproductive time is counted as waste.
- Contemporary - chiefly 'doing nothing / not in use' (an idle engine, idle hands) and 'pointless, groundless' (idle curiosity, an idle threat); the old moral sting has faded into plain description, lingering only in mildly disapproving phrases like 'the idle rich'.
