The history of allegiance is the history of loyalty turning from a personal kneeling into a modern citizenship — and then into a metaphor scholars use for devotion to ideas themselves. Its deepest root is the ancient word for a people growing together: reconstructed PIE *h1lewdh-, to grow or rise, which also yields words for the folk or tribe across the Indo-European family, from Slavic ljudije to German Leute.

From that root Germanic derived *liudi-, the people or folk, and by extension the sworn retainer drawn from them. Frankish warriors carried this vocabulary into Gaul, where Old French shaped it into lige, the liege man bound above all others to one lord. A liege lord was owed lige homage, the highest and most exclusive service, and the noun ligeance named exactly that bond. When Norman scribes brought the term to England after 1066, Middle English borrowed it as allegiaunce around 1300, already slightly respelled by confusion with the verb allege, to plead or declare upon oath — a fitting confusion, since allegiance was always declared by swearing.

From homage to treason:

  • Feudal England: allegiance is personal and graded — petty lords, great barons, and the king each claim their measure, and chronicles record transfers and renunciations as carefully as battles.
  • Statute law: the Treason Act of 1352 makes breach of allegiance to the king the gravest crime, converting a private bond into public law that still echoes in modern treason statutes.
  • Early modern state: as kings centralise power, allegiance migrates upward to the crown alone, and oath-taking becomes the machinery of conformity through Reformation and civil war.
  • Atlantic revolutions: American and French revolutionaries keep the word but change the master — allegiance is now owed to constitutions and republics, sworn by naturalised citizens to this day.

Sound and spelling shifts: The journey from lige to allegiance shows French suffixation (-ance forming the abstract noun), English prosthetic a- becoming al- by analogy, and the great modern regularisation of the plural in -s. Pronunciation softened the French nasalised vowel into the English diphthong, giving the present stress on the second syllable. Literary attestations run from Chaucerian homage through Shakespearean kings demanding allegiance to Miltonic angels debating to whom it is owed, and on to modern courtrooms where witnesses swear it with raised hands.

The modern metaphor: Once allegiance belonged to citizens, journalists and academics began lending it outward — party allegiances, tribal allegiances, brand allegiances, and, as here, allegiances to theories. Each extension preserves the old gravity while admitting the plural: where one lord once sufficed, modern people juggle many, and the word now often measures exactly that crowded, divided loyalty the source sentence gently mocks.
