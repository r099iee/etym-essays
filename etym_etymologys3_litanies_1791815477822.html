Greek origins. The word begins with Greek λιτή (litḗ), “prayer, entreaty, supplication”, a noun of the language of begging and petition. Related forms include the verb λιτανεύω (litaneúō), “to pray, entreat”, and the abstract λιτανεία (litaneía), “supplication”. The family is religious from the start: litē is the prayer of a suppliant, addressed to a god or a powerful man, and it belongs with λίσσομαι (lissomai), “I beseech”, in the vocabulary of urgent request rather than of praise or sacrifice.

An uncertain root. Greek litē has no secure Indo-European etymology. Beekes treats it as probably Pre-Greek, and the connection often proposed with lissomai does not yield a clean reconstructed root; the word may belong to the substrate vocabulary of Greek religious language. This is why the derivation chain starts with the attested Greek noun rather than with a starred PIE form. Asserting a PIE ancestor here would be guesswork dressed as philology.

Hellenistic and Christian adoption. In Hellenistic Greek litaneia develops the sense of a solemn, often public supplication, and the verb litaneuein is used of entreating God. The Greek-speaking Church took up the noun for a structured petitionary service, and by the fourth century litaneia could mean a procession with hymns and the repeated cry Kyrie eleison. These litanic processions became a characteristic urban rite of Constantinople, associated with John Chrysostom and resorted to in times of earthquake, famine and war. The Orthodox deacon’s ektenia (“stretching out”, i.e. earnest prayer) is the living descendant of the same practice.

Latin and the Western liturgy. Latin received the Greek noun as litania, and the word is attested in Western ecclesiastical writers from the fourth to the sixth century. The great Roman processional litanies — the Litania Major of 25 April and the three Rogation Days before Ascension — fixed it in the calendar, and Gregory the Great is credited with organising the Roman Major Litany in 590 during a plague, although the procession itself is older. In the West the litany became a text as much as an act: a deacon’s or priest’s series of petitions with a congregational refrain, chanted in procession, at ordinations, at the Easter Vigil, in penitential seasons and at the deathbed.

French and English. Old French letanie (twelfth century) passed into Middle English as letanie, litanye in the thirteenth century. The word arrived with the Norman and Angevin church and was first a technical term of the Latin rite, familiar from parish processions and primers. The modern spelling with i is a later Latinising correction of the French e, restoring the vowel of litania; the plural litanies follows the French pattern in -ies. English stress falls on the first syllable, /ˈlɪtəni/, against Greek litaneía and Latin litania, which stress the penult.

Reformation. Luther produced a German Litanei in 1529, and Cranmer an English Litany in 1544, printed in 1545 and incorporated into the 1549 Book of Common Prayer. The English litany became a signature of reformed vernacular worship, retained by Anglicans when much else was stripped away; “to read the litany” was a familiar parish duty, and the word acquired a faintly Anglican, slightly archaic colour that it still has in literary English.

From prayer to complaint. The figurative sense develops from the litany’s most obvious formal property: the repetition of the same response at length. A “litany of woes” or “of complaints” is a recital whose length and monotony are the point. By the twentieth century this is the dominant non-ecclesiastical use, and modern dictionaries list the liturgical sense first only because of chronology, not frequency. The pejorative charge is modern: the liturgical litany was never tedious to its users, but to outsiders it looked like mechanical repetition — exactly the impression Carlyle exploits when he prices the priests’ prayers by the hour.

Key stages and formal notes

  • Greek litē (5th c. BCE) “prayer, entreaty” → litaneia (Hellenistic) “solemn supplication” → Late Latin litania (4th–6th c.) “litany, procession”.
  • Old French letanie (12th c.) → Middle English letanie (13th c.) → modern English litany, plural litanies.
  • The Greek ending -eia was remodelled in Latin as -ia; the medial vowel then weakened in French to e, and English restored i under Latin influence in the sixteenth century.
  • The plural in -ies is the regular English reflex of French -ies; the capital L in the source is Carlyle’s stylistic choice, not a liturgical convention.
