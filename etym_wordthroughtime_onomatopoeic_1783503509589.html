Early 19th-Century Scholarly Coinage - Onomatopoeic emerges in English as a strictly technical adjective of rhetoric and grammar, used only by grammarians and critics to describe words formed by imitating natural sounds, and carries a learned, bookish register.
Later 19th-Century Linguistic Adoption - As philology professionalizes, onomatopoeic becomes a standard term in phonetics and comparative linguistics, moving from a term of praise for poets into a neutral descriptive label of word-formation, though still confined to specialist writing.
20th-Century Broadening - The adjective enters ordinary educated discourse through language teaching, dictionaries, and discussion of poetry and comics, so that buzz, cuckoo, and splash are now commonly called onomatopoeic by non-specialists, losing its exclusive scholarly air.
Contemporary Sense - Today onomatopoeic is a familiar, positive descriptor for words whose sound echoes their meaning, still precise in criticism (opposed to mere sound symbolism) but no longer restricted to rhetoric, with the noun onomatopoeia remaining the more everyday form.
