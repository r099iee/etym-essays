The history of the word heredity is the history of a single idea — that what is left behind is received by those who come after — migrating from the field of law into the heart of biology. The story begins not with genetics but with the most ancient of human institutions: inheritance. Its ultimate ancestor is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *gʰeh₁-, meaning "to leave, to leave behind, to abandon, to let go," a verb of parting and loss that the earliest Indo-European speech communities would have used for everything from a hunter abandoning a camp to a dying man leaving his possessions to his kin. Out of this root of departure and emptiness, the Latin legal world fashioned one of its most consequential words: hērēs, the "heir" — literally the person to whom the estate of the dead is "left." The genius of the Latin formation lies in its viewpoint: the root speaks of the one who lets go, and yet the derived noun names the one who stays to receive. In a society built upon testamentary law and patrician continuity, the hērēs was a figure of enormous social gravity, and from him the whole Roman vocabulary of succession was spun: hērēditās, the legal state of heirship; hērēditārium, that which is inherited; and the verb hērēditāre, to take as heir.

As the Latin language yielded to the Romance vernaculars, hērēditās passed by the ordinary sound changes of Gallo-Romance into Old French as eredité, later hérédité, the fixed term for inheritance in feudal society. Feudal Europe made the concept still more central: titles, lands, offices, and obligations all descended by heredity, and the medieval legal mind elaborated precise rules about who might inherit and in what order. It was this French word, carrying the full freight of feudal succession, that entered English in the fifteenth century as heredite. For three centuries the English word lived quietly in the legal register, meaning the transmission of estates and honours. A son inherited his father's land; the meaning was concrete, economic, and thoroughly social.

The great transformation came in the nineteenth century, and it came from two directions at once. On one side stood the developmental biology of the pre-Darwinian age, which had begun to ask how the characteristics of parents reappear in offspring; on the other stood the statistical and evolutionary thought of Herbert Spencer and Charles Darwin, which urgently needed a word for the organic transmission of traits, since natural selection could only operate if variations were heritable. In 1862–1863 Spencer pressed the old legal noun into scientific service, and the biological sense of heredity was born: the transmission of physical and mental characters from parents to offspring. The semantic bridge is almost poetically clean — the parent "leaves behind" a bodily and mental endowment exactly as the testator leaves behind an estate, and the child "succeeds" to it exactly as the heir succeeds to the land. Victorian science, needing a rigorous term, reached into the ancient vocabulary of succession and pulled out the perfect word.

The new sense flourished and quickly overshadowed the old. Gregor Mendel's laws, rediscovered in 1900, gave heredity its mechanisms; the word gene (1909) named its units; the rise of genetics as a discipline in the early twentieth century absorbed heredity into its machinery. Darwin's phrase "inheritance of acquired characteristics" and the controversy over Lamarckism turned heredity into one of the pivot terms of modern science. Alongside the scientific triumph, however, the word also acquired darker cultural meanings. The eugenics movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries seized upon heredity to argue that social worth, intelligence, and criminality were biologically fixed — a perversion of the concept that discredited it and its vocabulary for decades. In literature, heredity became the engine of naturalist tragedy: Zola's Rougon-Macquart cycle is built entirely on the premise that a single tainted heredity plays itself out across generations, in madness, alcoholism, and obsession. Here the word carries its old fatalistic charge: the individual is not the author of their nature but its heir, bound by what has been left to them.

In the twentieth century the concept evolved again. The rise of environmental science and developmental biology complicated the simple picture of genetic determinism: the modern discipline of epigenetics shows that the environment can influence how genes are expressed, sometimes even across generations. Sociologists coined social heredity for the transmission of customs and knowledge; anthropologists spoke of cultural heredity. The word thus radiates outward from the laboratory into law, medicine, sociology, and everyday speech — "a talent for music runs in his heredity." Yet every one of these uses retains the ancient core image of the root *gʰeh₁-: something is left behind, and someone comes after to receive it. Whether the inheritance is a field in ancient Latium, an estate in feudal France, a gene in a Victorian study, or a family curse in a naturalist novel, the grammar of heredity is the grammar of leaving and succeeding. It is a word that has carried human anxieties about mortality, family, and fate for over two thousand years, and it shows no sign of surrendering them.
