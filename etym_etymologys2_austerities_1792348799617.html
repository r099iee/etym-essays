Note on historical semantic evolution: Historical linguists (Pierre Chantraine, Michiel de Vaan, OED) trace Greek austēros to the PIE root denoting dryness (*saus- / *h₂ews-), cognate with Greek auein ("to dry up") and English sear. In early Greek medicine and culinary writing, austēros was strictly a gustatory term applied to dry, astringent wines or unripened fruits that puckered the tongue and drew moisture from the mouth. By philosophical metaphor in Plato and Aristotle, an "austere" person was someone whose character was dry, serious, and unsoftened by humor or flattery. Late Latin Christian monasticism transformed this philosophical gravitas into the voluntary physical hardships of ascetic mortification, which 19th-century Indologists subsequently adopted to translate the Vedic concept of tapas.
