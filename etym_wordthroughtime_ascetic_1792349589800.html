- [Classical Greece (5th–4th c. BCE)] - Greek askētḗs originally designated a professional athlete in rigorous bodily training, gymnast, or artisan who perfected their craft through disciplined physical toil - [perception: admired for physical prowess, athletic discipline, and bodily mastery]
- [Hellenistic & Roman Philosophy (3rd c. BCE–2nd c. CE)] - transferred by Cynic, Stoic, and Neoplatonist philosophers (Epictetus, Musonius Rufus, Plotinus) from athletic body training to spiritual and ethical self-mastery (áskēsis of the soul) - [perception: celebrated as the mark of philosophical wisdom, emotional tranquility (ataraxia), and independence from worldly passions]
- [Early Christian Patristic Era (3rd–6th c. CE)] - adopted by the Desert Fathers (Saint Anthony, Saint Pachomius) and Christian monasticism to describe radical hermits who mortified the flesh, fasted, and prayed in the desert - [perception: revered as holy champions fighting spiritual warfare against worldly corruption and demonic temptations]
- [17th c. Early Modern English] - borrowed into English during the religious upheavals of the Reformation and Counter-Reformation, denoting both the monastic practitioner (noun) and the austere way of life (adjective) - [perception: viewed with reverence by devout mystics, but occasionally viewed with suspicion by Protestant reformists who rejected Catholic monastic penance]
- [19th–20th c. Secular & Sociological Expansion] - broadened by Friedrich Nietzsche, Max Weber (The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism - 'inner-worldly asceticism'), and modern aesthetics - [perception: analyzed sociologically as the psychological foundation of modern capitalist labor discipline and artistic minimalism]
- [21st c.] - applied widely to minimalist design, extreme athletic regimens, financial frugalism (e.g. FIRE movement), and digital detoxing - [perception: viewed as a deliberate, empowering antidote to consumerist excess and digital overload]
