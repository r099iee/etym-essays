Evolution of "dells." The word is older than England's name: its root was already in the mouths of the Germanic peoples before they crossed the North Sea, and its modern life reaches from the shepherd's landscape to the fairytale wood. The history of dell is a history of shrinking and deepening, a word that began by meaning a whole valley and ended by meaning one hidden pocket of it.

1. The Indo-European root. The family begins with the reconstructed PIE root *dʰel-, "to curve, vault, arch," a root of hollow and arched shapes that also appears in Greek thólos, the round domed building, and in words for vaulted and concave things across the family. Whether this root and the Germanic words for valleys are truly one and the same is debated, but the picture is inviting: a valley is a hollow, and the earliest sense is of something scooped and curving in the earth. The Germanic branch took the root into *dalą, "a valley, a dale, a stretch of low-lying ground," a word of enormous vitality that survives in Old Norse dalr, Gothic dal, Old High German tal (modern German Tal), and in our own dale, Dale, and Dallas (from a Scottish Gaelic and Brythonic borrowing).

2. Old English: the diminutive is born. Alongside the plain dæl, "dale," Old English formed a jō-stem derivative, dell, literally "the little dale," a diminutive naming a smaller, shallower, more enclosed hollow. The pairing is the whole story in miniature: dale is the broad valley, dell the pocket within it. In the Old English period dell was a plain word of the working landscape, the word a shepherd or ploughman would use of a sunken bit of ground among trees, with no literary refinement whatsoever.

3. Middle English: the poets take it up. In Middle English the word continued as del, delle, but its use began to drift. Small and sheltered, the dell became a favourite of alliterative verse and early lyric poetry, where it gathered a gentle, homely, almost domestic colouring, a place of retreat, of herbs, of lovers, and later of shepherds. It was during this period that the word began to separate from plain landscape description and to take on the affective tint, sweetness shading toward secrecy, that it has never lost.

4. The early modern and antiquarian revival. In the 16th and 17th centuries dell was still an ordinary countryside word, recorded across the dialects, and the lexicographers duly listed it. But as English urbanised and its literary language turned toward French and Latin borrowings, native landscape words began to feel antique. In the 18th and 19th centuries the antiquarian and Romantic movements reached back for exactly this vocabulary: dell, dingle, dene, combe were revived as consciously literary words for native scenery, and the OED began to mark dell as chiefly archaic or dialectal. Its sweetness now shaded toward the eerie: a dell was a place to hide in, a place where the fairies might be found, or a place where a traveller might be lost.

5. Tolkien and the twentieth century. It was this revival that delivered the word to J. R. R. Tolkien, whose philologist's love of archaic native vocabulary made dell natural to his prose. In The Hobbit (1937) the plural appears in the topographical sentence of the descent toward Mirkwood: dells below the level of the path, overhung with bushes and low trees. Tolkien deploys the word with philological exactness, a small sunken hollow of exactly the sort an Old English speaker would have named dell, and he presses its accumulation into menace, each hollow a waiting pocket. Meanwhile the word was living a second life on the map: "the Dells," as a plural place-name for scenic river-cut ravines (the Wisconsin Dells above all), fixed the word's picture of natural scenery in American tourism.

  • Sound change: PIE *dʰ- → Proto-Germanic *d- (deaspiration), and the Germanic a-stem endings to the Old English -e of dell; no consonant shift marks the modern word, which is phonologically unremarkable and therefore often felt as homely and native.
  • Semantic drift: from "valley" (broad) to "small valley" (diminutive) to "secluded, wooded, hidden hollow" to, in place-names, "scenic gorge"; the word has steadily shrunk its landscape and deepened its secrecy.
  • Social register: from working-country word, to dialect, to poetical archaism, to fantasy vocabulary, to tourist place-name; every step down the social ladder has been offset by a step up in literary charm.
  • Literary attestations: in Old English glossaries and charters; in Middle English alliterative poetry and lyrics; in Spenser, Milton, and the Romantics; in Tolkien (1937) and later fantasy; in American place-names such as the Wisconsin Dells.

Contemporary state. Today dell is emphatically a word of the poets, the place-names, and the fantasy shelf. No surveyor writes "dell" on a map where "hollow" or "depression" would do, and no estate agent praises a property for its delis; but the word keeps its full power in narrative, where a dell is intimate, sheltering, and faintly ominous, a valley so small it can both harbour and hide. The plural dells inherits all of this and adds accumulation: not one secret hollow but a landscape of them, running below the path like a warning written in the ground itself.
