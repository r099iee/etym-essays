Proto-Indo-European *yewg- (also written *yug-) — "to join, harness, yoke" (reconstructed root; reflexes include Latin iungere "to join", Greek ζεύγνυμι "to yoke", Sanskrit yuj-, English yoke via Germanic *juką); c. 4500–2500 BCE ↓ Proto-Indo-European *yugóm — "yoke, the thing that joins" (derived noun from *yewg-; reflexes: Latin iugum, Greek ζυγόν zugón, Sanskrit yugá-, English yoke) ↓ Latin iugum — "yoke; (figuratively) the yoke of servitude, bondage" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g., Plautus; also in the military ritual phrase sub iugum mittere, "to send under the yoke") ↓ Latin subiugāre — "to bring under the yoke, subjugate" (verb; sub- "under" + iugum "yoke" + -āre; attested chiefly in post-classical and ecclesiastical Latin, e.g., the Vulgate, 4th c. CE, and in late imperial writers) ↓ Latin subiugātus — past participle, "brought under the yoke, subjugated" (the form English borrowed) ↓ English subjugate (v.) — "to bring under the yoke, conquer, subdue" (borrowed from Latin subiugātus in the mid-15th century, c. 1430–1450, in the era of Lydgate and Caxton) ↓ English subjugated — past tense and past participle, formed with the native English past/participle suffix -ed DEBATED / NUANCED: The Classical Latin word for "subjugate" was subigere (sub- "under" + agere "to drive"), which also meant "to conquer"; subiugāre, built on the noun iugum "yoke," is rare in Classical authors and becomes common only in Late and Church Latin. English borrowed the past-participle form subiugātus (as it did for many -ate verbs such as stimulate and terminate), which explains the English -ate ending. The PIE root *yewg- is well established; the immediate Latin chain iugum → subiugāre is securely attested, with the caveat above about Classical vs. Late Latin frequency.
