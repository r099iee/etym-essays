exemplary in the source sentence — Ruskin recalling his mother as a schoolgirl at the Misses Rice's establishment, being a girl of great power, with not a little pride, [who] grew more and more exemplary in her entirely conscientious career, much laughed at, though much beloved, by her sister — means conspicuously good, model, worthy of imitation: the pattern girl whose needlework, prayers, lessons, and manners are held up for others to copy. The sentence catches the word's characteristic social sting: the exemplary child is admired by teachers, loved in spite of herself by family, and laughed at by the spirited — goodness on display invites both reverence and teasing. Already pride is hinted as the motive force: she is exemplary not only from conscience but from power and pride disciplined into virtue.

In general modern use exemplary remains this double-faced adjective of models: describing conduct so good it ought to be copied — exemplary behaviour, exemplary pupil, exemplary service, exemplary courage, an exemplary husband — and, by a striking reversal, describing punishment so severe it warns others off — exemplary punishment, exemplary damages, exemplary sentence. Both faces share one logic: made into an example. The saint and the scapegoat are both exhibited; one invites emulation, the other deters by terror. Dictionaries list the praising sense first because it is commonest, but the punishing sense is equally central and much older in law.

  • Core moral-praising sense (source sense): ideal and imitable: so good as to be a model for others; e.g. exemplary conduct in class, exemplary attendance record, exemplary charity work, her patience with difficult patients was exemplary. Implies visibility: exemplary goodness is performed before witnesses and proposed for copying. Typical collocates are institutional — schools, armies, hospitals, courts praising exemplary records.
  • Illustrative-representative sense: serving as a typical example: well chosen to illustrate a rule or class; e.g. an exemplary passage from Gibbon, exemplary specimens of Gothic carving, this case is exemplary of wider neglect. Here the moral glow fades and the logical function remains: standing for the whole class. Academic prose prefers illustrative or representative today, but exemplary survives in formal criticism.
  • Punitive-deterrent sense: made an example of by severity: inflicted harshly precisely to frighten others; e.g. exemplary punishment, exemplary fine, exemplary damages (extra damages beyond compensation, awarded to punish and deter), the ringleaders received exemplary sentences. The bridge is exhibition: as the saint is displayed for copying, the offender is displayed for avoidance. Eighteenth-century judges openly called for exemplary executions; modern courts award exemplary damages against libellous newspapers and brutal employers.
  • Ecclesiastical and literary-historical sense: drawn from books of exempla: in medieval preaching an exemplum (plural exempla) was a short moral tale about a saint or sinner used to drive home doctrine; exemplary literature means collections such as the Gesta Romanorum or Chaucer's Pardoner's Tale apparatus. To call a life exemplary in this older register is to say it belongs in such a book — hagiography-ready.
  • Noun use (rare, formal): an exemplary as a model copy: an exemplar is the standard noun (a model, a typical specimen, a manuscript copied); exemplary as a noun occasionally means a transcript or attested copy of a document (an exemplary of the charter), now obsolete except in canon law and diplomatics. Do not confuse the pair: an exemplar is the model, exemplary describes model-ness.
  • Degree and irony: exemplary is a strong, formal, Latinate word; in everyday speech it can sound stiff or priggish, which is why Ruskin's laughing sister matters. Modern speakers often blunt it with adverbs — truly exemplary, absolutely exemplary, remarkably exemplary — or use it ironically: an exemplary detention record for a persistent truant. The irony works because the word's moral height makes the fall funny.
  • Domain extensions: exemplary damages (tort law), exemplary history (history written to supply moral models, after Livy and Plutarch), exemplary prophecy (theology), exemplary causation (scholastic philosophy: God creating after the pattern of divine ideas). Each carries the same cut-out-and-display mechanism from the Latin root: something taken out (ex-imere) to be shown.

Usage notes: prefer exemplary when visible model-ness is the point — records, service, behaviour held up for others. Prefer excellent for private quality needing no audience, admirable for warm approval, model as the plainer synonym (a model pupil). In legal writing, exemplary damages is a term of art distinct from compensatory and nominal damages; in school reports exemplary is the highest available praise-band, above good and satisfactory, and parents understand it as such.

The two great senses — praiseworthy model and frightening warning — meet in the schoolroom of the source sentence. The pattern girl is exhibited for emulation; her ungovernable sister, had she been flogged, would have been exhibited for deterrence. Both would have been exemplary. Ruskin, who spent his life preaching by examples — stones of Venice, laws of Fiesole, Fors letters — never abandoned the word's pedagogy: show one perfect needle, one perfect capital, one perfect deed, and the class may learn. Laughter, he implies, is the price exacted by the spirited for being taught.
