• Oarsmen: Similar because both words denote people who row boats with oars; differs because oarsmen is the older, male-marked term (paired with oarswomen), now literary or formal, while rowers is neutral and modern.
  • Oarspeople / Oarswomen: Similar because they are gender-inclusive or female variants for a person who rows; differs because they remain marked, rarer alternatives to the plain rowers.
  • Scullers: Similar because a sculler is a kind of rower working two oars (sculls); differs because it names a specific technique and boat type rather than rowing in general.
  • Paddlers: Similar because paddlers also propel a small craft by hand power; differs because they use free paddles rather than oars fixed against the boat, and belong to canoeing and kayaking.
  • Boatmen / Boatwomen: Similar because they work on and move boats; differs because the role covers ferrying, sail, engine and steering, not specifically pulling oars.
  • Watermen: Similar because in British usage a waterman was historically a boatman-oarsman plying a river for hire; differs because the term is old-fashioned and tied to the licensed hire trade of the Thames.
  • Mariners / Seamen / Sailors: Similar because all are people who work on watercraft; differs because they belong to sailing and seafaring, require no oars, and carry naval or mercantile connotations.
  • Coxswain (cox): Similar because the cox is part of a rowing crew and sits in the racing boat; differs because the cox steers and calls the rhythm without pulling an oar, so is not a rower in the strict sense.
  • Crew: Similar because in North America "crew" is the name for a rowing team or the sport itself; differs because it is a collective noun for the whole boat, not a single person.
  • Gondoliers: Similar because a gondolier propels a boat through water by hand; differs because gondoliers stand and push a single oar as a kind of stern scull, a Venetian speciality distinct from seated rowing.
