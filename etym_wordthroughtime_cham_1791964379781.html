- 13th–14th c. — European chroniclers turn the Mongol emperor’s title into a byword for limitless wealth and power, fixing it in Western geography as the name of a fabulous Asian ruler.
- 16th c. — English travel writing and geography compendia use Cham, chiefly in “the Great Cham of Tartary”; the word is exotic, bookish and faintly archaic even then.
- 1759 — Smollett’s joke applies the title to Samuel Johnson; Cham acquires a mock-heroic, affectionate-satirical tone it never had before.
- 19th c. — biographies and histories of literature harden “the Great Cham of literature” into a stock description of Johnson, while Cham outside the phrase falls out of ordinary use.
- 20th–21st c. — the epithet is used almost exclusively of Johnson, or by learned extension of any literary dictator; it now signals familiarity with eighteenth-century letters rather than with Asian history.
