Two root words (WET and NURSE), treated in isolation. CHAIN 1 — WET: → PIE *wed- 'water, wet' (reconstructed; root of the Indo-European water family: Greek hýdōr 'water', Sanskrit udán 'water', Latin unda 'wave', Hittite watar 'water', Russian voda 'water') → Proto-Germanic *wēta- / *wētaz 'wet, moist' (zero-grade formation from *wed-) → Old English wæt 'wet, moist, liquid' (adjective; also noun 'moisture', verb wǣtan 'to wet'; cognate Old Norse vatr, Old Frisian wet) → WET 'covered or saturated with water or other liquid' (Modern English; the first element of WET-NURSE, where it specifies the milk-giving/lactating function) CHAIN 2 — NURSE: → PIE *(s)nau- / *(s)na- 'to flow; to give suck, to suckle' (reconstructed; the base of the nourishing/suckling word family; its shape and extensions are debated — see note) → Latin nūtrīre 'to suckle, nourish, foster, support' (with nūtrix, nūtrīcis 'wet-nurse, she who suckles', and nūtrīcius 'that nourishes') → Late Latin *nūtrīcia 'nurse, governess' (feminine noun from nutricius) → Old French norrice / nourrice 'foster-mother, wet-nurse, nanny' (10th–11th c., from Late Latin nūtrīcia) → Middle English norice / nurrice 'woman who suckles or fosters an infant' (c. 1200, from Old French) → English nurse 'woman who suckles an infant' (noun and verb; from the 16th–17th c. broadened to any caregiver, prompting the explicit compound) → NURSE 'a person who cares for the sick or a child' (Modern English; the second element of WET-NURSE, which keeps the original suckling sense explicit; the compound wet-nurse is attested from the 17th c. in contrast with dry-nurse) Note: the reconstruction of the PIE base behind Latin nūtrīre is debated. The traditional *nu-tri- (from *(s)nau- 'to flow, to suckle') is repeated in many handbooks, but some scholars treat the internal structure of nūtrīre as obscure; the Latin-to-English path, by contrast, is securely attested.
