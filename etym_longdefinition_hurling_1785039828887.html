HURLING is the present participle of the verb to hurl: to fling, cast, or throw something with great force, speed, and often violence. In the stone-giant episode of The Hobbit, ch. iv ("Over Hill and Under Hill"), Bilbo, peeping out "in the lightning-flashes," sees the giants across the valley "hurling rocks at one another for a game, and catching them, and tossing them down into the darkness." The word is exquisitely chosen: it confers a monstrous, casual energy on the giants, who treat boulders like handballs; the force is real and terrifying, yet the mood is playful and almost sportive — hurling for a game. Its imitative-sounding root, suggesting the whirr and rush of a heavy body through the air, lets the reader hear the arc of each thrown rock even before it smashes among the trees below. The tone is elemental and grotesquely comic: raw power without malice, the mountain itself at play, and the travellers crouched helpless in a crack of the rock, reduced to spectators of a game played with boulders. Senses of the word (exhaustive)
  • Throwing with force (primary verb sense): to hurl is to throw violently and at speed, at or toward a target — hurling a spear, hurling a brick, hurling a challenge.
  • The action as process (participial): "hurling rocks" names the ongoing act of violent projection, exactly as in the Tolkien passage.
  • The Irish sport (noun): hurling, the ancient stick-and-ball game of Ireland (whose players hurl the sliotar with a hurley), a proper-noun use of the same verb, standard from the 19th century.
  • Verbal violence (extension): "to hurl insults, abuse, accusations, curses" — to cast words with the force and trajectory of a missile; the transfer is ancient and natural.
  • Archaic intransitive sense — to rush, dash, hurtle: in Middle English hurlen meant "to rush headlong" as well as "to fling"; this sense survives chiefly in the related word hurtle.
  • Figurative / personifying discharge: "the train hurled itself through the tunnel," "the waves hurled themselves against the cliff" — a violent motion attributed to a thing or a force.
Semantic bridge The two oldest senses — "to rush" and "to fling" — grew out of the same imitative sound-image: a heavy body tearing through the air. Hurling is thus the sound-word of violent projection, and that is why the verb lends itself so well to the comic sport of Tolkien's giants: the game is heard before it is seen, the whistle of each boulder doing the giants' work for them. The modern verbal sense ("to hurl abuse") transfers the same trajectory to language: words are thrown with the force and violence of a stone. Register and near-synonyms Hurling is a strong, physical verb of everyday force, equally at home in reporting ("police hurled tear-gas canisters") and in epic. Among throwing-words the shades are precise: throw is the neutral general verb; cast is deliberate and smooth, often with aim (a line, a net); pitch suggests an intended arc; toss is light and careless; fling adds abandonment and a whipping motion; heave stresses the effort of lifting and letting go; sling suggests a loose, whirling trajectory; launch implies sending something off with an engine or a push; catapult adds mechanical or magical projection. Hurl is the superlative of violence and speed — the word for a throw that the thrower means to land hard.
