Gnawing is the present participle and verbal noun of gnaw: to bite, chew, or wear away something persistently with the teeth, typically in small repeated bites rather than a single clean bite. Rats gnaw wood, a dog gnaws a bone, a child gnaws a pencil. The physical image is effortful, repetitive attrition — teeth working at resistant matter, leaving tooth-marks, fraying edges, slowly hollowing or severing. In the source sentence, "been gnawing on the thing at all?" uses this literal sense playfully: has the garment (a cravat) been chewed or worried at, perhaps by moths, mice, or nervous teeth?

Far more salient in modern literature is the extended, metaphorical sense: a gnawing pain, hunger, doubt, guilt, anxiety, or suspicion that torments continuously, as if chewing at one from inside. A "gnawing hunger" is not momentary appetite but an aching emptiness; "gnawing doubt" is a worry that will not leave the mind alone; "gnawing pain" in medicine is a dull, persistent, boring ache as opposed to sharp or throbbing pain. The participle functions both as progressive verb ("the rats were gnawing through the cable") and as adjective ("a gnawing sense of failure"), and as noun ("the gnawing of conscience"). In all figurative uses the semantic bridge is persistence plus erosion: just as teeth slowly destroy material, so worry slowly destroys peace of mind.

Grammatically, gnawing preserves the argument structure of gnaw: gnaw on / at / through something. Gnaw on suggests sustained chewing without necessarily consuming; gnaw at adds a nuance of distressing attack ("guilt gnawed at him"); gnaw through stresses penetration to the other side. The irregular past gnawed (older gnew) is now regular, and the participle doubles as an attributive adjective with gradability ("a more gnawing anxiety").

  • Literal chewing / biting persistently: to work at food or objects with the teeth. Example: "The puppy was gnawing on a rubber toy." This is the etymologically primary sense from Old English gnagan, shared with rodents whose incisors must be worn down.
  • Eroding / wearing away gradually: of water, wind, or time figuratively gnawing cliffs, foundations, or resolve. Example: "Waves gnawing at the shoreline." The bridge is visual analogy: attrition resembles chewing on a landscape scale.
  • Tormenting worry, guilt, or doubt: the dominant literary-psychological sense. Example: "A gnawing feeling that she had forgotten something." The mind is figured as a body being eaten; persistence is the shared element.
  • Persistent dull pain or hunger: medical and bodily usage. Example: "A gnawing ulcer pain woke him at night." Peptic-ulcer and hunger-pang descriptions favor gnawing because the ache feels like an internal biting.
  • Vexing / harassing (to gnaw at someone): to pester or carp continuously. Example: "Critics gnawed at the minister for weeks." Here the teeth are verbal; the bridge is harass-as-bite, an ancient metaphor.
  • Technical zoological / entomological: gnawing mammals (Rodentia) and gnawing mouthparts of insects describe a taxonomic feeding type. "Gnawing animals must wear down ever-growing incisors." This preserves the oldest classificatory use.

Connotation is uniformly uneasy. Unlike nibble (dainty, pleasant) or chew (neutral), gnawing implies compulsion, anxiety, or damage. A dog happily chews; an anxious mind gnaws. Collocations confirm this: gnawing doubt / fear / guilt / hunger / pain / suspicion / uncertainty — nearly all negative. Even the playful literal question about a cravat carries mock-suspicion: has something been damaging the garment behind one's back? Learners should therefore reserve the figurative adjective for persistent, low-level torment, not sudden intense agony.
