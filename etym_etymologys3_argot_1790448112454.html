The history of argot is unusually honest about its own ignorance: every major dictionary from Littre to the OED ends the etymology with origin unknown. What is securely documented begins in Paris in 1628, when the pamphlet Histoire generale des larrons uses argot to mean both a confraternity of beggars and the jargon they speak, glossed for respectable readers as the language of les gueux. Within decades French police reports, rogue-literature, and dictionaries of the langue verte use argot, argotique, argotier freely for the rotating lexicon by which pickpockets, fences, coiners, and prostitutes name the police, victims, tools, and punishments. The social mechanism is transparent even when the phonetic origin is not: a closed occupational group relexicalises dangerous referents to talk safely in public and to test membership.

Because the origin is obscure, proposed deeper derivations must be reported as debates rather than facts. The oldest guess links argot to Old Provincial argot meaning a cock spur or snag, supposedly the emblem of the beggar brotherhood, with a parallel in ergot (spur, ergot fungus). A second links it to the verb argoter (to quarrel, to scold) or to jargon, gargoter by expressive alteration. A third, beloved of 19th-century antiquaries, expands it as art gothique, thieves Latin as a Gothic art, which is phonetically impossible. A fourth sees a proper name, a Captain Argot. None commands consensus, and modern etymologists prefer to leave a gap rather than force a Latin or PIE root. That gap is itself instructive: underworld words often enter writing already fully formed, with oral prehistory lost because speakers wanted it lost.

  • Phonetic stability: from 1628 to today the form barely moves: argot pronounced /aʁɡo/ in French, borrowed into English as /ˈɑːɡəʊ/ with anglicised final vowel but kept silent t. Spelling never assimilates, which preserves its foreign, knowing flavour in English prose. Derivatives argotique, argotier, argotisme remain French-marked.
  • Semantic widening in French 1680-1900: from beggars corporation to beggars language to any professional jargon to general popular slang. By the time Balzac and Hugo devote whole chapters to it, argot is both a philological treasure and a moral symptom: energetic, metaphorical, cruel, and proof that the people create language faster than academies. Hugo calls it the language of misery, the wretched hive turned word-hoard.
  • Literary attestations: Villon 15th-century jobelin ballads are retroactively labelled argot; Rabelais parodies it; 17th-century Le Jargon de l argot reforme glosses it; 1840s Sue Mysteres de Paris and 1862 Hugo Les Miserables canonise long word-lists; 20th-century Celle, Bruant, and later San-Antonio keep it alive. Each wave both records and enriches the stock.
  • Borrowing into English 1860 onward: first in histories of Paris crime and in philological essays, then in Stevenson, Chesterton, and Wodehouse as a knowing exotic for any hearty in-group talk. English keeps the narrower sense of thieves slang plus the playful extension to schools, regiments, and hunting fields, without the full French generalisation to all slang.
  • Socio-cultural context: argot flourishes where surveillance is high and solidarity vital: prisons, barracks, docks, schools, and now forums and games. Modern French verlan (inversion), louchébem (butcher pig-latin), and banlieue borrowing from Arabic and Romani are productive argot engines performing exactly the 1628 function with new material.

In short, argot names a permanent social process with one securely dated French birth certificate and an irrecoverable oral prehistory. Its prestige in English comes precisely from that Parisian romance: to say argot is to invoke Villon, Hugo, and the hunting field rendered momentarily roguish, a learned wink that one knows thieves cant and chooses to hear sporting slang as its hearty country cousin.
