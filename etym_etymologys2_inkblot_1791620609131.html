This compound noun is formed from two distinct Germanic and Romance-derived English roots: ink + blot.

1. ink

Ancient Greek (c. 4th c. BCE)
ἔγκαυστον (énkauston)
"burned-in purple ink used by Roman and Byzantine emperors"
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th c. CE)
encaustum
"purple-red ink for imperial signatures"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
enque (later encre)
"writing fluid, ink"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th c.)
enke / inke
"dark colored fluid used for writing and printing"
↓
ink

2. blot

Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*blutaz / *blutt-
"bare, exposed, soft spot; blemish"
↓
Middle Dutch / Middle Low German (c. 13th c.)
blotte / blot
"bare spot, blemish, stain"
↓
Middle English (c. 1325)
blot / blotte
"a spot, stain, drop of ink spoiling paper; moral blemish"
↓
blot

Note: Middle English blot emerged in the 14th century, borrowed from Low German or Scandinavian dialects (compare Old Norse blettr 'spot, stain' and Middle Dutch blotte), and was phonologically reinforced by Old French bloter ('to soil, stain').

↓
Modern English (c. 18th c. / 1920s)
inkblot
"a drop or splash of ink on paper; a projective psychological stimulus"
