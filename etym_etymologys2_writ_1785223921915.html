The participle writ "written" and the noun writ "a formal legal order" share one Germanic heritage and are traced together below, with the participle branch leading to the root word.

  • PIE *wreid- "to tear, scratch, incise" (reconstructed root; the physical act of scratching a line into a surface).
  • Proto-Germanic *wrītaną "to scratch, tear, write" (reconstructed; the Germanic verb for making marks by incising).
  • Old English wrītan "to write, engrave, carve letters" (attested from the 8th century; a strong verb of the first class).
  • Old English past participle (ge)writen "written" — the strong participle from which the clipped form descends — and beside it the Old English noun (ge)writ "a writing, a document, a scripture" (both attested in Old English).
  • Middle English writen / ywritten "written" (the fuller participle) alongside the apocopated form writ "written" (a clipped variant following the strong-verb pattern of biten > bit and rīden > rid).
  • Early Modern English writ "written" (the short participle, preserved in the King James Bible, in legal and poetic diction, and in the fixed phrases writ large, writ in stone, writ on the wall) while the longer form written wins as the standard participle.
  • Modern English WRIT — the archaic participle "written", surviving in fixed idioms; and the legal noun writ "a formal court order" (from the Old English noun (ge)writ), a separate but same-form branch of the same stem.

Debate / nuance note: (1) The Germanic verb family is unusual in that the root's literal sense is physical — "to tear, scratch" — and "to write" is a specialized Germanic development: writing was conceived as incising marks into a surface, which is why Old High German rīzan means only "to tear" (Modern German reißen) while Old Norse rīta and Gothic writs ("a stroke, a letter, a mark") keep the inscriptive sense. (2) Italic took a different route (Latin scribere, also originally "to scratch"), so the "scratch = write" image is a Germanic specialization, not a shared Indo-European one. (3) The exact PIE shape is *wreid- as commonly reconstructed, but the attested Germanic *wrītaną is secure; the derivation of the participle writ by apocope (dropping the final syllable) in Middle English is well attested.
