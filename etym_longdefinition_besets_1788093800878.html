besets is the third-person singular present indicative of the verb beset, whose core meaning is “to surround or hem in on every side” and, by a natural figurative extension, “to assail or trouble persistently”. In the passage from The Hobbit, Roäc the Raven warns Thorin that the host of the lake-men and elves “besets” his company — pressing them in on every side so that escape is blocked and defeat is likely. The word conjures the picture of a complete encirclement from which there is no easy exit: the ring of enemies is the literal image, and the moral and political trap of the treasure-hoard is the wider image that the speech plays upon.

Primary sense — physical surrounding.

  • “To surround, hem in or enclose on all sides,” of armies, hosts, floods, ice or terrain: an army besets a valley; a town is beset by the enemy host. The idea is of a hostile or obstructive ring drawn completely around a person or place, leaving no way through.
  • “To occupy or garrison (a place), to post troops about it,” an older, now rare sense that shades into siege language: to beset a fortress is to invest it so tightly that neither supplies nor reinforcements can enter.
  • From the Old English inheritance, an archaic sense “to set about, stud or adorn (a surface)”: a garment “beset with pearls”, a “crown beset with jewels”. This ancient decorative meaning — literally setting ornaments all around an object — survives today only in archaic and poetic prose, where it still glitters in descriptions of treasure and regalia.

Secondary sense — figurative assailing. Because troubles, doubts, fears and temptations surround a person exactly as enemies surround a fortress, beset shifts naturally to “to press hard upon, attack from all sides, harass”.

  • Modern dominant usage is abstract: “beset by difficulties”, “beset with problems”, “beset on all sides by critics”, “a plan beset with obstacles”. The object – a person, an institution, a project – is imagined as a beleaguered centre.
  • The verb is overwhelmingly passive or participial in modern prose; the active third-person “besets” of the source sentence belongs to a dignified, slightly archaic literary register that Tolkien deliberately cultivates across his prose style.
  • Besetting sin — from the King James Bible, Hebrews 12:1, “the sin which doth so easily beset us” — became a stock phrase of moral and religious writing for the fault that persistently surrounds and overcomes the will; in the 17th–19th centuries it escaped theology into general prose (“his besetting sin is vanity”).
  • Polar and maritime idiom: a ship “beset in ice” is surrounded and frozen fast, helpless and immovable; the phrase is standard in Arctic and Antarctic expedition narratives, whaling memoirs and coastguard reports from the 19th century to the present day.

Semantic bridge. The whole family of senses flows from the two morphemes: the prefix be- (“about, all around”) plus set (“to place”). First one places things all around an object (decorative sense); then soldiers are placed all around a town (military sense); then enemies surround a person (assailing sense); and finally calamities, doubts and sins press in around a life (abstract sense). The trajectory is a steady march from concrete spatial arrangement to abstract psychological and social pressure — and as the senses grow more abstract, the verb grows more passive, until today the active form is rare and the participle is everywhere.

Register and morphology. Beset is formally irregular: the present, past and past participle are all beset (beset – beset – beset), a relic of the old Germanic strong-verb class to which the simple verb set once belonged. The word belongs to formal, literary and journalistic prose; it would be unusual in casual conversation. The rare noun besetment (“the state of being beset”) appears in 19th-century writing; unbeset is a poetic negative. The word’s antiquity, its biblical echoes and its siege-flavoured gravity make it a favourite of heroic and fantasy narrative — the exact register of Tolkien, who reanimates the archaic overtones of King James English throughout The Hobbit.
