• Knight: Similar because both are historical landed-military ranks in the same social world; differs because a knight is a dubbed mounted warrior, while a squire (his former attendant) need not fight and becomes the rural gentleman.
  • Esquire: Similar because both come from the same Old French word and denote the same rank; differs because esquire is the fuller legal-style courtesy form used in addresses, while squire is the everyday social and rural label.
  • Gentleman: Similar because both denote high social standing; differs because gentleman is broader and moral-social, while squire specifically implies land, a local estate and parish standing.
  • Landlord: Similar because both are men of landed property; differs because landlord stresses the property relation to tenants, whereas squire adds traditional community prestige and authority.
  • Yeoman: Similar because both describe rural men of standing; differs because a yeoman is a freeholding farmer below the gentry, lacking the squire's local authority and claim to gentlemanly rank.
  • Baron / lord: Similar because both denote men of rank over rural land; differs because baron and lord belong to the titled nobility, far above the modest country squire's provincial standing.
  • Shield-bearer / armour-bearer: Similar because both name the squire's original military function; differs because these are historical occupational terms, not modern social titles of estate ownership.
  • Country gentleman: Similar because both describe the landed rural male of property; differs because "country gentleman" is descriptive phrasing, whereas squire is the established title-word of the type and carries the historical chivalric echo.
