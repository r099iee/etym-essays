urgencies: the plural of urgency, denoting earnest and insistent necessities or importances requiring immediate attention; pressing, clamorous demands; the collective, enumerated pressures of a particular time. In this sentence from a critical essay on Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the plural noun urgencies denotes the pressing, clamorous demands of a particular historical era — the revolutionary upheavals, religious controversies, and surges of patriotic fervor that press in upon a writer from outside. The essayist observes that Gibbon, unlike many other great interpreters of the past, was "not enticed or driven to his subject by the urgencies of his time", and the deliberately chosen plural makes the abstraction concrete and enumerable: urgencies are a series of competing, current, topical pressures, each one capable of hijacking a scholar's attention. The tone is one of quasi-physical coercion — the word's core image is of pushing and shoving, so that the urgencies of an age appear as forces that drive a person from behind, rather than as quiet inner interests that pull him forward. What is being celebrated is a rare scholarly detachment: Gibbon chose his great subject from internal intellectual curiosity and sober judgment, remaining untouched by the agitation, the partisanship, and the fashionable causes of his own day, and it is precisely this immunity to "urgencies" that the passage presents as his greatness. The word carries a wider web of senses than the plural alone suggests.
  • Sense 1 — singular urgency, the state of being urgent. The pressing importance of a matter that requires speedy action: a matter of urgency, the urgency of the situation, an air of urgency. This is the common, everyday sense.
  • Sense 2 — plural urgencies, enumerable pressing demands. The bookish, literary plural used in the Gibbon passage: a series of competing, topical pressures, each capable of seizing attention. The plural turns an abstraction into a countable list — the urgencies of an age.
  • Sense 3 — the inherited Latin idea. Latin urgentia meant "a pressing, a pressure", the noun of the verb urgere, "to press, push, drive"; the older English sense of the word is a pressing claim upon attention or action, and that pressure is still felt at the word's core.
  • Sense 4 — medical and institutional. In modern French, urgence developed the institutional sense "emergency": a French hospital sign reading service des urgences is an accident-and-emergency department. In English and international medical usage, urgency is the triage priority of a case requiring prompt attention.
  • Sense 5 — rhetorical and political. A sense of urgency is the perceived need for immediate action in public discourse; politicians press for urgency, and urgency motions give a topic priority in a parliament. The word here names not the demand itself but the public mood that accompanies it.
  • Sense 6 — contextual shade. In the Gibbon passage the plural personifies history's pressures as forces able to drive a scholar — revolutionary, religious, and patriotic passions are enumerated as the urgencies of the time, opposed to the free internal pull of curiosity.
The semantic bridge runs from the physical to the social. The Latin verb urgere means to press, push, or shove a physical thing; from that base it extended to pressing a person, pressing a request, pressing an argument, pressing a soldier into battle. Urgency is therefore pressure made abstract — the quality of pushing upon us that demands haste — and the plural urgencies collects the pressures of a period into a force that can literally drive a person from behind. Gibbon's greatness, on this reading, is that he let neither the push of his age's causes nor the pull of its pleasures choose his subject for him. In usage the plural is a deliberate, literary choice. It collocates with of an age, of the time, of the moment, of the situation; it is paired with verbs like driven, impelled, seized, and distracted; and it is typically found in essayistic and historical prose rather than in speech. The singular is everyday, the plural is scholarly — and the plural is exactly the form that lets a writer count and name the pressures of an era one by one.
