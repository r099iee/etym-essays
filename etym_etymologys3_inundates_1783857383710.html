The biography of INUNDATES begins more than five thousand years ago in the reconstructed ancestor of most European and North Indian languages, Proto-Indo-European. There the noun for "water," *wédōr (genitive *udnés), was built on the root *wed-, whose zero grade *ud- named the element itself. This single root fanned out across the entire family, and its descendants still supply everyday vocabulary today. In Hellenic it became húdōr, the source of English hydro- (hydrant, hydrate, hydrogen); in Indo-Iranian it yielded Sanskrit ud-án and ud-aká, "water," the ancestor of the learned Hindi and Odia word udak; in Celtic a suffixed form produced Old Irish uisce, which survives in the phrase uisce beatha, "water of life," the etymon of English whiskey; in Balto-Slavic it hardened to voda, the ordinary word for water in Russian, Polish, and Czech; and in Germanic it grew into *watōr, which became Old English wæter and our own water, while the related formation *wēt- gave the adjective wet. Even the extinct Anatolian branch preserved it in Hittite wātar, and Tocharian in wär.

The Italic branch: from water to wave. The line that directly produces INUNDATES took the Italic route. From the zero grade *ud- the ancestors of Latin built the noun unda, "wave, water," and from it the verb undāre, "to rise in waves, to surge." The formation is old: the -n- in und- reflects a nasal extension of the root, and the word is attested from the earliest Latin literature (Plautus) through the classical poets, where unda is the standard poetic word for the sea's waves and the water of rivers.

Building the compound: in- + undāre → inundāre. Latin was fond of stacking prefixes onto its wave-verb to describe overflow in different directions. ab- + undāre produced abundāre, "to overflow, to abound," which gave English abound and abundant; re- + undāre produced redundāre, "to flow back, to overflow," which gave English redundant (literally "overflowing, more than needed"); and in- + undāre, with the prefix meaning "into" or "upon," produced inundāre, "to overflow, to flood, to cover with water." The verb is attested in the classical prose and poetry of the first centuries BCE–CE — Livy describes rivers inundating fields, Pliny the floods of the Nile — and its past participle inundātus supplied the stem that English would borrow.

Entry into English: the 17th-century borrowing. English took the word from the Latin participial stem inundāt-, and inundate is first recorded about 1620. In its first century in English the verb was literal: rivers, seas, and storm waters inundate land. It appears in early modern chronicles, in translations of classical historians, and in natural philosophy, always formal and Latinate — a word of science and serious prose rather than common speech. The related noun inundation entered English earlier (15th–16th century) and was used for the Flood and for any great overflow of water.

The figurative rise: from rivers to paperwork. The semantic drift from water to workload is already latent in Latin, where inundare could describe being overwhelmed by anything in quantity. In English the figurative sense grows steadily through the 18th and 19th centuries. By the age of steam and bureaucracy one is "inundated with letters," "inundated with petitions," "inundated with applications"; the expanding postal system and then mass media created the floods of paper that the metaphor needed. The 20th century brought the telephone call and then electronic communication, and the verb's figurative sense — overwhelming with messages, requests, work, or information — became its dominant meaning, until today the water is almost always metaphorical: an inbox is inundated with mail, a server with requests, a reader with references. The literal sense survives robustly in hydrology, flood science, and news reporting, but the overwhelming majority of everyday uses are figurative.

Sound and morphology along the way. The root *wed- shows the classic Indo-European ablaut: full grade wed- in Greek húdōr and Germanic *watōr, zero grade *ud- in Latin unda and Sanskrit udán. In Latin the sequence in- + undā- kept both consonants: inundāre /in.unˈdaː.re/. In English the word is trisyllabic in-un-date, stress on the second syllable — inundate /ˈɪnʌndeɪt/ (UK) — and the borrowed participial suffix -ate supplies the ordinary third-person present inundates. The spelling preserves the double u of the Latin compound, and the in- prefix, meaning "into/upon," is now all but invisible to the modern speaker, who reads the word as a single gesture of flooding.

Literary and modern attestations. The word's career can be charted through its appearances: literal in 17th-century hydrography and in John Evelyn's and Dryden's descriptions of floods; figurative in 19th-century letters and journalism ("inundated with correspondence"); and in the modern period ubiquitous in office English ("inundated with emails," "inundated with requests") and in the sciences, where "inundation maps" and "inundation modelling" are standard tools of flood management. The card's own context — "an epic catalogue or priamel deliberately inundates the reader with a dense, rapid sequence of historical and geographical references" — exemplifies the modern figurative sense: the reader is flooded by sheer volume of information, exactly as a valley is flooded by a river in spate.

Summary of the drift. Literal flooding (1620s) → figurative overload of letters and work (19th century) → dominant digital/metaphorical sense of overwhelming quantity (20th–21st century), with the literal hydraulic sense retained as a technical and news-register term. The unifying metaphor — quantity as rising water — has been constant from Latin inundāre to the modern inbox.
