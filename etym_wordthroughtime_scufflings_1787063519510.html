- c. 1570s–1600s: Emerged in Early Modern English as a frequentative verbal noun describing brief, chaotic hand-to-hand brawls, wrestling bouts, or disorderly shoving contests among disorderly crowds.
- 1650s–1720s: Expanded to describe acoustic friction and scraping movements, particularly the sound of feet dragging, rustling, or scurrying hastily along ground surfaces.
- 1800s–1880s: Widely adopted in Victorian nature writing, ghost stories, and gothic fiction to evoke uncanny, unseen animal or vermin activity rustling behind wainscoting or within dense undergrowth.
- 1900s–Present: Solidified dual usage: metaphorically denoting minor political, bureaucratic, or sporting disagreements, and literally denoting low-level nocturnal rustling or scraping sounds.
