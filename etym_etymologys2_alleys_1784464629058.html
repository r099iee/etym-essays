Uncertain deep stage: Latin ambulāre "to walk, go about" is built on the prefix ambi- "around, on both sides" plus a verbal base often connected with a PIE root *h₂el- "to wander, roam" (cf. Greek alánomai "I wander"); the exact pre-Italic reconstruction of this base is not securely settled. [DEBATED — treat the PIE stage as hypothetical.] → Latin ambulāre (to walk, go about, take a walk), attested from the 2nd century BCE → Vulgar Latin *alāre (to go, walk), traditionally derived from ambulāre by syncope and contraction through intermediate *amblāre / *amlāre; the exact pathway is debated (alternative sources such as *ambitāre have been proposed) [DEBATED] → Old French aler / aller (to go), attested from the 9th century CE → Old French alée / alee (a going; a walk, path, gallery, passage), noun formed on the feminine past participle of aler, attested from the 12th century CE → Middle English aleye / aley / alley (passage between buildings; a walk), attested from the 14th century CE → Modern English alley (narrow passage between buildings; garden walk; bowling alley), hence the plural ALLEYS of the card. NOTE: English alley and French allée are the same etymon but diverged in register — French kept the grand formal avenue or garden walk, while English narrowed the word toward the tight urban passage. The traditional Vulgar Latin *alāre from ambulāre is the standard etymology but is debated in detail.
