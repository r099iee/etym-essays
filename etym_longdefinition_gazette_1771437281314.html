Gazette is a formal term for a journal or newspaper, and in this literary context it denotes the official publication in which the results of the imperial examinations and the attendant appointments were announced. The narrator shows us Yu-cun alone in his quarters, urgently "hunting out" a copy — the verb betrays his anxiety, since everything he hopes for has been reduced to the single act of verifying a name in a printed list. The word's tone is doubly weighted. On one hand it is dry, bureaucratic, and terminal: a gazette is not a place of rumour or gossip but the authoritative record of the state, the text to which one repairs when oral report can no longer be trusted. On the other hand, because it is here the vehicle of a career's fate, it trembles with private urgency; the formality of the noun sharpens, rather than dulls, the emotional stakes. In the wider logic of the novel, the gazette represents the public machinery of success — the printed confirmation that separates a hopeful candidate from a confirmed official.

The word is polysemous, and every modern sense grows out of a single Venetian coin:

  • Sense 1 — the official journal of record (primary modern sense). A government's or an institution's official publication, in which appointments, promotions, honours, bankruptcies, and legal notices are made public and binding. In British and Commonwealth usage this sense is fixed by The London Gazette (founded 1665), the journal of record of the crown.
  • Sense 2 — a newspaper or journal generally (archaic and literary). For its first decades in English a "gazette" was simply a small newspaper; the word remained a stock title for newspapers and literary journals across Europe (The Gazette, La Gazette), and it survives in this general sense in historical prose and period titles.
  • Sense 3 — the verb to gazette. "To announce, publish, or appoint officially in a gazette" (1660s); a general or a peer is still said to be "gazetted" to his rank in British and Commonwealth usage. The adjective gazetted means "officially announced or appointed."
  • Sense 4 — gazetteer. Originally "a writer for a gazette" (17th century), then, through an 18th-century bestseller, "a geographical dictionary" — a remarkable and well-documented drift from newsman to atlas.
  • Sense 5 — the historical bridge. The word begins as the name of a small Venetian copper coin, the gazzetta (16th c.); the earliest news-sheets cost one such coin, and within a generation the price named the product — first the news-sheet, then the medium, then the state record. A rival theory derives the coin's name from Venetian gazza "magpie" (a chattering bird), a metaphor for gossipy news; the ultimate base is uncertain and is not Indo-European.

Its near-synonyms are best separated by register and function. Newspaper is the general, everyday word for a periodical of current affairs, without any suggestion of official authority. Journal stresses periodicity and can be neutral or literary, but it lacks the governmental aura of gazette. Chronicle implies a retrospective historical record of events in sequence, whereas a gazette is a live, official, in-the-moment announcement. Bulletin is an official notice or report, close in function, but it names the message rather than the periodical that carries it. In collocation, gazette pairs with administrative vocabulary — the official gazette, to publish in the gazette, to appear in the gazette, the London Gazette, a government gazette. Its register is formal and institutional, the language of law, government, and the press rather than of conversation; and that formality is exactly what makes its appearance in this scene so effective — the humble, anxious Yu-cun, searching a state record for his own name, is momentarily touched by the grandeur of the institution that will decide his fate.
