Harfoots (proper noun, plural) — In the legendarium of J.R.R. Tolkien, the most numerous, representative, and historically foundational of the three ancient breeds or kindreds of Hobbits (alongside the Stoors and the Fallohides). In the Prologue to The Lord of the Rings ("Concerning Hobbits"), Tolkien describes them as browner of skin, smaller and shorter of stature, strictly beardless and bootless, possessing feet with tough leathery soles covered in thick curling hair, and having an ingrained ancestral passion for digging and dwelling in underground tunnels and hillside burrows (smials).

Philologically, Harfoots represents an English translation and calque crafted by Tolkien to represent their original Westron (Common Speech) ethnonym, Kûd-dûkan ("hole-dwellers" / "hair-feet"). Utilizing his academic method of translating Westron texts into English, Tolkien derived the name from an Old English compound form *hǣr-fōt (literally "hair-foot"), celebrating the iconic physical trait of the Hobbit race: their tough, unshod feet clad in thick, warm fleece. In accordance with proper patronymic and ethnic compounding rules, Tolkien specifically pluralized the name as Harfoots rather than *Harfeet, reflecting regularized nominal morphology.

The distinctive cultural, historical, and physiological traits of the Harfoots comprise several key aspects of Tolkien's ethnography:

  • Physical Morphology and Appearance: Shorter and lighter than the Stoors and Fallohides, with hands neat and nimble. They were characteristically browner of skin and had hair that was dark and curly. They were entirely beardless, wore no footwear, and their feet possessed thick, leathery soles that rendered shoes superfluous while enabling them to move with uncanny, almost magical stealth.
  • Subterranean Architecture and Burrowing: The Harfoots preserved the oldest and most quintessential Hobbit architectural custom: tunneling into hillsides, sandbanks, and rolling downs to construct elaborate subterranean homes known as smials. While Stoors and Fallohides were more willing to build surface houses of wood, brick, or thatch, the Harfoots insisted that a true home must be carved into living earth, establishing the archetype immortalized in Bilbo Baggins's luxurious hole at Bag End.
  • Historical Alliances with Dwarves: During their ancient residence in the upper vales of the Anduin between the Misty Mountains and Greenwood the Great, the Harfoots lived in the highland foothills and maintained long, amicable, and fruitful relations with the Dwarves of the mountains. From the Dwarves they learned much of stonework, masonry, metal-craft, and tunneling, incorporating dwarven engineering techniques into their earthworks.
  • The Great Westward Migration (c. T.A. 1050): When the shadow of Sauron first began to darken Greenwood the Great (transforming it into Mirkwood) around the year 1050 of the Third Age, the Harfoots were the very first Hobbit kindred to pull up stakes and undertake the perilous westward crossing of the Misty Mountains. They pushed through the mountain passes into Eriador, moving westward as far as Weathertop while the other breeds still lingered in the eastern river valleys.
  • Comparative Tripartite Ethnography:
    • Harfoots: Browner, smaller, burrow-dwellers, mountain and foothill folk, friends of Dwarves, the demographic majority that established the Shire's cultural norm.
    • Stoors: Broader, heavier-built, river-dwellers, lovers of water, swimming, and boating; wore mud-boots, could grow down on their chins, and lingered longest in the Vales of Anduin (producing Sméagol and Déagol).
    • Fallohides: Fairer of skin and hair, taller and slimmer, woodland lovers, hunters, friends of Elves, daring and adventurous; though the least numerous, their aristocratic temperament led them to become the chieftains and leaders of the Shire (including the Took and Brandybuck lines).
  • Bedrock of Shire Culture: When all three breeds eventually converged and intermingled in the Shire after T.A. 1601, the Harfoots constituted by far the vast majority of the population. Consequently, the quintessential traits of the Shire Hobbit—love of good tilled earth, contentment with simple rural pleasures, distrust of boats and foreign adventures, round painted doors, and quiet subterranean comfort—are almost entirely the inherited cultural gifts of the ancient Harfoots.
  • Semantic and Philological Bridge: The term bridges Tolkien's invented philology with ancient Germanic folklore. By calquing the Westron name into an archaic English compound evoking "hairy feet" and "hare's feet," Tolkien grounded his diminutive heroes in authentic English rural landscape lore, transforming a whimsical fairytale element into a rigorously documented anthropological ethnicity.
