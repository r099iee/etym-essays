• Celtic: Similar as an overarching ethnolinguistic and cultural descriptor encompassing Cornwall along with Wales, Brittany, Scotland, Ireland, and the Isle of Man; differs because Celtic is a broad macro-family term, whereas Cornish refers specifically to the unique Southwestern Brittonic branch, landscape, and people of the Cornish peninsula.

  • Brittonic: Similar in historical linguistics as the designation for the Southern Celtic branch that produced Welsh, Breton, and Cornish; differs because Brittonic is a technical linguistic classification, whereas Cornish denotes the specific regional language, people, and culture.

  • Breton: Similar because Breton and Cornish are sister Southwestern Brittonic languages that diverged only in the early Middle Ages through emigration from southwestern Britain to Armorica; differs because Breton belongs geographically and politically to Brittany in modern France.

  • Welsh: Similar as a closely related Western Brittonic Celtic culture and language sharing deep Arthurian mythology and ancient Celtic roots; differs because Welsh pertains to Wales (*Cymru*) and possesses an unbroken literary tradition and hundreds of thousands of native speakers.

  • Devonian: Similar as the immediate geographic and cultural neighbor of Cornwall in the southwest peninsula of England; differs because Devonian pertains to Devon, which was integrated into Anglo-Saxon Wessex much earlier and lacks a distinct surviving Celtic language identity.

  • Gaelic: Similar as a sister Celtic linguistic group; differs because Gaelic (Goidelic) comprises Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx, which represent an entirely separate branch of the Celtic language tree with distinct phonology and grammar.

  • West-Country: Similar as a broad regional term covering the southwestern English counties; differs because West-Country is an informal English regionalism, whereas Cornish carries official Celtic national minority status.
