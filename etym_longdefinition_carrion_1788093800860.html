carrion is a noun meaning the dead, decaying flesh of an animal — the body after death has begun to rot — and, above all, that flesh as it is sought and eaten by scavengers: vultures, crows, ravens, kites, hyenas, and great soft-bodied insects. The word is visceral and precise: it is not a neutral term for a corpse but a term for flesh in the process of becoming — meat in decay, viewed from the point of view of the scavenging world that consumes it. It entered English around 1300 (Middle English caryon, carion) from Anglo-French, ultimately from Latin carō “flesh”, and it has carried an unfading stench in its connotation for seven centuries.

Primary sense. Carrion is the rotting flesh of a dead animal. In natural history it is the dead weight of the food web: every carcass becomes carrion, and carrion is the resource that sustains the great scavenger guild — the vultures that wheel over a kill, the crows and magpies of the roadsides, the burying beetles that inter small corpses. The word's defining grammatical habit today is attributive — carrion birds (birds that feed on carrion: vultures, crows, ravens, kites), carrion beetles, carrion flies, carrion flowers — a productive adjective use that began in the fifteenth century with the “carrion crow”.

  • Sense 2 — the human corpse as carrion. From its first English records the word is also applied to dead human bodies when they are left to rot — on battlefields, gallows, and plague pits — and the medieval imagination of the battlefield is saturated with it: carrion is what the dead become, the body relinquished to birds and worms. This darkest, most literal sense is the root of every figurative use that follows.
  • Sense 3 — figurative and abusive. Carrion is a classical term of contempt: a “carrion” person is loathsome, worthless, morally rotten; things “carrion” are corrupt, disgusting, or doomed. The semantic bridge is the oldest moral metaphor in Western writing — moral corruption as physical decay, the soul's worthlessness imagined as rotting meat. In the insult tradition of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word was a blunt instrument of abuse, and it remains available today in any prose that wants its objects to stink.
  • Sense 4 — science, names, and ecology. Modern biology has domesticated the word with lethal exactness: carrion beetles (the Silphidae, “undertakers” of the insect world that bury small carcasses), carrion flies (the blowflies whose larvae are the forensic entomologist's clock for dating death), and the so-called carrion flowers — Stapelia and the giant Rafflesia — which mimic the odour of rotting flesh to lure the flies that pollinate them. In ecology, carrion is treated as a keystone resource: its removal by scavengers drives energy through entire communities, and its study has become a discipline of its own (carrion ecology). The word, while staying vivid, has been made exact.
  • Sense 5 — related historical uses. In the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries “carrion” could spell a dead body generally and, in butchery, the waste flesh or offal of a slaughtered animal; in falconry manuals carrion was the coarse food of common hawks as opposed to the “blood” diet of noble ones; and the adjective fed colour language — a “carrion” complexion was the grey-green of putrefaction. None of these extends the word's sense; they extend its reach.

Usage. Carrion is standard and unmarked in ecology, ornithology, and forensic science, and fiercely figurative in literary prose. It is not a synonym of corpse (neutral, human, and often fresh), nor of carcass (the whole dead body of an animal), nor of offal (the inedible internal parts); carrion is the flesh — dead, decaying, and only the flesh — and the whole network of its senses hangs on that one brutal fact.
