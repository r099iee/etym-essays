Daresay is a fused English formula whose two elements have deep Indo-European pedigrees, and whose whole story is a study in how a phrase of boldness becomes a particle of politeness. The first element, dare, descends from PIE *dʰers- 'to dare, be bold, venture', a root attested across the family — Sanskrit dhṛṣṇoti 'he dares', Greek tharséō 'I am of good courage', Old Church Slavonic drъznǫti 'to dare'. In Germanic the root produced the preterite-present verb Proto-Germanic *durzaną 'to dare': a preterite-present is a verb whose old past-tense forms carried a present meaning ('I have dared' → 'I am bold enough to'), the same class that gave English may, can, shall, and must. Old English inherited it as durran, with the present singular dear — 'ic dear' = 'I dare'. Middle English kept durren/daren, and the modern verb dare still shows its modal ancestry in the bare-infinitive construction (he dare not go).

The second element, say, descends from PIE *sekʷ- 'to say, utter, tell' (the root also behind Lithuanian sakýti 'to say' and Greek ennépein 'to tell'), which gave Proto-Germanic *sagjaną and Old English secgan 'to say, tell, speak'. The Old English verb shows the weak-verb pattern (its past is sægde), and Middle English seien developed the modern say by ordinary vowel reduction. Neither element is borrowed: dare and say are among the most native words in the language.

The fusion. The phrase 'I dare say' is recorded from Middle English, when dar seyen (literally 'I dare to say') could already introduce a claim. What is remarkable is the phrase's gradual freezing and semantic reversal:

  • 14th–16th c.: 'I dare say' still sounds the boldness — the speaker dares to say something that might be disputed; it is an assertion with its chin up.
  • 17th–18th c.: the formula becomes a fixed conversational hedge. As polite conversation learned to soften disagreement, 'I dare say' slid from 'I risk asserting' toward 'I suppose, very probably' — assertiveness draining out, politeness flowing in. The fused spelling daresay appears as the phrase contracts into one word.
  • 19th c.: daresay is a staple of genteel spoken English, the property of hosts, narrators, and the novel of manners; Austen's narrators and characters use it constantly as the voice of reasonable, slightly self-effacing opinion. In this period the word is at the height of its social currency.
  • 20th–21st c.: the word recedes from informal everyday speech and survives as a dated but instantly recognisable idiom, more British than American, used for effect or warmth — a host's voice, exactly as Tolkien gives it to Bilbo when he tells the dwarves that 'I daresay you will think of something' if they sit on the doorstep long enough.

Grammatical life. Morphologically the word is a fossilised first-person phrase; it is rarely used in other persons, because the fused form is a fixed first-person formula while the two-word construction keeps full modal force. The fused form typically carries a reduced pronunciation of the second syllable, and the spelling hovers between daresay and dare say because speakers and printers never fully agreed whether the phrase had become one word. Sociologically, daresay is a hedge, and hedges are social grammar: the formula lets a speaker offer an opinion while signalling willingness to be corrected, which is why it flourishes in the courteous, hierarchical speech-world of the English novel and why a polite hobbit-host would naturally reach for it. From a PIE root meaning 'to be bold' to a particle meaning 'very probably', the word has travelled the whole road from courage to courtesy.
