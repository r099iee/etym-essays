1. The ancient waste. The ancestry of heath reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European root *kait-, "forest, wasteland, uncultivated land," a root attested across a wide band of the ancient vocabulary of wild country. Whether it is a true Indo-European root or a loan from an earlier European substrate is debated — the shape and distribution of *kait- have suggested to some scholars an origin in the pre-Indo-European languages of northern Europe — but within Germanic its history is secure. From *kait- the Germanic speakers built *haithiz, "heath, open uncultivated land, untilled waste," a word that must have described a large part of the landscape the Germanic peoples actually lived in: the open scrub and moor between the forests and the fields. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic form (roughly the middle of the first millennium BCE to the middle of the first millennium CE) gives the attested daughters — Old English hǣð, Old Saxon hētha, Old High German heida, Old Norse heiðr "heath, moor," Dutch heide, German Heide — a family united in meaning across the whole Germanic world.

2. Old English hǣð. In Old English hǣð means "untilled waste land, heath," and from its first attestations in the 9th century it is a real, everyday English word for the open country beyond the plowed fields — the wild, unenclosed margins of the settlement. It appears in poetry, in charters, and above all in the names of places, and it has an extraordinary derivative: the word hǣþen, "heathen, pagan," literally "dweller on the heath." The Christian missionaries of Anglo-Saxon England named the unconverted for the land they inhabited — the people of the open wild, outside the church and the settled fields — and thus one of the great religious words of English was quarried directly out of the landscape word. The heath is, in this way, written into the very vocabulary of European religion.

  • 9th century: Old English hǣð "untilled waste land" attested; the plural hǣðas "heaths" is the regular form.
  • Old English period: hǣþen "heathen" derived — "dweller on the heath" — the church's name for the unconverted.
  • 13th century: Middle English heth / hethe, plural hethes, continues the word unchanged in meaning.

3. The land of names. From the Middle Ages through the Early Modern period heath is an everyday English landscape word, and it pours into the names of the land: Hampstead Heath, Blackheath, Putney Heath, Barnes Heath, and dozens of smaller heaths across southern England. The word was so common that it became invisible — ordinary country, worth naming, worth owning, worth walking — and the place-names preserve the word even where the wilderness itself has disappeared under city and suburb. A Londoner who says "Hampstead Heath" uses a word that outlived the wild heath it originally named.

4. The Romantic heath. In the 1800s the heath is transformed from an ordinary landscape into a literary monument. The Romantics and Victorians discovered in the open, windswept scrubland the great English image of solitude and the sublime: the heath is the place of exposure — to weather, to history, to the self — where the enclosing comforts of valley and hedgerow are stripped away. The decisive literary event is Thomas Hardy's Egdon Heath in The Return of the Native (1878), described in the famous opening chapter as "a tract of unenclosed wild" on which the prehistoric past still breathes, older than the civilization that borders it. After Hardy, the heath is not merely land; it is a character — ancient, indifferent, sublime — and the word carries that weight into all later English, including C. S. Lewis's picture of the young king looking out on "wild heaths and blue mountains" and feeling small.

5. Ecology and the modern heath. In the 20th century the heath acquired a new, scientific life. Ecology gave it a technical name — heathland — and recognized it as a semi-natural habitat of high conservation value, a mosaic of heather, gorse, bracken, and coarse grasses maintained by grazing and periodic burning against the encroachment of birch and pine. Lowland heath became a protected European habitat, the home of the nightjar, the Dartford warbler, the sand lizard, and a host of insects that live nowhere else. The same word that named the wild margin of the Anglo-Saxon settlement now names a managed reserve — the wildness is now protected precisely because the plow and the suburb have taken so much of it.

6. The word today. In contemporary English heath has kept both of its identities. In its concrete sense it names a real, endangered British landscape, still used in place-names, in ecology, and in topographical writing; in its literary sense it carries the purple-and-gold scrub of Hardy's Egdon and the wild solitude of the traveller and the outlaw. The plural heaths, as in the passage — "wild heaths, and blue mountains" — stresses extent and multiplicity, the land stretching away open on every side. A word that has meant "untilled waste land" for more than a thousand years now names both an ecosystem and a state of the soul — and in both it has never lost its root sense of open ground beyond the field.
