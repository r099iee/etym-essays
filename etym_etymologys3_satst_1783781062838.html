The verb at the heart of satst is one of the most ancient and stable items in the Indo-European vocabulary. The root *sed- "to sit" is attested in nearly every branch of the family: Sanskrit sīdati, Greek hézomai, Latin sedēre, and the Germanic sit group. Because the root is so widespread and so stable in shape and meaning, its reconstruction as PIE *sed- is among the most secure in historical linguistics. Into Germanic it produced *sitjaną "to sit," which entered Old English as sittan, a strong verb of class 5: present sitte, past singular sæt, past plural sǣton, past participle seten. The vowel alternation i : a (sittan : sæt) is the inherited ablaut of the class, and it is exactly this alternation that survives in modern sit / sat.

The second-person singular past of Old English sittan was sǣte: the strong past singular sæt plus the ending -e that Old English used for the second person singular of the past tense. It is this small ending that carries the whole story of satst. In Middle English the final -e was lost (the great final-vowel reduction that reshaped the entire grammar), and at the same time the language was generalizing the ending -st across the second person singular: it was already the marker of the present tense (sittest, comest) and of the preterite-present verbs, and by analogy it swept through the strong past as well. The result was a second-person singular past sat + st, spelled variously satst, satest, sattest. This was a purely English development — no other Germanic language went quite this way — and it shows how analogical leveling can regularize even the most irregular corner of a verb paradigm.

In Early Modern English satst was a completely ordinary, living form. The thou-paradigm was fully functional: thou satst, thou sittest, thou satest were everyday grammar, used in speech, in the law, and above all in the two great literary monuments of the age, the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611) and the plays and poems of Shakespeare. The pronoun thou and its verb endings carried a special social and emotional weight: thou was the singular, intimate, or reverential form, used to God, to a close friend, to an inferior, or in high solemnity, and the -st forms were inseparable from that register. Milton exploits exactly this solemnity when, at the opening of Paradise Lost (1667), he writes of the Spirit who "satst brooding on the vast Abyss": the verb form places the reader in direct, reverential address to the Holy Spirit, and the archaic inflection reinforces the antique grandeur of the invocation.

The decline of satst is inseparable from the decline of thou itself. Between the late 17th and the 18th century, standard English collapsed the singular and plural into a single you, partly out of politeness (you was the respectful plural that came to be used to single persons) and partly through the general simplification of the inflectional system. With you, all persons took the same verb form, and -st endings, along with thou, vanished from ordinary speech, surviving only in the Bible, in liturgy, in the language of the Society of Friends (Quakers, who deliberately kept thou), and in archaizing poetry. Modern speakers meet satst almost exclusively in scriptural quotation and in the verse of the early modern canon, where it functions as a clear stylistic marker of the thou-register.

Summary of the evolutionary chain:

  • Phonology: PIE *sed- → PGmc *sitj- (root-vowel umlaut, gemination) → OE sittan; loss of final -e in Middle English; contraction of the ending in satst.
  • Morphology: OE class-5 strong verb with 2sg past -e → analogical generalization of -st to the strong past → satst (with variant satest).
  • Syntax and register: the living thou-paradigm of Early Modern English → the fossilized archaism of later centuries as you replaces thou.
  • Semantics: stable "to be seated" from PIE to today; Milton adds the brooding, incubating nuance of the Spirit resting over the waters.
  • Key attestations: Old English sǣte (e.g. in Ælfric); Authorized Version 1611 ("where thou satst"); Shakespeare; Milton, Paradise Lost I.20–21 (1667).
