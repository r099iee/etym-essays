- 14th c. — Middle English Entrance: Borrowed from Anglo-Norman abonder and Latin abundāre, originally describing physical rivers overflowing their banks and harvests filling storehouses with grain.
- 15th–16th c. — Biblical & Theological Canonization: Consecrated in early modern English Bible translations (Tyndale, Coverdale, Cranmer, and the King James Version), establishing abounding as the defining word for divine grace, love, and mercy surging over human sin.
- 17th c. — Natural Philosophy & Cosmic Plenitude: Adopted by Francis Bacon, John Milton, and Sir Thomas Browne to celebrate nature’s boundless generative fertility and cosmic fullness in scientific and poetic writing.
- 18th c. — Augustan Irony & Moral Rhetoric: In Augustan literature (Swift, Pope, Addison), abounding serves as a dignified, high-register term in moral philosophy, frequently deployed with cutting irony to contrast utopian virtue with human corruption.
- 19th c. — Romantic Ecology & Victorian Enterprise: Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Shelley) and Victorian naturalists (Darwin, Ruskin) use the word to celebrate ecological vitality, pristine landscapes, and the energetic expansion of commercial wealth.
- 20th–21st c. — Elevated Literary & Spiritual Adjective: Retains its place as a distinguished, evocative adjective and participle in high-register prose, nature writing, and theological discourse, symbolizing generous, uncontainable fullness.
