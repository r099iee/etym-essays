An idiom in three layers. Clipping her one on the ear-hole is not a single etymological object but a collision of three separate histories. To understand it, we have to follow the verb clip, the numeral-turned-noun one, and the compound ear-hole before seeing how Wodehouse fused them into a comic gesture.

Clip: from shears to smart blows. English has three homographs spelled clip. The first, “to fasten or clasp,” comes from Old English clyppan “to embrace,” from Proto-Germanic *kluppjan. The second, “to cut or shear,” enters Middle English around 1200 from Old Norse klippa “to cut, shear,” a Scandinavian loan from the Danelaw period; its deeper origin is probably echoic, imitating the sound of shears. The third, “to strike smartly,” is first recorded in the sixteenth century and is often explained as a development of the cutting sense: a clip is a sharp, cutting stroke, and the verb comes to mean a quick blow rather than a cut. The semantic bridge is the sharpness of the contact, not the instrument.

  • c. 1200: clippen “to cut, shear” is borrowed from Old Norse.
  • 16th c.: clip “to strike sharply” appears; clip round the ear becomes a familiar phrase.
  • 19th–20th c.: the noun clip “a sharp blow” and the idiom give someone a clip on the ear are fully established.

One: the numeral as a blow. The pronoun one descends from Old English ān, from Proto-Germanic *ainaz, from PIE *óynos “one, single.” Its use as a noun for a blow is a colloquial specialization: “give him one” means “give him a hit,” with one standing in for an unspecified stroke. The pattern is widespread in British slang (“have a go,” “give it a bash”) and is attested in the nineteenth century. In the phrase, one is not counting ears; it is the fossilized object of the verb.

Ear and hole: the jocular compound. Ear comes from Old English ēare, from Proto-Germanic *ausô, from PIE *h₂ows-. Hole comes from Old English hol “hollow, opening,” from Proto-Germanic *hulą. The compound ear-hole literally denotes the opening of the ear, but in colloquial British English it is used jocularly for the ear itself, much like ear-'ole in music-hall and working-class speech. The suffix gives the phrase a cheeky, physical concreteness: not the delicate ear but the hole you could clip.

Wodehouse and the comedy of restraint. The full phrase is a signature of P. G. Wodehouse’s narrator Bertie Wooster. Wodehouse (1881–1975) grew up in the world of English public schools and Edwardian society, and he mined its slang for comic effect. Bertie’s voice is full of clipping someone one, popping, pasting, and biffing, always undercut by the “code of the Woosters,” which forbids actual violence. The humour depends on the gap between the languid, well-bred surface and the schoolboy impulse beneath. The phrase in the card’s context is a perfect specimen: the narrator is prevented by politeness from doing what he would give a shilling to do.

Register and social history. The idiom belongs to a vanished register: upper-middle-class British colloquialism of the early twentieth century, with public-school and servant-talk overtones. It presumes a world in which a cuff round the ear was a normal, even affectionate, form of correction — for children, servants, and social inferiors. That world is precisely what Wodehouse’s comedy both inhabits and gently satirizes. Modern readers encounter the phrase as period flavour, and its casual violence can jar.

Semantic drift and survival. The phrase has not changed meaning; it has changed status. Once ordinary slang, it is now archaic and literary, kept alive by Wodehouse reprints, radio and television adaptations, and the wider nostalgia for Edwardian comedy. Its components survive independently: clip round the ear is still understood in Britain, give someone one remains slang, and earhole survives as a jocular or dialect word. The full phrase, however, is a quotation, not a living idiom.

Phonology and prosody. The phrase has a jaunty rhythm: CLIP-ing her ONE on the EAR-hole, with the stress falling on clip, one, and the first element of ear-hole. The alliteration of clip and the clipped consonants of ear-hole give it a physical, almost onomatopoeic crispness. That prosody is part of the comedy: the sentence sounds like the thing it describes.

Attestations and legacy. The phrase is recorded in Wodehouse’s interwar fiction and in later dictionaries of slang as a period expression. It belongs with a cluster of similar idioms — box someone’s ears, cuff, clip round the ear — that preserve an older, more physical model of discipline. Its linguistic interest lies in the way three ordinary words, each with its own long history, can be assembled into a single vivid and unmistakably dated image.
