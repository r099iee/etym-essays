The historical evolution of Latin tempora unveils how archaic Indo-European conceptions of physical division, bodily anatomy, and abstract duration intersected to produce one of the most durable concepts in Western political rhetoric and anatomical nomenclature.

I. The Indo-European Roots: Cutting Time or Stretching Span?

Historical linguists (including Michiel de Vaan and Alois Walde) have long analyzed two primary etymological hypotheses for Latin tempus:

  • The Division Hypothesis (PIE *temh₁-): The most widely supported derivation traces tempus to the Proto-Indo-European root *temh₁- ("to cut, slice, or divide"). In ancient cosmological thought, time was not conceived as a seamless, empty Newtonian continuum, but as discrete, carved-out segments: cycles of day and night, the four seasons, and astrological epochs. This identical root produced Ancient Greek temnein ("to cut"), tomē ("a cut", source of English anatomy and tome), and temenos ("a sacred precinct cut off from common ground"). Under this view, tempus was originally a "section or cut of duration".
  • The Extension Hypothesis (PIE *ten-): An alternative proposal links the word to PIE *ten- ("to stretch, extend"), viewing time as a continuous stretch or tension of existence (cognate with Latin tendere "to stretch" and tenere "to hold"). In this reconstruction, *ten-pos- signified a linear span of duration.

II. Proto-Italic Development and Latin Phonological Shifts

In Proto-Italic, the noun developed as an s-stem neuter noun: *tempos-. In early Latin (c. 3rd century BCE), the nominative singular was written tempos, with an oblique stem exhibiting ablaut alternation between *tempos- and *tempes- (preserved in archaic adjectives like tempestas "storm, season").

During the 4th and 3rd centuries BCE, regular Latin phonological shifts dramatically reshaped the word's paradigm:

  • Rhotacism: In all inflected forms where the stem consonant -s- stood between vowels, it voiced and became -r- (a universal Latin sound change known as rhotacism). Thus, archaic plural *tempos-a shifted to *tempor-a.
  • Vowel Weakening & Vowel Raising: In the nominative singular, the unstressed short -o- before final -s raised to short -u-, transforming tempos into Classical Latin tempus. In the inflected cases, the medial vowel remained -o- before -r-, establishing the familiar Classical paradigm: nominative singular tempus, genitive singular temporis, and nominative/accusative plural tempora.

III. The Dual Meaning: Time and the Anatomy of the Skull

In Classical Latin, tempora functioned simultaneously in two major semantic domains: chronological time and human cranial anatomy. In anatomy, tempora (almost invariably plural) designated the temples—the vulnerable flat lateral regions of the head above the cheekbone. Two compelling linguistic theories explain this dual usage:

  • The Chronological Theory of the Temples: Roman grammarians and lexicographers explicitly linked the temples to tempus ("time") because the hair covering the temples is notoriously the first place on the human body where gray hair appears, providing an undeniable physical marker of biological aging and the relentless passage of tempora ("times").
  • The Cut/Thinness Theory: Modern philologists note that if tempus descends from PIE *temh₁- ("to cut"), the anatomical tempora may have originated as a descriptive name for the "cut, flat, or thinned" portions of the skull wall, representing a deadly vulnerable target in hand-to-hand combat.

From this anatomical Latin plural tempora, Old French derived temple, which entered Middle English after the Norman Conquest, while medical terminology retained direct Latin derivations in os temporale ("temporal bone") and the lobus temporalis ("temporal lobe").

IV. Ciceronian Immortalization and Modern Rhetorical Life

The sociopolitical meaning of tempora as "the times" achieved immortality in November 63 BCE. When Marcus Tullius Cicero faced the conspirator Catiline in the Senate, he launched his opening speech with the thunderous apostrophe "O tempora, O mores!". Cicero's pairing of tempora (the objective era of crisis) with mores (the subjective moral decay of the ruling class) permanently established tempora in European humanism as the quintessential term for diagnosing historical crisis.

Throughout the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and into modern literature, tempora has been continually revived by satirists, poets, and political essayists to reflect on historical impermanence, cultural shifts, and the recurring tragedy of human politics.
