Ogres (the plural of ogre) denotes, in European folklore, the monstrous man-eating giants of hideous size and ravenous appetite who lurk in castles, caves, and other dark places, devouring human flesh — exactly the nursery-tale terrors that crowd Bilbo's memory in the passage: 'Poor Bilbo sat in the dark thinking of all the horrible names of all the giants and ogres he had ever heard told of in tales, but not one of them had done all these things.' The narrator's logic is precise and rueful. Bilbo has been raised on a storehouse of 'horrible names' — giants, ogres, trolls, goblins, and worse, the whole menagerie of cautionary fireside legend; but those creatures exist only in tales, safely rhetorical, whereas the nameless thing in the tunnel with him is real, present, and active. The word therefore arrives already ironized: ogre is a label for the bogeyman of story, invoked in a voice at once folkloric, childlike, and faintly comic. Tolkien deliberately picks the most generic and literary of monster-terms, one that carries the archaic flavour of medieval romance and fairy-tale translation, where ogres are creatures of pure appetite rather than of magic — they do not enchant, curse, or bargain; they catch, eat, and are outwitted.

Distinguishing ogre from its monstrous neighbours sharpens the meaning: giant names great size alone and need not be malevolent; troll is a Scandinavian, cave-dwelling being of magic or of petrifying power; goblin is small, malicious, and twisted; monster is a catch-all for anything out of nature; cannibal names the dietary practice without any folkloric identity. The ogre alone combines a humanoid giant's size, a demon's cruelty, and a specifically cannibal appetite, fixed in the literary fairy-tale tradition of France. It is precisely this blend — size plus appetite plus a pedigree in print — that makes 'ogres' the archetypal 'horrible name' for a frightened hobbit.


Polysemy and the full modern range of ogres:
  • Primary folklore sense: a man-eating giant of European fairy tale, dwelling in castles, towers, or dark forests, who catches, devours, and is typically outwitted by children — the figure fixed in print by Perrault's Le Petit Poucet (1697). This is the sense in the passage.
  • Figurative sense — a cruel or devouring person or institution: since the nineteenth century ogre has transferred to any terrifyingly cruel, hideous, or devouring human being — 'an ogre of a boss,' 'the ogre of hunger,' 'the corporate ogre.' Historical bridge: the fairy-tale monster's defining trait is that it consumes and terrorizes the weak; any person or system that does the same is metaphorically an ogre — appetite and bulk remain, the supernatural size drops away.
  • Modern fantasy and gaming jargon: in twentieth- and twenty-first-century fantasy literature, role-playing games, and video games (from Tolkien's own Middle-earth through Dungeons and Dragons and countless titles), ogre is codified as a stock species: huge, humanoid, crude, dim-witted, and carnivorous — a fixed entry in the monster-menagerie, catalogued as distinct from giant, troll, and goblin. Bridge: the folklorist's ogre becomes a standardized game creature, the old fairy-tale cannibal reduced to a monster stat-block.
  • Attributive and derived forms: ogreish and ogrish (adjectives, 'like an ogre; cruel, hideous'), and ogress (a female ogre, from French ogresse) extend the word; ogre is also used attributively — an 'ogre economy,' an 'ogre parent' — always importing appetite, bulk, and menace.
  • Journalistic and political rhetoric: ogre is a favourite of editorialists for any looming, devouring force — inflation, bureaucracy, 'the ogre of debt' — where the word supplies vividness and moral condemnation in a single noun, keeping the old image of the thing that eats the weak.
  • Literary-technical use (Tolkien studies): critics note that in The Hobbit (1937) Tolkien uses ogre precisely as a word from tales — a label signalling the story-within-a-story world of nursery legend, opposed to the physically present Gollum; the sentence in the passage is a quiet meditation on the difference between tale-terror and real terror.

Thus the modern semantic range of ogres runs from the literal child-eating giant of the conte, through the figurative devourer of people, budgets, and dreams, to the catalogued monster of games and the rhetorical bogeyman of journalism — every sense shares the same historical core, an appetite that consumes the weak, and every sense ultimately descends from a Renaissance word for a dark god of the dead (see the etymology).
