The biography of cognizance is a biography of knowing itself — a single word that carries within it the whole Indo-European family of knowledge and the entire story of how private awareness became public record. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵneh₃-, “to know, to perceive,” one of the most productive roots in the entire family. Every major branch of Indo-European built its vocabulary of knowledge on it. In the Indo-Iranian branch it became the Sanskrit root jñā-, “to know”: the verb jānāti, “he knows,” survives in Hindi as jānnā and in Odia as jāṇibā, “to know,” while the noun jñāna (ज्ञान), “knowledge, wisdom,” lives on in Hindi gyān and Odia gyāna. In Greek the root produced gignṓskō, “to know,” and the noun gnôsis, “knowledge” — the source of English gnosis and gnostic — together with gnômon, “one who knows” (the pointer of a sundial), agnostic (from ágnōstos, “unknown”), and the medical compounds diagnosis and prognosis. In the Germanic branch the root gave Proto-Germanic *knēaną, yielding Old English cnāwan — our know — and cunnan, “to know how,” the source of the auxiliary can, with German kennen, Dutch kennen, Gothic kunnan, and Old Norse kenna, whose dialectal English descendant ken still means “range of knowledge.” In the Balto-Slavic branch it gave Old Church Slavonic znati and Russian znat’, “to know.” From Iceland to India, the family’s verbs of knowing all descend from that one root — a fact that makes cognizance a cousin of know, gnosis, jñāna, and znat’. The immediate parent of our word is Latin. The root entered Latin as gnōscō / nōscō, “to get to know, to become acquainted with,” the initial g- of the root being lost in the Latin development, and with the prefix co-, “together, with,” it formed cognōscere, “to get to know fully, to learn, to recognize.” This verb was one of the most fertile in the language. From it came cognitio, “knowledge,” and the whole cognitive family; recognōscere, “to recall, re-examine,” gave recognize and recognition; and the verb’s participle cognit- lies behind cognition. In Roman law, cognitio also became a technical term: the cognitio extraordinaria was the formal imperial judicial inquiry, the official examination of a case by a magistrate — already, in the classical world, the association between ‘knowing’ and ‘judicial procedure’ that would define the word’s later English career. From Latin the word passed into the Romance vernacular. Old French developed conoistre / conoistre, “to know,” from Latin cognōscere, and from the verb built the noun conoissance, “knowledge, acquaintance, recognition,” attested from the twelfth century. It was through Anglo-French that the word entered English: the French-speaking court and law courts of medieval England used conissance / conisaunce for knowledge and legal recognition, and from this channel Middle English took cognisaunce around 1325. The earliest English senses were the general ones — knowledge, awareness, recognition — but the legal context was never far away, because the word’s first home in England was the court, where to “take conisaunce of” a matter was to agree to consider it judicially. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, English legal usage had hardened the phrase into a term of art: a court took cognizance of a fact or a case, meaning it formally accepted jurisdiction or notice of it. The spelling history of the word is a miniature of Renaissance philology. The medieval and early modern spellings were French-derived — conisaunce, conysaunce — but from the sixteenth century onward, learned writers and printers began to ‘restore’ the Latin forms: cognizance, with the g restored from cognōscere and the z reflecting the medieval Latin pronunciation. The result is the modern double spelling: the Latinized cognizance, standard in American English, and the French-flavoured cognisance, standard in Britain. Both spellings pronounce the same and mean the same; they record in the very letters of the word the tug-of-war between the French and Latin strands of its history. Semantically, the word underwent a distinctive narrowing and institutionalization. In medieval and Renaissance English, cognizance was a general word for knowledge and awareness — “God’s cognizance,” “man’s cognizance” — but its most important career was in law and heraldry. In heraldry, a cognizance was a badge, crest, or device by which a knight or noble house was recognized: literally a visual way of being known, worn by retainers as a mark of identity and allegiance. In law, judicial cognizance became the principle that a court will accept certain facts as true without proof — the calendar, the laws of the land, notorious public events. Modern English preserves both strands: a court ‘takes judicial cognizance’ of a fact, and a herald’s office grants a cognizance as a mark of recognition. The word’s cultural weight has always been official and institutional — the vocabulary of the court, the lawbook, the commentator, and the record office. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, cognizance had settled into its modern range: formal knowledge or awareness, and more precisely the official notice taken by an authority. It appears characteristically in legal, scholarly, and administrative prose, and nearly always in the phrase “to take cognizance of.” This is the register of the editorial sentence quoted at the head of this card: “He noted that cognizance of these other excellences can only be taken in the future parts of his work.” The word here performs its most characteristic act — knowing withheld, promised, and deferred; recognition formally granted to the record of a later page. Its rarity in ordinary speech is part of its meaning: cognizance is not what everyone knows, but what someone officially acknowledges. The word remains the linguistic emblem of knowing raised from the private mind to the public record — the moment awareness becomes an act.
