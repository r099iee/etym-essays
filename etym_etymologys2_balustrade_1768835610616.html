NON-PIE ORIGIN — stated explicitly: balustrade descends from a Pre-Greek (Mediterranean/Aegean substrate) flower-name with no secure Indo-European etymology. Its -αυσ- diphthong and -στ- cluster mark it as a Pre-Greek word (Beekes); no PIE ancestor is forced. Pre-Greek substrate *balaustion "the crimson blossom of the wild pomegranate (Punica granatum)" (the shape of the word — the -αυσ- diphthong and the -στ- cluster — marks it as Pre-Greek, non-Indo-European; attested in the Greek botanical-pharmacological tradition and used as an astringent by Dioscorides) → Ancient Greek βαλαύστιον balaústion "the flower of the wild pomegranate; the plant" (5th–4th c. BCE onward; Dioscorides; an astringent and a red dye) → Latin balaustium "pomegranate blossom" (1st c. CE; Pliny and the Latin pharmacopoeia, an astringent medicine and a red dye) → Italian balaustro "the turned, vase-shaped pillar of a railing" (Renaissance, 15th–16th c.) — the semantic pivot: turners saw the half-open pomegranate flower — narrow at top and bottom, swelling in the middle like a vase — in the profile of their turned posts → Italian balaustrata "a row or colonnade of balusters; a railing of small pillars" (balaustro + -ata, "a set of"; 16th c.) → French balustrade (17th c.) — borrowed from Italian → English balustrade (c. 1640s–1650s) — alongside English baluster (1602) and its folk corruption banister (18th c.) → English balustrade (present day) FALSE-FRIEND WARNING: Greek βάλανος bálanos "acorn, gland" is NOT related to balaustion — only careless folk-etymology connects them.
