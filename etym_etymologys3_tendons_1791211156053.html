The plural tendons continues a learned medical lineage from PIE *ten- to stretch through Latin tendere to Medieval Latin tendo to French tendon to English. It is the clinical twin of native sinew, created when medieval anatomists needed a precise Latin noun for the stretching band.

PIE *ten- to stretch is among the most prolific roots in English: thin, tender, tent, tendon, tennis, tone, tonic, tenet, tend, extend, intend, tense, tension, hypoteneuse. The core image is pulling taut. Latin tendere (past participle tensus, tentus) meant to stretch, spread, aim, and strive — a tent is stretched canvas, attention is mind stretched toward. From this verb Late Latin physicians derived tendo, tendinis (accusative tendonem), literally that-which-stretches, to name the white cord. The coinage is first securely found in medieval surgical texts (Lanfranc, Guy de Chauliac) translating Arabic watar, itself translating Galen's Greek tenon — Greek tenon from teinein to stretch is the exact cognate parallel, showing Greco-Latin physicians reaching for the same stretch-metaphor independently.

Transmission stages:

  • Classical Latin absence: Cicero and Celsus use nervus for both nerve and sinew; no tendo as anatomy. The need for distinction grows with dissection.
  • Medieval Latin creation (11th–13th c.): Salernitan and Montpellier schools formalize tendo beside ligamentum, musculus, nervus. Accusative tendonem becomes the dictionary stem, explaining French -on.
  • Old French adoption: Anglo-Norman surgeons bring tendon to England; Middle English tendoun appears in 14th-century translations of Chauliac alongside native sinew.
  • Early Modern fixation (1540s): Thomas Elyot's Dictionary (1538) and later Thomas Vicary's Anatomy define tendon in English vernacular, fixing spelling and /ˈtɛndən/ pronunciation. Vesalius's Fabrica (1543) illustrations give tendons visual precision.
  • Modern plural regularity: tendons adds voiced /z/ plural; medical compounds proliferate — tendonitis, tendinopathy, tendon graft, Achilles tendon.

Phonologically, Latin /ˈtendoː/ → French /tɑ̃dɔ̃/ → denasalized English /ˈtɛndən/ shows learned borrowing without Great Vowel Shift distortion: spelling retains French -on but pronunciation reduces to schwa. The root vowel /ɛ/ preserves Latin e of tendere.

Socio-cultural context explains why English kept both sinew and tendon:

  • Division of labour: butchers, bowyers, and poets kept sinew; surgeons, physiotherapists, and athletes use tendon. One is craft-poetic, the other clinical.
  • Sport and overuse era: 19th-century Achilles-tendon ruptures in runners, 20th-century tendinitis in tennis elbows and typing wrists, 21st-century tendinopathy science — tendons plural dominates case reports because injuries are multiple and bilateral.
  • Indigenous technology description: ethnographers like the source author use tendons scientifically when describing tendon-threads drawn fine enough for needle eyes, where sinews would sound literary. The plural here stresses countless animal fibres processed into sewing thread.

Literary and scientific attestations run from Chauliac through Shakespeare (who still prefers sinew), to Alexander Pope's translations of Homer's tendons cut in battle, to modern orthopaedics. The journey from PIE stretch to operating-theatre suture thus spans four millennia, yet the image never changed: a tendon is what stretches and is stretched, bearing pull without breaking.
