• Field gate: Similar because both are barred farm gates between fields; differs because field gate is the plain generic (any farm gate), while hunting-gates are field gates as the hunt uses and jumps them — the sporting sense, not the farming one.
  • Five-barred gate: Similar because both name the classic barred oak gate of the shires; differs because five-barred gate describes the construction (five horizontal bars), while hunting-gate describes the use — either may be jumped by the field.
  • Kissing gate: Similar because both are pedestrian-and-horse gates of the rights-of-way network; differs because a kissing gate swings in a U-frame to admit walkers while penning stock, whereas hunting-gates are full field gates the mounted hunt rides or jumps.
  • Wicket gate: Similar because both are small gates set for passing through; differs because a wicket gate is a small door within or beside a larger gate for people on foot, while hunting-gates are the full gates themselves, built for horses.
  • Farm gate: Similar because both are working agricultural gates of timber and iron; differs because a farm gate serves stock and machinery year-round, while hunting-gates earn the name in the November-to-March season when the field comes through.
  • Stile: Similar because both punctuate Cobbett's foot-and-horse country where wheels cannot go; differs because a stile admits walkers over a fence while barring stock, whereas hunting-gates admit (or challenge) the mounted rider.
  • Gateway: Similar because both mark the passage from field to field; differs because gateway names the opening or entrance itself (with or without gates), while hunting-gates are the physical barred leaves hung in it.
  • Turnstile: Similar because both control passage at an entrance; differs because a turnstile meters one person at a time for admission, while hunting-gates are flung wide for hounds and horses or jumped outright.
  • Sluice gate: Similar because both are gates that hold back and release a flow; differs because a sluice gate meters water in mills and fens, while hunting-gates meter the living flow of hounds, horses, and riders.
