• Mesh: Similar because both describe an open-weave or interlaced material with visible spaces between strands; differs because mesh is a broad, utilitarian, and often rigid structural term applying to wire fencing, heavy plastic screens, or sports netting, whereas gauze emphasizes softness, delicate transparency, fine textile weaving, or surgical absorbency.
  • Scrim: Similar because both refer to lightweight, sheer, translucent fabrics; differs because scrim is a specialized theatrical or industrial term specifically denoting heavy, coarse-threaded cotton fabric engineered for lighting transformations on stage or reinforcement in construction, whereas gauze is finer, lighter, and universally used in medicine and fashion.
  • Tulle: Similar because both are sheer, transparent fabrics; differs because tulle is an ultra-fine, stiffened, machine-made net fabric with hexagonal mesh, predominantly used for bridal veils, ballet tutus, and ballgowns to add dramatic volume and stiffness, whereas gauze is a plain or leno-weave fabric that is softer and drapes naturally.
  • Chiffon: Similar because both are sheer, lightweight, transparent textiles; differs because chiffon is a tightly woven, fluid, and slippery silk or polyester fabric characterized by high twist crepe yarns that give it a subtle texture and elegant drape for luxury eveningwear, whereas gauze is open-weave, airy, and textured with visible grid apertures.
  • Gossamer: Similar because both describe an ethereal, nearly weightless, translucent substance; differs because gossamer originally refers to the actual floating silk webs spun by tiny autumn spiders, functioning in literature as the supreme poetic abstraction for delicacy, whereas gauze is a tangible, woven textile product.
  • Webbing: Similar because both are woven fabrics; differs because webbing is a very strong, thick, and closely woven strap fabric (such as seatbelts or military harness straps) engineered for high tensile strength, whereas gauze is thin, porous, and fragile.
  • Voile: Similar because both are semi-sheer cotton drapery fabrics; differs because voile is a crisp, smooth, plain-woven cloth with a higher thread count than gauze, offering soft translucency for curtains and blouses rather than the open, loose mesh of gauze.
