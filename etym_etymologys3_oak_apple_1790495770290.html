OAK: the tree-word (complete essay). Oak continues Old English ac (masculine, genitive aces), from Proto-Germanic *aiks (oak), itself from PIE *h₂eyǵ- (oak, mighty tree — Pokorny *aig-). Cognates prove the antiquity: Old Norse eik (oak, also ship and tree generally), Old High German eih, German Eiche, Dutch eik, Gothic *aiks, Greek aigilōps (a kind of oak, literally goat-eye), Latin aesculus (a sacred oak, by Italic sound-change), and with laryngeal colouring Lithuanian ąžuolas (oak). The semantic field never wavers: wherever Germanic speakers settled, *aiks meant the dominant mast-bearing hardwood, Quercus robur and Q. petraea, source of timber, bark, acorns, and galls. Charter-bounds from the 8th century onwards mark þa ealdan ac (the old oak) as a landmark; Domesday woods are measured by swine they feed; place-names — Acton (oak-farm), Ackworth, Acomb, Oakhanger, Sevenoaks — sow the map with oaks, each proving the word was everyday long before it was poetic.

Phonologically Old English ac /ɑːk/ passes through Middle English ook, oke to modern oak /əʊk/ by the Great Vowel Shift (Middle English /ɔː/ to modern /əʊ/). Spelling adds o-a to mark length; the k never voices. No borrowing intervenes; the word is inherited straight from PIE without French mediation, which is why it sounds blunt beside Latinate quercine. Literary attestations run unbroken: Beowulf builds halls of oak, Lazamon's Brut launches oak ships, Shakespeare's Arden is oak-shaded, Evelyn's Sylva (1664) pleads for oak-planting to save the navy, and Ruskin himself moralises oak as the type of patient strength in Modern Painters.

  • Sacred-timber career (prehistory to navy): Druids (whose name Pliny linked to Greek drus, oak), Thor's oaks felled by Boniface, the Major Oak of Sherwood, the Boscobel oak that hid Charles II in 1651, and the hearts of oak of the Royal Navy (some 2,860 oaks per 74-gun ship) all make oak mean England itself. To wear an oak-apple is to wear a chip of that timbership.
  • Mast, bark, and gall economy: acorns fed swine (pannage rights in Domesday), bark tanned leather (oak-bark houses in the Weald and Cumbria), timber framed houses, churches, and men-of-war, while galls including oak-apples supplied country ink, dye, and children's play. Enclosure, iron, and imported teak ended the economy but not the emblem.
  • Sound-law summary for oak: PIE *h₂eyǵ- with palatal *ǵ gives Germanic *k by Grimm's Law, plus diphthong *ai from *ey; Old English monophthongises *ai to *a before *k (ac); Middle English rounds to o; modern diphthongises to /əʊ/. Every step is regular and attested in dozens of parallels, and Surrey oaks still drop oak-apples each May as they did for Charles.

APPLE: the fruit-word (complete essay). Apple continues Old English æppel (masculine, also fruit generally), from Proto-Germanic *ap(a)laz (apple, fruit), itself of uncertain deeper origin — a north-European Wanderwort shared with Celtic and Slavic but without secure PIE pedigree. Cognates: Old Norse epli, Old High German apful, German Apfel, Dutch appel, Gothic *aplus, Old Irish ubull, Welsh afal (in Avalon, apple-isle), Lithuanian obuolỹs, Old Church Slavonic ablŭko. De Vaan and Beekes agree the group is old but mark it as probably a pre-Indo-European substrate fruit-name borrowed independently into Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic, which is why no single PIE root commands assent and the protocol forbids inventing one. The Germanic sense was originally any round fruit (hence earth-apple potato, oak-apple gall, pine-apple), narrowing only in modern times to Malus domestica.

Phonologically Old English æppel /æppel/ (with geminate pp and front vowel æ) passes through Middle English appel, apple (with retraction of æ to a) to modern apple /ˈæpəl/ (with reduction of final -el to syllabic /əl/ and simplification of pp to /p/). The double pp in spelling preserves the old geminate; the initial a never rounds. The compound oak-apple joins the two nouns with a hyphen (19th-century standard) without changing either sound: /ˈəʊkˌæpəl/ keeps both stresses, oak primary, apple secondary.

  • Fruit-general to fruit-specific (Old English to modern): Anglo-Saxon leechbooks call cucumbers eorþ-æppel (earth-apples) and pomegranates gran-æppel; Middle English calls the gall an ok-appel by the same logic (round tree-fruit that is not fruit). Only enclosure orchards and cider-tax fix apple to Malus. The gall keeps the older, wider sense fossilised.
  • Restoration fusion (1651-1660 and after): Charles hides in the Boscobel oak (an oak that bore oak-apples each spring); Parliament makes his birthday-oak perpetual; the gall becomes the wearable relic of the tree that saved the king. Gilding it, as Ruskin's child did, turns entomology into sacrament — insect-nursery into crown-jewel. No other gall (marble, knopper, spangle) took this honour, because none looked so apple-like and none ripened in May for the 29th.
  • Sound-law summary for apple: PGmc *aplaz with *a plus geminate *pp (by Kluge's Law from earlier *n: *abn- to *app-) gives OE æppel by fronting of *a to æ before geminate; ME retracts to a; modern reduces final vowel. The deeper pre-Germanic *abel- (substrate) is marked as debated, not PIE, in all modern handbooks.

Together oak-apple is first recorded in Middle English as okappel, oke-appel (14th-century herbals) for any round oak-gall, calquing the folk logic fruit-of-the-oak. Early modern herbals (Gerard, 1597) figure it beside true galls; the Restoration (1660) lifts one spring gall into a badge; Victorian boys gild it; modern cynipidology (Mayr, Askew, Stone) dissects it into agamic and sexual generations on different oak organs. The hyphen, the gilding, and the date — 29 May — are the whole history in miniature: tree plus fruit plus king.
