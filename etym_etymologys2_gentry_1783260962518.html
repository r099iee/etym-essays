Stage 1 — PIE *Ƿenh₁- “to beget, give birth to, produce” (one of the best-attested roots of Indo-European, spread over every major branch: Sanskrit jánati “begets”, Greek gígnomai “to become, be born”, Latin gignere “to beget”) — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE. Stage 2 — Latin gignere “to beget, give birth to” → gens, gentis f. “clan, family, lineage; people, race, nation” — the Roman gens, the extended family sharing one name (gens Iūlia, gens Cornēlia; attested in classical Latin, 1st c. BCE). Stage 3 — Latin gentilis “of the same gens, belonging to the same family; of good family, noble; (Church Latin) of the nations, heathen” (the two senses — “of the family” and “of the nations” — diverge at this stage). Stage 4 — Old French gentil “noble, well-born, courteous” (from Latin gentilis; 10th–11th c.), the source of English gentle, gentleman, genteel. Stage 5 — Old French genterie / genterise “gentle birth, nobility, the rank of a gentleman” (gentil + the abstract suffix -erie/-ise, from Latin -āria; 12th–13th c.). Stage 6 — Anglo-French / Middle English gentrie, genterie “gentle birth, the class of gentle-born people” (14th c.). Stage 7 — Modern English GENTRY “the untitled landed class below the nobility” (16th c. onward; also the modern verb gentrify and noun gentrification, 20th c.). NOTE: the twin sense of Latin gentilis “of the nations” gave, through the Vulgate's gentes “the nations”, English Gentile “the non-Jew” — a different English offshoot of the same Latin stage.
