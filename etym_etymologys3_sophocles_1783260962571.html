The name Sophocles is a Greek compound, Σοφοκλῆς (Sophoklēs), literally "famous for wisdom," joining σοφός (sophos), "wise, skilled," with κλέος (kléos), "fame, glory." Each element possesses its own Indo-European biography, and the two must be traced separately before they fuse in the name of the greatest of the tragedians. Element one: the wisdom (PIE *swop-, "to taste, perceive"):
  • The first element, σοφός, is traditionally traced to the PIE root *swop-, "to taste, perceive," the same root that produced the Latin verb sapiō, "to taste; to know." The semantic arc from the physical sense "to taste, to have flavour" to the intellectual sense "to know, to be discerning" is a classic metaphor of sense-perception, preserved throughout the Italic family: Latin sapiens "wise" (as in Homo sapiens, "knowing man"), sapientia "wisdom," sapor "taste" (English savour), and the Romance verbs for "to know" — Italian sapere and French savoir.
  • In Greek itself the adjective generated the entire family of wisdom-words: σοφία (sophia), "wisdom, skill," lies at the heart of φιλοσοφία (philosophia), "love of wisdom," whence English philosophy, and σοφιστής (sophistēs), "expert," drifted in the fifth century BC from the neutral "skilled man" to the hostile "sophist, quibbler," thanks to Plato's polemics.
  • It must be added honestly that Beekes treats sophos as lacking a fully secure Indo-European etymology and admits the possibility of a Pre-Greek element; the *swop- connection with Latin sapiō nonetheless remains the standard scholarly account, and the "taste-knowledge" metaphor it encodes is richly confirmed by the parallel developments in Latin.
Element two: the glory (PIE *ḱlew-, "to hear"):
  • The second element, κλέος, rests on the thoroughly secure PIE root *ḱlew-, "to hear." Fame was conceived, etymologically, as what is heard — the report of a deed that travels on men's lips — and the root is among the widest-spread in the family. In Greek it gave κλέος itself, the "glory" that Homer's heroes fight for, and κλυτός (klytos), "famous, renowned"; in Latin clueō, "am said, am famous"; in Sanskrit śrávah, "glory, fame," and śru-, "to hear" (as in the sacred śruti, "that which is heard"); and in Germanic the same root underlies English loud, and Old English hlūd, "loud, famous."
  • Homer's phrase κλέος ἄφθιτον (kléos áphthiton), "imperishable fame," defines the heroic ideal: the glory that survives because it is heard and retold. It is exactly this sense of fame as report that the second element of Sophoklēs carries — the wisdom that is noised abroad, renowned among men.
The fusion and the name:
  • Sophoklēs, "glory of wisdom" or "famous for wisdom," is a transparent compound personal name of the classical Greek type, formed exactly like Dēmosthénēs, "strength of the people." It was a speaking name of the kind aristocratic families chose to proclaim the family's virtues, and its bearer, the son of Sophilus of Colonus, made the name prophetically true: the dramatist's wisdom, in the Greek sense of artistic skill and insight, won him the imperishable kléos of the great tragedian.
  • The name passed into Latin unchanged as Sophoclēs, and from Latin into English as Sophocles, a learned proper noun with no subsequent sound shift. English preserves the Greek accentuation Soph-o-kleez, softened through the Latin school tradition.
Sound, culture, and literary afterlife:
  • Sophocles' innovations — adding the third actor, reducing the chorus to a commenting presence, and focusing the drama on the individual hero and his catastrophe — made him the most successful playwright of the Athenian theatre: some twenty-four festival victories and never lower than second. The ancient Lives call him the "bee of Attica" for the sweetness of his diction, and the grammarian critics of the Byzantine world kept his seven plays as school-texts when hundreds of others perished.
  • Aristotle made Oedipus Rex the model of tragedy in the Poetics, and from that judgement the name became the measure of tragic structure: hamartia (the tragic error), hubris, anagnorisis (recognition), and the terrible peripeteia that the audience foresees — the essence of dramatic irony, of which Sophocles is the acknowledged master.
  • Freud's naming of the "Oedipus complex" after Sophocles' hero carried the dramatist's name into twentieth-century everyday language, and the adjective Sophoclean now denotes a fate that is at once inescapable and self-inflicted, the fate that a man brings on himself through his own excellence and blindness.
  • Macaulay's "the masterpieces of Sophocles, of Demosthenes, and of Plato" places the tragedian at the head of the Greek literary canon, the summit of poetry set beside the summits of oratory and philosophy — a canonising gesture that stretches from the Byzantine grammarians, through the Renaissance recovery of the seven plays, to the modern theatre, where Sophocles remains the most staged of the ancient dramatists.
Socio-cultural factors: The word's history condenses the entire rise of Attic drama: a "speaking name" born in the aristocratic world of the late sixth century BC, borne by the dramatist who shaped tragedy from choral ritual into the art of the individual hero, canonised by Aristotle, preserved by the Byzantine school, revived in the Renaissance, and naturalised in the modern psychological vocabulary. From the root of "hearing" comes the fame that is heard; from the root of "tasting" comes the wisdom that knows. The compound of the two — "fame of wisdom" — is, fittingly, a name that means exactly what its bearer's art achieved: the imperishable, widely-heard glory of supreme skill.
