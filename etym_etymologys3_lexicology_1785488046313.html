The story of lexicology begins not with books but with the physical gesture of gathering. The Proto-Indo-European root *leǵ- meant “to pick up, collect”, a word born of the farmer’s hand sweeping grain or fruit into a heap. In Greek this root flowered into λέγω (légō), which moved from “I gather” to “I count, reckon”, and then to “I say, speak” — for speaking was understood as picking out and stringing together words. From légō came λέξις (léxis), “a word, a phrase, a way of speaking”, and from léxis the adjective λεξικός (lexikós), “of or belonging to words”. Its neuter form λεξικόν (lexikón) hardened into a noun, “a word-book”, the direct ancestor of English lexicon — a book, that is, in which words are gathered together.

Meanwhile the same root produced λόγος (lógos), “word, speech, account”, whose combining form -λογία (-logía) means “the systematic study or discourse of something” — the -logy of biology, geology and theology. In learned Greek the two strands were braided into λεξικολογία (lexikología), literally “discourse about words”. The word entered European learning through the Latin school tradition as the rare, learned lexicologia, and appears in English in the late seventeenth century: the Oxford English Dictionary records lexicology from the 1690s, when it meant simply “the knowledge of words”. For nearly two centuries it lay quiet in dictionaries as a rare synonym of lexicography, a curiosity of the learned vocabulary.

The twentieth century transformed it. The rise of structural and descriptive linguistics — Saussure’s conception of language as a system, and the growing professionalism of the language sciences — gave the word a precise modern definition: the branch of linguistics that studies a language’s vocabulary as a system, its layers, its structure, and its change. Where lexicography kept the craft of making dictionaries, lexicology claimed the science of the word-stock itself. The word took its place beside phonology and grammar in the catalogue of the discipline, exactly as in our sentence, where the English Language Series lists “its lexicology” among the topics of the field. In the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries the computational turn extended the word once more: computational lexicology now models the lexicon in lexical databases, wordnet ontologies and semantic embeddings, the paper word-book of lexikón reborn as a knowledge graph.

The same PIE root ran independently through Latin legō, “I gather, choose, read”, producing lēx “law” — in origin, what is gathered and laid down — and lectiō “a reading”, ancestor of lecture and lesson, and collect. Lexicology thus carries, inside a word about dictionaries, a memory of the harvesting hand: the root that meant gathering grain in the fields now gathers the words of a language into a system.

  • Sound changes: PIE palatal *ǵ → Greek g before front vowels (légein); the -ikós adjective suffix and neuter -ón hardening to a noun (lexikón); Greek lexikología → Latin lexicologia → English lexicology.
  • Semantic drift: “gather” → “count” → “say, speak” → “word, speech” → “word-book” → “discourse about words” → “the knowledge of words” → “the systematic study of a language’s vocabulary”.
  • Literary and institutional attestations: the 1690s OED record; the rise of structural linguistics in the twentieth century; the computational modelling of the lexicon in modern NLP.
