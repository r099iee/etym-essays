The splitting verb cleave descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *gleubh-, "to tear apart, carve, hollow out," a root of cutting and separation whose other children are telling: in Greek it produced gluphein "to carve," and from the noun gluphe "carving" English draws glyph and, with hieros "sacred," hieroglyph — the sacred carving of Egypt; Latin probably preserves it in glubo "to peel, strip the bark." In the Germanic branch the root hardened into the strong verb *kleubanan, and Old English inherited it as cleofan, a class II strong verb meaning "to split, separate by a sharp blow," with past cleaf and past participle clofen.

Phonological development.

  • PIE *gleubh- to Proto-Germanic *kleubanan: under Grimm's Law the voiced stop g- and the aspirated medial *bh- shift; the voiced *g- gives Germanic *k-, and *bh- yields *b-; the diphthong *eu becomes the Germanic *eu of the class II strong verb.
  • Proto-Germanic *kleubanan to Old English cleofan: the class II strong verb is inherited intact, with the characteristic ablaut series cleofan – cleaf – clofen.
  • Old English cleofan to Middle English cleven: unrounding and levelling of the vowel; the strong past forms compete with new weak forms as the verb begins to drift.
  • Middle English cleven to Modern English cleave: the modern spelling is fixed; the verb retains a mixture of strong and weak forms (cleaved, cleft, clave, cloven), the residue of its long transition.

Grammatical fracture and the story of the forms. That single Old English verb has fractured, over a thousand years, into scattered modern forms: the northern past clave, common until c. 1600 and preserved in the King James Bible ("the rocks clave," "it clave"); the weak cleaved, from the 14th century; the later cleft; and the participle cloven, surviving chiefly in the cloven hoof. The noun cleft is an alteration of Old English clift "a fissure," and the pattern lives on in cleft lip and cleft palate. The cleaver, originally one who split boards with a wedge rather than a saw, became by the mid-15th century the butcher's long-bladed chopper. And cleavage, in geology and mineralogy from 1805, described a stone's tendency to break cleanly along natural planes before taking, in the mid-20th century, its modern sense of the shadowed cleft in a low neckline — a striking leap from a Doric quarryman's term to a fashion journalist's.

The collision with the other cleave. Meanwhile English has run into our verb a look-alike intruder: the other cleave, "to adhere, cling," from Old English clifian and PIE *gloi- "to stick" (the root of clay and glue), had begun to converge on the same sound by Middle English, when the splitting verb weakened. The confusion grew until both words were largely driven out of ordinary speech by stick, split, and cut, and the pair became a standing puzzle — celebrated by writers from Ben Jonson to Fowler, who warned of the "two cleaves." Dictionaries treat them as distinct homonyms; the splitting cleave is *gleubh-, the clinging cleave is *gloi-.

Register, semantics, and the Tolkienian use. The split-sense of cleave retreated into elevated, archaic registers — the Bible, epic, verse — and became the verb of ceremonial or heroic division rather than of daily carpentry. It is precisely this archaic, saga-like force that Tolkien exploits in the dwarves' song over Orcrist, the sword recovered from the trolls' hoard, when the blade is wished to "cleave goblins once again." The verb's Old English pedigree, its strong-verb history, and its descent from a root of carving and hollowing all combine in a single word that sounds, as it should, like the stroke of a blade: the diction of saga and old song rather than of modern report, a grim promise of vengeance carried over from the goblin wars.
