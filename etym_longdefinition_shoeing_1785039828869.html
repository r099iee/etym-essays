SHOEING is the verbal noun and present participle of the verb TO SHOE: the act or process of fitting a protective covering — a shoe — to a foot. In ordinary human use, shoeing is the putting on of footwear; in its most famous and specialized sense it is the craft of the farrier, fitting shaped iron (or modern aluminium and steel) horseshoes to the hooves of horses, ponies, mules, donkeys and other beasts of burden, nailing the shoe to the horn of the hoof to protect it from wear, bruising and hard terrain. The verb is transitive: you shoe a horse (or a pony, an ox, a mule, even a dog in modern veterinary practice), you shoe a child; shoeing names the whole action — measuring, trimming the hoof, shaping and fitting the shoe, nailing it, clinching the nails. It is a word of work, craft and care: shoeing is what a mount needs before a long journey, and the smith or farrier who does it is one of the most important craftsmen in any mounted society. In The Hobbit the word appears in the welcome-song sung by the Elves of Rivendell in Chapter 3, when the company first reaches the Last Homely House: "Your ponies need shoeing!" The line is part of the Elves' teasing, lilting verse — they sing that the ponies need shoeing, the elves need refreshing, the dwarves need feeding — and it is a perfect blend of mockery and care. On the surface it gently mocks the travel-worn dwarves, whose ponies have just come down the stony paths of the Misty Mountains and are footsore; but the practical, homely word also shows the Elves' watchful hospitality: they have noticed the state of the beasts, they offer rest and repair, and the promise of shoeing is part of the comfort and renewal of the whole company. The word is deliberately double-voiced — homely, practical, iron and nails, set inside a graceful, sing-song welcome — and it grounds the enchanted valley in real, earthy care. Polysemy & secondary senses:
  • Equine and animal care: the farriery craft — fitting horseshoes; also shoeing of oxen (with two-part ox shoes), mules, ponies, and modern dog shoeing; 'to shoe' is the verb, 'shoeing' the action, 'shod' the completed state ('well-shod'), 'unshod' the bare-hoof state.
  • Human footwear: the plain sense — putting shoes on a person, especially a child; archaic and biblical 'to shoe' means to furnish with shoes; 'shoeing-horn' (now 'shoehorn') is the curved implement for easing a foot into a shoe.
  • Engineering and industry: 'shoeing' and 'shoe' extend to any protective fitting or socket — brake shoes (the curved friction blocks in brakes), the shoe of a pile or of a ladder (its metal tip), the shoe of a cable or a pipe (its coupling end), the shoe of a plough or sledge runner; to 'shoe' a ladder or a pile is to fit such a metal tip; 'shoeing' in this technical sense is routine workshop English.
  • Electrical and structural: a 'shoe' is the sliding contact that carries current (as in an electrical shoe on a rail), and 'shoeing' such contacts is part of railway and tramway engineering.
  • Figurative: 'to shoe' is to equip, to arm, to provide with what is needed; in idioms, 'the shoe is on the other foot' reverses a situation, 'to step into someone's shoes' is to succeed them, and 'shoeing' appears in proverbial phrases about the care of the necessary (e.g. 'a good smith can shoe a goose,' a proverb for a very skilled or absurdly taxed craftsman).
Set against related words: farriery is the name of the whole craft of equine hoof care (trimming, shoeing, treating); horseshoeing names the animal explicitly; shoe-making (or shoemaking) is the manufacture of footwear, not the fitting of metal shoes; 'shod' marks the completed condition; unshoeing and reshoeing mark the reversal and repetition of the act.
