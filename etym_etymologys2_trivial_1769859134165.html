COMPONENT 1 — the numeral TRI- "three":
Proto-Indo-European *trei- "three" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE)
→ Latin trēs "three" (attested from the 6th–5th c. BCE) with its combining form tri- "three" (as in trivium, triplex, tribus)

COMPONENT 2 — the noun VIA "road":
Proto-Indo-European *wegh- "to convey, carry, travel, go" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE)
→ Latin via "way, road, path, street" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; from the feminine of an old adjective meaning "being conveyed," i.e. "the travelled way"; cognate with English way and wagon)

JOINT COMPOUND AND DESCENT:
Latin trivium "a place where three roads meet, a public crossroad" (tri- + via; attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Horace)
→ Latin triviālis "belonging to or at the crossroads; common, commonplace, vulgar, of the street" (attested 1st c. CE, e.g. in Quintilian)
→ Middle French trivial "commonplace, ordinary" (15th c.)
→ English trivial "common, of little importance" (first attested c. 1589)
→ Modern English trivial "of little value or importance" (present-day).

Note on the debate: The word is a genuine Latin compound of tri- "three" and via "road," so both root chains are required. The medieval academic trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic — the elementary third of the seven liberal arts) is a separate Latin word of different meaning, but scholarly and popular usage long confused the two trivia, and this confusion contributed to the modern sense "of little importance." The adjective triviālis itself derives from the "crossroads" noun, not from the curriculum word.
