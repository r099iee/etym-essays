• Cotton: Similar because calico is a type of cotton fabric; differs because cotton is the raw fiber, while calico is a specific weave and finish of that fiber.
  • Muslin: Similar because it's also a plain-woven cotton fabric, often unbleached; differs because muslin is typically finer and more loosely woven than traditional calico, often used for draping or delicate garments.
  • Chintz: Similar because it's a printed cotton fabric; differs because chintz is typically glazed to give it a polished, slightly stiff finish, whereas calico, even when printed, usually retains a softer, unglazed texture.
  • Gingham: Similar because it's a cotton fabric often used for clothing and household items; differs because gingham is characterized by its distinctive checked or plaid pattern woven into the fabric, not printed, and is usually yarn-dyed.
  • Toile: Similar because it's a printed fabric, often cotton, featuring intricate designs; differs because toile typically features monochromatic pastoral or historical scenes, while printed calico can have a wider variety of patterns and colors.
  • Mottled: Similar because it describes a patchy, multi-colored pattern, especially in the context of animal coats; differs because mottled is an adjective describing a pattern, while calico is a noun referring to the fabric or the specific tri-color pattern in animals.
  • Tortoiseshell: Similar because it describes a multi-colored pattern in cats, often confused with calico; differs because tortoiseshell cats typically have a mix of black, orange, and sometimes cream, but lack the distinct white patches that define a calico cat.
