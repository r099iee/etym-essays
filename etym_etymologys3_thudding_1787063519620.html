The historical evolution of thudding offers a compelling journey through Germanic historical phonology, phonaesthetic sound symbolism, and dialectal diffusion from medieval Northern English and Scots into the mainstream English literary lexicon.

The deep etymological ancestry of the root traces back to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *(s)tewd- or *tewd-, signifying "to strike, beat, push, or thrust." This ancient root generated cognate verbs across several Indo-European branches: in Classical Latin it produced tundere ("to beat, strike, pound," which through its supine stem tūs- gave English loanwords like contusion, obtuse, and pierce via Old French); in Sanskrit it appeared as tudáti ("he thrusts, pricks, strikes"); and in Ancient Greek it left traces in forms related to striking and pushing.

Within the Proto-Germanic dialect continuum (c. 500 BCE), this root developed into the weak verbal formation *þuddijaną ("to push, thrust, strike violently"). In the West Germanic branch that settled in Britain, this gave rise to the Old English verb þyddan (also attested as þuddan in certain dialectal glosses and compounds), which signified "to push, thrust, stab, or press with force." In Anglo-Saxon prose, the verb was primarily tactile and kinetic rather than acoustic, designating the physical application of blunt force, martial stabbing with spears, or heavy physical shoving.

During the Middle English period, following the disruptions of the Norman Conquest and the divergence of regional dialects, the verb receded from southern London court English but flourished vigorously in the Northern Middle English and Early Scots dialect zones. In 14th-century Scots literature, notably in John Barbour's national epic The Brus (c. 1375), the noun thud emerged to designate a sudden, howling blast of gale-force wind or the violent kinetic crash of a weapon against armor:

  • "With a thud" in Middle Scots captured both the aerodynamic force of a tempest crashing into a structure and the visceral sensation of a shockwave.
  • In Gavin Douglas's seminal 1513 Scots translation of Virgil's Aeneid (the Eneados), thud and thudding were deployed with masterful poetic frequency to describe the roaring blast of Boreas, the crash of falling timber, and the muffled doom of war engines hammering stone battlements.

Throughout the 16th and 17th centuries, the acoustic phonaesthetics of the word crystallized. The initial voiceless dental fricative /θ/ combined with the short central vowel /ʌ/ and the heavy voiced alveolar stop /d/ to create a sound-symbolic echo of a cushioned, low-frequency impact. The Scottish vernacular revival of the 18th century—led by figures such as Allan Ramsay, Robert Fergusson, and especially Robert Burns—introduced these expressive Scots words to a broader British and international audience. Burns frequently used thud to capture the visceral rhythm of rural labor, winter gales, and the pounding of the human heart in love and battle.

By the early 19th century, Sir Walter Scott incorporated thud and its participial form thudding into his widely read Waverley Novels, thereby formally naturalizing the term into standard literary Modern English. Victorian novelists including Charles Dickens and Thomas Hardy quickly embraced thudding for its evocative sensory richness, contrasting its deadened, heavy impact against the sharp, metallic sounds of the Industrial Revolution. In modern 20th-century fantasy and fiction, as seen in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, thudding serves as a quintessential sensory descriptor for unyielding mass, rhythmic gravity, and subterranean movement.
