PIE *kel- / *kele- 'to call, summon, shout, cry out' (c. 4000-3000 BCE) -> Proto-Italic *kalao 'to call out, proclaim' -> Latin calare 'to call out, proclaim solemnly, announce' (attested in classical Latin; from it calendae 'the calends', the day on which the priests announced the new moon) -> Latin concilium 'a calling together, an assembly, a council' (com- 'together' + calare; classical, 1st c. BCE) -> Latin conciliare 'to bring together, unite, win over, reconcile, make friendly' (verb from concilium; classical) -> Latin reconciliare 'to bring back together, restore, reconcile, win back' (re- 'again' + conciliare; attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero) -> Late Latin reconciliabilis 'able to be reconciled' (adjective in -abilis) and, negated with in-, Late Latin / medieval Latin irreconciliabilis 'not able to be reconciled' (attested chiefly in ecclesiastical and theological contexts) -> Middle French irreconciliable 'that cannot be reconciled' (attested 15th c.) -> English IRRECONCILABLE 'incapable of being brought into harmony or agreement' (attested c. 1590s-1600s, first in theological and political polemic). Note on the chain: the simple adjective 'reconcilable' (from Late Latin reconciliabilis) entered English somewhat earlier (16th c.), and the negative form was evidently coined in French and Latin independently of each other; the English spelling with -able follows French -able rather than Latin -abilis. There is no serious scholarly debate about the overall derivation; the only nuance is whether concilium was formed directly from calare 'to call' or through a compound *kom-kal-yo-m, but both analyses rest on the same PIE root *kel-.
