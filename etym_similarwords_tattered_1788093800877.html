• Ragged: Similar because both describe torn, uneven cloth or appearance; differs because ragged emphasises irregular tearing and uncouthness, while tattered emphasises being reduced to hanging scraps.
  • Torn: Similar because both describe something rended apart; differs because torn is neutral and generic for any tear, while tattered implies many tears, wear and ruin.
  • Shredded: Similar because both describe cloth cut or torn into strips; differs because shredded suggests deliberate fine cutting (a paper shredder), whereas tattered is the accidental decay of wear or battle.
  • Frayed: Similar because both describe worn textile; differs because frayed is the first stage of damage at the edges, while tattered is advanced disintegration into hanging pieces.
  • Threadbare: Similar because both describe worn cloth; differs because threadbare means worn smooth so that the weave shows — the result of long use but not necessarily of tearing, while tattered is torn and full of holes.
  • Worn: Similar because both describe damage from use; differs in being the mildest and most general term for use-marks, with no image of shredding.
  • Worn-out: Similar because both describe exhaustion by use; differs because worn-out implies finished utility (something useless), while tattered things may still be used — as Bilbo's tattered bundle still serves as a pillow.
  • Moth-eaten: Similar because both describe cloth full of holes; differs because moth-eaten specifies insect damage in wool and carries a comical, down-at-heel nuance.
  • Holey: Similar because both mean full of holes; differs as the informal, childish word, with no image of hanging shreds.
  • Shabby: Similar because both describe things in bad condition; differs because shabby stresses neglected, dowdy appearance rather than actual tearing.
  • Scruffy: Similar because both describe an untidy, ragged look; differs in applying to overall disorder of persons and surroundings rather than to the state of the cloth itself.
  • Dishevelled: Similar because both describe an untidy state, especially of dress or hair; differs because dishevelled applies to the whole person's appearance, not to damage of fabric.
  • Dilapidated: Similar because both mean falling apart; differs in applying to buildings and institutions, not textiles, and in its formal register.
  • In tatters / in shreds: Similar because the phrases use the same image of total ruin; differs in being idiomatic prepositional phrases stressing complete destruction rather than a describing adjective.
  • Frazzled: Similar for the figurative sense (frazzled nerves and tattered nerves); differs in register — frazzled is colloquial and about exhaustion, not physical shredding.
  • Tatterdemalion: Similar because it shares the same root and describes the ragged person himself; differs as a rare, mock-heroic noun or adjective ("a tatterdemalion beggar") rather than a plain adjective of cloth.
