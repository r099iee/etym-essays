- c. 1300 – shrillen: to sound with a high, piercing note — of voices, birds, instruments; purely acoustic and neutral.
- late 14th c. – the adjective shril(le) 'high-pitched, piercing' fixes the sound-quality; the trumpet collocation is born in epic and romance.
- 16th–17th c. – poets apply it to winds, whistles and war-horns; shrill is a heroic-epic sound, not yet an insult.
- 18th–19th c. – natural history adopts it for bird calls; in fiction 'a shrill voice' is coded as lower-class, female or uncontrolled — the word begins to judge.
- 20th c. – the figurative leap: shrill rhetoric, shrill criticism — strident, alarmist, excessive; the sound becomes a verdict on speech.
- 21st c. – 'shrill' is a live sociolinguistic battleground (often applied, controversially, to women's voices in public debate), while 'shrilling' keeps its literal life in literary prose — as in Tolkien's trumpets.
