Slavic is a word that began as somebody else's name for a group of peoples and ended as a scientific classification, picking up one of the heaviest moral associations in the English lexicon along the way. Its history divides into four long phases: the Byzantine ethnography of the sixth century, the medieval Latin entanglement with slavery, the early modern learned borrowing, and the nineteenth-century transformation into a family name for a branch of Indo-European.

First appearances: the Danube frontier. Byzantine historians of the sixth century — Procopius, Jordanes, Pseudo-Maurice — describe peoples north of the Danube called Sklabēnoi (Σκλαβηνοί) and Antai. The Greek form is generally taken to reflect a Slavic self-designation, Proto-Slavic *slověninŭ, plural *slověne, built on *slovo, "word, speech". On the most widely accepted account the name means "people of the word", i.e. those who speak a language one can understand — a common pattern in which a speech community names itself "the speakers" and calls its neighbours "mute" (compare the Slavic word for Germans, *němũci, from *němŭ, "mute").

The sound shape. The Greek Sklabēnoi shows the typical Byzantine rendering of Slavic sl- with an intrusive k, and the later Latin Sclavus follows the Greek consonant cluster. From *slovo to slověninŭ to Sklabos to Sclavus to Slavus the word loses its nasal vowels and its suffixes, and the modern English adjective is a nineteenth-century formation on the stem Slav- with the Latinate suffix -ic.

  • *ḱlew- "to hear" is the PIE root; it also gives Sanskrit śávas "fame" and Greek kléos "glory", the root of Kleopatra and of cluere in Latin.
  • *ḱléwos "hearing, fame" is the s-stem noun behind Slavic slovo and slava; the semantic drift from "what is heard about someone" to "word" is plausible but not universally accepted.
  • *slovo is the ordinary Proto-Slavic word for "word"; its descendants include Russian slovo, Polish słowo, Czech slovo and Bulgarian slovo.
  • *slověninŭ is the derived ethnonym, literally "word-person"; the same suffix builds *zemjaninŭ "earth-person, human".

The slavery entanglement. In medieval Latin, Sclavus (also Slavus) meant "Slav", but from at least the ninth century it also meant "slave", because Slavic captives from the Balkan and Danubian frontier were traded in large numbers through Venice, Prague and the Byzantine ports. Old French took the word as esclave, and Middle English as sclave, giving modern slave. The mechanism is well documented in the documentary record of the slave trade, although some historians argue that Greek Sklabos already carried the sense "captive" before it reached Latin. Either way, the ethnic name and the legal status became fused, and the fusion has never been undone; modern English therefore distinguishes carefully between "Slav" and "slave" even though they are doublets.

Early modern English. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries borrowed Sclavonian, Slavonian and Slavonic, chiefly in theological and geographical writing about the Orthodox East. The Authorised Version's preface and the antiquarian literature of the period use "Sclavonic" for the liturgical language and "Sclavonians" for the peoples. The form without the c, "Slavonic", gradually prevailed in the nineteenth century as philologists cleaned up the spelling; "Slavic" is the American-preferred and now dominant international form.

Nineteenth-century transformation. The Romantic national revivals gave the Slavs a heroic prehistory: Palacký in Bohemia, Mickiewicz in Poland, Karadžić in Serbia, Šafárik and Dobrovský in philology. Pan-Slavism, crystallised at the Prague Slavic Congress of 1848, made "Slavic" a political banner. At the same time comparative linguistics placed Slavic firmly among the satem branches of Indo-European, and the adjective became a technical term in the new university departments of Slavic philology. Racial anthropology then added a pseudo-biological layer — "the Slavic race", "Slavic physiognomy" — which is now thoroughly discredited but which explains why sensitive writers avoid the phrase.

Contemporary usage and literary record. In current English "Slavic" is neutral and academic: it names a language family, a cultural area and a field of study. The literary record runs from the Primary Chronicle and the Life of Methodius through the baroque scholarship of the Slavonic-Greek-Latin Academy to Pushkin, Mickiewicz, Mácha, Dostoevsky, Čapek and Andrić. Its modern coinages — Slavistics, Slavist, Slavonic studies — are the ordinary vocabulary of universities. The word has travelled from a self-description meaning "we who speak" to a global classificatory label, and it still carries the double memory of a common linguistic inheritance and a history of enslavement.
