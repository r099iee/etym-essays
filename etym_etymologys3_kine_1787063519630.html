The morphological and etymological evolution of kine offers one of the most illuminating case studies in Germanic historical linguistics, demonstrating the interplay of Indo-European root inheritance, Proto-Germanic consonant shifts, Old English vowel mutation (i-umlaut), and Middle English morphological restructuring.

The lineage begins with the ancestral Proto-Indo-European root *gʷōws, meaning "cow, bull, ox, or cattle." This fundamental pastoral noun was universally inherited across all major Indo-European branches: in Sanskrit it yielded gáus (whence Hindi gāy), in Ancient Greek it produced boûs (the source of English bovine and buccal via Latin loans), in Classical Latin it became bōs (genitive bovis), in Old Irish it gave bó, and in Proto-Slavic it yielded *govędo ("cattle").

As Proto-Indo-European developed into Proto-Germanic during the 1st millennium BCE, the word underwent regular sound changes governed by Grimm's Law. Specifically, the labiovelar voiced stop *gʷ shifted into the voiceless velar stop *k (with loss of the labial glide before back vowels), giving rise to Proto-Germanic *kūz (nominative singular) with an irregular root-noun plural reconstructed as *kwīz or *kōiz. This root is the common ancestor of Old Norse k实施/kȳr, Old High German chuo (Modern German Kuh), and Old Saxon kō.

In Anglo-Saxon England, Old English inherited the feminine athematic root noun cū (pronounced /kuː/). Because root nouns formed their plural by suffixing an inflectional syllable with an ancient front vowel (such as *-iz), the high back vowel /uː/ in the root underwent prehistoric English i-mutation (i-umlaut). The tongue anticipated the high front vowel of the ending, raising and fronting the /uː/ to the close front rounded vowel /yː/ (written as ȳ), while the inflectional ending was subsequently lost through apocope. Thus, throughout the Old English period (c. 700–1100 CE), the paradigm remained:

  • Singular: cū (/kuː/, "cow")
  • Plural: cȳ (/kyː/, "cows")

This vowel alternation was entirely regular and matched other Old English athematic pairs such as mūs / mȳs ("mouse / mice"), lūs / lȳs ("louse / lice"), fōt / fēt ("foot / feet"), and tōþ / tēþ ("tooth / teeth").

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Middle English underwent massive phonological and morphological leveling. The front rounded vowel /yː/ unrounded to /iː/ (spelled cy, ci, or ky). Simultaneously, the weak noun declension—which formed plurals with the suffix -en (from Old English -an, as preserved in modern oxen)—became extremely productive across southern and midland dialects of Middle English. Speakers perceived the monosyllabic mutated plural cy as irregular or insufficiently marked, and they analogically attached the weak plural suffix -en to the already-mutated plural stem. This created the classic 'double plural' forms cy-en, kyen, and kyn (later spelled kine with silent final -e).

During the 14th and 15th centuries, northern dialects of Middle English championed the generalized strong masculine plural ending -es / -s (from Old English -as), creating the regularized form cows. By the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras (c. 1580–1620), cows became the dominant colloquial plural in everyday speech. However, the venerable translators of the 1611 King James Bible deliberately selected kine for its rhythmic gravity and pastoral dignity, enshrining it in Western literary memory through Pharaoh's dream in Genesis 41. Through the 18th, 19th, and 20th centuries, Romantic and Victorian poets maintained kine as an evocative emblem of bucolic nostalgia, preserving a 5,000-year morphological journey in high English verse.
