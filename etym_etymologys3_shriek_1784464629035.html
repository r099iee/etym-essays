Shriek is an imitative, or echoic, English cry-word: its very shape mimics a thin, high, sudden outburst of sound rather than descending from a fully reconstructible Indo-European root with a clean set of regular cognates. Its history is therefore less a tree of sound laws than a history of acoustic imitation, spelling, and literary usage — but that history is rich, and it explains precisely why Tolkien chose this word to stage Bilbo's panic. 1. The imitative foundation. Across languages, words for sharp, high-pitched cries tend to be built from sibilants and fricatives — the hissing consonants that physically resemble such sounds. English shriek begins with /ʃ/ (the "sh" sound), continues with a liquid, and ends in a velar stop: the cluster itself feels thin and piercing. The same acoustic territory produced a whole Germanic family: screech, dialect skrike, screak, the adjective shrill, and on the Continent Old Norse skrækja "to screech," Middle Low German and Dutch shout-and-screech verbs such as schreeuwen. Northern Europe repeatedly coined, borrowed, and reshaped words for this one acoustic event, which is typical of echoic vocabulary: the sound demands to be named, and each dialect answers with a near-synonym.
  • Acoustic profile: high friction consonants + sudden release — /ʃr-/ and a hard velar stop at the end.
  • Cognate cluster: screech, screak, skrike, shrill; Old Norse skrækja; Dutch schreeuwen.
  • Classification: echoic, not a regular PIE root; no pre-Germanic ancestor can be reconstructed with confidence.
2. Old Norse and the Middle English entry. The most commonly accepted immediate source of the Middle English verb is Old Norse skrækja "to screech, cry harshly," a plausible borrowing given the heavy Norse influence on northern and eastern Middle English dialects after the Viking settlements. The Scandinavian connection fits the pattern of the many everyday Scandinavian loans in English (sky, skirt, scare, scream itself, which also has Norse ties). Middle English records the verb as shriken, schriken, and in variant spellings, meaning "to cry out shrilly," and the noun follows naturally: the cry and the act of crying are coined together. 3. Spelling and sound history. Middle English spelling of this word family was notoriously unstable — scribes wrote shriken, schreken, skriken, and scryke — because the underlying cluster /skr-/ was being palatalized to /ʃr-/ in the north while conservative spellings lingered. This is the same historical process that turned the sc- of shrill into the sh- spelling. By the modern period the form stabilized as shriek, with the -ie- spelling conventional and the sound /ʃriːk/ (in RP, /ʃɹiːk/). The noun ("a shriek") and verb ("to shriek") have traveled together ever since, with the participle shrieking and past shrieked as regular weak formations. 4. Semantic stability and range. Unlike many words in this deck, shriek has changed meaning very little over the centuries. Its core has always been a high, piercing, typically involuntary cry of fear, pain, or extreme excitement. What has expanded is the range of contexts: from genuine alarm cries (battle-shrieks, the cries of the wounded, the shrieking of birds at dusk) to, by the nineteenth century, lighter comic uses — people "shriek with laughter," and writers could compare a shriek to a steam-whistle emerging from a tunnel, as Tolkien would do. A further transferred sense licenses shriek for any high-pitched mechanical or animal noise (the shriek of brakes, of a whistle, of a kettle), a metaphor that works because the ear recognizes the same acoustic profile. 5. In the literature. Writers reach for shriek precisely when they need the ear to flinch. It appears in Gothic and Romantic prose for the sound of dread, in Dickens for both comic and horrible outcries, and in modern fiction for the sound of panic rendered in a single syllable. In The Hobbit, Tolkien stages the noun as an internal event first — "a shriek coming up inside" — then releases it as an explosion compared to a train whistle leaving a tunnel. The industrial simile is modern and comic; the panic is real. The card preserves that exact noun, the word for the sound that betrays Bilbo before the adventure has even left the Hill.
