The English adverb thereupon embodies the historical vitality of the Germanic pronominal adverb system—a morphological strategy developed to fuse spatial demonstratives with prepositions into unified, highly functional adverbs capable of navigating spatial, temporal, and abstract causal relationships.

1. Deep Indo-European and Germanic Foundations:

The first element of the compound traces to the Proto-Indo-European demonstrative pronominal stem *to- (producing Sanskrit tad, Greek to, and Latin tum). In Proto-Germanic, this stem was locatively extended into the locative adverb *þar, denoting "at that designated place" or "in that position." In Old English, this surfaced as þǣr (and Anglian þēr), serving as the foundational demonstrative adverb "there."

The second constituent derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *upo- ("under, up from under, over"), which developed in Proto-Germanic into the adverbial particles *upp ("upward") and *ana ("on, upon"). In Old English, these elements compounded into the preposition and adverb uppan or uppon, signifying "upon, on top of, or against."

2. Middle English Synthesis and Syntactic Motivation:

During the Old English period, expressions signifying "upon that" or "on it" were typically constructed using a preposition followed by an inflected dative or accusative pronoun, such as on þām or uppan þæt. However, following the Norman Conquest and the rapid erosion of the Old English case inflection system between 1150 and 1250, Middle English increasingly adopted the common Germanic syntactic habit of prefixing spatial adverbs to prepositions.

By compounding ther with upon, Middle English scribes produced ther-upon (alongside variants like therupone and þar-upon). This mirrored parallel developments in Continental West Germanic languages, such as Middle Dutch daer-op (modern Dutch daarop) and Middle High German dār-ūf (modern German darauf). In early attestations (such as the Ormulum and the Ancrene Wisse in the early 13th century), the compound initially retained a literal physical meaning: resting upon that specific object, piece of furniture, or piece of land.

3. Metaphorical Shift to Temporal and Causal Consequence:

Between 1300 and 1400, the word underwent a profound semantic expansion. As medieval narrative prose and chivalric romances flourished, writers required connectives that conveyed not merely sequential order, but the brisk, dramatic pace of cause and effect. Translators working from Latin historical and legal texts seized upon therupon to translate Latin constructions like deinde ("thereupon, then"), postea ("afterwards"), and ex quo ("whereupon").

  • Temporal Immediacy: In Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales and John Gower's Confessio Amantis, therupon appears frequently to mean "straightway," "forthwith," or "immediately upon the conclusion of that speech."
  • Causal and Deductive Linkage: By the 15th and 16th centuries, the word increasingly designated a consequence arising logically from antecedent events. The psychological metaphor was natural: just as a physical weight presses "upon" a surface, an outcome was perceived as resting "upon" its causative premise.

4. Early Modern Flowering and Literary Monumentality:

In the Early Modern English of the Renaissance, thereupon became an indispensable tool of rhetoric. In the plays of William Shakespeare, it signals prompt resolve and legal weight (e.g., in The Winter's Tale: "Thereupon / Did assist my continuance"). Most famously, in 1667, John Milton employed the word with monumental precision in the opening Argument to Book I of Paradise Lost:

  • "This first Book proposes, first in brief, the whole Subject, Mans disobedience, and the loss thereupon of Paradise wherein he was plac’t..."

Milton’s usage exemplifies the perfect synthesis of causal consequence and historical succession: the loss of Eden was not an arbitrary misfortune that happened after the fall; it was the loss that depended directly, legally, and theologically thereupon—flowing inexorably from the disobedience itself.

5. Institutionalization and Modern Legal Precision:

During the 18th and 19th centuries, while colloquial speech gradually abandoned pronominal compounds in favor of simpler prepositions ("after that," "on that," "immediately"), thereupon found a permanent sanctuary in statutory drafting, diplomatic dispatches, and chancery court procedure. In modern jurisprudence, it retains immense utility because it simultaneously establishes temporal proximity and causal nexus without requiring wordy explanatory clauses.
