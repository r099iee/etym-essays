The story of conveying begins on a Roman road, because its deepest ancestor is the word for the road itself. The reconstructed PIE root *wegh-, to go or to carry by vehicle, is one of the great motion roots of the Indo-European family: it gives Sanskrit vahati, he carries, Greek ochos, a carriage, Latin vehere, to carry, and English way, wagon, and vehicle. Latin also drew from this stream the small but mighty noun via, the road, which survives in every English via, viaduct, and trivia, literally the place where three roads meet.

When Latin speakers wanted a verb for accompanying someone along such a road, late and medieval usage prefixed com-, with or together, to via, producing conviare, to escort on the way. The formation is transparent: the guide goes with the traveller on the road. Monks, clerks, and notaries of the early Middle Ages used it for the practical business of escorting persons, parcels, and messages safely between towns, and from this administrative Latin the French took conveier in the 1200s, already shading from escorting people toward transporting things.

Middle English borrowed the French verb in the 1300s as conveyen, and the early English senses still smell of the highway and its dangers. To convey was to carry under protection, sometimes openly with carts and guards, sometimes secretly, slipping goods or persons past enemies, which is why Shakespeare can use convey for stealing away and for clever stage management as well as honest carting. The 16th and 17th centuries then stretched the road metaphor across the new sciences: physicians wrote of nerves conveying animal spirits, engineers of pipes conveying water, musicians of sounds conveying passion. Each new channel was simply another via, another road for another kind of load.

The decisive modern turn came when philosophers and letter-writers made meaning itself the freight. Locke and his heirs described words as conveyors of ideas, and polite society learned to convey thanks, compliments, and condolences as carefully as carriers conveyed china. Lawyers meanwhile froze an older shade into permanence: a deed conveys land, and the noun conveyance still names both the legal transfer and, in older usage, the vehicle that performs it. Industrial England added the conveyor belt, the road made endless, looping freight through mines and factories forever.

Landmarks of the journey:

  • PIE road-root: *wegh- sends wagons, ways, and vehicles across the whole family of languages, the deepest wheel-rut under the word.
  • Latin paving: via names the engineered Roman road, and com- plus via yields medieval conviare, the escorted journey.
  • French carting: Old French conveier carries the verb across the Channel with the Normans, already half escort and half transport.
  • Chaucerian highway: Middle English conveyen moves pilgrims, parcels, and purloined goods through 14th-century writing.
  • Early modern channels: pipes, nerves, and verses all convey their fluids and feelings in Tudor and Stuart prose and poetry.
  • Polite and legal fixity: deeds convey estates and gentlemen convey regards, preserving the word's dignity into the present.
  • Industrial loop: the conveyor belt literalises the metaphor, an endless road that never stops conveying.

Tolkien's laden carts rolling toward Buckland therefore carry the word's whole biography in miniature: an escort along the way, goods in transit, and, for the attentive reader, the quieter freight of meaning, as the Shire itself is conveyed, chapter by chapter, into our minds.
