Keel is a word of the sea and of the Germanic north, and its history begins not with the timber but with the whole ship. The underlying Germanic noun was *keluz or *keulaz, which could mean 'a ship' in general, and that ship-word is preserved with remarkable fidelity across the old Germanic dialects: Old English cēol, a poetic word for a vessel that sails in Beowulf; Old Norse kjǫlr; Old High German kiel; Middle Dutch kiel. Among the ancient Germanic peoples the ship was named, by the favourite trope of synecdoche, for its most conspicuous member — the deep longitudinal timber at the bottom of the hull that holds everything together, as the spine holds the body. It is telling that in the modern Scandinavian languages the same root still means both 'keel' and 'backbone, ridge': a mountain ridge is called a køl because it runs like a dorsal spine across the land, exactly as the keel runs along the bottom of the ship. The ship was thus defined by its spine, and the spine by the ship: one word carried both senses from the beginning.

The deeper ancestry of this ship-word is genuinely uncertain, and honest scholarship must say so rather than fabricate a root. Etymologists have proposed a connection with the PIE root *gʷel(h)-, 'to swallow', on the theory that the ship was conceived as a hollow vessel that 'gulps' the waves. There is a suggestive collateral fact: the Germanic languages also have a homophonous word meaning 'throat, gullet' (Old English cēol, German Kehle), and Latin's gula 'gullet' belongs to the same proposed family. But the correspondence is neither phonologically regular nor semantically certain, and standard authorities flag the link as speculative. The responsible statement is: the Germanic ship-word has NO securely attested PIE etymon; the 'swallow' theory is a hypothesis, not a fact. A chain that guesses here would be a fabrication, so the honest chain begins at Proto-Germanic and marks the deeper level as unknown.

The English form comes specifically from the Norse. The Scandinavian seafarers whose kjǫlr carried them to England, Ireland and the North Atlantic supplied the modern sense of the structural timber: recorded in English from the 14th century as kele, keel, the Norse word reinforced the native cēol and fixed the engineering sense of the lowermost longitudinal timber. Two other Germanic contacts left collateral traces. From Middle Dutch kiel 'ship, barge' English made a separate borrowing in the 16th–19th centuries: the keel, a flat-bottomed barge on the River Tyne, whose crew were the famous keelmen of Newcastle. And from Dutch kielhalen English took keelhaul (17th century), the grim punishment of dragging a man under the ship's bottom from one side to the other — a word that preserved the timber's position at the very base of the vessel in a practice of terrifying clarity.

As sail gave way to steel, the sense crystallised further. Modern naval architecture has its keels of iron and steel, its fin keels and bilge keels; the word is the engineer's name for the ship's backbone. In ordinary language it survives above all in the figurative idiom on an even keel — steady, balanced, undisturbed — and in the verb to keel over, of a ship capsizing and of a person collapsing. The ghost-story sentence of this card exploits exactly this double life: the dead men's weight makes the physical keel sink deep, and the whole passage runs on the old nautical word's gravity — a timber, a ship, a foundation, all at once. The word has come down from Viking longships to shipyard engineering and household idiom, but its core has never changed: the deep spine that holds the vessel, and everything that depends on it.

  • Literary attestations. Beowulf for Old English cēol; Old Norse sagas for kjǫlr; nautical handbooks from the 14th century; Dickens and the sea-novelists for the figurative senses; the translation tradition that produced this card's ghost-ferry scene.
  • Socio-cultural setting. The word's fortunes track the seafaring Germanic world — Viking ships, Hanseatic cogs and Dutch barges, Newcastle coal keels, and the age of sail — each contact re-supplying the same root with new shades of meaning.
  • Sound changes. Norse kjǫlr → Middle English kele/keel: loss of the initial kj- cluster to k-, monophthongisation of the vowel; Modern English /kiːl/.
  • Semantic summary. 'ship' (synecdoche) → 'the timber' (Norse, 14th c.) → 'the base/foundation' (figurative) → 'steady state' (idiom, 17th–19th c.) → 'ridge / carina' (science, 19th c.).
