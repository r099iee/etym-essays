Peripheral travels from Greek geometry to modern systems theory, and its career illustrates how a precise mathematical term for the edge of a circle became the master metaphor for anything secondary, marginal, or merely decorative — exactly the view of style that the source sentence rejects (“Style does not provide a peripheral cosmetic accompaniment”).

The Greek compound is transparent: peri- “around” (from PIE *per- “around, beyond,” also the source of around, far, peri-) + a derivative of pherein “to carry, bear” (from PIE *bher- “to carry,” source of bear, birth, fertile, metaphor). Periphereia in Euclid and Aristotle means the curved line carried around — the circumference — as opposed to diameter or centre. Late Latin peripheria preserves it as a learned geometrical term, passing into Middle French peripherie and Middle English peripherie/periphery (15th c.) with the same sense: the outer boundary of a round body or territory.

Key stages:

  • Greek geometry (Euclid c. 300 BCE): strictly technical. Periphereia is the boundary line, neither good nor bad, simply non-central. Perception is neutral-mathematical.
  • Late Latin and medieval science (200–1400 CE): commentators (Proclus, Boethius) keep peripheria for astronomy and optics — the periphery of vision, of the heavens. The centre/periphery opposition acquires cosmological dignity: centre is noble, periphery is distant but still part of the ordered whole.
  • Early English periphery/peripherical (15th–17th c.): Caxton and Elyot use periphery for city walls and bodily extremities. The adjective appears first as peripherical (1650s) then shortens to peripheral. Connotation remains spatial-literal: peripheral districts, peripheral nerves.
  • Anatomy, empire, and administration (18th–19th c.): two powerful extensions. Anatomists map the peripheral nervous system (nerves outside brain and spinal cord) versus central; imperial administrators speak of peripheral colonies, peripheral markets versus metropolitan core. Here periphery begins to imply subordination: what is peripheral is governed from elsewhere, less important. Victorian atlases color peripheries pink but faintly.
  • Twentieth-century abstraction (1900–present): the word detaches from space. Economists (Prebisch, Wallerstein’s world-systems theory) oppose core vs. periphery nations; psychology opposes central vs. peripheral routes to persuasion (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986); computing opposes central processor vs. peripheral devices (printers, scanners). In everyday prose peripheral now means “of secondary importance, tangential, incidental” — “a peripheral issue,” “peripheral characters,” “peripheral vision” (both literal edge-of-sight and figurative inattention). The source sentence leans on this fully bleached sense: style is not a peripheral garnish to ideas; it is the dance itself.

Phonology shows learned borrowing: Greek ph /pʰ/ → Latin ph /p/ → French/English /f/; stress settles on the second syllable /pəˈrɪfərəl/ in RP by the Latinate antepenultimate pattern after the suffix -al (< Latin -alis “pertaining to”) is added to periphery. The older peripherical preserves the full Greek stem; modern peripheral truncates it, a typical 19th-century scientific regularization (cf. peripheric in medicine).

Literary and scientific attestations mark the drift: Milton’s “periphery” of heaven; 19th-century medical texts (“peripheral irritation”); Conrad’s Heart of Darkness playing centre against periphery; T. S. Eliot’s “peripheral curiosity”; and modern computing manuals (“peripheral equipment”). Each step moves the word from line-on-a-diagram to judgment-of-value, which is why calling style “peripheral” in stylistics is not geometry but demotion — the exact demotion the author denies.
