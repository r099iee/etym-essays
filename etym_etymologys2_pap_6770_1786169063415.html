DERIVATION CHAIN — PAP
R → PIE *pa- — a prehistoric nursery syllable of feeding, an imitation of smacking lips or of an infant's first calls for food; the same reduplicated baby-talk appears across Indo-European (Latin pappa 'food', Greek pappa, Sanskrit pappatē) and in countless unrelated languages.
E → Latin pappa (baby-talk) 'food, pap' — attested in Varro and the grammarians as a child's word for eating; the same base also gives Late Latin pappa 'father', whence 'pope' and 'papa'.
D → Old French pape / pappe 'pap, mush, soft food' — the nursery word entering the Romance vernaculars.
C → Middle English pap / pappe (15th century) 'soft food for infants' — borrowed via Anglo-French; the plural pappis 'breasts, teats' is recorded by 1400.
B → Early Modern English pap 'soft semi-liquid food for infants or invalids; (plural) the breasts' — the standard word of weaning until the 19th century.
A → Modern English PAP 'soft food for infants; bland, trivial ideas' — the literal sense receding and the figurative 'pap for the masses' dominant since the 19th century.
Debate / nuance note: Pap is ultimately imitative nursery language, not a product of regular sound change, so no PIE root in the strict sense can be reconstructed; the label *pa- is a conventional reconstruction of the baby-talk syllable, not a documented proto-form. The link between the food sense and the 'paps = breast' sense is accepted (both are feeding), while 'Pap test' (from Papanicolaou) and 'papal' (from papa 'father') are unrelated false friends.
