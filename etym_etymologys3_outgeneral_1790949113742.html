The verb outgeneral represents an illustrative example of English morphological compounding, uniting a native Germanic comparative prefix (out-) with a classical Latinate military title (general). To understand its full historical depth, we must examine the isolated evolutionary trajectories of both constituent roots and analyze how their union formed a vivid strategic metaphor.

1. Evolution of the Prefix 'out-':

The prefix out- derives from Proto-Indo-European *ūd- / *ūt- ("up, out, forth"). In Proto-Germanic, this became the spatial adverb and preposition *ūt. In Old English, ūt- was extensively employed as a productive verbal and nominal prefix (e.g., ūtfaran "to go out", ūtlaga "outlaw"). During the Middle and Early Modern English periods, English developed a unique, highly productive morphological pattern: prefixing out- to nouns and verbs to indicate "surpassing, exceeding, or defeating in the quality of the base" (generating such terms as outrun, outdo, outshine, outbrave, and outwit).

2. Evolution of the Base 'general':

The root of general traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵenh₁- ("to produce, beget, give birth to"), which generated Latin genus (stem gener-), meaning "race, stock, family, kind, or species." From this noun, the Romans derived the adjective generālis, signifying "relating to the whole kind or class, universal, non-specific" (as opposed to speciālis, relating to a specific individual).

In Late Latin and medieval military administration, the term began attaching to military offices. A commander with authority over an entire armed force rather than a specific regional detachment was designated in Old French as a capitaine general ("general captain" or overall supreme commander). By the late 14th century, Middle English borrowed general from Anglo-Norman. Over time, the noun captain was dropped through ellipsis, leaving the adjective general functioning as an independent military noun for the highest rank of army leadership.

By the 17th century, the prestige of military command led English writers to convert the noun into a verb: to general meant "to lead, manage, conduct, or direct an army or campaign with strategic skill."

3. Synthesis: The Formation and Rise of 'Outgeneral':

During the 18th century—an era defined by the European Enlightenment and sophisticated military treatises on maneuvers by commanders like the Duke of Marlborough and Frederick the Great—the compound verb outgeneral was forged. It appeared in historical and military chronicles to describe instances where a general defeated an adversary through superior intellectual generalship (positioning, deception, and timing) rather than casualty-heavy frontal assaults.

In 1775, during the American Revolutionary War, commentators celebrated George Washington's tactical withdrawals and sudden counterstrikes (such as the Battle of Trenton) as masterclasses in outgeneraling British forces. Throughout the 19th and 20th centuries, the term broadened beyond the battlefield into parliamentary politics, corporate enterprise, and humorous social literature. Authors like P.G. Wodehouse utilized the grand military word to comic perfection, contrasting the monumental dignity of outgeneraling an army with Bertie Wooster's petty evasions of his domineering aunts.
