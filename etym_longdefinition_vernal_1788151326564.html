vernal (adjective) means “of, relating to, or occurring in spring” — from Latin vēr, “spring” — and, by a Renaissance extension, “fresh, youthful, or suggestive of springtime”. It is the formal, poetic member of the spring-family: where “spring” is the everyday season-word, “vernal” names the season's essence — its light, its promise, its motion — and it carries exactly the dignity the source passage needs when Wordsworth writes of days that “bring with them vernal promises, the hope / Of active days”.

Sense 1 — the season and the sky. The primary, literal sense: belonging to spring. Its fixed astronomical use is “the vernal equinox” — the moment around 20–21 March when the sun crosses the celestial equator northward and astronomical spring begins; because of the precession of the equinoxes, the “first point of Aries” has drifted from the constellation Aries into Pisces, yet the vernal equinox remains the zero-point of astronomical coordinate systems. Also ordinary: “the vernal season”, “vernal flowers”, “vernal weather”, and, in older natural history, the “vernal migration” of birds.

Sense 2 — youth and freshness (figurative). Since the Renaissance, “vernal” has described the springtime of life: “vernal years” (youth), “vernal bloom”, “a vernal freshness”. The metaphor is as old as seasonal language — youth is the spring of life, maturity its summer — and “vernal” supplies the formal register of that metaphor in English, exactly as “autumnal” supplies its closing term and the rarer “hiemal” / “estival” supply winter and summer.

Sense 3 — technical modern uses. (a) Vernalization (agriculture and botany, 20th century): the cold treatment of seeds or young plants that induces or accelerates flowering, classically applied to winter wheat and other cereals so that they behave like spring varieties — the term was coined in the inter-war years from vernal (after the Russian jarovizacija, from jarovoj “spring”). (b) A vernal pool is a seasonal wetland that fills in winter and spring and dries by summer — a conservation term especially in California and Mediterranean-type climates, famous for its specialist fauna such as fairy shrimp. (c) Sweet vernal grass (Anthoxanthum odoratum) is a common meadow grass whose coumarin scent perfumes hay — the name has been standard in botanical English for two centuries. Meteorology and geology also use “vernal” for phenomena of the spring season.

Register. Conversational English does not use “vernal”; one says “spring” (“spring flowers”, “spring term”). “Vernal” lives in literary prose and verse and in fixed technical phrases, and it reads as elegant, deliberate, faintly archaic — a word chosen for a reason, whether the reason is an equinox, a seed-treatment, or a stanza.

  • Seasonal: of spring (vernal equinox, vernal flowers, vernal migration).
  • Figurative: youthful, fresh, promising (vernal years, vernal bloom).
  • Technical: vernalization (cold-induced flowering), vernal pool (seasonal wetland), sweet vernal grass.
  • Degree: a formal/poetic register; stronger and more deliberate than plain “spring-like”.
  • Watch: not to be confused with “vernant” (archaic “flourishing, green”) or “springy” (elastic — unrelated).
