Etymology of OUTLANDISH — a native Old English compound ut + land + -isc (three Indo-European components): CHAIN FOR THE FIRST COMPONENT ut "out": Stage 1 (PIE): *ud- (PIE) — "up, out, outward" — the root of Sanskrit ud ("up, out") and of English "utter", "utterly". Stage 2 (Proto-Germanic): *ūt — "out, outside". Stage 3 (Old English, pre-8th c.): ūt / ūte — "out, outside". CHAIN FOR THE SECOND COMPONENT land: Stage 1 (PIE): *lendh- (PIE) — "open land, heath, uncultivated ground" — the root of Old Norse land, Dutch land, German Land. Stage 2 (Proto-Germanic): *landą — "land, ground, territory". Stage 3 (Old English, 7th c.): land / lond — "land, territory, ground". CHAIN FOR THE ADJECTIVAL SUFFIX -isc: Stage 1 (PIE): *-isko- (PIE) — adjective-forming suffix "of, pertaining to, of the nature of". Stage 2 (Proto-Germanic): *-iskaz — adjective-forming suffix (as in English "English", "Danish", "heathenish"). Stage 3 (Old English): -isc — adjective-forming suffix. COMPOUND: Stage 4 (Old English, c. 9th–11th c.): ūtlendisc (attested in Old English glosses and homilies, e.g. Ælfric, late 10th c.) — "foreign, of another country, of the out-lands". Stage 5 (Middle English, 13th–14th c.): outlandish / outlandissh — "foreign, alien; of or from another country". Stage 6 (Early Modern English, 16th–17th c., semantic shift): outlandish — "uncouth, barbarous, bizarre" — the meaning drifts from "foreign" to "grotesquely strange". Stage 7 (modern, present day): OUTLANDISH — "looking or sounding bizarre or unfamiliar; wildly unconventional or grotesque."
