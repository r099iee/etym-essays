Hooting is a word that has done nothing but make noise for eight centuries, and its whole biography is written in sound. It belongs to the largest and oldest class of English vocabulary: onomatopoeia — words coined to imitate the sounds they name. There is no Proto-Indo-European root behind "hoot", because the word was never inherited from an ancient ancestor; it was created, again and again, wherever a human throat tried to copy the hollow, carrying cry of an owl or a shout. The same imitation produced cognates across the medieval Germanic world: Middle Dutch huten "to hoot", Middle High German hūzen and hūchen "to cry out", and the uncertain Old Norse hóta "to threaten", which some scholars keep in the family and others set apart as an independent formation. The very spread of these near-identical forms is the proof of the word's nature: independent speakers, hearing the same hollow call, made the same mouth-noise, and the sound itself became the word. Middle English: the bare shout. Around 1200 the word enters English as Middle English houten, meaning simply "to shout, call out" — a plain, hollow, carried cry of the human throat, with no colour of mockery yet and no animal echo. This is the raw onomatopoeic stratum: the word is the noise, and it names the act of making that noise, nothing more. Its earliest life is human and social — calling across a field, raising the alarm, greeting at a distance — and its register is the ordinary speech of a village or a marketplace. The owl's cry and the grammatical engine. The mid-fifteenth century brings the decisive specialization: the word is first applied to bird cries, above all the owl's deep, wavering, melancholy call. From a shout it becomes a sound that names a sound, and the compound "hoot owl" (1826) later sets the hooting, true owl apart from the screech owl — the two birds being named by exactly the cries they make. Meanwhile the participial form was taking shape by ordinary English grammar: the present participle in Old English ended in -ende, but in Middle English that ending converged with the gerund ending -ing(e), and the resulting -ing participle, "hooting", is attested from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Nothing about the form is irregular: it is the standard modern participial machinery, and it gives the word its adjectival and adverbial mobility — "hooting owls", "hooting laughter", "hooting and hallooing". c. 1600: the crowd's weapon. Around 1600 the social sense completes its arc: "to hoot" comes to mean "to shout in disapproval or scorn". Crowds hoot at bad performers; mobs hoot politicians from the hustings; the cry becomes the weapon of public derision, giving contempt an open, wordless voice. This is the sense that makes "hooting" so potent in political and theatrical history — the sound of an audience or a populace rejecting what it hates, an animal verdict rendered without argument. The eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries then shrink the sound into idiom: "not worth a hoot" and "I don't give a hoot" (early 19th c.) reduce the cry to the smallest possible unit of value — a damn, a shrug — the sound of indifference where the word once meant the loudest noise a throat could make. And in 1926 the derision softens into delight: something hilarious is "a hoot", the crowd's contempt turning into the crowd's amusement, the least hostile descendant of the jeer. Tolkien and the sound of pursuit. In The Hobbit (1937) the goblins come down from the trees "hooting and hallooing, and hunting among the trees". Tolkien's use is deliberately regressive: he takes the word back to its most primitive register, before derision and before comedy, to a noise that is neither language nor threat but the animal cry of the hunt itself. The pairing with "hallooing" — the archaic hunting shout — deepens the atavism: these are not articulate enemies but sounds on the hunt, "hooting" with the owl's hollow tu-whit and the mob's jeer fused. Contemporary English still keeps both strands: the owl still hoots in the dark woods, ships and trains hoot their horns, crowds hoot performers, and a joke can still be "a hoot". The word's career — from village shout (c. 1200), to owl-cry (15th c.), to jeer (c. 1600), to idiom of indifference (19th c.), to laughter (1926) — is a history of the human voice in public, and the participle "hooting" remains its most ancient and most vivid form.
