wheezing is the present participle and verbal noun of wheeze: breathing with a high-pitched whistling, whistling-rattling or laboured hissing sound because air is forced through narrowed or obstructed airways, and by extension making any similar laboured whistling noise. In Dickens — “go wheezing up and down” — it describes frozen pedestrians whose breath is audible as they pace to keep warm, half verb of motion, half sound-word for cold-air bronchospasm.

Core medical sense and its dimensions:

  • Respiratory symptom: continuous musical sound on breathing out (sometimes in), signalling asthma, COPD, bronchiolitis, croup, allergy, heart failure or simply inhaled cold fog. Doctors distinguish expiratory wheeze (lower airways) from inspiratory stridor (upper airway) and from crackles. Patients report “tight chest, whistling.”
  • Manner of breathing: panting, gasping, puffing audibly after exertion, in old age, or in extreme cold. “He came wheezing up the hill.” The participle stresses ongoing, habitual action, not a single gasp.
  • Sound of animals: horses with “heaves,” pugs, bulldogs, cats with asthma — any beast that whistles when it breathes.
  • Sound of things: bellows, bagpipes, concertinas, accordions, radiators, kettles, leaky tyres, old buses or lifts that emit a breath-like whistle. “The bus wheezed to a stop.” Here the metaphor maps lungs onto machines.
  • Figurative for people / jokes / institutions: old, worn-out, out of breath, laboured. “A wheezing gag,” “a wheezing empire,” “the wheezing old guard.” Implies something kept alive by effort and likely to collapse, often with affectionate contempt.
  • Noun wheeze and slang: a single whistling breath (“a wheeze in his chest”); in British informal use, a clever plan or trick (“a tax wheeze”). The participle wheezing can also be an attributive adjective: “wheezing chest,” “wheezing harmonium.”

Grammar and collocation:

  • Forms: wheeze – wheezes – wheezed – wheezing. Intransitive (“he wheezes”), sometimes transitive with out (“wheeze out an apology”). Adjectival: “wheezing cough.”
  • Typical collocates: wheezing breath, wheezing cough, wheezing chest, wheezing laugh, go wheezing, come wheezing, still wheezing.
  • Degree: from faint musical whistle only heard with a stethoscope to loud audible distress. Dickens’s outdoor crowd is nearer the loud, visible end: beating hands, stamping feet, breath clouding.
  • Connotation: literal-medical is neutral-clinical; descriptive-literary is vivid, slightly comic-pitiful (the body labouring); figurative-political is dismissive (past its prime).
  • Distinctions: gasping is sudden and silent-ish; panting is rapid and breathy; wheezing is specifically whistling/musical and obstructed. Snoring is in sleep; snorting is nasal and contemptuous.

Why cold makes people wheeze bridges physiology and history: cold dry air cools and dries airways, triggering bronchoconstriction even in healthy lungs, worse in coal-smoke fog. Victorian readers instantly recognised the sound-picture of a December court: stamping, slapping, visible breath and audible whistle. To master wheezing is to hear that whistle — lung, machine or empire — labouring to push air through too narrow a passage.
