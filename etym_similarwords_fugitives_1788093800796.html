• Refugees: Similar because both are people forced to flee their homes; differs because "refugee" is a legal status under international law (1951 Convention), usually involving a border crossing and an entitlement to protection, while "fugitive" implies pursuit or criminal flight and carries no protected status.
  • Runaways: Similar because both are persons who flee; differs because "runaway" is casual register and usually domestic — a child, a slave, or an animal running from authority — whereas "fugitive" is formal and suggests a serious, ongoing hunt.
  • Escapees: Similar because both have broken free from confinement; differs because "escapee" emphasizes the act of getting out of a specific institution (prison, camp) and is neutral news-reporting language, while "fugitive" stresses the continuing danger of recapture.
  • Absconders: Similar because both flee to avoid legal process; differs because "absconder" is strictly legalistic (leaving secretly to evade charges, bail, or parole) and narrower than fugitive, which also covers flight from any enemy or danger.
  • Deserters: Similar because both abandon their post or side; differs because "deserter" is specific to military (or institutional) duty and implies a breach of allegiance, while a fugitive may simply be fleeing, not betraying anyone.
  • Outlaws: Similar because both are persons outside the law's protection; differs because "outlaw" is archaic and implies a formal declaration of outlawry (living "outside the law"), while a modern fugitive is usually merely accused and sought.
  • Exiles: Similar because both are separated from home by compulsion; differs because exile is a punishment imposed by authority — the person is sent away — while fugitives leave of their own volition and are chased, which makes exile's register more solemn and sometimes honourable.
  • Outcasts: Similar because both involve ejection from a community; differs because outcast is social rejection, often figurative, with no physical pursuit at all.
  • Displaced persons: Similar because both are uprooted by war or disaster; differs because "displaced person" is a bureaucratic 20th-century term (post-WWII) emphasizing homelessness and aid eligibility, with no connotation of being hunted.
  • Émigrés: Similar because both are people who left their country, often for political reasons; differs because "émigré" is genteel and evokes voluntary intellectual exile (post-Revolutionary France), the opposite emotional colour of the hunted fugitive.
  • Defectors: Similar because both cross from one side to another against authority; differs because "defector" implies joining another side (Cold War politics), and the defector usually gains protection rather than being pursued.
  • Asylum seekers: Similar because both flee danger and seek safety; differs because "asylum seeker" is a precise legal category inside the refugee-determination process, while "fugitive" carries no procedural meaning.
  • Fugitives from justice (compound sense): the legal sense of the word itself — a person who flees a jurisdiction to avoid arrest or trial; differs from the humanitarian uses above only in context, not in root meaning: all fugitives flee.
