**GONGS: a colonial history written in bronze** The history of GONGS is a colonial history written in bronze. Unlike the native Germanic vocabulary of English, the word has no Proto-Indo-European ancestry at all; its lineage runs instead through the Austronesian languages of Southeast Asia, and its ultimate source is nothing more or less than the sound of the instrument itself. In Malay the word *gong* is an echoic formation that reproduces the deep, booming, resonant tone of a large suspended bronze disc when struck; related forms such as Javanese *agung* / *ageng* and the reduplicated *gonggong* show the same expressive principle at work across the family. For centuries these instruments were central to the music of island Southeast Asia, above all in the great gamelan orchestras of Java and Bali, where tuned gongs and gong-chimes mark the phrase-structure of the music with their long, throbbing strokes. **The age of exploration and the loan into Europe** The word entered European languages in the sixteenth century, when Portuguese navigators and traders in the East Indies adopted the Malay term and carried it home, giving Portuguese *gongo* or *gongue*. From Portugal it passed into French (*gong*), Dutch, and German (*Gong*), and by about 1600 it appears in English, at first in the travel-writings describing the exotic instruments of the Orient. The initial consonant cluster was unproblematic to European speakers, and the monosyllabic, echoic shape of the word — a sound in search of its referent — made it easy to assimilate. The noun at first named the instrument purely as an Oriental curiosity; the deep, foreign, barbaric associations that clung to it in English lasted for centuries and are precisely the associations Tolkien exploits when the goblins of the Misty Mountains beat their gongs underground. **From temple instrument to domestic signal** As a noun the word spread from the instrument itself to the act of signalling — a gong summoning servants, marking meals, or announcing performances — and in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the dinner gong became a standard household object of the European bourgeoisie, an Orientalist strangeness still clinging to its boom. From 1853 the word is used as a verb, *to gong*, meaning to summon or signal with a gong. In twentieth-century show business the gong acquired a comic-sinister role as the instrument that dismissed a failing act in the "gong-show," and in British armed-forces slang of the World War I era "gongs" came to mean medals — the shiny bronze discs on a soldier's chest recalling the instrument's shape and gleam. **Sound shifts and form** Because the word is a recent loanword rather than an inherited word, it has undergone almost no sound change in English: the Malay monosyllable passed into English essentially unchanged, and the plural simply takes the regular *-s*. The only notable feature is the retention of the voiced velar *g-* and the open back vowel, which give the word its deep, booming acoustic character — a word that sounds like its referent, exactly as it did in the Malay archipelago. **Literary and cultural life** Throughout English literature the gong kept its aura of the foreign and the fateful: its deep, slow, enveloping tone was the sound of Eastern temples, colonial announcements, and impending doom. In the goblin chant "Knocker and gongs!" Tolkien reaches for this accumulated resonance — the metal disc that does not ring like a bell but booms and hangs in the air, issuing as if from the very rock of the mountain. The plural form completes the picture of an industrial percussion arsenal: the sharp knocker and the booming gong together compose the brutal, mechanical din of the goblin host, keeping time with their cruel march and the flogging of their prisoners.
