- 1790s–1810s: German philosophical translation - Emerged in English as an intellectual calque of Kant's übersinnlich, denoting the realm of pure reason and noumena inaccessible to empirical sensation.
- 1810s–1850s: Romantic and Transcendentalist adoption - Championed by Samuel Taylor Coleridge, Thomas Carlyle, and American Transcendentalists to describe sublime spiritual intuition and the divine soul of nature.
- 1860s–1900s: Aesthetic and theological extension - Employed by Victorian art critics and mystical theologians to describe sublime aesthetic sensations and spiritual contemplation that transcend material bodily pleasure.
- 1900s–1950s: Anthroposophical and esoteric formalization - Adopted by esoteric thinkers like Rudolf Steiner to designate higher clairvoyant faculties and supersensuous realms of knowledge.
- Contemporary usage: Scholarly philosophy and cultural theory - Retained as a specialized, evocative term in metaphysics, theology, and philosophy of art to describe dimensions of meaning that surpass sensory evidence.
