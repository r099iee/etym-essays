- 14th–15th c. — Initial emergence: Borrowed into Middle English as an ethnonym and adjective denoting an inhabitant of Greece or anything of Greek origin, used interchangeably with Greek.
- 16th c. — Scholarly and biblical distinction: Employed in the Renaissance and early English Bible translations to designate Hellenized Jews or Greek-speaking subjects of the Roman Empire, as distinct from native Hebrews.
- 17th–18th c. — Classical and aesthetic elevation: Specialised into an elevated stylistic descriptor denoting ancient, classical Hellenic civilization, architecture, and literature, contrasting with the colloquial Greek.
- 19th c. — Romantic Hellenism and fashion: Epitomized by Keats’s Ode on a Grecian Urn, denoting idealized artistic harmony, classical physical features (the ‘Grecian profile’), and fleeting Victorian trends such as the ‘Grecian bend’.
- 20th–21st c. — Stylistic preservation: Retained primarily in art history, classical architecture, and literary aesthetics, while everyday political, national, and linguistic reference is ceded exclusively to Greek.
