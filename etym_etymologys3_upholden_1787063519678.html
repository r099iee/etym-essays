Two roots, one compound.

Upholden joins the adverb/preposition up with the archaic past participle holden of the verb "to hold." Its history therefore combines the long Germanic story of up with the development of hold and its strong participial forms — and the word's fate is really the story of how English lost most of its strong -en participles while a few survived as archaic jewels.

The ascent of "up."

The English word up descends from Old English up, upp "up, upward, aloft," from Proto-Germanic *upp (cf. Old Norse upp, German auf, Dutch op), ultimately from the Indo-European root *upó "under, from under, up, over" — a root whose sense of "rising from beneath" yields English "up," "over," "open," and "above." The word has been a stable adverb of direction for over a thousand years, and as a verbal prefix it has multiplied into a vast family: uplift, uphold, uproot, upbringing, upset. In upholden the prefix contributes "aloft, in an elevated position" and, by extension, "in support."

The participle "holden."

  • Old English healdan "to hold, keep, contain, possess, guard, govern" descends from Proto-Germanic *haldaną (cf. Old Norse halda, German halten). The PIE root is usually given as *kel- "to drive, urge" with a dental extension, but the ultimate origin is debated; some connect it to a root meaning "bend, incline."
  • It is one of the older strong verbs: its Old English past participle was healden, carrying the -en ending characteristic of strong verbs.
  • In Middle English the verb was holden (also halden, houlden), and its past participle was regularly holden — "I have holden," "he was holden" (= regarded, considered, bound). This is the form embedded in upholden.
  • The strong -en participle survives in modern English only in a handful of forms: "gotten" (US), "shaken," "forsaken," and the archaic "holden," "upholden," and "beholden" (the last still current in the transferred sense "indebted, obliged").

Uphold and upholden in early modern English.

  • The prefixed verb "to uphold" appears in Middle English (up + holden) meaning "to hold up, support, sustain; to keep from falling; to maintain, defend."
  • In 16th–17th-century English "upholden" was a normal, if somewhat formal, past participle: the Geneva Bible (1560) uses it, and it appears in the Book of Common Prayer and among Shakespeare's contemporaries.
  • The King James Bible (1611) and the liturgical tradition preserved the -en participial forms — "holden," "gotten," "upholden" — lending them a biblical, reverent register that they never lost.

Decline and archaisation.

  • From the 17th century onward English increasingly formed the participle of "uphold" as "upheld," a weak-style form that won ordinary prose by the 18th century.
  • "Upholden" retreated into elevated, poetic, and archaising contexts — hymnody ("his kingdom shall be upholden"), historical romance, and pastiche of biblical style.
  • By the 20th century "upholden" was a deliberate archaism, used by poets and stylists for grandeur and antique dignity.

Tolkien and the word in The Hobbit.

  • Tolkien's dwarf-song line — "His crown shall be upholden, His harp shall be restrung" — uses "upholden" as an archaising strong participle: a crown sustained in place, a kingship maintained.
  • The -en ending ("restrung" in the same stanza plays on another strong form) evokes the antique, the sacred, and the heroic — exactly the register of a prophecy-song of a kingdom's restoration.
  • Modern readers hear "upholden" as elevated, old-fashioned, and ceremonial, precisely what Tolkien intended, drawing on centuries of biblical and poetic association.

Summary of the drift.

  • From an ordinary strong participle in Middle English (holden) to a normal literary participle in early modern English (upholden) to an archaic/poetic survival today.
  • The meaning barely drifted — "upheld, supported, maintained, held aloft" throughout; what changed was the word's status and emotional register, from workaday to reverent.
  • Its closest living relative, "beholden," remains current with a transferred sense ("indebted"), showing how the -en family has dwindled to a few specialised survivors.
