The word tithe is a decimal fossil: it is, at bottom, the old Indo-European word for “tenth,” and it has carried the number ten down through four millennia without losing its fraction. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *déḱm̥, “ten,” with the ordinal *déḱm̥tos, “tenth,” which the daughter languages inherited with remarkable fidelity. In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit दश (dáśa) “ten” and दशम (daśamá) “tenth,” living on in Hindi das and the ordinal dasvāṁ and in Odia dasa; in Avestan it is dasa, in Persian dah. In Hellenic it yielded Greek δέκα (déka) “ten,” with δεκάτη (dekatē) “a tenth part”—the word the Greek Bible used for the sacred tenth, the same institution English would later call the tithe—and the root lies beneath English decade, decagon, and decathlon. In Italic the root became Latin decem “ten,” with decimus “tenth” and the noun decima “a tenth part,” the standard Latin word for the tax or due of one-tenth; from decima the Romance languages made their words for tithe—Old French dîme, modern French dîme, Italian decima, Spanish diezmo—and from Old French disme English drew dime, the American coin minted since 1786 as a tenth of a dollar. Latin decima also bred decimāre, “to take a tenth (as a punishment),” whose English descendant decimate means today “to destroy a great proportion of,” and decanus, “chief of ten,” gave English dean, the head of ten monks or of a cathedral chapter. The Germanic branch kept the root in *tehun “ten” and the ordinal *tegunþō “tenth”: Old English tīen became modern ten, and Old English tēoþa “tenth” developed two descendants—tenth, the pure ordinal, and tithe, the special word for the tenth of one’s produce owed to the church. The phonetics of the Germanic line are governed by Grimm’s and Verner’s Laws: the PIE *ḱ of *déḱm̥ became Germanic *h (hence *tehun), and the ordinal *déḱm̥tos became *tegunþō, with the voiced spirant þ that shows up in Old English tēoþa. In late Old English and Middle English the form settled as tithe/teothe with the interdental spirant, the final -a dropping away; the modern spelling tithe, rhyming with scythe, crystallised in the later Middle Ages. Meanwhile the pure ordinal was re-formed by analogy with ten and the suffix -th, producing the doublet tenth—two English words from one Old English ancestor. The social history of the word is as important as its sounds. From the earliest Anglo-Saxon law codes (the laws of Ine of Wessex) and the decrees of the church councils, the tithe was a sacred obligation—a tenth of the year’s grain, flocks, and herds, owed to God and His clergy. Domesday Book (1086) records tithe renders across England; the medieval parish built its tithe barn to store the tenth of the harvest; church courts enforced payment with spiritual sanctions. The word is woven through English agrarian and legal life for half a millennium. In the sixteenth century the English Reformation and the dissolution of the monasteries transferred many tithes into lay hands—impropriated tithes owned by gentry—and tithe disputes became a staple of litigation, of Parliamentary controversy, and later of Victorian rural fiction. When the phrase “a tithe of” enters literary English, it therefore brings with it the whole apparatus of church law, harvest dues, and barns. Sound shifts, semantic drift, and literary use:
  • Sound shifts: PIE *ḱ → PGmc *h (déḱm̥ → *tehun); PIE *d → PGmc *t; the ordinal *-m̥tos → *-gunþō (Verner’s Law voicing); Old English tēoþa → Middle English tithe (loss of -a, retained spirant); the doublet tenth re-formed by analogy in later Middle English.
  • Semantic drift: “tenth” (ordinal) → “a tenth part” → “the church tenth” → “a legal ecclesiastical tax” → “a voluntary religious offering” → “a small exact portion” (modern, often ironic).
  • Literary and documentary attestations: the Anglo-Saxon laws and Domesday Book; the medieval homilies and the summae of canon law; Langland’s Piers Plowman and the Pardoner’s controversies over tithes; the English Prayer Book; the tithe-riots and tithe-farm records of the 18th–19th centuries; and, in the couplet introducing Dai-yu in The Story of the Stone, the word’s modern literary use as a precise, quasi-fiscal measure of grief.
  • Cultural weight: the word retains its sacred register even in secular use—to pay a tithe is still to acknowledge a debt that is at once economic and owed to God, which is precisely the double force the couplet exploits.
