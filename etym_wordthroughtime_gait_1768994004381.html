- 14th c. - gait (from Old Norse gata) enters English meaning 'a way, path, or road' and, by extension, 'a going, a journey'; it names the route itself rather than the walking.
- 16th c. - the sense narrows from 'the way' to 'the way of going': a person's or horse's manner of stepping; the word begins to describe a walk as an observable, measurable performance.
- 17th–18th c. - horsemanship fixes the technical meaning: the 'gaits' of a horse are its distinct footfalls (walk, trot, canter, gallop); in polite prose it also names the dignified, stately step of the well-born.
- 19th c. - the novel and the clinic make it the standard term for a person's characteristic way of walking, connoting a cool, diagnostic attention; the gait of the ailing and of the confident alike can be 'read' like an instrument.
- 20th–21st c. - chiefly literary and technical: medical records speak of 'gait abnormalities' and animal science of equine gaits, while in prose the word lends observation a clinical, clockwork precision - exactly the narrator's tone in this passage.
