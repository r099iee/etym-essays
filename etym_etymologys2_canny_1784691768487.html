Derivation chain for CANNY: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *ǵneh₃- 'to know' (reconstructed; attested reflexes include Sanskrit jānāti 'he knows', Greek gignōskō 'I know', Latin (g)nōscō 'I get to know', and the Germanic preterite-present *kunnaną). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *kunnaną 'to know, to be mentally able, to have learned' — a preterite-present verb, i.e. a verb whose past-tense forms took on present meaning; cognates include Old Norse kunna, German können, and Gothic kunnan. Stage 3 — Old English cunnan 'to know, to know how, to be able' (recorded from the 8th century); the first- and third-person singular present is can, the past participle cūþ 'known', surviving in uncouth and the reborrowed couth. Stage 4 — Middle English can (present tense of cunnen) 'to know, know how, be able' — the direct ancestor of the modern auxiliary can. Stage 5 — canny (adjective), a Scottish and northern English formation from can with the adjectival suffix -y (Old English -ig), 'knowing, wise' (first recorded in the 1630s). Stage 6 — Modern English canny 'shrewd, cautious, prudent; careful, skilful, frugal; (Scots) safe, trustworthy, pleasant, lucky'. Final node: CANNY. Note on nuance: the sense development is continuous from 'knowing' to 'cautious, shrewd' (18th–19th centuries) and, in the superstitious register of Scots, to 'safe, lucky, of good omen'. The negative uncanny (un- + canny) follows the same chain in reverse: originally 'not safe to deal with, mischievous' (1590s), then 'unreliable, untrustworthy' (17th century), then 'supernaturally eerie' (19th century).
