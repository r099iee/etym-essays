- c. 1300: merely the humble wild flower of woods and banks — the plant, plain and unremarkable, from Old French violete.
- 14th–15th c.: the word slips from flower to hue — the colour: anything bluish-purple is "violet"; Newton later fixes it as the last band of the visible spectrum.
- 17th–18th c.: the sentimental century adopts it — the emblem of modesty: floriography and poetry make the half-hidden flower the type of shyness and faithful quiet love.
- 1815: the Napoleonic season — the political violet: Bonapartists wear the flower at Napoleon's return from Elba; "Corporal Violet" is the Emperor's nickname and the violet a badge of hope.
- 1801–1900s: science takes the name — the invisible violet: ultraviolet is discovered beyond the violet band; "violet rays" become a health fad and the colour enters physics and medicine.
- Victorian–20th c.: the shrinking violet becomes the standing idiom for shyness, and the liturgical violet of Advent and Lent keeps its penance in the churches.
- Today: the word simultaneously names the plant, the perfume note, the colour in design and physics, and the idiom of timidity — the plural still summons spring, modesty and a Victorian sigh.
