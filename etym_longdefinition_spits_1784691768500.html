Spits is the plural of the noun spit: a long, slender, pointed rod — originally of wood, later of iron or steel — on which meat is impaled so that it can be roasted evenly over an open fire, the rod being turned by hand or by a mechanical jack so that the joint cooks on all sides without burning. The word is bound up with medieval and pre-industrial cookery, in which a whole carcass or large joint was threaded onto the spit and suspended horizontally above the hearth or embers, with a dripping-pan beneath to catch the fat; spits were part of the permanent furniture of a great kitchen, and turning them was the task of a servant or spit-boy (in great houses, of the turnspit dog, a small long-backed dog bred to run in the wheel that turned the jack).

In the passage from The Hobbit, the spits are the trolls' roasting rods, and the phrase pinched the very mutton off the spits is the narrator's comic index of supreme burglary: the legendary thief would not merely raid the larder but would remove the meat from the very rods that held it above the fire — a theft so bold and so deft that it would pass unseen. The word here is concrete and period-flavoured: spits belong to the world of open fires, whole roasted animals, and troll kitchens, and they help give the trolls' campfire its rude, archaic, appetising reality.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The noun spit has branched into several distinct meanings, and it must be carefully separated from the homonymous verb:

  • Roasting rod (primary): the slender pointed rod for impaling and roasting meat — "on a spit", "spit-roast", "spit-roasted"; the word is alive today in barbecue and rotisserie cookery.
  • The whole apparatus: the spit, its supports, and the turning mechanism — a hand-cranked or clockwork "roasting-jack"; "turnspit" names both the servant and the dog that powered the wheel.
  • Depth of a spade (obsolete measure): "a spit's depth" — the depth to which a spade-blade can be driven, roughly a foot; a measure used by gardeners and diggers, from the pointed blade by the same shape-extension as the rod.
  • Narrow point of land (geography): "a sand-spit" or "a spit of land" — a long, narrow tongue of sand or shingle projecting from the coast into the water, so named by the pointed, tapering shape it shares with the roasting rod; first recorded in this sense in the 17th century.
  • Narrow bar or sliver (transferred): by the same pointed-shape extension, a "spit" can be any slender projection — a thin slice of food, a projection on a machine, a narrow protrusion in engineering.
  • Verb "to spit" (roast sense, conversion from the noun): "to spit" meat = to thread it on a spit; "spitted" = impaled. This verb belongs to the rod family, not the saliva family.
  • Homonymous verb (separate word): "to spit" = to eject saliva from the mouth; "spit it out"; "spit and polish" (military neatness); "spitting image" and "the very spit of" (exact likeness, from the old idea that one's spittle carried one's nature); "it's spitting" (light rain). This verb, from Old English spīwan / spǣtan, is etymologically a completely different word from the roasting rod — the pair is a classic accident of English homophony, unconnected since their separate Old English ancestors.

Historical bridge. All the noun's senses share one shape: long, slender, and pointed — the rod, the spade-blade, the tapering tongue of land. The word is the same as that for a "point", and its geography sense ("sand-spit") and its cookery sense ("spit-roast") are the two great survivors; the saliva verb, meanwhile, is an unrelated word that happens to have the same sound, and speakers have treated the pair as accidentally identical for over a thousand years.
