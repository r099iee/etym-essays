ivory (noun and adjective) — A dense, hard, fine-grained, creamy-white modified dentine tissue that constitutes the elongated tusks of elephants, mammoths, hippopotamuses, walruses, sperm whales, and narwhals; by extension, any carved decorative object made from this substance, or a warm, delicate creamy-white colour resembling the polished material.

Primary material and aesthetic properties. In material science and craft history, ivory is prized above ordinary vertebrate bone due to its exceptional microstructural density, absence of coarse vascular pores, and unique intersecting arc patterns (known as Schreger lines or "engine-turning" striations). These structural properties render it silky to the touch, capable of taking an exceptionally high polish, and remarkably amenable to intricate three-dimensional relief carving without splintering. Throughout antiquity and the Middle Ages, ivory stood alongside gold, gems, and silk as one of the ultimate luxury trade commodities.

Polysemic extensions and cultural metaphors:

  • Complexion and aesthetic pallor — In classical, Renaissance, and Victorian literature, ivory is a classic poetic epithet for aristocratic, bloodless, or love-stricken skin. In the reading context from Oscar Wilde's "The Nightingale and the Rose", the young scholar's face is described: "passion has made his face like pale ivory, and sorrow has set her seal upon his brow." Here, ivory conveys an ethereal, aestheticized, marble-like pallor born of intense romantic and intellectual suffering.
  • Ivory tower — A famous metaphorical idiom denoting an idealized state of intellectual, academic, or artistic detachment, seclusion, or unworldliness, isolated from the practical, gritty realities of everyday life. Coined in modern literature by French critic Charles Augustin Sainte-Beuve (1837) to describe the poetic withdrawal of Alfred de Vigny.
  • Musical colloquialism ("the ivories") — In musical slang, piano keys are traditionally referred to as "the ivories" (e.g., "to tickle the ivories", meaning to play the piano skillfully), derived from the historical practice of covering wooden piano keys with thin veneers of polished elephant ivory.
  • The Ivory Coast — The historical West African coastal region (now the Republic of Côte d'Ivoire), named during the European colonial mercantile era for the heavy trade in elephant tusks.
  • Billiard and gaming balls — Historically, the finest billiard balls, chess pieces, and dominoes were manufactured from solid ivory due to its uniform weight and elastic coefficient.
  • Colour denomination — In interior design, fashion, and paint manufacturing, ivory denotes an off-white shade featuring a subtle warm yellow undertone, softer and warmer than stark alabaster or pure chalk white.

Conservation, ethics, and legal standing. In contemporary international law and wildlife conservation, commercial trade in elephant ivory is strictly regulated and largely prohibited under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES, 1989 ban). Modern artisans and industries utilize ethical alternatives such as fossilized mammoth ivory, vegetable ivory (tagua nuts from the palm Phytelephas macrocarpa), and synthetic polymer resins (e.g., Elforyn and casein-based plastics).
