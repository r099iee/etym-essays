Planes in this context is the plural of the tool noun plane: a woodworking (originally also stone-working) hand tool with a flat sole and a protruding angled blade, pushed or pulled across a surface to shave off thin layers and leave it flat and smooth.

A typical bench plane has a long wooden or iron body, a flat bottom, a handle (tote) behind and knob in front, and an iron cutter wedged at about 45 degrees with its edge just proud of the sole. As it slides, it peels a continuous curling shaving. Variants include jack planes for rough levelling, trying planes for trueing long edges, smoothing planes for finish, rebate planes for steps, moulding planes for profiles, and block planes for end grain. Primitive stone planes were simply flat abrasive slabs dragged to grind down wood or bone.

  • Core joinery sense (source sense): Smoothing tools for wood. In the passage - "saws, planes, wedges..." - stone planes mean abraders that levelled wooden shafts or smoothed planks before metal existed.
  • Geometric / physical sense: A plane is a flat, two-dimensional surface (Euclidean plane, Cartesian plane) and by extension any flat level (plane of a table). In physics, the inclined plane is one of the simple machines, conceptually kin to wedge and lever.
  • Aviation clipping: A plane is short for aeroplane / airplane - a powered heavier-than-air aircraft. Plural planes then means airliners. This shares the flat-wing idea but is etymologically a separate clipping.
  • Botanical sense: Plane or plane tree (Platanus) is a tall urban tree with mottled bark - unrelated etymologically (from Greek platanos) but homographic.
  • Figurative senses: To plane means to level or smooth (to plane down objections); on the same plane means at the same level of thought. The adjective plain (flat, simple) is the cousin that kept the -i- spelling.

Technical domains sharpen the meaning. In carpentry, planing is distinct from scraping or sanding: it cuts fibres cleanly rather than abrading them, leaving a glossy, tear-free surface that takes polish. In masonry, a float or plane levels plaster. In metalwork, a planer is the machine-tool analogue. The semantic bridge from tool to geometry is flatness: the tool both has a flat sole and creates flatness; the geometric plane is idealised flatness itself.

Connotation is quiet competence and finish. Saws feel violent, hammers feel forceful, but planes feel meditative, precise, and civilising - the tool that turns rough timber into furniture, noise into whisper-thin curls. In plural, planes evokes a workbench row of wooden bodies, a museum case of Roman iron soles, or, confusingly for learners, a sky full of aircraft - a polysemy that must be disambiguated by context.

Finally, the maintenance ritual of planes teaches their philosophy: flatten the sole, hone the iron at 25 degrees with a 30-degree micro-bevel, set the cap iron hair-close, advance the iron until it kisses the wood, then take gossamer shavings you can read through. Owners who master this report a distinctive sound change from scraping chatter to a soft hiss. That hiss, prized in Japanese kanna as much as in English jack planes, is the acoustic signature of true flatness being born, and it links the Stone Age rubbing slab in the source list directly to the modern smoothing plane.
