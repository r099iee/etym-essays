STAMPED is a word whose whole biography can be read in its two modern lives — the foot slammed down in a percussive thud of feeling, and the die that presses an official mark onto paper or metal. Both descend from a single ancient root meaning "to support, place firmly," which in the Germanic branch of Indo-European took a nasalized form meaning "to pound, crush." The Proto-Indo-European base is usually reconstructed as *stebh- (with variant *stembh-), "to support, place firmly on, steady," a root with a wide and instructive family: Greek stembein means "to trample, tread down, misuse," Sanskrit stabhnāti means "he supports," whence stambha, "post, pillar," the ancestor of modern Hindi and Odia words for a pillar; and the same root gave English staff, a thing that supports. The coexistence of "support" and "tread down" within one root shows how the Indo-Europeans themselves connected firmness and force.

In the Germanic branch the root was nasalized — a -n- or -m- inserted before the final consonant — producing a stem *stamp-/*stemp- meaning "to pound, crush." The evidence is rich and consistent across the older Germanic languages: Old Norse stappa "to pound, stamp," Middle Dutch stampen, and Old High German stampfon, "to stamp with the foot, beat," whose modern descendant is German stampfen, "to stamp, pound." In Old English the verb appears as stæmpan or stempan, "to pound, crush, bray in a mortar" — the heavy pestle falling and rising in a rhythm of blows in every Anglo-Saxon kitchen and workshop. The verb and noun of pounding were among the most concrete words in the language, naming the everyday labor of grinding grain and crushing herbs.

Middle English inherited the crushing sense. Around 1200 stampen meant "to pound, crush, bray in a mortar," and the spelling and vowel show the influence of Scandinavian forms (Old Norse stappa) that reshaped the English word. From this pounding base the meaning extended, in the fourteenth century, from the pestle to the human body. By the mid-1300s the verb was used intransitively of striking the foot forcibly downward — to stamp in anger or impatience — and by the end of the century of treading heavily. The transitive sense, "to strike someone or something by a forcible downward thrust of the foot," followed around 1500. The modern noun stampe (mid-15th century) named the pounding or crushing tool; by the 1540s it named the instrument that impresses an official mark by pressure, and from the seal-maker's die came the postage stamp after 1840. Every step of this chain is the same physical idea: force applied through a flat surface leaves a mark.

The word crossed the Channel into Romance as well, carrying the imprint sense with it. Italian stampa means "stamp, impression, printing press," and Spanish estampar means "to stamp, print"; the same Spanish word-group produced estampida, "crash," borrowed into English as stampede in the 1830s for the panic-stricken rush of cattle — a descendant that still carries the ancient image of feet in violent, thudding motion. A separate English offshoot, stomp, is the heavier dialectal variant of stamp that rose especially in American English for an emphatic, violent foot-thud.

Socio-culturally, foot-stamping has always been the gesture of emphatic feeling — the audience's applause, the child's tantrum, the soldier's drill — and the word kept pace, moving from the mortar to the theater floor. The weak past tense STAMPED, formed with the ordinary -ed suffix, is thus the culmination of a chain running from "supporting post" through "pounding pestle" to "slammed-down foot" and "impressed mark." In the Great Goblin's hall the word lets Tolkien's mob beat out its glee in a single percussive word: laughter, stamping, and clapping become one rhythmic wall of mockery, and the reader feels the shuddering stone floor. The etymology guarantees the effect — the whole crushing weight of six millennia of pounding lies behind the goblins' stamped feet, and the word still carries both the noise and the imprint.
