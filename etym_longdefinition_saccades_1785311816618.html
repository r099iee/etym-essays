SACCADES is the plural of saccade, a noun denoting the rapid, jerky, ballistic movements of both eyes that abruptly shift the point of fixation from one location to another. A saccade is not a smooth glide: the eye jumps. In the course of a single saccade the eyeball is repositioned within a few tens of milliseconds — often too fast for the mover to watch — and the brain, in a process called saccadic suppression, briefly blanks out the visual input during the jump so that the world does not appear to smear. The purpose of the jump is to bring a new target of interest onto the fovea, the small central region of the retina where visual acuity is highest; between saccades the eye holds still in a period of fixation, during which detailed information is acquired. The word appears in the card’s sentence inside the compound micro-saccades: “Roughly 30% to 50% of the human cerebral cortex is dedicated to visual processing (V1 through V5, spatial orientation, edge detection, and micro-saccades).” Micro-saccades are the tiny, largely involuntary saccadic movements — only a few minutes of arc in amplitude — that the eyes make even when a person tries to hold a perfectly steady gaze on a single point; they continuously refresh the image on the retina and thereby prevent neural adaptation from making a stationary scene fade from view. In this scientific register the word is precise and mechanistic: a saccade is a discrete, jump-like event with a measurable amplitude, velocity, and latency.

Polysemy and the range of senses.

  • Ophthalmology and vision science (the core sense): the standard term for the rapid conjugate movements of the two eyes that relocate the line of sight; saccades are measured in eye-tracking research to study reading, visual search, and scene perception.
  • Neuroscience and psychophysics: saccades are generated by brainstem burst-generator circuits, supervised by the superior colliculus and the frontal eye fields, and their kinematics (latency, peak velocity, duration) are studied as indices of neural health and of attention.
  • Reading research: reading is accomplished by a sequence of saccades (roughly 7–9 letter-spaces long) separated by fixations of about 200–250 ms; reading-skill research, typography, and screen design all draw on saccade metrics.
  • Micro-saccades (the card’s compound): tiny fixational saccades that occur several times per second even during attempted fixation; they refresh the retinal image, combat neural adaptation, and have been linked to attention shifts and to the perception of binocular depth.
  • Pathology and clinical testing: abnormal saccades (too slow, too fast, dysmetric, or oscillatory) are diagnostic signs in neurology — in multiple sclerosis, cerebellar disease, Parkinson’s disease, and in the intrusions and oscillations of the saccadic system.
  • Figurative and technical extensions outside the eye: by analogy, engineers and computer scientists speak of “saccadic” attention, and the word is occasionally applied to any abrupt, jump-like redirection of gaze, focus, or sampling — for example in robotic vision, where cameras are moved in “saccades” to fixate on salient regions.

The historical bridge. The word’s jump from the stable to the laboratory is fully transparent: saccade is French for the sharp tug of a rider on a horse’s reins, a sudden, violent jerk that brings the mount to attention or redirects it. In the 1890s the French ophthalmologist Émile Javal, studying the movements of the eye during reading, recognised that the eye itself moves in exactly such abrupt jerks rather than in smooth glides, and he borrowed the equestrian word as a scientific label. The metaphor persists in the modern definition: a saccade is ballistic — once launched it cannot be corrected mid-flight — just as a tug on the reins is a single decisive pull rather than a modulated pressure. The plural saccades is the ordinary technical form in English, and its diminutive compound micro-saccades (from Greek micro-, ‘small’) names the smallest members of the same family.

Grammar, register, and collocations. Saccade is a countable noun (a saccade, the saccades); the plural saccades is used both for the individual movements in a sequence and for the class of movements as a whole (“the study of saccades”). The adjective is saccadic (saccadic suppression, saccadic eye movements), and the verb is rarely used. Technical collocations include saccadic latency, saccadic amplitude, saccadic velocity, saccadic suppression, conjugate saccades, micro-saccades, square-wave jerks, and anti-saccade (in tasks where a subject must suppress a reflexive glance). The register is coolly scientific: in vision science a saccade is never a “glance” or a “look,” which would import a casual, intentional flavour the word’s users carefully avoid. Saccades are contrasted within the typology of eye movements with smooth pursuit (continuous tracking of a moving target), vergence movements (opposite-direction rotations for near and far), the vestibulo-ocular reflex (stabilising the gaze against head movement), and fixation and drift (the pauses and slow wanderings between saccades).
