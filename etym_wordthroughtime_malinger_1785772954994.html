- early–mid 18th century — English borrows from French malingre ‘sickly’; initial contact still close to ‘ailing appearance,’ but military speech begins to specialize the form.
- late 18th century — malinger established in British army/navy slang and writing as ‘pretend sickness to avoid duty’; moral charge of cowardice/laziness attaches immediately.
- 19th century — regulations, court records, and medical officers normalize malingering as an offense and a clinical suspicion; civilian workplaces begin to adopt the charge; agent noun malingerer common.
- early 20th century / World Wars — mass conscription multiplies public and official use; shell shock and genuine trauma complicate the politics of the accusation (false positives become a historical controversy).
- mid–late 20th century — psychiatry and forensic medicine define malingering against factitious disorder and somatization; insurance and disability systems institutionalize testing for feigned impairment; word remains strongly accusatory in popular speech.
- 21st century — continues in HR, sports medicine, school attendance, and online discourse (‘malingering’ as insult for perceived laziness); public perception still: conscious faking of illness to get out of work or gain benefit—not ordinary rest, not proven disease.
