**From being struck to being amazed: STUPENDOUS and the stun-family** The biography of STUPENDOUS is really the biography of an idea about the mind: that astonishment is a blow. From its first Latin ancestor to its modern colloquial uses, the word has carried the image of a person struck so hard that the mind stops working, gapes, and stands still. **The borrowing of the seventeenth century** Stupendous enters English in the 1650s, in the generation of the English Civil War and Commonwealth, taken directly from the Late Latin gerundive stupendus, "that is to be wondered at," formed from the verb stupere, "to be struck senseless, to be stunned, to be paralyzed with astonishment." A Latin gerundive expresses obligation or worthiness, so stupendus literally means "such as must be marvelled at" — the passive, object-centred quality is built into the grammar. The Romans themselves used stupendus sparingly, mainly in elevated or poetic prose; it is precisely this impersonal grandeur, with the emphasis falling not on the observer but on the object that forces wonder, that English preserved. From the first, an English stupendous thing was not a thing one merely admired; it was a thing at which one was obliged to be stunned. **The Latin family: one blow, many outcomes** The verb stupere belongs to a family of Indo-European words that grew around the root *steu-, "to push, strike, knock," recorded by Pokorny. The same root, with various extensions, underlies Latin tundere, "to beat, pound" (whence English contusion, literally "a beating together"), and the Germanic verb reflected in Middle Low German stoten, "to thrust," and in English stutter, whose stammering was conceived as a repeated striking of the same sound. The basic mental image is physical: a blow that knocks one senseless. When the Romans said that a man stupet, they meant that he stood motionless and gaping, as though struck; this is why the same verb produced stupor, "numbness" (English stupor), stupefaciō, "to stun" (English stupefy and stupefaction), and stupidus, "stunned, then dull-witted" (English stupid). Astonishment and stupidity were, in Latin, two possible outcomes of the same blow: a man knocked senseless might be overwhelmed with wonder, or he might simply be, as we still say, struck stupid. **The Romance sisters and the drift of wonder** In the Romance languages the family prospered. Italian stupendo and Spanish estupendo preserved the gerundive directly and mean simply "marvellous, wonderful," having lost the insistence on overwhelming magnitude: in these languages the everyday word for "great!" is the descendant of a word that once meant "to be wondered at." French preferred the same family through a different channel, in étonner (from extonare, "to thunder out") and in ébahir, so that French never adopted stupendus itself. English alone kept the gerundive's full claim, and the difference is instructive: where Romance softened the blow into general admiration, English preserved the original violence of being stunned by size. **Magnitude enters the meaning** In its first English uses, stupendous meant simply "astonishing, wonderful" — a synonym of the marvellous. But a semantic shift began almost at once: because the most stunning things are also the largest, the word began to fuse wonder with magnitude. By the eighteenth century it was applied to stupendous mountains, stupendous cliffs, stupendous efforts and stupendous undertakings, and the association with sheer scale hardened. The nineteenth century was the word's golden age. Victorian biography, travel writing, and popular science all reached for stupendous to describe natural wonders and engineering marvels — stupendous waterfalls, stupendous railway cuttings, stupendous feats of endurance — and it is in this register that the sentence about Mill does its work: "His industry was stupendous." The brevity is the point. A Victorian biographer measuring an entire lifetime of prodigious intellectual labour in a single laconic clause is performing awe, not exaggerating; the understatement makes the claim feel like awed testimony. **Dilution and survival** In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word has been diluted by enthusiastic overuse. "Stupendous" appears in advertising and journalism as a mere intensifier — "a stupendous bargain," "stupendous sales," "a stupendous party" — and in this loose colloquial register it loses almost all of its paralyzing wonder. Yet the older force survives intact in formal and dramatic prose, and the word still carries its etymological memory. A thing that is truly stupendous still stuns: it is so vast, so powerful, so extraordinary that the mind is briefly knocked senseless — struck, as the Romans said, the way a blow strikes a man, and left standing still and gaping in front of it. In that single word, the whole history of a metaphor is preserved: astonishment is a blow, and stupendous is the size of the fist.
