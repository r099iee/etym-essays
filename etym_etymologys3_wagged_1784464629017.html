The past form wagged belongs to one of the most ordinary English verbs there is — wag — and its story is the story of a tiny Germanic motion-word that has wagged its way through a thousand years of English and into a rich set of idioms.

The deep Indo-European root

At the deepest reconstructable layer stands Proto-Indo-European *weǧh-, “to go, move, transport, convey” — the root of a whole travel-family: English way, wagon, weigh (originally “to lift, carry on the way”), wain, and Latin vehere “to carry” (hence vehicle). The root's core idea is movement from one place to another, usually by carrying or traveling. In Proto-Germanic this root gave both the noun *wagnaz “wagon” (a thing that carries) and a verbal stem *wag- specialized toward a different kind of movement: not journeying but swaying — the back-and-forth rocking of something loosely held or attached. The semantic bridge is easy to imagine: a moving wagon rocks; the general “move” of the root narrows to “sway, shake, rock.” This Germanic specialization is reconstructed as *wagōną, “to sway, rock, move to and fro.”

Old English: wagian

Old English wagian meant “to move, shake, totter, fluctuate.” It appears in Old English prose and verse in contexts of unsteady movement — things that quake, wobble, and sway. Already the word was a humble, physical motion-verb of the everyday world, quite distinct from the grand verbs of deliberate travel that its cousin-root produced elsewhere. The doublet pattern is instructive: the same PIE root sent way and wagon down the “journey” path and wag down the “shake” path — two faces of movement.

Middle English: waggen

Middle English waggen (attested by c. 1300) continues the sense: to swing, wobble, or move briskly to and fro. In the medieval period the word is used of swinging things, of swaying motions, and — crucially — the idiomatic life begins. Scandinavian contact is often cited as a reinforcement: Old Norse vagga meant both “to rock (a cradle)” and “a cradle,” a North Germanic parallel that may have strengthened the English verb's rocking, side-to-side image.

Modern English: the verb and its idioms

  • The core physical sense survives unchanged: tails, fingers, heads, beards, and tongues all wag. The regular weak past wagged is attested as soon as the verb takes its modern shape, and it has been fully ordinary since — no archaism attaches to the form itself.
  • Gossip: “to wag one's tongue” and “tongues wag” are attested in Early Modern English — the image of the tongue moving quickly back and forth like a tail becomes the standard idiom for gossip. To “set tongues wagging” is to provoke talk.
  • The joker: by the 17th–18th centuries “a wag” is a witty, jesting person — one whose tongue and body are always in lively motion — and “to play the wag” means to act the merry prankster. The noun is now archaic or literary, but it was a living word for two centuries.
  • The truant: in 19th-century British school slang, “to play the wag” (to play about instead of attending) produced “to wag school” and “wag” as a truant; the usage survives especially in Australian and New Zealand English (“to wag it”).
  • The tail wagging the dog: the great 19th-century American idiom (popularized in 20th-century politics) imagines a minor element — the tail — directing the major one. It plays on the absurdity of the dog being wagged by its own tail, the perfect inversion of the word's small-lively-motion core.

Sound and form

Phonetically the verb is unremarkable and stable: Old English wagian → Middle English waggen → modern wag, with the weak past wagged formed by the ordinary -ed suffix. No sound change of note interrupts the line; the interest of the word is entirely semantic, in the parade of idioms built on the same tiny image of quick, repetitive motion.

In The Hobbit

Tolkien's sentence — “the shadow of Gandalf's beard wagged against the wall” — uses the verb in its plainest physical sense, and that is exactly right. In the firelit April dark of Bag End, while the dwarves play on, the wizard's long-bearded silhouette keeps time on the plaster: a homely, comic, warm detail that grounds the epic singing in domestic reality. Wagged is the perfect verb because its whole semantic history — quick, short, rhythmic, faintly funny motion — is compressed into the bobbing shadow of a beard keeping time with music and breath. The idiom-family of wag (gossip, jest, truancy, inversion) all hang unspoken behind that one little past-tense wagging shadow.
