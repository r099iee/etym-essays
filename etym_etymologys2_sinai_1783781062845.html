Origin of the name uncertain; no Indo-European root. The attested transmission: Biblical Hebrew proper noun Sînay (סִינַי), "the mountain of the Law," and the surrounding wilderness (Exodus 19–20, c. 9th–6th c. BCE strata). → Ancient Greek Σινᾶ Sina / Σινά Siná (Septuagint transliteration, 3rd c. BCE). → Latin Sinai / Sina (Jerome's Vulgate, c. 405 CE). → English SINAI (entering English c. 1300 through the Vulgate and Anglo-Norman; standard form in all English Bibles). Debated / nuanced: the etymology of the Hebrew name itself is genuinely unsettled, and any reconstruction before the Hebrew form is guesswork. The chief proposals are (1) derivation from the Semitic moon-god Sîn (Mesopotamian lunar deity of Ur and Harran), making Sinai "the mountain of Sîn," reinforced by the nearby "wilderness of Sin" (Exodus 16:1); (2) derivation from a Semitic root meaning "clay, mud" (Arabic ṭīn), giving "the place of clay"; (3) derivation from šinn-, "tooth," giving "the toothed, notched peak," suited to the serrated granite summits; (4) folk-etymology from Hebrew seneh, the burning bush of Exodus 3. Modern scholarship judges none certain; some specialists suspect a pre-Hebrew (Amorite or Egyptian) substratum behind the name. State explicitly: the word's ultimate origin is unknown; what is certain is only its transmission from Hebrew through Greek and Latin to English.
