On the wild path below the Misty Mountains, gullies are the narrow, steep-sided channels that melt-water and storm-rain have cut down through the crumbling slope. The phrase 'that they could almost leap over' establishes the word's signature paradox: a gully can be treacherously narrow in width yet appallingly deep, 'very deep with waterfalls in them'. The noun names a specific scale of landform — smaller than a valley, larger than a groove, carved by running water rather than by a permanent river — and in this passage it contributes a mounting sense of obstacle and risk. Each gully is a wound in the terrain that the Company must either jump or laboriously work around, and the 'waterfalls in them' turn what looks like a stride into a deadly fall: the very narrowness that tempts the traveller is what hides the depth until it is too late. The plural gullies multiplies the hazard, scattering the ascent with concealed chasms, and the tone is matter-of-fact but quietly menacing — the narrator reporting danger in the same level voice he uses for weather, understatement that makes the menace worse.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The noun gully and its plural gullies have a compact but real range of meanings:

  • Geographical core sense: a narrow channel or ravine worn in the earth by running water, typically on a hillside, dry except during rain — the sense here.
  • Historical water sense: in older English, a 'gully' (spelled 'gulley') could also mean a narrow channel of water or a small gutter; closely related to 'gullet', the throat, via the image of a narrow passage.
  • Sporting/technical senses: in cricket, 'gully' is the fielding position between the slips and point, so called from the narrow 'guilty' gap it covers (the image is again of a narrow cleft); in mining and quarrying, a gully is a channel cut for water drainage.
  • American idiomatic use: 'gully-washer' (1887) is an Americanism for a heavy rainstorm that scours gullies in the earth — the word's most vivid modern offshoot.
  • Metaphoric extension: by analogy, 'gully' can name any deep narrow gap — between buildings, in ice, in a reef — though the topographic sense dominates.

Nuance against near-synonyms. The differentiating words sharpen the image. A ravine is far larger and steeper-walled — too wide to leap and too sheer to climb — and implies a major torrent; gorge and canyon are grander still, with canyon carrying overtones of the American Southwest and the Spanish cañón, 'tube, pipe'. Gulch is a New World variant of roughly gully's meaning. Arroyo names a desert watercourse, usually dry, and wadi an Arabian one, while ditch and furrow are made by human hands. Gullies is thus the precise mid-scale word for water-carved channels on a wild mountain flank — narrow enough to tempt a leap, abrupt enough to kill — and the sound of falling water inside them makes the risk immediate and audible.
