Middle Low German plunder (n.) "household goods, clothing, baggage; lumber, odds and ends" (attested from the 14th–15th centuries in the Low Countries and northern Germany) → Middle High German plunder (n.) "household stuff, clothes, baggage" (14th century) → Middle High German plundern / German plündern (v.) "to take away household goods; to strip of one's possessions; to sack, loot" (from the 14th–16th centuries; the modern German form with umlaut, plündern, became the standard verb) → continental West Germanic parallel verbs — Dutch plunderen, Low German plunderen (v.) "to plunder" (attested from the 15th–16th centuries; they reinforced the continental pattern) → English plunder (v.) "to take goods by force, especially from a captured place; to loot" (borrowed from the German/Low German war vocabulary during the Thirty Years' War era, 1630s) and plunder (n.) "goods taken by force" (1640s) → English PLUNDERING (present participle, gerund, and adjective) — the card's target form: the ongoing act of stripping a place of its movable goods, or the agent of such stripping. NOTE on the debate: the ultimate origin of the continental noun is unknown — no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed, and this is a rare case where an honest etymology must stop at a West Germanic noun of obscure deeper history. The noun is often linked to the idea of "lumber, trash, odds and ends" (the goods of a poor household), and some handbooks note that the German verb plündern may have been shaped by or mixed with Middle Dutch plunderen; the direction of borrowing between the German and Dutch forms is not fully settled, though the English verb clearly came through the German/Low German Thirty Years' War vocabulary. Do not attach plunder to Latin plumbum "lead" or to any PIE "swim/float" root — such links are folk-etymology.
