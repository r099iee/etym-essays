Sophocles (proper noun) — The ancient Athenian tragedian Sophocles (c. 496–406 BC), one of the three great masters of Greek tragedy alongside Aeschylus and Euripides, author of the seven surviving plays including Oedipus Rex, Antigone, and Electra, and the dramatist credited with introducing the third actor and perfecting dramatic irony. Primary historical senses:
  • The tragedian: Sophocles was the most successful playwright of the great age of Athenian drama, winning some twenty-four victories at the City Dionysia and never finishing lower than second. Seven of his plays survive intact: Ajax, Antigone, Women of Trachis, Oedipus Rex, Electra, Philoctetes, and Oedipus at Colonus. His innovations — the introduction of the third actor, the reduction of the chorus, and the deepening of the individual tragic hero — transformed tragedy from choral ritual into character drama.
  • The canonical dramatist: Aristotle, in the Poetics, made his Oedipus Rex the model tragedy, and through antiquity Sophocles stood as the very summit of Greek dramatic art; the ancient Lives say he was called the "bee" of Attica for the sweetness of his style.
  • The name: Sophoklēs is a compound Greek personal name meaning "famous for wisdom" or "glory of wisdom," from sophós "wise" and kléos "fame, glory" — a speaking name of the standard Greek aristocratic type.
Secondary and transferred meanings:
  • The Oedipus complex: In psychology, the "Oedipus complex" of Freud's psychoanalysis is named after Sophocles' hero Oedipus, whose play the audience knows he has unknowingly killed his father and married his mother; the term has kept the dramatist's name in general currency in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.
  • Benchmark of tragedy: In dramatic criticism, "Sophoclean" denotes the classical tragic structure of hamartia, hubris, and catastrophic recognition, and the name is used as the measure of tragic art against which later playwrights are placed.
  • Fate and hubris: In literary and popular culture, Sophocles stands for the tragedy of fate — the hero crushed by prophecy and the gods — and for the theme that the great man's fall comes from his own flaw; the phrase "tragic flaw" itself is a modern coinage built on Aristotle's reading of Sophoclean heroes.
  • Eponyms and technical uses: The adjective Sophoclean, the cycle titles (Oedipus cycle, Theban plays), and the institutional name of the Sophoclean canon keep the name alive in education, theatre history, and classical scholarship.
Semantic bridge: From the transparent Greek compound "fame of wisdom" (sophós "wise" + kléos "fame, glory"), the name was borne by a playwright whose wisdom, in the Greek sense of skill and insight, won him unending fame; the metonymy then moved from the historical person to tragic perfection itself, to the Oedipus complex of psychoanalysis, and to the general ideal of the tragic art. In the source text: Macaulay names Sophocles as the first of his triad of Greek masterpieces — "the masterpieces of Sophocles, of Demosthenes, and of Plato" — still being read and interpreted in the decaying Byzantine world. The name here stands for the summit of Greek dramatic poetry, preserved as a school-text among pedants "destitute of taste, sense, and spirit," a civilisation's greatest art reduced to grammatical exercise. Register and collocation: Sophocles is a formal proper noun of literature and history, invariable and singular. It collocates with the tragedies of Sophocles, Oedipus Rex, Antigone, the Theban plays, the Sophoclean chorus, and the phrase "one of the three great tragedians"; the adjectives Sophoclean and the modern everyday phrase "a Sophoclean fate" (an inescapable, self-caused catastrophe) are learned and rare.
