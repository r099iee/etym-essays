Cornucopia is a Latin phrase turned English noun, fusing two venerable roots into a single image of endless giving. The first element, Latin cornu horn, descends from PIE *ḱer- horn, head, topmost part — source of Greek keras horn, keratos, English keratin, rhinoceros nose-horn, unicorn one-horn, capricorn goat-horn, and Germanic *hurnaz horn. Horns mattered: they were weapons, trumpets, drinking cups, and symbols of power; to be horned was to be potent. The second element, copiae (genitive singular of copia), joins intensive prefix co- together + ops, opis power, means, wealth, help — from PIE *h₃ep- / *op- to work, produce, bring forth — source of opus work, opera, opulent wealthy, optimus best, and goddess Ops Plenty herself, consort of Saturn. Copia thus originally meant together-power: resources pooled, then abundance in general; plural copiae even meant troops, supplies.

Greek myth supplied the narrative that Latin named. The infant Zeus, hidden from Cronus, was nursed by the goat Amalthea on Crete; one horn broke off and was blessed to fill unceasingly with food and drink — the nurse’s horn. Hellenistic poets (Callimachus, Ovid Fasti 5, Metamorphoses 9 where Heracles wrenches Achelous’s horn) wove variants: Zeus sets the goat among stars as Capricorn; nymphs fill the horn; river-gods wear it. Romans visualized cornu copiae on denarii of Fortuna holding rudder and horn, on altars of Abundantia, Concordia, and Annona (grain supply), promising that empire meant no hunger. The phrase appears in Plautus, Cicero, and Livy both literally and proverbially for inexhaustible source.

Medieval Latin kept the phrase in bestiaries and Ovidian moralizations; Renaissance humanists revived it as artists’ motif — Raphael, Cellini, printers’ devices curling horns around title-pages to promise intellectual plenty. English first borrows cornucopia as a solid foreign word c. 1500–1510 (in Hawes, Elyot) for the classical emblem, then naturalizes pronunciation to /kˌɔːrnjuːkˈəʊpiə/ with yod and secondary stress. American Thanksgiving (19th century) domesticated it as wicker basket spilling pumpkins and maize — horn of plenty as table pun — while economists (Malthus vs cornucopians) and editors (a cornucopia of verse) made it metaphor for inexhaustible supply versus scarcity, exactly as in this cornucopia [anthology] is high in level.

  • Phonology: Latin cornū cōpiae /ˈkor.nuː koːˈpi.ae̯/ → Late Latin univerbation cornucopia → English with Great Vowel Shift and yod-coalescence /kɔːnjuːkəʊpiə/; spelling -ae plural gives learned cornucopiae.
  • Morphology: noun + genitive noun phrase fused to feminine first-declension noun; both elements remain productive: corn- in cornet, corner, cornucopian, cop- in copious, copy (originally abundance of transcripts).
  • Semantic drift: mythic goat horn → state propaganda of prosperity → decorative harvest symbol → abstract inexhaustible source → praise-term for rich collections; never pejorative, always celebratory.
  • Cultural load: state seals, bank architecture, Thanksgiving kitsch, and literary blurbs all trade on instant legibility: curve + overflow = generosity without effort.

To speak of a cornucopia of writing is thus to invoke goat, goddess, and horn in one breath — classical myth condensed into modern praise for editorial plenty.
