SHEATHS: The plural of sheath — close-fitting protective cases, coverings, or casings that enclose a blade, tool, instrument, cable, or biological structure. The defining idea of a sheath, against all its near-synonyms, is that the covering clings to what it covers: it is moulded to the object, drawn over it like a skin, not a box that the object sits in. The singular and plural belong to one word-family: sheath (noun), sheathe (verb, "to put into a sheath; to enclose; to retract claws"), sheathing (the covering layer), and unsheathed ("drawn, exposed"). Nuance in this context. In the card's sentence — "The knights drew their swords from leather sheaths, the sound ringing through the courtyard" — the plural noun operates at the heart of a deliberately archaic, martial tableau. The detail "leather" roots the word in its oldest material reality — a flexible cover stitched to follow a blade — while "drew ... from" and "ringing" conjure the medieval ceremony of the sword being freed from its scabbard. The tone is heroic and slightly romanticized, the vocabulary of chivalric fiction rather than of a museum catalogue; the sheath is here not merely a container but a ritual object, the place where violence is safely stored before it is unleashed. The plural matters: a row of knights, a row of sheaths, a courtyard filled with the collective sound of steel leaving leather. Why "leather sheaths" is so resonant. Historically the finest swords — the Viking sword, the knightly longsword — travelled in wooden scabbards wrapped in leather, often tipped with a metal chape, while the everyday knife lived in a soft leather sheath sewn to follow its outline. The card's detail therefore reaches for the humble, intimate covering rather than the ceremonial scabbard, and "leather" quietly supplies the sensory texture — the creak of the strap, the smell of oiled hide, the grip of the pocket on the blade. The whole scene is a small symphony of material culture, and "sheaths" is the word that lets it be so.
  • Sense 1 — blade coverings (core, oldest sense): the leather, wood, or metal case for a sword, dagger, or knife; the English word's original meaning, attested from Old English scēaþ. A sheath is typically soft (leather) or split wood, in contrast to the reinforced scabbard.
  • Sense 2 — covers for tools and instruments: "the scalpel's plastic sheath," "a chisel in its sheath" — the everyday protective tube for any pointed or delicate tool.
  • Sense 3 — anatomy: enveloping membranes and layers. "myelin sheath" (the fatty layer around nerve fibres), "tendon sheath" (the membrane tube around a tendon), the fascial sheath around muscles and organs — the biological sense, where a protective envelope clings to the structure it insulates.
  • Sense 4 — botany: the leaf sheath. In grasses, sedges, and palms, the basal part of a leaf wraps around the stem like a sleeve; "the blade and sheath of a grass leaf" is standard botanical terminology.
  • Sense 5 — engineering and everyday technology: "cable sheath," "wire sheathing" — the insulating or protective layer around electrical conductors, optical fibres, or hoses.
  • Sense 6 — the verb sheathe (closely related): "to put (a sword) into a sheath"; figuratively "to cover or enclose" ("trees sheathed in ice"); of animals, "to retract" (a cat sheathes its claws); in construction, sheathing boards cover a frame.
  • Sense 7 — historical euphemism: in older English, "a sheath" was a plain term for a condom — a close-fitting protective covering — recorded from the 17th century; the protective logic is identical to the blade-cover sense.
The semantic bridge. All these senses hang on one root idea: a sheath is a split-off piece — the word descends from a PIE root meaning "to cut, split," and the first sheaths were pieces of wood split or hollowed to grip a blade. The image of the splitting that separates the blade from the world explains everything: the covering that separates a nerve from its neighbours (myelin sheath), the sleeve of grass that separates the young stem from the elements (leaf sheath), the insulation that separates a live cable from human touch (cable sheath). In each case the sheath is a boundary, a case that clings and insulates. Register and usage. In the martial and craft registers the word is concrete and slightly archaic — the language of armouries, museums, and historical fiction. In science and engineering it is precise and technical. The plural sheaths is pronounced /ʃiːðz/ — note the voiced final consonant — and is the regular plural of sheath (like path/paths, mouth/mouths). Related compounds: scabbard (see below), sheath-knife (a knife worn in a sheath), sheath dress (a close-fitting, straight dress, named for its clinging fit — a striking modern figurative extension of the same idea).
