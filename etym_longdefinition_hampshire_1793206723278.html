Hampshire is, in its primary modern sense, a historic and ceremonial county on the south coast of England, with Winchester — the ancient capital of Wessex and of England — as its county town, and Southampton and Portsmouth as its great maritime cities. Chalk downland, the New Forest, and a long Channel shoreline give it some of the most written-about English landscape anywhere, from Cobbett's corn country to Austen's lanes.

In the source sentence the young Cobbett rides to Uphusband near Andover, in the north-west corner of Hampshire, on the first of the journeys that became Rural Rides: the county is his native heath and his measuring-rod for all England, the place whose farmers and labourers he means to see with his own eyes rather than through the reports of landlords and pamphleteers.

Core meaning in detail: The historic county — often shortened to Hants on envelopes and signposts — runs from the Berkshire downs to the Solent, taking in the New Forest, the South Downs edge, the Test and Itchen chalk streams, and the naval harbour towns of Portsmouth, Gosport, and Southampton. The modern ceremonial county keeps the name while the two cities stand as separate unitary authorities; the cricket club, the regiment, and the pig all carry it further still.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The English county (primary sense, from the 10th c.): the shire of the Hamtun district, recorded as Hantescire in Domesday. The bridge is administrative endurance: a Saxon district becomes a Norman shire, a Tudor county, and a modern postcode, never once renamed.
  • Hampshire County Cricket Club (sporting metonymy, from 1863): as in a Hampshire victory at the Rose Bowl. The bridge is the club that bears the county's name and colours, so that Hampshire can mean eleven men in a single afternoon.
  • The Hampshire pig (agricultural breed, 19th c.): a black American breed with a white belt, named for the English county of its supposed origin. The bridge is the county's fame for hogs and bacon — the proverbial Hampshire hog — exported with the settlers and fixed in a breed standard.
  • Ships named Hampshire (naval metonymy): notably HMS Hampshire, the cruiser sunk in 1916 with Lord Kitchener aboard. The bridge is the Royal Navy's habit of christening warships for counties, so the landlocked shire sails and sinks at sea.
  • New Hampshire and Hampshires abroad (transferred sense): the American state and scattered townships christened by emigrants. The bridge is homesickness made administrative: settlers plant the old name in new soil, adding New where pride requires it.

Register and connotation: Hampshire sounds deep-rural yet establishment — chalk streams, thatch, admirals, and Austen — the England of cricket whites and cathedral closes rather than mills and mines. Cobbett loved it as the honest corn county betrayed by fundholders and tax-eaters; the Navy prizes it as the county that guards the Channel; the novelist reaches for it whenever a heroine needs three miles of beech avenue. Abbreviated Hants, it keeps the Domesday -scire taboo of the post office: never write Hampshire-shire, for the -shire is already inside.
