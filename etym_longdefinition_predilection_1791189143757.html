Predilection is a noun denoting a settled preference, partiality, or special liking for something, often one that is habitual, slightly idiosyncratic, and indulged with quiet pleasure. To have a predilection for early mornings, for detective fiction, or for a particular football club is to be predictably drawn to it, to favour it over alternatives without necessarily condemning those alternatives. The word carries a literate, mildly formal register; it dignifies a taste by presenting it as a bent of judgment rather than a passing whim or crude appetite.

  • Core sense: habitual preference: A predisposition to like or choose one thing above others, e.g. a predilection for red wine, for solitude, for irony. It implies duration and recurrence, not a single choice. One does not have a predilection for a meal just eaten once; one has it when one repeatedly seeks it out.
  • Predilection for versus predilection toward: The canonical collocation is predilection for + noun/gerund (a predilection for adventure). Predilection toward(s) and in favour of occur with persons or policies. The construction predilection to + infinitive is rarer and often criticised as unidiomatic.
  • Benign partiality vs. bias: Unlike bias, prejudice, or partisanship, which imply unfairness and harm, predilection is normally benign or affectionately tolerant. A judge's predilection would be improper, but a friend's predilection for puns is endearing. Context can darken it, as when a passionate predilection drives excess.
  • Psychological and behavioural use: In psychology, criminology, and medicine, predilection denotes a non-deterministic vulnerability or leaning: a predilection for risk-taking, for addiction, for violence, for allergic response. It stops short of compulsion or pathology, marking elevated likelihood shaped by temperament, learning, or physiology.
  • Aesthetic and intellectual taste: Criticism uses predilection for an artist's recurrent choices: Hitchcock's predilection for icy blondes, Montaigne's predilection for digression, a chef's predilection for acidity. Here it explains oeuvre coherence and becomes a tool of authorship attribution and stylistic analysis.
  • Theological and philosophical residue: Historically predilection meant God's special fore-love for the elect or a saint's preferential love of virtue. This survives in phrases like divine predilection and in Catholic theology of predilection, where it still means gracious preference, not mere taste.
  • Predilection vs. penchant, proclivity, propensity, preference: Preference is neutral and momentary; penchant is livelier and more whimsical, often French-flavoured; proclivity and propensity lean toward undesirable or morally suspect inclinations and collocate with toward; predilection is warmer, more established, and more forgivable, typically introduced by for and softened by adjectives like lifelong, marked, well-known, unfortunate.
  • Countability and gradability: Usually countable (a predilection, predilections) but also used mass-wise (much predilection). It grades with strong, passionate, decided, pronounced, peculiar, unfortunate and negates with no predilection, without predilection to signal impartiality.

In Will Durant's sentence the taste once formed survived the shortage, and became a passionate predilection, the word performs ironic elevation. Cannibal or blood-drinking custom, described elsewhere as savage, is granted the language of connoisseurship, as if preferring blood were like preferring claret. The adjective passionate intensifies what is usually a cool noun, producing an oxymoronic tension between polite partiality and vehement appetite. Modern readers should hear both senses: a durable liking that persists after its original necessity has vanished, and a cultural judgment that what began as hunger has hardened into cherished habit. To master predilection is to recognise when a preference has become part of identity, defended with affection rather than argument.
