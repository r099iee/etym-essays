To expatiate is, etymologically, to step outside the enclosure and walk freely. The English verb was borrowed in the seventeenth century from Latin exspatiari (also written expatiari), a compound of the prefix ex-, 'out of,' and the verb spatiari, 'to walk about, to take a stroll.' That verb belonged to the vocabulary of the Roman park and the colonnade: spatium meant 'open space, extent, room,' and specifically the broad walking-spaces and racecourses of the Roman world, and spatiari described the leisurely pacing of the man of leisure in the shady porticoes. Latin authors were the first to bend the physical image to rhetoric: a writer or speaker who exspatiatur steps outside the narrow track of his subject and roams at large over the surrounding territory — the standard description of the formal rhetorical digression, as when a river exspatiatur, overflowing its banks across the plain.

The English expatiate therefore entered the language in 1650 with a spatial, almost geographic sense, 'to wander, to roam abroad'; the modern sense, 'to speak or write at length and in detail,' developed in the mid-eighteenth century, and the present participle expatiating settled into the polished essay prose of the age. The semantic bridge is transparent: to wander freely over a physical landscape became to wander freely over a subject, ranging at large through all its parts. The word belongs to a family that reached English in two waves:

  • Via Old French espace, Latin spatium gave English space (c. 1300) — 'extent, room' — and from the same stem the adjectives spacious (via Old French spacieux, from Latin spatiosus 'ample') and spatial (17th century) were formed.
  • The Romance languages kept the whole family alive: Italian spazio 'space' and spaziare 'to roam, to range, to expatiate,' Spanish and Portuguese espacio, French espace and espacement 'spacing' — all direct descendants of the same Latin material.
  • In English the verb produced the noun expatiation 'the act of discoursing at length' and the agent noun expatiator 'one who expatiates,' both rare but attested.

Honesty requires noting that the deeper prehistory of spatium is obscure: the traditional handbooks suggest an Indo-European root *spe(i)-, 'to spread, to expand,' which would connect spatium to such words as Greek spáō 'to draw, pull out' and Old Norse spá, but the connection is not secure, and cautious etymologists leave spatium as an Italic form of uncertain origin. The word must also be protected from a false friend: expatriate 'to drive out of one's country' is a different compound, ex- + patria 'fatherland,' and has nothing to do with walking.

The nineteenth century was the golden age of the word in English biography and essay prose, and it is in exactly that register that John Stuart Mill's biographer uses it: Mill 'was never weary of expatiating on all the advantages of so singular a relationship.' The phrase 'was never weary of' completes the spatial metaphor — the topic is an open ground the speaker never tires of crossing. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word has retreated from everyday speech, surviving in formal and literary registers, but it has never lost the image that gave it life: to expatiate is still, underneath, to walk out of the narrow course and range freely across the whole subject with relish.
