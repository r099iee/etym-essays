Within the strict hierarchy of the Jia household in Dream of the Red Chamber, the people the text calls chaperones are not casual escorts but older, trusted nurses charged with guarding the moral conduct, safety, and propriety of the young masters and mistresses. The staffing arrangement — that each child is to have four other nurses to act as chaperones, plus maids as body-servants — places the word inside a world where status is measured by layers of human protection. The tone is institutional and domestic-Confucian: chaperones enforce boundaries of contact, reputation, and physical care. They are living rules of propriety, mobile hoods of supervision around privileged children, at once servants and the visible conscience of the nursery. The word is used in the plural here because it names a category of staff, not one person, and that plural form (chaperones) is the card's lemma. In ordinary modern English the core sense of chaperone is 'a person who accompanies and looks after another person or group of people,' with a strong historical emphasis on a trusted adult — traditionally an older, respectable woman — who escorts and supervises a younger, especially unmarried, woman in public so as to protect her reputation and enforce conventions of propriety. From that social core the word extends to any adult who supervises young people in a semi-formal setting: teachers who chaperone a school trip, parents on a dance chaperone rota, group leaders on an outing. The verb to chaperone is fully standard, and the noun is used both countably ('a chaperone') and as a plural group noun ('chaperones'), exactly as in the source sentence. The word is richly polysemous in modern English. The main senses and their historical bridges:
  • Moral guardian of reputation (primary sense): the adult escort who protects a younger person's reputation and enforces propriety in courtship and public life (e.g., 'she acted as chaperone for her younger sister at the dance'). This sense arose by metaphor from the hood: as a hood covers the head, so a chaperone 'covers' and screens another's reputation.
  • Generalised supervision sense: any responsible adult in charge of the safety and conduct of a group of young people, as in 'parental chaperones on a school trip' or 'school-trip chaperones'. This is the most common modern usage and is fully gender-neutral.
  • Medical sense (professional-ethics jargon): a 'medical chaperone' is a trained third party present during an intimate clinical examination to protect both patient and clinician; this specialised usage dates from the late 20th century.
  • Biochemical sense (modern scientific jargon): a 'molecular chaperone' is a protein that assists other proteins to fold correctly and prevents aggregation; this purely metaphorical extension of the protective-guide image has been current in molecular biology since the 1980s.
  • Historical literal sense (now obsolete): in the 14th–17th centuries chaperon meant a hood or cap, a head-covering worn by knights and later by women; the social sense developed by metaphor from this garment.
  • Verb sense: to chaperone, 'to accompany and supervise,' with the forms chaperoning and chaperoned; the agent noun chaperoner and the abstract noun chaperonage (the practice or system of supervision) also exist, chiefly in formal or historical writing.
The historical bridge is transparent and worth spelling out. The word begins as cloth — a hood or cape that covers the head — and moves by metaphor to a person who 'covers' another's reputation. Just as a hood shelters the head, a chaperone shelters the charge from scandal, gossip, and impropriety; the semantic shift from garment to guardian, visible already in 16th-century French, is why the same word can name both a medieval hood and a modern supervisor. Related household roles differ sharply: a guardian holds legal or long-term responsibility; an escort is temporary and non-moral; a governess educates; a bodyguard protects against violence; a duenna is the culturally specific Spanish/Romance older-woman escort. The chaperone alone combines accompaniment with the policing of conduct and reputation. Register is formal-to-neutral: the word is at home in historical fiction, etiquette discussion, education administration, and clinical guidance, and it is slightly old-fashioned in everyday speech, where 'supervisor' or 'adult in charge' sometimes replaces it. Common collocations: act as a chaperone, chaperone duties, school-trip chaperones, without a chaperone, her nurse acted as chaperone, chaperone policy (medical), molecular chaperone. Note the alternative older spelling chaperon (without final -e), still occasionally used, and that the final -e form now dominates for both noun and verb.
