mail: armor composed of interlocking metal rings (chain mail) or of overlapping small plates, forming a flexible protective fabric; loosely, any armor, and by extension any natural protective covering. In the passage the vortlups' scaly hide is called "scaly mail": the narrator borrows the armorer's craft into natural history, making the beasts walking fortresses whose overlapping scales shed the abrasive storm like a wall, while the earthmen are stung and blinded.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Primary armor sense — a flexible defensive fabric of interlocked metal rings ("chain mail," a modern pleonasm), or of overlapping plates ("plate mail," "scale mail," "lamellar mail").
  • Extended literal sense — any armor, or any natural protective covering likened to armor: a turtle's mail, an armadillo's mail, the crocodile's mail of scales.
  • Figurative sense — a protective integument or barrier of any kind; also "a mesh" (from the word's "net" history).
  • Homograph warning — "mail" the post — letters and the postal system, from a completely different word (Old French male "bag, wallet," of Frankish / Germanic origin). The two are unrelated but collide in spelling; "mail" the armor is the older English sense, and the collision makes the armor-word sound faintly antique.
  • Related technical vocabulary — "hauberk" (a long coat of mail), "mail-coif," "mail-shirt," "mail gauntlets"; in French textiles, "maille" means "mesh" or "stitch."
  • Context and texture — the vortlups' scaly hide is "wholly insensible" to the blowing sand; calling it "scaly mail" makes their skin a manufactured armor. The author invites us to see the beast's overlapping scales as the armorer's overlapping plates, so that the creatures stand calm while the earthmen are stung and blinded — the natural world is armed, the human expedition is not, and the passage's whole economy of protection turns on this word.
  • Collocations — "chain mail," "a coat of mail," "a mesh of mail," "ringed mail," "scale mail," "a suit of mail"; in natural history, "the mail of the crocodile," "the mail of the armadillo."
  • Grammatical note — "mail" is a mass noun: one speaks of "mail" (the armor material), not "a mail," except in modern fantasy and gaming prose, where "a suit of mail" and even plural "mails" appear.
  • Modern revival — in fantasy and gaming the word has been richly revived: "plate mail," "elven mail," "mail of the serpent," "magic mail" — a whole fictional armory built on the 14th-century English word, which has thus outlived the armored knight who first wore it.
  • The "stain" face of the ancestor — Latin macula also meant "spot, stain," and through that sense it reached English in the learned adjective "immaculate" (spotlessly clean, from Latin immaculātus, "unstained"); the mail-wearing knight and the immaculate saint are, etymologically, cousins — two descendants of the same spotted-and-meshed Latin word.
