*flapp- — Proto-Germanic sound-symbolic (onomatopoeic/expressive) base, imitative of the sound of a flat blow or a beating wing; part of the large Germanic f-l- expressive family (flip, flab, flabby, flipper, fluster). There is NO Proto-Indo-European ancestor to reconstruct; the word is native Germanic but cannot be traced beyond it. → flappen — Middle Dutch and Middle Low German, 'to strike, flap' (14th c. cognates showing the base was inherited, not borrowed). → flappen — Middle English, 'to strike, slap' (mid-14th c.), a transitive verb of blunt physical force. → flap — Early Modern English verb (16th c.), 'to move (wings, arms) up and down' — the semantic shift from the effect (the blow) to the instrument (the wing); by the 18th c. also intransitive 'to flutter, swing loosely' (of sails, flags, clothing); the noun flap 'broad piece attached at one edge' likewise dates from the 16th c. → flapped — Modern English past tense, 'moved something broad and flat up and down or back and forth' — the form in 'He flapped his hand in the dark.' NOTE ON DEBATE: the ultimate base is not a recoverable PIE root but an expressive Germanic formation; its age and exact shape are inferable only from the daughter forms (Middle English, Middle Dutch, Middle Low German), and no earlier stage can be reconstructed with certainty.
