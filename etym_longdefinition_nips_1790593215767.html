Nips in the sentence a motor-boat nips on ahead means moves quickly, darts forward, hurries ahead with nimble, almost cheeky speed. It is the third-person singular present of the verb nip in its motion sense: to go briskly, to slip swiftly, to nip along, nip off, nip out, nip ahead. The image is not of ponderous steaming but of a light, agile spurt, a small craft making a quick dash and leaving a trailing rope behind it.

Nip is a classic short, sharp Germanic word with a cluster of senses linked by the idea of sudden pressure or sudden movement. The core historical sense is to pinch, squeeze, bite, or sting with cold or with pincers: frost nips the buds, a crab nips the finger, she nipped his arm. From this pinching action come several extensions, each preserving suddenness, smallness, and sharpness. Understanding nips as darts requires seeing how a pinch becomes a dash.

Principal modern senses include:

  • To pinch, bite, or sting: to nip the skin, to nip off a bud with fingernails, frost nipping at the nose. Concrete, painful, small-scale compression.
  • To move quickly and nimbly: to nip out for milk, to nip round to the shops, to nip on ahead, to nip through traffic. Chiefly British, informal, implying a short, purposeful journey, often on foot or in a small vehicle or boat. This is the sense in the motor-boat example.
  • To steal or snatch: to nip a wallet, to nip off with the takings, blending pinch-as-steal slang with quick movement.
  • To sip liquor: to nip whisky, a nip of brandy, originally a nipped-off small measure, a small pinch of drink, now a small dram, especially in American use.
  • To check growth: to nip something in the bud, to nip a rebellion, metaphorically pinching off incipient growth before it flowers, now a fixed idiom for pre-emptive suppression.
  • Nautical and mechanical: a rope nips, meaning it jams or grips tightly, and to nip a line means to seize it briefly, preserving the grip sense.

The polysemy bridge from pinch to dart is metonymic and expressive. A pinch is sudden, brief, and sharply delimited; so is a nippy movement. To nip out is to make a movement as quick and neat as a pinch. Nineteenth-century slang already speaks of nipping along, nipping home, as if the traveller pinches off a piece of distance. The diminutive phonetics help: nip with its short /ɪ/ and final /p/ sounds quick, light, and abrupt, unlike lumber, plod, or trundle. Small creatures nip: terriers nip at heels and then nip away. Small boats by analogy nip on ahead: they do not surge or plough, they nip.

Aspect and collocation refine the motion sense. Nip almost always implies short distance, brief duration, and light effort. One nips to the corner shop, not nips to Australia, unless humorously understating a long trip. It often takes particles: nip off, nip out, nip back, nip along, nip through, nip past, nip on. It suggests informality, familiarity with the route, and often a hint of mischief or eagerness, as if slipping away while no one watches. A motor-boat nipping on ahead trailing a rope is therefore vividly under-described: the heavy towing vessel lumbers, the light boat cheekily darts, inviting the swimmer or aquaplaner to follow.

Register is informal British, affectionate, and slightly old-fashioned in the jaunty Wodehouse manner, though still alive in everyday phrases like Ill just nip to the loo. It is never solemn, technical, or grand. Its brevity makes it ideal for comic narration where pace matters: boats nip, footmen nip, taxis nip, thoughts nip. To master nips is to master the English love of expressing speed through smallness rather than power.
