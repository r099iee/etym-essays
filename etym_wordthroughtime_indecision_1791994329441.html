- 17th c. (c. 1650s) — First recorded in Early Modern English (adapted from French indécision) in theological and legal contexts to describe unsettled disputes, doctrinal ambiguity, or unadjudicated court causes.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment) — Shifted into moral philosophy, essays, and early novels (Samuel Johnson, Richardson) to depict a moral character flaw or weakness of volition in romantic and ethical dilemmas.
- 19th c. (Romantic & Victorian era) — Prominent in literary analysis and political history as the tragic flaw of intellectuals and statesmen (epitomized by analyses of Shakespeare's Hamlet or Cicero's political paralysis in 49 BCE).
- 20th c. — Formalized within cognitive psychology and behavioral economics to describe decision fatigue, choice overload, and analysis paralysis in organizational behavior.
- 21st c. — Ubiquitous in modern self-improvement, executive coaching, and neuroscience to describe executive function overload in an era of information saturation.
