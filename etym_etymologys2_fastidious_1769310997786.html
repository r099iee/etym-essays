An honest derivation chain for fastidious must begin with a caveat about the very first stage, because the origin of the Latin word at the head of the chain is unknown. Stage 1 — Latin fastus "haughtiness, disdain, pride, contempt" (attested in Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE): the origin of this noun is UNKNOWN — it has no securely established Indo-European etymology, and the occasional claim that it descends from a PIE root meaning "point, bristle" or from a root *bhē- "to shine" has not survived scholarly scrutiny. The chain therefore begins at the attested Latin noun itself, flagged as of unknown prehistory. Stage 2 — Latin fastīdium "loathing, disgust, squeamishness" (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE, e.g. Cicero): traditionally analysed by the Latin grammarians as fastus "haughtiness, disdain" + taedium "weariness, disgust", i.e. the disdainful weariness of one who is weary of anything below his standard; some modern scholars prefer to derive it directly from fastus with the suffix -īdium without invoking taedium — a noted nuance of the analysis. Stage 3 — Latin fastīdiōsus "full of loathing, disdainful, squeamish, hard to please" (Classical Latin): the adjective derived from fastīdium with the suffix -ōsus ("full of"). Stage 4 — Old French fastidieux "loathsome, repulsive, causing disgust" (14th c.): inherited from Latin fastīdiōsus, keeping the passive sense. Stage 5 — Middle English fastidious "causing disgust, repulsive, loathsome" (15th c.): borrowed from Old French with the passive sense — a "fastidious disease" was a disgusting one. Stage 6 — English fastidious "having disgust, filled with distaste for the imperfect, excessively particular, hard to please" (17th c.): the sense rotated 180 degrees from "causing disgust" to "feeling disgust", producing the modern meaning. Stage 7 — Modern English FASTIDIOUS "very attentive to accuracy and detail; hard to please" (18th c. onward; the microbiological sense "demanding specific growth conditions" developed in the 20th c.). Note on the debate: the traditional analysis of fastīdium as fastus + taedium goes back to the Roman grammarians and is widely repeated, but some modern etymologists treat it as a popular etymology, deriving fastīdium straightforwardly from fastus; the ultimate origin of fastus itself remains unknown. The word's Romance descendants diverge sharply: Italian and Spanish fastidioso mean "annoying, troublesome", while English kept and refined the "loathing" sense into "fastidious".
