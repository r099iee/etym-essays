• Ice age: similar because both name a period of expanded ice cover; differs because "ice age" is the everyday, popular term and can be loose about boundaries, while "glaciation" is the technical word that specifies the process or the individual glacial episode.
  • Glacial period / glacial epoch: similar because both name a time of relative cold and ice advance; differs because "period" and "epoch" are formal geological time units (e.g., the Pleistocene is an epoch) and foreground chronology, whereas "glaciation" foregrounds the icy process itself.
  • Ice-sheet advance: similar because both describe the expansion of ice over terrain; differs because "ice-sheet advance" is narrowly the spreading of a continental ice cap (a movement, not a span of time), while "glaciation" covers alpine glaciers, the process, and the period in one word.
  • Freezing: similar because both can mean the turning of water into ice — indeed the oldest sense of "glaciation"; differs because "freezing" is everyday and momentary (a puddle freezes overnight), while "glaciation" implies mass, duration, and geological scale.
  • Congelation: similar because both denote the action of freezing solid; differs because "congelation" is a rare, purely physical term for solidification, with no geological or historical sense at all.
  • Icing: similar because both name the formation of ice on a surface; differs because "icing" is small-scale and operational (icing on aircraft wings, icing on roads), entirely outside the geological register of "glaciation."
  • Refrigeration: similar because both imply the action of cold; differs because "refrigeration" is technological and domestic — the artificial keeping of things cold — with nothing of ice ages in it.
  • Glacierization: similar because both describe the spread of glaciers over a region; differs because "glacierization" (a rarer word) refers strictly to conditions favorable to glacier formation, while "glaciation" includes the ice itself and the time it occupies.
  • Deglaciation: similar because both are process words of the same family; differs in direction — "deglaciation" is the retreat of ice — making it the opposite sense within the same climate machinery.
  • Cryogenic period / snowball Earth: similar because both name dramatic planetary cold episodes; differs because "snowball Earth" is a specific Neoproterozoic hypothesis of near-total ice cover, far more extreme and more contested than the ordinary glaciations of the Quaternary.
