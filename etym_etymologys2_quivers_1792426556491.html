Note on historical debate and homophonic bifurcation: English has two historically distinct words spelled quiver. The noun (an arrow case, attested in the plural quivers) was borrowed from Anglo-Norman quivre (Old French cuevre/coivre), which itself entered Gallo-Romance from a Germanic source (Proto-Germanic *kokur- / Old High German kohhar / Old English cocur "quiver"). Philologists (including the Oxford English Dictionary and Guus Kroonen) note that Germanic *kokur- has no regular Indo-European etymology and is widely believed to be an early borrowing from an Eurasian nomadic horse-archer culture (such as the Huns, Sarmatians, or Scythians) whose composite bows and specialized wooden quivers influenced European warfare. Conversely, the homophonic verb quiver ("to shake, tremble") developed independently in Middle English from the Old English adjective cwifer ("lively, nimble, active"), cognate with quick (from PIE *gʷeyh₃- "to live"). The acoustic and semantic overlap between the trembling of a shot arrow and the archer's arrow-sheath reinforced their convergence in the English poetic imagination.
