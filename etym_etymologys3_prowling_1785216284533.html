PROWLING: an English isolate that learned to hunt A VERB BORN IN THE DARK The word prowling begins in late fourteenth-century English as prollen, first recorded around 1386 with the meaning "to rove or wander in a stealthy manner, to move about in search of something." It belongs to the England of Chaucer and the great poem cycles, but it is not a word Chaucer would have recognized as high or courtly: it smells of the hedge, the wood, and the night watch. Its origin is unknown, and unlike nearly every word in this vocabulary, it is a genuine isolate — the OED and modern etymologists agree that prollen has no known cognates in any other language and that no Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it. Earlier dictionaries sometimes suggested an Old French source, but the evidence for any such loan is lacking, and the guess has been rejected. The near-identity of its sound with that of "prey" is a pure coincidence — the two words have nothing to do with one another — but it has coloured the feel of the verb ever since, as if the word carried its hunting meaning in its own mouth. GROWTH AND DERIVATION Within English the word developed quickly and productively. In the mid-fifteenth century the verbal noun prollinge (c. 1440s) appears with the sense "searching, seeking," showing that the base verb was already being used nominally. Around 1500 the spelling gained an inorganic -w-, giving prowl, by analogy with such words as bowls and whole; yet the word continued to be pronounced with a long "ô" — something like "prôle" — well into the eighteenth century, so that the modern spelling long predated the modern pronunciation. The dialectal forms prole, prol, and provle in Scots and northern English suggest that the verb lived comfortably in speech as well as in print, and the -w- spelling eventually won everywhere. The family then spread by derivation: prowler, "one who prowls," appears by the 1510s, and the noun phrase on the prowl is fixed by 1803, at first of beasts and burglars and later of anyone seeking prey of any kind, including sexual conquest. THE SHIFT TO THE TRANSITIVE Semantically the most important step came in 1580, when a transitive sense emerged: "to go stealthily over an area, as one in search of prey or plunder." The intransitive "roving about" and this new transitive "quartering an area" have run side by side ever since — a wolf prowls about, a burglar prowls the streets — and the transitive sense is what allows the word to take an object of place: the forest, the neighbourhood, the corridors. From that same point the word's social career darkened. In the nineteenth century the prowler slipped from beast-of-prey to burglar, and by the twentieth century it had fixed the night-time human predator: the prowler in crime reports is a person lurking about property with criminal intent, and on the prowl became a standard phrase for a man seeking sexual conquest. The American police term prowl car — a patrol car — shows the verb colonizing institutional jargon, the word moving from the animal's territory to the state's. SOUND AND FORM The sound of the word is notable for what happened late. The medieval form prollen was pronounced with a long open o; the -w- added around 1500 was purely graphic, and the modern pronunciation /pɹˈaʊlɪŋ/ — with the /aʊ/ diphthong of "owl" — did not settle until the eighteenth-century Great Vowel Shift had completed its work, when the spelling finally began to drive the sound. The present participle prowling is formed with the ordinary -ing suffix, and it is this form that lets the verb describe continuous, habitual, restless motion — exactly the quality the passage needs. IN THE HOBBIT When Tolkien writes that Gollum "went prowling about," he is using the word in its oldest and still central sense — the intransitive roving in search of prey — with every later association flowing in behind it. Gollum prowls as a beast prowls, silent and patient and endlessly hungry; he prowls as a burglar prowls, furtive and alone; and the narrator's droll "not a gourmet" plays against the word's menace to produce grim comedy. The isolate verb of 1386, whose very origin no one can trace, turns out to be the perfect word for a creature whose own origin is equally unknown — a small slimy thing that came from nobody knows where, roaming the dark in search of something raw and fresh. The derivation chain, from the earliest attested stage: Middle English prollen "to rove stealthily, move about in search" (c. 1386; obscure origin, no cognates, no PIE root) → verbal noun prollinge (mid-15th c.) → prowl, spelling with inorganic -w- (c. 1500; still pronounced "prôle") → transitive sense "go stealthily over an area" (1580) → prowler (1510s), on the prowl (1803) → English prowling, present participle.
