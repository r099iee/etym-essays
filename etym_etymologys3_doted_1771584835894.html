The biography of doted begins not in the Indo-European homeland but in the Low Countries, among the coastal dialects of the medieval North Sea. In the thirteenth century Middle English acquired the verb doten, "to be silly, to act foolishly", a word it shared with Middle Low German doten and Middle Dutch doten, "to be foolish", and these belong to a wider family - Icelandic dotta, "to nod, doze, slumber", and modern Dutch dutten, "to snooze" - built on an expressive base *dut- that seems to mimic the nodding head of a sleepy or senile person. The origin is obscure: the OED marks the etymology as uncertain, and the word has the look of an onomatopoeic or nursery formation rather than a regular Indo-European derivative, which is why no PIE root core can be safely reconstructed for it. The earliest English sense of dote was exactly that: to be in one's second childhood, to have one's wits fail through age.
  • The leap to love: from "losing one's wits with age" the meaning broadened, by the fourteenth century, to "to be silly or weak in mind generally", and from there the word made a remarkable psychological jump: the mindless tenderness of the elderly produced the modern sense, "to be foolishly and excessively fond of someone". The logic is tender and a little cruel. A person in dotage loves without discrimination, so "to dote on" someone means to love them with a love that has left judgment behind - the doter is, etymologically, a little doting, a little daft.
  • The family grows: "dotage" (fourteenth century) is the condition of senile foolishness and, by extension, of doting fondness; "dotard" is the senile fool, a word that haunts medieval romances and Shakespeare's age; and the participial adjective "doting", once simply "acting foolishly", has settled into the affectionate sense that dominates today - "a doting mother", "a doting grandfather".
  • The verb retires into collocation: the verb itself, used absolutely, has largely retired, surviving chiefly in the fixed collocation "dote on / dote upon", so that even the past tense "doted" that stands on this card keeps its companion preposition in real use: Grandmother Jia doted on Bao-yu.
Socio-culturally, the word has always walked a line between indulgence and folly. From Chaucer's dotard to the Victorian "doting mother", the family of dote has been used to describe love that is so total that it forfeits wisdom - a theme that the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century novelists explored endlessly, since the doting grandparent is the engine of so many spoiled heirs and ungovernable children. In the passage, that is precisely the work the word does: Grandmother Jia's doting is the shelter that lets Bao-yu's "queer nature" flourish uncorrected, and the narrator's word choice quietly marks the cost - a love so uncritical that it shields the boy from every reproach, including the reproaches he needs. The etymology completes the portrait: to dote is, at bottom, to be a little dotty, a little soft in the head - and the novel invites us to see both the tenderness and the doting in the grandmother's love, a word that has carried the shadow of folly inside it since the thirteenth century and still does today.
