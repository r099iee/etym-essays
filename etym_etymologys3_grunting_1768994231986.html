Grunting begins not with a root but with a sound. The word belongs to that ancient class of expressive, onomatopoeic vocabulary in which a language imitates the noise it names, and its biography is therefore less a chain of inherited forms than a series of independent re-creations across the tongues of Europe. Old English grunian, "to grunt," already contains the whole physics of the word — a closed throat, a burst of air, a low vowel sitting under a consonant cluster whose r approximates the pig's guttural rasp. Around it grew a frequentative, grunnettan, "to keep grunting," an -ettan formation of the type that makes words for repeated action, and it is from that frequentative that the modern verb descends through Middle English grunten. The same imitation is at work across the Germanic family: Old High German grunnizōn and its modern daughter German grunzen, Dutch gronzen, Danish grynte, Swedish grymta — each the local rendition of the same porcine snuffle, each apparently coined separately rather than inherited from a single proto-form, because onomatopoeia is the one area where languages may start over at any time. The temptation to reconstruct a tidy PIE root behind all of these must be resisted: the handbooks treat the grunt-family as parallel sound-imitation, not as proof of a shared inherited form, and the similarity between, say, English grunt and Latin grunnīre is acoustic accident across a common observation of pigs, not common descent.

Across the Alps the Romans made the same observation and gave it the same shape. Latin grunnīre, "to grunt," is likewise imitative, and it fathered the entire Romance line: Italian grugnire, Spanish gruñir, Portuguese grunhir all still grunt with the pigs, while French grogner, an independent Gallic formation, drifted toward human grumbling — the grumbler is un grognon. Latin also gave the sea its name for a grunting fish: the gurnard, so called because it "grunts" when pulled from the water, passed into Old French as gournart and into English around 1300 by metathesis, the r-shift that turns grun- into gurn-. The same zoological eye would later name the American grunt fish (1713) for the identical reason, and the sound-word has been pressed into service for frogs, toads, and any creature with a low, burping, throaty call.

In English the word's career has run from the sty to the barracks to the boardroom. In the 16th century grunt was a plain animal word; by the 17th it was applied to humans straining at work, the porters and rowers and wrestlers whose effort leaks out as noise. The noun form — "a grunt," the sound — is attested from the 1550s, and the transferred use of grunt for "a pig" (slang, 17th c.) kept the animal association alive even in the kitchen. The great social extension came in the 20th century. In U.S. military slang the "grunt" became the low-ranking infantryman, the foot-soldier who does the bodily work of war (the word solidifying in the Vietnam era, c. 1969), and "grunt work" (1977) generalised the idea to any menial, physical, unrewarding labour. Meanwhile gruntle, a Middle English frequentative "to grumble, mutter," survived long enough to be negated into disgruntle (1680s) and disgruntled — a word whose etymology few speakers suspect hides the very grunt-noise of the pig. In computing the word resurfaced as the name of a JavaScript build tool (Grunt, from the labour of building software) and as the name of a background process or "grunt" worker, proving that the semantic core — low, effortful, menial — remains as live today as in the sty.

  • 8th–9th c.: Old English grunian "to grunt"; frequentative grunnettan "to keep grunting."
  • 14th c.: Middle English grunten "to grunt"; 1550s noun grunt "the sound"; 16th–17th c. transfer to human effort.
  • c. 1300 / 1713: the gurnard and the grunt fish named for their noise.
  • 17th–20th c.: grunt "a pig" (slang); gruntle → disgruntle (1680s) → disgruntled.
  • 1969 / 1977: U.S. military grunt "infantryman"; grunt work "menial labour."
  • Contemporary: grunting speech = animal, mindless, dehumanised — the very charge the passage levels at the foreign sailors.
