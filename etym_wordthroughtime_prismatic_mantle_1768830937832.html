- Early 17th century - the mathematician's word - "prismatic" is coined from "prism" in its strictly geometric sense, meaning "shaped like a prism," a solid with parallel, congruent polygonal bases; a dry technical term of optics and geometry.
- 18th century - Newton's colors - after Newton's prism experiments, "prismatic" attaches itself to the spectral band of colors into which white light is split; it names the rainbow band precisely and scientifically, still disciplined and exact.
- 19th-20th century - the rainbow generalizes - the geometric sense fades from daily use and "prismatic" comes to mean simply "iridescent, rainbow-hued, many-colored," any brilliant play of separated color, though a crystalline, lapidary precision still lingers beneath.
- Old English - the garment - "mantle" (from Latin mantellum, via Old French) first names a loose sleeveless cloak worn over the shoulders, the everyday outer garment of the medieval world.
- Middle Ages - the mantle of office - the word gains ceremonial gravity: the mantle of kings, clergy, and heralds becomes the cloth of dignity, so that "to assume the mantle" of a role already begins to mean taking on its authority.
- 17th-19th century - the enveloping layer - science and metaphor widen it: geologists speak of the earth's mantle, ornithologists of a bird's feathery mantle, poets of a mantle of snow or night - any covering that wraps, conceals, and enfolds.
- Contemporary - the double life - "mantle" keeps both senses alive at full strength: still the cloak, and still the enveloping metaphor ("a mantle of leadership," "a mantle of silence"), each sense enriching the other.
