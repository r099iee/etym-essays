Atticus is a place-name turned into a surname, and then into a moral portrait. Its root is the Greek Ἀττική (Attikē), the name of the peninsula around Athens, and its immediate parent is the adjective Ἀττικός (Attikos), "of Attica, Athenian". Latin took the adjective over unchanged in form as Atticus, and because the Romans commonly used such adjectives as surnames, the word became a name. The English Atticus is not a native formation at all: it is the Latin name, transmitted through classical scholarship and, much later, through literature.

The disputed place-name. The ancients themselves were uncertain about Ἀττική. One tradition derived it from ἄκτη, "headland, coast", which suits the geography of the Attic peninsula but is phonetically awkward; another invented the hero Atthis as an eponym, a familiar Greek way of explaining a name by giving it a founder. Modern linguists are divided between a Greek derivation of this kind and the view that the name is pre-Greek — a survival from the population that preceded the Greek speakers in Attica. There is no secure Proto-Indo-European etymology, and the honest chain therefore begins with the attested Greek place-name rather than with a reconstructed root.

Attic, Attica, Atticism. The same adjective produced a small family of English words. Attic means "of Athens or Attica" and, in literary and architectural contexts, "refined, restrained, classical": the Attic style is the plain, powerful manner of the Athenian orators, as opposed to the florid Asiatic style. Atticism is a turn of phrase characteristic of Attic Greek, and Atticist is the name of the purist party in the Greek rhetorical quarrels of the Roman Empire. Attic in architecture — the low storey or parapet above a main façade, and hence the modern attic room — comes through French attique from the same Latin adjective, originally describing a style thought to be Athenian. None of these is the man; all of them share his name's root.

Roman names and the agnomen. A Roman citizen's name was a small curriculum vitae. The praenomen was the personal name, the nomen identified the clan, and the cognomen distinguished the branch; a fourth element, the agnomen, could be added for a particular distinction. Agnomina were awarded for victories and conquests — Africanus, Germanicus, Creticus, Numidicus — and, less often, for a place of residence. Atticus belongs to this second, quieter class. It did not commemorate a defeated enemy but a chosen city, and it advertised not military glory but philhellenic culture. In the late Republic, when Greek learning was both admired and suspected, that was a statement about the kind of Roman one chose to be.

The man behind the name. Titus Pomponius Atticus was born in 110 BCE into a wealthy equestrian family and educated alongside Cicero. In 86 BCE, with Rome in the grip of civil violence, he moved to Athens and remained there for about twenty years, studying philosophy, collecting antiquities and managing his affairs at a distance. He returned to Rome in 65 BCE, refused a senatorial career, and lived as an independent man of business and letters. His daughter Caecilia Attica married Marcus Agrippa, which tied his family to the Augustan settlement; his own tastes, however, remained those of a private Epicurean. Cornelius Nepos, who knew him, wrote his life and presented him as a model of loyalty, tact and equilibrium.

The publisher and the letters. Atticus's household included skilled copyists, and Cicero relied on him to reproduce and circulate his works. The letters Cicero sent him — sixteen books, from 68 to 44 BCE — are the most revealing documents of late-Republican politics and of Cicero's own temperament. They survived because Atticus kept them, and they were published, in all likelihood, after Cicero's death. When Petrarch found a manuscript of the letters at Verona in 1345, the discovery transformed the Renaissance image of Cicero: here was the statesman not in rhetorical pose but in panic, irritation and affection. Ad Atticum has been a synonym for intimate correspondence ever since.

Modern afterlife. In eighteenth-century England Atticus became a type-name for the polished patron of letters; Pope's portrait of Addison under that name in the Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot (1735) fixed it as a byword for urbane, slightly vain cultivation. In 1960 Harper Lee gave the name to Atticus Finch, and the novel's enormous reach has made "Atticus" in modern English primarily a given name associated with quiet moral courage. The Roman original, who chose Athens over the forum and survival over heroics, is now less familiar than the fictional Alabama lawyer — a striking reversal in the name's fortunes, and a reminder that literary fame can outlive historical fame.
