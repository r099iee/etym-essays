Corbusier (invariably known with the definite article as Le Corbusier) was the professional pseudonym and architectural persona of Charles-Édouard Jeanneret-Gris (1887–1965), the Swiss-French architect, designer, urban planner, painter, and polemicist who stands as one of the most influential, celebrated, and fiercely debated titans of 20th-century modern architecture and the International Style.

The core architectural doctrines, historical monuments, and semantic applications of Corbusier include:

  • The Five Points of a New Architecture (1927): Le Corbusier codified modern architectural dogma into five revolutionary structural principles enabled by reinforced concrete (*béton armé*):
    • Pilotis: A grid of slender, reinforced concrete columns elevating the primary volume of the building above ground level, freeing the earth beneath for pedestrian movement, gardens, or vehicle circulation.
    • Free Plan (Plan Libre): The complete elimination of interior load-bearing masonry walls, replacing them with structural point-load columns and allowing partition walls to be placed freely and reorganized at will.
    • Free Façade (Façade Libre): Detaching the exterior envelope of the building from the load-bearing skeleton, transforming the façade into a light, independent membrane of glass and panels.
    • Ribbon Windows (Fenêtres en Bandeau): Continuous horizontal bands of glazing cutting across the façade, admitting uniform natural light and eliminating traditional punched-hole masonry windows.
    • Roof Garden (Toit-Jardin): Flat reinforced concrete roofs transformed into lush domestic gardens and sun decks, reclaiming the building's footprint for nature and protecting concrete slabs from thermal expansion.
  • The Machine for Living (*Machine à Habiter*): In his groundbreaking 1923 manifesto Vers une architecture (translated as Towards a New Architecture), he famously proclaimed that "a house is a machine for living in." Rather than reducing human life to soulless mechanics, this doctrine was a call to emancipate architecture from historical pastiche, academic ornament, and inefficient bourgeois clutter, aligning design with the functional precision, hygiene, and aerodynamic elegance of modern steamships, automobiles, and airplanes.
  • The Modulor: In 1948, Le Corbusier patented Le Modulor, an anthropometric scale of visual proportions derived from human anatomy (based on a six-foot-tall human body), the Golden Ratio, and the Fibonacci sequence. Intended as a universal harmonious measurement system for industrial mass manufacturing and spatial design, the Modulor bridged imperial and metric systems.
  • Monumental Works and Brutalism: His career evolved from the pristine white geometry of Purist villas—epitomized by the masterwork Villa Savoye (1929) in Poissy—to the raw, sculptural monumentality of post-war béton brut (raw, exposed concrete imprinted with the grain of wooden formwork, which gave rise to the term Brutalism). Masterpieces of this era include the revolutionary collective housing block Unité d'Habitation in Marseille (1952), the pilgrimage chapel of Notre-Dame du Haut at Ronchamp (1954), the Dominican monastery of Sainte Marie de La Tourette (1960), and the Carpenter Center for the Visual Arts at Harvard University (1963)—his sole completed building in North America.
  • Urban Planning and Polemical Controversy: As an urban visionary, Le Corbusier championed dramatic, utopian megastructures designed to relieve congested historic capitals. His radical proposals—such as the Ville Radieuse (Radiant City) and the notorious Plan Voisin for Paris (which proposed demolishing a vast swath of historic central Paris to erect cruciform glass-and-steel skyscrapers amid parkland)—fundamentally inspired mid-century public housing projects worldwide. While celebrated for visionary social housing and light-filled civic space, his car-centric, totalizing urban schemes faced intense criticism from urbanists like Jane Jacobs for destroying organic street life and human-scale neighborhood cohesion. His grandest urban achievement was realized in India, where Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru commissioned him to design the master plan and colossal civic monuments of Chandigarh, the newly founded capital of Punjab.
  • Eponymous Adjective and Cultural Metonymy: Today, the adjective Corbusian denotes stark geometric functionalism, pilotis-supported masses, primary-color accents, and raw sculptural concrete. Seventeen of his architectural masterworks across seven nations were collectively inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2016.
