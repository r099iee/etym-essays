TACT begins in the body. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *tag-, "to touch, handle, take hold of," a verbal core whose descendants spread across the ancient language families. In Greek the root produced tattein, "to arrange, order," with its participle taktos and the derivative taktikos, "skilled in arranging," from which English later borrowed tactics — the art of arranging troops and arguments — so that even in antiquity the idea of touching was pressed into service for the abstract work of ordering soldiers and speeches. The standard spelling of the root is *tag- (with the voiced velar), though the Greek forms show the expected palatal treatment, and some reconstructions write the laryngeal-laden form *h₂eǵ- for the broader "drive" root, which is a different though related etymon; for tact the chain is the touch root *tag-, uncontroversially the base of Latin tangere and its family. In Italic the root yielded Proto-Italic *tag- and then Latin tangere, "to touch," attested from the second century BCE, the pivot on which the whole later history turns. The crucial semantic development happened inside Latin through the participle and nominal system. The verb tangere was a nasal-infix present — the root *tag- appears with the infixed nasal as tag-ng-, tang- — and its past participle tactus, "touched," supplied the abstract noun tactus, "the sense of touch, handling," built on the weakened stem tact-. From this same stem Latin generated a family of words that English later borrowed whole: tactilis, "that may be touched," the source of English tactile; contingere, "to touch closely," the source of contact and contingent; contagio, "contact, contagion," the source of contagion; and the adjective integer, literally "untouched," hence "whole, unharmed," the source of integral and integrity. Each preserves the root's primitive meaning of physical contact, and each would carry its metaphor in a different direction. The frequentative verb taxare, "to appraise or assess" (from a touching, a testing by touch), branched off separately and is the ancestor of English tax and the word's related card taxed. The sound history is regular and transparent: the PIE *t is preserved as Latin t, the *g appears in the present stem tang- with the nasal infix and then is lost in the participle tact-, and the Italic consonant sequence settles into the familiar forms. French was the direct conduit for tact itself. Latin tactus, "sense of touch," passed into French as tact, which for centuries retained the physical meaning — the bodily sense of touch, the hand's faculty of feeling. In the eighteenth century, at the height of the Enlightenment and of the culture of politeness, the word began to shift ground: writers of the period, in aesthetics and in moral essays, began to speak of a figurative "touch" — the mental delicacy by which one grasps nuance in art, in morality, and in conversation. Just as the fingers read the texture of a surface, so the discerning mind was said to have tact, a fineness of perception that grasps what is fitting, what is subtle, what should and should not be said. The metaphor was taken up eagerly in French salon culture and in English letters, and English borrowed the word in precisely this sense during the mid-eighteenth century, around the 1740s, when the culture of politeness was at its height. The physical root of "touch" was retained as a guiding metaphor: tact is a "touch" in the social sense, the delicate contact with other people's sensibilities. The English sense settled quickly into its modern form. By the early nineteenth century tact meant the adroitness of saying and doing the right thing, avoiding offence and preserving harmony — a settled, admired virtue of manners, education, and business, the indispensable grace of the gentleman, the hostess, the teacher, and the diplomat. The word's history is thus a pure semantic migration from the physical to the social: no sound change, no borrowing of a new form, but a metaphor that travelled from the fingertips to the manners. The privative tactless, recorded from the late eighteenth century, names the absence of the faculty — the blunt, offensive, well-meaning clumsiness of one who has no "touch" for the feelings of others — and the derivative tactful, tactfully, and tactfulness complete the set. The word has no other families in English beyond these derivatives; it remains a singular, compact noun, entirely occupied by the social sense it acquired in the eighteenth century. The word's social history is inseparable from the history of politeness itself. In the eighteenth-century culture of refined manners, tact was prized as the oil that made society run smoothly, the intuitive grace of the well-bred. In the nineteenth century it became a professional and institutional virtue, the required skill of the diplomat, the clergyman, the schoolmaster, and the tradesman who must not offend a customer; the moral essayists made it a modest, practical counterpart to the great public virtues, the small kindness of never needlessly wounding. In the twentieth century the word became contested: critics of a certain honesty set it in opposition to truth, treating tact as the polite self-censorship that prefers harmony to candour, and it is exactly this valuation that Empson exploits in the passage of this card, where the honest man makes the ignoring of tact a point of honour. The word therefore carries, in its modern life, the whole argument of the ages of politeness: the same faculty that was once the summit of social grace can now be suspected as the smoothness that blunts the truth — yet it remains, in ordinary usage, the generous skill of not hurting others when the truth must still be spoken.
  • PIE *tag- "to touch, handle" → Greek tattein/taktos (arrange), Latin tangere (touch).
  • Latin tangere, nasal-infix present "to touch" → past participle tactus "touched" → noun tactus "sense of touch, handling."
  • Latin family: tactilis (tactile), contingere (contact, contingent), contagio (contagion), taxare (tax).
  • French tact "sense of touch" → figurative "mental delicacy" (18th c.) → English tact (mid-18th c., c. 1740s) "delicate social sensitivity."
  • Derivatives: tactful, tactfully, tactlessness; tactless (late 18th c.) and tactfully complete the set.
