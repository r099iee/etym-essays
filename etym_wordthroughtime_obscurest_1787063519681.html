- 15th c. - Obscurest, superlative of the borrowed adjective obscure, first appears with the literal sense “darkest, dimmest, most hidden from sight”.
- 16th-17th c. - The word extends to the mind and to society: the “obscurest” meaning is the most abstruse, the “obscurest” person the most unknown and uncelebrated; the -est form is standard literary usage.
- 18th-19th c. - Writers begin preferring most obscure in plain prose, while obscurest survives in verse and in high rhetorical register, where it now sounds stately and old-fashioned.
- 20th-21st c. - Obscurest becomes a deliberate archaism — the word a narrator uses to evoke ancient song, legend, and dignity (as in Tolkien); perception shifts from ordinary superlative to crafted, antique elegance.
