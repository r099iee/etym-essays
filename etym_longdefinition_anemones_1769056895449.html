anemones (plural of anemone) is a word with two heads, because it names a flower in the garden and a creature in the sea. The primary, older meaning is botanical: an anemone is a delicate plant of the genus Anemone in the buttercup family (Ranunculaceae) — the "windflower", with showy sepals and a fragile, cup-like bloom that quivers in the breeze. The secondary, transferred meaning is zoological: a sea anemone is a soft-bodied marine polyp of the order Actiniaria, which anchors itself to rocks and waves a crown of stinging tentacles that look startlingly like flower petals. In the passage from C.S. Lewis's Prince Caspian the word appears in the narrator's dismissive catalogue of the island shore: "Shells and seaweed and anemones, or tiny crabs in rock-pools, are all very well, but you soon get tired of them if you are thirsty." The tone is deliberately deflationary. The narrator takes the tide-pool's showiest, most beautiful inhabitants — creatures that look like jewelled flowers — and files them away as mere ornaments, "all very well", the idle pleasures of a bored visitor. The point is that visual delight is not nourishment: the anemone is the very image of useless beauty on a day when what the children need is water. The word therefore carries a quiet irony — beauty counted, weighed, and found wanting. PRIMARY MEANING — THE FLOWER:
  • The windflower. The Greek name anemōnē means literally "daughter of the wind" (anemos, wind), because classical writers believed the flower opens only when the wind blows, or because it is born when spring winds return. Garden species (poppy anemone, wood anemone) have long been cultivated for their bright, poppy-like blooms.
  • Botanical detail. Anemones are herbaceous perennials with tuberous roots; the "petals" are actually colourful sepals, and the flower cup nods and sways in the lightest air — hence the name.
SECONDARY MEANING — THE SEA ANEMONE:
  • The marine polyp. In the 18th century naturalists extended the flower name to the animal: the sea anemone is a solitary polyp whose ring of stinging tentacles mirrors flower petals, so the same word came to name both a plant and an animal. They are close relatives of corals and jellyfish (phylum Cnidaria).
  • In context. Lewis's "anemones" are plainly the sea creatures, found with shells, seaweed and crabs in rock-pools — nature's ornaments laid out for the visitor's eye.
MODERN TECHNICAL AND FIGURATIVE USES:
  • Split by habitat. Today anemone alone usually means the flower; sea anemone means the animal. Both are common in aquarium and tide-pool vocabulary, and "anemone" appears in reef-keeping jargon for the tank's flower-animals.
  • Metaphor. Anything flower-like yet secretly animate can be called anemone-like; the word is used in marine biology metaphor ("the anemone's tentacle crown") and occasionally in literary imagery for fragile, wind-shaken beauty.
  • Medical/technical fringe. In medicine the name of the wood anemone's toxin has entered pharmacology; the Greek root anemos (wind) reappears in anemometer (wind-speed instrument) and anemonefish (the clownfish that lives among anemones).
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE: The whole semantic career of anemone is a series of transfers across the boundary between kingdoms. First the Greeks named a flower after the wind (anemōnē from anemos, "wind"); the Romans borrowed the flower-name as anemōnē; English took it in the late 16th century from Latin, at first purely as the garden plant. The great transfer happened in the scientific age: in the 1760s and 1770s, when British naturalists began describing the vivid marine polyps of the shore, the obvious name was "sea anemone", because the animal's tentacle-crown made it look exactly like the windflower. One word thus crossed from botany to zoology by pure visual metaphor — and it now names a flowering plant, a stinging polyp, a reef-keeping hobby animal, and the fish that shelters in it. In the novel the plural form sums up the whole tide-pool aesthetic: beautiful, harmless-looking, and useless to a thirsty child. Exhaustively: anemones = (1) windflowers, plants of genus Anemone; (2) sea anemones, marine polyps of order Actiniaria; (3) by metaphor, any flower-like-yet-animal marine ornament; (4) in technical jargon, the anemone's tentacle crown and the anemonefish of coral reefs; and (5) in this context, the jewelled but useless beauties of a rock-pool on a thirsty day.
