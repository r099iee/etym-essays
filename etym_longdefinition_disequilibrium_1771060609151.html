disequilibrium (noun) — a state of disruption in balance or stability; the condition of a system that has lost its evenness or poise and is no longer at rest. In the literary context given — "the former are symptomatized by the ____ of society and the latter by its disequilibrium" — the word is set against the earlier "equilibrium" in the same sentence, and the pairing is deliberate: equilibrium and disequilibrium name the two poles of social health, balance and its loss. The tone is clinical yet ominous: the writer treats society as a physical system that can be in a state of harmonious counterpoise or thrown catastrophically out of it. The blank for disequilibrium is filled by the word that denotes the negative pole — the collapse of that balance, the symptom of "noxious ethereal influences." The word here is not merely a synonym for disorder; it presupposes that a prior state of balance existed and has been actively lost, so it carries a sense of regression, of a system fallen from its proper condition.

Primary modern senses

In modern English, disequilibrium operates across several technical domains, all sharing the core meaning "loss of balance in a system."

  • Economics and finance: disequilibrium is the condition of a market or economy in which supply does not equal demand, or in which the forces of the economy are not balanced — the state that classical equilibrium analysis treats as temporary, driving prices and quantities toward a new balance. Phrases such as "market disequilibrium" and "disequilibrium in the balance of payments" are standard in textbooks and central-bank commentary.
  • Physics and thermodynamics: disequilibrium names any state in which the forces or gradients acting on a system are not in balance — for example, a system with a temperature or pressure difference that will drive change until equilibrium is reached. The phrase "disequilibrium conditions" describes processes that are not reversible or static.
  • Physiology and medicine: disequilibrium denotes a disturbance of bodily balance, most commonly the loss of vestibular (inner-ear) balance that produces dizziness, vertigo, and unsteadiness. A patient with "vestibular disequilibrium" cannot maintain stable posture or gait.
  • Psychology and education: following Jean Piaget, "cognitive disequilibrium" is the state of mental discomfort that arises when new information does not fit existing schemas, and which drives learning as the mind reorganises to restore equilibrium. The term is a pillar of constructivist learning theory.
  • Sociology and ecology: "social disequilibrium" describes a society whose institutions, norms, and structures have fallen out of balance; ecologists speak of ecosystems pushed into disequilibrium by disturbance, with modern ecology even celebrating disequilibrium as a normal, creative state rather than a pathology.
  • Chemistry and biology: a reaction in disequilibrium is one proceeding toward equilibrium; biological systems are said to maintain a dynamic disequilibrium with their environment, constantly exporting entropy to sustain life.
  • Figurative and everyday sense: in general writing, disequilibrium means any mental, emotional, or organisational instability — a person thrown into disequilibrium by grief, an institution in disequilibrium after a scandal. This informal use extends the technical image of scales tipped out of level.

Semantic structure and historical bridge

The word is a transparent compound of the prefix dis- ("apart, in a different direction, reversal") and equilibrium ("a state of balance"). Its meaning is therefore built on negation of a prior state: disequilibrium is not the absence of any order but the loss of a specific, defined balance. This distinguishes it sharply from chaos (no order at all) and imbalance (a general, often informal unevenness). The historical bridge is the mechanical image of the balance scale: the Latin aequilibrium was literally a scale with equal weights on both sides; add the privative dis- and you get the scale tipped, weights unequal, the whole apparatus no longer at rest. From that simple mechanical picture, science and society extended the word to every kind of system that can lose its poise — markets, minds, ecosystems, nations.

Grammar, register, and collocations

Disequilibrium is an abstract uncountable noun, usually singular, drawn from the formal Latin-Greek learned register. It is at home in scientific prose, academic economics, psychological theory, and clinical writing, and carries a precise, technical flavour in all of them. Common collocations: "economic disequilibrium," "market disequilibrium," "state of disequilibrium," "disequilibrium in the balance of payments," "cognitive disequilibrium," "vestibular disequilibrium," "disequilibrium model." The plural disequilibria occurs in technical economics. Its register is distinctly more formal and theoretical than the plain word "imbalance," and it implies a reference point — an equilibrium that ought to exist — which the more general words do not. In the literary context, the word performs this technical work in a moral register: society, like a scale, has a true balance, and its loss is disequilibrium.
