• salvoes: Similar because both refer to the simultaneous or rapid discharge of multiple artillery pieces or severe verbal criticisms; differs because a *salvo* can be fired from any formation, battery, or group of weapons (not restricted to the flank of a warship), whereas *broadsides* specifically originates from the entire flank of a vessel.
  • volleys: Similar because both denote coordinated simultaneous discharges of weapons or rapid bursts of spoken words; differs because a *volley* typically refers to small arms or arrows fired simultaneously by infantry, whereas *broadsides* denotes heavy naval artillery or massive comprehensive denunciations.
  • bombardments: Similar because both involve intense, damaging barrages; differs because *bombardment* implies sustained, prolonged, and continuous shelling over time (often against a fortress or city), whereas a *broadside* is a single, crushing simultaneous release of all available flank guns.
  • fusillades: Similar because both represent rapid bursts of gunfire or rhetorical attacks; differs because a *fusillade* refers to a rapid succession of individual firearm discharges rather than a single simultaneous heavy cannon blast.
  • barrages: Similar because both describe an overwhelming wall of fire or criticism; differs because a *barrage* is typically an artillery barrier designed to protect advancing troops or deny ground through continuous area-saturation, whereas a *broadside* is direct offensive fire.
  • polemics: Similar because both can describe passionate, aggressive verbal or written attacks; differs because *polemics* refers specifically to formal philosophical, theological, or intellectual disputations, whereas *broadsides* evokes explosive, forceful, and multi-faceted public assaults.
  • diatribes: Similar because both are fierce verbal attacks; differs because a *diatribe* is a prolonged, bitter, often rambling oral or written denunciation, whereas a *broadside* emphasizes a sudden, powerful, coordinated strike.
  • broadsheets: Similar because both historically refer to single-sheet printed papers; differs because *broadsheet* now primarily refers to full-sized, serious newspapers as opposed to tabloids, whereas *broadsides* retains the active senses of naval artillery and verbal barrages.
  • tirades: Similar because both involve angry outbursts of condemnation; differs because a *tirade* is an angry, protracted speech by an individual, lacking the multi-faceted tactical weight implied by *broadsides*.
