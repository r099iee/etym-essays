Nineveh is the name of one of the largest and most storied cities of antiquity: the last capital of the Assyrian Empire, built on the eastern bank of the Tigris River opposite the modern city of Mosul in northern Iraq. Its mounds — Kuyunjik and Nebi Yunus — sit at the heart of the Nineveh Governorate (Arabic Ninawa). For roughly eighty years (c. 704–612 BCE) it was the command center of the greatest military machine of the ancient Near East, and when it fell it fell completely: so thoroughly was the city destroyed and abandoned that later generations remembered it chiefly from the Bible and from legends, until nineteenth-century archaeologists dug it back out of the earth.

Historical senses and facts.

  • The imperial capital: Sennacherib (704–681 BCE) rebuilt Nineveh as his ‘palace without rival’, ringed it with a wall about 12 km long carrying fifteen gates, and fed it with an elaborate system of canals and the great aqueduct at Jerwan; the city’s population is plausibly estimated at 100,000 or more, making it among the largest cities of its age.
  • The library: Ashurbanipal (668–631 BCE) stocked a palace library of thousands of cuneiform tablets — 25,000–30,000 in the British Museum today — including the standard text of the Epic of Gilgamesh, omen series, and the sciences; this collection is the single richest textual window on ancient Mesopotamia.
  • The fall: in 612 BCE a coalition of Medes and Babylonians sacked Nineveh; the Babylonian Chronicle records that the city was turned into ruins and mounds, and Assyria vanished as a state. The site was never reoccupied on the same scale.
  • Biblical layer: the Book of Jonah makes Nineveh the great pagan city that repents at the prophet’s preaching (‘an exceeding great city of three days’ journey’); the prophets Nahum and Zephaniah pronounce its doom (‘Nineveh is laid waste: who will bemoan her?’). It thereby became the biblical type both of mercy toward repentant sinners and of judgment upon pride and cruelty.
  • Rediscovery: the ruins were identified and first excavated by Paul-Émile Botta (at Khorsabad) and above all Austen Henry Layard (1845–51), whose finds from Kuyunjik — winged bulls, the Lachish reliefs, and the library tablets — transformed the British Museum and created the modern image of Assyria.

Polysemy and symbolic uses.

  • Proper noun (the only nominal sense): Nineveh is a toponym; but proper names accrue rhetoric. Since Kipling’s ‘Recessional’ (1897) — ‘Lo, all our pomp of yesterday / Is one with Nineveh and Tyre!’ — the name has been the standard literary shorthand for a great power’s inevitable eclipse.
  • Figurative ‘doomed city’: in political rhetoric, a Nineveh is any hubris-fat empire or metropolis awaiting its 612 BCE; the word implies totality of ruin rather than decline.
  • Religious typology: in homiletics Nineveh stands for both penitence (the Ninevites’ fast) and judgment (Nahum), and ‘the repentance of Nineveh’ remains a live motif.
  • Modern geography: Nineveh lives on as the name of the Iraqi governorate (Ninawa), of the Nineveh Plains region, and of the ancient site’s heritage zone; the walls and gates were partly bulldozed by ISIL in 2015, making ‘Nineveh’ again a headline of cultural destruction.
  • Adjectival derivations:Ninevite (a native or inhabitant; also the name of a Neolithic ceramic culture found at the site) and Ninevitish (rare).

Linguistic note. The English form descends through Latin and Greek from the Hebrew name in the Bible, itself the heir of the city’s Akkadian name Ninua, probably connected with the goddess Ninâ (Iḡtar of Nineveh) — the city’s patroness from the third millennium BCE.
