Chain for SLUNG (single root):
1. Proto-Indo-European *sleng- (dialectal variant *slenk-), “to turn, wind, twist” (reconstructed; pre-3rd millennium BCE) — a root of winding, pliant motion; zero-grade forms *slng-/*slunk-. The velar form *sleng- feeds Germanic “sling,” while the dental *slenk- feeds “slink” (“to creep stealthily”). No secure Greek or Indo-Iranian reflex is attested; Sanskrit candidates such as sraj- “garland” derive from a different root (*sreǵ-) and are not the same etymon.
2. Proto-Germanic *slingwanan, “to wind, twist, swing, fling” (class III strong verb; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) — the winding motion extended to throwing because a thrown object rotates as it flies.
3. Old Norse slyngva (also slyngja), “to sling, fling, hurl” (Viking Age, before c. 1000 CE) — the form that entered English with the Scandinavian settlers.
4. Middle English slingen, “to hurl with a swinging rotation of the arm; to throw” (borrowed c. 1200–1300) — alongside native Old English slingan “to turn, twist, wind.”
5. English slung (16th c. onward; the ablaut past/past participle of sling, from the strong pattern sling–slang–slung, later levelled to sling–slung–slung) ── OUR WORD.
Note on nuance: the strong-verb levelling (the old past “slang” yielding to “slung” for both past and participle) took place in the 16th–17th centuries; “slang” survives only as a noun and verb of a different history (“thieves’ slang,” to insult).
