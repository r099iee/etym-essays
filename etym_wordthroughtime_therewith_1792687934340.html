- [Old English (c. 9th–11th c.)] — Attested as þǣrwiþ, expressing physical opposition, proximity, or direct interaction ("against that", "alongside that", "in return for that") — [perception: a concrete spatial and relational compound used in legal codes and chronicle narratives]
- [Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.)] — Spelled therwith / þerwiþ, expanding dramatically in romance literature and poetry to signify both instrumental agency ("by means of that") and immediate temporal succession ("thereupon", "at that moment") — [perception: an essential, versatile storytelling connective embraced by Chaucer and courtly poets]
- [Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)] — Standardized in the King James Bible, Cranmer's liturgy, and Tudor drama as a mark of balanced, rhythmic rhetorical style — [perception: regarded as dignified, elegant, and standard formal English, conveying both spiritual and moral weight]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Romanticism] — Began fading from everyday vernacular speech, becoming an intentional archaism in Romantic verse (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Keats) to evoke historical grandeur or reflective solemnity — [perception: viewed as high-literary, elevated, and poetic, distinct from common colloquial talk]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Largely obsolete in conversational English, surviving almost exclusively in specialized legal drafting (statutes, contracts, patents) and historical fiction — [perception: perceived as dense legalese or self-conscious antique styling]
