ROOT WORD 1 — GALLOPING Frankish (Germanic) *wala hlaupan "to run well" (c. 6th-7th c.) — a compound of *wala "well" + *hlaupan "to run, to leap," from Proto-Germanic *hlaupaną "to leap, to run" (ultimate PIE origin uncertain and debated; the Germanic root *hlaup- has no secure Indo-European etymology) ↓ Old French galoper "to gallop, of horses" (attested c. 9th-11th c.) — the Frankish phrase, applied to the running of horses, borrowed into the Gallo-Romance of northern France ↓ Middle French galoper / galop "to gallop; the gallop" (12th-14th c.) — verb and noun, spreading to the rest of Romance (Italian galoppare, Spanish galopar) ↓ Middle English galopen "to gallop" (13th c., borrowed from Old French) — English took the word directly for the horse's gait ↓ galloping "present participle of gallop" (14th c. onward) — the -ing form adjectivized for anything moving at a runaway gallop ROOT WORD 2 — HOOVES PIE *koph₂- "to beat, to strike" (c. 4000-3000 BCE) — the hoof was named as "the striking foot," the thing that beats the ground; Sanskrit śapha "hoof, claw" preserves the family ↓ Proto-Germanic *hōfaz "hoof" (c. 500 BCE - 200 CE) — descendant of the strike-root; Germanic daughters include Old English hōf, Old Norse hófr, Dutch hoef, German Huf ↓ Old English hōf "hoof" (7th-11th c.) → Middle English hof "hoof" (12th-15th c.) ↓ Modern English hoof (singular); plural hooves (regularized from the 17th-18th c. by analogy with the f/v alternation of wolf/wolves, alongside the older plural hoofs) ROOT WORD 3 — LUNGING PIE *dl̥nghós "long" (c. 4000-3000 BCE) — the long-root; Sanskrit dīrghá "long," Greek dolikhós "long," Latin longus "long," Proto-Germanic *langaz "long" ↓ Latin longus "long" (attested c. 3rd-1st c. BCE) — source of longitude, prolong, elongate ↓ Late Latin allongāre "to lengthen" (from ad- "to" + longus; c. 6th-8th c.) — also elongāre "to lengthen" ↓ Old French alongier / allonger "to lengthen" (9th-12th c.) ↓ French allonge "an extension; a thrust with the sword extended" (fencing terminology, 16th-17th c.) — the noun from the lengthened, extended arm-and-body attack ↓ English lunge (fencing term, borrowed 18th c.) → lunging "present participle of lunge" — the throwing of the whole body forward in one explosive extension Note on "lunging a horse": the equestrian training term is properly longe (from French longe, "a long rope," related to long) and is spelled differently from lunge "a thrust," although the two are often confused in modern usage.
