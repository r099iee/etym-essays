Spur (noun) has three great families of meaning, all connected by the same mental image: a pointed thing that juts out and drives forward. Its first and oldest sense is the riding instrument — a metal point or toothed wheel worn on a rider's heel for urging a horse. From that concrete tool spring both the abstract sense (anything that incites or urges) and the geometric senses (anything that projects like a point, from a mountain ridge to a flower's nectary). In the reading context the word is used geographically: a 'great spur of the Mountain' — a lateral ridge flung out from the main mass — toward which the travellers draw near.

Primary senses.

  • The riding instrument: a metal device strapped to the heel, ending in a spike (prick spur) or a revolving toothed wheel (rowel spur), used to urge a horse forward. Historically the spur was also a badge of rank: golden spurs were conferred at knighthood, and 'to win one's spurs' meant to be knighted, later to earn any distinction.
  • Figurative stimulus: anything that incites, urges, or drives action — 'the spur of necessity', 'the spur of ambition', 'a spur to effort'. The idiom on the spur of the moment (on a sudden impulse, without reflection) transfers the instant heel-touch of the rider to any unplanned action.
  • Geography: a lateral ridge or projection descending from a mountain or hill — 'a spur of the Mountain' — a long tongue of high ground running out from the main mass. This is the sense used in the source sentence and is standard in topography and mountaineering.
  • Botany: a slender tubular projection at the base of a flower's petal or sepal, often containing nectar — the defining feature of larkspurs, columbines and many orchids. The plant name larkspur (1570s) is itself 'lark + spur', from the flower's resemblance to a lark's heel-spike.
  • Zoology and anatomy: the horny pointed growth on the leg of a bird (the cock's spur) or on the hind limb of some mammals; in medicine, a calcaneal spur is a bony outgrowth on the heel bone — a usage that completes a neat circle, since the word began at the heel.
  • Railways and industry: a spur line is a short branch line connecting a main railway to a mine, factory or dock; the word names anything that branches off and projects sideways.
  • Architecture and other technical senses: in architecture a spur is a bracket or buttress at the base of a column; in geology it is a projecting ridge of rock; in forestry and viticulture it is a short shoot pruned back to bear fruit; in sailing a 'spur' may be a projecting timber on a boat's bow.

Semantic bridges. Every sense radiates from the instrument: (1) its function — urging a horse on — becomes the abstract 'incitement'; (2) its shape — a projecting point — becomes every kind of projection, from a mountain ridge to a flower's nectar tube to a railway branch. The ancient Germanic word for the instrument was itself derived from the word for 'ankle', since the spur is worn on the heel: the tool, the body part, the mountain and the metaphor are all one family.

Grammar and derivatives. As a verb, to spur means to urge on with or as if with spurs ('spurred by hunger'); the participle spurred is used literally and figuratively. Compounds include spur-of-the-moment, spur-wheel (a gear with projecting teeth), spurge (the plant, from Latin expurgare, unrelated), and the surnames Spurr and Spurdle. The word's energy is always forward: a spur pushes, pokes, and sets things in motion.
