The verb posited exhibits a deep and structured evolutionary history, illustrating how physical actions of placement and settlement in the ancient Mediterranean world evolved into sophisticated intellectual and philosophical operations.

1. Indo-European and Latin Origins:

  • The word derives from the Latin verb pōnere (contracted from Old Latin po-sinere, formed from the prefix po- meaning "away, off" and sinere meaning "to leave, let down, permit"), which was ultimately influenced by the widespread PIE root *dʰeh₁- ("to put, place, make").
  • The perfect passive participle of pōnere is positus ("having been placed, situated, or established"). From positus and its nominal derivative positio ("a placing, posture, theme, affirmative assertion"), Latin writers derived a wide network of philosophical, rhetorical, and legal terms.
  • In Classical Latin, Cicero and Seneca used pōnere not only for physically placing objects on ground or building walls, but also for setting down premises in philosophical dialogue (ponere quaestionem, "to pose a question or set a problem").

2. Medieval Scholasticism and Legal Positivism:

  • In medieval Scholastic philosophy and jurisprudence, the concept of lex posita ("posited law" or "positive law") emerged to distinguish laws enacted by human municipal authorities from lex naturalis ("natural law" discovered through divine reason). To posit something was to authoritatively establish it in written form.
  • The Latin participle positus served as the direct root for numerous English cognates, including position, positive, deposit, opposite, and composite.

3. Emergence in English and German Idealist Influence:

  • The English verb posit first appeared in philosophical writing during the mid-17th century (c. 1640–1660), coined directly from Latin positus to serve as a precise technical equivalent to "lay down as a postulate or fundamental proposition."
  • During the late 18th and 19th centuries, the term received a massive intellectual boost through English translations of German Idealist philosophy (Immanuel Kant, Johann Gottlieb Fichte, and G.W.F. Hegel). German philosophers made heavy use of the verb setzen ("to set, place, posit") and Setzung ("positing"). Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre (1794) established the act of the Ego positing itself (Tathandlung) as the foundational starting point of all human knowledge. English translators standardized posit and posited as the exact renderings for setzen and gesetzt.

4. Modern Scientific and Critical Theory Adoption:

  • In 20th-century philosophy of science (from logical positivism to Karl Popper and W.V.O. Quine), posited became standard terminology to describe theoretical entities—such as electrons, quarks, or latent variables—that are not directly observed but are posited by scientific models to explain experimental data.
  • In literary and cultural theory, Roland Barthes, Michel Foucault, and Jacques Derrida used the concept of positing to analyze how discourses construct reality by laying down invisible baseline assumptions. When Barthes writes of what the Jesuit linguist Jac van Ginneken "posited between writing and language," he highlights the creative, daring theoretical leap that bridged somatic physiology with textual semiotics.
