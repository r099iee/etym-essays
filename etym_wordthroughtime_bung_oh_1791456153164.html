1920s — First recorded as a hearty drinking toast among fashionable British speakers; a marker of cocktails, public-school high spirits and upper-class good fellowship.
1930s — Extends to a breezy farewell in Wodehouse and other comic fiction; now strongly associated with Bertie Wooster’s world of “old fruit” and “right-ho”.
1940s — Wartime and services slang keep it alive, but the tone becomes increasingly self-conscious and “period”.
1950s — A rare adjectival play (“quite bung-ho”) shows the phrase being used as a general label of approval; the expression is already nostalgic.
Late 20th c. — Falls out of everyday speech; survives in reprints, adaptations and affectionate pastiche of interwar Britain.
21st c. — Archaic and dated in dictionaries; recognised mainly through Wodehouse, Sayers, Hemingway and period drama, and used with deliberate comic or nostalgic effect.
