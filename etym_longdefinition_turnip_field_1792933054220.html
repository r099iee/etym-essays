Turnip-field is a compound noun naming a piece of farmland sown or planted with turnips: the round white-and-purple roots swelling underground while broad hairy leaves spread above.

In the source sentence the travellers pass along the edge of a huge turnip-field and come to a stout gate. The hugeness matters: this is not a cottage garden patch but open-field farming on a generous scale, the crop standing in long drilled rows to the horizon, and the gate marks the working entrance where carts enter for lifting and sheep enter for folding.

Core meaning in detail: The turnip (Brassica rapa) is a fast brassica grown for human tables and, since the agricultural revolution, for winter livestock feed. A turnip-field in autumn shows rank on rank of blue-green tops with the shoulder of each root pushing through the soil. Farmers hoe between drills in summer, thin the seedlings, lift roots from autumn onward, or fold sheep directly onto the standing crop so that the flock eats tops and roots while manuring the ground for next spring corn.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Sown acreage of turnips (primary literal sense, the source use): the standing crop in the ground, whether for table or fodder. The bridge is composition itself: turnip plus field on the model of cornfield and potato-field.
  • The Norfolk rotation engine (historical agricultural sense): since Townshend turnips enabled winter feed, the turnip-field became the hinge of the four-course rotation, abolishing the fallow year. The bridge is economic: without roots there are no winter cattle, without cattle no manure, without manure no wheat.
  • Sheep-fold and fertility (pastoral extension): a turnip-field eaten off by folded sheep is simultaneously larder and manure-spreader. The bridge is husbandry practice: the field feeds the flock and the flock feeds the field in one winter movement.
  • Comic rusticity (figurative and proverbial): turnips proverbially mark the simpleton, the turnip-head, the man raised among roots. The bridge is social snobbery: the cheapest common root becomes shorthand for the clod, so that the field itself smells of honest but unpolished country life.
  • Bloodless ghost and lantern (folklore offshoot): turnip lanterns hollowed for Hallowe'en before pumpkins arrived. The bridge is the root made vessel: the field crop becomes the carved glowing head on the gatepost, linking harvest to haunting.

Register and connotation: turnip-field is plain working English with a warmly rustic flavour. Agronomists file it under root breaks; novelists use it to place a scene instantly in autumn farmland, damp, earthy, and productive. Against wheat romance it is deliberately humble: no golden shimmer, just blue-green tops and muddy boots. Tolkien chooses it for exactly that honesty, the huge field and the stout gate announcing capable farming country where travellers can expect fences kept, stock fed, and gates shut behind them.
