- Old English — “lēaden” is a materials word: made of lead, used of pipes, roofs, weights, and glosses for Latin “plumbeus”.
- Middle Ages — “leden” adds weightiness; alchemy pairs lead with Saturn, the slow, cold, melancholy planet, seeding the word’s gloomy colour long before the poets find it.
- 16th–17th c. — poetic “leaden”: Spenser’s god of Sleep strikes with a leaden mace; “leaden sleep” and “leaden age” carry the metal’s dull heaviness into psychology and metaphor.
- 18th–19th c. — Romantic poetry deepens the word: Keats’s “leaden-eyed despairs” (1819); “leaden sky” becomes the stock phrase for a heavy, unmoving overcast; Wilde’s Happy Prince (1888) fuses literal and figurative — a heart of lead that can still break.
- 20th–21st c. — literary and journalistic: a leaden performance, a leaden pace, leaden gloom; slightly archaic, always heavier than “grey” or “slow”, because the metal itself remains in the word.
