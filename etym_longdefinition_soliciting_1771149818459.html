soliciting is the present participle and gerund of the verb to solicit, meaning fundamentally "asking for, requesting, or attempting to obtain something from someone", almost always with an undertone of persistence, urgency, earnestness, or repeated effort. In the literary context supplied, it captures a person in the thick of an intense, almost desperate campaign: he "had been dashing about, pulling strings and soliciting help from every potential backer he could find." Here the word conveys far more than a polite request — it signals an urgent, exhaustive, network-wide appeal in which the subject actively stirs up and agitates his entire circle of contacts, pressing each potential supporter until help is secured.

Etymological bridge. The word reaches back through Latin sollicitare "to disturb, agitate, stir up," built on the adjective sollicitus "thoroughly stirred, troubled, anxious," a compound of sollus "whole, entire" and citus "moved, roused" (from ciere "to set in motion"). The original image is of agitating someone's entire being — stirring the whole person into action. The sense of "stirring up" remained live in English for centuries: to solicit once meant "to disturb, to trouble, to excite to action," and only gradually narrowed toward "to entreat, to petition, to request earnestly."

Full range of modern senses (polysemy).

  • To ask for something earnestly or formally — e.g., to solicit donations, advice, help, or opinions; the plainest current sense and the one at work in the reading context.
  • To approach someone with a request or business offer — a company solicits customers; a salesperson solicits orders; a lawyer solicits clients. In business jargon, unsolicited offers are those that arrive unrequested.
  • To appeal for votes, subscriptions, or patronage — politicians solicit support; charities solicit subscriptions; canvassers solicit pledges.
  • To accost for the purpose of prostitution — a prostitute solicits clients on the street; "soliciting" is the legal charge for publicly offering sexual services. This is the sense most familiar in criminal law and everyday urban speech.
  • Legal-technical sense: solicitation of a crime — in law, to solicit is to incite, encourage, urge, or invite someone to commit an offence (e.g., solicitation to commit murder, soliciting a bribe).
  • Archaic: to disturb, trouble, or agitate — the root sense inherited from Latin, still visible in poetic or older prose ("solicitous care").
  • Related word family — solicitation (the act of requesting, especially legally or commercially), solicitor (a person who solicits; in British law, the junior branch of the legal profession), and solicitous (anxious, concerned, eager).

Register and nuance. Soliciting is more active and formal than "asking": it implies a deliberate, often repeated approach, frequently in public or institutional settings, and it carries a faint suggestion of pressure or self-interest. It differs from begging (which implies neediness and appeals to pity), from entreating (which implies earnest, emotional pleading), and from requesting (which is neutral and non-persistent). Because of its powerful association with street prostitution and with aggressive marketing, the word has acquired a slightly negative or cautious flavour in some contexts, yet in charitable and political settings it remains entirely respectable.

Usage notes. The verb commonly takes a direct object ("solicit contributions") or an infinitive construction ("solicit someone to sign"), and it is frequently paired with nouns like help, support, business, votes, donations, and custom. The gerund soliciting is also used as a noun ("the soliciting of funds"). In all its senses the word keeps the root image of deliberately moving another person out of passivity — of stirring the whole person to act.
