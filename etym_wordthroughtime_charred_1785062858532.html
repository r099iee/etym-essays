- 14th-16th centuries - 'Char' emerges from 'charcoal', the fuel made by slowly burning wood to carbon; to char is to turn the surface of something into coal-like blackened matter, the deliberate craft of the charcoal-burner.
- 16th-18th centuries - The word extends from wood to food and drink: charred crusts and charred meat, and in the 18th century the strongly roasted, literally charred barley that gives London porter its deep colour and roasty flavour.
- Contemporary sense - Charred still specifies a surface burned to carbon while the interior stays intact - a grilled crust, a scorched beam, a roasted barley kernel; it is a term of deliberate, controlled scorching rather than accidental fire.
