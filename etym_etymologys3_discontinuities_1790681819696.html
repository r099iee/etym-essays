The history of discontinuities is the history of continuity negated, and continuity itself is a Latinate abstraction built on one of the oldest Indo-European verbs of holding. PIE *ten- meant to stretch, extend, and by metaphorical extension to hold taut. In Italic this yielded Latin tenēre, to hold, keep, occupy — one of Latin's most prolific verbs, source of tenet, tenure, contain, obtain, sustain. Prefixed with con- (together), it produced continēre, to hold together, and the adjective continuus, literally held-together without gaps, hence uninterrupted in space or time. Cicero and later imperial writers already use continuus for unbroken walls, successive days, and continuous discourse, showing the spatial-to-temporal-to-logical bridge fully formed in antiquity.

From continuus Late Latin formed continuitās, an abstract noun of scholastic utility denoting unbroken connection. Borrowed via Old French continuité into Middle English as continuite (Chaucerian and Lydgatean attestations in senses of perseverance and unbroken extent), it remained a learned, chiefly philosophical word through the 16th century. The negative discontinuity was coined in the 17th century, the great age of prefixal analysis, by adding Latin dis- (apart, asunder, from PIE *dwis-, twice, in two, apart, also source of dish, distant, divide) to express want or reversal of continuity. Early attestations in Boyle, Barrow, and the Royal Society denote physical and mathematical breaks: discontinued motion, discontinuity of parts. The formation parallels discontinue, discontinuous, disunion, all products of the same early-modern appetite for precise privatives.

  • Phonetic path: PIE *ten- with nasal infix and Italic vowel lengthening gives Latin tenēre with stable /t/ and /n/; French palatalisation and loss of final syllables turns continuitatem into continuité /kɔ̃tinɥite/; Middle English re-Latinises spelling to continuity while anglicising stress to antepenultimate; prefix dis- assimilates only before certain consonants and remains /dɪs/ before /k/; plural -ies /ɪz/ follows the regular English morphophonemic rule for consonant + y.
  • Semantic drift: from concrete holding-together → abstract uninterruptedness → negated gaps in matter, motion, and argument → classified mathematical objects → Foucauldian epistemic ruptures. Each step preserves the core image of a line that either holds or snaps.
  • Socio-cultural context: the Reformation and Scientific Revolution needed a vocabulary for breaks with authority; the 19th-century professionalisation of mathematics needed classified discontinuities; the 1960s human sciences needed a banner word for anti-evolutionism. The plural discontinuities flourishes precisely in interdisciplinary polemic, where listing many mismatches is more damning than alleging one.
  • Literary attestations: beyond mathematics, Gibbon writes of discontinuities in empire, Lyell of discontinuities in strata, Foucault of discontinuities in discursive formations, and modern critics of C-B-S (conditioning-behaviourism-structuralism) of discontinuities with neighbouring behavioural disciplines — exactly the source usage, where the plural accuses a school of multiple, manifest failures to join up.

In contemporary Received Pronunciation /ˌdɪskɒntɪˈnjuːɪtiz/ the word retains its Latinate weight: five syllables, secondary stress on dis-, primary stress on -nu-, signalling learned register. The singular/plural alternation discontinuity/discontinuities is fully productive, and the mass sense (discontinuity as such) coexists with the count plural denoting tokens. Debated nuance: some historians derive Latin dis- strictly from PIE *dwis- while others posit contamination with *de-; the distinction does not affect the English sense, but lexicographers note it to avoid overstating a single clean root. What matters for learners is the transparent morphology: dis- undoes continuity, and the -ies counts the undoings.
