- 16th-17th century - 'industry' enters English (via Latin industria) meaning diligence, zealous application, purposeful activity — a private, personal virtue praised in conduct books and sermons; the industrious man is the man of steady habits.
- 18th century - the Enlightenment and political economy enshrine industry as the cardinal civic virtue; Adam Smith and his contemporaries treat it as the engine of national wealth, still an individual virtue but already economic in ambition.
- 19th century - the Industrial Revolution migrates the word from person to process: 'industry' comes to mean organized manufacturing, the factory system, and a branch of economic activity (the cotton industry); the personal-virtue sense recedes but stays alive in biography and literature.
- Contemporary - overwhelmingly economic and institutional: industry is business sectors, corporations, and production; the old virtue-sense survives chiefly in phrases like 'the industry of scholarship,' where it reads as deliberately old-fashioned.
