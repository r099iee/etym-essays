The biography of loquacity is the biography of a word that has always lived at a polite distance from ordinary speech. Unlike native English words for talking — speak, talk, chat, all from Germanic soil — loquacity arrived in English through the learned channels of Renaissance humanism, and it has never lost the slightly elevated, faintly censorious flavour of a word that was born in the Latin schoolroom rather than on the street. The Latin foundation.
  • At the heart of the word stands the Latin deponent verb loquī, "to speak, talk, say," used by every Latin author from Plautus and Terence to Cicero and Augustine. A deponent verb is one whose form is passive but whose meaning is active — loquor looks like "I am spoken" but means "I speak" — and this grammatical oddity is part of the word's texture in the source language.
  • The deep Indo-European ancestry of loquī is the great difficulty of the etymology. The handbooks sometimes print a root *tolkw-, "to speak, talk," and point to possible distant relatives, but the loqu- family is attested almost exclusively in Latin and its descendants. There is no secure Germanic cognate, no secure Sanskrit sibling, no secure Greek cousin. This absence means the PIE reconstruction rests on very slender evidence, and many careful etymologists simply label the origin as uncertain, noting that the root may be Italic-specific or even pre-Italic in origin.
From verb to adjective to abstract noun: Latin morphology.
  • Latin built its vocabulary of talkativeness by regular derivation. From loquī came the adjective loquāx (genitive loquācis), "talkative, given to talking," formed with the suffix -āx, which marks a tendency or inclination. This is the same suffix that produced audāx, "bold" (from audēre, "to dare"), tenāx, "tenacious" (from tenēre, "to hold"), and rapāx, "greedy" (from rapere, "to seize"). A loquāx person was one naturally inclined to speak.
  • From loquāx, Latin then derived the abstract noun loquācitās (genitive loquācitātis) by adding the suffix -tās, which turned adjectives into abstract qualities — the ancestor of English -ity and French -ité, as in civitas, "citizenship," from civis, "citizen." Loquācitās thus means literally "the quality of being talkative," i.e., talkativeness or garrulity.
The wider loqu- family and its learned richness.
  • The same root flowered across Latin into a whole garden of speech-words, and Renaissance English imported nearly all of them as learned loans. From loquī and its compounds English took eloquent and eloquence (from ēloquī, "to speak out," with the prefix ē-, "out"), elocution (from ēlocūtiō, "a speaking out"), colloquy and colloquial (from colloquium, "a speaking together," with con-/com-, "together"), soliloquy (from soliloquium, "talking alone," solus + loquī), obloquy (from obloquī, "to speak against," with ob-, "against, at"), and allocution (from allocūtiō, "a formal address," with ad-, "to").
  • This cluster shows a striking pattern: English did not inherit a single native word for "speak" from this root — it borrowed the whole family wholesale, at intervals between the 14th and 17th centuries, as part of the deliberate enrichment of English from Latin and Greek that accompanied the Renaissance. Loquacity entered alongside these companions, in the early 1600s, and the adjective loquacious followed in the 1660s.
Semantic drift within English.
  • When loquacity entered English it was a formal synonym for talkativeness, with a distinctly scholarly and slightly critical register. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century essayists and moralists used it of the fluent talker whose copiousness wearies the company, so that the word carried a mild social censure from the start.
  • In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word could also be used neutrally, or even with a touch of admiration, for the energetic abundance of the born conversationalist — the register in which Carlyle deploys it. In the famous passage that anchors this card, loquacity is not censured as chatter; it is described, with dark irony, as Mill's desperate defensive strategy. The fluency is still there, but its motive is terror, and the word's openness to this reading shows how flexible its valuation had become: pure abundance, morally colourless, waiting to be judged by the situation.
  • In the modern period loquacity has receded from everyday speech, displaced by "talkativeness," but it survives in literary, journalistic, and clinical usage. In clinical psychology, the related term logorrhea (Greek logos, "word," + rhein, "to flow") names the pathological, pressured overflow of speech seen in mania and certain neurological conditions, and "loquacity" is still used in medical descriptions of unusually copious but controlled talk.
  • The word's continuing life is thus double: in polite usage it names a human temperament with a faintly ironic tinge; in technical usage it approaches the clinical. It has never become colloquial, and its learned Latin costume is precisely what has let it carry the full weight of Carlyle's irony about a philosopher who talked on, desperately, rather than speak the truth.
Conclusion. Loquacity is a word whose history is written in Latin derivational machinery and Renaissance borrowing policy. From a disputed Indo-European root, through the classical Latin verb loquī and its derivative loquācitās, to the French loquacité and the English loquacity of the early seventeenth century, it has remained a word of the study and the essay — fluent, exact, and faintly amused at the human weakness it names.
