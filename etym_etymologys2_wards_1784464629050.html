CHAIN for the guard-root (the source of the lock sense). Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *wer- "to cover, watch, guard, perceive" (reconstructed, 5th–4th millennium BCE; an attention/protection complex attested by Latin verērī 'to fear, respect', vervex-related forms, and the wide Germanic guard-family). [Note: a separate homonymous PIE root *wer- 'to turn' produced the directional suffix -ward(s); the two roots have converged in spelling in English and must not be merged.] → Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *wardaz "guard, watcher" and *wardōną "to watch, guard" (reconstructed, late 2nd–1st millennium BCE; cf. Gothic -wards in place-keeper compounds, Old Norse vǫrðr 'watchman', Old High German wart 'watcher'). → Stage 3 — Old English weard "guard, watchman, keeper; protection, custody" (attested from the 8th century CE) and the verb weardian "to guard, keep watch"; also the productive compounds stig-weard 'hall-keeper' (steward) and weard-setl 'watch-post'. → Stage 4 — Middle English ward / warde "guard, custody, guardianship; a guarded place or room" (12th–15th centuries CE; also the verb warden 'to guard'). → Stage 5 — Modern English ward (noun and verb) "guard, custody, a guarded place, a person under guardianship", from the 16th century CE onward; the locksmithing specialization attaches the guard-image to the internal projections of a lock. → Stage 6 — Modern English WARDS — the card's target form: the plural of the lock-craft sense, "the internal ridges or projections of a lock that guard against any key without matching grooves." NOTE on the history: the lock-sense is a native specialization of the guard-meaning (the ridges 'guard' the mechanism) and not a borrowing; it is attested in English from the medieval/early modern locksmiths' trade. The distinct directional suffix -ward(s) "toward" comes from a different PIE root *wer- 'to turn' and is only a spelling-convergent relative. Deeper Indo-European comparisons of *wer- 'to guard' are debated in detail, but the Germanic and Old English chain is secure.
