- Old English — the compound is born: wegside names the side of the way, the margin every road implies, in charters and boundaries.
- Middle Ages — piety plants it: wayside crosses and chapels rise for travellers’ prayers, and the margin becomes holy ground.
- 1611 — the parable fixes the idiom: the Authorised Version’s seed that fell by the way side gives English its great metaphor for wasted beginnings.
- 18th–19th c. — inns and cottages: the wayside inn and the wayside cottage become stock settings of novels, rest and lowliness made place.
- 20th c. — motors pave it: verges, lay-bys, and service stations inherit the margin’s function while the old word keeps its literary glow.
- Today — margin as moral: literal use stays pastoral and nostalgic, while fall by the wayside thrives in pulpits, barracks, and boardrooms.
