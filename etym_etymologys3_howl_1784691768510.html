The word howl is one of the oldest kinds of word in human language: an imitation. It descends from an Indo-European expressive base *kēu- or *ū-, formed directly from the sound of a long, open-mouthed cry — the same raw material that produced Latin ululāre, Greek ololyzein and the Sanskrit ululīkāra, the ritual wailing cry of the Rigveda, and that named the owl (Old English ūle) after its hollow hooting. Such words are born fully formed, mimicking the sound they name, and they survive with astonishing fidelity across millennia: the shape of the cry — a long, open, rising vowel, often preceded by a hoot-like consonant and followed by a liquid or continuant — is recognisably the same in Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Germanic and English. 1. The Germanic family and the sound changes The Germanic branch built from this base the verb *hūlijanan / *hūlōną, and its descendants ring in every northern European language: German heulen, Dutch huilen, Swedish and Norwegian yla, Danish hyle. The English word itself may have a native Old English ancestor in hūlian, but the Middle English verb houlen, first recorded in the thirteenth century, may equally have been reinforced from Middle Low German hūlen or from Scandinavian yla during the Viking and Hanseatic centuries — the word was current along every channel by which northern speech entered England. The phonological development is the story of the long open vowel: the Germanic ū sound, already the very sound of a howl, was diphthongised in the course of the Great Vowel Shift in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, so that hūlen /huːlən/ became modern howl /haʊl/ — the vowel itself sliding into the long, rising, wailing shape that the word names. 2. The early record and the settled senses From its first records, houlen was the verb of wolves and dogs, of the wind, and of human beings overtaken by grief beyond the reach of words. The Old Testament's "howling wilderness" (King James Bible, 1611) fixed the sound in the landscape of desolation, and the noun howl, recorded from the sixteenth century, allowed the cry to be pointed at: the howl of a wolf, the howl of a gale, the howl of a man in pain. In the nineteenth century the jocular howler — a yowling error, a glaring blunder — showed how far the word could travel from its canine origins while keeping its note of uncontrolled release. 3. The senses in summary
  • Imitative base *kēu- / *ū- (PIE): the open-mouthed cry; Latin ululāre, Greek ololyzein, Sanskrit ululīkāra, Old English ūle "owl."
  • Proto-Germanic *hūlijanan / *hūlōną → Old English hūlian; Middle English houlen (13th century), possibly reinforced from Middle Low German hūlen and Scandinavian yla.
  • Core senses (13th century–present): wolves, dogs, the wind, and grieving or anguished people howl.
  • Noun howl (16th century–present): the cry itself; the howl of wolf, gale, or man.
  • Extended senses (19th–20th centuries–present): sirens and storms howl; a howl of laughter; a howler as a glaring blunder.
4. Socio-cultural and literary life Through all these centuries the word never narrowed; it remains what it always was — the raw, ungoverned, open-throated vocalisation of animal distress, whether the animal is a wolf, a wind or a man. Its register is deliberately undignified: to howl is to lose speech, to fall out of language into pure sound, and that is why writers reach for it at moments of extremity and of comedy alike. Tolkien chooses it with exact comic cruelty in The Hobbit, Chapter 2: the troll, a being that has stolen human speech and conducted a homely domestic squabble in rustic English, is reduced by one blow to a howl — the inarticulate cry of a struck dog, the sound of appetite and brute sensation beneath the borrowed language. The narrator's relish ("I can tell you") completes the effect: the reader is invited to share the pleasure of hearing a monster, for once, speak in its true voice. A word five millennia deep in imitative history, born as the sound of a long open cry, is used to strip a troll of its humanity in a single syllable.
