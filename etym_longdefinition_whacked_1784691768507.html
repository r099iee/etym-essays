Whacked is the past tense and past participle of the verb whack, "to strike forcefully with a sharp, resounding blow," especially with a stick, branch, or flat object. The core physical sense is a single, sharp, ringing strike — crisper and brighter than a thump, harder and heavier than a slap. Beyond this physical core the word has developed a remarkable family of informal senses that a full account must separate carefully: as an adjective, chiefly British, Australian and New Zealand, whacked means "exhausted, utterly tired" (I'm whacked); in American slang whacked out means "crazy, eccentric" or "under the influence of drugs"; the noun whack means "a sharp blow," but also "a share or portion" (a fair whack, top whack, chiefly British) and "an attempt" (have a whack at it); the phrase out of whack, chiefly American, means "out of order, not working properly"; the agent noun whacker (British and Antipodean colloquial) means "something remarkably large"; and in American crime slang, to whack someone means to kill or murder them, the sense behind the phrase whack job for a killer. Like thump and thwack, the word is echoic in origin: it was coined to imitate the crisp crack of a solid blow, and its shape — brisk w or wh-, short vowel, abrupt -ack — is its meaning. The sense in Tolkien's The Hobbit, Chapter 2. The narrator describes how, as Bert and William roll about "kicking and thumping" near the fire, "Tom whacked at them both with a branch to bring them to their senses — and that of course only made them madder than ever." The word is chosen with exact acoustic discrimination: thumping, in the same sentence, is the dull, heavy music of the wrestling trolls, while whacked is the bright, ringing crack of a branch swung by a third troll — a sound of quite different pitch, whose very sharpness conveys the hopelessness of the gesture. The verb also carries a fine colloquial energy; it is a homely, vigorous word, perfectly at home in the narrator's fireside storytelling voice, and its briskness makes the failure of the attempt all the funnier. Primary senses of whacked / whack
  • Past tense and past participle of whack (the card's core sense) — struck forcefully with a sharp, resounding blow, especially with a stick, branch or flat object.
  • Adjective, chiefly British, Australian, New Zealand — exhausted, utterly tired, worn out ("I'm whacked").
  • American slang, whacked out — crazy, eccentric; also under the influence of drugs.
  • Noun whack — a sharp blow; also, chiefly British, a share or portion ("a fair whack", "top whack") and an attempt ("have a whack at it").
  • Phrase out of whack, chiefly American — out of order, not working properly; also "out of line, inconsistent."
  • American crime slang — to whack = to kill or murder; whack job = a killer.
  • Agent noun whacker (British and Antipodean colloquial) — something remarkably large.
Polysemy: how the senses developed
  • The physical blow came first: an echoic coinage (c. 1712, probably from thwack) for a sharp, ringing strike. The noun "a blow" and the verb "to strike" are the stem from which everything else grew.
  • The "share" sense arose in the 18th–19th centuries from the image of dividing spoils or goods by giving each person his whack — his allotted portion, as if cut off by a decisive blow.
  • The "attempt" sense (have a whack at it) grows naturally from the idea of taking a swing at a task: to try something is to take a crack at it, a whack at it.
  • Out of whack, recorded in late 19th-century American English, was first used of machinery that had been knocked out of alignment by a blow — a thing literally whacked out of line.
  • The "exhausted" sense of whacked (20th century, British and Antipodean) pictures the strength knocked out of a person as by a blow: one is whacked, done in, flat.
  • Whacked out and the murder sense are darker American extensions of the same image — the mind or the victim knocked out of proper working order.
Distinctions from near-synonyms Whacked must be distinguished from its echoic neighbours. Thwacked is the older form (attested from the early 16th century) and survives as the word's dignified elder sibling, more bookish and archaic; thumped and pounded are heavier and duller, describing impact without a sharp crack; smacked, of the open hand, is sharper still but lighter and lacks the weight of a stick; clouted (from clout, "a blow") is an older, blunter word; walloped is more violent and thudding; and belted is stronger slang for a heavy blow. Whack sits among them as the crisp, resounding, idiomatically fertile middle term.
