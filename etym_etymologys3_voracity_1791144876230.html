voracity descends from one of the oldest and most visceral Indo-European roots for consumption, PIE *gʷerh₃- meaning "to swallow, to devour." This root is remarkable for its stability: across thousands of years it consistently denoted violent, open-throated ingestion, whether by humans, animals, fire, or disease. It produced Sanskrit girati "he swallows," Greek bora "food, prey," Latin vorare "to devour," and through Latin a whole family of English words including devour, carnivore, herbivore, omnivore, and voracious. The history of voracity is therefore the history of how a physical act of the throat became a moral and psychological metaphor.

In Latin, vorare was never neutral eating. Edere meant to eat; vorare meant to gulp, bolt, swallow whole. From it came vorax (genitive voracis), "greedy, ravenous, insatiable," applied to people, beasts, fire, whirlpools, and even time. Cicero and Sallust used vorax morally, to condemn gluttons and spendthrifts. Voracitas, first attested in late Republican and Imperial prose, nominalised this quality: the state or habit of devouring. It belonged to moral philosophy and natural history — Pliny uses cognates to describe ravenous animals and consuming fires.

  • PIE to Italic: *gʷerh₃- yields Proto-Italic *gʷor-ā-; initial labiovelar *gʷ- regularly gives Latin v- (compare *gʷih₃-wo- > vivus). The long vowel and -are infinitive fix vorare as a first-conjugation verb of forceful consumption.
  • Classical Latin expansion: vorax, voracitas, vorago ("abyss, chasm that swallows") all radiate from the same throat-image. Vorago shows the metaphor already widening from stomach to landscape.
  • Late Latin and Christian moralisation: Church fathers use voracitas among vices of gula (gluttony). The word gains a sin-register: not just hunger but disordered desire.
  • Old and Middle French mediation: Old French vorace (13th c.) and Middle French voracité (14th–15th c.) carry the Latin moral-natural sense into vernacular bestiaries and sermons. English, which already had greedy and ravenous, borrows the learned form for elevated prose.
  • Early Modern English borrowing (c. 1520s–1600s): Voracity enters English directly from Latin voracitas and via French voracité. Early attestations (Thomas Elyot, later Bacon and Browne) use it for both literal appetite and bookish hunger — "voracity of reading."
  • 18th–19th century natural history: Naturalists describe the voracity of sharks, wolves, insects, and fire. Durant's sentence — "indiscriminate voracity" limited by fire and agriculture — echoes this usage: primitive man as an omnivorous devourer, eating whatever could be seized.
  • Modern metaphorical widening: Economic voracity, sexual voracity, voracity of war, media voracity. The throat-metaphor now covers any system that consumes resources, attention, or people without discrimination.

Phonetically the word barely changed: Latin /woːˈraːkitaːs/ > French /voʁasite/ > English /vəˈræsɪti/. Stress shifted to the second syllable, vowels reduced, but the v-r skeleton survived intact for three millennia. Semantically the shift is from act (swallow) to character (greedy) to abstraction (the quality of being insatiable). That is why voracity today sounds more literary and condemnatory than hunger or appetite: it retains the Latin moral judgment that to devour indiscriminately is to be beast-like, uncontrolled, and dangerous. Durant chooses it precisely to evoke that pre-civilised, animal condition.
