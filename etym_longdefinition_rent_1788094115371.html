In the sentence at hand, RENT is the past tense (and past participle) of the archaic but still living verb rend — 'to tear, split, or wrench apart with force'. The cries and howls of the wolf-riders rent the air: the sound violently pierced and split the stillness of the sky, as if the noise itself were tearing a fabric. This violent, physical sense is the root from which all the 'tear' meanings of the word branch. Crucially, however, modern English RENT is really two separate words that happen to share one spelling and one pronunciation (a homographic pair): the 'tear' word (past tense of rend) and the 'payment' word (from Old French rente). Any full definition must treat both families, because both are fully alive in the language today.

1. RENT as the past of REND — 'tore, split violently'

  • Core sense: to tear or lacerate with a violent pulling or wrenching action; to split apart along a line of weakness. 'The cries rent the air' = the sound tore through the air; 'a lightning bolt rent the oak' = split it in two. The verb rend is recorded from Old English rendan 'to tear, cut, lacerate' (8th century), and its past form has been rent since Middle English.
  • Past-participial adjective: rent also means 'torn, split, disrupted': 'a rent cloak', 'the veil of the temple was rent in twain' (the famous image of Matthew 27:51 in the Authorised Version), 'rent asunder' (torn apart — of a marriage, a kingdom, a church, a friendship).
  • Noun from the verb: a rent is the tear or gap left behind: 'a rent in the sail', 'a rent in the social fabric'. This noun is recorded from the late 14th century and survives in literary and technical prose (a rent in geological strata, a rent in a membrane in biology).
  • Register today: the 'tear' family is now markedly literary, archaic, or biblical; everyday speech uses tore or ripped. But the word remains fully current in high registers: headlines speak of a party 'rent by factionalism' or a country 'rent apart' by civil war.
  • Metaphorical bridge: because rending is violent and leaves ragged, hard-to-repair edges, rent metaphorically denotes any painful, violent division — of communities, loyalties, families, or of silence and the air themselves. The semantic logic is direct: what is rent is not merely separated but wounded.

2. RENT as payment — 'money paid for the use of property'

  • Core sense: a periodic (usually monthly, quarterly, or yearly) payment made by a tenant to a landlord for the use of land, a building, or an object. Recorded in English from the late 12th–13th century via Old French rente.
  • Verb use: 'to rent' = to pay for temporary use ('rent a flat', 'rent a car', 'rent a bike'); the landlord's side in British English is 'to let' or 'to rent out'. The verb is recorded from c. 1500 and has become the standard word for hiring property and vehicles.
  • Legal and economic senses: ground rent, rack rent (a rent at the full value of the land), peppercorn rent (a nominal rent kept to preserve a lease), rent review, rent arrears, rent control and rent regulation (state limits on increases).
  • Economic jargon: economists use economic rent and rent-seeking for income derived not from productive effort but from ownership, monopoly, or privileged position — a 19th–20th-century extension of the medieval idea of 'rente' as unearned regular income.
  • The French connection: in French-derived finance, 'rentes' are government bonds or annuities, and a 'rentier' lives off investment income — the old sense of 'regular revenue' that the word carried when first borrowed.

3. The convergence — the semantic bridge between the two words

  • The 'tear' word descends from Old English rendan; the 'payment' word descends from Latin reddita 'things given back' via Old French rente 'income'. They collided in Middle English (both spelled rente) and have been homographs ever since.
  • Native speakers usually assume one word with two meanings; in fact they are two words with one shape. That is why 'rent a car' has nothing to do with tearing, and 'a rent in the curtain' has nothing to do with money — a classic example of convergent etymology that any full definition must flag explicitly.

Distinguishing the two RENTs: the tear-word is a native Germanic verb with the -t past pattern (send/sent, lend/lent); the payment-word is a Romance borrowing. They rhyme, are spelled alike, yet diverge completely in origin — a fact that vexes learners of English and is a favourite of lexicographers illustrating how spelling and sound can mask utterly separate histories.
