- 15th century irrelevance - enters English from Latin meaning not pertinent to the matter: impertinence first names irrelevance, impropriety of relation, discourse that does not belong to the subject.
- 16th-17th century impropriety - shifts from "not pertinent to the subject" toward "not pertaining to one's own business or station," and begins to carry the sting of rudeness and presumption.
- 18th-19th century insolence and intrusion - settles as a formal word for meddling, prying, or presumptuous conduct; as the Mill passage shows, it censures the trespass of lifting a veil on a private mystery.
- Contemporary use - still formal and literary, meaning insolence, cheek, or unwarranted intrusion; in casual speech it is somewhat dated, replaced by "cheek," "nerve," or "rudeness."
