- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Attested as ġehēawen (past participle of hēawan), describing the physical felling of trees, the cutting of wood and stone, and brutal sword blows in battle (hacking through armor and shields in heroic poetry like Beowulf).
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - Appears as hewen / y-hewen, solidifying as the technical architectural term for dressed masonry (freestone) and timber squaring for cathedral rafters and castle battlements.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - Maintained in the King James Bible (e.g. "hewn stone", "rough-hewn") and Shakespeare to signify both physical masonry and the rustic, unpolished character of country folk.
- 18th to 19th Century - Develops extended figurative and philosophical applications; compound rough-hewn becomes a standard character descriptor for rugged, honest, but unpolished individuals, while the verbal idiom to hew to emerges to mean strictly adhering to a line or standard.
- Contemporary Usage - Retained primarily as an evocative participial adjective in architectural, fantasy, and literary prose describing monumental stone excavation, timber craft, or rugged human countenances.
