BEBOTHER is a transparent English compound of two elements; the linear chains for each element follow in isolation. Chain 1 — the intensive prefix be-: PIE *h₁epi 'at, on, near, by' (reconstructed; the source most often cited for Germanic *bi) → Proto-Germanic *bi- 'by, near, about; productive verbal prefix' (attested through reflexes) → Old English be-/bi- 'around, about; intensifying and transitivizing verbal prefix' (attested from 8th c. manuscripts) → the productive English pattern be- + noun/verb (e.g. beset, bedevil 1570s, bewilder 17th c., belittle 1780s, befuddle 19th c.) → be- + bother → BEBOTHER (late 19th c. jocular intensive). Chain 2 — the base verb bother (origin uncertain; non-PIE): Theory 1 (Anglo-Irish): Old Irish bodar 'deaf' → Irish bodhar 'deaf, deafened, stunned with noise' → Irish bódhradh 'a deafening, an annoyance' (the 'deafen with clamour' image) → English bother (verb and noun, 'to trouble, pester, vex', rising c. 1718; strong in Anglo-Irish literary use — Swift, Sheridan). Theory 2 (Scots): Scots bauther 'to trouble, fuss, confuse' — grouped with the expressive fuss-words pother/puther (16th–17th c., origin uncertain). → English bother (standard colloquial; also the interjection 'bother!' and noun 'a bother') → + be- → BEBOTHER. Note on the debate: the origin of bother is genuinely uncertain. The Irish bodhar link is the leading proposal but is not universally accepted; the Scots bauther/pother cluster is a competing or complementary line. The b-v-th-r shape is sound-symbolic of muttered complaint, and the word family is too late and too colloquial for any secure ancient ancestry. The be- element, by contrast, is a securely attested Germanic inheritance; whether PIE *h₁epi alone or a conflation with *h₂m̥bʰi 'around' produced Germanic *bi is debated among handbooks.
