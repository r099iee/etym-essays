Artifices is the plural noun of artifice (pronounced ART-i-fiss-iz), denoting cleverly contrived devices, expedients, stratagems or tricks. In the source sentence innumerable artifices for fishing and hunting, it means ingenious tools, contraptions, methods and ruses devised by early humans — nets, traps, snares, lures, weirs, hooks — admired as inventive craftsmanship rather than condemned as deceit.

The word lives between two poles that share one idea: skilled making. At the neutral-technical pole, an artifice is an ingenious contrivance, an artificial aid that extends nature: a fishing weir is an artifice, a lever is an artifice, a mnemonic is an artifice of memory. At the moral-pejorative pole, an artifice is a cunning trick intended to deceive or manipulate: flattery is an artifice, a forged document is an artifice, greenwashing is an artifice. Plural artifices multiplies either: either many inventions or many wiles, and tone must be read from context.

  • Technical-device sense (as in source): artifices of prehistoric life — barbed harpoons, tidal fish-traps, decoy ducks, pitfall snares, poisoned baits. Here artifices is laudatory, close to technologies, contrivances, appliances, innovations. Anthropologists still write of lithic artifices (worked stones) in this neutral archaeological sense.
  • Stratagem-trick sense (commonest today): political artifices, amorous artifices, legal artifices, rhetorical artifices. Here artifices is pejorative, close to ruses, wiles, ploys, dodges, subterfuges. Example: he saw through her artifices; by what artifices did they win the election?
  • Rhetorical-literary sense: artifices of style — alliteration, plot twists, unreliable narration, cliffhangers. Neutral to mildly critical: admired as craft by some, dismissed as artificiality by others who prefer plain nature.
  • Social-manner sense: artifices of politeness — studied smiles, calculated compliments, strategic silences. Implies insincerity without outright fraud.

Polysemy development is a classic amelioration-to-pejoration loop. Latin artificium meant craftsmanship; skill naturally evokes both admiration (what skill!) and suspicion (what cunning conceals?). English split the evaluation: art kept the prestige, artificial acquired the taint of fakeness, artificer remained honourable (a skilled maker), while artifice/artifices drifted toward trick because observers focus on the concealment inherent in clever devices — a trap works by hiding, a rhetorical flourish works by dazzling. The source sentence preserves the older admiring sense because traps for fish are openly admired; no moral victim is deceived except prey.

  • Number nuance: singular artifice often denotes the abstract quality (full of artifice, without artifice meaning with/without cunning), while plural artifices almost always denotes countable instances (many artifices). Innumerable artifices thus stresses prolific inventiveness.
  • Collocations: devise, invent, contrive, deploy, employ, resort to, see through, expose artifices; cunning, subtle, ingenious, innumerable, wily artifices. Prepositions: artifice for + purpose (artifices for hunting), artifice of + domain (artifices of diplomacy).
  • Register: formal-literary. Everyday speech prefers trick, gadget, device, ploy. Artifices appears in history, criticism, law and moral essay, lending an elevated, slightly archaic gravity.
  • Distinctions: artifice vs artifact/artefact — an artifact is the surviving object (a pot), an artifice is the cleverness or trick behind it; artifice vs artfulness — artfulness can be charming, artifice leans scheming; artifices vs artificers — artificers are the makers, artifices their makings/tricks.

In short, artifices means many clever makings, celebrated as ingenuity when they overcome nature, censured as trickery when they manipulate people. Readers of early-human history should hear inventive praise; readers of political novels should hear wary condemnation.
