• Clepsydra (Water Clock): Similar because it is an ancient timekeeping device developed to measure the progression of hours; differs because a clepsydra measures time through the regulated flow of liquid and functions equally well in darkness or indoors, whereas a sun-dial depends entirely on direct sunlight and solar shadows.
  • Hourglass (Sandglass): Similar because it is an analog instrument used to track the passage of time without mechanical gears or electrical power; differs because an hourglass measures a fixed, finite duration of elapsed time (e.g. one hour) upon being inverted, whereas a sun-dial indicates the continuous, absolute solar time of day.
  • Astrolabe: Similar because it is a sophisticated historical astronomical instrument utilizing celestial coordinates and solar angles; differs because an astrolabe is a multi-purpose portable handheld navigational and astronomical calculating disc, whereas a sun-dial is specifically designed and fixed to project shadows onto a calibrated dial face for telling time.
  • Clock (Mechanical Horologe): Similar because both display hours and minutes on a numbered face; differs because a mechanical or electronic clock operates via an internal escapement, pendulum, quartz crystal, or battery to indicate uniform mean time independently of weather, whereas a sun-dial tracks variable apparent solar time dictated by Earth's orbital rotation.
  • Gnomon / Shadow Clock: Similar because it operates on the same optical principle of casting a shadow to measure solar movement; differs because a simple gnomon is merely the upright rod or pillar itself, whereas a sun-dial comprises both the gnomon and the mathematically calibrated, graduated dial plate marked with specific hour lines.
  • Chronometer: Similar because both serve to measure time; differs because a chronometer is an ultra-precise, portable mechanical or digital instrument designed for maritime navigation and exact standard timekeeping regardless of environmental conditions, lacking the solar-shadow mechanism of a sun-dial.
  • Armillary Sphere: Similar because both use rings and axes aligned with Earth's celestial equator and rotational axis to model celestial mechanics; differs because an armillary sphere is primarily a three-dimensional astronomical globe of interlocking rings used for theoretical modeling and observation, though equatorial sundials may be constructed in armillary form.
