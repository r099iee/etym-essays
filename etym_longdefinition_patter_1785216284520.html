Patter is a noun denoting a rapid succession of light, quick, tapping sounds — the pattering of rain on leaves, of small feet on a floor, of hail on glass, or, as on this card, of a beating heart. It is intimately onomatopoeic: the syllables pat-pat-pat imitate the very sound they name, a series of light, rapid, slightly irregular impacts. As a verb, to patter means to make such a sound, or to move with light quick steps; the noun is the sound itself made substantive. The word is built from the echoic verb pat (“to strike lightly,” 14th century) with the frequentative suffix -er, which marks repeated or continued action — the same machinery that gave clatter, chatter, and flicker. A patter is therefore not a single tap but a stream of taps, a small percussion played at speed.

In the literary context preserved on this card — “his heart all of a patter and a pitter” — patter is J.R.R. Tolkien’s word, from The Hobbit (1937), for the panicky rhythm of Bilbo’s heart as he trots through a dark tunnel with his little sword held in front of him and one hand feeling the wall. The nuance is everything. A heart that goes “all of a patter” is not merely beating fast; it is fluttering and tripping over itself, a small animal’s panic made audible. The phrase patter and a pitter, with its rhyme and playful doubling, also carries Tolkien’s characteristic narrative tone: the moment is genuinely frightening, but the narrator describes the fear with gentle, almost amused affection, turning the sound of the heart into a nursery jingle. This is the childlike music of anxiety, and patter is its leading note. Heavy words would ruin the effect: thump and pound would make the fear gross and overwhelming, whereas patter keeps it small, quick, and almost comical.

The word is genuinely polysemous, and the senses fall into two families of quite different origin. The sound family (imitative) covers:

  • Rapid light taps, noun: the quick succession of impacts made by rain, hail, small feet, or a racing heart.
  • To make such a sound, verb: “rain pattered on the roof”; “his feet pattered along the corridor.”
  • To move with light quick steps, verb: of an animal or child scampering, “to patter across the floor.”
  • As a component of reduplicatives: pitter-patter, pit-a-pat, pitty-pat, all imitating the double beat of heart, feet, or raindrops.

The speech family is a separate word — a homograph of unrelated origin — that has come to dominate a different register of English:

  • Rapid glib talk, noun: a salesman’s or showman’s smooth, quick stream of words — “the patter of a conjuror,” “a sales patter.”
  • To talk rapidly and glibly, verb: “he pattered on about his wares.”
  • Underworld jargon, historical: 17th–18th century thieves’ cant, called flash patter or canting patter — the coded fast-talk of rogues.
  • Patter song, theatrical jargon (19th c.): a comic song delivered at great speed, perfected by W.S. Gilbert and Arthur Sullivan in operettas such as The Mikado (1885); also any rapid comic monologue.
  • Magician’s and showman’s chatter, modern: the running commentary that covers a trick or presents an act.

The two families meet only in sound — both are quick, light, and rapid — and their shared register of rapidity is what lets a single English word cover both raindrops and rhetoric. The historical bridge for the speech sense is striking: the word comes from paternoster, the Latin for “Our Father,” the opening of the Lord’s Prayer. In the Middle Ages, to “patter” meant to recite the paternoster, hence to mumble prayers; since prayers were often muttered quickly and mechanically, the verb shifted to meaning any rapid mumbling or talk, and by the 17th century the noun patter meant glib, fast speech — especially the fast talk of street salesmen and rogues. Modern English thus keeps one patter for the sound of rain and another for the sound of a salesman, and context alone tells them apart.
