The hora in this passage is a Tahitian plant name denoting a fish-stupefying plant whose pounded material, mixed with the huteo nut, was thrown into streams to intoxicate fish so they floated and could be caught by hand. It belongs to the Pacific fish-poison complex centred on Barringtonia asiatica (fish-poison tree, hutu / hotu / futu), whose seeds, bark and leaves contain saponins that stun fish. In short, hora here means the intoxicating Tahitian plant used as a narcotic fishing admixture.

This sense must be separated from the far commoner homograph Latin hora meaning hour, season or time, source of English hour and horoscope. The two words share spelling by coincidence across unrelated language families — Austronesian versus Indo-European — and have no semantic connection. Dictionaries that gloss only hour / time are describing the Latin homograph, not the Tahitian ethnobotanical term in this context.

Detailed senses and nuances:

  • 1. Core ethnobotanical sense — Tahitian fish-poison plant: the primary meaning in this passage; a shore plant or tree product pounded and cast into water to stupefy fish; used with the huteo nut as a binary mixture; implies saponin or rotenone-like ichthyotoxic action, slow release while sinking, fish floating leisurely and gathered at will.
  • 2. Extended sense — the stupefying preparation itself: by metonymy the word can cover the intoxicating mixture prepared from the plant, parallel to how huteo covers both nut and pounded pulp; the passage says mixture prepared from the huteo nut or the hora plant, treating each as a material source.
  • 3. Taxonomic neighbourhood — Barringtonia / hutu complex: comparative Polynesian linguistics links hora to variants hutu, hotu, huteo, futu and Proto-Oceanic *putun for Barringtonia asiatica; in modern botany the accepted name is Barringtonia asiatica (family Lecythidaceae), with vernaculars fish-poison tree, putat, butong, hutu; hora survives as a historical Tahitian variant rather than a current standard common name.
  • 4. Possible narrower sense — companion herb rather than the tree itself: because the text pairs huteo nut or hora plant, hora may denote a secondary leafy admixture pounded with the nut, comparable to Tahitian horahora for mustard-like shore herbs; the source does not resolve whether hora is the tree itself or its herbal adjunct, and both readings occur in the fishing literature.
  • 5. Latin homograph — hour, season, appointed time: not the sense here but essential for disambiguation; Latin hora from Greek hōra means division of time, season, hour, and by extension the Horae (Seasons), horoscope and canonical hours; English hour, horary, horology descend from it; no bridge connects this to fish poisoning except accidental spelling identity.
  • 6. Figurative and transferred uses — none established for the plant: unlike the Latin word, Tahitian hora has no recorded metaphorical extension in English to time, opportunity or ritual; it remains a quoted exoticism confined to anthropological and botanical catalogues.
  • 7. Register and usage constraints: always a foreign borrowing, typically lower-cased and unitalicised in older anthropology, never pluralised or verbalised in English; appears only in lists of ichthyotoxic plants alongside derris, tephrosia, mundulea and Barringtonia; modern writers prefer the scientific name and relegate hora to a historical synonym.
  • 8. Semantic bridge summary: plant to poison to method — the plant name extends to the pounded pulp, then to the act of stupefying fish, then to the ease of capture described as fish drunk with it floating leisurely; the bridge is purely technological and ecological, driven by saponin chemistry, not by any abstract temporal metaphor.

In the reading-immersion sentence the function is recipe-like: tribes threw narcotics into streams; the Tahitian example specifies the huteo nut or hora plant as the intoxicant source. Recognising hora as ethnobotanical material, not time, unlocks the whole scene of dammed pools, slowly sinking pulp, drunken floating fish and anglers gathering them at will.
