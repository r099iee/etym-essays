“Maiden” entered English from its Old English ancestor meaning an unmarried young woman — a girl of marriageable age, with a core of virginity and innocence.
“Maiden” through the centuries remained the elevated, literary word — ballads, romances, and formal writing preferred “maiden” to plain “girl,” and it gathered the colour of purity, defence, and pathos.
“Maiden” also built a family of secondary senses: a maiden voyage (a ship’s first), a maiden speech, a maiden name — the word meaning “first” or “unused,” spreading from the young woman to anything in its first state.
“Maidens” today survives mostly in archaic, literary, or fairy-tale register — the vulnerable young women of ballads and dragons’ ravages — a word that marks age, sex, and defenselessness at once.
