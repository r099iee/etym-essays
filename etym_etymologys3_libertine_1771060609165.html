The history of libertine is the story of a word that travelled from the slave-market to the philosopher's study to the scandal-sheet, carrying with it the West's oldest anxiety about freedom. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *h₁lewdʰ-, "to grow up, to rise, to mount," a root whose most striking legacy is the way it named communities. Across the Indo-European world, the people of a tribe were conceived as "those who have grown up together" — the collective that shared the same soil and the same blood. This social sense is preserved almost intact in the Germanic and Slavic branches: Old English lēode, modern German Leute, and Russian ljudi all mean simply "people." It was the Latin world that took this collective idea and drew a line through it. Where the tribe was the body of the free, membership in it became the very definition of freedom: to belong to the people was to be liber, "free," as opposed to the outsider, the slave, the one who stood outside the community of rights.

Roman society gave this idea its sharpest institutional form. The free citizen was liber; the man who had been a slave and had been set free was a lībertus, a freedman; and from lībertus came lībertīnus, the adjective and noun naming the freedman class and everything pertaining to it. In the Roman legal and social order, libertini formed a distinct and not entirely respectable stratum — free, but never quite as free as the freeborn, marked forever by their origin in servitude. The word itself was therefore socially charged: to call a man a libertinus was to name both his liberty and its limits, his rise and the stain of his past. The biblical text of the Acts of the Apostles even speaks of a synagogue of "Libertines" (Acts 6:9) — the freedmen of Jerusalem, a community known by exactly this name.

When the word crossed into French in the sixteenth century as libertin, it brought this Roman social sense with it but immediately acquired a new intellectual one. This was the age of the Reformation and the wars of religion, an age in which the most dangerous freedom was not legal but mental. A libertin in the French sense was a freethinker — one who claimed liberty of conscience against the authority of the church, who doubted dogma, who asked the questions orthodoxy preferred not to hear. In the hands of the French libertins érudits, figures like Gabriel Naudé and Pierre Gassendi's circle, the term named a genuine philosophical stance: a sceptical, materialist questioning of religious and moral authority conducted in the name of reason. English borrowed the word in 1563 precisely in this sense — a libertine was a freethinker, one who had shaken off the restraints of religious orthodoxy.

Then came the catastrophic slide. The theological polemicists of the age argued that freedom of thought must end in freedom of conduct: he who doubts God will soon flout morals, and he who flouts morals will sink into debauchery. Within decades the intellectual sense was swamped by the moral one. By the seventeenth century, and decisively in the eighteenth, the libertine was no longer a philosopher but a rake — an aristocratic man of wit and leisure who treated pleasure as his right and conscience as his enemy. The novels of Richardson, Fielding, Laclos, and the countless retellings of the Don Juan story made the libertine the great villain-hero of the age: seducer, duellist, wit, and moral catastrophe. The word's meaning had completed its migration from slave-status to thought to conduct — from the freedman's legal liberty, through the freethinker's intellectual liberty, to the libertine's licentious abuse of both. The semantic bridge is the ancient fear that liberty and license are indistinguishable, that the man set free from external bonds will become a slave to his appetites.

In modern English, libertine is wholly condemnatory. It names a person — almost always a man — who behaves without moral principles or responsibility, particularly in sexual matters, and the word carries a grave, almost moralistic finality that the friendlier rake or rogue lacks. It is at home in historical novels, moral criticism, and discussions of aristocratic decadence; its philosophical cousin libertinism and its French form libertinage preserve the intellectual sense in scholarship. In the literary context given — "Sir Jia Zheng is mistaken in treating the boy as a future libertine" — the word does all of this work at once: it predicts a young man who will convert freedom into license, who will abandon responsibility for appetite, and who will stand as the moral antithesis of the virtuous gentleman his guardian imagines. Two and a half millennia after a root meaning "to grow up together" named the free members of a tribe, the word still carries the entire drama — the promise of freedom, and the fear of what freedom becomes in the wrong hands.
