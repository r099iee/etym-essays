- 16th–17th century - Formed in English from “rival” (one who shares the same stream, a competitor) with the negative prefix; literally “no rival”, the language of contest and supremacy.
- 18th–19th century - A staple of elevated panegyric and biography; Mill's “all but unrivalled wisdom” softens the superlative with a near-qualifier, claiming peerlessness without arrogance — grief-stricken veneration.
- 20th century - Moves into advertising and commercial hyperbole (unrivalled quality, unrivalled service), diluting force while keeping the formal double-l spelling.
- Contemporary - Middle-to-high register, preserving a faint adversarial image — the arena where no opponent can match the champion — used of qualities, achievements, collections, and reputations.
