PEAT is a northwestern European culture-word whose ancestry is Celtic and whose English form came through Medieval Latin. The chain: 1) Proto-Celtic *kʷett- / *pett- “piece, bit, portion, thing” (reconstructed; a noun of portioning; the Brittonic branch shows Welsh peth “thing, piece”, Cornish peth “piece”, Breton pez “piece”; the root is often linked, ultimately, to the Indo-European interrogative stem *kʷet- “how much?” — the semantic idea being a counted, cut-off portion) — projected c. 1000–500 BCE. 2) British Celtic / Insular Celtic speech of the bog country — the cut sod of a bog conceived as a countable piece (“a piece of turf”) in the fuel economy of western Britain, Ireland, and Scotland; the source of the word later Latinized in British documents. 3) Medieval Latin (British Isles) peta “piece of turf; peat” (a Latinization of the local Celtic or northern vernacular term for a cut bog sod, used in charters and estate accounts) — attested c. 13th c. 4) Middle English pete / peat “peat, cut turf for fuel” (borrowed from the Anglo-Latin or the vernacular stream, recorded especially in northern and Scottish usage) — attested from c. 13th–14th c. 5) Modern English peat “the partially decayed organic accumulation of bogs, used as fuel and in horticulture” — PEAT. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The Celtic derivation is the standard and widely accepted etymology — Medieval Latin peta and Middle English pete clearly belong to the Brittonic family of peth “piece” — but the ultimate Indo-European ancestry of that Celtic base is debated: some scholars derive it from the interrogative *kʷet- “how much” (a piece as “a certain amount”), others leave the deeper root unclassified. (2) English peat must be kept apart from the unrelated Classical Latin petra “stone, rock” (Greek pétra) and from English pit, despite superficial resemblance; the word is a bog word, not a stone word.
