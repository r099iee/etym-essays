Proto-Algonquian (c. 1000 BCE)
*ka·kawa·sowa
"one who advises, urges, or counsels"
↓
Virginia Algonquian (Powhatan) (early 17th c.)
cawcawaassough
"tribal elder, captain, or counselor"
↓
American English (Boston) (c. 1760s)
caucus
"private political club or strategy meeting"
↓
Modern English (19th–21st c.)
caucus
"closed political meeting or legislative faction"

Note: Most philologists (including J. H. Trumbull and the OED) trace caucus to Virginia Algonquian cawcawaassough ('counselor, one who speaks/urges'), recorded by John Smith in 1624. An older competing folk etymology derived it from Boston's 18th-century 'Caulkers' Club' (shipyard workers), or Medieval Latin caucus ('drinking vessel'), but indigenous Algonquian transmission via colonial contact remains the leading linguistic consensus.
