The biography of incrassate is a portrait of a word that never became popular and therefore never had to change its clothes. It arrived in English already fully dressed in Latin, and it has stayed in the Latin costume ever since, moving from the learned medicine of the 17th century to the botanical manuals and dictionary pages where it survives today. Where most English words drift through centuries of folk use, incrassate is a deliberate act of borrowing, and its whole character — precise, heavy, slightly intimidating — was fixed at the moment of its creation in Rome. That creation happened in two steps, both inside Latin. The first was the adjective crassus, 'thick, dense, solid, fat,' a word of everyday Latin used of hide, soil, broth, and bodies, and attested as early as Plautus in the 2nd century BCE. Its origin is a mystery: unlike most Latin core adjectives it has no solidly agreed Indo-European parentage, and the old guess connecting it to a root of 'growth' is now generally abandoned — a reminder that not every Latin word can be walked back to a PIE formula, and that the honest answer to 'where did crassus come from?' is 'we do not know.' What matters for English is what Latin did with it. By the 1st century CE, technical writers — above all Pliny the Elder in his Natural History, but also the medical authors — had formed the intensive verb incrassāre, 'to make thick,' by prefixing in- (here an intensifier meaning 'thoroughly,' not a negation) to crassus. The participle incrassātus supplied the form that English would later adopt. Latin crassus was prodigiously fertile, and two of its other offspring deserve mention because they show how a single adjective can radiate into very different corners of English. In Vulgar Latin the 'fatness' of crassus produced *crassia, 'fat, grease,' which travelled through Old French craisse/graisse into Middle English and gave us modern 'grease' — so the lowliest kitchen word and the haughtiest medical word are cousins. And crassus gave English 'crass' itself: the 'thick' of matter became the 'thick' of mind, so that a crass remark is a dense, coarse, insensitive one. Thickness, in the Latinate imagination, was always a double-edged quality — solid and substantial, but also heavy, dull, and unrefined. English took incrassate in the 17th century, in the great age of scientific Latin borrowings, and its home was the medical theory of the humours. In humoral physiology the body's balance depended on the fluidity of blood, phlegm, and bile; when that balance failed, the fluids 'incrassated' — thickened into viscous, sluggish matter that clogged the organs and had to be purged or thinned. It is precisely this event the card's sentence describes: 'When the balance is lost, the humours begin to ______ and lose their natural fluidity.' The word's whole emotional colouring — thickening as a loss of healthy motion, a fall from flow into stagnation — comes from this medical history. Related vocabulary grew up around it: 'incrassants' were medicines that thickened, 'incrassative' the quality of thickening, 'incrassation' the process itself. As humoral medicine died in the 18th and 19th centuries, incrassate lost its most natural register; but it did not die. In botany and natural history it settled into a quieter life as an adjective, applied to leaves, stems, and body parts that are thickened, swollen, or fleshy — the succulent leaf of a desert plant that has 'incrassate' tissue for storing water. In this specialised sense it is still standard technical vocabulary. For the general writer, meanwhile, the word became a trophy: to use 'incrassate' today is to announce a deliberately archaic or mock-scientific register, and writers of comic or pompous prose deploy it for the heavy, greasy effect its history supplies. Its arc is thus the opposite of most words': it was born technical, has never been popular, and has aged in place, a perfect fossil of the learned Latin that once clothed English science.
