Derivation chain for the noise word RACKET: Stage 1 — No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon. The word rests on an expressive, onomatopoeic base of the form *rak- / *rek- 'clatter, noisy disturbance', typical of imitative vocabulary for clattering and rattling; the clearest formal parallels are Middle Dutch racken 'to make a noise' and related Low German forms, though none of these can be shown to be the direct ancestor. Stage 2 — Possible reinforcement from Gaelic racaid 'riot, noise, disorderly play', which may have reached English through Anglo-Irish contact in the 16th century (debated). Stage 3 — English racket (noun) 'noisy play, riotous merrymaking, frolic' (first recorded 1560s). Stage 4 — English racket 'a loud, confused, and disturbing noise or din' (17th century). Stage 5 — English racket 'a boisterous disturbance, uproar, or social commotion' (17th–18th centuries). Stage 6 — American English racket 'a dishonest scheme or fraudulent business' (1920s), with the agent noun racketeer (1920s, formed with -eer by analogy with buccaneer, pioneer). Final node for this chain: RACKET. Note: the links from the imitative base to Middle Dutch racken are analogical parallels rather than proven lines of descent, and the Gaelic influence is a plausible reinforcement, not a demonstrated source. Derivation chain for the sporting implement RACKET (homograph and homophone, no etymological connection to the noise word): Stage 1 — Arabic rāḥat (also spelled rāḥah) 'palm of the hand'. Stage 2 — Old French rachete / rechette 'a palm-net or paddle used for the game of tennis' (14th century), borrowed from the Arabic word in the crusading period or via the Italian game. Stage 3 — Middle English raket 'a sports bat, a racket for tennis' (15th century). Stage 4 — Modern English racket, also spelled racquet (under French influence), 'the bat used in tennis, squash, badminton, and similar games'. Final node: RACKET.
