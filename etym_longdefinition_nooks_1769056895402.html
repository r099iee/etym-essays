nooks

1. Primary sense. A nook is a small corner, recess, or secluded spot — a place where two surfaces meet in an angle and, above all, a place that is tucked away, sheltered, and private. The plural nooks (as here) gathers such places together: all the little hidden angles of a room, a garden, or a whole landscape. The core meaning has two inseparable ingredients: smallness and concealment. A corner can be big and public; a nook cannot. It is, by definition, the kind of place that wraps around whatever hides in it.

2. The domestic nook. In ordinary modern use the word is strongly domestic and cosy. A reading nook is a snug corner furnished for reading; a breakfast nook is a small alcove or corner with table and benches, a feature of mid-twentieth-century American and British houses; a window nook is the sheltered angle of a bay window. The "snug nook" of a fireside, window-seat, or library is the archetype: small, warm, enclosing, and safe. The same cosy colouring gives the word its affectionate, fairy-tale warmth — a nook is a place you curl up in.

3. The nook of the landscape. The word also, and older, belongs out of doors. A nook of a field is a corner of ground; a nook of a coast or a wood is a sheltered angle of the landscape, a hidden pocket where one can take refuge. In this literary context — "search through all the nooks and wild places of the land" — the word carries this older, wilder, more enchanted sense: the secret, sheltered pockets of a land under occupation, hollows under roots, clefts in rock, shadowed angles of wood and valley where a remnant people could survive hidden. Here the nook is not cosy but precious: the landscape's own place of refuge, the last address of a hunted people.

4. The idiom "nooks and crannies." The word's most famous pairing is the alliterative doublet nooks and crannies, fixed in the language by the early modern period. A cranny is a narrow crack or fissure (from Old French cran "a notch, a crack"), and the pair together means "every conceivable hiding place, however small." "Search every nook and cranny" is the standard idiom for exhaustive, loving thoroughness, and it is precisely this idiom that the passage taps, split open into "nooks and wild places."

5. Secondary and extended senses. The word has quietly extended itself in a few directions. In furniture and building, a nook can be any small recessed space (a "niche"), and the distinction among nook, niche, alcove, and cubbyhole is a favourite of careful writers. As a brand, the Barnes & Noble Nook (2009) borrowed the word's snug, bookish connotations for an e-reader. Figuratively, "a nook of the mind" or "a nook of contentment" is a small private corner of one's inner life — the metaphor transferring the sheltered place to a sheltered state. In Scots and northern English dialect the word survived with its oldest sense, "a corner or projecting bit of ground," and the plural nooks in literary prose keeps that old rural geography alive.

  • Corner: the generic term for any angle; nook adds seclusion, shelter, and smallness.
  • Recess: architectural, a space set back from a wall; nook is smaller, cosier, and implies hiding.
  • Cranny: a narrow crack or fissure; almost always paired with nook in the idiom "nooks and crannies."
  • Niche / alcove / cubbyhole: recesses of different sizes and functions; nook is the cosiest and most protective of the set.

6. In this context. Doctor Cornelius tells Caspian to "search through all the nooks and wild places of the land to see if any Fauns or Talking Beasts or Dwarfs are perhaps still alive in hiding." Here the plural nooks does not mean ordinary domestic corners but the secret, sheltered pockets of a wild landscape — the geography of survival, the possibility that the Old Narnians are "perhaps still alive," tucked into the land itself. Paired with "wild places," the word moves from cosy domesticity toward a more ancient, enchanted sense of hidden refuge, and the hushed, hopeful tone is exactly right for Narnia. The mnemonic for this card links the word to the Hindi नुक्कड़ (nukkaṛ), "a tucked-away street corner" — a perfect cross-linguistic image of the same sheltered angle.

  • The word is nearly always small: a nook cannot be grand or public.
  • It implies concealment and protection: a place that wraps around its occupant.
  • Its register spans cosy domesticity (reading nook) and enchanted wilderness (nooks and wild places).
  • Its most famous use is the idiom "every nook and cranny" — total, loving thoroughness.
