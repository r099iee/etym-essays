A rag is a piece of old, torn, worn cloth — a tattered fragment cut or ripped from a garment or fabric — and in the plural the word commonly denotes ragged clothing itself: to be "in rags" is to be dressed in tatters, the badge of extreme poverty. In its concrete, material senses the word covers any torn, shred-like remnant: scraps of cloth used for cleaning, wipes, or packing; tattered fragments of paper; and, by a striking transferred extension, strips of cloud torn by the wind. The plural form rags carries a strongly tactile charge: rags are rough-edged, frayed, threadbare, wispy, and the word brings with it the feel of torn cloth, of fibres pulling apart along a jagged line.

Polysemy: the full semantic range.

  • Piece of torn cloth (singular): a small, worn, frayed fragment of fabric, typically of no further use as clothing but still serviceable as a cleaning cloth, wipe, or binding — "a dust rag", "a polishing rag".
  • Ragged clothing (plural): garments worn to tatters, the outward sign of extreme poverty — "dressed in rags", "in rags and tatters"; the plural has been a synecdoche for destitution since the sixteenth century.
  • Transferred / meteorological sense: any torn, shred-like remnant of material — scraps of paper, strips of cloud, tatters of mist; in the passage from The Hobbit, the "flying rags" are wind-torn strips of cloud across the moonlit sky.
  • Idiomatic and figurative uses: "from rags to riches" (the ascent out of poverty); "ragged" as an adjective for anything jagged, uneven, or straggling; "red rag" (a provocation, a flag of revolt, as in the proverb "a red rag to a bull"); "to rag on someone" (to tease, berate, or complain); "to rag" (to torment or play tricks on, especially at university); "the rag" (British slang for a newspaper, originally one of sensational character); "ragtime" (a musical style, from its "ragged" or syncopated rhythm); "rag-and-bone man" (a collector of waste cloth and scrap).
  • Slang and colloquial extensions: "to get one's rag out" (to lose one's temper); "chew the rag" (to grumble or to chat); "on the rag" (vulgar slang for menstruation, from the old sanitary cloth) — all drawn from the literal cloth.

The sense in the context. In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the word performs a striking metaphorical transfer from textiles to meteorology. The wind has just broken up the grey clouds of the long storm, and "a wandering moon appeared above the hills between the flying rags". The clouds are no longer a solid ceiling but shredded fragments, torn apart by the gusting wind and driven across the sky like tattered cloth; the moon, itself personified as a wanderer, appears and disappears between them. The image is entirely visual and entirely concrete — Tolkien gives the sky the texture of worn linen — and it captures the restless, half-revealed quality of the night: the moon showing itself in gaps, veiled and unveiled by strips of cloud that look exactly like rags streaming in the wind.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. Neighbouring words refine the picture differently. Tatters emphasises hanging and trailing strips, and is virtually synonymous with ragged clothing; shreds suggests smaller, narrower torn pieces; scraps any small detached fragments, of cloth or of anything else; fragments is the colourless, technical word for broken pieces of anything; clout is the old English word for a patch or piece of cloth. Rags sits among them as the word that keeps both the poverty and the texture.
