Pitter is a noun meaning a light, rapid, fluttering tapping sound — the individual quick beat of a racing heart, a tiny footstep, or a falling drop — and it lives almost always in the company of its rhyming twin patter. The pair pitter-patter is a reduplicative: English repeats a base with a changed vowel to suggest repetition, alternation, or smallness, exactly as in tick-tock, ding-dong, and riff-raff. Within the pair, pitter is the smaller, faster, lighter member: its short, closed vowel i makes a thinner, higher, more delicate sound than the open a of patter. Rain that patters is made of full, rounded drops; a heart that pitters is going like a tiny bird’s — quick, tremulous, almost a whisper of a beat.

In the literary context preserved on this card — “his heart all of a patter and a pitter” — pitter completes J.R.R. Tolkien’s onomatopoeic pair in The Hobbit (1937), and its nuance is inseparable from that pairing. The two words together, patter and a pitter, mimic the heart’s double beat: pit-ter, pat-ter, two syllables for two beats, the rhyme and rhythm of the words copying the rhythm of the pulse. Used of rain, of small feet, or of a heart, pitter suggests something tiny, rapid, light, and slightly tremulous — the flutter of a small creature’s chest — and in Tolkien’s sentence it turns Bilbo’s fear into something almost tender and comic: the hobbit’s heart is going like a little bird’s. The tone of the passage is a delicate blend of tension and affection, and pitter, the softer half of the pair, is the word that makes the fear sound childlike rather than dreadful. A pounding heart would be terrifying; a pittering heart is endearing.

The word is narrowly polysemous, and its senses are best set out explicitly:

  • Noun, the sound itself: a single light, fluttering tap — “she felt a faint pitter in her chest” — and, most commonly, the lighter element of pitter-patter.
  • Verb: to make such a sound, or to move with light quick steps — “her feet pittered along the passage.”
  • Adverbial formula “pit-a-pat” (pitapat): the older shape of the same imitative word, meaning with a quick trembling beat — “my heart went pit-a-pat.”
  • In the idiom “the pitter-patter of tiny feet”: a fixed modern phrase, traditionally used of the approaching footsteps of children — often, in jokes and cards, of an expected baby — i.e. a small, rapid, endearing sound as a sign of new life.
  • Extended adjectival / attributive use: “a pittering rain,” “pitter sounds” — describing anything tiny, rapid, and rhythmic.

Each sense is a bridge from the core onomatopoeia. The base pit imitates a light, quick tap (contrast the heavier pat), and the suffix -er marks repetition, so a pitter is inherently a repeated small tap; the verb follows directly from the noun; the adverbial pit-a-pat is simply the same imitative base extended into a rhythm; and the idiom with “tiny feet” applies the sound to the lightest, quickest steps of all — those of small children — making the word a shorthand for gentleness and new life. In every use, pitter denotes the small and the quick: it is patter’s “baby brother,” the lighter, faster half of the pair, and it never entirely escapes that nursery rhythm.
