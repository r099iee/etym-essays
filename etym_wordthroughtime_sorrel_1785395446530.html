- [Old French: surele, 'sour little one'] - The plant's name is a diminutive of 'sur' (sour), coined for its acidic leaves; sorrel is identified from the outset by taste.
- [15th century Middle English: sorel] - The word enters English naming Rumex acetosa, a wild dock-family green eaten as pot-herb, salad leaf, and children's foraged nibble; a parallel French source yields the horse-colour 'sorrel' and the surname.
- [16th-19th century: the sour pot-herb] - Sorrel keeps its place in English country cooking — sorrel soup, sorrel sauces — its oxalic-acid tartness lending bite; in folklore and medicine it is the cooling, cleansing green.
- [20th century: decline and rediscovery] - As cultivated vegetables displace wild greens, sorrel shrinks to a forager's and herbalist's plant; its name also travels to the Caribbean, where a hibiscus brew (Hibiscus sabdariffa) is called sorrel.
- [Contemporary sense] - A gourmet and foraged salad green, rustic, astringent, faintly bitter — in Tolkien the thin, sour nibble that marks how near starvation the party has come.
