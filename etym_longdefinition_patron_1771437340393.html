Patron denotes a person who gives financial or other support to another person, institution, or cause, and in this literary context it names the powerful benefactor whose backing has made Yu-cun's career possible. The scene gives the word its full social charge: Yu-cun makes an "elaborate bow," thanks the man "profusely," and only then dares to ask his question. The patron is thus presented as the hinge of his fortune — the one whose good will converts ambition into opportunity. The tone is one of intense, almost theatrical deference, and patron carries the whole weight of that hierarchy. Etymologically the word descends from the Latin pater, "father," and in this context that paternal undertone is fully alive: the patron is not merely a supporter but a father-figure, an authority whose protection is personal, quasi-familial, and conditional on gratitude. The word quietly marks the difference between a benefactor and a friend: the patron is owed homage, and the relationship is formalised in the bow and the profuse thanks that frame the request.

The word is polysemous, and every sense grows out of the Latin idea of the patronus, the man who stood to his dependents as a father stands to his children:

  • Sense 1 — a benefactor of the arts, a person, or a cause (primary modern sense). One who gives sustained financial or other support: a patron of the arts, patron of literature, a royal patron, a wealthy patron, a patron of a cause. This sense reached its glory in the Renaissance age of artistic patronage, when princes, popes, and merchants — the Medicis, the guilds, the great families — sheltered painters, poets, and musicians.
  • Sense 2 — a patron saint. A saint regarded as the special protector and intercessor of a church, town, trade, or group (patron saint of lost things, patron saint of sailors); the sense fixes the word's religious, protective face (16th century).
  • Sense 3 — a regular customer or client (chiefly American, 19th century). A patron of a shop, restaurant, theatre, or library is a customer whose custom supports the establishment — polite and slightly formal, the business extension of the benefactor sense.
  • Sense 4 — the historical Roman patronus. In Roman law, a former master who, after freeing a slave, remained that man's legal protector and guardian; more broadly, any influential protector of clients, of a city, or of a cause. The institution was patrocinium, "patronage," and it bound patron and client in a web of duties and rights — support in exchange for loyalty and deference.
  • Sense 5 — patroness, patronage, and the split verb patronize. Patroness is the female patron; patronage is both the support given and the power to appoint (political patronage). The verb patronise has two live senses: "to support as a patron" and, with an 18th-century shift, the condescending "to treat someone as an inferior" — a reminder of how easily protection slides into superiority.
  • Sense 6 — the historical bridge and the pattern split. Latin pater "father" → patronus "one who stands in a father's place" → Old French patron, which meant both "protector" and "model, sample" (the patron who commissions a work is also its model). English carried both senses in one word until c. 1600, when the spellings diverged: patron kept the protector, while pattern became the model — so a sewing "pattern" and a "patron of the arts" are etymologically the very same word.

The boundaries around patron are best drawn against its neighbours. Sponsor (Latin spondēre, "to promise solemnly") emphasises a formal, often contractual undertaking for a specific project or person. Benefactor (Latin bene + facere, "to do good") stresses the giving of money or help but is emotionally cooler, describing an act rather than a relationship. Backer is informal and financial. Supporter is the broadest and least hierarchical of the group. Patron alone implies a sustained, often public relationship between a protector and a client, and it alone carries the Roman legal echo of the patronus — the only word in the set that makes the relationship feel like a kind of kinship. In collocation it belongs to a formal, respectful register: patron of the arts, patron saint, royal patron, under the patronage of, patron and client, patroness.
