- [Late 16th c.] - Enters English as the noun of 'to compose', meaning the act of arranging or putting parts together: an orderly arrangement, a settled composition. Register neutral, physical and artistic.
- [17th-18th c.] - The sense shifts inward to mental ordering: a composed temperament, self-possession. Composure comes to mean the calm achieved by gathering one's scattered feelings and holding them in order, prized by the culture of sensibility as a virtue set against agitation and passion.
- [Modern usage] - The standard word for calm self-control under strain. Connotation is admiring and suggests disciplined mastery rather than mere placidity — keeping oneself 'put together'. It reads as an inner victory over panic, not the absence of feeling.
