In Macaulay's History of England, the sentence "how her __________ and her martial glory grew together" names, with deliberate majesty, the swift enrichment of England after the Glorious Revolution of 1688. Opulence here is not personal luxury but national abundance: the swelling wealth, resources and commercial riches of a rising power. Macaulay sets it in a grand anaphoric chain ("how... how... how...") in which the "auspicious union of order and freedom" produces prosperity, credit, commerce and maritime might; opulence and "martial glory" are twin fruits of the same constitution. The word's tone is triumphalist and majestic — the vocabulary of the Whig chronicler celebrating material progress as a moral triumph. Crucially, opulence is paired with glory, not with idleness or display; it implies wealth that is productive, expansive and, in the older Latin sense, "powerful" — the wealth of an empire on the rise.

The full polysemy of opulence. The word has grown a small but telling fan of senses.

  • 1. Wealth, affluence, abundant resources (the card's general sense). The neutral-to-elevated word for riches in bulk: the opulence of a nation, a city, a merchant class, a dynasty.
  • 2. National or collective abundance and power (the Macaulay sense). In 17th- and 18th-century prose, opulence is the wealth of states and empires — productive, expansive, paired with power. 'The opulence of England' means her resources and might, not her chandeliers.
  • 3. Lavish, sumptuous, ostentatious display (the modern flavour, 19th century onwards). Opulence of courts, palaces, interiors, fashion: wealth as a visible, aesthetic quality — gilded ceilings, marble, jewels, a court in full array. Here opulence slides toward excess and spectacle.
  • 4. Figurative — richness of style, language, colour, sound. 'The opulence of the prose', 'an opulence of colour': abundant, luxurious fullness applied to non-material things. Semantic bridge: the same word that describes wealth piled up describes anything piled up to luxurious excess.
  • 5. As a quality — the state of being plentiful or abundant in general. Rare, mostly transferred from money to goods, harvests, ornament: 'an opulence of detail' in a painting or a description.

Historical bridge. Latin opulentia is built on ops, 'power, might, plenty, wealth, resources' — the same root as opus 'work'. The original Indo-European idea was that wealth is the product of work and that plenty and power are one thing. As the word travelled into English (16th century, via French), it first kept that heavy sense of power and resources; the 19th century, in the age of great fortunes and gilded interiors, tilted it toward display. Macaulay, writing in that century, still uses it with full Augustan gravity: opulence is national abundance growing together with martial glory — the two rise together because they are the same force seen from two sides.

Boundaries. Wealth is the general, neutral term for all economic resources. Riches is its plural, concrete twin, suggesting visible possessions. Affluence stresses the continuous inflow of money, comfort without display. Prosperity is the thriving condition of a person or nation. Luxury names the indulgent comforts that wealth buys; opulence is the wealth itself in its fullness. Lavishness and sumptuousness focus on the spending and the display; grandeur and magnificence add majesty to the picture; profusion is abundance of quantity without the money-connotation. The special distinction of opulence is its combination of sheer quantity with visible, material quality — riches that are on show.

Collocations. Opulence and luxury, the opulence of the court / palace / empire, ostentatious opulence, gilded opulence, an air of opulence, growing opulence; the adjective opulent ('an opulent merchant'), the adverb opulently, the near-synonym pair wealth and opulence. In the sentence of the card, 'opulence and martial glory grew together' uses the word at the height of its national, productive sense — the word of a Whig historian for whom trade, credit and empire were the proof of English liberty.
