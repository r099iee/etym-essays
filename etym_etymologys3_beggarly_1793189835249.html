The pedigree of beggarly is short, sturdy, and honest — except for its very first step, which disappears into one of English etymology's favourite fogs. The adjective itself is a transparent late-Middle-English formation, beggar + -ly, first recorded in the 15th century and never mysterious. But the verb it stands on, Middle English beggen (‘to ask alms’, c. 1200), has no agreed ancestor, and scholars have argued over its cradle for a century. The word thus opens in mystery and proceeds in daylight — a foggy dawn followed by a well-mapped road.

The beggen problem: three rival accounts compete for the origin of beggen, and each has respectable champions. The nativist account derives it from an unrecorded Old English *bedecian, related to bedu (‘prayer, request’) and Gothic bida (‘prayer’) — begging as formalised asking, the prayer of the poor. The continental account derives it from the Beghards and Beguines, the lay mendicant orders of the 13th-century Low Countries, whose members' street-begging gave their name to all alms-askers (compare how boycott and sandwich spread from names). A third account blends the two: a native asking-word reinforced and reshaped by the famous order's name and by Old French begart (‘mendicant’). The dictionary consensus is cautious agnosticism with a lean toward the Beghard connection — but every honest entry prints the word perhaps, and so must we.

From asker to station to quality: once beggere (c. 1300) named the social station — the recognised figure of the alms-asker at the church-porch and market-cross — the rest of the derivation is mechanical and well attested. Tudor vagrancy law gave beggar a razor edge: the sturdy beggar (whipped), the impotent beggar (licensed), the beggar's badge, the beggar's opera of cant and disguise. By the 15th century the adjective beggarly converts that whole world into a portable quality: anything — coat, cottage, portion, excuse, army — could now be sentenced with one word to the church-porch. Shakespeare seized it early and used it often, and his beggarly fears and beggarly thanks show the moral sense already mature: poverty of spirit, not of purse.

Key stages and attestations:

  • Middle English beggen (c. 1200): ‘to ask alms’ — origin disputed (native *bedecian? Beghard orders? Old French begart?); the debate itself is part of the word's story.
  • Middle English beggere/beggar (c. 1300): ‘alms-asker’ — agent noun, possibly with reinforcement from the continental order-names; sharpened by Tudor poor-law into a legal category.
  • Late Middle English beggarly (15th c.): ‘like a beggar’ — adjective of likeness covering looks, amounts, and character from its first records.
  • Elizabethan (1590s–1610s): Shakespeare's beggarly lexicon — fears, thanks, attire — fixes the word's high-literary moral range.
  • 18th c.: the polemical age — beggarly revenues, beggarly crops, beggarly excuses arm pamphleteers from Swift to Junius.
  • 19th c.: Dickens and the reform press make it the standard sneer-pity word for lodgings, wages, and portions; the Authorised-Vocabulary of charity runs on it.
  • Modern: retired from official poverty discourse as stigmatising, but alive in literature, period film, and stable-yard fable-insults, where nothing else lands quite so fast.

Sound shifts: there is little phonology to report, which is itself the point: beggarly is a young, transparent formation whose sounds have barely moved since the 15th century. The hard g doubled in spelling (begg-) to keep the short vowel; the -ar of beggar is the agent suffix levelled to a schwa; the -ly keeps its clear lee. The word's interest lies not in its sounds but in its sociology — five centuries of English attitudes to poverty, packed into three syllables.
