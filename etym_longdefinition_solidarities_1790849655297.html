The plural noun solidarities refers to distinct, multifaceted instances, structures, networks, or manifestations of mutual support, common interest, moral unity, and collective responsibility binding individuals or groups together. While the singular solidarity often denotes an abstract ideal or unified political sentiment, the plural form solidarities emphasizes the concrete, diverse, and intersecting communal bonds—whether political, intellectual, labor-based, regional, ethnic, cultural, or ideological—that exist across complex societies.

To fully appreciate the polysemic and contextual depth of solidarities, several distinct arenas of application must be delineated:

  • Political, Labor, and Class Coalitions: In political organizing, socialist theory, and trade unionism, solidarities denotes the deliberate forging of mutual alliances across differing factions, working-class sectors, or international borders. Rather than assuming a monolithic working class, modern political theorists analyze "transnational solidarities," "cross-sectoral solidarities," and "intersectional solidarities" to describe how diverse oppressed groups unite around shared emancipatory struggles against common systems of exploitation.
  • Sociological and Structural Cohesion (Mechanical vs. Organic): In classical sociology (specifically Émile Durkheim's foundational framework in The Division of Labour in Society), the term refers to the fundamental social mechanisms that hold human societies together. Durkheim contrasted "mechanical solidarity" (cohesion based on shared traditions, likeness, and collective conscience in pre-modern societies) with "organic solidarity" (cohesion arising from specialization, functional interdependence, and mutual reliance in modern industrial societies). Sociologists use solidarities in the plural to evaluate the shifting, overlapping social contracts operating across modern institutions.
  • Intellectual, Academic, and Avant-Garde Cliques: In intellectual history, cultural theory, and literary criticism (such as Roland Barthes's critique of Philippe Sollers's experimental text Lois), solidarities refers to the established orthodoxies, mutual intellectual pacts, peer consensus, and shared ideological alliances of the intelligentsia. When an avant-garde writer attacks "intellectual solidarities," they dismantle the comfortable, unspoken agreements, shared dogmas, and collective self-satisfaction of academic or cultural elites.
  • Legal and Civil Law Origins (Joint Liability): In Roman civil law and modern continental jurisprudence, the legal root solidum / obligation solidaire refers to joint and several liability, where each debtor is individually responsible for the entire collective debt. Legal scholars refer to legal solidarities as statutory frameworks that bind co-signers, partners, or corporate entities into unified financial obligations.
  • Humanitarian, Global, and Intersectional Networks: In contemporary ethics, global development, and human rights discourse, solidarities describes practical networks of mutual aid, climate justice cooperation, and grassroots disaster response that operate horizontally between communities across the globe, transcending national borders and governmental hierarchies.

The semantic bridge connecting the ancient legal concept of undivided debt to modern cultural and political alliances is the core idea of being undivided and mutually accountable: individuals within a network of solidarities stand as an interconnected whole, where an injury or responsibility to one is an injury or responsibility to all.
