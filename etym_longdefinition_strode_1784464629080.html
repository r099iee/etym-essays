STRODE is the simple past tense of the strong verb stride: walked with long, decisive, purposeful steps — covering ground with measured energy rather than shuffling, scuttling, or sauntering. The gait implies confidence, intent, or controlled power; the legs take full length, the pace is regular, and the body moves forward as if carrying purpose in every step. In the card's context — "Then he strode away, just about the time when Bilbo was finishing his second cake" — Gandalf's exit is clean, firm, and adult against the hobbit's sedentary munching; the strong past freezes a completed departure of unhurried authority. Tone: firm, slightly theatrical, self-possessed; the wizard's stride is the exact bodily opposite of Bilbo's later scuttle, and the book's body-language is built on that contrast.

Grammatical status — a strong verb with a fossilized past: Strode is the past tense of stride, one of the small set of English strong (irregular) verbs with the pattern stride – strode – stridden. The past form descends directly from the Old English class-I strong past strād; it is not strided (a form that appears only as a rare, non-standard alternative), and the participle is stridden (as in "he has stridden"). The form must be preserved exactly: the narrative uses STRODE, not strided.

Primary sense — the literal gait: The core meaning is long, regular, decisive stepping: "to walk with long steps". It applies to people and large animals (a man striding across a field, a horse or giraffe striding), and it characteristically takes a direction or distance complement ("strode away", "strode into the hall", "strode the length of the room"). The noun stride is a single such step, and the word also serves as a rough unit of distance (a "few strides" away).

Secondary sense — the straddle/bestride family (archaic): The same verb once had the senses "to straddle", "to stand or sit astride", and "to bestride" (to sit astride a horse, or figuratively to dominate something). The celebrated instance is Shakespeare's Julius Caesar (c. 1599): "Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus". These astride senses are now archaic for the simple verb but survive in bestride and in the derived verb straddle, which is a nasalized relative of the same Germanic family. The historical bridge: to take a long step is to spread the legs, so "step widely" naturally produced both "long-step walk" and "stand with legs apart".

Figurative and idiomatic senses: The noun stride is the base of a rich idiom family that transfers the physical image of confident progress to achievement: to make strides (to make clear progress), to hit one's stride (to settle into an efficient, confident pace of work), to take something in one's stride (to deal with it without disruption), to get into one's stride, and great strides forward. In these idioms "stride" means controlled, forward momentum — the gait as a symbol of competence and progress rather than mere walking. The verb itself is used figuratively in the same way: "the company has stridden ahead of its rivals".

Specialist senses: In computing, a stride is the distance in memory between successive elements of an array that are accessed in sequence (the "stride length" of a loop); the term transfers the stepping image to the pattern of memory access. In swimming, a stride or striding stroke was an early term for a long overarm stroke. In horse-riding, stride measures the full distance covered by one beat of a horse's canter or gallop, central to jumping ("ride the horse's stride"). None of these specialist senses conflicts with the gait sense; all extend the same stepping image.

  • Sense 1 — gait: to walk with long, decisive, purposeful steps (past strode, participle stridden).
  • Sense 2 — noun: a single long step; a unit of distance ("a few strides").
  • Sense 3 — archaic: to straddle, to bestride, to sit astride (Shakespeare's "bestride the narrow world").
  • Sense 4 — idiom: to make strides, to hit one's stride, to take it in one's stride — confident progress.
  • Sense 5 — computing: the memory offset between successive elements in an array.
  • Sense 6 — equestrian: the length of a horse's canter or gallop beat.
