The story of ADUMBRATES begins, like the word's own image, in shadow. The Latin noun umbra "shadow, shade" carried for the Romans a double value: it named the literal darkness cast by a body, and it could stand, by a natural metaphor, for the faint, indistinct image of a thing — the ghost, the shade of the dead in the underworld, the mere appearance as opposed to reality. The verb umbrāre "to shade" was already in use by the time of Plautus, and from it Latin built adumbrāre, "to shade over, to outline faintly", by prefixing ad-, "towards". For a Roman painter adumbrare was the work of the first stage of a painting: to lay in the shadowed outline of a figure before the colours were filled in. For the orator — and it is Cicero who most fully naturalises the metaphor — adumbrare described the habit of throwing up a rough sketch of one's case in advance, so that the audience could glimpse the shape of the argument before it was argued in full. Pliny uses the word of drawing and of the shadow that gives a portrait its depth. The noun adumbrātiō, "a faint sketch or outline", and the participle adumbrātus, "shaded, unreal, counterfeit", completed the family.

The English borrowing was a double one. The noun adumbration entered the language around 1560, and the verb adumbrate in the 1580s, both as learned formations drawn directly from Latin by writers in divinity and rhetoric. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word moved through two registers at once. In theology it could denote the imperfect earthly image of a heavenly reality — the Old Testament adumbrates the New, the sacrifice adumbrates the atonement — and in philosophy a shadowy, imperfect representation. In practical use it meant to foreshadow or prefigure, and, in its rarer negative strand, to obscure or overshadow, a sense that survives only as an archaism.

While adumbrate made this way through learned English, the same Latin umbra was quietly fathering a remarkable family of ordinary words. Italian ombra "shade" produced ombrello, literally "a little shade", which English borrowed as umbrella in 1609. Spanish sombra "shade", probably compressed from sub-umbra "under the shadow", gave sombrero, the broad-brimmed hat that shades its wearer. French ombrage, from Latin umbrāticum, gave English umbrage in 1604, a word that shifted from "shade, foliage" to "suspicion, offence" through the metaphor of taking umbrage at something — of being overshadowed or aggrieved. These are all sister branches of adumbrate, radiating from a single Latin root for shadow.

The semantic development of adumbrate since its borrowing can be set out in stages:

  • 16th–17th c.: "to shade a drawing, to cast a shadow over" (literal, pictorial) and "to represent faintly" (theological, philosophical).
  • 17th–18th c.: "to foreshadow, to prefigure, to give an anticipatory indication"; the temporal, forward-looking sense becomes dominant in English because the shadow image is naturally read as a shadow cast ahead.
  • 18th–19th c.: "to outline or sketch roughly", the essayist's word for giving the general form of a plan or theory without detail.
  • Modern use: adumbrate is the scholarly, cautious verb for preliminary outline and foreshadowing; an introduction adumbrates the arguments to come. The shadow persists — the reader is handed a silhouette, not a photograph.

The word has never become colloquial. Its register is fixed: academic prose, the essay, the treatise, and high journalism. It survives because English has no other verb that combines outline, anticipation, and deliberate vagueness in a single learned word — foreshadow is the popular, narrative substitute; outline is plainer and loses the temporal twist; sketch is visual and lacks the Latinate gravity. In the sentence of this card the choice of adumbrates performs exactly the work the word was created to do: it tells the reader that the arguments are announced in shadow now and will stand in full light later.
