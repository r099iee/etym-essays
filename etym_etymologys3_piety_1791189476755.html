Piety descends from one of Rome's load-bearing moral words, pietas, itself from pius meaning dutiful, faithful, affectionate toward those to whom duty is owed. The PIE root is *peh₂- meaning to protect, shepherd, feed, which also yields Latin pater, pastor, pabulum, and English feed and food. The semantic core is therefore not private feeling but protective duty performed: feeding, honouring, and obeying father, kin, country, and gods.

In Republican and Augustan Rome pietas was a supreme public virtue. Virgil's Aeneas is pius Aeneas not because he is sweetly religious but because he carries his father Anchises from burning Troy, obeys the gods, and founds a lineage — duty to family, gods, and fatherland fused. Cicero defines pietas as duty to kin, country, and gods; coins show PIETAS with stork (which feeds its parents) and altar. Inscriptions praise emperors as pius for honouring predecessors and gods.

  • Christian narrowing: As Latin becomes the Church's language (3rd-6th c.), pietas narrows from civic-family duty to devotion to God. Pius becomes saintly, merciful; pietas becomes godliness, compassion, pity. The doublet pity/piety splits in French and English: pity keeps the mercy-to-sufferers sense, piety keeps the devotion-to-God sense.
  • Old French to Middle English: Old French piete (12th c., Chanson de Roland) means religious devotion and mercy. Middle English piete (c.1300, Ancrene Wisse, Chaucer) covers both pity and godliness. By 1500 the spellings diverge: pity for compassion, piety for reverent devotion.
  • Reformation contest: Protestants and Catholics both claim true piety while accusing the other of false piety — outward show masking cruelty. Montaigne (Essais, 1580, Of Cannibals) exploits exactly this: Christians who torture under cover of piety are more barbarous than Brazilian cannibals who eat the dead. Piety here means professed religious zeal used to justify persecution.

Phonetically piety /ˈpaɪəti/ preserves Latin -etas via French -ete with English -y. The -t- remains voiceless, unlike pity /ˈpɪti/ which shortened its vowel. The doublet pious (from Latin pius directly) and pietism (17th-c. German Pietismus, a Lutheran reform movement stressing inward devotion) reinforce the inward-devotion pole.

The 18th-19th centuries add filial piety under Confucian translation (Chinese xiao rendered as filial piety by Jesuits), re-importing the original Roman family-duty sense via China. Victorian usage sentimentalizes piety as gentle female devotion, then secular modernity ironizes it: pious becomes sanctimonious, pietistic becomes hypocritically unctuous.

  • Modern senses: (1) devout reverence and dutiful worship; (2) outward religious observance that may be sincere or hypocritical; (3) dutiful respect for parents, ancestors, traditions (filial piety, classical piety).
  • Literary attestations: Virgil's pius Aeneas; Dante's pietà; Shakespeare's piety and pity wordplay; Wordsworth's natural piety; modern debates on filial piety in East Asian ethics.
  • Debate note: Etymologists agree on pius/pietas but debate the deeper PIE link: de Vaan connects pius to *peh₂- protect via *peh₂-io-, while others suggest an independent Italic cultic term. The protective-duty core is undisputed; the exact laryngeal reconstruction varies.

To torture under cover of piety is thus to invert the word's origin: protective duty to kin and gods turned into a cloak for harming kin under God's name — precisely Montaigne's charge.
