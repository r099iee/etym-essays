- 17th Century - Coined in Early Modern English amidst the European vogue for Renaissance emblem books; strictly applied to allegorical woodcuts, heraldic badges, and moralizing mottos that contained cryptic philosophical or religious wisdom.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Broadens into political theory, aesthetics, and neoclassical art; describes allegorical statues, monuments, and paintings that visually embody civic virtues such as Justice, Prudence, or Liberty.
- 19th Century (Romanticism to Victorian Era) - Deepens across literary criticism and historical novels; applied to fictional characters, historical personages, or natural landscapes that personify the spirit of an age (the *Zeitgeist*) or the moral struggles of a nation.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Adopted by modernist literary critics, semioticians, and phenomenologists; used to characterize mundane everyday objects (such as Proust's madeleine or Joyce's epiphanic Dublin sights) that crystallize existential and temporal truths.
- Late 20th Century - Expands across sociological critique, cultural studies, and political journalism; commonly deployed to analyze consumer goods, media scandals, or urban architecture as symptomatic tokens of late capitalism.
- 21st Century - Pervades scholarly essays, investigative journalism, and cultural commentary; universally recognized as the prime term for any concrete artifact, policy, or incident that epitomizes a larger cultural, political, or philosophical reality.
