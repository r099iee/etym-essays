Root word: rubble (English noun). The origin of this word is OBSCURE: it has no securely reconstructable Proto-Indo-European etymon, and the chain below follows the attested record, stating uncertainty explicitly. Linear derivation chain (earliest attested to present; no fixed stage count): 1. Origin unknown / obscure. There is no securely reconstructable PIE or even Proto-Romance ancestor. The oldest attested forms appear in the fourteenth century in the Anglo-French and Old French building vocabulary, and modern dictionaries (OED) judge the word's ultimate source uncertain. (A once-popular derivation from Latin ruere "to fall, collapse" — see debate note — is rejected.) 2. → Old French robel / Anglo-Norman robel, roble "rubble, bits of broken stone, small stones used in building" (attested 14th c.). Of uncertain origin within French: possibly a diminutive in -el of some base noun, and sometimes connected with Old French robe "plunder, stolen goods" (via rober "to seize, to rob"), the semantic idea being "torn-off fragments," but this is speculative rather than demonstrated. 3. → Middle English robyl / rubel "bits of broken stone, rubble, the rough stone used to fill the cores of walls" (attested c. 1387, late 14th c., in building and surveying contexts; e.g., in the account books and technical texts of the period). 4. → Early Modern English ruble / rubble "broken stones, rough masonry" (spelling stabilizes 16th–17th c.; the -bb- spelling reflecting the spoken double consonant becomes standard). 5. → Modern English rubble "broken fragments of stone, brick, or concrete, especially the debris of a demolished or collapsed structure; rough undressed stone used in building" (present day). Debate/nuance note: The etymology is genuinely contested and must be stated as uncertain. (a) The traditional, widely repeated derivation from Latin ruere "to fall, to collapse" (via ruina, the ancestor of English "ruin") is NOT accepted by modern lexicography: the regular development of Latin rui- would not yield Middle English robyl/rubel, and there is no attested Latin or Romance intermediate carrying the sense of "broken building stone." (b) The OED and Etymonline treat the word as probably from Anglo-French robel, itself of obscure origin, with a possible connection to Old French robe "plunder" (from rober "to rob"), the "torn-off fragments" idea — a connection supported by the parallel formation of "rubbish," whose origin is similarly obscure and possibly related. (c) Some scholars have suggested a diminutive formation in -el on a Germanic or Romance base meaning "broken bits," but no secure base has been demonstrated. The honest summary: the word appears in the Anglo-French building vocabulary of the fourteenth century, of uncertain origin, probably connected with the "robbing/torn-off fragments" idea and possibly entangled with "rubbish"; it cannot be traced further back with confidence, and no PIE reconstruction is possible.
