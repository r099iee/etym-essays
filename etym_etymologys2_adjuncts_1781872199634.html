PIE *yeug- (also written *yewg-) 'to join, yoke, harness' (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE) → [semantic core: the yoking of draft animals = binding two things into a working unit] → Latin iugum 'yoke' and iungere (jungere) 'to join, unite, yoke' (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin adiungere 'to join to, fasten on, attach' = ad- 'to, toward' + iungere (attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Latin adiūnctus 'joined to, connected' (past participle; attested 1st c. BCE) → Late Latin / Medieval adiūnctum 'a thing joined on, an addition, an attribute' (attested late antique–medieval) → English adjunct (noun and adjective) 'something added, joined, subordinate' (borrowed late 16th c.; attested from 1591) → English plural ADJUNCTS. Note: The prefix ad- is a separate morpheme joined only at the Latin compounding stage (adiungere); it is not part of the PIE root. English also inherits the same PIE root natively as yoke (Old English geoc), so the language holds the root twice—once as a native word, once as a learned Latin borrowing.
