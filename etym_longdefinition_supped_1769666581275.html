supped (verb, past tense and past participle of sup): (1) took the evening meal — ate supper; (2) drank (liquid) in small amounts, sipped. The two senses correspond to two historical verbs that merged in English.

Primary Sense 1 — To Eat Supper (the Evening Meal)

The dominant sense of supped is "ate the evening meal": to take one’s supper. Historically supper was the last, often light, meal of the day, and to sup meant to consume it, usually at home in the evening. "Here they supped on food they had brought with them" (the C.S. Lewis passage on this card) means the party stopped, rested, and ate their carried provisions for their evening meal. The verb is used intransitively ("we supped early") or with "on" + food ("supped on bread and cheese").

Primary Sense 2 — To Drink in Small Amounts, to Sip

The older English sense of sup is "to drink in small quantities, to sip, to take liquid food from a spoon or cup" — the direct descendant of Old English sūpan, "to sip, drink." In this sense "she supped her tea slowly" means she drank it in small sips. The two senses have coexisted since the Middle English period and are often impossible to separate in earlier texts, since the evening meal itself was historically a broth or sop that was literally supped (sipped or dipped) from a bowl.

Secondary & Extended Senses

  • Figurative — "supped full with horrors": Shakespeare’s Macbeth declares "I have supp’d full with horrors" (Macbeth V.5) — so saturated with terrors that fear can no longer touch him. Here "sup" = "consume, take in," treating experience as a meal. This is the word’s most famous literary figurative use.
  • Proverb — "He that sups with the devil must have a long spoon": A cautionary proverb (recorded from the 17th century) warning that dealings with dangerous or evil partners require careful distance — the "long spoon" keeps you safe from a diabolical host at the supper table. "To sup with the devil" is now an idiom for dangerous collaboration.
  • Historic meal structure: In pre-industrial England, "supper" was the final, light meal of the day, eaten after the main "dinner" (historically at midday); "to sup" named the act of taking it. The word carries rustic, intimate, and slightly old-fashioned associations.
  • Archaic register today: In modern English, "supped" is largely literary, historical, or humorous; everyday speech prefers "had dinner," "ate," or "had supper." It is frequently used in fiction for period flavor (as in the Lewis and Shakespeare texts cited here).

Semantic Reasoning & Historical Bridge

The two senses of supped trace to two closely interwoven historical sources. The "sip" sense descends directly from Proto-Germanic *sūpaną, "to drink in sips," via Old English sūpan and Middle English suppen. The "evening meal" sense entered English through Old French souper, "to take the evening meal," from soupe, "broth, sop," a word the French had themselves borrowed from the Germanic vocabulary of sopping bread in broth (Frankish *soppa). The two verbs — native "to sip" and borrowed "to eat supper" — were near-homophones and merged in Middle English into a single sup whose past tense is supped. The historical bridge between drinking and dining is the meal itself: the evening meal was traditionally a liquid broth into which bread was dipped, so "to sup" (sip/drink) and "to sup" (take the evening broth-meal) were literally the same act.

Register & Usage Notes

  • Regular past form: sup → sups → supped (spelled with a double p).
  • Related words: supper (the meal), soup, sop (bread soaked in liquid).
  • Distinguish from sipped (strictly small liquid tastes) and dined (the main formal meal).
  • Do not confuse with sued (took legal action) or sopped (dipped bread in liquid) — common learner errors.
