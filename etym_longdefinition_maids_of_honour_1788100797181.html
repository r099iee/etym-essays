MAIDS-OF-HONOUR (plural noun; singular maid-of-honour) denotes, in its oldest and still most prominent British and royal-court sense, the young women — traditionally unmarried and of gentle or noble birth — who serve in the personal household of a queen, princess, or other exalted female member of a royal or aristocratic family. The office is one of the most ancient in European court life: a maid of honour attended her royal mistress in the privy chamber, helped with dressing and the toilette, carried letters and messages, stood attendance at state banquets, progresses, and court balls, and acted as companion, confidante, and social ornament. In the passage from Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince that supplies this note, the poor seamstress is embroidering passion-flowers "on a satin gown for the loveliest of the Queen's maids-of-honour to wear at the next Court-ball": the phrase there carries its full ceremonial glamour — the maids-of-honour are the privileged, beautiful young attendants of the Queen for whose finery the working classes toil.


Primary senses today.



  • Court office (British royal household): From the medieval period until the mid-twentieth century, "maid of honour" was a specific rank of female attendant upon a queen or queen consort, junior to the ladies-in-waiting and to the Mistress of the Robes but senior to ordinary chamber servants. The word maid signalled unmarried status; the position, reserved for daughters of the aristocracy, conferred prestige, proximity to power, and exceptional marriage prospects — several maids of honour later became royal mistresses, duchesses, or queens themselves.

  • Principal bridesmaid (the dominant modern sense): Since the nineteenth century, and now the everyday meaning in most of the English-speaking world, a maid of honour is the bride's chief female attendant, who stands beside her at the altar, holds the bouquet and the ring cushion, and co-ordinates the other bridesmaids. A married chief attendant is a matron of honour; the bride's junior attendants are bridesmaids; the groom's counterpart is the best man. This sense is a transparent metaphorical extension of the court office: the wedding party is a miniature court, and the bride its sovereign, so her principal lady-in-waiting becomes the "maid of honour".

  • Regional confectionery (northern England): In Yorkshire and the north, "maids of honour" are small round curd-cheese tarts flavoured with lemon, brandy, or nutmeg, traditionally associated with Richmond Palace in the reign of Henry VIII, where the Queen's maids of honour were said to have devised the recipe. The title thus leaps from the court to the kitchen by folk attribution — a commemorative naming that keeps the old phrase alive in a wholly domestic register.


Secondary, tertiary, and shifted senses.



  • In historical fiction and literary prose, the phrase may denote the female attendants of any great lady — a duchess, an empress, a countess — and is often deliberately archaic in flavour.

  • By humorous extension, any young woman attending a ceremony — a pageant queen's attendant, a debutante's companion, a festival princess — may be jokingly styled a maid of honour, borrowing the regal register of the original office.

  • Spelling variation: British English writes honour; American English writes honor. Both are current; the compound is hyphenated in attributive use ("maids-of-honour") and open in ordinary prose ("maids of honour").

  • The plural is formed on the first element (maids-of-honour), a fixed pattern for noun + postmodifying prepositional-phrase compounds, and the singular form is maid-of-honour.


Semantic bridge. The thread joining all senses is the image of a young unmarried woman holding a position of honour beside a central female figure — first the queen, then the bride, then (by affectionate legend) the cooks of a queen's palace. The polysemy here is not a branching of unrelated meanings but a migration of the same courtly image from the throne room to the wedding aisle to the pastry shop; every sense retains the core of youthful, honourable service.
