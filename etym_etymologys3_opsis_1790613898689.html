Opsis descends from one of the most stable and prolific roots in Indo-European: *h₃ekʷ- eye, to see. This root yielded Latin oculus eye, oculist, inoculate; Greek ophthalmos eye (with labial shift), optikos optic, autopsia autopsy; Sanskrit akṣi eye; Old English ēage eye; and a whole family of Greek verbs of seeing built on ablaut grades op-, opt-, ophth-. In Greek the future stem ops- (as in opsomai I shall see) combined with the abstract noun suffix -sis denoting process or state — as in poiesis making, mimesis imitation — to form opsis: the act or faculty of seeing, then that which is seen, appearance, sight, view.

Before Aristotle, opsis was ordinary: Herodotus uses it for eyesight and visible aspect; Hippocratic writers for visual symptoms; Thucydides for the look of a place. Plato contrasts opsis with nous intellect, already privileging invisible Forms over visible shows. Aristotle in the Poetics (c. 335 BCE, Chapter 6) technicalized it as the sixth morion part of tragedy, defining it as the equipment of staging and explicitly demoting it: hē de opsis psychagōgikon men, atechnotaton de kai hēkista tēs poiētikēs — spectacle is emotionally alluring but most inartistic and least proper to poetics. The passage reflects Athenian performance reality: the Theatre of Dionysus seated 15,000, used painted skenographia, wheeled ekkyklema to reveal interiors, and crane mechane to fly in gods (deus ex machina). Aristotle, defending poetry as a verbal-rational art readable without actors, needed to quarantine this technology.

Transmission preserved the demotion. Arabic commentators Averroes and Avicenna, lacking Greek theatre, puzzled over opsis; Renaissance humanists Giorgio Valla (1498 Latin aspectus) and Castelvetro rendered it as apparato or spettacolo. English criticism from Dryden to Butcher transliterated it as opsis or translated as spectacle. The Greek form survived because no single English word captures both the neutral sense of visual design and the philosophical demotion.

  • Phonology: PIE laryngeal + labiovelar *h₃ekʷ- gives Greek op- by loss of laryngeal with o-colouring and delabialization before consonants; -sis forms deverbal abstract with sigma; accent ὄψις on first syllable, retained in RP /ˈɒpsɪs/.
  • Morphology: root op- + future sibilant -s- + nominal -is; cognate formations include synopsis joint view, autopsis self-seeing, enopsis view in; English optic, optical, optician, synoptic share the root but not the suffix.
  • Semantic drift: faculty of sight → visible appearance → staged show → Aristotelian term of art → modern polemical shorthand for image-culture; each step narrows from biology to aesthetics to critique.
  • Cultural reuse: 20th-century theorists (Brecht on opsis vs lexis, Debord’s society of the spectacle, film scholars on spectacle vs narrative) revived Aristotle to attack Hollywood, television, and now digital media — exactly as in the source passage lamenting Mr Willis’s films and picture-led newspapers.

Thus opsis travelled from an everyday Greek noun for eyesight to an enduring critical weapon: to name something opsis is still to call it alluring, artful, and intellectually suspect.
