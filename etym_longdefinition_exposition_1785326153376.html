EXPOSITION is a noun whose core meaning is the systematic setting-forth, description, and explanation of an idea, theory, doctrine, or text — an act of laying a subject out in its parts so that its structure and grounds can be clearly seen and understood. In the passage of the card — “His exposition of this theme demands an initial examination of the partly distinct, partly intertwined theories” — the word names a scholarly act: the author’s methodical explanation of a theme, an analysis that unfolds the subject by first examining the theories relevant to it and showing how they are “partly distinct, partly intertwined.” The tone is deliberate, methodical, almost architectural: an exposition does not merely state a claim but arranges its definitions, grounds, and consequences for inspection, and it is judged by its clarity and completeness.

Polysemy and the full range of senses.

  • Scholarly / rhetorical sense (the card’s sense): a comprehensive and orderly explanation of an idea or theory; “expository writing” is one of the classical modes of composition, distinct from narration, description, and persuasion, and its aim is to make a subject intelligible rather than to argue a case.
  • Narrative and literary sense: in the study of fiction and drama, the exposition is the opening portion of a story that establishes setting, characters, and prior events before the conflict begins — the background information a reader needs to understand what follows (traditionally first of the five parts of plot, along with rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution).
  • Music: in sonata form, the exposition is the first major section of a movement, in which the principal themes are presented in the home key and then in a contrasting key; composers from Haydn and Mozart to Beethoven and beyond built the sonata’s drama on this initial thematic display.
  • Public exhibition sense: an exposition is a large public exhibition or fair at which goods, art, manufactures, or inventions are displayed — the Great Exhibition of 1851 and the world’s fairs or expositions universelles of Paris, Chicago, and other cities; the register here is commercial and spectacular, from trade expositions and agricultural expositions to expositions of science and industry.
  • Religious / scriptural sense: historically, the exposition of scripture was the preacher’s or commentator’s act of expounding the meaning of a text — the homiletic and exegetical sense that dominated the word’s early English life.
  • Legal and formal usage: a full “exposition of the law” or “exposition of the facts” is the complete, systematic statement of a position, rule, or set of circumstances, prized in judgments and treatises for its completeness.

The historical bridge. All the senses share the Latin image of placing something out into the open: exposition descends from exponere, “to set forth,” a compound of ex-, “out,” and ponere, “to put or place.” To expose (in the older sense) or to set forth is to lay a thing where it can be seen; hence the “setting forth” of an argument for the mind’s inspection and the “setting forth” of goods for the public gaze are the same action performed on different objects. The narrative and musical senses are the same picture applied to structure — the plot “lays out” its material at the start, and sonata form “presents” its themes before developing them. The word thus holds a single underlying metaphor across all six uses: opening a subject to view, whether before the mind’s eye or before the public.

Grammar, register, and collocations. Exposition is a countable noun (an exposition, the expositions). It collocates with adjectives of quality (a clear, lucid, systematic, masterly, lengthy exposition), with of + subject (an exposition of the theory/doctrine/argument/theme), and appears in the technical compounds expository writing, expository prose, and the noun of agent expositor (“one who sets forth or explains”). Its register is formal and intellectual — at home in academic, legal, and rhetorical prose and in the official titles of exhibitions. Related words in the same Latin family include expose (the verb), exposure, expound (from Latin exponere via Old French), and the wholly separate noun exhibition. In narrative and musical contexts the word is used unselfconsciously by critics and teachers; in commerce it names a trade fair; and in all contexts it implies that a subject, text, theme, or collection has been deliberately laid open to inspection.
