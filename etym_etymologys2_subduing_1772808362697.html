SUBDUING is a single English word (the -ing form of "subdue"), but it is built on two inherited morphemes, each with its own Indo-European root. The two chains converge in Latin and proceed to English together. CHAIN 1 (the prefix sub-): - PIE *upo "under, below, up from under" (reconstructed root; well attested in Indo-Iranian, Greek, Latin, Germanic) to Proto-Italic *supo (with s-extension) to Latin "sub" "under, below" (preposition, attested from the earliest Latin inscriptions of the 7th-6th c. BCE) to Latin prefix "sub-" "under, from below, secretly" (productive prefix, classical Latin). CHAIN 2 (the verbal base ducere): - PIE *deuk- "to lead, draw, pull" (reconstructed root; cf. English "tow" via Germanic, Latin "dux" "leader") to Latin "duco, ducere" "to lead, draw, guide" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE) to forms the stem "duce-". COMBINED CHAIN (the word as a whole): - Latin "subduco, subducere" "to draw away from below, withdraw, remove secretly" (compound of sub- "under/away" + ducere "to lead, draw"; attested from classical Latin, 1st c. BCE). - to Old French "souduire" / "soduire" "to seduce, deceive, lead astray, corrupt" (attested from the 12th c.; the "drawing away" sense of subducere hardened into the moral sense of leading someone off the right path). - to Middle English "subduen", "sodewen" "to seduce, deceive, lead astray, corrupt" (attested from the 14th c.). - to English "subdue" "to conquer, bring under control, overcome, tame" (the modern sense, established by the 15th c.; the shift was driven by association with Latin "subdere" "to put under, subject" and "subigere" "to bring under the yoke, subjugate", which supplied the military and taming meanings; the "seduce" sense died out by the 16th c.). - to English "subduing" "bringing under control or into obedience" (present participle / gerund, formed with the native suffix -ing; attested in this form from the 16th c.) to SUBDUING (modern English form). DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Two points deserve explicit notes. First, the modern sense of "subdue" is not a natural continuation of the Latin "subducere" but a semantic re-alignment in late Middle English under the influence of the phonetically similar Latin verbs "subdere" ("to put under") and "subigere" ("to subjugate"); the inherited Old French meaning was "seduce, corrupt". Second, the PIE root *upo reconstructed for "sub-" appears with a prothetic s- in Latin ("sub" vs. Greek "hypo"), a variation that is attested but not fully explained.
