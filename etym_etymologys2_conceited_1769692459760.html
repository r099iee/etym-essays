PIE *kap- "to grasp, seize, take, hold" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; source of Latin capere "to take"; also Germanic *habjaną "to have" via *kap-) → Latin capere "to take, seize, grasp, capture" (attested 1st c. BCE; source of capture, captive, capable, capacity) → Latin concipere "to take in, receive, conceive; to take together" — con- "together, with" + capere "to take" (attested 1st c. BCE; used both of conceiving offspring in the womb and of taking in ideas in the mind) → Latin conceptus "conceived, taken in" — past participle of concipere (attested 1st c. BCE; source of English concept, conception) → Old French conceit / conceyte (past participle of concevoir "to conceive," used as a noun) "a thing conceived, a notion, thought, fancy" (attested 13th c. CE) → Anglo-Norman / Middle English conceit / conceyte "a thought, opinion, concept, notion; a fancy, a witty idea" (attested 14th c. CE) → English conceited "full of a high opinion of oneself, vain" — formed in English from conceit + -ed (attested 16th c. CE; the earlier sense was "full of fanciful ideas, clever, imaginative," recorded from the 15th c.) → English conceited "having an excessively high opinion of oneself" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The chain is securely attested. Two nuances: (1) The adjective was formed in English from the noun conceit, not borrowed ready-made; its earliest recorded senses (15th–16th c.) were "having understanding, intelligent" and "ingenious, full of witty fancies" — the negative "vain, self-important" sense is first recorded in the 16th century and dominates from the 17th onward. (2) The spelling history is tangled: Middle English used conceit/conceyte (reflecting Anglo-Norman), while the related verb "conceive" was respelled under Latin influence; the noun kept the French -ei- spelling. The root itself, PIE *kap- "to grasp," also produced Germanic words for "to have/hold" (English have, heavy via "difficult to hold"), showing the same "grasp" image in both branches.
