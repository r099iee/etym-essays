mein (noun, archaic) — a variant spelling of mien: a person's look, manner, or bearing, especially as indicating their character or mood. The word describes not merely the face but the whole outward self — features, posture, carriage, and expression fused into a single readable impression. In the classic formulation, a person's mein is what is seen of their character before they speak a word. Historically mein, mien, mene, and myen all circulated for the same word from the mid-sixteenth century; today mien is the standard spelling and mein survives only as an archaic or dialectal variant, chiefly encountered in older texts.

Grammatical profile and usage
  • Function: noun, always singular and usually with a possessive or descriptive adjective (“his noble mein”, “a sorrowful mein”).
  • Register: archaic-to-literary; in modern prose the spelling mien is preferred, and the word itself has largely retreated to dignified, historical, or poetic contexts.
  • Collocations: noble mein, grave mein, haughty mein, sorrowful mein, the mein of a scholar.
  • Derived forms: none in common use; the word is a bare noun.
Polysemy — senses
  • Primary sense: a person's look or manner as indicating character or mood — the dominant sense in the Austenian use “noble mien” of Darcy.
  • Related sense: the general appearance or bearing of a person, understood as the outward index of the inner temper, often with a qualitative adjective attached (“a grave mein”, “a majestic mein”).
  • By extension (rare): the dignified outward appearance of non-human things — “the majestic mein of the mountains” — transferring the human character-reading sense to places or objects.
  • Spelling variation: mein must not be confused with the German possessive mein “my”, nor with the English verb mean; these are unrelated homographs.
Connotation
Mein (like mien) is a word of elevated, characterological observation. It assumes that outward bearing is legible — that posture and expression testify to inner worth, rank, or temper. It is therefore at home in descriptions of dignity, gravity, and nobility, and in the language of the character novel, where first impressions are read from a person's whole figure.

Semantic boundaries against near-synonyms
  • demeanor: similar because both describe outward bearing; differs because demeanor covers conduct and behaviour, while mein focuses on facial look and carriage.
  • bearing: similar because both describe how a person carries themselves; differs because bearing emphasises physical posture and dignity, while mein is broader, including the face and mood.
  • air: similar because both describe a general aura; differs because air is more vague and diffuse, while mein is more specific and literary.
  • countenance: similar because both describe the face's expressive appearance; differs because countenance is the face itself and its expression of moral state, while mein is the whole figure read as an index of character.
  • presence: similar because both describe the impression a person makes; differs because presence suggests commanding charisma, while mein is the quieter visible bearing.
  • appearance: similar because both describe how a person looks; differs because appearance is neutral and factual, while mein implies character-revealing bearing.
  • aspect: similar because both can denote look or appearance; differs because aspect is more formal and may apply to things, while mein applies to a person.
Historical bridge
The word entered English around 1515 from Middle French mine “appearance, countenance”, whose own origin is debated (see the derivation chain): it may stem from a Celtic base meaning “muzzle, snout, brow”, or from a clipping of demean, or be connected with Latin minārī “to threaten”. The free spelling of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries produced mene, miene, mein, and mien; the last became standard, while mein remained a recognised if archaic variant. The word flourished while physiognomy — reading character from faces — was in fashion, and retreated as that science and its vocabulary fell out of use.

Literary attestations
Austen's “fine, tall person, handsome features, noble mien” (Pride and Prejudice, 1813) is the word's most famous use; the form mein appears in earlier and dialectal writing of the 16th–19th centuries and in editions that preserve older orthography.
