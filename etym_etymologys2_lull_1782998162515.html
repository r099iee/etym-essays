LULL is of imitative (echoic) origin, so it has no reconstructable PIE root; the chain runs through attested English alone. 1. (Prehistoric, sound-symbolic) The base *lul(l)- — a reduplicated crooning syllable imitating the soothing sound of a parent's voice; never written, it is inferred from the medieval forms that follow. This is a reconstructed imitative base, NOT a PIE root. 2. Middle English lullen "to calm, quieten, soothe to sleep" (verb; attested c. 1225, e.g. in the life of Saint Juliana; also spelled lollen). 3. Middle English lullay / lullai "lullaby refrain" (12th-15th c.; the carol tradition, as in the Coventry Carol "Lullay, lullay, thou little tiny child," preserves the form). 4. Modern English lull (the verb, continuous from Middle English; also the noun "a temporary quiet," a back-formation attested from the 17th century). 5. LULL (the verb, the card's target form). Debate/nuance note: because the word is echoic, its "etymology" cannot be traced to an earlier root; the OED classes it as imitative or echoic. Some scholars point to cognates such as Middle Low German lollen and Dutch lollen, and to Latin lallare "to sing a lullaby" (from the baby-word lalla), but these are independent parallel formations across languages, not proof of descent from a shared ancestor — a classic case of convergent sound-imitation rather than inherited vocabulary. No PIE stage should be invented.
