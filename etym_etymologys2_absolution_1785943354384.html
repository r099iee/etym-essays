PIE *leu- 'to loosen, to set free, to divide, to release' (also *leue- / *leuH-; the source of Greek luein 'to loosen, to release' and of Latin luere 'to loosen, to free, to pay') -> Latin solvere 'to loosen, to unbind, to set free, to dissolve, to pay' (traditionally analyzed as se- 'apart' + luere 'to loosen'; attested from the 3rd c. BCE) -> Latin absolvere 'to set free, to release, to acquit; to finish, to complete' (ab- 'away from' + solvere; in Roman legal prose the standard verb for acquittal; attested from the 1st c. BCE; past participle absolutus 'loosed from, finished, complete' is the source of English absolute) -> Latin absolutio 'acquittal, release, completion' (abstract noun in -tio; the legal term of art for a verdict of not guilty, and in later ecclesiastical Latin the term for the release of a penitent from sin) -> Old French absolution 'forgiveness, remission of sins' (from ecclesiastical Latin absolutionem, the accusative of absolutio) -> Middle English absolucion 'remission of sins, formal forgiveness' (13th c., attested c. 1200 in Ancrene Wisse; also spelled absolucionn) -> English ABSOLUTION 'formal release from guilt, sin, or punishment'.

Note on the debate: the segmentation of solvere is debated in detail. The traditional analysis is se- 'apart' + luere 'to loosen', from PIE *leu-, and that is followed here. Some scholars, however, connect luere's 'wash' sense (the root of diluvium, abluere) and see the 'loosen' and 'wash' senses as converging within the one verb; a minority view derives solvere from *se-lu-, 'to set apart and free'. The composition of absolvere as ab- 'away' + solvere, and the chain from absolvere to absolutio to the English noun, are secure on any of these accounts.
