RENOUNCE means to formally declare that one gives up or abandons a claim, a right, a title, a belief, an allegiance, or a practice - or to break off connection with someone or something by an explicit, usually public, statement. The word's essence is the announcement of abandonment: renouncing is not quietly letting go but declaring that one lets go, often before an authority or an audience. In the sentence behind this card - 'Or will they rather not renounce her?' - the question is whether they will formally repudiate or disown her, publicly abandon their connection to her, and the word's formal, declarative force is precisely what makes the question so stark.

CORE SENSES
  • To declare the abandonment of a right, claim, or title: to renounce citizenship, a claim to the throne, an inheritance, a patent, or a share of property - the legal sense, requiring a formal statement.
  • To declare the abandonment of a belief, allegiance, or practice: to renounce a religion, a party, an oath, a doctrine, violence, or a habit - the ideological sense, usually deliberate and often public.
  • To break off connection with a person: to renounce a son, a spouse, an associate - to disown or repudiate, formally cutting the tie.
  • Religious and liturgical sense: to renounce the devil, the world, or one's former sins - as in the baptismal vows and the liturgy of exorcism, where renunciation is a formal act of spiritual separation.
  • Cards and games (now archaic or historical): to renounce a suit - to fail to follow suit when one has no card of the led suit, or deliberately to refuse it; the word was standard in whist and bridge terminology.
POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY MODERN MEANINGS IN DETAIL
  • Law and citizenship - 'renounce citizenship', 'renounce the throne', 'renounce an inheritance': the formal legal act of surrendering a status or right by declaration; in many legal systems renunciation must be explicit and recorded to be valid.
  • Politics - 'renounce violence', 'renounce terrorism', 'renounce war': the public declaration by which a party or movement disavows a means of struggle; a key term in diplomacy, negotiation, and international agreements.
  • Religion - 'renounce the devil and all his works', 'renounce the world': the formal liturgical act of rejecting sin and worldly attachment; in baptism and the vows of religious orders, renunciation precedes commitment.
  • Personal and moral life - 'renounce worldly pleasures', 'renounce revenge': the deliberate, principled giving up of a gratification or a course of action, often for ethical or spiritual reasons.
  • Disowning persons - 'renounce her', 'renounce one's son': the cold, formal breaking of a family or personal bond, stronger and more public than merely turning away.
HISTORICAL AND SEMANTIC BRIDGE
The bridge runs from 'sending word back' to 'declaring against'. Latin renuntiare was built from re- 'back, against' plus nuntiare 'to announce, report, bring word' (from nuntius 'messenger'). Its literal sense was 'to bring back word, to report', and Roman usage developed two formal applications. In politics, a magistrate renuntiabat the results of an election - reported them officially back to the people - and, in a crucial extension, a consul or tribune who resigned reported his own renunciation; the passive renuntiari was the standard term for the formal relinquishment of office. In law, renuntiare acquired the sense 'to declare against, to disown', the declaration that one repudiates a claim or a connection. Medieval Latin and Old French inherited this double value - formal report and formal repudiation - and English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century with the 'declare against' sense dominant, which is the sense it has kept: to renounce is to send back word that one no longer holds.

REGISTER AND USAGE
  • Renounce is formal and performative: it does not merely describe giving up, it performs the giving up by the very act of declaring it; a renunciation is a speech act.
  • It is stronger and more public than give up or abandon; it implies a stated, deliberate, often witnessed decision, and its natural companions are nouns of status, belief, and allegiance.
  • The noun is renunciation (from Latin renuntiatio 'a reporting back, a declaration against'), and the phrase 'renunciation of ...' is the standard formal pattern.
  • Renounce collocates with objects of public commitment: citizenship, throne, title, claim, inheritance, faith, allegiance, violence, the world, a person.
