Part 1 — écriture (writing): from cutting to scripture to personal hand. The noun écriture descends from Latin scriptura via an unbroken scribal tradition, and its story is the story of Western literacy itself.

  • PIE cutting-root: Latin scribere "to scratch, incise, write" continues PIE *sker- / *skribh- "to cut, separate, scratch" (same family as English shard, shear, carve via Germanic, and Greek skariphos sketch). Writing was first scratching — stylus on wax, reed on clay, chisel on stone. The semantic bridge cut → mark → record is universal.
  • Latin elaboration (8th c. BCE-5th c. CE): scribere, scriptus generated a rich derivational web: scriptura "act of writing, text, Scripture," scriptor scribe, scriba clerk, describere describe, inscribere inscribe. Scriptura narrowed in Christian Latin to Holy Scripture, acquiring sacred aura. Phonology was stable: skriːb- with distinct p-t cluster in the noun.
  • Gallo-Romance palatalisation (5th-12th c.): In northern Gaul, Latin scriptura underwent characteristic Old French sound shifts: initial sk → esk → es- (prosthetic e before s+consonant: scribere → escrire), intervocalic p loss/voicing, -ura → -ure. Old French escripture, escriture (12th c., Chanson de Roland) meant both sacred text and handwriting. Scribes in monasteries copied escritures.
  • Middle to Modern French (14th-18th c.): Loss of pre-consonantal s (escripture → écriture, with circumflex marking the lost s), stabilisation of feminine -ture nouns (lecture, peinture, nature). By Classical French écriture covered four layers: (a) system (alphabetic vs phonetic), (b) act (l'écriture d'un livre), (c) personal hand (avoir une belle écriture), (d) sacred writ. The Academy (1694) codified spelling écriture.
  • Barbier's re-semanticisation (1815-1822): Barbier called his dot code écriture nocturne and also sonographie, shifting écriture from ink to embossed dots, from eye to finger. This tactile turn prepared Braille's coinage écriture Braille.

Part 2 — nocturne (of the night): from Night the goddess to Chopin and night-vision.

  • PIE night-root: Latin nox, noctis continues PIE *nekwt- "night" — one of the most stable roots, cognate with Greek nyx, Sanskrit nakta, English night (via Germanic *nahts), Welsh nos. The -t- extension and -urnus suffix are Italic innovations.
  • Latin derivation: nox → adjective nocturnus "belonging to night, happening by night" (Classical Latin, Cicero: sacra nocturna nocturnal rites; fur nocturnus night-thief). The form combines noct- + -urnus (as in diurnus daily). Late Latin also had nocturna as noun for night prayers (matins).
  • Old French inheritance (9th-13th c.): Direct learned borrowing nocturne (rather than popular evolution which would have given *nuitier) preserved Latin shape for liturgical and astronomical use: office nocturne, oiseau nocturne. Popular speech used nuit for night itself.
  • Early Modern expansion: Nocturne spreads to music (a dreamy night piece, Field/Chopin), painting (Whistler's Nocturnes), military (ronde nocturne, écriture nocturne), and natural history (animal nocturne). Gender agrees: un écrit nocturne / une écriture nocturne.
  • Compound phrase (1815): Barbier's écriture nocturne juxtaposes sacred-literate écriture with military-clandestine nocturne — writing that works when light is forbidden. Phonologically both words show French nasal vowels and uvular r; morphologically adjective follows noun, feminine agreement -e.

Together the phrase fuses two deep histories — scratching marks and night itself — into a Napoleonic-era assistive technology that rerouted literacy through touch and paved the way for Braille, Moon type, and digital haptics.

Deep dive — why twelve dots failed and six dots won: ergonomics and orthography. Barbier's cell measured roughly 2 cm × 5 cm — far wider than an adult fingertip pad (~1 cm). Readers had to scrub, losing place. Moreover phonetic spelling (36 sounds for French) collapsed distinctions vital for grammar (au/o, é/er), infuriated teachers, and prevented numbers, music, and mathematics. Braille kept the awl, slate, and dot-raising technique but: (a) shrank to 3×2, (b) restored orthographic spelling, (c) added contracted shorthand and music notation. Media archaeologists thus read écriture nocturne not as failure but as proof-of-concept for binary tactile encoding — the same logic as punch cards (Jacquard 1804), Morse dots, and 6-bit Braille ASCII. Socio-culturally Barbier — hearing artillery officer — exemplifies top-down assistive design corrected by lived expertise (blind teenager Braille). The phrase survives in French school curricula, the Musée Valentin-Haüy, and every history where embossed letters, night writing, and awl-like stylus appear together, as in your batch.

  • Literary attestations: Barbier, Essai sur divers procédés d'expéditive française (1815); Institution des Jeunes Aveugles reports 1821-1823; Braille, Procédé pour écrire les paroles, la musique et le plain-chant au moyen de points (1829); Pierre Henri, Vie de Louis Braille (1952); Weygand, Les causes de la cécité; recent media theory (Kittler, Gramophone, Film, Typewriter).
  • Phonetic shifts recap: Latin scriptura /skripˈtuːra/ → Old French /eskripˈtyrə/ → Modern /ekʁiˈtyːʁ/ with loss of pre-consonantal s (circumflex), palatalised u, uvular r; Latin nocturnus /nokˈtur.nus/ → French /nɔkˈtyʁn/ with nasalised o and fronted u. Both illustrate Gallo-Romance lenition and learned half-palatalisation.

Together the two halves — cutting-marks and night — explain why French says écriture where English says writing/handwriting/script, and why nocturne remains learned-poetic while nuit is everyday. Master both and the compound becomes transparent: script-for-night, felt not seen.
