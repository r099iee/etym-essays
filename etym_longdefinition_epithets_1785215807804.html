EPITHETS — characterizing words or phrases fastened upon a name An epithet is a word or short phrase added to, or standing in place of, a name in order to express a characteristic quality of the person or thing named. In the reading passage — "Carlyle, in his reminiscences, described her with ambiguous epithets" — the plural noun refers to the descriptive labels Thomas Carlyle applied to Harriet Taylor Mill in his Reminiscences, and they are called "ambiguous" because each can be read either as tribute or as veiled disparagement: a hallmark of Carlyle's famously double-edged style. The word is therefore never neutral: an epithet is a label that imposes an interpretation on its bearer, and choosing one is always a rhetorical act. SENSE 1 — The classical, literary, and grammatical sense At its root, an epithet is an "added name," an attributive word or phrase set alongside a noun to qualify it. In this oldest and still dominant sense it is simply any descriptive adjective or adjectival phrase attached to a name or thing to point out its characteristic quality:
  • "Swift-footed Achilles," "rosy-fingered Dawn," and "wine-dark sea" are the classic Homeric epithets — fixed, formulaic tags repeated to identify a hero or object and to fill the epic metre.
  • In grammar, the ancient Greek epitheton onoma ("the added name") was the grammarians' technical term for the adjective; English adjective itself is a calque of this Greek phrase, and "epithet" in old rhetorical textbooks still means simply "a descriptive adjective."
  • In modern critical usage a stock epithet is any conventional, repeated characterizing phrase, from "the Bard of Avon" to "the Iron Lady," whether Homeric, poetic, or journalistic.
SENSE 2 — The transferred, personal, or interpretive sense Because an epithet selects and foregrounds one quality, it inevitably interprets: it tells the hearer how to see the bearer. Any pointedly interpretive label fastened on a person — "the great," "the terrible," "the Unready," "Madonna," "the Maid of Orleans" — is an epithet, and the same word is used of descriptions that carry judgment or innuendo. This is the sense at work in Carlyle's reminiscences, where the epithets chosen for Harriet Taylor invite the reader to admire and doubt her in the same breath. SENSE 3 — The abusive and sociolinguistic sense (a modern secondary meaning with an explicit historical bridge) Since the nineteenth century, and with increasing force in the twentieth, the plural has darkened into a standard word for hostile, derogatory, or contemptuous labels:
  • Dictionaries record "an abusive or contemptuous word or phrase" as a settled sense; the older, purely honorific force survives only in clearly marked contexts.
  • "Racial epithets," "ethnic epithets," and "anti-gay epithets" are the modern collocations naming slurs — the historical bridge being that any charged characterizing tag, once it attaches to a stigmatized group, hardens into an instrument of insult.
  • The plural "epithets" in journalistic prose frequently means simply "terms of abuse" or "insults," as when a heckler "shouts epithets at" a speaker.
SENSE 4 — Technical and transferred extensions The word has been borrowed into several specialized registers:
  • In rhetoric, an epithet may be ornamental (the "ornatus" tradition praised the decorative epithet as dignity, while Johnson mocked it as padding).
  • In epithet transfer (also called transferred epithet or hypallage), the quality is shifted from the logical subject to a related noun — "a sleepless night," "the angry sea" — and such phrases are themselves called epithets.
  • In taxonomy and classification the term is loosely used for a name-giving word; in botany the specific epithet is the second part of a two-part scientific name (as "nobilis" in Rosa nobilis), where the word keeps its old sense of "the added, distinguishing name."
THE SEMANTIC FIELD AND ITS BOUNDARIES
  • A sobriquet is a personal nickname or byname; an epithet need not be personal and often names a quality rather than the person.
  • A title is an official designation of rank, office, or nobility; an epithet carries no office.
  • A description is a neutral, objective account; an epithet is pointedly interpretive and usually brief.
  • A metaphor is a figure of comparison; an epithet need only attach a quality, though epithets often work metaphorically.
  • An insult or slur is the hostile endpoint of the range; the plural "epithets" is a polite or formal way of saying "terms of abuse."
COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER
  • Typical phrases: "stock epithets," "Homeric epithets," "fixed epithet," "epithets of praise," "racial epithets," "to heap epithets on someone," "chanted epithets."
  • Register: literary, rhetorical, poetic, and journalistic; more formal than "name-calling" and carrying a classical, Greek-derived dignity; in the plural, euphemistically formal for "abuse."
  • In the Carlyle passage the register is that of Victorian literary reminiscence, where "epithets" is the decorous word for character-portraits that are at once generous and barbed.
WHY THE WORD MEANS ALL OF THIS The semantic journey is unbroken from the concrete to the abstract: Greek epitheton was first the grammarians' "added name" (an adjective appended to a noun), then the rhetoricians' decorative tag, then any characterizing label; because labels judge, the word gradually absorbed the hostile charge, so that today "epithet" can name a slur while its twin "adjective" stays purely grammatical. Carlyle's "ambiguous epithets" sit exactly at the hinge: they are added names that both praise and wound, and the single word epithet is the precise instrument for that double-edged characterization.
