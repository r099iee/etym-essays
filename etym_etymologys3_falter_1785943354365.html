THE VERB THAT FELL AND THEN STUMBLED

Falter is a very English-looking word — short, native-sounding, built with the homely Germanic frequentative suffix -er that also gives us wander, hover, flounder, and flicker — yet its deepest attested history points north, to the Scandinavian speech that flowed into England during the Viking Age and the Danelaw. The verb falteren is first recorded in English texts of the fifteenth century. Middle English falteren means "to stagger, to totter, to stumble in walking, to reel," and almost at once it also means "to stammer or speak brokenly." Both senses — a tripping body and a tripping tongue — are present from the beginning, and the two have run side by side through the whole later life of the word.

Behind the English verb stands the Old Norse reflexive verb faltrask, "to be encumbered, to hesitate, to falter," formed from the adjective föltr "awkward, encumbered, difficult to manage." A Scandinavian verb meaning "to be burdened, and so to stumble" crossed the North Sea with Norse settlers and their descendants in northern and eastern England, where words of Scandinavian origin took firm root in everyday speech. The -er of falteren is the Old English and Old Norse frequentative suffix, which turns a base into a repeated or continuous action: just as hover is "to hove again and again," so falter is "to stumble again and again," to keep losing one's footing. The word's phonetic shape is pure English; its earliest meaning is squarely physical.

The first recorded English uses describe bodies failing. In late medieval and Tudor texts, men "falter" as they walk in sickness, weariness, or age; horses falter on rough ground; a sword-arm falters under fatigue. The word belongs to the world of the tottering, the reeling, and the stumbling, and it is strongly associated with the knees and feet giving way. This is the image that everything later builds on: a falter is an interruption of steady, continuous motion, a moment when the body folds or trips and forward progress is broken.

  • Sound and speech: by the sixteenth century falter is thoroughly established for the voice and the tongue. A man "falters in his speech"; "his tongue falters"; the voice "falters" with weeping or fear. The Elizabethans and Jacobeans use it in sermons and plays for the broken speech of grief, guilt, and age. The dramatic power of the word lies in the fact that a body's stumble and a voice's crack are the same failure of steadiness.
  • Courage and resolve: from the same period falter extends to the will. To "falter in one's purpose" is to lose one's firmness of mind, and the negative construction — "he did not falter" — becomes a formula of praise for steadfastness, loyalty, and heroic constancy. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century moral prose uses it constantly for wavering faith, allegiance, and honour, and it is the stock word of the loyal soldier and the faithful friend.
  • The Romantic and Victorian literary life of the word: poets of the Romantic age exploit falter for both sound and feeling. Wordsworth and Tennyson use it for voices, footsteps, and fading courage; Dickens uses it liberally for characters whose nerve or health gives way. In Victorian fiction "his voice faltered" is a standard marker of a character overwhelmed by emotion, and the word became a shorthand for the whole Victorian drama of repressed feeling breaking through.
  • Modern expansion to institutions and markets: in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries falter migrated decisively outward from persons to impersonal processes. Journalism applies it to economies, stock markets, currencies, industries, political campaigns, and diplomatic negotiations that "falter" when they slow, stall, or lose momentum. Sportswriters apply it to runners, teams, and careers. The original picture of a stumbling body has become the standard metaphor for any enterprise that loses its forward drive — the economy "falters" exactly as an exhausted climber falters on a mountain face, and the metaphor works in both directions at once.

The semantic career of falter is thus a story of a concrete physical failure being progressively abstracted. The fifteenth-century word for a tripping body first lends itself to a cracking voice, then to a wavering will, and finally to the imagined "movement" of economies and institutions. What makes the word so effective at every stage is that its core image never changes: something steady has begun to tremble, break, and lose its way. The card's context — "We will not let the queen ..." — sits squarely in the long tradition in which not faltering is the badge of courage and loyalty, while faltering is the sound of a breaking world. The word remains one of the few in English that can describe, with equal seriousness, a slip of the foot, a crack in the voice, and a wobble in the global economy.
