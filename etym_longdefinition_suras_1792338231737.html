Suras (noun, plural; singular sura or surah; Arabic سُوَر, suwar, singular سُورَة, sūrah) — The primary structural divisions or chapters comprising the sacred text of the Qur'an. In Islamic scripture and theology, the Qur'an consists of exactly 114 distinct suras, which vary significantly in length, internal structure, literary style, and historical context of revelation. Each sura is composed of a series of rhythmic, rhymed verses known as āyāt (singular āyah, literally meaning "signs" or "miracles"). With the sole exception of the ninth chapter (Surat at-Tawbah), every sura begins with the sacred opening invocation known as the Basmala (Bismillāhir-Raḥmānir-Raḥīm, "In the name of God, the Lord of Mercy, the Giver of Mercy").

Structural Organization and Canonization. Unlike the biblical books, which are organized primarily along chronological, historical, or genealogical trajectories, the 114 suras of the Qur'an are arranged roughly in descending order of length, beginning with the lengthy legislative discourse of Surat al-Baqarah (286 verses) and concluding with brief, highly poetic theological invocations such as Surat al-Falaq and Surat an-Nas (5 and 6 verses respectively). The prominent exception to this descending schema is the opening chapter, Surat al-Fatiha ("The Opening"), a seven-verse prayer that occupies the foremost place in the codex due to its foundational liturgical function in daily Muslim prayers. Each sura bears one or more traditional titles derived from a striking keyword, central theme, or unique narrative element occurring within it (e.g., "The Cow", "The Cave", "The Light", "The Poets", "The Bee"), rather than an authorial or protagonist dedication.

The Chronological and Thematic Taxonomy: Meccan vs. Medinan Suras. In classical Islamic hermeneutics (tafsīr) and modern comparative textual studies, suras are bifurcated into two foundational chronological and stylistic categories based on whether they were revealed prior to or following the Hijra (the Prophet Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE):

  • Meccan Suras (approximately 86 chapters) — Typically shorter, intensely lyrical, ecstatic, and rhythmic. Their linguistic texture is characterized by short, rhymed cadence (saj'), vivid apocalyptic and eschatological imagery, dramatic oaths sworn upon cosmic phenomena, impassioned defenses of strict monotheism (tawḥīd), and sharp moral warnings against idolatry and social injustice directed toward the pagan aristocracy of Mecca.
  • Medinan Suras (approximately 28 chapters) — Substantially longer, characterized by expansive, measured prose. These chapters address the practical legal, civic, economic, and institutional requirements of an autonomous, emerging community-state in Medina. They delineate complex legal statutes governing marriage, inheritance, warfare, diplomacy, dietary restrictions, financial contracts, penal ordinances, and theological dialogues and polemics with Jewish and Christian communities ("People of the Book").

Polysemy, Secondary Meanings, and Domain-Specific Usages. Beyond the primary textual definition, the word sura and its plural suras have acquired multiple specialized and metaphorical applications across related religious, scholarly, and artistic disciplines:

  • In Islamic Jurisprudence (Uṣūl al-Fiqh) and Hermeneutics: A sura is treated as an autonomous hermeneutical matrix. Jurists examine the chronological sequencing of suras to determine principles of abrogation (naskh), where later Medinan rulings qualify, restrict, or supersede earlier provisional instructions delivered in Mecca. The integrity of the sura is also studied through the lens of munāsaba (the thematic and structural coherence uniting seemingly disparate passages within a single chapter).
  • In Liturgical Practice and Ritual Recitation (Ṣalāh and Tilāwah): In Muslim daily ritual prayers, the recitation of al-Fatiha must be followed in the first two units (rak'ahs) by an additional recitation, commonly referred to colloquially and legally as reciting a "sura". In this liturgical context, sura often functions synecdochically to denote either a complete short chapter or any contiguous portion of verses selected for devotion.
  • In Architectural, Decorative, and Epigraphic Arts: Specific suras—most notably Surah Yā-Sīn (often styled the "heart of the Qur'an") and Surat al-Ikhlāṣ—function as architectural emblems. Their complete calligraphic texts are inscribed around the friezes, minarets, domes, and miḥrābs of classical mosques, serving as protective, sanctifying monumental boundaries in stone, stucco, or mosaic.
  • In Literary Theory and Comparative Religion: Western orientalist scholarship and comparative literature use sura to designate a distinct genre of prophetic oracular composition, contrasting its non-linear, associative, and choral structure with the linear historical narratives found in the Torah or Christian Gospels.
  • Metaphorical and Literary Extensions: In Arabic, Persian, and Ottoman literary prose, sūrah has historically been employed metaphorically to indicate a distinct "degree", "lofty rank", "enclosed garden of discourse", or an unsurpassed masterpiece of literary eloquence demonstrating rhetorical inimitability (i'jāz).

Syntactic Collocations and Register. In English prose, suras appears predominantly in academic religious studies, historical non-fiction, and theological discourse. Common collocations include Meccan suras, Medinan suras, opening suras, shorter suras, recitation of the suras, and chronological order of the suras. While the anglicized plural suras (or surahs) is standard in contemporary English writing, scholarly works frequently employ the authentic Arabic broken plural suwar when emphasizing philological precision.
