STOAT is the common English name for the small carnivorous mustelid Mustela erminea, a long-bodied, short-legged predator of the weasel family (Mustelidae) found across the northern hemisphere — Europe, northern Asia, and North America, where it is called the short-tailed weasel or (in its winter form) the ermine. The stoat is often confused with the least weasel (Mustela nivalis), but it is larger, and its most reliable distinguishing marks are the black tip on its tail (the weasel's tail is brown and untipped) and its greater size. In summer the stoat's coat is reddish-brown above with a creamy-white belly and the black-tipped tail; in the cold north, as winter approaches, it molts to a pure white coat — the celebrated ermine — with only the tail-tip remaining black. The species is a fierce and effective hunter, able to kill rabbits far larger than itself, voles, birds, and eggs; it is famous for the "weasel war dance," a frenzy of leaps and twists thought to confuse prey. The stoat has been introduced to New Zealand, where it is a damaging invasive predator.

Core sense (the animal): The primary meaning of stoat in British and Commonwealth English is simply the living animal, year-round and in either coat. It is the native, everyday word; ermine, by contrast, is reserved for the white winter pelage and, especially, for the fur as an article of trade and ceremony. In North America stoat is rare; the standard terms there are short-tailed weasel and ermine.

Secondary sense 1 — the winter fur, "ermine": Historically the most famous sense of the word lies in its winter form. The pure white winter coat, taken with its black tail-tip intact, has been a luxury fur for centuries, used to trim the ceremonial robes of royalty, judges, and high officials; the ermine-trimmed coronation robe and the judges' ermine cape are its best-known appearances. The card's context speaks of exactly this: "The white winter fur of the __________, used historically for the robes of royalty and high officials."

Secondary sense 2 — heraldry: In heraldry, ermine names a specific white tincture (fur) powdered with small black shapes — conventionally shown as three dots with a tail, representing the black tail-tips sewn onto the white winter fur. An ermine-field coat of arms, or an ermine-lined mantle, is a mark of high rank.

Secondary sense 3 — symbol of purity: Medieval bestiary tradition held that the ermine would die rather than soil its white coat, making it an emblem of purity, chastity, and unstained honor; this symbolism entered European art and royal iconography, where the ermine appears in portraits as a badge of virtue.

Secondary sense 4 — colloquial metaphor (British dialect): In British dialect and informal usage, stoat can be applied to a person characterized as cunning, lithe, sneaky, or wily — the predatory quickness of the animal transferred to human character. Relatedly, regional usage treats the animal as the type of the sharp, darting, opportunistic creature.

Distinguishing and related terms:
  • Weasel: smaller, no black tail-tip; the word is also a metaphor for a deceitful person ("weasel words").
  • Ermine: the same species seen in its white winter coat, or the fur; in zoology also the alternative species name for Mustela erminea as a whole.
  • Ferret, polecat, mink, marten: other mustelids with distinct habits and looks (see similarWords).

Etymology and name history: The word appears in Middle English as stote around the late fourteenth century (c. 1400), of uncertain origin; proposed connections include Old Norse stuttr 'short' (the stoat's stumpy, short-coupled build) and the Germanic base of stout 'bold, proud' (the animal's boldness). No secure PIE root can be reconstructed, and older suggestions of Dutch or Low German links are unproven. The dual vocabulary — brown stoat and white ermine — reflects the same animal's two seasonal identities, and it is precisely this duality that gives the word its unusual cultural range, from wild predator to the emblem of royal purity.
