PIE *weǵʰ- 'to go, to move, to carry, to travel in a vehicle' (reconstructed; one of the best-attested verbal roots, the source of the way/weigh/wagon family) → Latin via 'road, way, journey' (derived from the same root in the sense 'that along which one carries or travels'; attested 3rd c. BCE; note that some authorities instead connect via with PIE *weyh₁- 'to go, chase', a debated point) → Late/Vulgar Latin *conviāre 'to go along the way with, to accompany, to escort' (prefix com- 'with, together' + via; reconstructed, not directly attested, but securely inferred from the Romance descendants) → Old French convoier, conveier 'to escort, conduct, send on the way' (12th c.) → Anglo-French / Middle English conveien, conveyen 'to transport, conduct, carry' (14th c.) → English convey 'to transport; to transmit (news, meaning); to transfer (property) legally' (15th-16th c.) → English conveyance 'act or means of conveying; a legal instrument transferring title' (verb convey + -ance; 15th c.) → Modern English conveyance 'the action or process of transporting someone or something from one place to another; a means of transport; the legal transfer of property'.

Note on the debate: The intermediate *conviāre is reconstructed rather than attested; classical Latin preferred comitārī 'to accompany', and the compound may have been reshaped within Gallo-Romance itself on the pattern of voie/veier. In addition, the ultimate derivation of Latin via (*weǵʰ- vs *weyh₁-) is debated by authorities. Both uncertainties are stated explicitly rather than resolved by guesswork.
