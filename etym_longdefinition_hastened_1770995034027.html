hastened: moved or acted quickly because of urgency or pressure; also, caused something to happen sooner than it otherwise would. The word is the past tense and past participle of the verb hasten, which is built on the noun haste ("speed, quickness, urgent hurry"). Core sense - to make haste, to move quickly. When a person hastens, they move with deliberate speed toward a goal, compressing the time normally spent in preparation or deliberation. The emphasis is not merely on velocity but on urgency: a hastened action is one that cannot wait. In the context sentence, Feng Su "hastened to urge upon his daughter the importance of complying with this request from the mandarin" - the verb conveys the nervous, socially charged acceleration of a father who feels that delay is dangerous. The urgency is driven by an external authority (the mandarin) and by the stakes of family honor, so the haste is a product of anxiety rather than mere impatience. This is the word's distinctive register: it describes speed that is loaded with a reason, a pressure, an anticipated consequence. Transitive sense - to cause something to happen sooner. Hasten's second major sense is transitive: to hasten an event or process is to bring it about sooner than its natural course would allow. One hastens one's departure, hastens the end, hastens a decision, hastens a ruin. Here the subject acts as an accelerator upon an external event rather than moving only one's own body; the person or force becomes the cause of the earlier arrival of a moment. This sense developed and consolidated in the 17th century and is heavily used in formal, historical, and literary prose ("the storm hastened their departure", "he hastened the ruin of the empire"). It is the sense that turns the word from a description of bodily motion into a description of agency over time. Full polysemy map:
  • To move quickly; to make haste (intransitive) - "She hastened to catch the last bus before the station closed."
  • To act quickly and eagerly toward a purpose (intransitive with infinitive) - "He hastened to reassure them of his loyalty."
  • To cause an event to happen sooner; to accelerate (transitive) - "The approaching storm hastened their departure from the island."
  • To hurry someone along; to press or urge a person forward (rare, transitive) - "She hastened her companion through the crowd."
  • As participial adjective: describing an event, journey, or process compressed by urgency - "a hastened departure", "a hastened conclusion".
  • Archaic/poetic: to come quickly into a state - "he hastened to his ruin."
  • Fixed idioms of the family: "in haste", "make haste", "hasten away" - all built on the same noun-verb core.
The semantic bridge. The verb hasten is a transparent native derivation: the noun haste ("quick action, urgency") + the verbal suffix -en ("to make, to do, to become"), the same pattern seen in heighten, strengthen, and frighten. Because the noun haste carried the double idea of speed and risk (fixed in the proverb "haste makes waste"), the verb inherited both a neutral register of efficiency and a cautionary overtone of rashness. In modern English hastened has drifted upward in register: in spontaneous speech people hurry, rush, or dash, while hastened survives in writing, narrative, journalism, and formal contexts, where the deliberate, anxious acceleration of events is being highlighted. It is exactly this formal, weighted quality that makes it the right word for the card's context, where urgency is a matter of social survival rather than mere speed. Register and comparison:
  • Hastened vs hurried: hurried implies flustered, disorderly speed and possible carelessness; hastened implies deliberate, purposeful acceleration.
  • Hastened vs rushed: rushed emphasizes being pressed for time and often sloppy haste; hastened stresses intentional acceleration toward a goal or event.
  • Hastened vs quickened: quickened is more neutral and often internal (a quickened pulse, a quickened interest); hastened is outward movement or the bringing about of an event sooner.
  • Hastened vs expedited: expedited is a formal, official term for speeding up a process or task; hastened is general and applies to any action or event.
  • Hastened vs accelerated: accelerated is technical and refers to an increase in rate or speed; hastened adds the human dimension of urgency and purpose.
