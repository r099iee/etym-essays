Root word: mummers (English noun, plural; singular mummer). This word has NO securely reconstructable Proto-Indo-European root; its origin is onomatopoeic/imitative, and the chain below is the attested historical record. Linear derivation chain (earliest to present; no fixed stage count; non-PIE origin stated explicitly): 1. Onomatopoeic base "mum" — an imitative closed-lip hum representing muffled speech, muttering, or imposed silence (prehistoric, arising independently across Europe; not an Indo-European root). The same imitative base underlies English "mum" (silent, as in "keep mum"), "mumble," and "murmur" (Latin murmurare is an independent parallel imitation). 2. → Middle Dutch mommen "to go about in disguise, to wear a mask, to mask oneself" (attested 14th c.) and Old French momer "to disguise oneself, to play masked, to act in a mummer's play" (attested 13th–14th c.). Both are formed on the same imitative base; the exact direction of borrowing between the Romance and Germanic forms is DEBATED (see note). 3. → Old French mommerie / momerie "a masked performance, a mummery, a disguising" (attested 13th–14th c.), the abstract noun for what a mome/momer does; Middle French mommon "a mask" also appears. 4. → Anglo-Norman and Middle English mumerie / mummery "a masked play, a disguising, a ceremonial charade" (attested 14th–15th c.), the performance-noun imported with the practice. 5. → Middle English mumere / momere "one who performs in a masked or disguised play; a mummer" (attested 15th c., c. 1425–1475 in civic records and literary texts). 6. → Early Modern English mummer "a masked or disguised performer, especially at festivals" (16th c.), retaining the original sense. 7. → Modern English mummer; the plural form used on this card, mummers "performers of the traditional masked folk play, especially at Christmas and midwinter festivals" (present day). Debate/nuance note: The ultimate source is genuinely uncertain. The OED and most dictionaries judge the word to be of imitative origin, formed on the sound "mum," and point to Middle Dutch mommen and Old French momer as the two plausible immediate sources, with the French forms themselves possibly borrowed from a Frankish or Low German base. It is not settled whether English took "mummer" directly from Old French momer or through Middle Dutch; both routes were open in the medieval North Sea trading and cultural zone. The old popular notion that the performers were always literally silent (hence "mum") is a simplification — many mummers spoke in distorted voices or delivered dialogue — but the muffling of the face and voice is precisely what the word points to. No connection to any PIE root such as *mū- (mute) can be securely reconstructed because imitative vocabulary lies outside the regular comparative method; the closest relatives are "mum," "mumble," "mute," and "murmur," all independent onomatopoeic formations.
