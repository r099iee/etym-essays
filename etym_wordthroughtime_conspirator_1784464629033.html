“Conspirator” has meant one who joins a secret plot since the word entered English in the medieval period — a member of a conspiracy, with a dark, treasonous charge.
“Conspirator” in Renaissance and early modern politics was a figure of real danger — gunpowder plotters and court cabals — and the word was spoken with dread and punishment in mind.
“Conspirator” in the 19th–20th centuries widened and lightened: anyone secretly cooperating in a plan could be called a conspirator, and the word gained a playful, half-ironic use for partners in mischief.
“Conspirator” today keeps both registers at once — the criminal plotter and the mock-grand fellow-plotter of a toast — from dark treason to affectionate hyperbole.
