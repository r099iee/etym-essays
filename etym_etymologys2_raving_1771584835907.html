Chain for RAVING (origin unknown; NO PIE root can be established). Variable stages = 5. The origin of rave is obscure, and the chain is therefore short and honest about its gaps. 1. Old French resver (12th c.) “to dream; to wander, prowl; to behave madly” — of unknown origin; no Latin etymon and no PIE ancestor can be securely proposed. 2. → Old French raver, variant of resver (13th c.) “to rave, to be mad” — the variant form that gave English its word. 3. → Middle English raven (early 14th c.) “to show signs of madness; to rage in speech” — borrowed from Old French raver; the earliest English attestations are c. 1300. 4. → English rave (verb) “to talk wildly and incoherently, as in delirium; to rage” (14th–15th c.). 5. → English raving (present participle, used as adjective, noun, and intensifier) — Final node: English RAVING (rootWord), the card's form, 14th century to present. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The ultimate origin of Old French resver/raver is unknown; the great dictionaries decline to reconstruct an ancestor. (2) The tempting connection with Latin rabere “to be mad” (source of rabies and rage) is REJECTED by the OED: the phonetic route from rabere into Old French raver cannot be made to work. (3) Old Norse rafa “to wander, stray” gives the English verb rove “to wander”, a different word of different origin that must not be merged with rave. (4) The related noun reverie (Middle English reuerye) descends from the same Old French family (resverie “delirium, wild conduct”) but is a separate English word, re-borrowed from French in the 1650s.
