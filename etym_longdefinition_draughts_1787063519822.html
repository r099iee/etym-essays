DRAUGHTS is the plural of draught (British spelling; American drafts), and it means currents of air — streams of air that flow into or through an enclosed space, especially cool air drawn in through an opening. The singular draught belongs to a large family of meanings built around the core notion of "drawing, pulling, or a thing drawn", and the plural is used most commonly of air currents and of quantities drawn or consumed.

In The Hobbit, Tolkien writes of the dwarves' torches "fluttering in the draughts": the flames waver and dance in the cool currents of air moving through the vast stone tunnels of the Lonely Mountain. The word is chosen for its old-fashioned British flavour and its exact physical meaning — a draught is air that is drawn or pulled through a space, exactly the air that makes a torch flutter underground.

Core meaning: Currents of air; plural of draught, itself "a pulling, a draw, or a thing drawn".

  • Primary sense (currents of air): A draught is a current of cool or cold air moving through an enclosed space, such as a room, chimney, or tunnel. "There's a draught by the window", "the torches fluttered in the draughts". This is the dominant modern sense and the one used in Tolkien's sentence.
  • Sense of liquid drawn (a drink): A draught is a quantity of liquid drunk at one go — a swallow, gulp, or drink. "He took a long draught of ale." By extension, draught beer (American draft beer) is beer drawn from a cask rather than bottled.
  • Sense of depth (nautical): A ship's draught is the depth of water it requires to float, that is, the depth to which it "draws" water. "The vessel had too deep a draught for the harbour."
  • Sense of pulling (work and animals): A draught animal is one used for pulling heavy loads (a draught horse). A draught is also a single pull, tug, or strain.
  • Sense of drawing a net: A draught of fish is the catch of a fishing net drawn through water — "a draught of fishes", the King James Bible phrase.
  • Sense of a preliminary version (draft): The spelling split between draft (American) and draught (British) in the 19th century left "a draft of a document" to American English and "a draught of a document" (now rare) to British English; the "preliminary sketch" sense is the same word, meaning something "drawn or sketched out".
  • Sense of the board game (draughts): Draughts (singular in form) is the British name for the board game Americans call checkers, played with draughtsmen (checker pieces). The name comes from the action of "drawing" or moving the pieces forward — the word is literally the same draught.

Connotations and register: In British English, draught is a perfectly ordinary word for a cold current of air and for the other senses listed; in American English the spelling draft is used for nearly all of them, and draught appears mainly in literary or British-flavoured contexts. The word is formally neutral but carries a faintly traditional, bookish tone outside Britain — which suits Tolkien's prose, written in an English that often keeps the older form.

Grammatical notes: Draught is a noun (also used attributively: draught beer, draught horse) and occasionally a verb (to draught a document, i.e. to draft it). The plural draughts is used for multiple currents of air, multiple swallows, or (as a singular-looking noun) the game. The British/American spelling split is purely orthographic; the pronunciation is identical: /drɑːft/ in RP, /dræft/ in American English.

Related semantic fields: current, breeze, gust, wind, pull, draw, sip, swallow, depth, checkers.

In the context of the card: "torches fluttering in the draughts" — the plural stresses the many currents of air moving through the mountain's passages, and the whole phrase is a masterstroke of atmosphere: the deep, cold, moving air of the dwarven halls makes the light of the torches restless and uncertain as the company descends into the dark.
