The biography of curtseyed begins at a farmyard fence. The PIE root *gʰer- “to enclose, surround” produced, on one side, Greek χόρτος khórtos, the enclosed feeding-yard where animals were penned and fodder kept, and on the other the Latin hortus, the walled private garden of a Roman townhouse — a space deliberately fenced off from the street. From hortus the same root branched into cohors, the “shared enclosure”: literally the courtyard of the household, and metonymically the throng of people gathered in it. The earliest senses of cohors thus mean “yard” and then “band of men,” a classic place-for-people shift. When Rome organised its army, the cohors became a tactical unit of three hundred to six hundred men — the cohort of the legions, familiar from Caesar's De Bello Gallico — and when the late-imperial and medieval court became the seat of power, the enclosure became the court. The same *gʰer- root radiated across the family: into Germanic as *gardaz, giving English yard and garden and the Slavic *gordъ that became Russian го́род gorod “city,” the walled town that was, etymologically, still just an enclosure.

As Latin decayed into the Romance vernaculars, cortem/corte drifted from “enclosed yard” to “princely residence” and then to “the circle around a prince” — the audience chamber of kings. Around this centre the chivalric civilisation of twelfth-century France built a whole moral vocabulary: cortois, “of the court, courtly,” and cortoisie, “courtly conduct,” the elaborate code of refined manners, courtesy to women, and polished speech that the troubadours and verse romances carried across Europe. Norman England received these words through Anglo-French: Middle English curteisie (13th century) gave courtesy, “the polite, considerate conduct of a well-bred person.”

The decisive narrowing came in the sixteenth century. As a courtesy was originally any courteous act, the word began to be specialised to the bodily courtesy — the bow of respect — and then to the woman's version of that bow, the dip of the knee. This is the point at which pronunciation and spelling began to part ways: the everyday spoken form “courtesy” was contracted in the gesture's name to “curtsey,” and the spelling curtsey (and later curtsy) emerged as a distinct word in the 1530s–1570s. The past-tense form curtseyed belongs to this moment, and it is the form the note preserves.

  • Sound change: the trisyllabic courtesy (three syllables) reduced to the disyllabic curtsey by syncope of the middle syllable and merger of the vowels — modern /ˈkɜːtsi/, rhyming with “heartsy.” The spellings -tsey and -tsy reflect the older and newer spellings of the same contracted pronunciation.
  • Semantic drift: “courteous conduct” (moral and social) → “a courteous gesture” (bodily) → “a woman's gesture of respect” (gendered). The general ethical word became a single physical act.
  • Gender specialisation: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the courtesy was performed by both sexes; over the following centuries it became exclusively feminine, leaving men with the bow — a social division now hard-wired into the word's definition.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the curtsey is the bodily grammar of hierarchy — the ballroom, the drawing-room, the royal presentation. As egalitarian manners spread in the twentieth century the gesture retreated to formal royal, theatrical, and balletic contexts, and the word acquired its modern air of ceremony and nostalgia.
  • Literary attestations: the word runs through the English canon from Shakespeare and the Elizabethan stage through the etiquette books of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to modern historical fiction; in the Dream of the Red Chamber, David Hawkes uses it to transplant a Qing-dynasty Chinese woman's greeting into English, because the two cultures share the grammar of the lowered body.

Today curtseyed names an act that is simultaneously social, gendered, and historical — a single verb that carries the whole trajectory from the PIE enclosure, through the Roman cohort and the medieval court, to the etiquette of the modern monarchy and the translation of an eighteenth-century Chinese novel.
