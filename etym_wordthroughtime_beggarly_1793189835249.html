- 13th–14th c. — the root arrives: beggen (to beg) and beggere (beggar) enter Middle English, naming the new urban army of alms-askers.
- 15th c. — the adjective is born: beggarly appears for persons and things marked by beggary — ragged looks, scanty livings, mean conditions.
- 16th–17th c. — Shakespeare gilds it: beggarly fears, thanks, and attire parade through the plays, fixing the word's moral-mocking range for all time.
- 18th c. — the political sneer: pamphleteers fling beggarly at rival nations' economies, beggarly harvests, beggarly funds — poverty as polemical ammunition.
- 19th c. — Dickensian staple: beggarly lodgings, beggarly wages, and beggarly excuses crowd the novels; reformers use it to shame, satirists to sting.
- Today — too blunt for reports, perfect for novels: official prose says deprived, but storytellers keep beggarly for its unmatched two-in-one of pity and scorn.
