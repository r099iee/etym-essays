- Classical Latin — copia / copiosus / copiose perceived as abundance of means, supplies, troops, and (in rhetoric) words; positive fullness
- Late Middle English — copious enters as learned/French-mediated adjective: plentiful, well supplied
- Renaissance 16th–17th c. — Peak rhetorical prestige: copious style, copious invention; English adverb copiously available for abundant action and expression
- 18th–19th c. — Natural philosophy, medicine, and letters apply copiously to rain, secretions, evidence, annotation; less exclusively rhetorical
- 20th c. — Solid general formal adverb of large quantity; schoolroom collocation “copious notes” remains iconic
- 21st c. — Still slightly elevated vs. “a lot” / “heavily”; common in writing for volume of flow, output, or documentation; Latin copia root opaque to most speakers
