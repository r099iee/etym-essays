Linear derivation chain for SCRUTINY (root word one of two): R — PIE *(s)ker- (to cut, separate, divide) — the probable source of the Latin root, via the image of cut-off scraps; the link is plausible but not universally accepted. → E — Latin scrūta (plural: rags, trash, refuse, broken odds and ends) — literally "cut-off scraps," the refuse of the household. → D — Latin scrūtārī (to rummage through, to search, to sift trash) — the scavenger's verb, later elevated metaphorically; → Late Latin scrūtinium (a search, a formal examination). → C — Old French scrutine (a scrutiny, examination) — borrowed from Late Latin. → B — Middle English scrutiny / scrutinie (14th century: a formal search or examination, esp. in canon law). → A — scrutiny (modern English: close, critical, item-by-item examination) ◄ TARGET. Note on the debate: The derivation of Latin scrūta from PIE *(s)ker- "to cut" is probable but not proven; the semantic bridge runs through "cut-off pieces, scraps, refuse." The Latin chain (scrūta → scrūtārī → scrūtinium) and the Old French-to-English chain are certain.
Linear derivation chain for CONJECTURE (root word two of two): R — PIE *yeh₁- (to throw, hurl) — the root of throwing, casting, and aiming. → E — Latin iacere (to throw, hurl, cast) — the plain verb of throwing. → D — Latin conicere (con- "together" + iacere: to throw together, to put together in the mind) — the compound of assembly; → coniectūra (a casting together, a guess, an inference; also a divination by signs). → C — Old French conjecture (a guess, an inference) — borrowed from Latin coniectūra. → B — Middle English conjecture (14th century: a guess, an inference from signs). → A — conjecture (modern English: an opinion or conclusion formed on incomplete evidence) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain *yeh₁- → iacere → conicere → coniectūra → conjecture is fully secure. No genuine Sanskrit or Germanic cognate of iacere is generally agreed upon, so those branches are omitted.
