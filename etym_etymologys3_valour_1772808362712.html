The history of valour is the history of strength acquiring a moral price tag. Its Indo-European root, reconstructed as *wal-, carries the sense of being strong, prevailing, or having the capacity to rule — strength not merely as bulk but as effective force that directs outcomes. Nothing of the root survives in writing; it is inferred from daughter branches, and the reconstruction is not without controversy: the precise form is variously given as *wal- or *h₂welh₁-, and some linguists would separate the branches. What is clear is the pattern of reflexes across the family. In Indo-Iranian, Sanskrit bála- 'strength, force' (with its Hindi descendant bal) is widely cited in the same family; in Celtic, Old Irish flaith 'lordship, rule' and Welsh gwlad 'country, realm' continue a rule-and-territory sense; in Germanic, Proto-Germanic *waldan 'to rule, wield power' yields Old English wealdan 'to rule, control,' modern English wield, German walten 'to rule, prevail,' and the name element -wald in Oswald and Harold. The root's career is never merely muscular: strength implies the right and the power to direct. Latin valēre 'to be strong, be well, be worth, prevail' is the Italic pillar and the immediate ancestor of valour. Attested from the 3rd century BCE, it runs through the whole of Roman idiom: the imperative valē! 'be well, farewell' was everyday farewell speech; derivatives fill Latin and later English: validus 'strong' (English valid, invalid), valēscere 'to grow strong' (English convalesce), praevalēre 'to be stronger' (English prevail, prevalent), and the abstract noun valōr-/valōrem 'worth, value, strength.' Already in Latin the root is triply loaded: physical force, health, and worth or value. It is the semantic hinge of the whole story that strength and worth are the same root-word; one 'prevails' by being strong, one is 'valid' by standing firm, and one's 'value' is one's strength as measured by others. Late Latin valōrem, accusative of valor 'worth, value, strength' (attested in the 4th–5th century CE), crystallizes the economic-moral sense that would later split into the separate English word value. It was Old French, above all, that redirected the noun toward courage. In Old French valor/valour (11th–12th century), under the pressure of chivalric culture — the world of the chansons de geste and the romances, where a knight's 'worth' was measured in arms — the sense of 'worth' specialised toward 'bravery in battle.' This is the decisive semantic shift: from generic strength and value to the publicly recognized virtue of the warrior. The shift was enabled by the social institution of chivalry, which made martial courage the index of a man's worth; a valiant knight was literally 'worth something' because he fought bravely. The related adjective valiant (French vaillant, from valēre) freezes the same value-courage equation into a character trait. Middle English took the word in the 13th century (attested c. 1300) with both senses present: 'worth, value' and 'courage.' As English professional armies replaced the knightly host, valour settled into disciplined martial courage — the bravery of soldiers under arms — so that by the 16th–17th centuries the modern sense was fully established, and Gibbon in the 18th century could write of the 'disciplined valour' of Roman legions guarding the frontiers. The British spelling -our (American -or) preserves the Old French form; valorous is the English Latinate adjective of the same stock. The sound changes themselves are routine (Latin valēre > Old French valor > Middle English valour > Modern English /ˈvælə/), but the semantic shift from worth to battle-courage is a social and cultural event, not a phonetic one. The register history matters as much as the sound history. In the medieval period valour was the knight's honour-virtue, tied to the codes of chivalry, heraldry, and romance. In the early modern and imperial centuries it became the soldier's and the state's word — military citations, medals, official panegyric ('Cross for Valour,' 'Victoria Cross for Valour'). In the 20th–21st centuries it receded from speech, surviving in ceremonial, military, and literary registers while courage and bravery dominate the everyday. Its semantic twin value, from the same Latin root, quietly reminds us that valour began as worth: courage that counts, courage that is valued, courage worth the showing. Gibbon's 'disciplined valour' completes the word's institutional turn. Valour is no longer only the knight's personal worth but the trained strength of professional troops guarding frontiers — strength drilled into a system. The root that gives value and valid here names the empire's credible force: courage that counts because it is organised. Sister words — wield, prevail, valid, value, convalesce — still whisper the original strength, health, and worth. Key stages at a glance: - PIE *wal- 'to be strong, to rule' (reconstructed; shape debated, sometimes *h₂welh₁-). - Germanic *waldan → Old English wealdan, wield, German walten: the rule/wield branch. - Latin valēre 'to be strong, be well, be worth, prevail' (3rd century BCE). - Late Latin valōr-em 'worth, value, strength' (4th–5th century CE). - Old French valour 'worth, courage' (11th–12th century); chivalric shift to martial courage. - Middle English valour (c. 1300) → English valour 'great courage, especially in battle' (from the 13th century; -our British, -or American). - Modern: ceremonial, military, and literary register; semantic twin value.
