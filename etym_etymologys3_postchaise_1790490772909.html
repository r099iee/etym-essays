POST — from Latin behind to relay stations: post- in postchaise has nothing to do with after in time but with posting inns. It continues Latin postus / positus via posta, a station, placed. Classical Latin post (behind, after) continues PIE *pos-ti or *apo-ti, after, behind — cognate with Greek apo (off, from), Sanskrit apa, English off, of. The verb ponere (to place, put, set — perfect posui, participle positus) gives Late Latin posta, a fixed place, relay, military post. Italian posta and French poste in the 15th–16th c. mean a relay of horses set at intervals for couriers, then the house that keeps them, then the system itself. English borrows post in the 1500s for the courier service (King's posts), then for the stages (post-horse, post-boy, post-house). To travel post is to ride from post to post, changing horses, not to carry mail — though the same horses often carried it.

  • Sound path: Latin posta /ˈposta/ passes through Old French poste with final -e and English stress shift to /pəʊst/. Spelling keeps -st cluster; meaning narrows from any placed station to horse relays, then widens again to postal service generally.
  • Institutional history: Tudor royal posts for despatches, 1635 public inland post, 1784 mail coaches, 1800s posting directories listing every inn, mileage, and charges. The 1820s — Ruskin's summer tours — are the peak: 300 posting houses on the Great North Road alone, ostlers, postilions in livery, turnpike trusts maintaining macadam.
  • Compounding habit: post-horse (1550s), post-boy (1600s), post-chaise (1650s), postilion, postage, postal. The hyphen marks the system-plus-vehicle logic: a chaise worked by posts.

CHAISE — from Greek seat to French carriage: chaise continues one of the great sitting words. PIE *sed-, to sit, be seated — cognate with Sanskrit sad-, Latin sedere, English sit, seat, saddle, settle — gives Greek hedra, seat, chair, base, with prefix kata- (down) forming kathedra, a down-seat, armchair, teacher's chair (source of English cathedral as the bishop's seat-church, and chair itself). Latin borrows cathedra for a sedan and lecture seat; Late Latin keeps it for bishops. Old French palatalises to chaiere / chaere (12th c.), modern French chaire (pulpit) beside popular chaise (ordinary chair) by regular -r- to -s- differentiation. Chaise first means a carried sedan chair, then c. 1700 a light wheeled chair-cart, then the closed travelling body. English borrows chaise /ʃeɪz/ in the late 17th c. with French /ʃ/ kept and spelling kept, pluralising as chaises /ˈʃeɪzɪz/.

  • Phonetic French-to-English: Latin cathedra /kaˈteːdra/ loses medial /d/ and /r/ metathesises in French; chaise /ʃɛz/ enters English as /ʃeɪz/ with diphthongisation. The ch- stays French /ʃ/, never English /tʃ/, marking it as a fashionable loan.
  • Vehicle evolution: sedan chair (carried by men) to two-wheeled shay (chaise clipped and folk-etymologised as shay) to four-wheeled postchaise body on C-springs. Pattern-books distinguish chaises, chariots, coaches by springs, doors, and boxes.
  • Literary fixing: Fielding, Sterne, Austen, and Dickens make postchaise the novel's engine: elopements to Gretna, pursuits to London, sick travellers laid on cushions. The word carries speed, privacy, and expense in every citation.

Recombined, postchaise is first recorded in the 1650s (as post-chaise) when French-style chaises meet English posting roads under Charles II. Its classic century runs c. 1720–1840, peaking in the Regency when turnpikes, job-masters in Long Acre, and sporting prints make the yellow-bodied chaise-and-pair the icon of genteel mobility. Railways after 1830 hollow it out: posting inns become railway hotels, postboys become porters, and by the 1860s postchaise is already nostalgic in Trollope and Ruskin — a childhood memory of panoramic windows, bracket seats, and hired horses, preserved in memoirs just as the infrastructure vanishes.
