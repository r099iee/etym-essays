nimble: quick and light in movement or action; agile, dexterous, and alert — applied to bodies, hands, feet, and minds. Primary sense — quick and light movement. A nimble person or animal moves quickly and easily, with lightness and flexibility rather than mere speed: nimble fingers move fast and precisely; nimble feet step lightly and change direction instantly; a nimble body darts, dodges, and recovers without stiffness or clumsiness. The word implies not just velocity but a ready, responsive, graceful quality — quickness without heaviness, speed with suppleness. Mental sense — quick-witted. Since at least the 16th–17th centuries, nimble has applied to the mind: a nimble mind is quick, sharp, and adaptable, fast to grasp ideas, to invent solutions, and to turn an argument. "Nimble-witted" and "a nimble intellect" name mental agility — the swift seizure and handling of thoughts. This mental sense is the direct ancestor of modern business and technology jargon: "nimble companies", "nimble economies", "nimble startups" are organizations that are flexible, quick to respond, and able to change direction fast. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • Quick and light in bodily movement; agile, sprightly — primary physical sense.
  • Dexterous and precise, especially of hands and fingers ("nimble fingers") — specialized physical sense.
  • Quick-witted, mentally alert, resourceful ("a nimble mind") — 16th–17th century onward.
  • Flexible, adaptable, quick to respond (of businesses, economies, political maneuvering) — modern jargon/metaphor.
  • Obsolete/rare: apt, ready, or clever at some particular thing ("nimble at excuses").
The historical bridge. The word's ancestry is the idea of "taking, grasping". Old English numol meant "quick at taking, receptive, holding fast" — built on the verb niman, "to take, seize". To be nimble was originally to be quick to seize or grasp: a fast hand catching a falling object, a quick mind seizing a point. The physical grasp extended to mental grasp — a quick mind "catches" ideas — and the sense of quickness-in-seizing became general quickness and lightness of movement. The same root produced, on its passive side, the word numb (literally "seized" by cold or shock): nimble and numb are linguistic twins from the same root, one meaning "quick to grasp", the other "gripped and deadened". Near-synonym differentiation:
  • Nimble vs agile: agile stresses whole-body athletic coordination and ease over a wide range of motions; nimble stresses lightness, speed, and delicate precision, often of the hands, fingers, or feet.
  • Nimble vs spry: spry is used almost exclusively of active elderly people; nimble applies to all ages and to mental as well as physical quickness.
  • Nimble vs deft: deft foregrounds practiced skill and competence without necessarily implying speed; nimble joins speed with light, flexible grace.
  • Nimble vs fleet: fleet refers strictly to swift running or footedness; nimble includes running but also covers fingers, hands, and mind.
  • Nimble vs quick: quick is the general word for speed; nimble adds lightness, ease, and readiness — quickness without heaviness.
  • Nimble vs supple: supple stresses flexibility and bendability of the body; nimble stresses speed and readiness of movement.
  • Nimble vs alert: alert emphasizes watchfulness and readiness of attention; nimble emphasizes the speed and lightness of the response itself.
Register and usage. Nimble is uniformly admiring — sprightly, lively, and positive. Common collocations: nimble fingers, nimble feet, a nimble mind, nimble wit, nimble as a squirrel. In modern business and technology writing, "nimble" has been adopted as a praise-word for organizations that are fast, flexible, and adaptive. In the test sentence, the elderly plum-gatherer is called "a nimble plum-gatherer for an old man" — the point is that despite his great age (hyperbolically "two thousand, if he is a day"), he is still quick, light, and deft on his feet and with his hands, plucking delicate fruit swiftly without damaging it. The tone is admiring and slightly playful, celebrating an unexpected vitality that defies the frailties normally associated with extreme old age.
