- Early 17th century nautical coinage - astern appears as a- (on, toward) + stern in sea manuals; technical adverb for position beyond stern, terse command language among sailors.
- Age of Sail 17th–19th century - full institutionalization: dead astern, go astern, fall astern become standard in navigation, logs, and naval tactics; connotation of discipline, seamanship, empire built on precise orientation.
- 19th century literary romanticizing - poets and novelists (Coleridge, Marryat, Melville) use astern for nostalgia and loss: home left astern, youth astern; shifts from pure technique to elegiac metaphor for past.
- Early 20th century steam and naval engineering - slow / half / full astern as engine-order telegraph commands; mechanical, industrial, associated with liners, dreadnoughts, and wartime manoeuvre.
- Mid-20th century space-opera transfer - science fiction maps sea onto space: empires astern, enemy astern, planets astern; playful anachronism that keeps nautical flavour alive among readers who never sailed.
- Contemporary residual use - still alive aboard yachts and warships, otherwise archaic-literary; land speakers meet it in history, fantasy, and sci-fi as flavour word for behind with wake and voyage implied.
