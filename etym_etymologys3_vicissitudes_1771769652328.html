THE EVOLUTION OF "VICISSITUDES" 1. The turning root. Vicissitudes reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European root *weik- / *weyk-, whose physical meaning was to bend, wind, or turn — something pliable that can be bent out of shape or wound along a path. Out of that concrete image grew two families of abstract ideas: yielding under pressure (the thing that bends gives way), and turning in succession (things that turn come round again). On the Germanic side the root yielded words for giving way (German weichen, and the adjective weak, "that which yields or bends under force") and for cyclical succession (English week, the turning of the calendar cycle). These sister branches show how one root could specialize either toward "yield" or toward "turn in regular alternation." 2. Latin: vicis and its family. In Latin the "turn" idea crystallized in the noun vicis (attested especially in oblique case forms), meaning a turn, change, succession, or reciprocal place — the idea of one thing taking the position of another in rotation. Latin built on it a constellation of derivatives: the adverb vicissim, "in turn, by turns"; the abstract noun vicissitūdō, "alternation, change, succession," formed with the abstract suffix -tūdō; and the stand-in nouns vice "in place of" and vicarius "deputy," which yield English vicar, vicarious, and the productive prefix vice- (viceroy, vice-president). The Latin family thus carried two complementary ideas: alternating turns in time, and occupying another's station in a hierarchy. 3. The French bridge. French transmitted vicissitude into English in the early modern period; the English form is first attested in the 1560s–1570s. At first the English word preserved the neutral Latin-French sense of simple alternation — the succession of day and night, of seasons, of conditions. In this early register, vicissitude was a calm, almost scientific word for change considered as a regular rotation. 4. The darkening of the word. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, literary and moral usage steadily darkened the word's colouring. The neutral "succession" of the source became the loaded "reverses of fortune": the alternation was read through the ancient metaphor of the wheel of fortune, which raises the mighty only to dash them down. By the time of Gibbon and the historians who followed him, "the vicissitudes of empire" — of life, fortune, war, and politics — almost always meant the hard, unwelcome turns that no power escapes: glory followed by decline, security followed by crisis. The plural form became idiomatic precisely because the adversity was conceived as a series, a succession of tests, rather than a single blow. 5. Literary attestations. The collocation "vicissitudes of fortune" is a stock phrase of high moral literature from the seventeenth century onward; "the vicissitudes of empire" echoes through Gibbon's Decline and Fall and the historiography it inspired. Biography adopted the formula for careers; the nineteenth-century novel used it for the reversals of personal life. The word's plural rhythm suited the sweep of epic and the moralists' warning that nothing human is stable. 6. The modern register. Today vicissitudes is plural, formal, and slightly archaic — a word of historical, biographical, and editorial prose. It survives in the collocations "the vicissitudes of life/history/empire," "weather the vicissitudes," and "the vicissitudes of the market," the last a modern transfer from polity to economy. Colloquial English prefers "ups and downs" and "twists and turns." The word thus carries with it the accumulated gravity of the wheel of fortune — unchanged in form since its Latin and French ancestors, but emotionally matured from neutral rotation to weathered hardship.
