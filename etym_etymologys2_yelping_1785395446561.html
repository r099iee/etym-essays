Proto-West Germanic *gelpan (to cry out loudly, to boast, to exult; a strong verb — PIE etymon NOT securely established; the root is attested only within Germanic) → Old English gielpan (strong verb, 'to boast, to exult, to cry aloud'; noun gielp / gilp, 'boasting, arrogance, vainglory', condemned in Anglo-Saxon homilies) → Middle English yelpen (13th century; 'to cry out, to shout; to boast'; by the 14th century applied to hounds, 'to give tongue, to cry shrilly') → Middle English, 15th century (sense narrows to the short, sharp cry of a dog in pain or sudden excitement) → English yelp (noun and verb, a short sharp cry, especially of a dog) → English yelping (present participle, formed with the -ing suffix of continuous action) → YELPING Note on debate: a Proto-Indo-European etymon is sometimes floated for the Germanic *gelp- root, but no secure PIE ancestor has been established; the family is best treated as a native Germanic formation. The root itself shows an ancient split between 'cry aloud' and 'boast' — both faces of loud self-assertion — visible from the earliest records in Old Norse gjalpa (to yelp; to brag) and Old Saxon gilpan (to boast).
