- [12th-13th Century] - Borrowed into northern Middle English from Old Norse *þrífast* as a strong Class I verb; originally meant 'to grasp to oneself, catch hold of, prosper through vigorous effort.'
- [14th-16th Century] - Spread into general Middle and Early Modern English, stabilizing the ablaut preterite *throf* / *throve*; established as the prime verb for commercial wealth, agricultural fertility, and martial vigor.
- [17th-19th Century] - Celebrated in Renaissance and Romantic literature (Shakespeare, Milton, Wordsworth) as an emblem of organic flourishing in harmony with nature or divine favor.
- [20th-21st Century] - While weak form *thrived* entered colloquial American speech, *throve* persists in high-register literary narrative, developmental psychology, and ecological prose to evoke deep, resilient flourishing.
