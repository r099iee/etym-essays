Tantamount enters English as a whole Anglo-French phrase tant amunter "to amount to as much," built from tant "so much" (Latin tantus) and amunter "to amount, ascend" (Latin ad + montem). Its story therefore requires two Latin tributaries that meet in medieval France and cross to England together.

Tributary 1: tant < Latin tantus. Latin tantus "of such size, so great, so much" belongs to the correlative series tantus...quantus ("so much...as much") and tam...quam. It is usually derived from PIE demonstrative stem *to- "that" + quantitative suffix, cognate with tam "so" and English thumb-scale quantifiers only distantly. Old French reduced tantus > tant (c. 9th–11th c.), losing endings and final -s voicing, keeping the nasal vowel /tɑ̃/. Anglo-French kept the same form. Its sense never moved: quantity or degree.

Tributary 2: amount < Old French amunter < Latin ad montem. Latin mons, montis "mountain, hill" (from PIE *men- "to project, stand out," whence eminent, prominent) combined with preposition ad "to, up to" in Vulgar Latin *admontare "to go up, climb, rise to a sum." Old French amonter, amunter (11th–12th c.) meant both "to climb" and, figuratively, "to rise in number, to total." Middle English borrowed amounten in the 13th–14th c. with both senses; the climbing sense died, the accounting sense survived. Phonologically ad- assimilated to a-, -mont- kept its nasal, and English stress settled on the second syllable.

  • Anglo-French phrase: medieval legal and commercial Anglo-French used tant amunter / tantamount as a fixed predicate meaning "to come to as much, to be worth as much" in accounts and statutes (13th–14th c.). It was written as two words, then hyphenated, then solid.
  • Adoption into English (c. 1600s): English lawyers and chroniclers, already code-switching between English and Law French, carried the phrase into English sentences: "this is tantamount to..." Early citations (1640s–1670s) show verb, noun, and adjective uses — "to tantamount," "a tantamount," "tantamount unto." The OED's first adjective citation is mid-17th c.
  • Freezing as predicative adjective: by the 18th century the verbal and nominal uses fade; tantamount survives almost exclusively as predicative adjective governing to. Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines it as "equivalent," and 19th-century parliamentary, legal, and journalistic prose makes it a workhorse for imputed equivalence.
  • Modern rhetoric: 20th–21st c. usage is predominantly moral-political: courts ("negligence tantamount to fraud"), diplomacy ("a test tantamount to a declaration"), and comedy — Wodehouse's Bertie solemnly accusing Jeeves of an intonation "tantamount to 'Oh, yeah?'" parodies precisely this inflated forensic register by applying it to a raised eyebrow in the voice.

Thus tantamount preserves, inside one English adjective, a miniature Roman survey (mountain-climbing sums) and a Roman quantifier (so-much), fused in a French counting-house and polished in an English courtroom into a tool for saying: different words, same weight.
