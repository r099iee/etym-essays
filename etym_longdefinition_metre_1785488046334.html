In this passage metres — the plural of metre, signalled by the trailing “s” on the blank — refers not to verse in general but to the named rhythmic patterns imported from classical prosody: the iamb, the trochee, the dactyl, the spondee, the anapaest, and the more exotic combinations of these feet. The sentence’s irony is deliberate and layered. The schoolroom handbooks “parade lists of Greek terms and recherché metres culled from Swinburne and Bridges,” so these metres are presented as classroom curiosities — rare, showy, self-consciously learned verse-forms dug out of the metrical experiments of late-Victorian poets, impressive to name but incapable of explaining how ordinary English verse actually moves. The tone is that of a historian smiling at the futility of an earlier fashion: the “recherché metres” are precious, exotic, and faintly ridiculous precisely because they treat English as though it were a dead quantitative language whose lines could be scanned by the rules of classical Greek and Latin. PRIMARY SENSE — prosody The core, oldest sense of metre is the measured, countable system of feet that organises a line of verse:
  • Accentual-syllabic metre: the system of English verse in which the pattern is built on alternating stressed and unstressed syllables, counted in regular groups (feet) — iambic pentameter, trochaic tetrameter, anapaestic trimeter.
  • Quantitative metre: the classical system of Greek and Latin in which the pattern is built on the duration of long and short syllables rather than on stress — the system the passage is mocking the handbooks for importing into English.
  • Named feet: the iamb (u—), trochee (—u), dactyl (—uu), anapaest (uu—), spondee (——), pyrrhic (uu), amphibrach (u—u), and others, each a fixed grouping of positions within the line.
  • Metrical theory: prosody is the whole study of verse-sound; scansion is the act of marking and naming the feet of a given line; metre is the patterned system itself that scansion analyses.
SECONDARY SENSE — the unit of length Since the late eighteenth century metre (American meter) has also been the fundamental SI unit of length, defined at Paris after the French metric reforms of the 1790s as one ten-millionth of the quadrant of the earth’s meridian. The historical bridge is the shared root: Greek metron meant “a measure,” and the same word that measured verse was pressed into service to measure distance. The English spelling metre is shared by the prosodic and the measuring senses in Britain (only context distinguishes them), while American spelling reform distinguished the unit as meter — a spelling that in British English still denotes a measuring instrument (a thermometer, a gas meter). The two senses are historically separate extensions of one root: the verse sense is ancient and continuous; the unit sense is a deliberate modern recoinage. TERTIARY SENSE — music In music metre (or time) is the rhythmic pattern of a bar — the regular grouping of beats into twos, threes, fours, or other divisions, shown by the time signature (3/4, 4/4, 6/8). Greek rhythmic theory passed into medieval music theory, and the same word came to govern both verse and melody; the metronome, the device that marks tempo, is built on the same root (Greek metron “measure” + nomos “law”). A musical bar is often itself called a measure in American usage, preserving the measuring metaphor in the name. METAPHORIC AND TECHNICAL EXTENSIONS
  • Metric / metrical: the adjectival forms serve prosody and, by extension, any measured or counting discipline — metric geometry, metric spaces in mathematics, the metric system of measurement.
  • Metrology: the science of measurement itself, another reflex of the same root.
  • Figurative “measure”: in older and poetic usage metre shaded toward “due measure, proportion, moderation,” a sense preserved in the archaic verb mete (to measure, to portion out), from the same PIE root *meh₁-.
  • Derived borrowings: geometry (Greek geōmetría, “earth-measuring”), symmetry (“measured together”), diameter, perimeter — the whole measuring family radiates from the same word.
SYNONYM BOUNDARIES
  • Rhythm: Similar because both name patterned movement of sound; differs because rhythm is the broader, older, felt pulse of language, music, and the body, present in prose and speech where no metre exists, while metre is one deliberate, rule-governed, countable subset of rhythm.
  • Cadence: Similar because both describe the rise and fall of sound; differs because cadence is the natural, ungoverned music of a voice or sentence, especially in prose, with no fixed foot, whereas metre imposes a fixed, measured pattern.
  • Prosody: Similar because both belong to the sound-structure of verse; differs because prosody is not a pattern at all but the whole theory and study of versification — metre, rhyme, and sound.
  • Scansion: Similar because both concern the feet of a line; differs because scansion is the analytical act of marking and naming the feet, while metre is the patterned system being analysed.
  • Beat / measure / time: Similar because all name the counting pulse of music or verse; differs because a beat is the individual pulse, a measure is a single bar of fixed length, and time is the everyday term for the metre of music, whereas metre is the overall organising scheme of a line or a piece.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE The word entered English in the fourteenth century from Old French metre, from Latin metrum, from Greek métron “measure.” Its oldest English sense was the measured rhythm of verse; the unit-of-length sense arrived only with the French Revolution; the two senses have coexisted in the single British spelling ever since. It is precisely this double life — the same word for the metre of a poem and the metre of a race — that lets the passage’s “recherché metres” carry a faint smile at scholarly pedantry: these are metres that measure nothing that English verse actually does.
