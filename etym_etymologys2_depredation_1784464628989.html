PIE *ghend- "to seize, take, grasp" (c. 4500–3000 BCE; zero grade *ghnd-) → Latin *prai-hedā > praeda "booty, plunder, prey" (attested from Plautus, 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin praedārī "to plunder, to prey upon" (1st c. BCE) → Late Latin dēpraedārī "to plunder thoroughly, to lay waste" (dē- intensive; 1st c. BCE–4th c. CE) → Late Latin dēpraedātiō(n-) "a plundering, depredation" (3rd–4th c. CE) → Middle French déprédacion / depredacion "plundering, depredation" (12th–14th c.) → English depredation "an act of plundering" (attested late 15th c.) → Modern English DEPREDATION Note on the debated stage: the derivation of Latin praeda from *prai- + *ghend- (de Vaan, Etymological Dictionary of Latin) is the standard account and explains the spelling via *praihedā; it is cognate with Latin prehendō "to grasp" and Greek khandánein "to hold", both from the same root *ghend-. The root *ghend- must not be confused with the distinct PIE root *ghed- "to grasp" which underlies English get.
