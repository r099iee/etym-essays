CHAIN 1 — RAMPARTS PIE *perh₃- 'to provide, produce, bring forth' (reconstructed; also written *perə-; attested by Sanskrit pṛṇāti 'he grants, bestows') → Latin parāre 'to make ready, prepare' (Classical Latin, 2nd–1st c. BCE) → Vulgar Latin *imparāre 'to make ready, to defend' (prefix in- + parāre) → Old French emparer 'to take possession of, to fortify' (10th–12th c.) → Old French remparer 'to re-fortify, to defend anew' (prefix re- + emparer) → French rempart 'a rampart, fortification' (the thing re-fortified; 15th c.) → English rampart 'a defensive wall or earthwork' (16th c.) → English ramparts (plural; target root word) CHAIN 2 — HOVELS (No secure PIE root: the word is Germanic, of obscure origin. The chain below follows the most probable internal derivation and states the uncertainty explicitly.) Proto-Germanic *hufą 'enclosure, yard, court, dwelling' (reconstructed) → Old English hof 'enclosure, yard, court, dwelling' (attested before the Norman Conquest; cognate with German Hof 'court, farmyard', Dutch hof 'court, garden') → Middle English hove 'roof, chamber, dwelling' + diminutive suffix -el (a little court or shelter) → Middle English hovel 'a rough shed for cattle' (15th c.), then 'a miserable dwelling' (16th c.) → English hovel 'a small, wretched dwelling'; plural hovels (target root word) NOTE ON NUANCE: No PIE ancestry for *hufą is established; a speculative link to PIE *keu- 'to bend, vault' has been proposed but is generally rejected and is omitted here. The derivation of hovel from Old English hof + -el, though the most probable, is not certain: some scholars derive the word from an unrecorded continental source. The sense shift from 'cattle shed' to 'wretched human dwelling' is dated to the 16th century. CHAIN 3 — LIMPETS PIE *lab- 'to lick' (reconstructed; attested by Latin lambere 'to lick', Greek láptein 'to lap, lick', Old English lapian 'to lap') → Latin lambere 'to lick' → Late Latin lampetra 'lamprey' (the 'rock-licker', traditionally lambere + petra 'rock', because the lamprey was seen as licking the rocks with its sucker-mouth; true origin debated) → Medieval Latin lampreda / lamprida 'lamprey' → Old English lempedu / lempedū 'limpet, lamprey' (borrowed from Medieval Latin before 1000 CE; the small shellfish was given the lamprey's name because both cling to rocks) → Middle English lempet / lempid 'limpet' (14th c.) → English limpet 'a small conical mollusc that clings to rocks'; plural limpets (target root word) NOTE ON DEBATE: The derivation of lampetra from lambere 'to lick' + petra 'rock' is traditional but not certain; some scholars propose a different source, and the double-borrowing into Old English (the lamprey came as lampreda, the limpet as lempedu, from the same Medieval Latin stock) means the limpet literally carries the lamprey's name — two creatures collapsed into one word because both were seen clinging to rocks.
