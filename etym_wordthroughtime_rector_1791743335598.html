- Classical Roman Antiquity - A civil governor, provincial magistrate, or maritime steersman exercising supreme administrative and directional rule over a province or vessel.
- Late Antiquity & Early Christian Church (4th–6th c.) - Adopted by Christian theologians (notably St. Gregory the Great in his *Regula Pastoralis*) to describe the bishop or parish priest as the *rector animarum*—the spiritual governor and shepherd of souls.
- High Medieval Universities (12th–14th c.) - Established as the elected supreme executive officer of the corporate academic guild (*universitas magistrorum et scholarium*) in Bologna, Paris, Oxford, and Prague, possessing judicial and disciplinary jurisdiction.
- Post-Reformation English Parish Law (16th–18th c.) - Firmly distinguished in English ecclesiastical law as the incumbent parson entitled to full tithes (great and small), possessing a freehold over church and glebe.
- Colonial American Academies (1701–1745) - Employed as the resident head and chief instructor of infant collegiate schools (e.g. Yale College under its 1701 charter) before royal reincorporations replaced the title with 'President.'
- Modern Era (19th–21st c.) - Preserved across Continental Europe and Latin America as the title for university chief executives, in Scotland as the democratically elected student representative on the university court, and in the Anglican/Episcopal churches as the rector of a self-supporting parish.
