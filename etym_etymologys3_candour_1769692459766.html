Candour is a word of light, and its history is the slow movement of that light from the physical world into the moral one, and then from the heart to the tongue. In two thousand years it travels from white-hot metal to spotless purity, from spotless purity to impartial judgement, from impartial judgement to the warm generosity of Austen's Jane Bennet, and finally from warmth to the cool bluntness of modern honesty. The shining root. The chain begins in the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *kand- (or *kend-) "to shine, glow, be white-hot". It is a root of fire and light, and it scattered into a surprising range: in Indo-Iranian it named the most luminous body of the night sky - Sanskrit candra "the shining one, the moon" (whence Hindi chand "moon", and the fragrant pale candana "sandalwood"); in Balto-Slavic it produced the smoke and glow of incense in Old Church Slavonic kaditi "to burn incense" (Russian kadilo "censer"); and in Italic it produced the blazing core of the Roman word-family: candere "to shine, glow, be white-hot". The Roman light machine. From candere, Latin forged a brilliant family of light-words, each with a physical base and a moral extension:
  • candela "a candle" (English candle) - the wax light.
  • incendere "to set on fire" (English incendiary, incense) - the act of kindling.
  • candidus "dazzling white, spotless, pure" - the adjective.
  • candor "brilliant whiteness, radiance" - the abstract noun.
  • candidatus "one clothed in white" - the Roman candidate for office, who wore a toga bleached with lye to signal the spotlessness of his character and public record (hence English candidate).
The crucial move happened inside Latin itself: candor, literally "whiteness", came to mean "purity, freedom from malice, impartiality". A Roman who judged a case "candidly" was one whose judgement was white - unstained by prejudice, bribery, or private hatred. Whiteness of dress signalled whiteness of soul; the physical radiance became the moral one. Through France. Latin candor passed into Old French as candeur, meaning "whiteness, purity, openness", and English borrowed it in the 16th century, spelling it candour (keeping the French -our, where the later American spelling candor restores the Latin -or). At entry the English word still shimmered with its literal sense: candour could mean dazzling whiteness of light or complexion. But within a generation the moral sense dominated: candour was moral whiteness, freedom from prejudice, an unclouded and impartial judgement. The 18th-century warm sense. In the eighteenth century, the English word took on its warmest colour. In the moral vocabulary of the age - in the essays, sermons, and novels of the period - candour came to mean not merely freedom from prejudice but a positive generosity: a disposition to think well of others, to put the best construction on their actions, to refuse bad motives. This is the candour of Jane Austen. In Pride and Prejudice (1813), Chapter 4, Elizabeth tells Jane that the "affectation of candour is common enough", but that to be candid "without ostentation or design - to take the good of everybody's character and make it still better, and say nothing of the bad - belongs to you alone." Here candour is a bright, blameless purity of heart: Jane's judgement is "white" because it is unclouded by suspicion. It is a generous fairness, the opposite of cynical shrewdness - and it is also, Austen gently notes, a kind of sweet blindness to the faults of others. The modern shift. In the nineteenth century the word began to cool and move from heart to tongue. Candour increasingly named openness of speech - saying plainly what one thinks - rather than generosity of judgement. By the twentieth century, candour meant frankness, outspoken honesty, even bluntness: "let me speak with candour" = "let me be blunt". The old warmth faded; the modern sense is straighter and colder. The movement is the reverse of most moral words, which drift from speech to character; candour drifted from character to speech. The legal and institutional afterlife. In modern English, candour has acquired precise institutional uses. In law, the "duty of candour" requires parties and public bodies to make full and frank disclosure to a court, revealing material even when it hurts their case. In medicine and public administration, statutory "duties of candour" require professionals to be honest with patients and the public about mistakes and failings. These uses preserve the deep Latin insight: candour is whiteness of record, an unstained honesty that conceals nothing. Bullet summary of the trajectory:
  • PIE *kand- "to shine, glow, be white-hot" - fire and light.
  • Sanskrit candra "the shining one, moon"; Old Church Slavonic kaditi "to burn incense" - parallel radiance and smoke.
  • Latin candere "to be white-hot" - candor "whiteness; purity; impartiality" - the moral turn inside Latin.
  • Latin candidus "dazzling white, spotless"; candidatus "white-robed office-seeker" - candidate.
  • Old French candeur "purity, openness" - English candour (16th c.) - at first literally "whiteness", then morally "fairness of judgement".
  • 18th c. - the warm sense: generous, benevolent freedom from suspicion (the Austen virtue).
  • 19th-20th c. - the cool sense: frank, open, blunt honesty of speech.
  • Modern institutional use - the legal and medical "duty of candour": full, frank disclosure.
Candour is therefore a word whose entire history is contained in its first syllable: it is about being white - white-hot, white-robed, white-hearted - and every later sense, from impartiality to generosity to plain speaking, is a different form of the same purity. The light began in the furnace and ended in the clear statement of an honest mind.
