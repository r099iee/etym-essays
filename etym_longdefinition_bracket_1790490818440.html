bracket in the source sentence is the child’s little folding seat in front of a hired post-chaise: “my seat was a little bracket in front, (for we used to hire the chaise regularly … and so could have it bracketed and pocketed as we liked).” The family rented the same carriage for two months each summer and had the interior fitted with a small projecting jump-seat (“bracketed”) plus side pockets, turning a two-seater into a snug three-seater with a forward panoramic perch for the boy. The sense is the oldest English one: a small projecting support or shelf fixed to a wall — here, miniaturised as a portable carriage seat.

Modern speakers meet bracket most often as punctuation or as tournament grouping, but those are late extensions of the same architectural image: a pair of marks that stick out and hold something together, like the stone corbels that hold up a shelf. The Oxford English Dictionary records the architectural support from the 1570s–1580s (“bragget, bracket”), the shelf itself by the 1600s, the punctuation marks from the 1750s, the verb “to enclose / to group” from the 1760s–1790s, and the sporting-tournament table from the 1920s. All share the schema of two arms clasping a middle.

  • Core architectural sense: projecting support. A bracket is an L-shaped or triangular fixture — wood, stone, iron — jutting from a wall to carry a shelf, cornice, statue, lamp, or balcony. Corbels, consoles, and angle-irons are all species of bracket. E.g. “the shelf rests on two brass brackets.” The carriage jump-seat is this sense made mobile: a small shelf-seat projecting into the cabin.
  • Shelf / seat / fixture sense. By metonymy the thing carried takes the name: a gas-bracket (wall lamp), a wall-bracket for candles, a taxed “bracket clock” hung on a wall support. The hired post-chaise’s “little bracket in front” is exactly a detachable wall-shelf re-imagined as a child’s stool, screwed or strapped to the carriage front.
  • Verb sense in the source: to fit with brackets. “Could have it bracketed and pocketed as we liked” uses bracket as a workshop verb meaning to equip with folding seats, and pocket to equip with side pouches for books, rugs, and lunches. Coachbuilders in Long Acre (then London’s carriage-building street) offered such reversible customisation for long summer hires — the Regency equivalent of adjusting car seats and door-pockets today.
  • Punctuation sense: enclosing marks. Round brackets ( ), square brackets [ ], curly braces { }, angle brackets < > — each pair clasps inserted matter the way a wall-bracket clasps a shelf. British “brackets” usually means ( ); American “brackets” usually means [ ], with “parentheses” for ( ). E.g. “the date is given in square brackets.” The bridge is visual analogy first noted by printers in the mid-18th century.
  • Grouping and classification sense. To bracket things together is to join them as alike for comparison — “critics bracket Dickens with Thackeray” — and a price, tax, or age bracket is the interval that holds its members: “the 20–30 age bracket,” “a higher tax bracket,” “bracket creep.” Tournament brackets (March Madness, Wimbledon draws) diagram the same clasping on paper. The bridge from punctuation is functional: marks that group words came to name any grouping device.
  • Technical senses: optics, carpentry, engineering. Bracket fungus (shelf fungus jutting from trees), a bracket clock, a camera flash-bracket, dental brackets cemented to teeth to hold the archwire, an artillery “bracket” (two ranging shots falling one short and one beyond the target, clasping it). Each replays the jut-and-hold geometry in a new material, showing how productive the schema remains.

Grammar: noun countable (“two brackets, a set of brackets”); verb regular (“bracketed, bracketing”) with two frames — bracket X (fit with supports) and bracket X with/together (group). Pronunciation /ˈbrækɪt/ with short a; plural brackets. Register is neutral and workmanlike across DIY, office, and academic prose, with the grouping sense slightly formal (“bracketed together in literary history”). The child’s “little bracket” charmingly domesticates a builder’s word: what holds up a cathedral cornice here holds up a four-year-old, giving him the best view in the carriage.
