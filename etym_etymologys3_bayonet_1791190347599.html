Bayonet is a toponymic eponym: it is named for Bayonne, the Basque-French city at the Adour estuary where, by tradition, the device was invented or first mass-produced around the 1640s. French baïonnette, dagger fixed to musket, is first attested in the 1640s–1650s during the Fronde and Franco-Spanish War, with the city-name plus diminutive suffix -ette transparent to contemporaries. English borrows bayonet in the 1650s–1660s in newsbooks and ordnance papers describing French practice. There is no Proto-Indo-European root because the base is a place-name of disputed Basque-Latin origin, not a common noun. Responsible etymology therefore starts with Bayonne, not with PIE.

Bayonne itself continues Latin Baiona, Boia with Basque Baiuna, Baiona, harbor town of whalers, shipwrights, and cutlers. Whether peasants thrusting hunting knives into musket muzzles after running out of shot, or cutlers systematizing the plug dagger for the regiment of Armand de Gramont, the story localizes innovation in a frontier arms trade between France and Spain. Marshal Puységur's memoirs and the 1647 regulations for dragoons are early witnesses. Vauban standardizes the socket ring that lets musketeer fire with blade fixed, and by Steinkirk 1692 and Neerwinden 1693 every major army is converting pike to bayonet-tipped musket, abolishing the pikeman as a separate arm.

  • Toponym Bayonne (Roman to medieval): Latin Lapurdum / Baiona, Basque Baiuna, good harbor; strategic fortress and cutlery center; name etymology itself debated between Basque bai, river, plus una, and Latin personal name, but irrelevant beyond supplying the base.
  • French baïonnette (c. 1647 onwards): baïonnette à manche, plug dagger; then baïonnette à douille, socket; spelling with diaeresis marks hiatus ba-i; contemporary gloss explicitly as from Bayonne.
  • English borrowing (c. 1657 onwards): bayonet, bagonet spellings in parliamentary ordnance and Evelyn's Diary; anglicized stress and loss of French nasal; plural bayonets.
  • Technical diversification (18th–19th c.): socket to sword to knife; British Land Pattern, French Chassepot, German Seitengewehr; American Springfield and Enfield patterns named in Civil War ordnance.
  • Doctrinal apogee and decline (1914–1945): mass drill, sawback controversies, trench usefulness debated; post-1945 retention for ceremony, guard, and aggression training despite rare battlefield use.
  • Engineering transference (19th c.): bayonet joint, bayonet cap, bayonet light bulb borrow the push-twist-lock motion of fixing, carrying the name into domestic technology worldwide.

Phonetically the French nasalized baïon- with three syllables simplifies to English three or two syllables with plain vowels and initial stress. Spelling stabilizes as bayonet after 18th-century bagonet / bayonette variants. Semantically the word never leaves its origin: every use still implies Bayonne's dagger, whether as relic plug in a museum, socket on a Brown Bess, M9 on an M16, or twist-lock bulb in a ceiling. That transparency makes it a textbook eponym like shrapnel, mansard, leotard.

Cultural attestations fix the image: Puységur and Vauban for invention, British 1689 regulations ordering plug replacement, Wolfe and Clive for 18th-century push of bayonet, Napoleonic memoirs for column with cold steel, American Civil War surgeons for wound statistics, Remarque and Graves for World War I disillusion, and modern drill manuals preserving fix bayonets as theatre and discipline.
