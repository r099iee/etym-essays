vellum:
- The skin of the law (14th c.) - Middle English velym, from Old French velin, 'of a calf,' names the fine prepared calfskin on which illuminated books, charters, and law codes are written - a material so durable it can be scraped and reused
- Challenged by paper (15th-16th c.) - the coming of paper presses displaces vellum from the ordinary book, but it remains the substance of records, charters, and deluxe volumes - the writing surface of permanence, ceremony, and command
- Rare and revived (19th-20th c.) - machine-made 'vellum paper' imitates its surface cheaply, while true vellum retreats to ceremonial documents, bookbinding, and specialist manuscripts, gathering a patina of antique prestige
- The membrane of tradition (contemporary) - vellum still means the dynastic and immemorial - a royal charter or a law cut from the yearling's hide; where paper is disposable, vellum endures

battlements:
- The fighting wall-top (14th c.) - from Old French bataillement, a fortification, the word names the notched parapet of castle walls, its solid merlons alternating with crenel gaps through which defenders shoot
- Strictly defensive (medieval) - throughout the castle age battlements are military architecture - the archer's station atop tower and curtain wall, built for fighting and named for it
- Romance and ruin (19th c.) - the Gothic revival turns battlements into the picturesque signature of the castle, reproduced decoratively on Victorian villas and railway stations; the romance outlives the defense
- Castle shorthand (contemporary) - still a genuine defensive feature in name, battlements now almost always evoke the medieval and the romantic rather than the practical - the notched skyline that says 'castle'

cantonment:
- Quartering of troops (mid-18th c.) - borrowed from French cantonnement, 'the quartering of troops in a district,' from canton, 'a corner or district'; the word names the temporary housing of an army within a set area
- The age of empire (18th-19th c.) - in British India cantonment becomes the great sense of the word - a permanent military station with parade grounds, bazaars, and barracks, a walled town of empire on the subcontinent
- Military-bureaucratic and residual (20th-21st c.) - in general use the word survives in formal and military registers for permanent military lodging, while in South Asia it lives on as a place-name and a mark of colonial-era urbanism
