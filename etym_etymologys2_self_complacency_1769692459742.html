Component 1 — SELF:
1. Proto-Indo-European *se- / *swe- "self, one's own" — the reflexive root (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE; cf. Sanskrit sva "one's own", svayam "oneself", Latin se "himself/herself", Greek hé "himself").
2. Proto-Germanic *selbaz "self" (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE; cf. Gothic silba, Old Norse sjálfr, Old High German selb).
3. Old English self / seolf "self, own" (attested from c. 8th c.).
4. Middle English self "self" (c. 12th–15th c.).
5. English self (modern) — first element of the compound.

Component 2 — COMPLACENCY:
1. Proto-Indo-European *plāk- "to be flat, smooth; to calm, soothe, please" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE; cf. Greek plax "flat surface", Germanic *flakaz "flat" → English flat). NUANCE: the reconstruction for placēre is debated; some scholars propose *pleh₁- "to fill" or a separate root, but the "flat/smooth → soothe → please" semantic chain is the standard account.
2. Latin placēre "to please, be acceptable to" (Classical Latin, from c. 3rd c. BCE).
3. Latin complacēre "to please greatly, be entirely agreeable to" — com- (intensive) + placēre (Classical–Imperial Latin).
4. Late/Medieval Latin complacentia "thorough pleasure, satisfaction, delight" (Church Latin, c. 4th c. CE onward).
5. English complacency "calm contentment, satisfaction; (later) smug self-satisfaction" (borrowed from Latin, first attested c. 1640s–1650s). SEMANTIC SHIFT: the theological sense "delight in God's creation" widened and then acquired the modern pejorative sense in the 18th–19th centuries.

Compound assembly — SELF-COMPLACENCY:
1. English self + complacency combined as self-complacency "satisfaction with oneself" (first attested c. 1640s–1670s, in theological and moral writing).
2. English SELF-COMPLACENCY "smug, uncritical self-satisfaction" (modern) — final node.
