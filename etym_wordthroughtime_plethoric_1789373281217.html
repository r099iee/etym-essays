- [Early 17th Century] - Emerges in English medical treatises (initially as plethoric or plethorical) directly from Late Latin plethoricus and French pléthorique, strictly denoting a pathological bodily state characterized by an excess of blood (*plethora*) within the vascular system - [connotation: clinical pathology, humoral imbalance, dangerous vascular congestion]
- [18th Century] - Extends metaphorically into political economy, social satire, and moral philosophy; used to describe overfed, gouty aristocrats, excessively wealthy merchants, and markets drowning in surplus goods - [connotation: decadent luxury, physical overindulgence, financial surfeit]
- [19th Century] - Thoroughly embraced by Victorian literary critics and essayists (such as Thomas Carlyle and Matthew Arnold) to characterize prose, poetry, or historical chronicles choked with an exhausting superabundance of ornamentation, catalogued details, and sprawling clauses - [connotation: stylistic overgrowth, lack of aesthetic discipline, intellectual heaviness]
- [20th Century to Present] - Maintained in modern hematology and pathophysiology as an objective descriptor of conditions involving elevated red blood cell mass (polycythemia) or hypervolemia, while operating in elevated literary discourse as an exquisite epithet for any system, catalogue, or composition suffocating under its own excessive richness - [connotation: critical sophistication, overwhelming abundance, diagnostic precision]
