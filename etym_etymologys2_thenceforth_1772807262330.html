Derivation chain for the first element — THEN: - PIE *to- "this, that" (demonstrative stem), reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. - → Proto-Germanic *þan- / *þana "then, at that time," reconstructed, c. 500 BCE – 200 CE. - → Old English þanne / þonne "then, at that time," attested 8th–11th c. - → Middle English thenne / thanne "then," attested 12th–15th c. - → then, modern English. Derivation chain for the element THENCE (the adverbial form of then): - Middle English thenne "then" + the inherited genitive/adverbial -s (the same suffix seen in hence, whence, and once), producing thennes / thens "from that place, from that time," attested 13th c. - → Early Modern English thence "from that place / from that time," attested 14th–16th c. Derivation chain for the second element — FORTH: - PIE *per- / *pro- "forward, before, in front," reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. - → Proto-Germanic *furþ- "forward, forth," reconstructed, c. 500 BCE – 200 CE. - → Old English forþ "forth, forward, onward," attested 8th–11th c. - → Middle English forth "forth, forward," attested 12th–15th c. - → forth, modern English. Combination of the two elements: - Middle English thennes + forth → thennesforth / thensforth "from that time forward," attested late 14th c. - → regularized spelling thenceforth "from that time onward," attested c. 1475 (15th c.). - → THENCEFORTH "from that time onward," modern English (archaic/literary register). Debated / nuanced points: - Thenceforth is a native English compound, not a borrowed word; all its parts descend continuously from Germanic material. - The -s of thennes / thence is the old genitive/adverbial -s, but some grammarians read it as the plural or as an analogical extension from hence/whence; the precise morphological category is disputed. - OED and most authorities derive then from the same PIE demonstrative stem *to- that gives English the, that, and there; a minority view would separate the temporal adverb from the demonstrative adjective, but the historical evidence (Old English þonne beside þæt "that") strongly supports the shared origin.
