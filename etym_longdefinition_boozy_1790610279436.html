Boozy is an informal adjective meaning drunk or befuddled by alcohol, strongly smelling or suggestive of drink, or habitually given to drinking and convivial excess.

Its core sense describes a person temporarily intoxicated: boozy generalities are remarks made in a drunken haze, a boozy lunch is one soaked in wine, a boozy voice is slurred and over-loud, a boozy breath reeks of beer. Unlike clinical intoxicated or crude pissed, boozy is affectionately comic, slightly old-fashioned British, evoking flushed cheeks, bonhomie, and muddled thinking rather than violence or addiction. To call answers boozy is to say they are loose, vague, overconfident, and warmly self-satisfied, as if composed after sherry.

An extended sense describes occasions, atmospheres, or products defined by alcohol: a boozy weekend, boozy Christmas cake, boozy brunch, boozy novel. Here boozy does not mean drunk itself but alcohol-laden, alcoholic, drink-centred. Food writing loves this: boozy trifle, boozy sauce, boozy chocolates promise rum-soaked indulgence. Travel writing likewise sells boozy Paris, boozy Berlin, boozy cruises as destinations where drink flows freely.

A third sense marks habitual character: a boozy general, boozy actor, boozy uncle. This verges on boozy as mild euphemism for alcoholic, but with protective humour. British tabloids call celebrities boozy rather than alcohol-dependent to keep the tone gossipy, not medical. The line between jolly boozy and problematic drinking is context and frequency, and modern sensitivity increasingly prefers precise terms for dependence.

  • Intoxicated state: currently drunk or tipsy; e.g., boozy students, boozy laughter, boozy generalities uttered without precision.
  • Alcohol-laden thing or event: containing or accompanied by much drink; e.g., boozy lunch, boozy pudding, boozy night out.
  • Habitually drinking person: given to drink; e.g., boozy journalist, boozy clubman. Comic-euphemistic for heavy drinker.
  • Suggestive of drink: smelling, sounding, or looking drunk; e.g., boozy breath, boozy grin, boozy stagger. Metonymic transfer from person to sign.
  • Figurative looseness: intellectually befuddled, vague, sentimental; e.g., boozy sentimentality, boozy nostalgia. Even without literal drink, wandering and maudlin.

Polysemy flows from cause to effect to atmosphere: drink makes a person boozy; the person makes the party boozy; the party leaves even ideas boozy. The suffix -y, as in sleepy, woozy, muzzy, turns the noun booze into a state adjective redolent of condition. Register stays informal, journalistic, and comic; one would not write boozy cirrhosis in a medical chart but boozy lunch in a memoir. The quoted “boozy generalities and self-satisfied value-judgments” uses the figurative sense brilliantly: criticism that should be sharp and sober is instead flushed, fuddled, and pleased with itself.

Grammar is gradable: boozy, boozier, booziest; very boozy, slightly boozy. Collocates with lunch, dinner, weekend, afternoon, breath, grin, laugh, generalities, novelist. Antonyms include sober, abstemious, teetotal, clear-headed, sharp.
