From Semitic spice to Greek science. The practice of embalming is Egyptian — Herodotus, in the fifth century BCE, describes the three grades of mummification in Book 2 of his Histories with the precision of an ethnographer — but the English word arrives by a different road: through the spice trade. At the root of the family stands a Semitic word for aromatic resin, Hebrew bōśem (בֹּשֶׂם, "spice, perfume, fragrant resin"), which appears throughout the Old Testament — in Exodus, where the holy anointing oil is compounded from choice spices, and in the Song of Songs, where the beloved is compared to the balsam tree. Greek borrowed a Semitic form carrying an -l-, probably via Aramaic (balsāmā), producing bálsamon (βάλσαμον), "balsam, the fragrant resin of the balsam tree" — current in Aristotle's school and standard in Theophrastus and the Hellenistic botanists, who describe the opobalsamum of Judea and Jericho as one of the most valuable commodities of the ancient world. From Greek, Latin took balsamum: Pliny the Elder devotes a whole chapter of the Natural History to the balsam tree, noting that it grew only in Judea, that it had become an imperial monopoly, and that it was carried in triumph by the emperors.

Through Old French into English. Latin balsamum passed into Old French as baume ("balm, aromatic resin"), and the French verb embaumer — en- "in" + baume — meant "to steep in balm", applied from the eleventh and twelfth centuries to the treatment of royal and noble corpses with spices, a practice that the crusaders and the merchant cities of Italy had made routine for transporting the bodies of the great. Middle English took the verb as embaumen in the late fourteenth century — the Wycliffite Bible uses it of the patriarchs, and contemporary chronicles of kings — and the past participle embalmed follows in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Genesis 50, where Joseph has his father Jacob embalmed and the Egyptians mourn him for seventy days, and Mark 16, where the women bring spices to anoint the body of Jesus, kept the word in every English Bible from Coverdale to the King James Version, so that "embalmed" and "spices" remained inseparable in the language's most-read text.

The nineteenth-century transformation. Two revolutions reshaped the word's social field. First, the anatomical revolution: the Paris hospitals and the new medical schools of the 1700s needed preserved subjects, and the injection techniques developed by anatomists — notably the preparations of the Scottish surgeon William Hunter and his pupils — turned embalming into a clinical science. Second, the American Civil War of 1861–65: the scale of death and the distance of the battlefields from home made the preservation of soldiers' bodies for return and burial an industry. Dr. Thomas Holmes, the "father of American embalming", and the undertaker-embalmers who followed the armies turned embalming into a mass, commercial, and democratic service. Wartime embalming decisively separated America from Europe: in the United States the practice became normalised, professionalised, and eventually expected; in Britain and on the continent it remained exotic. The twentieth century saw formaldehyde replace arsenical salts from about 1900 onward, the rise of the state-regulated funeral industry, and the notorious 1898 "embalmed beef" scandal, in which the indigestible tinned beef of the Spanish-American War gave the word an ironic military afterlife.

Today. In the twenty-first century the word carries a double consciousness: an ancient, reverent, liturgical resonance — Egypt, the kings, the odour of sanctity — and a modern, clinical, commercial one — the funeral parlour, the viewing room, the formaldehyde certificate. The green-burial and natural-death movements now actively contest the practice, yet the word itself, with its root sense of being steeped in sweet balm, still softens the reality it names, which is why it has been so readily borrowed for anything "embalmed" in memory, in amber, or in tradition.
