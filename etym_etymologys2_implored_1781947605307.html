Derivation chain for IMPLORED (single root word, the preterite/past participle of implore): 1. Latin plōrāre "to cry out, weep, lament, wail" — attested 2nd c. BCE (Plautus). Origin debated: no secure Proto-Indo-European etymology; standard handbooks treat it as an expressive or echoic (onomatopoeic) formation imitative of weeping, and older attempts to connect it with the root of Latin pluere "to rain" are rejected. 2. Latin implōrāre "to call upon for help, to entreat, to invoke with tears" — a compound of in- "in, on, towards" + plōrāre; attested 1st c. BCE (Cicero, also in legal and religious rhetoric). 3. Old French implorer "to implore, to invoke" — borrowed from Latin implorare; attested 12th c. 4. Middle English imploren "to implore" — borrowed from Old French; attested late 14th c. (c. 1387–1400, in the generation of Chaucer). 5. English implore, with the preterite and past participle IMPLORED — formed with the native English past-tense suffix -ed; attested from the 15th–16th c. Note on the debate: the ultimate origin of plōrāre is unknown. Because no Indo-European cognates are securely identified, the word is best described as a Latin expressive formation (possibly sound-imitative), and the chain above begins at the first attested Latin form rather than at a reconstructed PIE root — a deliberate statement of what the record allows.
