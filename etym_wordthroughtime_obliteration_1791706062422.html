- Late 16th to early 17th Century: Literal textual erasure and archival blotting - Used primarily in scholastic, legal, and clerical contexts for striking out letters, scraping wax tablets, or erasing written records from parchment.
- Mid-17th to 18th Century: Extension to cognitive memory and existential oblivion - Applied to the natural fading of human recollections, the loss of ancient languages, and the consigning of historical reputations to oblivion.
- 19th Century: Catastrophic physical ruin and pathological codification - Adopted by military historians and geologists for the total pulverization of cities and landscapes, while simultaneously codified in medical histology for the fibrous closure of blood vessels and anatomical ducts.
- 20th to 21st Century: Nuclear apocalyptic discourse and institutional/cultural erasure - Evokes total atomic devastation and systemic existential nullification, alongside the sociopolitical erasure of academic disciplines, indigenous cultures, or digital footprints.
