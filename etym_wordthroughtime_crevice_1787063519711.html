- 14th–15th c.: a “crevice” means any narrow crack, chink, or split — in earth, timber, plaster, or stone — a plain everyday word for any rent or opening, with no drama attached.
- 16th–17th c.: increasingly used of cracks in stone walls and masonry; the word begins to sound structural and precise.
- 18th c.: naturalists and travel writers adopt crevice for fissures in rock faces and cliffs, giving it a quiet geological tint.
- 19th c.: in adventure and gothic fiction the crevice becomes a stock feature of cliffs and mountains — a hiding place, a point of peril, a place where secrets lodge.
- 20th–21st c.: crevice remains mildly literary and precise, nestled between the everyday crack and the dramatic glacier crevasse; it names a narrow natural opening in rock or stone, with a hint of hidden depth.
