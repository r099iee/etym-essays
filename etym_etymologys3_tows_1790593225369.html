The verb tow is a direct inheritance from the Germanic world of pulling, with a lineage that reaches back to one of the most productive Indo-European roots for motion, *deuk-, to pull, to lead. That root also yields Latin ducere to lead, source of duke, duct, educate, and German ziehen to pull, showing how pulling and leading were once the same act: to lead was to pull forward.

In Proto-Germanic, *deuk- becomes *teuhaną to pull and its iterative *tugōną to keep pulling, to tug repeatedly. Old English preserves this as togian, also spelled togen, meaning to pull, drag, or draw with effort, used for dragging nets, pulling ropes, and drawing carts. It sits alongside teon to pull and tyge a pull, forming a tight family with tug and team, originally a line of animals pulling together. The semantic field is agricultural and maritime: peasants tow harrows, sailors tow boats into harbour against tide.

Middle English refines the word toward rope-work:

  • Nautical specialisation: by the 13th-14th centuries, towen increasingly means to pull a vessel by another vessel or from shore with a rope, distinct from rowing or sailing it. Harbour records speak of fees for towing ships through narrow channels.
  • Towpath landscape: with the growth of river navigation and later canals in the 17th-18th centuries, tow becomes infrastructure. The towpath is the path trodden by men and horses towing barges. Entire economies of horse-towing, tow-ropes, and towage dues grow up along English rivers and canals.
  • Tug differentiation: tug emerges as the frequentative, to keep tugging, for short sharp pulls, while tow retains the sense of long steady traction. A tugboat is literally a boat that tugs, but its function is to tow, preserving both forms in one hull.

The industrial revolution mechanises towing without changing its name. Steam tugs tow sailing ships, motorboats tow aquaplanes and water-skiers, tractors tow ploughs and trailers, aeroplanes tow gliders and advertising banners. Each new technology reuses the old verb because the geometry is unchanged: powered leader ahead, unpowered follower behind, flexible link between. Automotive culture adds the tow-truck, tow-bar, tow-rope, tow-away zone, and the phrasal to be towed away, blending assistance and punishment.

Phonologically, tow illustrates the Great Vowel Shift and smoothing of Old English diphthongs: togian /toɡian/ loses its medial -g-, vocalises to towen /tɔwen/, then to modern /təʊ/, rhyming with go, flow, and tow. Spelling retains w to mark the vanished guttural, distinguishing tow pull from toe digit. The third-person tows adds regular /z/, sounding identical to toes in speech but distinct in writing and context.

Figurative extension to people, to have someone in tow, to tow a child round museums, appears in the 18th-19th centuries and flourishes in comic writing. It treats social influence as rope, affectionate compulsion as traction. Wodehouse delights in this image: aunts tow nephews, valets tow masters, enthusiasms tow victims. The humble Germanic pull-word thus ends as both harbour law and holiday fun, from tugging nets in the North Sea to dragging an aquaplaner across a sparkling lake, one continuous line of traction through a millennium.
