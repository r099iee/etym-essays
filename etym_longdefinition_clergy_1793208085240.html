Clergy names, in its central and defining sense, the collective body of ordained ministers of religion — in Christian use the bishops, priests, and deacons set apart by ordination for the ministry of word and sacrament, as distinct from the laity, the ordinary faithful. The word is inherently collective and plural in force though grammatically singular: one does not say a clergy of one man but a clergyman, a clergywoman, a member of the clergy. In the source sentence it completes the trinity of estates — nobility, gentry, and clergy — rushing in 1797 to sanction repression: the established Church of England, its bishops voting in the Lords and its parsons preaching loyalty in ten thousand pulpits, acting as the praying wing of the propertied order.

The deepest curiosity of the word is the bridge that carries it from lots to liturgy. Greek klēros meant a lot cast for decision, then an allotted portion, an inheritance — and the early Christians called the ministry God allotted portion, or themselves God lot and heritage, until the word for the sacred allotment became the name of the allotted servants themselves. Whether the ministers are God portion among men or God portion of men was disputed by the Fathers themselves, and the beautiful ambiguity survives in the English word: the clergy are both set apart for God and belonging to God in a special sense. No other European priesthood carries quite this etymological poetry, and preachers have drawn sermons from it for fifteen centuries.

Core meaning in detail: In the Church of England the clergy are those in holy orders — deacons, priests, bishops — ordained by a bishop, licensed to preach and celebrate the sacraments, and historically distinguished by education, dress, tithe income, and a separate estate in Parliament through the Lords Spiritual. Roman Catholic and Orthodox use matches this in structure with its own theology of sacramental priesthood; Presbyterian and Nonconformist churches apply equivalent terms — ministry, ministers — while sometimes resisting clergy itself as implying an unscriptural gulf between minister and people. Looser journalistic use extends the word to the religious functionaries of any faith — the Muslim clergy, Buddhist clergy — a convenient but theologically imprecise borrowing, since Islam and Buddhism know no ordination in the Christian sense. Adjectival and institutional extensions abound: clerical dress, clerical errors (from the copyists), the clergy list, benefit of clergy — the medieval privilege by which a clerk in orders, and eventually anyone who could read, escaped the secular gallows.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The ordained ministry collectively (primary sense, from the 13th c.): the bishops, priests, and deacons as a body — the clergy gathered in convocation. The bridge from Greek klēros runs through the Fathers: the ministers as God allotted portion, the lot that fell to divine service.
  • The clerical estate as a political order: the First Estate in France, the Lords Spiritual in England — the clergy as landlords, legislators, and magistrates, as in the source sentence. The bridge is establishment itself: where church and state interlock, the priesthood becomes a branch of government, taxing tithes and trying morals.
  • Ministers of non-Christian religions (extended modern use): the Iranian clergy, the Tibetan clergy. The bridge is functional analogy: robed, trained, authoritative interpreters of a tradition look like clergy to Western eyes, though the theology of ordination is absent.
  • Learned or literate men generally (medieval sense, obsolete): in the Middle Ages clerk and clergy shaded into learning itself, because only churchmen could read — hence clerical skill, a clerkly hand. The bridge is the monastic monopoly of letters: the man of God was the man of the book, and his name became the name of literacy.
  • Office and administrative workers — clerks (diverged modern sense): the office clerk descends directly from the medieval clerk in minor orders who kept accounts and copied rolls. The bridge is the pen: the church servant literate enough for paperwork became the secular servant defined by it, until clerical work now means typing and filing with no church in sight.
  • Benefit of clergy and its legal fossils (historical legal sense): the privilege of trial in church courts, later the ability to plead reading of the neck verse to escape hanging. The bridge is jurisdictional: the clergy were subject to their own courts, and the exemption outlived its rationale by centuries before abolition in the 1820s.

Register and connotation: clergy is formal and institutional — newspapers, statutes, and church reports — while ordinary speech prefers ministers, priests, vicars, or the church. Historically the word attracted both reverence and the fiercest anticlerical satire: Chaucer sly friars, Reformation polemic, and radical broadsides like the source sentence, where the clergy appear as the sanctifiers of power. Modern secular use is neutral to mildly distant — the clergy were consulted — while within the churches the word remains honourable and everyday. The enduring tension of the term is the one the radicals exploited: servants of God who are also servants of the establishment, the heavenly lot entangled with earthly rents.
