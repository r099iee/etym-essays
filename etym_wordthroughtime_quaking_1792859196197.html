- Old English — cwacian: to shake or tremble, used of bodies, earth, and shaking objects alike; a physical verb of vibration with no specialised fear sense yet.
- Middle English — quaken: the shaking word of devotional terror, as preachers describe sinners quaking before judgement and the earth quaking at the Crucifixion.
- 17th c. — the Quakers are named: trembling under spiritual conviction becomes a sect’s badge, and quaking gains a religious association of holy dread.
- 18th–19th c. — novelists make it the default verb of abject fear: villains quake, knees quake, hearts quake; the earthquake noun quake spins off as its own modern form.
- Today — the participle survives as storytelling shorthand for visible terror, as in Sam’s cry: quaking marks the moment courage trembles but holds, the body shaking while loyalty speaks.
