• fortress: Similar because it denotes a large fortified place; differs because a fortress is a purely military term and carries no association with imprisonment or with the French Revolution.
  • citadel: Similar as a strong fortification, often dominating a city; differs because a citadel is the defensive core of a town, usually garrisoned, whereas the Bastille was a detached gate-fortress turned prison.
  • stronghold: Similar because it names a securely defended place; differs because it is often figurative (“a stronghold of privilege”) and implies a centre of power rather than a building with a specific history.
  • castle: Similar because both are medieval fortified structures; differs because a castle is a noble residence as well as a defence, while the Bastille was never a domestic palace and became a prison.
  • keep: Similar as the strongest part of a fortification; differs because a keep is the central tower of a castle, not a free-standing fortress guarding a city gate.
  • bastion: Similar because it belongs to the same etymological family and denotes a projecting defensive work; differs because a bastion is a section of a rampart, not a whole building, and the word is far more common in metaphor (“a bastion of freedom”).
  • prison: Similar because the Bastille held prisoners; differs because a prison is a generic institution defined by custody, whereas Bastille names one building and the political meaning attached to it.
  • dungeon: Similar in its associations of dark, unjust confinement; differs because a dungeon is a cell, usually underground, and the word is archaic and melodramatic, whereas the Bastille was a fortress with apartments as well as cells.
  • gaol: Similar as a place of confinement; differs because it is the ordinary British word for a local prison, with no political or historical charge.
  • tower: Similar because the Bastille was a tower-fortress; differs because a tower is a single structure, and the word lacks the institutional and revolutionary associations.
