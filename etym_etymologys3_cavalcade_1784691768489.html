Cavalcade begins in the stables of the Roman world. Classical Latin had two words for the horse. Equus was the noble, literary word — the horse of poetry, of the cavalry class, and the source of English equestrian and equine. Caballus, by contrast, was the plain word for a work-horse, a nag, a pack-horse, or a gelding: the kind of animal a farmer or a soldier rather than a poet would own, the mount of the common man. The two words coexisted for the whole of Latinity, and the difference between them was social before it was lexical. Caballus itself is not an inherited Italic word: it is very probably a loan from a western Mediterranean or Gallic substrate — the Gaulish word for horse is recorded as caballos — and its deeper history is debated. Some scholars have proposed a connection to a Proto-Indo-European root *kab- 'to seize', on the guess that a horse is 'the seized, the captured' animal, but this is a minority position and cannot be demonstrated; the honest verdict is that caballus is a substrate word of uncertain, probably Celtic-adjacent origin, with no secure PIE etymon. What happened next is one of the great demographic facts of linguistic history. As the Roman world gave way to the Romance-speaking kingdoms, the aristocratic equus died out of the spoken language almost everywhere, and it was the humble caballus that became the ordinary word for 'horse' in every major Romance language: Italian cavallo, French cheval, Spanish caballo, Portuguese cavalo, and Romanian cal all descend from it. A low word, the word of nag and pack-horse, had inherited the earth. From caballus, late Latin built a small family of riding words. The verb caballicāre, 'to ride on horseback', passed into the daughter languages — Italian cavalcare, French chevaucher, Spanish cabalgar, Old Occitan cabalgar — and from the verb was formed a noun meaning a ride, a mounted excursion, and by extension a company of riders: Late Latin caballicāta, the ancestor of Italian cavalcata, Spanish cabalgada, Portuguese cavalgada, and Old French chevauchie (modern French chevauchée), which in English history denotes a mounted raid. The English word comes through the Italian and French channels. Italian cavalcata meant a ride on horseback and, by extension, a troop of horsemen riding out together; French borrowed it in the sixteenth century as cavalcade, a procession of riders; and English took the word from French in the late sixteenth century, in the closing years of Elizabeth I's reign, during the great wave of Italian and French cultural influence that also brought the English language such company as cavalier, cavalry, chevalier, and chivalry — all four of which, remarkably, descend from the same lowly caballus. The first recorded English uses of cavalcade refer to a ride on horseback and to a train of riders, and from the start the word carried a hint of spectacle: a cavalcade is something to be watched, whether a royal entry, a noble arrival, a military review, or a long line of riders winding through a landscape. The semantic drift is steady and instructive — from a common noun for a work animal, to a verb for riding it, to a noun for a ride, to a noun for a formal procession of riders, to a general word for any dignified or impressive line of travel — a four-hundred-year climb from the stables to the pageant. The word's register in English has never lost that theatrical edge. A cavalcade is a procession with an audience in mind: it is public, ceremonial, and conspicuous, and to call something a cavalcade is to dignify it, to frame it as spectacle. It is this grand, pageant-like colouring that Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit, chapter 2, where the word performs a delicate act of perspective-shifting. The narrator has just reported Bilbo's sniffing complaint about 'all this dwarvish racket', and then adds, in an urbane, conspiratorial aside, that he does not suppose the reader 'would have noticed anything at all on a windy night, not if the whole cavalcade had passed two feet off.' The thirteen dwarves and one reluctant hobbit, striding through the dark with their gear and ponies, are suddenly dignified into a mounted procession — a courtly image that sits in comic contrast with the 'racket' of the preceding sentence. The humour works by incongruity: Bilbo hears a clatter, while the narrator calls the same company a cavalcade; the grandeur of the word measures how far ordinary human senses fall short of the hobbit's, and it quietly underlines the theme of the chapter, in which the respectable Bilbo is being drawn, against his will, into an adventure far more impressive and dangerous than he imagines. In modern English the word retains its riders and its grandeur. It still properly insists on people on horseback — or, by reasonable extension, on ponies, in carts, carriages, or vehicles — and on a certain ceremonial or conspicuous character; a cavalcade may be grand or merely long, but it is always a public spectacle rather than a mere mode of transport. The figurative extension to any continuous and impressive succession, as in 'a cavalcade of events' or 'a cavalcade of history', remains rare and literary. The word has also generated its own back-formations: motorcade, coined in American English in the twentieth century for a procession of motor vehicles (modelled on cavalcade by replacing the horse-element with motor-), and the rarer autocade, camelcade, and even bicyclecade — living evidence that English speakers still feel the word's shape as 'a ride + a procession'. In historical writing cavalcade remains the standard word for the mounted processions of the old world, and it sits in the English dictionary as one of the clearest examples of how a colloquial word for a nag rose, through four centuries and two languages, to become a word for the most dignified form of public travel. Milestones in brief: - Uncertain substrate origin (possibly Gallic caballos); Latin caballus 'work-horse, nag' attested 1st century BCE (Varro), opposed to equus. - Romance inheritance: Italian cavallo, French cheval, Spanish caballo, Portuguese cavalo, Romanian cal. - Late Latin caballicāre 'to ride'; caballicāta 'a ride, a company of riders'. - Italian cavalcata; French cavalcade (16th century). - Late 16th century: English cavalcade 'a ride on horseback; a procession of riders'. - 17th century on: the sense settles on the formal, ceremonial mounted procession; family words cavalier, cavalry, chevalier, chivalry enter English. - 20th century: back-formation motorcade; figurative 'cavalcade of events'. - 1937: Tolkien's 'the whole cavalcade' in The Hobbit.
