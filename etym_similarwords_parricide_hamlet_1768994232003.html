• Patricide: Similar in naming the killing of a parent; differs because "patricide" is strictly the killing of one's father, the narrowest and commonest substitute, while "parricide" covers a parent or any near relative at once.
  • Matricide: Similar in belonging to the family of -cide crimes; differs because "matricide" is the killing of one's mother specifically, a rarer and more particular horror than the broad "parricide."
  • Fratricide: Similar in being a -cide crime against kin; differs because "fratricide" is the killing of a brother specifically — also used of civil war and of killing one's own kind — while "parricide" covers parent or near kin.
  • Sororicide: Similar in being a -cide crime against a sibling; differs because "sororicide" is the killing of a sister specifically, far rarer and narrower than the family-wide "parricide."
  • Homicide: Similar in being a legal term for killing; differs because "homicide" is the neutral word for any killing of a human being, while "parricide" is freighted with the moral weight of the domestic taboo.
  • Regicide: Similar in being a solemn crime against a symbolic father; differs because "regicide" is the killing of a king, public and political, while "parricide" is the more intimate and unbearable murder of one's own flesh and blood.
  • Infanticide: Similar in being a -cide crime within the family; differs because "infanticide" is the killing of an infant, a crime against the child, while "parricide" is the child's crime against the parent.
  • Filicide: Similar in being a parent's killing of kin; differs because "filicide" is the killing of one's own child, the reverse direction of the family bond from "parricide."
  • Uxoricide: Similar in being a -cide crime of the household; differs because "uxoricide" is the killing of one's wife specifically, narrower than the family-wide "parricide."
  • Village: Similar to hamlet in being a small settlement; differs because a "village" is larger, has its own church and community institutions, and is the standard next step up from a hamlet.
  • Settlement: Similar in naming a place where people live; differs because "settlement" is the general term for any inhabited place, while "hamlet" is the smallest specific kind — a bare cluster of dwellings.
  • Town: Similar in being a settled place; differs because a "town" is a market-and-civic centre with charters and streets, while a "hamlet" is a handful of houses with no church and no market.
  • Crossroads: Similar in naming a small rural meeting-place; differs because "crossroads" names the junction itself, not the settlement around it, though a hamlet often grows exactly there — as in the passage's "crossroads hamlet."
  • Dwelling: Similar in naming human habitation; differs because a "dwelling" is a single house, while a "hamlet" is a whole cluster of dwellings forming the smallest settlement.
