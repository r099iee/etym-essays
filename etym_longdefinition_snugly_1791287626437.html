Snugly is the manner adverb of the adjective snug, meaning in a cozy, tight-fitting, warmly enclosed and comfortable way, with no loose space, draught, or insecurity. To do something snugly is to do it so that closeness itself produces comfort: to fit closely, to rest warmly, to sit securely within protective surroundings.

In the source phrase "nestling snugly abaft the shoulder blades," snugly modifies nestling and tells how the nestling happens: not loosely or precariously, but tucked in tight, warm, and secure, comfortably cradled by the hollow behind the shoulders.

The core image combines two ideas that English usually keeps apart: tightness and pleasure. A lid can fit tightly but not snugly; a blanket can be comfortable but not snug if it hangs loose. Snugly requires both: close contact and felt comfort. It implies just enough pressure to feel held, sealed, and safe, never constricted or painful.

  • In a warmly cozy, comfortable manner (bedding, clothing, embrace): to sleep snugly under blankets, to wrap up snugly, to cuddle snugly. The semantic bridge is bodily warmth: enclosure traps heat and banishes exposure. This is the dominant modern sense.
  • In a tight-fitting but comfortable way (garments, housings, joints): shoes fitting snugly, a plug fitting snugly into a socket, insulation fitting snugly between joists. Here the emphasis is on exact fit without pinching or rattling; engineers and tailors use snugly as a term of praise for zero-gap precision that remains comfortable.
  • In a sheltered, compact, well-protected position (buildings, landscape): a cottage sitting snugly in a valley, a harbour tucked snugly behind a headland, a baby strapped snugly into a carrier. The extension moves from body to place: as clothes hug the body, hills or walls hug the house.
  • Securely and neatly, without looseness or risk of slipping: to tie something snugly, to hold a baby snugly, to strap luggage snugly. The sense shades toward safety and competence: snug handling prevents accident.
  • Smugly or self-satisfiedly (secondary, via adjective snug = self-contented): to sit snugly in privilege, ensconced snugly in office. This rarer, often ironic extension draws on the idea of being too comfortable, insulated from criticism or hardship.
  • Affectionately and intimately (with verbs of closeness): to nestle snugly, to curl up snugly, to nuzzle snugly. The adverb intensifies tenderness; it turns mere proximity into felt coziness.

Register and connotation are almost always positive: snugly evokes domestic warmth, good craftsmanship, maternal care, winter shelter, and well-managed compactness. Unlike tightly (which can imply restriction, tension, or miserliness), firmly (strength), or closely (neutral proximity), snugly adds pleasure to proximity. To say a baby is held tightly may alarm; to say held snugly reassures.

In short, snugly means in a close, warm, secure, and comfortable manner where tightness is experienced as coziness rather than constraint.
