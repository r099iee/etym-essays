- Old English 'thrysce' - the plain native name of the mottled songbird of field and hedgerow; for medieval countryfolk the thrush was a familiar, homely bird prized for its clear, loud song.
- Medieval & early modern folklore - the thrush gains its signature behaviour and proverbial reputation: the 'song thrush' that breaks snail shells on a chosen stone anvil; proverbs and bird-lore link its song with the return of spring, making it a marker of the countryside's liveliness.
- 19th century - naturalists divide the family precisely (song thrush vs mistle thrush), and Victorian bird-writing makes the thrush, with the blackbird and nightingale, an emblem of the English pastoral idyll and its dawn chorus.
- Contemporary - the word remains the standard, unsentimental name of a common garden bird; its soft song still carries faint old-fashioned associations of hedgerow England, and it is the bird Tolkien reaches for as the mythic messenger that knocks at the grey stone.
