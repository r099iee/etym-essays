Mirkwood is a modern English place-name with two very different histories woven into one compound. Its second element, wood, is one of the oldest and most ordinary forest-words of the Germanic languages; its first element, mirk, is the archaic-poetic spelling of murk, a word of darkness whose Germanic pedigree goes back to the age of the rune-stones. The compound as a whole, however, is not inherited from any prehistoric ancestor: it is a literary coinage by J.R.R. Tolkien in 1937, built transparently on the model of the Old Norse legendary forest-name Myrkviðr, and its power lies precisely in being both perfectly plain and perfectly old. PART ONE — WOOD: an unbroken forest of a word The word wood belongs to the deepest stratum of Germanic vocabulary. Its chain runs from Proto-Indo-European *widhu- "tree, wood" (the same base visible in Old Irish fid and Welsh gwydd) through Proto-Germanic *widuz to Old English wudu / widu, attested from the eighth century and meaning tree, wood, forest, and timber at once. In Middle English the form becomes wode, and by the early modern period it settles as wood. Throughout its history it has been the everyday word of trees and forest — the word English speakers reach for when naming a piece of country covered in trees — and it has been extraordinarily productive in place-names and compounds: Blackwood, Redwood, Greenwood, Hollywood, wooden, woodman, woodland. When Tolkien needed an English second element for his dark forest, wood was not an exotic choice but the most natural one available, the same element that had been making forest-compounds in English for a thousand years. PART TWO — MIRK: the dark northern word The first element has a more dramatic pedigree. The Germanic family of dark-words is reconstructed as Proto-Germanic *merkwaz, "dark, gloomy," and specialists connect it, though with debate about the exact shape, to a Proto-Indo-European root associated with dimness and flickering light (*mer-). The family has two great daughters relevant here. In the North, Old Norse myrkr "darkness" became the standard word of gloom, and it lies behind the modern Scandinavian words for darkness and the famous place-name itself. In the West, Old English mirce / myrce "dark, murky" is attested but rare; the word survives into Middle English as mirke, merke, murk, and then into Modern English chiefly as murk (noun and adjective) and murky (dark, obscure, and by metaphor morally shady). The spelling mirk was preserved in northern and Scots English and was taken up by Romantic and later poets as an archaic, atmospheric form — precisely the register Tolkien wanted. "Mirk" is thus not a Tolkien invention but a real, if marginal, English word that he chose for its darkness and its age. PART THREE — MYRKVIÐR: the Norse model The key to Mirkwood is that it was not invented from nothing. Old Norse heroic literature contains a famous dark woodland called Myrkviðr — literally "dark forest" (myrkr + viðr) — appearing in Eddic poetry and legendary sagas as the mysterious, perilous forest separating realms. Tolkien, a professional philologist with deep expertise in Old Norse and Old English heroic poetry, knew this name well, and when he needed a great dark forest to cross on the road to the Lonely Mountain he reached for the legendary model and anglicized it: not Myrkviðr but Mirkwood, a transparent Modern English compound that any reader understands while still feeling its archaic, northern weight. PART FOUR — the name as history within the story Within the legendarium the name carries an internal biography that mirrors its real etymology. The forest was long known as Greenwood the Great, a name of life and green; when a shadow and a necromancer established themselves at its southern margin, the forest was renamed Mirkwood — the change of name recording the change of the land. After the shadow's fall in the events of The Lord of the Rings, the elves rechristen it Eryn Lasgalen, "Wood of Greenleaves," closing the circle. The history is embedded in the vocabulary: Greenwood, Mirkwood, Wood of Greenleaves are three names of one place, each transparent, each registering a stage of its story. PART FIVE — the map-talk in The Hobbit In the scene from which this card is drawn, Thorin says simply, "And I know where Mirkwood is, and the Withered Heath where the great dragons bred." The word arrives as casual geography — a landmark named by a dwarf who has not yet had to walk it. The reader, however, already hears the whole inheritance: the Old Norse legend, the Old English dark-words, the poet's archaic spelling, and the transparent compound meaning "dark wood." It is a warning label worn as a place-name, and the fact that it sounds old is not decoration but function: the darkness is baked into the very name before any spider appears. This is Tolkien's philology made story — an etymology that reads as dread.
