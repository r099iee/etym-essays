English COARSELY (adverb, formed in the 16th–17th c. by adding the adverbial suffix -ly to the adjective coarse, which itself was split off in spelling from course in the 16th c.) — from Middle English cours "ordinary, common quality" (the adjective use of the noun course) — from Old French cors / cours "course, path, way, order" — from Latin cursus "a running, a course" — from currere "to run" — from PIE *kers- "to run." Stage chain (single linear derivation): 1) PIE *kers- "to run" (reconstructed; the root of the Latin verb; also spelled *ḱers- in some handbooks) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE; cognate with Greek epíkouros "helper" and various "run" words in the family (the reconstruction is standard but the semantic spread is debated). 2) Latin currere "to run" (verb, attested from the 3rd c. BCE), with supine stem curs- — the source of the noun. 3) Latin cursus "a running, a course, a way, an order, a career" (noun, attested from the 1st c. BCE) — also "ordinary course of things, customary order." 4) Old French cors / cours "course, path, way, order, sequence" (noun, attested from the 9th–10th c. onward) — development of Latin cursus. 5) Middle English cours / course "course, path, way, customary order" (noun, borrowed from Old French, attested from c. 1300) — also used adjectivally for things "of the ordinary course or common quality," which is the semantic bridge. 6) English course (adjective use, 14th–15th c.) "of the ordinary, common, everyday quality" — applied to cloth, fare, and goods of everyday kind; this "common" sense is the ancestor of coarse. 7) English coarse "rough, harsh, unrefined; of large grain or texture; vulgar in manners" (adjective, spelling split from course in the 16th c. as the senses diverged; the "rough, vulgar" sense established by the late 16th c.). 8) English COARSELY "in a rough, harsh, unrefined, or vulgar manner; in large grains or pieces" (adverb, coarse + -ly, first attested late 16th c.) — the target root word. Note on debate: The chain from PIE *kers- to currere to cursus to Old French to English course is standard and secure. The interesting and slightly debated step is the adjective: dictionaries differ on whether coarse is a direct semantic development of the adjective use of course ("of ordinary quality") or a distinct dialectal form; the mainstream view (OED, Etymonline) is that coarse is the same word as course, split in spelling in the 16th century when the quality sense ("common," hence "rough, inferior") diverged from the path/sequence sense. The adverb coarsely is straightforwardly coarse + -ly, attested from the late 16th century.
