DERIVATION CHAIN 1 — MUSH
R → PIE *meik- 'to mix' — the ancestral root of words for mixing, blending and kneading (Latin miscēre 'to mix', Greek mignymi 'to mix', English 'mix'); the food sense grows from the idea of mixing meal with liquid.
E → Proto-Germanic *maisk- / *maiskaz 'crushed, mixed, mashed' — reconstructed on the strength of German Maische 'crushed malt, mash', Middle Dutch metscen 'to mash', and the English family.
D → Old English māscan 'to mash, crush into pulp' (with the noun māsc 'mashed wort, brew'), the direct ancestor of English 'mash'.
C → Middle English maschen / mash 'to crush, pound, mix into pulp' — the source verb; 'mush' is a 17th-century dialectal variant of the same word, specialised as the cornmeal porridge of North America (with possible reinforcement from German Mus 'soft food, porridge', a parallel formation).
B → English mush (1670s) 'boiled cornmeal porridge' — the American staple sense, quickly extended to any soft pulpy mass.
A → Modern English MUSH 'a soft, pulpy mass' — with the figurative 'mushy' sentiment (19th c.) and the dog-sled command 'Mush!' (19th c.).
Debate note: The PIE reconstruction *meik- 'to mix' rests on the Germanic and Italic agreement but is not universally accepted; some etymologists treat 'mash' as of obscure, possibly imitative origin. The 17th-century 'mush' may equally derive from German Mus (a Pennsylvania-German contribution to American English) rather than as a native variant of 'mash'; both routes converge on the same word and the same soft-food meaning.

DERIVATION CHAIN 2 — RICKETY
R → PIE *bhreg- 'to break' (Pokorny) — the root of 'break' and 'fragile'; Greek rhēgnymi 'to break, burst' derives from a related *wreg- with an 'r' prefix, and the Greek noun rhachis 'spine, ridge, backbone' is plausibly built on it (the spine as the ridged 'broken line' of vertebrae). [The connection of rhachis to *wreg- is plausible but not certain.]
E → Greek rhachis 'spine, ridge, backbone; a stake or palisade of brushwood' — attested in anatomical and botanical Greek for the backbone and for the axis of a frond.
D → Greek rhachitis (feminine adjective) '(the disease) of the spine' — rhachitis nosos 'disease of the spine', the medical term for spinal inflammation.
C → New Latin / English rickets (1630s) — a corruption of rachitis: the Latin rh became English 'r', and the ending was popularised (probably under the influence of 'wrick' 'to twist' and the dialect word 'rickets' for convulsions) into the familiar form.
B → English rickety (1720s) 'affected with rickets' — the adjective built from 'rickets' + '-y'; by the 1840s extended to mean 'loosely jointed, weak, liable to collapse' of furniture, buildings and bodies.
A → Modern English RICKETY 'shaky or likely to collapse' — the common sense of the card.
Debate note: The pre-Greek stage is uncertain. 'Rickets' itself is a documented English corruption of rachitis, but the exact influence of 'wrick' and of folk-etymology is debated. The ultimate PIE root for rhachis is disputed — connections to *bhreg- 'to break' and to a separate *wreg- 'to bend' are both proposed; the safest statement is that the Greek medical word is securely attested from antiquity, while its Indo-European ancestry remains speculative.
