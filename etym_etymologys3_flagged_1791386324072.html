The past tense and participial form flagged represents one of the most fascinating convergence points in Germanic historical linguistics. In contemporary English, a single phonetic form /flæɡd/ performs three utterly divergent semantic duties: it describes a conversation or stamina that has withered into exhaustion; a transaction or email that has been marked for regulatory scrutiny; and a courtyard that has been paved with heavy stone slabs. Disentangling the historical development of flagged requires tracing three independent lexical streams that arose from distinct Indo-European and Proto-Germanic roots before converging phonologically in the Early Modern English period.

  • Stream 1 — The Drooping and Languishing Verb: The sense directly operating in the Wodehouse source context (“conversation inevitably flagged a bit”) derives from an ancient Germanic imitative root, *flak-, itself commonly linked by Indo-Europeanists to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *plāk- or *plek- (meaning “to strike, beat, or flap”). In North Sea Germanic and Scandinavian dialects, this root formed verbs describing the loose, fluttering motion of cloth, birds’ wings, or sails hanging loosely in a dying breeze. Old Norse records flakka (to flap about, flutter, or rove aimlessly), while Middle Dutch had vlaggren and Middle High German possessed vlackern (to flicker or flare uncertainly). In northern Middle English and Early Scots texts of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the word appears as flakken and flaggen, signifying to flap or flutter without direction. By the 1540s, a crucial semantic shift occurred in Tudor English: speakers focused not on the vigorous flapping of fabric in a gale, but on what happens to fabric when the wind abruptly dies. To flag came to mean to hang down limp, to droop flaccidly, and to collapse under one’s own weight. Within three decades, Elizabethan poets and dramatists (including Shakespeare and Spenser) executed the metaphorical leap from physical fabric to human vitality. In Henry VI and contemporary dramas, wings flag, courage flags, and spirits flag. By the eighteenth century, the image of a windless sail hanging limp upon a mast had transferred permanently to human discourse: an intellectual argument, a political campaign, or a social conversation that lost its animating breeze was described as having flagged.
  • Stream 2 — The Cloth Banner, Signaling, and Algorithmic Surveillance: Entirely distinct in its early nominal morphology, the noun flag (a piece of cloth displaying an emblem, attached to a staff or halyard) entered English during the mid-sixteenth century (attested in naval inventories of the 1570s). Etymologists suspect it was borrowed from sixteenth-century Scandinavian (compare Danish flag, Swedish flagg) or Middle Low German vlagge, ultimately sharing an ultimate onomatopoeic kinship with the fluttering motion of Stream 1, but developing through an independent nominal channel. As naval warfare and merchant shipping grew increasingly complex in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, ships communicated tactical orders across vast stretches of water through predetermined systems of colored signal flags (a practice formalized by Admiral Howe and Lord Nelson). From this maritime protocol arose the transitive verb to flag — to communicate with, signal, or order to halt via a flag. By the mid-nineteenth century, this expanded to railways (railway flagmen warning of track hazards) and urban streets (waving down a hansom cab or modern taxi). In the mid-twentieth century, computer engineers working on early mainframe architectures adopted “flag” as a metaphor for a single-bit memory register that signaled the presence or absence of a specific programmatic condition (true/false). This computing usage directly spawned the modern digital landscape, where billions of emails, financial transactions, and social media posts are automatically flagged by software filters every second.
  • Stream 3 — The Old Norse Stone Slab (Flagstone): The third homographic stream traces back to Old Norse flaga, denoting a flat slab of stone, a flake, or a strip of turf cut from the soil. This word entered northern English during the Danelaw period, surviving as dialectal flag (a flat paving stone). In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the compound flagstone crystallized, and builders derived the transitive verb to flag, meaning to lay or pave a walkway with these heavy, flat stones. Thus, a “flagged courtyard” bears no etymological relationship to drooping sails or signal banners, but descends directly from Viking stonecutters.

When Bertie Wooster observes that “conversation inevitably flagged a bit,” the listener hears the historical echo of Stream 1: the comic pathos of social energy deflating like a windless sail on an August afternoon, demonstrating how an ancient Germanic verb of fluttering limpness found its ultimate satirical home in the drawing rooms of English comedy.
