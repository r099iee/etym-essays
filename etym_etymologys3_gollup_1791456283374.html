Gollup has never belonged to the formal register. Its history is a history of speech rather than of literature: it comes out of the unrecorded, expressive vocabulary of eating and drinking, where sound symbolism and bodily imitation do most of the work. That origin explains both its shape and its restricted survival. It is a word that was born in the mouth and has stayed close to it.

An imitative core. The gulp family to which gollup belongs is dominated by a recurring g-l-p cluster: Middle Dutch and West Flemish gulpen “to gush, pour forth, swallow,” German Low German gulpen “to gush out, belch,” West Frisian gjalpe “to gush, spurt,” dialectal Swedish glapa “to gulp down,” and Old English galpettan “to gulp down, devour.” The same consonants keep returning because they imitate the same event: a wet, throaty, sudden intake, with the tongue closing off the flow at the end. This is classic sound-symbolic behaviour, and it means that the word’s phonetic history cannot be separated from its meaning.

From gulp to gollop. Middle English gulpen is recorded in the late fourteenth century; its closeness to Middle Dutch and West Flemish gulpen points to contact, though the direction of influence is uncertain. By the sixteenth century the shortened gulp is the standard English form, and it is from this stock that the dialectal gollop develops. The change is small but expressive: the vowel is lowered and backed, and a syllabic second element is added, so that the word acquires a rounder, more leisurely, more bodily shape. Gollop is attested in Scots and in English regional dialects, and Collins describes it simply as a dialect variant of gulp. The spelling gollup is a further dialect notation of the same pronunciation, not a separate etymology.

Scots and northern evidence. The Dictionary of the Scots Language is the richest record of the word. It heads the entry at gollop, notes “also gollup,” and defines the verb as “to swallow hastily or greedily, to gulp,” marked as general Scots and also current in colloquial or dialect English. Its citations are vivid and date the word firmly in modern speech: a 1930 Angus source describes a man who “golloped Chesterton” (that is, devoured his books); a 1934 Banffshire poem tells of a hungry heron that “nabbit th’ puddock and gollup’t him up”; and a 1946 Scottish novel describes a mule “breathing great gollops of air.” The noun is likewise glossed as “a hasty or greedy gulp.”

Slang dictionaries and comic literature. The word also surfaces in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century slang collections, where gollop is glossed “to swallow greedily; to gulp” and grouped with wolf and gobble. This is the register in which P. G. Wodehouse uses it: a tankard of home-brewed beer, a first rapturous gollup, a narrator who sounds pleasantly astonished by his own appetite. In such comic prose the word does delicate social work. It lets an upper-class narrator borrow the hearty, bodily language of the farmyard and the tavern, and the mild incongruity is the joke.

Pressures and shifts.

  • Phonetic: the drift from gulpen to gulp to gollop/gollup shows vowel lowering and expressive lengthening, typical of imitative verbs that resist standard spelling.
  • Semantic: the sense narrows from the general notion of gushing or pouring to the specifically human act of swallowing, and then broadens again to the amount swallowed.
  • Social: the word’s migration from everyday regional speech into literary dialogue marks it as a class and place marker, signalling rusticity or heartiness.
  • Stylistic: as standard English became more regulated, gollup was pushed to the margins, where it acquired its present flavour of affectionate archaism.

Modern trajectory. In present-day English the word is rare in edited prose and absent from technical and official language. It survives in regional speech, in Scottish writing, in dialect glossaries, and as a deliberate choice in humorous or historical narrative. Its connotation has remained remarkably stable: warm, physical, slightly old-fashioned, and faintly comic. To call a drink a gollup is to invoke a world of tankards and farmhouse tables rather than cocktail bars.

Why the word matters. Gollup is a small case study in how English keeps two parallel vocabularies for the same act: a neutral, standard one (swallow, drink) and an expressive, regional one (gollup, swig, guzzle). The second is where the language stores its attitudes — its relish, its mockery, its sense of bodily pleasure — and words like this survive precisely because those attitudes still need a name.
