EMBERS is the plural noun (a singular ember exists but is rare) for the glowing remains of a fire — the small pieces of coal or wood that continue to smoulder, radiating a dull red or orange heat, after the open flames have died down. The word names a distinct stage in a fire's life: the point at which flame gives way to sustained, even heat, when the fuel no longer leaps and flares but glows quietly within a crust of ash. Embers are eminently practical — they will cook food, dry clothes, and keep a camp or hearth warm through the night — and they are also eminently hopeful, since a live ember can be blown back into flame; which is why the word has long served as a metaphor for anything smouldering beneath the surface: resentment, love, revolt, or memory that still glows when the visible flame is gone.

Polysemy and modern usage:

  • Physical sense (the core sense): the glowing, smouldering residue of a fire — the small pieces of coal or wood that continue to radiate heat after the flames have sunk; almost always plural in ordinary use, since embers exist as a scattered bed of glowing fragments.
  • Culinary and domestic sense: embers as a cooking medium — to toast, roast, or bake in or over the embers, whose steady radiant heat cooks without the scorching of open flame; historically the embers of the hearth were banked overnight and raked out next morning to carry fire from one day to the next.
  • Figurative and emotional sense: anything that continues to exist or smoulder beneath the surface when the visible, public flame has died — 'the embers of resentment', 'the embers of a romance', 'the embers of revolt', 'the last embers of hope'; the metaphor stresses latent, banked feeling that can be rekindled by a breath of attention.
  • Literary and poetic sense: the ember as the image of what outlives the blaze — memory, love, anger, a cause, or a life — the quiet persistence of warmth after the conflagration; the word is a favourite of poets for its combination of heat and hush, of danger and tenderness.
  • Technical and scientific extension: by analogy, the glowing remains or residual activity of any burning process — embers of a volcano, embers of a dying star, embers of a pandemic — any smouldering residue that might flare again.

Historical and semantic bridge: the word's history is remarkably stable in its core physical sense — the glowing remains of a dying fire have been called embers for over a thousand years — but its figurative reach has grown steadily. The bridge is the ember's essential quality of latency: an ember is neither full flame nor dead ash, but a state in between, holding heat and potential. Anything that persists unseen beneath the surface, whether hope, anger, love, or memory, could therefore be called an ember; and because a live ember can be blown back into flame, the metaphor is always tinged with the possibility of renewal. The plural-only habit of the word reinforces this: embers are not one thing but a dispersed, glowing bed of possibilities.

In the literary context of this card (from Chapter 2, 'Roast Mutton', of The Hobbit), the word appears in the aftermath of the troll episode, when the danger is over and the company sits down to the spoils of the encounter: 'Now they had bread and cheese, and plenty of ale, and bacon to toast in the embers of the fire.' The scene is one of relief and homely comfort — the fire that the trolls built to roast their captives now serves their intended victims as a cooking hearth, and the steady, ash-covered heat of the embers is exactly right for toasting bacon, which would scorch and blacken over open flame. For Bilbo, fresh from the terrors of the dark forest, the embers mean warmth, food, and safety: the fire is tamed, the monsters are stone, and the ancient symbol of the hearth glows quietly in the night while Gandalf puffs at his pipe and the dwarves sharpen their new swords.

Related terms and register: ashes are the powdery, dead residue of complete combustion; cinders are partly burned, hard, brittle fragments no longer giving useful heat; coals are pieces of fuel in the act of burning, whether blazing or merely glowing. Embers specifically names the glowing stage between flame and ash, and carries a warmth of connotation that the others lack — the ember is alive, banked, and rekindlable. In register the word is neither formal nor slang: it belongs equally to the hearth, to poetry, and to the metaphor of latent feeling.
