- 1630s to 1650s - René Descartes publishes *Discours de la méthode* (1637) and *Meditationes* (1641), hailed as a revolutionary intellectual titan whose mathematical geometry and mechanical physics challenged the scholastic hegemony of European universities.
- 1660s to 1700 - Faces severe theological and ecclesiastical backlash; his philosophical works are placed on the Catholic Church's *Index Librorum Prohibitorum* in 1663, even as Cartesian physics and analytic geometry rapidly conquer university faculties across the Netherlands, England, and France.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Celebrated by Voltaire, D'Alembert, and the French Encyclopedists as the heroic liberator of human reason who overthrew medieval superstition, while his speculative physics (vortices) is superseded by Newtonian gravitation.
- 19th Century - Canonized as the supreme symbol of the French national intellect—*l'esprit cartésien*—characterized by crystalline logical clarity, architectural rigor, and methodical doubt, while German Idealists reexamine his *Cogito* as the dawn of subjective self-consciousness.
- 20th Century to Present - Reevaluated across modern intellectual disciplines: in phenomenology (Husserl's *Cartesian Meditations*), linguistics (Chomsky's *Cartesian Linguistics*), and neurobiology/philosophy of mind (Antonio Damasio's *Descartes' Error*), serving as the foundational touchstone against which modern non-dualist theories of embodied mind are framed.
