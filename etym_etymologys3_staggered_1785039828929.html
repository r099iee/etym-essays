**The tottering course of STAGGERED: from a shove to a schedule** The story of *staggered* begins deep in the Germanic north, and it begins, fittingly, with the idea of being pushed off balance. Reconstructing backward, scholars point to a Proto-Germanic verb *stakaną meaning "to thrust or push," itself traced (more tentatively) to a Proto-Indo-European core *steg- / *stak- glossed as "to be stiff, to stand firm" — the same family that gives English *stake* and *stack*, the mental image being a rigid pole thrust into the ground. It is worth stressing how much of this is reconstruction: the OED describes English *stagger* as "probably Scandinavian" in origin and marks the ultimate etymology as uncertain, so the PIE step is best treated as the standard proposed ancestor, not an assured fact. **The Scandinavian frequentative** - Old Norse built its unsteady-walk word on a verb staka "to push, cause to stumble," and then formed the frequentative stakra "to push, shove, totter, stagger." - A frequentative marks repeated, iterative action — the suffix -er- (also in wander, flicker, glimmer) turns a single push into a series of pushes, and it is precisely that series of off-balance thrusts that produces a wobbling, reeling walk. - The image is tactile and physical: to keep pushing someone until they totter and reel. That is the exact physical core of the English word. **The borrowing into English: sound changes in the Danelaw** - When Scandinavian speech met English in the Danelaw and in later medieval contacts, Old Norse stakra was borrowed into Middle English as stakeren, attested from the early 14th century, meaning "to totter, reel" — gradually displacing older native terms for swaying. - By the mid-15th century the spelling had altered to stagger, with the voiceless -k- of stakeren voiced to -g- and a reshuffling of the consonant sequence; philologists describe this as an obscure dialectal alteration, part of the same unstable handling of Scandinavian loans that produced other irregular English forms. - The borrowed word kept its physical sense entire: to stagger was to reel, to lose one's footing, to move as if about to fall. **The great expansion: from body to mind to calendar** The modern richness of *staggered* is the result of a series of transfers, each taking the same image of lost alignment and moving it to a new domain: - 1550s — the transitive sense: to stagger a person is to make their mental footing give way, to shock or bewilder. The King James Bible (1611) gives the classic literary use, "he staggered not at the promise of God" (Romans 4:20), where the physical word stands for unshaken faith — its precise opposite in literal terms, which makes the metaphor so strong. - c. 1600 — a noun stagger for an unsteady walk or a state of astonishment. - 1570s — the staggers, a reeling veterinary disease of horses (later of sheep and other animals), and by extension a slang term for giddiness; the plant-name staggerwort records folk belief that the herb caused or cured the disease. - 1856 — the scheduling sense: to stagger arrangements so that they do not coincide — staggered payments, staggered hours, staggered elections — where rows or duties are set deliberately "out of step" with each other, like the alternating feet of a drunken walk. - 19th–20th centuries — military and engineering extensions: staggered ranks in formation, staggered joints in brickwork, and the stagger of a biplane's wings (the fore-and-aft offset between upper and lower surfaces, named precisely for the stepped, out-of-line look). **Semantic drift and register** - The literal sense has never weakened: English still uses staggered for the unsteady motion of the exhausted, the injured, the drunk, and the terrified. - The figurative sense (staggering = astonishing) is now so common that its physical origin is invisible to most speakers — "staggering wealth" does not call up a man reeling, yet that is exactly where it came from. - The technical scheduling sense runs in the opposite register: orderly, planned, and precise, yet it borrows its very name from the most disorderly motion English has — proof that metaphor can carry a word from chaos to the calendar. - In The Hobbit, however, staggered returns the word to its oldest physical sense: Bombur's heavy, sweat-soaked body reeling onward through the dark, "as if about to fall" at every step. **Literary attestations across time** - Early 14th century — stakeren first appears in Middle English texts as a word of toppling and reeling. - 1611 — the King James Bible immortalizes the figurative sense in Romans. - 1570s–1600s — the staggers enters veterinary and colloquial record; the noun stagger follows. - 1856 — the scheduling sense is attested as the industrial world began to stagger working hours. - 1937 — Tolkien's The Hobbit gives the physical sense its most vivid modern outing, and the word in Bombur's story does what it has always done: puts a failing body before the reader's eyes, step by swaying step.
