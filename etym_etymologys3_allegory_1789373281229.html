The etymological evolution of allegory represents one of the most illustrious intellectual genealogies in Western philology, moving from the open civic assemblies of classical Athens, through Hellenistic literary scholarship and early Christian hermeneutics, across medieval French romance and scholasticism, into the heart of modern narrative literature and deconstructive critical philosophy.

  • Proto-Indo-European Roots and Greek Compounding: The word is a classic morphological compound built from two primary Greek constituents. The first element is the adjective ἄλλος (állos, "other, another, different"), which derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂el- (extended as *h₂elyos), meaning "other, beyond, else." This ancient root is exceptionally prolific across Indo-European, producing Latin alius ("other"), alter ("the other"), and aliēnus ("belonging to another," English alien), Old High German eli-lenti ("in a foreign land," modern German Elend), and Old English elles (English else). The second element is the verb ἀγορεύειν (agoreúein), meaning "to speak in public, harangue, address an assembly, or proclaim." This verb is a denominative derivative of ἀγορά (agorá, "a gathering of people, a public assembly, town meeting, or marketplace"), which traces to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₂ger-, meaning "to gather, assemble, collect" (cognate with Greek ageirein "to gather," and the root of English gregarious and congregate via Latin). Thus, the compound nominal formation ἀλληγορία (allēgoría) literally denoted "speaking other than what is said openly in the public assembly"—that is, saying one thing publicly while intending another hidden, private, or deeper meaning.
  • Hellenistic Rhetoric and the Hermeneutic Turn: In archaic and classical Greece, the earlier rhetorical term for veiled or allegorical speech was often hyponoia (ὑπόνοια, literally "under-thought" or "underlying meaning"). The term allēgoría began gaining prominence in the Hellenistic period (c. 1st century BCE) in the writings of rhetorical theorists like Philodemus and grammatical commentators. Hellenistic Stoic philosophers and Alexandrian scholars faced a major cultural challenge: how to reconcile the sacred, culturally foundational epics of Homer and Hesiod—replete with deities committing adultery, deceit, jealousy, and war—with advanced philosophical theology. By treating Homeric myths as allegories, philosophers reinterpreted Hera as the element of air, Zeus as cosmic fire, and Athena as divine wisdom, transforming scandalous mythic narratives into profound cosmological and ethical treatises.
  • Classical Latin Adoption (allegoria): In the late Roman Republic and early Empire, Roman rhetoricians incorporated the Greek concept directly into Latin as allegoria. In Book VIII of the Institutio Oratoria, the Roman rhetorician Quintilian provided the classic classical definition: "Allegoria, quam inversionem interpretantur, aut aliud verbis aliud sensu ostendit, aut etiam interim contrarium" ("Allegory, which is translated as inversion, either shows one thing in words and another in sense, or sometimes even the complete opposite"). Cicero and Quintilian described allegory as an extended, continuous metaphor (continua metaphora). Roman poets like Horace embraced allegory in famous odes, such as portraying the Roman state during civil war as a battered ship tossed on tempestuous seas (Ode 1.14).
  • Patristic Hermeneutics and Medieval Fourfold Exegesis: The decisive conceptual expansion of allegory occurred when early Jewish and Christian scholars adapted Alexandrian hermeneutics to holy scripture. Philo of Alexandria applied allegorical reading to the Torah; subsequently, Christian theologians such as Origen of Alexandria, Saint Jerome, and Saint Augustine formalized allegorical reading as the core of Christian theology. This culminated in the celebrated medieval doctrine of the Quadriga—the four senses of scripture:
    • Sensus litteralis / historicus: The historical facts and literal narrative.
    • Sensus allegoricus / typologicus: The prophetic, Christological, or ecclesiastical meaning (what one must believe).
    • Sensus tropologicus / moralis: The ethical instruction for the soul (what one must do).
    • Sensus anagogicus / eschatologicus: The heavenly, eternal mystery (what one may hope for).
    This fourfold framework ensured that throughout the Middle Ages, allegory was not regarded merely as an artificial literary trope, but as the supreme divine key to deciphering the metaphysical architecture of the cosmos and the Word of God.
  • Vernacular French and English Transmission: From Late Latin, the word passed into Old French as allegorie during the 12th and 13th centuries, flourishing in vernacular narrative poetry such as Guillaume de Lorris and Jean de Meun's monumental Roman de la Rose (an elaborate allegorical dream-vision of courtly love). The word entered Middle English in the late 14th century (first recorded in John Wycliffe's Bible translation c. 1382, rendering Galatians 4:24: "Whiche thingis ben seid bi allegorie"). Geoffrey Chaucer, William Langland (Piers Plowman), and later Renaissance masters like Edmund Spenser (The Faerie Queene, 1590) elevated English allegory into an exquisite artistic medium synthesizing classical mythology, Christian morality, and national dynastic history.
  • Modern Deconstruction and Walter Benjamin: In the 20th century, German cultural philosopher Walter Benjamin radically inverted the traditional aesthetic evaluation of allegory in his 1928 treatise The Origin of German Tragic Drama (*Ursprung des deutschen Trauerspiels*). Benjamin rescued allegory from Romantic denigration (which had favored the organic, timeless symbol), asserting that allegory is the supreme art form of historical crisis, melancholy, and transience. In allegory, every object points outside itself to the inevitable ruins of history, revealing the inescapable mortality of human civilization. This insight laid the cornerstone for post-structuralist theorists like Paul de Man, who demonstrated that all literary language is inherently allegorical, perpetually deferring ultimate presence and literal closure.
