amiable (adjective) — having or displaying a friendly, pleasant, and good-natured manner; likable, agreeable, and easy to be with. Amiable describes a person's temperament or disposition rather than a single act: an amiable person is warm, kindly disposed, and sociable by nature, someone others find pleasant to be around. It is a largely favourable word — in modern English it carries little irony — and it belongs to a somewhat formal or literary register, though it remains perfectly natural in everyday speech (“He's an amiable man”). Because it describes a settled quality of character, it is usually applied to people (“an amiable host”, “an amiable colleague”), though by extension it can describe a person's manner, smile, or expression (“an amiable smile”, “an amiable tone”).

Grammatical profile and usage
  • Function: attributive and predicative adjective (“an amiable woman”; “she was amiable”).
  • Register: formal-to-literary; slightly old-fashioned in tone, warmer and more personal than the bureaucratic “friendly”.
  • Collocations: amiable manner, amiable disposition, amiable character, amiable man/woman, amiable smile, amiable host, amiable companion.
  • Derived forms: amiability (noun, “good nature”), amiablely (adverb, rare), amiableness (noun, rare).
  • Negation: unamiable (rare) — lacking in good nature.
Polysemy — senses and shifts
  • Primary sense (17th c.–present): having a friendly, good-natured manner; agreeable and pleasant. This is the dominant current meaning: “Mr. Bingley … had a pleasant countenance and easy, unaffected manners … Such amiable qualities must speak for themselves” (Austen).
  • Older sense (14th–16th c.), “lovable, worthy of love”: borrowed from French with the meaning “worthy of love, delightful in itself.” This sense faded from English by the 17th century but explains the word's Latin ancestry (amābilis “lovable”) and still glimmers beneath modern use — an amiable person is, etymologically, one easy to love.
  • Extended uses (18th c.–present): applied to a person's manner, smile, or features (“an amiable smile”), and occasionally, by poetic extension, to things that are pleasing in character (“an amiable climate”).
  • Distinction from amicable: the classic trap. Amicable (also from Latin amicus “friend”) applies to relations, agreements, and settlements between people or groups — “an amicable divorce”, “an amicable resolution” — and never to a person's temperament. Amiable describes the person themselves.
Connotation
Amiable conveys warmth, sweetness, and genuine good nature. It is gentler than genial (which radiates cheerful energy) and more inward than sociable (which loves company). An amiable person is pleasant to be with not because they perform friendliness but because their disposition is naturally warm. The word also carries a slight suggestion of mildness or agreeableness that stops short of forcefulness — an amiable man may be soft-spoken and accommodating.

Semantic boundaries against near-synonyms
  • friendly: similar because both describe warmth toward others; differs because friendly is the broad everyday word for behaviour, while amiable denotes a settled sweet temperament.
  • affable: similar because both describe approachability; differs because affable stresses warm conversational ease and readiness to be spoken to, while amiable emphasises overall good nature.
  • genial: similar because both suggest pleasant warmth; differs because genial implies energetic, radiating cheerfulness, while amiable is quieter.
  • amicable: similar in root and in basic friendliness; differs because amicable applies to relations between parties, not to a person's temperament.
  • good-natured: similar because both describe an easy temper; differs because good-natured stresses tolerance and lack of ill-will, amiable the active warmth that draws people in.
  • pleasant: similar because both describe an agreeable person; differs because pleasant is much broader (weather, taste, sound) and less about character.
  • kind: similar because both are favourable character traits; differs because kind centres on active benevolence and deeds, while amiable centres on manner and disposition.
  • sociable: similar because both describe being easy to be with; differs because sociable means liking company and seeking it, while amiable is about disposition.
  • agreeable: similar because both mean pleasant to others; differs because agreeable suggests readiness to please and conform, while amiable implies genuine warmth.
  • sweet-tempered: similar because both describe consistent good humour; differs because sweet-tempered is more informal and implies mildness under provocation.
  • lovable: similar because both derive from Latin amāre “to love”; differs because lovable names the feeling others have, while amiable names the person's own friendly manner.
  • charming: similar because both describe people who please; differs because charming implies deliberate magnetic attraction, while amiable is plain good nature.
Historical bridge
Amiable entered Middle English around 1300 from Old French amiable “friendly, kind”, itself from Late Latin amicābilis “friendly” (Latin amicus “friend”, from amāre “to love”), but the Old French form was drawn toward amable “lovable” (from Latin amābilis), so the two meanings — “lovable” and “friendly” — fused in the single word. In English the “lovable” sense dominated for two centuries before giving way to the “friendly, good-natured” sense that prevails today. This fusion explains both the word's warmth and its confusion with amicable: all these words are children of the same Latin verb for love.

Literary attestations
“Such amiable qualities must speak for themselves” (Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, 1813), where Bingley's easy good nature is the sunny counterpart of Darcy's pride. The word was a staple of 18th–19th-century polite vocabulary and continues to appear in literary and formal prose as a settled, admiring verdict on character.
