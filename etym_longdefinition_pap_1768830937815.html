PAP is a small word with an enormous, strangely contradictory career. It is at once a nursery food, a medical dietetic, an anatomical name for the breast, and — with delicious irony — one of the sharpest words in English for intellectual blandness. In the Vance passage, Turjan feeds his newly made homunculus "a bowl of pap" with a long-handled spoon: the scene is the apotheosis of the word's primary sense, and the creature's helpless infancy is exactly what pap has always meant. Below is the full exegesis of its senses. SECTION 1 — THE LITERAL NOUN: SOFT INFANT FOOD In its primary culinary sense, pap is soft, semi-liquid, easily digested food prepared for infants, young children, and the toothless or sick. It was made by soaking or boiling bread, flour, meal, or rusks in milk or water, often sweetened and flavoured. Before the invention of commercial baby foods, pap was the universal weaning diet of Europe, and it remained in ordinary use into the nineteenth and even twentieth centuries.
  • Preparation: breadcrumbs or wheat flour boiled in milk or water to a gruel-like consistency, sometimes enriched with sugar, honey, butter, or a drop of wine; served warm from a special vessel, the "pap-boat" or "pap-spoon."
  • Medical dietetics: through the 19th century pap was prescribed for invalids, convalescents, and the dyspeptic; a thin pap was the first food taken after a fast. The word thus carried a wholly nourishing, gentle, motherly connotation.
  • Nursery register: the word is a reduplicated nursery formation, of the baby-talk family (Latin pappa), and its very phonetics — two bilabial stops framing a low vowel, /pæp/ — mimic the smacking of a feeding mouth.
SECTION 2 — ANATOMICAL SENSE: THE BREAST A second, formally distinct pap names the breast or nipple: "the paps of a woman" is the archaic term for the breasts. This word is probably of separate origin (likely Scandinavian or a further nursery word), though the two have long been homophones and are historically entangled because the nipple is the source of infant food.
  • Literary use: the anatomical paps appears in older poetry and in the King James Bible ("thy two breasts... are like two young roes that are twins," Song of Solomon), where the feeding image keeps the two words in natural union.
  • Bridge to pap: the association of breast and soft food reinforces the word's cluster of meanings around nursing, infancy, and dependence.
SECTION 3 — THE FIGURATIVE SENSE: BLAND, TRIVIAL, INTELLECTUALLY INFANTILE By far the most important sense in modern English is the figurative one: pap means material that is soft, undemanding, and intellectually infantile — trivial journalism, sentimental entertainment, undemanding ideas "spoon-fed" to a passive public. The famous phrase "pap for the masses" transfers the image of a mother spooning soft food into a baby's mouth to the media feeding the public pabulum.
  • Semantic bridge: the shift runs through the root idea of softness and undemandingness. Pap is easy to swallow and requires no chewing — so pap is easy to believe and requires no thinking. What was nourishing for infants becomes stunting for adults.
  • Register: the figurative use is always contemptuous — to call writing "pap" is to dismiss it as comfort-food for the mind, sentimental, low-grade, and unchallenging.
  • Related forms: "pablum" (a trademarked infant cereal, generalized to any bland intellectual fare) is a Latinized variant of the same word, and the adjective "pappy" means soft, pulpy, or feebly sentimental.
SECTION 4 — TECHNICAL AND JARGON SENSES Beyond the food and the metaphor, pap appears in a scatter of technical corners. In dialect and older English, a pap could be any soft pulpy mass. In the visual arts of the past, a "pap-dish" was the dish of infant feeding. In some dialects, to "pap" is to feed with a pap-bottle — and by extension to treat someone with excessive, coddling indulgence. Each of these uses, old or technical, circles the same central image: something soft, spoon-fed, and dependent.
