- 12th to 13th Century - Originates in Old French as *recerche*, denoting an earnest seeking-out, the tracking of quarry in hunting, or an official judicial examination of witnesses.
- 16th to 17th Century - Elevated into philosophical and epistemological discourse during the French Classical Age; utilized by René Descartes and Nicolas Malebranche (*De la recherche de la vérité*, 1674) to signify the systematic, rational pursuit of metaphysical and scientific truth.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Broadens across European intellectual culture to encompass academic scholarly inquiry; simultaneously, the participial form is borrowed into Anglophone connoisseurship and aristocratic fashion (*recherché*) to denote rare delicacies, exquisite taste, and fastidious elegance.
- 19th Century - Solidified across civil administration, criminal jurisprudence (*mandat de recherche*), and institutional laboratory science; in Victorian English letters, the adjective takes on a subtle satirical shade, targeting affected, far-fetched, or precious literary conceits.
- 1913 to 1927 - Marcel Proust publishes *À la recherche du temps perdu*, indelibly transforming *recherche* into the preeminent international symbol of voluntary and involuntary memory, phenomenological recovery, and modernist aesthetic salvation.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Continues as the foundational French term for cutting-edge scientific research (*la recherche*), industrial innovation, and computational search engines (*moteurs de recherche*), while retaining its supreme status in world literature as the definitive signifier of the existential quest.
