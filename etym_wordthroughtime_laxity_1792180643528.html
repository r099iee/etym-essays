- 15th c. (Late Middle English) — Borrowed from Old French laxité and Latin laxitās primarily in physical, spatial, and physiological contexts, describing the spaciousness of vessels or loose bowel movements (medical laxative connection).
- 16th–17th c. (Early Modern English) — Expanded rapidly into theological, ethical, and judicial registers to denounce spiritual sloppiness, weak church governance, and the failure of magistrates to enforce penal codes.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment & Moral Philosophy) — Deployed extensively in political theory and casuistic debates to contrast civilized self-restraint with decadent social decline or philosophical skepticism.
- 19th c. (Victorian Era) — Consolidated across two divergent fronts: specialized clinical pathology (anatomical looseness of ligaments, tissue relaxation) and imperial anthropology/sociology (characterizing "primitive" sexual freedom and customary matrimonial looseness, as in Durant).
- 20th–21st c. — Dominated by orthopedic terminology (joint hypermobility, ligamentous laxity) and financial/administrative discourse (regulatory laxity, border laxity, corporate oversight failures).
