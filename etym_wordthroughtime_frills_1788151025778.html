- [1590s] - 'frill' appears in English as a fluted or ruffled edging on a garment; the origin is obscure, the usage purely fashionable - [perception: showy dress detail]
- [17th c.] - Frills multiply on collars, cuffs, and shirtfronts; the word also names a curl or crimp in hair or paper - [perception: ornament of the genteel, fussy finery]
- [18th–19th c.] - Victorian and Edwardian dress makes the frill a marker of feminine delicacy and domestic plenty; 'frills and furbelows' becomes the stock phrase for fussy trimming - [perception: pretty, slightly excessive]
- [late 19th c.] - The figurative leap: 'frills' = superfluous additions, needless ornament in prose, oratory, or affairs; plainness is praised as 'without frills' - [perception: the non-essential, dispensable]
- [20th c.] - 'No-frills' hardens into a commercial idiom for budget goods and services, from airlines and supermarkets to lodgings - [perception: cheap, functional, no ceremony]
- [technical branchings] - The frilled lizard (named 1825) and photographic 'frilling' of emulsion edges send the same flared-edge image into natural history and darkroom jargon - [perception: specialist terms spun off the same picture]
