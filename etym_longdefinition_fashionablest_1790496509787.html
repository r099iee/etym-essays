fashionablest in the source sentence — Ruskin describing his Croydon aunt's little house still standing, the fashionablest in Market Street, having actually two windows over the shop, in the second story — is the superlative of fashionable, meaning the most stylish, most genteel, most up-to-date house in that modest street. The context is gently ironic and deeply affectionate: a baker's shop-house with two upstairs windows is hardly fashionable by London standards, but in Market Street, Croydon, in the 1820s, it is the height of elegance — the aunt's pride, the nephew's remembered kingdom of shop, bakehouse, spring, and Towzer the dog. Ruskin keeps the childish superlative (-est rather than most fashionable) to preserve a boy's scale of grandeur: what is first in Market Street is first in the world.

In general language fashionable means conforming to the prevailing custom in dress, manners, speech, or taste at a given time and place — a fashionable dress, fashionable address, fashionable quarter, fashionable writer — and by extension frequented or worn by elegant society: a fashionable hotel, fashionable hours, fashionable church. The superlative fashionablest is today rare and marked: standard modern English prefers analytic most fashionable for a four-syllable base, reserving fashionablest for playful, archaic, childlike, or emphatic effect. Its very oddness is its meaning: to write fashionablest is to wink at older prose or to speak as a child speaks.

  • Core stylish sense: in accordance with current fashion (source sense): up-to-date in cut, decoration, or appointment; e.g. the fashionablest house in the street, the fashionablest bonnet in church, a fashionablest watering-place. Implies comparison within a small field: Market Street has few houses, so two windows suffice for supremacy. The bridge from manner to merit is social consensus: what the best people approve becomes the best.
  • Society sense: belonging to or frequented by fashionable people: the fashionable world, fashionable society, a fashionable audience, fashionable Bath, fashionable Mayfair. Here fashionable no longer describes the thing's look but its company: a dull room is fashionable if duchesses fill it. Nineteenth-century novels capitalise it as the Fashionable World, a power with laws, gazettes, and excommunications.
  • Temporal sense: current, in vogue, all the rage: a fashionable doctrine, fashionable charity, fashionable melancholy, it is fashionable to despise.... Ideas, griefs, and politics all take fashion: Byron makes melancholy fashionable, Evangelicalism makes Sunday schools fashionable, Ruskin himself makes Gothic fashionable. The sense is often censorious — fashionable implies transient, adopted for show.
  • Noun fashion behind it: prevailing manner or custom: the fashion of the time, after a fashion (in a rough way), in fashion and out of fashion, fast fashion, high fashion (haute couture), men's fashion week. From shape to habit to industry: fashion names first how a thing is made, then how people behave, then the business of clothes. Modern fashion industry, fashion victim, fashionista, fashion-forward belong to this commercial superstructure.
  • Verb to fashion: to make, shape, form: to fashion a tool from flint, to fashion a government, fashioned by adversity, a well-fashioned limb. This is the oldest English sense, still literary: God fashions man from clay, the smith fashions iron. Fashionable in this light means well-shaped — fit to be shown.
  • Morphology: why fashionablest jars: fashion (noun) + -able (fit for) + -est (most). Fashionable (first attested c. 1600) already contains two suffixes; adding a third (-est) makes a triple-suffixed giant that eighteenth-century grammarians condemned as harsh. Most fashionable won in polite prose by 1800, leaving fashionablest to children, provincials, humorists, and Ruskin remembering childhood. Using it now signals deliberate archaism or nursery voice.
  • Collocation and irony: fashionable collocates with dress, quarter, hotel, restaurant, opinion, nonsense. Fashionablest collocates with little superiorities lovingly recalled — houses, bonnets, streets, pews. Ruskin's sentence teaches the usage: praise the best in a small street as warmly as the best in London, and let the superlative confess both pride and disproportion.

Register: fashionable is neutral-standard; fashionablest is conspicuously quaint. In an examination or business report write most fashionable; in memoir, comic fiction, or pastiche, fashionablest recovers a period flavour — Fielding, Fanny Burney, and early Ruskin all use it unselfconsciously. Pronunciation follows the base /ˈfæʃnəbəl/ with added /əst/: /ˈfæʃnəbləst/, the middle vowels reduced almost to nothing, the whole word tripping like a child running upstairs to count windows.

The polysemy resolves into one history of making: what is well-fashioned (shaped) becomes the fashion (the approved shape), what is the fashion becomes fashionable (wearing it), what is most fashionable in its street becomes, to loving eyes, the fashionablest — shaped, shown, and crowned. Two windows over a baker's shop suffice, if love does the counting.
