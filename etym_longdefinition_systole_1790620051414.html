Systole is, in its dominant modern sense, the phase of the cardiac cycle in which the heart muscle contracts, building pressure in the chambers and ejecting blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery; it is the active, squeezing half of the heartbeat whose partner is diastole, the relaxation and filling phase.

In clinical use the word appears most famously in blood pressure readings such as “120 over 80,” where the higher, first number is the systolic pressure — the peak arterial pressure produced during ventricular systole — while the lower number records diastolic pressure during relaxation. Physicians further divide the event into atrial systole and ventricular systole, and describe systolic murmurs, systolic failure, systolic function, and the systolic interval measured on echocardiography and phonocardiography.

The reason your context pairs “diastole and systole of reading itself” is that the medical pair has become a general literary metaphor for any rhythmic alternation of expansion and contraction: taking in and giving out, opening and closing, wandering and gathering, reading “through” in a linear flow and reading “at” in a fixed imagistic gaze. To call these the diastole and systole of reading is to say that reading, like a heart, lives by alternate dilation and contraction.

  • Physiology / cardiology (primary sense): contraction of heart muscle, especially the ventricles, expelling blood; the reasoning is direct inheritance from ancient Greek medicine, where systolē meant a drawing-together or contraction, applied by Galen to the heart’s beat and pulse.
  • Blood pressure and diagnostics: “systolic” as the maximum pressure during contraction; the semantic bridge is metonymy from the event to its measurable effect, so “systolic hypertension” means hypertension of the contraction-phase peak.
  • Generalized contraction / shortening: in older physiology, systole could describe contraction of any muscle or the retraction of a body part; the bridge is analogy from heart to any contractile tissue.
  • Prosody / classical metrics (secondary, archaic): the shortening of a syllable naturally long, or a foot shortened; historically the same Greek word was used by grammarians for “drawing together” a long vowel into a short one, a metaphorical transfer from bodily contraction to syllabic compression.
  • Figurative rhythm / pulsation: any cyclic tightening after loosening — breath, attention, crowds, markets, prose rhythm; the bridge is the heartbeat as the prototype of all pulsing systems, so writers borrow systole whenever they need the contraction pole of a diastole–systole pair.

Register and usage notes: systole is a formal, technical noun, almost always paired with or contrasted against diastole; outside medicine it is self-consciously learned and metaphorical. It is countable (“several systoles”), though clinicians more often use the adjective systolic. Do not confuse it with a “beat” as a whole: one heartbeat equals one systole plus one diastole. Pronunciation in RP places stress on the first syllable with a long final vowel, and the plural is systoles.
