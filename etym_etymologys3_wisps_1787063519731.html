The history of wisps begins in the 14th century with the singular wisp, recorded in Middle English around 1300. Its immediate source is Old Norse visp, a small bundle or handful of hay, straw, or similar material. English and Norse were in constant contact from the Viking Age onward, and Norse farm vocabulary entered English especially through the northern and eastern dialects, which is exactly where the earliest wisp records cluster. The Middle English spelling oscillates between wisp and wips, a metathesis of the two final consonants that persisted for centuries; the modern standard wisp eventually won out.

In its first two centuries of English life the word is purely practical. A wisp of straw might wipe a bench, feed a horse, thatch a roof, light a fire, or — bound tight — serve as a small torch. In the 16th century this torch sense feeds the remarkable compound will-o'-the-wisp: the name of the flickering phosphorescent light seen over marshes (the ignis fatuus of Latin tradition), imagined as a demon or fairy named “Will” carrying a wisp of burning straw to lure night travellers into the bog. The compound survives today as the standard metaphor for anything delusive, seductive, and ultimately unattainable.

The decisive semantic shift occurs in the 19th century. The Industrial Revolution made smoke and steam ubiquitous: chimneys, foundries, and locomotives all produced thin, curling trails of vapour, and the language reached for wisp to name them. The first recorded uses of “wisp of smoke” and “wisp of steam” date from the mid-19th century, and the word simultaneously extends to hair (“wisps of hair”) and, in weather writing, to the feathery trails of cirrus cloud. It is during this period that the older “bundle of hay” sense begins to recede into dialectal and historical use, while the vapour sense takes over as the primary meaning — a reversal of the word's centre of gravity.

In the later 19th and 20th centuries the metaphorical sense flourishes. A “wisp of a smile”, a “wisp of hope”, a “wisp of a girl” all appear, transferring the word's physical lightness to emotions and to people. The derived adjective wispy (1850s) becomes a standard term in cloud classification and, by the 20th century, in descriptions of thin hair and delicate textures.

Modern use keeps all these strands alive. The plural wisps in the source context — wisps of vapour floating up from a sweating body — is squarely within the 19th-century atmospheric tradition: the word's connotation is ghostly, delicate, and faintly beautiful, its “bundle of hay” origin now invisible to most speakers. The deep etymology is uncertain; the link to Norse is secure, but the pre-Norse root is debated, with proposed connections to Germanic words for “twist” or “bend” remaining unproven. The word's career — from farm tool to fairy light to poetry of the industrial age — is a small case study in how material life reshapes vocabulary.
