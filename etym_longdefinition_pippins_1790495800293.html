pippins is the plural of pippin: properly, an apple raised from a pip (seed) rather than grafted, hence a distinct seedling variety of dessert apple, and loosely any choice eating-apple; figuratively, a person or thing of rare excellence, a beauty, a crackerjack. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita moralising his growth, as I grew wiser, the desire for sweet pippins instead of bitter ones, and Living Kings instead of dead ones, appeared to me rational as well as romantic; and gradually it has become the main purpose of my life to grow pippins — sweet apples and living kings are paired sacraments of life against bitterness and death. To grow pippins is to cultivate nourishing, living, fruitful order — orchards, institutions, souls — rather than dissecting dead crowns. The double blank (sweet pippins, to grow pippins) makes the word both fruit and vocation. The asterisk points to his lifelong horticultural and social project, from Brantwood orchards to St George’s Guild.

In ordinary pomology a pippin is any of the old English seedling apples: Ribston Pippin, Cox’s Orange Pippin, Golden Pippin, Newtown Pippin. They are dessert apples, eaten fresh rather than cooked or pressed for cider, prized for balanced sugar and acid, aromatic flesh and keeping quality. Gardeners contrast pippin (dessert) with codlin or codling (cooking) and crab (wild sour). A pip is the seed itself; a pippin is what the pip becomes when sown and selected — hence the proverbial uncertainty (sow a pip, get a crab or a pippin) and the triumph when a Cox or Ribston emerges. The tree is a small spreading standard, blossoming white-pink in May, fruiting September to October, stored through winter in lofts and cellars.

  • Core pomological sense: seedling dessert apple: e.g. a dish of golden pippins, Ribston pippin trees against the wall, the orchard was all pippins and no codlins. This is the dictionary head sense, countable. The semantic bridge from pip (seed, Old French pepin) is causal: the apple is named for how it is propagated — from seed, not graft — even after the variety is fixed by grafting. Adjectives divide sweet from bitter, dessert from cider, keeping from early.
  • General choice-fruit sense and American extension: in North America Newtown Pippin (also Albemarle Pippin) was the colonial export apple of Virginia and New York, praised by Franklin and Jefferson; pippin became generic for a fine eating-apple. Settlers carried pippin seeds west (Johnny Appleseed’s cargo was pips for pippins and cider). The bridge is prestige: the best keepers that survived a sea voyage deserved the seedling name.
  • Figurative excellent-person-or-thing sense: a beauty, a dandy, a humdinger: e.g. she’s a pippin, that shot was a pippin, a pippin of a sermon. Recorded from the early 19th century in sporting and theatrical slang, now chiefly arch or jocular American. The bridge is gustatory praise transferred to human and moral taste: as a sweet pippin delights the palate, so a fine girl, horse or joke delights its judge. Ruskin’s pairing of sweet pippins with Living Kings leans on exactly this moralised flavour — goodness tastes sweet.
  • Pineapple-pippin confusion and other transferred uses: pippin also names the barytone pip of the pipped call in cricket? No — that is pip. But orange-pippin, pine-pippin occur as fanciful apple names; pippin-face means pock-marked like an apple; pippin-hearted is stout-hearted. None is common, but all show the apple as template for roundness, ruddiness and wholesomeness, against crabbed, crabby, sour for the opposite temper.
  • Bitter-versus-sweet moral botany (Ruskin’s sense): Ruskin habitually moralises fruit: sweet pippins are cultivated, sunny, honest labour; bitter ones are neglected, blighted, acid — wild crabs, cider-sour, or diseased. To desire sweet pippins instead of bitter ones is to choose life-giving culture over corrosive criticism, building over pulling down. The same antithesis returns in Unto This Last and Fors Clavigera: garden versus wilderness, living king versus dead king, sweet versus bitter political economy.
  • Domain notes: horticulture keeps the technical sense alive in heritage orchards and apple societies; cookery distinguishes pippin (eat) from codlin (cook) and warden (bake); slang keeps the figurative sense in period fiction and Westerns. Modern supermarkets rarely label pippins, preferring Cox, Gala, Braeburn — all pippins by descent, seldom by name.
