The name Chittagong is an Anglicized spelling of Bengali Chattagram / Chattogram, but its ultimate origin is debated among historians, linguists, and local tradition. No single derivation commands universal assent, so any honest account must present the leading hypotheses and the colonial process that fixed the English form.

  • Leading hypothesis — Four villages: The most widely cited learned derivation traces the name to Sanskrit chatura (four) + grāma (village, settlement), yielding Prakrit Chaturgrama and Bengali Chattagram — the town of four villages or four settlements. This fits the common South Asian pattern of -gram place names and the early settlement morphology of river-mouth towns. Sound change from Sanskrit grāma to Bengali gram/gong involves regular cluster simplification and nasalization in eastern Indo-Aryan.
  • Competing hypothesis — Arakanese phrase: Arakanese (Rakhine) chronicles associate the area with the phrase tsit-ta-goung meaning there will be no war or to make war is not good, allegedly uttered by an Arakanese king marking a boundary. This would make the name Tibeto-Burman rather than Indo-Aryan in origin, reflecting the region's position between Bengal and Burma.
  • Folk and devotional hypotheses: Local traditions invoke chatta (parasol or umbrella, emblem of Buddhist stupas) + gram (village), or link the name to the Sufi saint Badr Shah's chati (lamp), lit on a hilltop. Mughal administrators renamed the conquered port Islamabad (Abode of Islam) in 1666, while Portuguese mariners called it Porto Grande (Great Port) — both overlays that never displaced the Bengali base name among locals.
  • Phonetic path to English: Bengali [ˈtʃɔtːoɡram] was heard by Portuguese as Chatigão / Xatigão, by Dutch and early English factors as Chatigan, Chittigong, and was standardized by the British East India Company after 1760 as Chittagong. English spelling replaced Bengali retroflex and geminate consonants with doubled tt, rendered final -gram as -gong under English nasal perception, and fixed stress on the first syllable: /ˈtʃɪtəɡɒŋ/.
  • Socio-cultural layering: Each regime reinscribed the toponym: Sultanate coinage, Mughal Islamabad, Portuguese Porto Grande, British Chittagong District with its Hill Tracts dependency, Pakistani Chittagong Division, and Bangladeshi Chattogram (official since 2018 to reflect Bengali pronunciation). The 1930 Armoury Raid gave the name anti-colonial resonance; the post-1971 container boom gave it commercial resonance.
  • The Hill-tribes usage: British gazetteers grouped diverse Tibeto-Burman peoples (Chakma, Marma, Tripuri, Bawm, etc.) as Chittagong Hill tribes because they were administered from Chittagong town. The source sentence preserves exactly this colonial classificatory habit. Modern ethnography and Bangladeshi administration use specific ethnonyms and the term Hill Tracts peoples.

Debated nuance: scholars explicitly disagree whether the Indo-Aryan four-villages etymology or the Arakanese no-war etymology is primary, and folk derivations (parasol-village, lamp-on-hill) lack documentary support but remain culturally alive. The only undisputed facts are the attested Bengali form Chattagram, its Mughal synonym Islamabad, its Portuguese synonym Porto Grande, and its British standardization as Chittagong. The 2018 official renaming to Chattogram does not change the historical English form encountered in older books.
