• Nordic: Similar because it covers the same northern European region and is often used interchangeably; differs because it is broader, including Finland and usually Iceland, and is the preferred term in official and academic contexts.
  • Norse: Similar because it refers to the people and language of Scandinavia, especially medieval Norway and Iceland; differs because it is historical and linguistic, not a modern regional adjective.
  • North Germanic: Similar because it is the precise linguistic label for the Scandinavian languages; differs because it is technical and excludes the geographical and cultural senses.
  • Viking: Similar because it evokes the Scandinavian world of the early Middle Ages; differs because it names raiders and explorers, not the region or its modern inhabitants.
  • Danish: Similar as a national adjective within Scandinavia; differs because it refers only to Denmark and its people or language.
  • Norwegian: Similar as a national adjective within Scandinavia; differs because it refers only to Norway.
  • Swedish: Similar as a national adjective within Scandinavia; differs because it refers only to Sweden.
  • Icelandic: Similar as a North Germanic language and culture; differs because Iceland is Nordic but not strictly Scandinavian, and the language is more archaic.
  • Fennoscandian: Similar as a geographical term for the northern peninsula; differs because it includes Finland and the Kola Peninsula and is geological rather than cultural.
  • Baltoscandian: Similar as a geological and geographical term for the Baltic–Scandinavian region; differs because it includes the Baltic states and is not used of people or languages.
