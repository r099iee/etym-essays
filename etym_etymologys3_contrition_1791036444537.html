Contrition begins at the mill. PIE *terh1-, to rub, turn, bore through by friction — source also of English throw, throe, trauma (wound by rubbing/boring), Greek teirein (to wear), Latin terere (to rub, thresh, wear out: terere tempus, to wear away time) — is extended by Latin prefix con- (together, utterly) into conterere: to grind together, crush to powder, bruise, destroy by rubbing. Plautus uses it for squandering money (grinding it away); Caesar for crushing the enemy; agricultural writers for grinding corn. Its past participle contritus means ground down, bruised, worn — a millstone adjective. Phonologically the cluster is regular: con- + terere → conterere → contritum by syncope, with -tritio abstract noun in -io giving contritio, contritionis.

The moral earthquake is Christian Late Latin. Translating the Psalms, Augustine and Cassiodorus meet cor contritum et humiliatum (a broken and humbled heart, Psalm 50/51) and contritio for Hebrew nibh-crushing. What was millstone vocabulary becomes heart vocabulary: God grinds the proud heart to powder so it can be remade. Monastic rules, Irish penitentials, and Carolingian confessors build a discipline of contritio cordis — tears, fasting, breast-beating — as the inward half of penance, outward half being confessio and satisfactio. By the twelfth century Peter Lombard and Aquinas systematise: contritio (perfect, from love) versus attritio (imperfect, from fear; literally a rubbing, not a full crushing). Trent will dogmatise the distinction.

  • French and English borrowing: Old French contricion (12th century, sermons, Chanson confessions) passes to Middle English as contricioun, contricion around 1340 — Chaucer's Parson preaches at length on contrition as the first part of penance, Rolle and Langland weep with it. Early spelling with -cioun shows French nasal vowel; Caxton and the Authorised Version stabilise -ition under Latin influence. RP /kənˈtrɪʃ.ən/ shows reduction of the first vowel, stress on the penult, and palatalisation ti → ʃ before -on, exactly as in nation, attrition.
  • Semantic drift: grind corn → crush bodies → bruise hearts → godly sorrow → secular remorse. Each step keeps crushing but moves the millstones inward. The Wodehouse phrase manly words of contrition is the secular terminus: no priest, no Latin, just plain English owning-up imagined as manly — yet the metaphor still assumes a crushed pride that must speak.
  • Socio-cultural context: medieval confession made contrition legally and socially load-bearing: without inward crushing, absolution was invalid, pilgrimages were tourism. Reformation polemic turned it explosive — Luther insisted contrition without faith is despair; Trent insisted attrition plus sacrament suffices. Puritan diaries obsess over whether sorrow is contrite enough. Victorian public schools translated it into manliness: the decent chap owns up, takes his beating, apologises without snivelling — precisely Tuppy-era manly words. Modern restorative justice and press-apology culture keep the structure while dropping the Latin: we audit tears for genuineness.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer's Parson's Tale (catechism of contrition); Everyman and morality plays; Shakespeare's contrite Claudius failing to pray; Donne's battered heart (Batter my heart as contrition poetics); Austen heroines' contrition after misjudgement; Dostoevsky's crushed Raskolnikov; Wodehouse's comic deflation where cosmic contrition shrinks to a few manly words that might have saved a flirtation.

Why does English keep so crushing a word for saying sorry? Because sorry is cheap and contrition claims cost. Its grinding etymology performs sincerity: to say contrition is to invoke millstones, bruises, and broken hearts, promising that the speaker has been ground by guilt and will not spring back into the same shape. Whether before God, a lover, or a press scrum, the word audits the heart for powder — and finds Tuppy's few manly words, had they come, just powdery enough to relaunch love's frail craft.
