abortive is an adjective meaning brought forth prematurely, failing to reach completion, or ending without the intended result. Its central image is biological: a birth or growth cut short before maturity, transferred to plans, efforts, movements and organs. An abortive attempt, abortive coup, abortive negotiation or, as in Walter Pater’s Renaissance, an abortive effort to revive antiquity, is one that showed promise, began brilliantly, but proved in part fruitless — begun but not carried through, like the brilliant but incomplete twelfth-century renaissance Pater describes. The word is formal, literary and slightly clinical, carrying disappointment without contempt: it dignifies the effort even as it records failure.

Beyond this general sense, abortive retains technical uses. In medicine and biology an abortive treatment aborts a disease (abortive migraine therapy stops an attack after onset); an abortive infection, ovule or organ is one arrested in development (abortive stamens in a flower, abortive limbs). In military and engineering usage an abortive launch, mission or test is one deliberately terminated early for safety. In older rhetoric an abortive child or birth meant a miscarriage or stillbirth, now replaced by euphemism but visible in 17th–19th-century texts. The adverb is abortively, the noun abortiveness.

  • General sense — unsuccessful, fruitless, untimely ended: the dominant modern use. An abortive plan starts but never matures. Bridge: from Latin abortus (miscarriage) via metaphor — a plan miscarries like a pregnancy. Collocates with attempt, effort, uprising, coup, escape, negotiation, revival. Pater’s “brilliant, but in part abortive effort” is canonical: splendid yet historically unable to complete what the fifteenth century later completed.
  • Medical sense — cutting short a disease: abortive treatment arrests a condition after symptoms begin (abortive polio, abortive migraine drugs). Bridge: directly therapeutic — to cause to abort, to interrupt a morbid process before full development. Neutral-technical, not judgmental.
  • Biological sense — arrested in development: abortive organs, seeds, spores or infections remain rudimentary (botany: abortive ovules never form seed; zoology: abortive metamorphosis). Bridge: literal embryology — development halted. Essential for natural-history reading.
  • Military / space / technical sense — deliberately aborted: an abortive launch or sortie is called off. Bridge: procedural — to abort (verb) gives abortive (adjective) for the terminated event. Slightly euphemistic, implying prudent cancellation rather than failure.
  • Obsolete obstetric sense — miscarried, premature: 16th–19th-century abortive birth, abortive child. Bridge: original Latin sense. Now avoided for sensitivity; replaced by premature, stillborn, miscarried. Important when reading older literature and law.
  • Register and connotation: formal and bookish; in conversation people say failed, unsuccessful, fruitless. Abortive adds a classical, almost tragic colour — something conceived hopefully but born too soon — which is why Pater, writing high Victorian aesthetic prose, chooses it for a medieval revival that flowered too early.

To master abortive, hold the miscarriage metaphor lightly: not crude, but structural — a beginning without a full term. Whether of a Provençal poem, a coup, a migraine or a rocket, abortive marks the pathos of the incomplete, and in Pater’s long sentence it lets him praise the twelfth century’s sweetness while explaining why only the fifteenth could bring it to term.
