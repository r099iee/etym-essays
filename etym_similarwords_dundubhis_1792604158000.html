• bherī: Similar because both are large ancient Indian martial drums used to rally warriors and signal charges; differs because the bherī is typically a massive, shallow kettle-drum with a metallic or wood frame known for a sharp, piercing, aggressive crack, whereas the dundubhi produces a deeper, lower-frequency hollow roar.
  • mṛdaṅga: Similar because both are venerable classical Indian percussion instruments; differs because the mṛdaṅga is a horizontal, double-headed barrel drum tuned with precise black paste (siyahi) for refined classical music and delicate hand play, whereas the dundubhi is a heavy, single- or double-headed martial drum beaten forcefully with wooden sticks.
  • paṇava: Similar because both are military drums mentioned together in Sanskrit epic battle scenes; differs because the paṇava is a smaller, portable hand-drum or hourglass-shaped tabor played with quick rhythmic strokes, whereas the dundubhi is a stationary or chariot-mounted heavy bass drum.
  • paṭaha: Similar because both are percussion instruments used for royal proclamations; differs because the paṭaha is a herald's drum beaten to announce civic decrees, news, or public gatherings in royal streets, whereas the dundubhi is primarily a battlefield engine and sacrificial liturgical drum.
  • kettledrum: Similar because both refer to large, deep-toned hemispherical or bowl-shaped drums; differs because kettledrum is a general English term (often designating modern orchestral timpani or cavalry drums), whereas dundubhis designates the specific ancient sacred and martial drums of Indo-Aryan civilization.
  • nagada: Similar because both are thunderous South Asian folk and martial kettle-drums struck with heavy wooden sticks; differs because the nagada (naqqāra) is a medieval and early modern drum of West Asian / Islamic origin adapted into Indian royal courtyards and temple folk music, whereas the dundubhi is an ancient Vedic and Sanskrit precursor.
  • tympan: Similar because both are ancient kettle-shaped percussion instruments; differs because tympan refers to the classical Greco-Roman hand-held frame drum or tambourine (tympanum) used in the rites of Cybele and Dionysus, whereas dundubhi is an enormous, booming martial war drum.
  • tom-tom: Similar because both are non-Western cylindrical membranophones struck for rhythmic communication; differs because tom-tom is an informal onomatopoeic colonial term for indigenous African or Asian drums, lacking the profound sacred, ritual, and epic majesty of dundubhis.
