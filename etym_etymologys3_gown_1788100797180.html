1. A word without an Indo-European pedigree. Unusually for a core garment word, “gown” has no established Indo-European etymology. Its recorded history begins in Byzantine Greek: the lexicographer Hesychius of Alexandria records γούνα (gouna), “a thick fleece, a fur garment”, probably in the fifth–sixth centuries CE, and the word’s own ancestry is uncertain — it may be a loan from an oriental (possibly Iranian or Semitic) source, and no Greek verbal root underlies it. From this Greek base the word entered medieval Latin as gunna, “a fur garment, a robe”, attested in glossaries and documents from about the eighth and ninth centuries, where the garment is characteristically an outer wrap of skins or fur. Some scholars have floated a Celtic origin instead; the handbooks simply record the route while declaring the ultimate source unknown. The lesson is lexicographical honesty: not every everyday English word is Indo-European, and “gown” is the proof.

2. Old French and the road to England. From Late Latin gunna the word passed into Old French as gonne, goune, gune — “gown, robe, dress” — attested from around the tenth to twelfth centuries. The garment by then was a loose outer covering, no longer necessarily furred: a gown was what you wore over your shirt and breeches when you went out, the ancestor of both the modern gown and the modern coat. It passed into Anglo-Norman, the French of the English court and law courts after 1066, and from Anglo-Norman into Middle English about 1300 as goune. All this traffic is typical of the clothing vocabulary of the period: England supplied the wool and the wearers, France the fashion words (“gown” arriving alongside “robe”, “mantle”, “surcoat”, “kirtle”).

  • Sound change Old French → Middle English: the Old French nasal vowel of gonne was repointed in English as ou, giving goune; the Middle English pronunciation (approximately /ɡuːn/) and the spelling “gown” settle in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries as the Great Vowel Shift raises Middle English /uː/ to modern /aʊ/ — the same shift that gives “town”, “down”, “brown”, which is why “gown” rhymes exactly with “down” today (RP /ɡaʊn/).
  • Semantics in Middle English: the word is completely general — a gown is any loose outer garment for man or woman, from peasant’s woollen gown to king’s furred gown; the modern specialisations are all later.

3. The gown becomes official (14th–18th centuries). From the fourteenth century the gown acquires its institutional life: university statutes regulate the gowns of masters and scholars; the courts prescribe the gowns of judges and serjeants; clergy wear preaching gowns; civic authorities dress aldermen and officers in gowns of office. Graduation, judgment and ministry are all performed in gowns to this day. The phrase “town and gown” — opposing the university (gown-wearers) to the town — is attested at the English universities from the eighteenth century and has become a general term for the academic–civic relationship (also in “gown-and-town” arrangements, and in the historic “town-and-gown” riots of Oxford and Cambridge). The institutional sense is the one that never weakened: a gown, unlike a dress, is something you put on to take office.

4. The woman’s gown: from everyday dress to ceremonial dress (16th–20th centuries). In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries “gown” gradually attaches to women’s dress: women of all classes wore gowns over kirtle and smock, and by the eighteenth century the gown is the woman’s principal garment — the hooped mantua gown, the robe à la française, the sack gown of the Rococo era. The industrial and couture centuries follow: Regency empire gowns, Victorian crinolines and bustles, Edwardian tea gowns and evening gowns. The wedding gown becomes an institution — decisively after Queen Victoria married in white satin and lace in 1840, making white the bridal colour and the wedding gown a sacred object of the trade. The satin gown of Wilde’s The Happy Prince (1888), embroidered with passion-flowers for a court ball, is this exact garment: the formal feminine gown whose making employed the seamstress whose story the passage tells.

5. The twentieth-century proliferation of specialised gowns. The twentieth century completes the word’s division of labour. On one side, “gown” in fashion narrows to the most formal and feminine items — evening gown, ball gown, bridal gown (casual one-piece dresses are always “dresses” now). On the other, the institutional and utilitarian compounds multiply: graduation gown, academic gown, judicial gown, chorister’s gown, nightgown (a garment for bed, eighteenth century onward), dressing gown, morning gown, and the hospital gown — the open-backed clinical garment of the twentieth century that every patient knows. The word’s range is now a perfect map of its history: ceremony (wedding, graduation, court), profession (bench, bar, pulpit, laboratory), and the simplest bodily coverings (night, morning, sickbed). “Gowned” — dressed in such a garment — applies to brides, surgeons, judges and guests alike, holding the whole history in a single participle.
