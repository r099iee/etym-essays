- Early 15th century - "forgetfulness, the state of being forgotten" - borrowed from Old French and Latin oblivio, the word enters English as a formal, literate term for the erasure of memory.
- 16th-17th century - "acts of oblivion" - in legal and political usage it names official amnesty, the deliberate remission of past offenses, giving the word an institutional, fate-deciding weight.
- 18th-19th century - "the abyss of oblivion" - Romantic and philosophical poetry deepens it into the grand, metaphysical dark: death as a state of being as if never known, a void of nothingness far beyond mere forgetting.
- 20th-21st century - "fade into oblivion" - modern usage domesticates the word to describe cultural and public neglect, anything fallen out of memory, while the adjective "oblivious" has drifted from "forgetful" to "unaware, heedless," losing the grand sense entirely.
