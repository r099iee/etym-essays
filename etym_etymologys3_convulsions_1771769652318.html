THE EVOLUTION ESSAY OF CONVULSIONS 1. THE ROOT OF TURNING AND TEARING The story of 'convulsions' begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *uelh₁-, whose basic sense appears to have been 'to turn, wind, roll' — a root of circular or twisting motion that fed verbs of rolling, winding, and, in a secondary development, of pulling and tearing at something twisted or entangled. It must be said at once that the precise PIE ancestry of the Latin verb vellere, 'to pluck, pull, tear,' is not settled: the great etymologists treat the Latin base as *uels-, and the further connection with *uelh₁- rests on probability rather than proof. Some scholars group vellere with Greek helikē, 'a winding, a spiral' (whence English helix), and with the Germanic base behind English 'well,' the verb of water bubbling and surging up, and behind German walzen, 'to trundle, roll'; but the reconstruction is offered with caution, and the attested history of the word begins firmly in Latin. 2. THE PHYSICAL VERB VELLERE What is certain is the Latin picture. Vellere was an earthy, physical verb, attested from the second century BC onward. Romans used it for plucking out hairs, pulling wool, stripping feathers from a bird, tearing leaves from a branch, wrenching a weapon from a wound. It is the verb of violent, repeated pulling at something rooted or fixed — the tug, the wrench, the pluck. It spawned a family of prefixed verbs that show the root's reach: āvellere, 'to tear away, pluck off,' whose noun of action āvulsiō gave English avulsion, the surgical tearing away of a part; ēvellere, 'to pluck out, uproot,' whose ēvulsiō gave English evulsion; revellere, 'to pull back, tear back,' whose revulsiō gave English revulsion — the violent drawing back that became, by metaphor, a violent repugnance of feeling; and dēvellere, 'to tear off.' Each of these compounds is built on the same physical image: a part of the world being wrenched away from the whole by main force. 3. CONVELLERE: WRENCHING TOGETHER The compound that concerns us is convellere, formed with the prefix con- or com-, whose force is 'together,' 'thoroughly,' 'with force.' In Latin compounds con- often intensifies the action — 'to tear thoroughly' — and it also carries the sense of tearing at several points at once, wrenching the parts together. Convellere meant 'to wrench, to tear to pieces, to shake violently.' Roman writers used it of pulling down buildings, of tearing up the ground, of the violent wrenching of limbs, and, figuratively, of the shaking of a state. The participle convulsus, 'violently wrenched, torn, shaken,' continues that double life: a body convulsum is a body wrenched by force or disease; a state convulsa is a state torn by faction. 4. THE MEDICAL NOUN CONVULSIŌ From the participle stem Latin formed the noun of action convulsiō (genitive convulsiōnis) with the ubiquitous suffix -iō, which turns verbal actions into abstract nouns. Convulsiō in classical Latin carried both a literal and a medical sense: a cramp or violent, involuntary contraction of the muscles — the body's parts 'wrenched together' — and, by extension, any violent shaking or convulsive movement. It is a significant fact that the earliest Latin uses are precisely physical and therapeutic, the language of physicians describing epileptic seizures and tetanic spasms: Celsus and the medical writers of the early empire use the word for the clenched, shuddering fit of the body. The metaphorical leap to politics and the natural world came later and belongs to a broader European habit of speaking of a state or an empire 'in convulsions.' The accusative form convulsiōnem, with its characteristic Romance ending, passed into Old French as convulsion, a medical word for 'a fit, a cramp,' and from Old French the word entered Middle English in the fourteenth century as convulcioun, spelled convulsion in Early Modern English. 5. THE SEVENTEENTH AND EIGHTEENTH CENTURIES: FROM BODY TO BODY POLITIC In the seventeenth century the word generalized beyond medicine. A society seized by unrest, a kingdom torn by civil war, a church rent by schism — these came to be described as 'convulsions,' and the metaphor of the body politic made the word natural for political writers: the state, like a sick body, could be seized by a convulsion it could not command. In the eighteenth century the word was extended a step further, to nature itself. This was the age of the sublime, when earthquakes, volcanoes, and storms were described as 'convulsions of the elements' or 'convulsions of nature.' Gibbon's use is the literary summit of this development: in his account of the terrible earthquakes that shook the cities of the eastern Roman Empire, he writes that man 'has much more to fear from the passions of his fellow-creatures than from the convulsions of the elements.' The word lends the earth itself a body-image — the planet seized by a paroxysm it cannot command — and the shuddering sublimity of that image sets off the cold, deliberate, infinitely more destructive passions of men. The medical image had travelled, across a century and a half, from the sickbed to the planet. 6. THE NINETEENTH AND TWENTIETH CENTURIES: EMOTION AND FIGURATIVE BREADTH In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word broadened again into the emotional and the everyday. 'Convulsions of laughter' and 'convulsions of sobbing' describe the involuntary seizures of strong feeling; a man 'convulsed with laughter' or 'convulsed with grief' is momentarily beyond himself, caught in a fit he cannot control. This emotional use retains the intensity of the medical word but denotes a controlled, brief burst — laughter, not epilepsy. In the same period the political sense continued in the language of history and journalism: empires, states, and societies are said to be 'in convulsions' when torn by revolution, civil war, or collapse. In the twentieth century the word extended to the economic world — 'convulsions in the markets,' 'the convulsions of the global economy' — and to a final broadened use in which any sudden violent turmoil can be called a convulsion. 7. THE MODERN WORD: A FIT OF NATURE, A FIT OF STATE In the present day, convulsions keeps all its registers alive at once. In medicine it remains the clinical word for violent involuntary muscle contractions — the plural form 'convulsions' is the standard shape, whether of a patient in a fever or an epileptic seizure. In geology and meteorology, 'convulsions of nature' and 'convulsions of the earth' survive as the elevated, literary-philosophical way of speaking of earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and upheavals of the elements — the direct descendant of Gibbon's sublime usage. In politics and economics the word describes violent social or financial upheaval, retaining the sense of sudden, uncontrollable, clenched violence that the Latin participle stamped upon it. The whole career of the word is one long metaphor: a convulsion is always, at bottom, the body's violent involuntary wrenching, and the earth, the state, the market, and the laughing man are all, in their different convulsions, bodies seized by a fit they cannot command. From the hair-plucking vellere of a Roman bathhouse to the 'convulsions of the elements' of an Enlightenment historian, the word has never lost the image of something rooted being wrenched violently, again and again, until it gives way.
