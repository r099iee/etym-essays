The Indo-European root. Wary descends from one of the oldest and best-attested roots of the Indo-European family, the verbal root *wer-, "to perceive, to watch out for, to be on one's guard". The reconstruction is secure: the root appears in Sanskrit várūtra (protection), Greek horáō ("I see", from the same watchful source), Latin verērī ("to fear, to revere" — and thus English revere, reverend, reverence), and across the Germanic family. The semantic core of the root is the act of attentive looking — the perception on which survival, and self-restraint, both depend. From this root Proto-Germanic formed the adjective *waraz, "aware, cautious, watchful", the ancestor of a remarkable cluster of English words: aware, beware, wariness itself, and, through the related verbal stem *warōn- ("to watch, to guard"), ward, warden, warn, and — via Frankish *wardōn and Old French garder — guard, regard and guardian. Latin's verērī shows the same root taking a different turn: the vigil that in Germanic is aimed outward at danger becomes, in Latin, the reverence owed to what one fears to offend.

Old English: wær, the sensible word. Old English inherited *waraz as the adjective wær, "watchful, aware, prudent, on one's guard", attested from the eighth century. It is a word of sober and practical wisdom: wær describes the man who keeps watch, who is not easily tricked, who knows the value of silence and patience. The related noun waru ("watch, guard, protection") and verb warian ("to guard, to beware") fill out the semantic field of vigilance in Old English, and wær appears in gnomic and proverbial verse with the flavour of hard-won experience: the wary man survives the ambush the careless one walks into. The same adjective existed all across the Germanic world — Old Saxon waru, Old High German giwar, Old Norse varr ("wary, aware") — so that behind the English word there stands a millennium of Germanic watchfulness.

Middle English: war and ware. In Middle English the word appears as war or ware, "cautious, aware, on one's guard", and it is in this period that the great imperative compounds solidify: beware! (from be + ware, literally "be watchful") becomes the standard English cry of warning, and aware (Old English gewær, "conscious, knowing") settles into its modern sense of "conscious, informed". Throughout this period the bare adjective remains common in the romance and the sermon: a ware ruler, a ware traveller, a ware confessor.

  • Mid-16th century: wary is coined. Around the 1550s, English forms the new adjective wary by attaching the native Germanic suffix -y (as in hairy, sticky) to ware, and the new form gradually displaces the bare ware in most senses — the same pattern by which wary, spicy, cheeky later dotted the language with suffixed adjectives from older stems. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century usage is strongly practical and strategic: chroniclers and dramatists speak of wary commanders, wary policy, wary steps, wary foes — the word is at home in the theatre of war and politics.
  • 17th–18th centuries: stabilisation. The word hardens into its modern shape, with the collocations wary of, ware of giving way to wary of + noun or gerund. Eighteenth-century usage (in the essay, the sermon and the novel) treats wariness as an unflashy virtue — the wisdom of experience, the hedge of the prudent man — while already hinting at its colder face: the man who is wary of everyone.
  • 19th century: the word of the frontier and the market. Across the nineteenth century, wary appears everywhere in the literature of frontiers, shops, courts and parlours: the wary settler, the wary buyer, the wary witness. It becomes the standard English adjective for the suspicion that reason and experience teach — without the moral condemnation that attached to outright distrust.
  • 20th–21st centuries: everyday prudence. Today wary is thoroughly standard but keeps a slightly formal, watchful dignity. "Wary of scams", "wary of commitments", "wary of change": the word names the default posture of the modern consumer-citizen, and its ancient root — the sentry's vigilance at the edge of the settlement — still glimmers under the modern surface of hesitancy and risk aversion.

Attestations and literary life. The family is attested continuously from Old English wær (8th century) through Middle English beware to the present. Among its notable appearances in the period's literary art, Shakespeare uses the related aware and the imperative beware constantly (the multiplied warnings of Macbeth and Hamlet), and the eighteenth-century novel of manners makes the wary eye a social instrument. In the source text of this card, The Hobbit (1937), Thorin — a king who has just recovered his hoard and immediately expects treachery — sends word bidding Dáin come "with wary speed": the adjective fuses haste and vigilance, and it is precisely the word a philologist-novelist would choose for a dwarf-king whose whole culture is one of dwarvish watchfulness.

Assessment. Wary is an ancient word with a modern face: three thousand years of Indo-European root, a thousand years of Germanic attestation, and a five-hundred-year-old English adjective form. Its meaning has never drifted far — watchfulness against harm — but its social colour has moved from heroic sentry duty to everyday prudence, and in so doing it has become one of the quiet workhorses of English caution.
