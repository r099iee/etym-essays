Parsley — a biennial Mediterranean herb of the carrot family (Apiaceae / Umbelliferae), Petroselinum crispum, grown for its finely divided, mildly aromatic leaves, used as a culinary seasoning or garnish and, traditionally, as a folk digestive remedy. In Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit, parsley is the green corrective: after gorging on lettuces, French beans, and radishes, Peter, "feeling rather sick," goes "to look for some parsley," drawing on an old kitchen-garden pharmacopoeia in which the herb was prized as a carminative and digestive soother. CORE SENSES
  • Culinary herb. The leaves (flat-leaf/Italian or curly) used fresh or dried to season soups, sauces, stuffings, and stews, and as the garnish that finishes a plate; parsley is one of the foundational European pot-herbs, the mild green that seasons without dominating.
  • Folk remedy. From antiquity the herb was taken as a carminative and diuretic, chewed to sweeten the breath after garlic and onions, and prescribed for the stomach; the "queasy after a feast, look for parsley" gesture in Potter is this old remedy made narrative.
  • Biennial plant of the carrot family. Botanically Petroselinum crispum, a close cousin of celery, fennel, dill, and carrot, with the flat, branching, fine-leaved habit typical of the family, and a long taproot.
SECONDARY AND MODERN MEANINGS AND LORE
  • Garnish par excellence. Since the nineteenth century parsley has been the default decorative garnish of restaurant and home cooking, the green note that signals "finished" on a plate — hence the figurative "to garnish with parsley" for any ornamental addition.
  • Flat-leaf vs curly. Modern culinary trade distinguishes Italian (flat-leaf) parsley, preferred by cooks for flavour, from the curly variety, preferred for decoration — jargon of the kitchen and the seed catalogue.
  • Folk superstition. The herb carries a rich superstition: parsley seed was said to go to the devil and back nine times before germinating; to transplant parsley brought ill luck; and a withering parsley bed in a house was an omen of death — lore that gave the plant a strange, half-sacred aura in the European garden.
  • Root and seed uses. The long taproot of turnip-rooted (Hamburg) parsley is eaten as a vegetable, and the seed has been used medicinally and in apothecary preparations through the centuries.
WHY THIS WORD IN THIS SCENE The word's register is domestic and old-fashioned. Potter does not use parsley as a garnish but as the garden's quiet antidote to excess, the green corrective after the reckless feast — a use that only makes sense with the herb's ancient reputation as a stomach-soother. The word thus carries a cozy, homely, medicinal weight rather than a purely culinary one. REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS Collocations: a sprig/bunch/handful of parsley, flat-leaf parsley, curly parsley, parsley sauce, parsley butter, parsley tea. Register is everyday kitchen-garden English, without elevation — the plain name of a common herb.
