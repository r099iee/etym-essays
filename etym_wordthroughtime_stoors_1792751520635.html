- [Middle English & Early Modern Dialectal Era (c. 13th–17th c.)] — Used in Northern English and Scots as the adjective stour / stoor, denoting a person, animal, or structure that was physically massive, hardy, stout, fierce, or unyielding — [perception: admired for physical resilience, rustic ruggedness, and unpolished fighting strength]
- [18th–19th c. Dialectal & Antiquarian Decline] — Receded from standard literary English into northern provincial vernaculars and Scots ballads, often retaining connotations of stubborn bulk, harsh weather, or dust storms (stoor) — [perception: regarded as quaint, provincial, or dialectal archaic vocabulary]
- [Mid-20th c. Tolkienian Invention & Legendarium (1954–Present)] — Revived and nominalized by J.R.R. Tolkien in The Fellowship of the Ring to designate the stockiest, water-dwelling breed of Hobbits — [perception: recognized globally within fantasy literature and fandom as the river-going, boat-rowing, boot-wearing branch of Halflings from which Gollum arose]
