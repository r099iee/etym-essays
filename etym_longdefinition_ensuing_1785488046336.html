Here ensuing — the present participle of the verb ensue, functioning as an attributive adjective — means “coming immediately after, following as the direct consequence of what has just happened.” In the sentence the word governs a historical inevitability: once English poets had set “the verse of Greece and Rome on the highest of pedestals,” it was equally inevitable “that in the ensuing judgement the home-grown product should be found wanting.” The nuance is measured, almost fatalistic. The judgement did not merely come later; it followed from the exaltation, arriving as a matter of course, as a verdict the comparison had already rendered. The tone is that of a calm historian narrating an intellectual verdict no one could have avoided, and ensuing is the hinge that converts temporal sequence into logical consequence. PRIMARY SENSE — immediate temporal consequence
  • Ensuing (attributive adjective) describes what comes next in time AND as a result: “the ensuing silence,” “the ensuing weeks,” “the ensuing chaos,” “the ensuing debate.”
  • To ensue (intransitive verb, formal) means “to follow as a consequence or result”: “a fight ensued,” “confusion ensued.” The verb is impersonal and event-like: what ensues is an event or state, not a person — one does not “ensue someone.”
  • The word carries a strong causal flavour: the ensuing thing grows out of what preceded it, so closely that it seems almost predictable — which is exactly the “equally inevitable” the sentence asserts.
  • Register: formal and slightly literary; it belongs to narrative, journalism, and history rather than casual speech. Journalists and novelists use it precisely because it turns an event into a miniature narrative.
ETYMOLOGICAL STRUCTURE Ensue is Latin insequī “to follow upon, to pursue” — in- “upon, after” + sequī “to follow” — passed through Old French ensuivre and Anglo-French ensuen into Middle English in the 14th century. The prefix contributes the “immediately after” force: to ensue is to follow hard upon. The same Latin root sequī produced a vast English family: sequence, subsequent, consequence, consequent, pursue, pursuit, execute, execution, sect, society, social, second, suit, suitor, and acolyte (Greek akólouthos “follower”). The boundary word among these is consequence: what is consequent follows from a stated cause, while what ensues follows on the heels of an event, with both temporal and causal force. SYNONYM BOUNDARIES
  • Following: Similar because both name what comes next in time or order; differs because following is the neutral, colourless word for anything next, with no implication of causation or closeness, while ensuing implies immediate consequence.
  • Subsequent: Similar because both mark a later position in time or sequence; differs because subsequent is more formal and merely notes a later point, leaving the relation between events unstated, while ensuing asserts that the later thing grew out of the earlier.
  • Consequent / resultant: Similar because both name what follows as an effect of a cause; differs because consequent and resultant are stricter and more causal, demanding a stated cause, whereas ensuing blends immediacy with consequence and lets the connection feel inevitable.
  • Succeeding: Similar because both describe what comes after; differs because succeeding emphasises orderly position within a series (succeeding kings, succeeding chapters), without the dramatic, causal growth that ensuing carries.
  • Resulting: Similar because both denote what emerges from a prior event; differs because resulting stresses the outcome or product of a process, while ensuing stresses the immediate temporal succession that follows an event.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE In Middle English ensuen first meant simply “to follow after” in place or time (the pursuit sense inherited from Latin insequī); over the 16th–17th centuries the causal sense — “to follow as a result” — crystallised and triumphed, while the pursuit sense died. By the modern period the verb means almost exclusively “to result, to come after as a necessary effect,” and the participle ensuing has settled into stock prenominal use, carrying a hint of inevitability. In the passage the participle does its historical work: the “ensuing judgement” is not merely next but the inevitable result of the pedestal placed before it.
