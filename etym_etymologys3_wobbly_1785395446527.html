WOBBLY is a young word, but its ancestry is as old as the sound of instability. The verb wobble entered English in the mid-seventeenth century, earlier as wabble, borrowed from the North Sea Germanic dialects that traded and fished along the southern shores of the North Sea: Low German wabbeln, 'to shake, quiver, move unsteadily', with cognates in Dutch wabbelen and West Frisian wabbelje. None of these can be carried back to any reconstructible Proto-Indo-European root. They belong instead to an expressive word-family built on the imitative base *wab-, a sound-symbolic syllable in which the initial glide and the lax vowel recreate a loose, rocking, side-to-side motion — the very motion of a wheel out of true, a jelly on a plate, or a man just back from the goblin-tunnels. Expressive families behave differently from regular inheritances: they tolerate variation, permit parallel coinage, and resist the neat sound-laws that bind ordinary Indo-European vocabulary, which is why wobble has cousins rather than ancestors and why the tree for it must stop at the North Sea rather than climb toward PIE. The same Germanic family produced some of English's most cherished verbs. Old English wafian meant 'to wave the hands, to be restless or agitated', the source of our wave; Old Norse vafra, 'to flicker, hover, move unsteadily', gave us waver; and the dialectal waffle, 'to vacillate', keeps a third member alive in modern politics. All four — wobble, wave, waver, waffle — are loose-jointed descendants of one expressive cluster, words that mean what their mouths do. The adjective was formed from wobble in the usual English way, with the suffix -y, which continues Old English -ig, from Proto-Germanic *-īgaz and, at the farthest reach, the Indo-European adjectival suffix *-ikos, the ancient 'having the nature of' element seen in Greek -ικός and Latin -icus. Here, for once, the derivational machinery really is Indo-European: Old English mihtig 'mighty', hālig 'holy', and wīndig 'windy' all show the same -ig, and modern English builds wobbly, jumpy, and rocky on the identical pattern. WOBBLY itself is first recorded in 1851, making it a thoroughly Victorian coinage — an old Germanic expressive base fitted with an ancient Indo-European suffix to name, with perfect transparency, anything that rocks instead of staying put. The semantic history runs from object to person to idea:
  • 17th century — wabble/wobble appears as a dialectal and nautical word for the rocking, swaying motion of objects — cart wheels, jelly, loose loads; a homely, onomatopoeic-sounding term.
  • 18th-19th century — the adjective wobbly extends from objects to people: wobbly legs and wobbly knees become the everyday name for faintness, dizziness, and fear; WOBBLY is first recorded in 1851.
  • 20th century — the word moves onto non-physical subjects: wobbly plans, wobbly prices, a wobbly argument; 'to throw a wobbly' enters British slang for a fit of temper or panic.
  • Scientific century — technical wobbles multiply: wheel wobble, the genetic wobble hypothesis (1966), planetary wobble detected by astronomers — the old rocking sense put to precision use.
Phonetically the word is almost onomatopoeic in itself: the /w/ and the lax vowel rock, the doubled /b/ jiggles, and the light -y ending softens the whole into something homely and comic rather than alarming. That double quality — the exactness of the motion and the warmth of the sound — is why Tolkien chooses it for Bilbo's spent legs: it lets the reader feel the wobble from inside while the narrator smiles. WOBBLY is thus a perfect miniature of expressive word-formation: no PIE pedigree, no long inheritance, just a Germanic sound that means what it does and an ancient suffix that makes it an adjective. It is a word whose very form is its history.
