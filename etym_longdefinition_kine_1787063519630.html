Kine is an archaic, dialectal, and elevated poetic plural noun referring collectively to cows, female bovines, or domestic cattle maintained for dairy production, breeding, or pastoral grazing. Historically celebrated as one of the most famous examples of an English 'double plural' (combining an ancient Germanic mutated vowel with a Middle English weak plural suffix), the word encapsulates centuries of agrarian life, pastoral verse, and scriptural translation in the English-speaking world.

In traditional lexicography, kine functions as a collective substantive that stands apart from modern regularized plurals. Whereas modern English universally favors cows for female cattle and cattle for mixed bovine livestock, kine preserves a dignified, pastoral, and sacred resonance. It rarely denotes a single individualized animal; rather, it portrays herds of dairy cows grazing in bucolic harmony, moving across dewy meadows, or serving as foundational markers of wealth and nourishment in ancient pastoral societies.

The multifaceted semantic dimensions, domain registers, and polysemous extensions of kine encompass the following spheres:

  • Primary Agrarian & Pastoral Collective Sense: In traditional farming communities and historical agriculture, kine referred specifically to dairy cows kept for milking and calf-bearing, as distinguished from oxen (castrated male bovines trained as draft animals for plowing and hauling heavy wagons) or bulls (intact breeding sires). The distinction is clearly preserved in traditional pastoral phrasing, such as "kine and oxen," delineating dairy producers from working beasts of burden.
  • Scriptural & Biblical Register: The word achieved monumental cultural permanence through the 1611 King James Version of the Bible. Most famously, in Genesis 41, Pharaoh recounts his prophetic dream to Joseph: seven fat and well-favored kine ascend from the River Nile to feed in a meadow, followed by seven lean, gaunt kine that devour the sleek ones without growing any fatter. Through this narrative, kine became an enduring scriptural and economic archetype for alternating cycles of agrarian abundance and devastating famine ("fat kine" vs. "lean kine").
  • Poetic, Romantic, & Bucolic Register: In English Romantic and Victorian poetry (from John Milton and William Wordsworth to Alfred Lord Tennyson, Matthew Arnold, and J.R.R. Tolkien), kine is invoked as a deliberate archaism to conjure an idealized, pre-industrial countryside. It evokes quiet English meads, winding brooks, the sweet scent of clover, and the lowing of peaceful herds at twilight, imparting a timeless, pastoral tranquility.
  • Feudal Legal & Manorial Inventories: In Middle English and Early Modern legal documents, wills, estate settlements, and manorial tenancy records, kine was standard legal terminology for counting livestock assets, assessing tithes owed to the parish church, and assigning dower rights to widows (e.g., bequeathing "pasturage for four kine upon the common waste").
  • Metaphorical & Prophetic Satire: In biblical rhetoric and literary satire, the term has occasionally been applied figuratively to wealthy, self-indulgent, and indifferent populations. In the Book of Amos (4:1), the prophet denounces the decadent aristocratic women of Samaria who oppress the needy, addressing them directly: "Hear this word, ye kine of Bashan... which oppress the poor, which crush the needy." Here, the image of pampered, fattened cows grazing upon the fertile pastures of Bashan becomes an emblem of luxurious complacency.

Historical Semantic Bridge & Morphological Evolution: The survival of kine alongside the dominant standard form cows represents a fascinating chapter in English historical morphology. In Old English, the singular cū formed its plural as cȳ through Germanic i-mutation (umlaut), exactly parallel to fōt/fēt (foot/feet) and mūs/mȳs (mouse/mice). During the Middle English transition, speakers in southern and midland dialects felt that the mutated plural cy was morphologically opaque, and they reinforced it by adding the productive weak noun plural suffix -en, creating cy-en or kyn (a double plural, identical to how childer became children and brether became brethren). When the northern regular -s plural ending swept south in Early Modern English producing cows, kine was preserved in liturgical texts, folk song, and high poetry, acquiring an indelible patina of pastoral reverence.
