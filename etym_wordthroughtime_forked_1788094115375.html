- c. 1300: the adjective forked(e) is coined in Middle English from the noun fork, which had arrived centuries earlier as the pronged pitchfork; the word immediately names any Y-shape — tools, then natural forms
- 14th–16th centuries: forked spreads to nature — forked trees, forked rivers, forked tongues — and the serpent's forked tongue acquires its sinister charge (speaking double), giving the word its only dark connotation, still active in 'speaks with a forked tongue'
- 17th–18th centuries: the verb 'to fork' (of roads and rivers) is established, and 'fork' begins its career as a noun for the branching point itself ('the fork of the river')
- 19th century: 'fork out/up' appears as slang for paying (1830s); the American frontier phrase 'speak with a forked tongue' becomes a staple of Western fiction; the table fork completes its rise from aristocratic novelty (introduced to England c. 1600, mocked as effeminate) to universal cutlery
- 20th–21st centuries: computing takes the word over — Unix's fork() (1971) names the system call that splits a process in two; GitHub's 'fork' (2008) turns 'forked' into a household word of open-source collaboration, applying the oldest shape-image of the word to the newest technology
