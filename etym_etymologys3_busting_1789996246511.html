busting — present participle of colloquial bust (to burst, break) — continues one of the oldest Germanic breaking-verbs, reshaped in America by a sound-swap, while its identical twin bust (sculpted torso) comes from an entirely different Italian-Latin source. The two must be held apart.

PART A — BUST = BURST (your sense).

  • PIE *bhres- (to burst, crack, crash). Pokorny reconstructs *bhres- as an expressive root of sudden noisy breaking — the crash of splitting wood, bursting bladders, cracking ice. The initial *bhr- cluster itself mimics eruption. Reflexes are chiefly Germanic, suggesting a northern expressive formation rather than a pan-IE term of high culture.
  • Proto-Germanic *brestaną (to burst). Regular Germanic reflex with *-t- formative: Gothic *bristan? Old Norse bresta (to burst, to crash), Old High German brestan, Old Saxon brestan. The sense covered both transitive (to break something) and intransitive (to break apart, to escape by breaking). The *-r-* stood before the vowel: *bre-.
  • Old English berstan (to burst, break, escape). Strong verb Class III: berstan – bærst – burston – borsten. Beowulf and the Chronicle use it for byrnies bursting, bonds bursting, abscesses bursting. Metathesis already flickered: the past participle borsten versus infinitive berstan shows the vowel + r instability that would later produce bust. Pronunciation: /ˈber.stɑn/ with trilled r.
  • Middle English bresten / bursten / barsten. After the Norman Conquest, spelling multiplied: bresten, bristen, bursten, barsten, bersten — scribes struggling with the er/ur vowel before r. Chaucer uses brest for bursting hearts and breaking dawns. The strong conjugation began weakening toward burst – burst – burst (no vowel change), a process completed by 1700.
  • Early Modern burst standardised (1500s–1700s). Printed Bibles fixed burst: new wine bursteth the bottles (Tyndale), burst into tears, burst into flame, burst upon. The verb colonised emotion and sudden action: to burst into song, laughter, applause. It was vivid but respectable — Shakespeare and Milton use it freely.
  • American bust by metathesis (1764 onward). In colonial speech, burst /bɜːrst/ swapped its r and vowel to bust /bʌst/ — the same metathesis that gave hoss for horse, ax for ask in dialects. First OED citation 1764 (American). Initially stigmatised as vulgar, bust prospered because it was shorter, punchier, and free to develop slang senses without dragging literary burst down: bust a lock, bust a bronco, bust down the door, bust someone (punch), bust (arrest), go bust (bankrupt), bust to private (demote). The progressive busting became the natural spoken form: busting into things sounds like a complaint, bursting into things like a report. Your text chooses voice over formality.
  • 20th-century slang explosion. Police procedurals gave drug bust, bust a dealer; the military gave busted to private; finance gave go bust; gambling gave bust (over 21); hip-hop gave bust a move, bust a rhyme. Each preserves the core image of abrupt rupture — of doors, careers, fortunes, or rhythms. RP pronunciation /ˈbʌstɪŋ/ with short stressed /ʌ/ and velar nasal /ŋ/.

PART B — BUST = SCULPTED TORSO (homograph to exclude).

  • Latin bustum (funeral pyre, tomb, monument). De Vaan derives bustum from com-bustum (burnt up thing), neuter participle of comburere (to burn up, cremate): com- (together) + *bustum (burnt). A bustum was the place where a body was burnt and buried, then any tomb with a memorial effigy. The sense shifted from pyre to monument to sculpted memorial.
  • Italian busto (trunk, torso, bust). By the Renaissance, busto meant the trunk of the body (as opposed to limbs and head) and by extension a sculpture showing head, shoulders, and chest. Cellini and Vasari use busto di marmo (marble bust). French borrowed it as buste (16th c.), English as bust (1690s, Evelyn's Diary). No breaking, no bursting — purely commemorative carving.
  • Why confusion is easy but must be resisted. Both busts are spelled and pronounced identically (/bʌst/), both form busted, busting in loose usage (busting a sculptor? busted statue?), and both involve force (chisel breaks stone). But their histories never touch: one is Germanic crash, the other Italic cremation. Dictionaries list them as bust (1) and bust (2). Your busting into things is unambiguously bust (1) = burst: to barge in, not to carve.

Synthesis: when the major complains of Lanarck busting into things on his own initiative, he invokes 1,500 years of bursting — Germanic bonds snapping, wine-skins popping, broncos bucking, doors splintering — compressed into a single affable American participle. The headstrong lieutenant bursts because bursting is what keen juniors do; the language, like the man, refuses to stay shut.
