The word conquests is a battlefield with a Latin soul and a French uniform. Its root is the ordinary Latin verb quaerere, "to seek" — one of the most productive verbs in the language — and its meaning had to travel from "asking" to "seeking" to "gaining" to "winning by force of arms" before it could describe the Roman absorption of the Mediterranean world. The trail runs through the question words of Indo-European, through Roman comedy and the Roman army, through the Norman Conquest of England itself, and finally into the historiography of Gibbon, where the plural names an entire epoch. The Indo-European question-mark. The traditional story begins with PIE *kwo- / *kwe-, the interrogative-relative stem that underlies "who" and "what" across the family — Greek tis, Gothic hwas, Latin quis. From the question "who? what?" Latin developed quaerere, "to ask, to seek". The semantic bridge is natural: to ask a question is to seek information. But this first step is also the most fragile: modern scholarship regards the origin of quaerere as genuinely uncertain, and the connection to *kwo- is traditional rather than proven. The safest statement is that quaerere is a Latin verb of seeking whose prehistory is opaque; everything after it, from Plautus onward, is securely attested. The verb of seeking. In classical Latin, quaerere already carries the double charge of asking and striving: one quaerit an answer, a thing, profit, or glory. Prefixed forms carve out specialized niches, and these are among the most familiar words in English: quaerere gives question and inquest; inquirere gives inquire; requirere gives require and request; acquirere gives acquire and acquisition; exquirere gives exquisite ("carefully sought out"). The whole family is the vocabulary of getting — by asking, by effort, by purchase. The military turn. The decisive formation is conquirere, with the intensive prefix con-: to seek thoroughly, to hunt up, to scrape together, and finally to procure and win. When the object of conquirere is a people or a land, the seeking becomes taking. The past participle conquisitus and its feminine conquisita named "what has been sought out and won", and from that participial noun Vulgar Latin produced *conquesta. This is the word Old French inherited as conqueste — both the act of conquering and the thing conquered. The verb conquerre travelled the same road, giving English conquer and conqueror. The Norman Conquest and English. English received conquest in the thirteenth century through Anglo-Norman and French — and history made the word's arrival dramatic. The Norman Conquest of 1066 was the defining event of English political memory for centuries; the legal phrase "by right of conquest" framed English titles to the kingdom, and conquest entered the language already heavy with the sense of "a kingdom taken by force". The capitalized proper noun "the Conquest" (or "the Norman Conquest") is still a technical label in English history. Gibbon, writing of the Romans, deliberately reactivates this military-sacred weight. The plural as epoch. Gibbon's phrase "the principal conquests of the Romans" uses the plural to mean not individual battles but the accumulated annexations of the Republic — provinces won by the senate's policy, the consuls' competitive ambition, and popular martial enthusiasm. The word carries his whole argument in one noun: the Republic was the conquering machine; the Empire was the conserving inheritor. Etymologically the conquests are "the things thoroughly sought and won" — and that is precisely how Gibbon views them: objects of deliberate republican policy, not accidents of imperial glory. The figurative expansion. From the nineteenth century onward the word widened far beyond armies. One speaks of the conquest of space, the conquest of a mountain, the conquest of a disease or of fear; a "conquest" in romance is a person won over; in business one speaks of "market conquest"; in Latin American historiography la conquista denotes the Spanish Conquest of the Americas with all its moral and political complexity. Every one of these retains the etymological core — something obtained by persistent, forceful effort — while diluting the literal shedding of blood. Law and restraint. In international law, conquest was long recognized as a mode of acquiring territory; the twentieth century (Covenant of the League, UN Charter) largely abolished acquisition by force, making "conquest" in the strict legal sense a term of prohibition rather than title. The word, so old and so loaded, thus continues to shift under new circumstances, exactly as it did when the Roman "seeker" of a province first became its taker. Conclusion. From a question-word, a verb of asking, a verb of seeking and procuring, an intensive of hunting up, a participle, a Vulgar Latin noun, an Old French conqueste, and a Middle English conquest — to the Gibbonian plural conquests. The military sense is a late and violent specialization of the humble act of seeking. That is why Gibbon's "conquests" still sound, at root, like the things the Republic went out and looked for.
