To flake is to come away in small, thin, flat pieces — and a flake is any one of those pieces. The core physical sense is of a delicate, broad but very thin fragment split off or peeled from a larger surface, as when a flake of rock breaks from a cliff wall, a flake of paint curls off wood, a flake of snow drifts down, or a flake of dead skin separates from the scalp. The word combines the idea of flatness with the idea of separation: a flake is flat, light, brittle, and detached.

  • Primary sense — a thin flat fragment: "a flake of rock," "a snowflake," "a flake of paint," "flakes of rust." The object is broad and flat relative to its thickness and has come away, or can come away, from a larger mass. This is the sense used in the sentence "A flake of rock split from the wall and fell."
  • Verb sense — to flake (off/away): to come away or detach in thin layers: "the old varnish began to flake away." Causatively, "to flake something" = to split or remove it in flakes, as in cookery where cooked fish "flakes" and is served flaked.
  • Material and industrial senses: flakes of mica, graphite, or mineral in geology and materials science; flake food for aquarium fish (manufactured as compressed dried flakes, 20th century); corn flakes and other flaked breakfast cereals, launched commercially in the 1890s by Kellogg's.
  • Person senses (American slang, 20th century): a "flake" is an eccentric, unreliable, or vague person — one who "flakes out," fails to follow through, or acts flightily; "flaky" describes such behaviour and temperament (a flaky friend, a flaky excuse).
  • Criminal slang: a "flake" of cocaine (a small flat piece of the drug, from the 1920s); "to flake" someone = to plant false evidence on them, to frame them (US police slang, mid-20th century).
  • Behavioural idiom — "flake out": to collapse from exhaustion, to fall asleep suddenly, or (informally) to fail to show up or to renege on a commitment.
  • Technical "flaky" senses: in computing, a "flaky test" or "flaky network" fails intermittently and unreliably; in geology, building science, and materials science, "flaking" names the delamination of surface layers.
  • Figurative — "snowflake": the uniquely fragile and special individual (from the idiom "no two snowflakes are alike"), later, since the 2010s, a dismissive political insult for someone perceived as over-sensitive.

Semantic logic of the secondary meanings: every sense rests on the image of thin, flat pieces coming loose or failing to hold together. An unreliable person "flakes off" commitments the way a flake separates from a surface; the criminal "flake" is a planted piece of evidence, something detached and dropped onto an innocent person; the computing "flaky" system works and then stops, like a surface that intermittently sheds layers. The word thus moves effortlessly between geology, cookery, computing, and character judgment while keeping its core physics: flatness plus loosening.

Register and style: "flake" is unmarked and versatile, at home in a geology textbook, a weather report, a recipe, and a joke about a friend's unreliability. Its compounds and derivatives include "snowflake," "cornflakes," "flaky" (adjective), "flaked" (past participle), "flake out" (phrasal verb), and the informal American "flakiness" for unreliability. Because it names such a homely object, the word has repeatedly been available for slang and metaphor, which is why a single noun can mean a fragment of rock and, later, a frame-up and a fickle friend.
