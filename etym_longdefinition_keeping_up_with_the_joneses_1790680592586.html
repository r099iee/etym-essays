Keeping up with the Joneses is an idiom for the competitive pursuit of social standing through visible consumption: buying, displaying, and upgrading houses, cars, clothes, holidays, and gadgets so as not to fall behind one’s neighbours, colleagues, or peers. The Joneses are imaginary — a generic prosperous family next door whose new car, extension, or kitchen sets the local standard. To spend your life keeping up with them is to let emulation rather than need or taste dictate spending, and the phrase is almost always gently mocking: it diagnoses affluence-driven anxiety, the treadmill on which each rise in the neighbours’ fortunes becomes a demand on your own.

The core sense is status emulation through consumption. Prototypical uses involve houses and cars, school choices, weddings, phones, and holidays — goods whose value is partly positional, meaning they matter because others can see them. Economists call this positional or conspicuous consumption, after Veblen: the purchase signals rank as much as it satisfies use. The idiom captures the dynamic element — keeping up — the sense of running to stand still, since the target moves every time the neighbours upgrade. Closely attached is the notion of living beyond one’s means: the phrase often implies debt, strain, or quiet desperation behind the gleaming façade, the household stretching itself to sustain appearances.

A second family of uses extends the idiom to any peer-benchmark race: departments keeping up with rival universities, nations keeping up in arms spending, parents keeping up with other parents’ tutoring bills, teenagers keeping up with influencers. Here the Joneses need not be neighbours at all — they are whatever reference group defines normal. The verb phrase also detaches freely: to keep up with the Joneses, a keep-up-with-the-Joneses mentality, Joneses-style competition. In recent decades the reference group has migrated online, and commentators routinely describe social media as a globalised Joneses machine, multiplying the neighbours from one street to the whole visible world.

Tone and register matter. The idiom is wry, moralising, and self-exculpating: speakers apply it to other people — exactly as in the source sentence, where the neighbours preen while we remain sensible. It belongs to informal and journalistic prose, satire, sermons, and personal-finance advice, rarely to formal economics (which prefers status competition, expenditure cascades, positional goods). Used of oneself it is usually rueful or ironic. Underneath the lightness lies a serious claim about motive: that much human effort is neither practical nor playful but emulative, driven by comparison — the claim Veblen, and the surrounding passage, press against our flattering self-image as purely commonsensical agents.

  • Status emulation: matching the visible spending and lifestyle of a reference group to defend one’s rank; the central sense.
  • Conspicuous consumption: buying signal-heavy goods (cars, houses, brands) whose point is partly display; the economist’s translation of the idiom.
  • Living beyond one’s means: the implied cost — debt and strain incurred to sustain appearances.
  • Generalised peer races: extended to schools, firms, nations, and online audiences competing on visible metrics.
  • Self-exculpating satire: the pragmatic sting — the phrase accuses them while flattering us, a move the source passage deliberately exposes.
  • Anti-materialist moral: in sermons and thrift literature, the idiom functions as a warning against envy and debt, urging contentment instead.
