1. PIE *demh₂- "to tame, subdue, break in" - a root of taming; the same root also yields Latin domus "house" (the place of the tamed) and Greek damazo (damazo), "I tame". 2. Sanskrit root dam (dam) "to tame, restrain" - with damayati (damayati) "he tames, subdues", damana (damana) "taming, restraint" (giving Hindi दमन daman, "suppression, repression"), and daman (daman) "rope, cord" (the binding strap). 3. Latin domare "to tame, subdue, conquer" (Classical Latin) - cf. domitor "tamer"; with the frequentative domitare "to tame again and again, to keep on taming, subdue habitually". 4. Old French danter / dompter "to tame, conquer, quell" (from Vulgar Latin *domitare; the p of dompter is a learned spelling). 5. Anglo-Norman danter "to tame, subdue" - passed into Middle English. 6. Middle English da(u)nten, c. 1300, "to subdue, vanquish, overawe". 7. English daunt (verb, 16th c.) "to discourage, intimidate, frighten" - the psychological shift from "subdue" to "frighten off". 8. English daunting (present participle used as adjective), first recorded c. 1656, "that daunts; intimidating, discouraging, forbidding". Debate: some scholars separate the "tame" root *demh₂- (Latin domare, Greek damazo, Sanskrit dam-) from a distinct "build" root *dem- (Latin domus, Greek domos, English timber), while others unite them under one root "to tame / domesticate"; the taming family itself is uncontroversial.
