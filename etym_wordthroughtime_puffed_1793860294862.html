- Late Old English / Early Middle English (ca. 11th–13th c.) - Originated as an onomatopoeic verbal formation (pyffan, puffen) directly mimicking the explosive sound of air forced through pursed lips; used for gusts of wind and sharp exhalations.
- Middle English (14th c.) - Extended to physiological breathing during heavy toil; began developing early figurative senses of vanity, describing persons swollen with self-importance or pride (foreshadowing biblical translations).
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - Firmly established in theological and moral writing ('puffed up with pride' in Tyndale and the King James Bible, 1 Corinthians); entered costume terminology for slashed, billowing sleeves stuffed with contrasting silk linings.
- 18th to 19th c. - Acquired a major commercial sense in publishing and theatrical criticism, where 'puffing' meant writing extravagantly complimentary reviews or fabricated notices for commercial gain (whence 'puffery'); culinary vocabulary adopted 'puff pastry' and 'puffed' grains; British colloquial speech popularized 'puffed out' for being completely out of breath.
- 20th to 21st c. - Ubiquitous across informal English for physical fatigue ('I am totally puffed'); standard in dermatological descriptions ('puffed eyes'); and pervasive in the modern food processing industry for expanded cereals and extruded snacks ('puffed rice', 'cheese puffs').
