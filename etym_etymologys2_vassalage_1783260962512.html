The English noun vassalage is a two-part formation: a base vassal (the feudal tenant) plus the abstract-noun suffix -age. Each element has its own derivation chain. CHAIN 1 — the base vassal: Proto-Celtic *wasto- "servant, boy, young man" (PIE ancestry NOT secure — see note below) → [Gaulish] *wasso- "young man, servant, squire" (attested in Continental Celtic personal names and in the Gallo-Roman record) → [Late Latin] vassus "servant, retainer, man" (recorded in 8th–9th c. Frankish legal documents, the Capitularies) — a Latinized Gaulish loanword → [Medieval Latin] vassallus "vassal, man bound by homage" (vassus + -allus) — the feudal revolution's word → [Old French] vassal "vassal, feudal tenant; (adj.) bold, heroic" (9th–12th c.) → [Middle English] vassal "vassal, feudal tenant" (14th c., Chaucer) → [English] vassal CHAIN 2 — the suffix -age: [Latin] -aticum "pertaining to; the condition or result of" (neuter of the adjective suffix -aticus) → [Old French] -age (abstract noun-forming suffix, "the state, service, or condition of") → [English] -age (same suffix, in homage, marriage, pilgrimage, and, on native stems, usage, storage, wreckage) MERGER — the English word: [Old French] vassal + -age → vasselage "the state/service of a vassal; homage; also valour, prowess" (11th–12th c.) → [Anglo-French / Middle English] vassalage (14th c.) "the condition of a vassal; service under homage" → [English] vassalage "state of feudal obligation or political subordination" NOTE ON THE DEBATED STAGE: Proto-Celtic *wasto- has NO secure PIE etymon. The often-quoted derivation from PIE *upo-sth₂- "to stand below" (the servant as one who stands beneath his lord) is attractive but phonologically speculative; it is noted here as a hypothesis only, and NO deeper Indo-European stage is asserted.
