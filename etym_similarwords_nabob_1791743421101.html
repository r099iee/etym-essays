• Nawab: Similar because nawab is the direct Urdu and Arabic ancestor from which nabob was anglicized, originally denoting an imperial governor or sovereign prince in Mughal India; differs fundamentally in cultural and linguistic register because nawab remains a legitimate, dignified dynastic and aristocratic title in South Asia (such as the Nawab of Awadh or Bengal), whereas nabob is an English colloquial and satirical term primarily denoting a nouveau-riche merchant returning from colonial exploitation.
  • Magnate: Similar because both describe individuals possessing immense wealth, industrial power, and social influence; differs because magnate emphasizes vast industrial, commercial, or media dominance (such as a shipping magnate or oil magnate) acquired through business enterprise, without the specific colonial, corrupt, or ostentatious baggage inherent to nabob.
  • Tycoon: Similar because both refer to fabulously wealthy business leaders with outsized public presence; differs because tycoon (derived from Japanese *taikun*, 'great lord') typically denotes an energetic, self-made industrial empire builder (e.g., a real-estate tycoon or tech tycoon), whereas nabob evokes luxurious leisure, conspicuous consumption, and colonial fortune-hunting.
  • Plutocrat: Similar because both designate persons whose enormous financial assets translate into political power; differs because plutocrat is a structural political term denoting rule by the wealthy class as an oligarchy, whereas nabob emphasizes individual ostentation, social climbing, and exotic luxury.
  • Mogul / Moghul: Similar because both words derive from the Islamic imperial rulers of northern India and came to mean colossal wealth in English; differs because a mogul in modern usage specifically denotes an influential power-broker in the entertainment or media industry (e.g., a Hollywood movie mogul), whereas nabob denotes flamboyant personal fortune and leisure.
  • Croesus: Similar because both are figurative archetypes of boundless, legendary wealth; differs because Croesus is a classical allusion to the ancient King of Lydia renowned for tragic gold, whereas nabob carries eighteenth-century colonial, political, and satirical undertones.
  • Grandee: Similar because both denote individuals of elevated wealth, rank, and haughty bearing; differs because a grandee originates as a high-ranking Spanish or Portuguese nobleman (*grande*) possessing inherited aristocratic blood, whereas a nabob is typically a self-made commercial parvenu who purchased social standing with colonial spoils.
  • Fat cat: Similar because both are critical, colloquial terms targeting wealthy elites who exert improper political influence; differs because fat cat is a modern American political idiom highlighting greedy campaign donors and corporate executives, lacking the exotic historical romance and imperial lineage of nabob.
