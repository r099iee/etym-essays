- (1590s) - “Rekindle” is coined in English from re- + kindle, at first with the literal sense “to light (a fire, lamp) again”, alongside the older “kindle”.
- (17th–18th centuries) - The figurative extension blooms: wars, heresies and seditions are rekindled; the word becomes standard in political and homiletic prose for the revival of dangerous passions.
- (19th century) - The Romantics fix “rekindled love and hope” in poetry and fiction; the ember metaphor gives the word its tender, nostalgic colouring.
- (20th century) - Journalism adopts rekindled for feuds, rivalries, debates and peace talks — a headline staple for anything that flares up again.
- (Today) - The word lives mostly in relationship and lifestyle registers (“rekindle the spark”, “rekindled romance”) and in commentary; the literal fire-lighting sense has receded to poetry and period prose, completing a four-century drift from the hearth to the heart.
