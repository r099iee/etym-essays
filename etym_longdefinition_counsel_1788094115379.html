In the source passage — “I will not say if this counsel be good or bad” — the word means advice, specifically a piece of considered, weighty advice offered in a moment of decision. The register is deliberately formal and slightly archaic, the language of councils and kings: the speaker is weighing whether the proposed plan is wise. This is the word's core meaning, but counsel carries a far wider range than “advice” alone.

The principal senses of counsel today:

  • Advice, especially formal or considered advice — “good counsel”, “a word of counsel”, “he offered his counsel freely”. The word implies wisdom, deliberation and experience rather than casual suggestion.
  • Deliberation, consultation — “to take counsel together”, “to hold counsel”, “after due counsel”. Here the word names the process of weighing a matter jointly before deciding.
  • A legal adviser; lawyers collectively — “counsel for the defence”, “the right to counsel”, “leading counsel”, “counsel appeared before the judge”. In legal English “counsel” is both singular and plural and refers to the barrister or team of barristers conducting a case; the American guarantee of “the right to counsel” (Sixth Amendment) makes the word part of constitutional vocabulary.
  • A person who gives advice (archaic/literary) — “a wise counsellor”; the Bible's “Wonderful, Counsellor” (Isaiah 9:6) shows the older personal sense.
  • Private intention, secret design (archaic) — “to keep one's own counsel” (to keep one's plans or opinions to oneself), “to keep counsel” (to keep a secret). This sense preserves the old meaning of counsel as inner deliberation and purpose.
  • The verb — “to counsel” means to advise, to recommend (“I counsel patience”), and, in modern professional use, to provide guidance, especially psychological or pastoral guidance (“to counsel a grieving family”). The noun “counselling” and the agent noun “counsellor” (American “counselor”) extend the word into the modern therapeutic register.

Fixed phrases. “A counsel of perfection” (advice that is admirable but impossibly ideal — from the theological idea of the “evangelical counsels” of perfection), “to keep one's own counsel”, “counsel of despair” (advice born of hopelessness), “to take counsel with oneself” (to reflect alone).

The semantic bridge. The word comes from Latin consilium, “deliberation, advice, plan”, built on the verb consulere “to consult, to deliberate, to take counsel”. The core image is people sitting together to think a matter through — hence the slide from “deliberation” (the process) to “advice” (the product) to “adviser” (the person). The legal sense is simply the professionalisation of the adviser: the barrister is the person one consults. The modern therapeutic sense completes the journey, moving the word from the council chamber to the consulting room.
