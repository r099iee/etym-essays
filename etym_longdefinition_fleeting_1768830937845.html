fleeting: Lasting for only a very short time; passing swiftly away; transitory and ephemeral, existing but for a brief moment before vanishing from view or from existence. The word names brevity itself — the property of things that appear and are gone, that cannot be held, kept, or preserved because their very nature is to be in motion toward their own end. In the literary context preserved on this card — a visionary landscape in which the colors of the flowers and the trees are denominated "but __________ functions of the sky" — the word is used with a precision that goes beyond mere duration. The passage does not simply say that the colors change; it says they are functions of the sky, contingent by-products of light borrowed from an external source, exactly as the color of a drifting cloud or of running water is borrowed. The word fleeting thus becomes the hinge of a metaphysical observation: the hues are not inherent properties of the flowers but temporary accidents of the light falling on them — "salmon tint" and "dreaming purple" are colors that drift across the blooms like reflections crossing water, and the instant the light shifts they are gone. A "fleeting function" is therefore a function that does not abide, a property loaned rather than owned, and the whole passage reads as a meditation on the impermanence even of beauty: the world itself is in flux, and what seems fixed and colored is in fact a flicker. The etymology reinforces the reading. To fleet (from the verb fleet, "to float, to drift") is to be carried along on a current; a fleeting thing is literally something that floats past the eye and is gone, so the passage's "fleeting functions of the sky" are colors adrift on the stream of the sky's light. Primary sense: Passing quickly; of very brief duration; transient.
  • Of moments and events: a fleeting moment, a fleeting visit, a fleeting opportunity — anything that occupies a very short span of time.
  • Of perceptions: a fleeting glimpse, a fleeting glance, a fleeting impression — things seen or felt only briefly, and often all the more precious or tantalizing for their brevity.
  • Of emotions and states: fleeting happiness, fleeting fame, fleeting beauty — temporary states that will not last, usually regretted or loved precisely because they pass.
Secondary and figurative senses:
  • Of thought: a fleeting thought — an idea that appears and disappears quickly, never developed; in modern psychology and mindfulness discourse the word is standard for the transience of mental content ("thoughts are fleeting").
  • Of reference in time: in the phrase "in the fleeting present," the word marks the moment between past and future as essentially ungraspable.
  • Of memory: a fleeting memory — a recollection that surfaces and vanishes, hinting at something not fully retained.
  • Of opportunity: "fleeting chance" — an opening that must be seized immediately or lost; the modern idiom "a fleeting window of opportunity" transfers the brevity of a moment to the brief availability of a chance.
Modern technical and jargon senses:
  • In computing and technology: fleeting refers to data or content designed to disappear quickly — e.g. "fleeting messages" in ephemeral messaging apps, where the word names deliberate impermanence of communication.
  • In optics and physics: a "fleeting image" is a transient visual impression; in astronomy fleeting describes phenomena of very short visibility such as meteors.
  • In rhetoric and stylistics: "fleeting" is a standard term for a deliberately brief, undeveloped reference or motif.
Semantic bridges and historical development: The adjective is the present participle of the verb to fleet, which in Old English meant "to float, to drift, to flow." A thing that floats is a thing in motion and not at rest; what drifts past a stationary observer does not remain. The transfer from water and space to time — from "drifting past" to "passing quickly" — happened by the fourteenth century, and from that point the word's core meaning became pure brevity. The buried image of the floating thing survives, however, giving fleeting its distinctive elegiac cast: what is fleeting is not merely short but in motion away from us, carried on a current we cannot control. Related words in the same family — fleet (swift), fleetingly (adverb), fleetingness (the quality of passing swiftly) — all share the same underlying image of motion and impermanence, and the modern idioms ("fleeting beauty," "fleeting glance") all retain the quiet melancholy of something loved that cannot be held.
