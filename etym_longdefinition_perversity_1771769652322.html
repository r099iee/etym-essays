In the passage from which this card is drawn — "The cast of an eye, the excess of an appetite, the perversity of tastes, the beauty or deformity of stature—he witnessed all these with delight" — the word "perversity" names the wayward, contrary, deliberately off-center quality of the tastes Gibbon observed in his fellow human beings. The word's Latin root is pictorial: pervertere, "to turn thoroughly aside," gives "perversity" its core image of something twisted or turned away from the straight, normal, or expected course. A perverse taste is not simply a different taste; it is a taste that pointedly refuses the natural or conventional path — a liking for what is crooked, unusual, or inverted, for its own sake. Crucially, the tone of the passage is not condemnatory. Gibbon witnesses these perversities "with delight" — the relish of a connoisseur of human oddity who finds the crooked turnings of temperament entertaining and instructive. The word here is closer to "contrariness" and "waywardness" than to the modern "perversion" with its heavy moral and sexual overtones; it describes an endearing, faintly absurd twist in the grain of a personality. In its oldest and still central sense, "perversity" is a quality of character or conduct: a deliberate or habitual disposition to behave in an unreasonable, contrary, or unacceptable way — the dogged insistence on doing the opposite of what is expected, advised, or sensible. It is the noun behind the adjective "perverse," and it denotes the state of being turned the wrong way in judgment or will. A person who argues simply for the sake of contradicting, who rejects good advice out of sheer oppositional stubbornness, who does the harmful thing because it is the thing they were told not to do, is acting with perversity. This sense is morally marked but not necessarily depraved: it is contrariness hardened into a principle, "wrong-headedness" as a settled bent of temperament rather than a sudden wickedness. The word has a rich and shifting polysemy that repays close attention:
  • Contrariness / waywardness (the core historical sense): a deliberate or habitual disposition to do the opposite of what is reasonable or expected; stubborn, willful opposition. This is the sense in Gibbon and in most eighteenth-century prose.
  • Moral corruption / wickedness (the intensified sense): in theological and moralistic writing, "perversity" can mean a serious turning from truth or virtue — a corruption of the moral order, sometimes barely distinguishable from depravity. Here the "turning aside" becomes a falling away from God, right reason, or nature.
  • Modern "perversion" territory (the sexual/clinical drift): from the late nineteenth century, "perversity" and especially "perversion" develop a specialized sexual sense — a deviation from the socially or medically defined norm of sexual desire or practice. "Sexual perversity" in modern usage usually implies this clinical-moral meaning, which is a narrowing and darkening of the older, broader "contrariness."
  • Of things, not persons: "perversity" may be predicated of inanimate forces or situations that act contrary to expectation — "the perversity of the weather," "the perversity of fate," "the perversity of inanimate objects" — a humorous anthropomorphism in which objects seem to conspire against us. (Note the example sentence on this card: "The perversity of the weather caused the event to be cancelled.")
  • In argument and logic: "perversity" can denote the deliberate misreading or willful distortion of a position — the refusal to take a statement in its plain sense, twisting it to suit one's own ends ("a perverse reading of the evidence").
  • Aesthetic perversity: in art and criticism, the word can name a principled delight in the strange, the inverted, or the taboo — a cultivated taste for what flouts convention, often admired as originality rather than condemned as vice.
The semantic bridge between these senses is the root image of turning. The Latin pervertere means literally "to turn thoroughly" (per-, "thoroughly, completely," + vertere, "to turn"): to pervert is to turn something all the way over, to invert it, to twist it out of its proper course. From this image every sense follows: the contrary person is "turned away" from right reason; the corrupt person is "turned" from virtue; the perverse reading is a text "turned" from its meaning; the sexual pervert is imagined as a desire "turned" from nature. The word's history is thus the history of an image — a turning, a twisting, a standing upside-down — progressively applied to ever deeper registers of human life, from a mild quirk of taste to the darkest judgments of morality and medicine. Register matters greatly. In modern English, "perversity" retains a moral ambivalence that its lighter near-synonyms lack. "Contrariness" is mild and colloquial; "waywardness" is softer and more sympathetic; "obstinacy" and "stubbornness" are neutral about right and wrong; but "perversity" insists that the behavior is unreasonable and unacceptable — that the person is not merely different but wrong, and wrong on purpose. Yet the word has not entirely lost its old lighter tone: in literary and ironic use it can still describe a charming, wayward quirk of taste, as it does in Gibbon's sentence, where the "perversity of tastes" is witnessed "with delight." This double life — condemning in ordinary modern use, indulgent in literary and historical use — is exactly what makes the word delicate to translate and useful to the historian of the human temperament.
