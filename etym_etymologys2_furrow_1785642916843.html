PIE *perk- (also written *perḱ-) 'to dig out, tear out, pull up' (the reconstructed root of the ploughing words, with an agential and a passive series in different languages) → Proto-Germanic *furh- 'a furrow, a trench cut by a ploughshare' (Grimm's Law shifted initial PIE *p to Germanic *f; the u-grade supplies the vowel; the family includes Old Saxon furh, Old Norse for, Old High German furuh, German Furche, Dutch voor — and the Latin cognate porca 'a ridge between two furrows', which shows the same root naming the raised part in Latin and the cut trench in Germanic) → Old English furh (genitive furge) 'furrow, trench in the earth' (attested before 1000) → Middle English furwe, forwe, furgh 'furrow' (12th–15th c.; the modern -ow spelling descends from the old genitive/oblique form furwe) → modern English FURROW (noun; the verb 'to furrow' is first attested in the 16th c., formed from the noun — first of ploughing, then figuratively of creasing the brow). Debated/nuanced stages: The PIE reconstruction *perk- 'to dig out, tear out' is standard (Pokorny's *perk-), and the Germanic and Latin reflexes are secure; the only notable nuance is the semantic divergence within the family — Latin porca names the raised ridge between furrows, Germanic furh the cut trench — a reminder that the root meant 'to turn up the earth' and each language foregrounded a different result. The development of the English -ow spelling from the oblique form furwe in Middle English is well documented, not an invention.
