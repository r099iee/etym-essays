connoisseur — one who has expert knowledge and trained, discriminating judgment in matters of taste — the fine arts above all, but also wine, music, porcelain, coffee, and the rest — from French, literally "one who knows." In Ruskin's sentence the connoisseur is the "ordinary connoisseur" to whom his own opinions "will sound heretical," and the word is loaded with barely concealed irony. A connoisseur by definition claims cultivated judgment; but the qualifier "ordinary" marks the type as predictable, formulaic, and complacent — the professional appreciator whose taste is a set of inherited rules, fashionable names, and shop-worn critical clichés rather than a direct response to nature. Throughout Modern Painters Ruskin wages war on exactly this class of judges: men who judge a Turner by what they have been taught to admire, not by what they see. The noun therefore presents a smooth, courteous surface with a satirical blade beneath it — Ruskin borrows the connoisseur's own vocabulary in order to expose its bankruptcy. The tone is elegant disdain rather than open attack: the connoisseur is respectable, orthodox, and precisely for that reason an obstacle to true perception. Polysemy in historical order.
  • Sense 1 (the core, early 18th c.): "a person of expert, cultivated judgment in the fine arts" — the learned judge of painting, sculpture, and architecture, associated with the connoisseurship of the Academy, the Grand Tour, and the great collections.
  • Sense 2 (19th c. onward, broadened): "an expert judge in any field of taste" — a connoisseur of wine, of music, of porcelain, of food, of cigars, of cinema.
  • Sense 3 (20th c., enthusiast sense): "a devoted and discerning amateur or collector" in a particular domain, whose credential is refined experience rather than professional certification.
  • Sense 4 (ironic and satirical): the complacent professional appreciator whose taste is inherited rules rather than direct perception — Ruskin's "ordinary connoisseur," the satirical sense that shades into a synonym for pretension.
  • Related formations: connoisseurship (n., 1714, "expert knowledge of the arts; the practice of the connoisseur"), connoisseurial (adj., rare), connoisseuse (the feminine form, rare), to connoisseur (verb, rare and jocular).
The semantic bridge. The word is built from the French verb connaître, "to know," plus the agent suffix -eur, which names a person by the activity he performs: a connaisseur is literally "one who knows." The French verb descends from Latin cognoscere, "to get to know thoroughly, to recognize," the intensive form of noscere, "to come to know" — so that the connoisseur is, etymologically, the person who has come to know deeply, who recognizes the genuine article at a glance. The shift from "one who knows" to "one who knows what is good" happened inside French in the seventeenth century, when the verb came to mean knowing in matters of taste; English borrowed the finished word in the early eighteenth century precisely because it had no single native word for the refined, educated judge of the arts. Boundaries against near-synonyms.
  • Expert is the broad, neutral term for anyone with deep knowledge of a field and implies nothing about aesthetic refinement.
  • Specialist narrows expertise to a single sub-field, again without sensory discrimination.
  • Authority implies institutional trustworthiness and verifiable knowledge, not taste.
  • Critic is the professional who publishes judgments and may judge anything; the connoisseur judges privately by cultivated taste.
  • Aficionado is the enthusiastic amateur whose credential is love rather than learning; the connoisseur's credential is trained judgment.
  • Dilettante is the near-antonym — a pretended lover of the arts whose cultivation is superficial, though historically it too meant "one who delights in the arts."
  • Cognoscente is the Italian-derived doublet of connoisseur, meaning the same thing; it is rarer and more pretentious in English.
  • Judge names one who decides between works or performances, with judicial overtones the connoisseur lacks.
Grammar and register. Connoisseur is a countable noun used with of ("a connoisseur of wine," "a connoisseur of Renaissance art") and sometimes absolutely. It is pronounced with the French-style initial /kɒn/ and final /ˈsɜː/ (RP /kˌɒnəsˈʊə/), and its spelling — connoisseur, three s's, the -eur of French — marks it as a loanword even to the eye. The register is elegant and faintly social: the word belongs to the drawing room and the auction house rather than the laboratory. Modern usage keeps a watchful irony close at hand — the connoisseur can all too easily become the "ordinary connoisseur" of Ruskin's satire, the man whose knowledge has become a set of reflexes instead of an openness to perception. Modern use. In contemporary English connoisseur appears most often in (1) the art and antiques market, (2) the world of wine, spirits, coffee, tea, and cigars, where "connoisseur" is a mark of refined appreciation, (3) criticism and cultural commentary, and (4) mock-pretentious usage, where the word itself becomes a performance of knowing. The adjective connoisseurial is a scholarly coinage; connoisseurship denotes the discipline — the attribution, dating, and evaluation of works of art — that grew up around the connoisseur in the eighteenth century. The word has never lost the Gallic elegance of its birth: to call someone a connoisseur is to pay a compliment with a French accent, and to call them an "ordinary connoisseur," as Ruskin does, is to pay that compliment and then withdraw it.
