Ancestral meaning: prosperity, not velocity. The deep ancestor of speed is the PIE root *spē-/*speh- "to thrive, prosper, succeed" (Pokorny's *spē-), which produced a Proto-Germanic noun *spōdiz carrying the sense "success, prosperity" and, by natural extension, "progress" and "swiftness". Cognates across the family preserve the success sense: Old High German spuot "success, quickness", Middle Dutch spoet, and modern German sich sputen "to hurry". It is a striking fact that English speed began life meaning "thriving, wealth, good fortune" — closer to modern prosperity than to modern velocity.

Old English: a word of blessing. In Old English the noun spēd ("success, prosperity, wealth, abundance, opportunity") was a dignified and frequent word, and the derived weak verb spēdan meant "to prosper, succeed, get on, attain one's aim". The old blessing formula "God spede you" — "may God prosper you" — was a genuine wish of fortune, not a wish of haste. This is the semantic stratum fossilised in the modern interjection Godspeed.

The Middle English pivot: from thriving to hurrying. The great semantic drift happened in Middle English. Because "success" and "progress" were felt as movement forward, spēden slid from "to prosper" to "to make progress, get on", and from "getting on" to "getting on quickly". By the 12th–13th centuries the verb was in regular use for "to go quickly, hasten" — and the noun simultaneously acquired "rate of going" as one of its senses. Around 1200 the past tense sped crystallised by analogy with strong verbs of the same shape (lede → led, meten → met), replacing the older weak spēdde. By the 16th–17th centuries the polarity had fully inverted: speed now first meant swiftness of motion, with "prosperity" surviving only in the frozen blessing Godspeed and in archaic phrases such as "well sped".

Chaucer, Shakespeare, and the literary past. Chaucer uses speden for both prospering and hastening; Shakespeare exploits the double register, using speed for success ("God speed the mark") and for haste alike. Throughout Renaissance and Enlightenment prose, sped was the natural past for quick motion, and it carried a faint nobility: to speed was to move with purpose and luck on one's side, the old blessing still audible behind the new velocity.

The industrial and motoring century. The 19th century's steamships, railways, and factories turned speed from a quality into a quantity — a measurable rate of motion — and the 20th-century motor age added speed limits, speeding offences, and speed cameras. In this technical register the verb split its inflections: for "to move fast" the past is sped; for "to exceed the limit" or "to accelerate a process", English prefers the regular speeded. The two past forms thus divide the verb's labour between the literary/kinetic and the technical/procedural.

Modern status. Today sped is the elegant, one-syllable past of swift motion — standard in fiction, news, and everyday speech ("the car sped past", "he sped off", "the years sped by"). Its long journey from "prospered" to "hurried" is one of the cleanest demonstrations in English of how a word about thriving became a word about velocity, with the blessing Godspeed standing as the fossil of the earlier world. The Tolkien sentence, in which the coiled dragon sped from lair to gate, uses the word in its fully modern, purely kinetic sense — the old prosperity is gone, but the swiftness that once signalled divine favour still carries an undertone of unstoppable purpose.
