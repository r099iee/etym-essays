Linear derivation chain for WINDING (single root word): PIE *wendʰ- (to turn, wind, weave, bend) — reconstructed root, c. 4500–3500 BCE; the source of turning-and-binding words across the Indo-European family (Latin viere "to bend, weave", vincire "to bind"; Lithuanian výti "to twist"; Old Church Slavonic veno "wreath"; Old Irish fet "chain"; Tocharian B wänt "turban"; Sanskrit vandhura- "curved, the curved chariot frame"). → Proto-Germanic *windaną (strong verb, class 3: to turn, twist, coil; to plait, weave) — reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE. → Old English windan (to wind, twist, plait, weave; strong verb windan–wand–wunden) — attested from the 8th century CE (e.g., in Beowulf and the Anglo-Saxon riddles). → Middle English winden (to wind, twist, coil, turn) — 12th–15th century. → Modern English wind (to coil, turn, move in a twisting course; to tighten a spring by turning a key) — 16th century onward. → winding (present participle, gerund, and participial adjective) ◄ TARGET. Note 1: The noun wind (moving air) descends from a DIFFERENT PIE root, *h₂weh₁- (to blow), and is entirely unrelated to the coiling verb; the participle winding belongs only to the turn-verb. Note 2: The exact laryngeal configuration and ablaut grade of the PIE root are matters of minor scholarly debate (some handbooks write *wendh- without the aspiration mark), but the Germanic chain *windaną → windan → winden → wind → winding is historically secure at every stage.
