Nicking is the present participle of the verb to nick, meaning in its most literal sense to make a very small cut, notch, or chip in a surface, and by extension to remove something with a tiny, quick, deft movement of the fingers, or in British informal use to steal something quickly.

In the Wodehouse context — nicking a speck of dust from lace — the sense is delicate and physical rather than criminal: Bertie flicks or brushes the speck away with a fingernail or fingertip, a gesture that is quick, precise, almost dismissive. It conveys ease, fastidiousness, and upper-class nonchalance: the speck is so trivial that it can be despatched with a nick rather than a brush or shake. More generally, to be nicking something in standard English means to be in the act of chipping, grazing, or barely cutting it, as when a razor nicks skin, a blade nicks wood, or a stone nicks a windscreen.

In British slang, which dominates everyday spoken use today, nicking means stealing, especially petty, opportunistic theft, and in the passive to get nicked means to get caught or arrested by the police. This slang layer coexists with the standard layer without confusion because context and syntax disambiguate: nicking a car or nicking cheese from a shop is theft, while nicking ones finger or nicking dust off a cuff is a small cut or flick.

Polysemy and secondary senses today include:
  • To make a small cut or notch: the core craft sense — to nick an artery in surgery, to nick a cable, to nick the paintwork. The semantic bridge is direct from the late medieval noun nick as a notch in a tally-stick.
  • To flick, whisk, or brush lightly away: to nick a speck off lace, to nick a fly off ones arm. The bridge is metonymic: the tiny cut becomes any tiny, sharp hand movement that dislodges something.
  • To steal, especially quickly or pettily (chiefly British): to nick a bike, to nick someones chips. The bridge is via gamblers and thieves cant: to hit the mark narrowly, to snatch in an instant, to nab.
  • To arrest or catch (chiefly British, passive): to get nicked for speeding, he was nicked last night. The bridge is from to catch or nab, itself from the snatching sense.
  • To arrive exactly in time / to win narrowly (in the phrase in the nick of time, to nick a win): the notch as the exact mark. The bridge is the tally sense where the nick marks the precise point.
  • In sport and gaming: in cricket to nick the ball means to edge it faintly with the bat; in darts or bowls to nick means to graze. The bridge is again the slight-touch sense.
Register varies sharply: the cutting and flicking senses are neutral and universal, the stealing and arrest senses are informal and markedly British, and the sporting senses are domain-specific. All share the same semantic kernel: smallness, sharpness, suddenness, and precision.

Idiomatically, nicking also survives in fixed phrases that reinforce precision and narrowness: in the nick of time for exactly at the critical instant, to nick a point or goal for scoring by the smallest margin, and in engineering to hold tolerances to a nick. Each preserves the tally-notch image of hitting the exact mark and no more.
