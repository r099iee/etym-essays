- Late 15th to mid 16th century: Erupted in Early Modern English primarily as a physical adverb describing an oblique, sideways, or out-of-the-corner-of-the-eye bodily look (*to look askance*), neutral or slightly playful.
- Late 16th to 17th century: Adopted by Elizabethan poets (such as Spenser and Shakespeare) and prose writers to convey coy disdain, flirtatious glancing, or subtle scorn, cementing the metaphorical transition from physical angularity to emotional detachment.
- 18th to 19th century: Hardened in Victorian literary English into an idiomatic marker of severe moral disapproval, social skepticism, and class-conscious mistrust, almost exclusively collocating with verbs like *look*, *view*, *eye*, and *regard*.
- 20th to 21st century: Standardized in journalistic, legal, and literary prose as a formal idiom indicating institutional skepticism, financial wariness, or critical scrutiny towards suspicious or irregular behaviors.
