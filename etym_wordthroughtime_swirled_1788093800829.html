- [Early 15th century: "to whirl, eddy"] - A purely physical word for circular liquid and wind motion; plain and descriptive, with no special tone — the vocabulary of water and weather.
- [16th–17th centuries: transitive force] - "To cause to whirl" — still strictly physical (stirred liquids, smoke, dust); perception: neutral, demotic, drawn from everyday life rather than literature.
- [18th–19th centuries: sublime nature] - Romantic and Gothic prose seizes on swirled water, flame and fog; the word gains a dramatic, sublime register — wild, uncontrollable, even menacing force.
- [20th century: the figurative leap] - Emotions, memories, rumours and ideas "swirl" in minds and crowds; journalism and psychology adopt the vortex as the image of mental turmoil; "swirled" also turns adjectival in design (swirled patterns, marbled colours).
- [Contemporary: elegant turbulence] - Cream swirled into coffee, politics swirling around a leader, a dancer's swirling skirt: the word now blends menace with beauty — kinetic, hypnotic, often admiring.
