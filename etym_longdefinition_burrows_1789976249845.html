burrows as used here is the plural noun: holes, tunnels or underground dwellings excavated by small mammals — rabbits, foxes, badgers, moles, prairie dogs, voles — typically with an entrance hidden under tree roots, banks or scrub, leading to a branching system of galleries and nesting chambers. Your context — covered with burrows hidden under the roots of trees, and the creatures at daybreak frisk about, running in and out of the holes — is classic rabbit-warren scenery on the Seine islands in Maupassant: a honeycombed bank where animals shelter by day and emerge to play at dawn. The plural matters: a single hole is a refuge, many holes are a colony, a landscape, an ecosystem.

The same spelling is also the third-person singular present of the verb to burrow — he burrows into the blankets, the journalist burrows into archives — meaning to dig, tunnel, or metaphorically to embed oneself deeply and hide. Capitalised Burrows is a common English surname (and occasional place-name), borne for example by textile inventor and by literary figures, originally denoting someone who lived near a fortified mound or warren. Learners must therefore distinguish noun-plural, verb-form and proper-name uses by capitalisation and syntax.

  • Zoological core (noun): excavated shelter — rabbit burrows, badger sett, fox earth, mole run. Distinctions: a warren is a whole network of rabbit burrows; a sett is a badger burrow system; a den or lair may be a burrow or a surface hollow; a tunnel stresses passage, a hole stresses entrance. Bridge from digging action to dwelling is metonymy: the act names the result.
  • Verbal senses: literal digging (voles burrow under snow), concealment (to burrow under blankets), investigation (to burrow through files), parasitism (mites burrow into skin). All share pushing into and disappearing inside a mass. Modern journalism loves the metaphor: burrowing into a scandal, burrowing into data.
  • Ecological-engineering sense: burrows aerate soil, redistribute seeds, provide shelter for commensals (burrowing owls nest in prairie-dog burrows; tortoise burrows shelter 300+ species). Conservation language therefore treats burrow density as a biodiversity index.
  • Military and technical extensions: burrow charges, burrowed shelters (dugouts), electronics buried vias by analogy, and computing burrowing through menus. Child language: burrow into mother's lap for snuggling, keeping warmth and safety connotations without literal digging.
  • Proper-name sense: Burrows as surname from Middle English burgh, burwe (mound, earthwork where rabbits burrowed) — e.g. Edgar Rice Burroughs variant spelling. No semantic content today beyond identity, but origin reminds us burrows cluster around mounds.

Collocations: to dig burrows, to line burrows, to block up burrows, burrow entrance, burrow system, abandoned burrows. Register is neutral-naturalistic, slightly literary when pluralised as landscape (a hillside pocked with burrows). Connotation is cosy-hidden yet vulnerable: burrows protect but also trap (ferreting, flooding, myxomatosis). In the Maupassant passage the hidden burrows under roots signal fecundity and secret night-life that poachers exploit at dawn — nature's tenements glimpsed at playtime.
