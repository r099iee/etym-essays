The etymological progression of jostling is a fascinating case study in morphological hybridization and semantic democratization, illustrating how a formal Latin preposition expressing spatial proximity evolved into an aristocratic martial tournament term in medieval France, before being reshaped by native English grammar into a vibrant descriptor of rough urban friction.

The ultimate root is the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *yewg-, signifying "to join, unite, bind together, or yoke." This highly productive root yielded cognates across the Indo-European family, including Sanskrit yoga ("union, joining") and yugam ("yoke"), Ancient Greek zygon ("yoke"), and Classical Latin iugum ("yoke") and iungere ("to join," source of English junction, joint, and conjugal).

In Classical Latin, an adverbial and prepositional derivative was formed: iuxtā (originally *iug-sto-, meaning "standing joined or closely bound"), signifying "close to, near, alongside, or next beside" (which survives in the English loanword juxtaposition). During the transition to Late Latin and early Romance (c. 4th–6th century CE), this spatial particle was verbalized into the first-conjugation verb iuxtāre, meaning "to approach, bring together, draw near, or come side-by-side."

In Old French and Anglo-Norman, iuxtāre underwent regular Romance phonetic evolution (the palatalization of initial /j/ to /dʒ/ and vowel rounding), emerging in the 12th century as jouster or juster. Under the influence of high medieval chivalric culture, the verb was specialized into a technical martial term: it designated the formal meeting of two mounted, armored knights galloping toward one another along a barrier (the tilt-list) to strike each other with lances. When the Normans conquered England in 1066, they brought this chivalric vocabulary with them, embedding jousten into Middle English around the early 14th century.

However, during the late 14th century, native Middle English speakers performed a remarkable linguistic transformation on the borrowed French verb. They attached the native Germanic frequentative-iterative verbal suffix -le (from Proto-Germanic *-ilōn / *-lōną, identical to the formative element in sparkle from spark, crumble from crumb, and wrestle from wrest) to the base joust, producing the new hybrid verb jostelen or justlen. This morphological innovation shifted the meaning from a single, grand, formal clash of two knights to a succession of continuous, small, erratic bumps and knocks.

By the 16th and 17th centuries in Early Modern English, justle and jostle had completely broadened beyond the tournament field into everyday social life. Tudor dramatists, including William Shakespeare (in The Tempest and Henry IV), used jostle to portray the unruly, packed crowds of London markets, where apprentices, fishwives, and watermen bumped shoulders in narrow alleys. Over the 18th and 19th centuries, the spelling stabilized around jostle (with the silent t reflecting the older consonant cluster), while its participial form jostling became an indispensable literary tool for depicting physical crowding, turbulent fluid movement (as in Tolkien's cascading barrels), and relentless commercial and political rivalry.
