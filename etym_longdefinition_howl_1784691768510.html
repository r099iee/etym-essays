Howl (verb and noun) is to utter a long, loud, doleful cry, made with the mouth open and the sound prolonged: the characteristic vocalisation of the wolf and the dog, of the wind tearing through a valley or round a house, and of a human being in the extremity of pain, grief or fury. As a noun, a howl is such a cry itself. The word sits at the animal end of the scale of human shouting — it is the cry that escapes control, that carries no words and very little dignity, and it is almost always heard as expressing suffering rather than threat. A howl may also be comic, as when a clown, a cartoon wolf or a struck troll produces one; the word's long, wailing vowel makes it as much a sound-painting as a label, and its uses have spread far from the kennel: sirens howl, storms howl, wind howls through gaps, amplifiers and audio feedback howl, and a crowd "howls" with laughter. The verb also names the act of weeping loudly and brokenly (to howl with grief), and the noun has bred the jocular howler, "a glaring, yowling blunder," and the informal phrase a howl, "an uproariously funny moment." The sense in Tolkien's The Hobbit, Chapter 2. The narrator interrupts his own narrative for a wink: Thorin, having leapt at the trolls with a burning branch, strikes the leading troll across the nose — "It made him howl, I can tell you." The aside is pure narrative relish. The troll, which has been conducting a domestic squabble in rustic English about whether to roast, mince or squash its prisoners, is abruptly reduced to the inarticulate animal register of a struck dog: it howls. The verb is chosen precisely because it strips the troll of its human pretensions. Trolls in Tolkien are hulking, hideous beings who have stolen human speech; howl reminds us that underneath the borrowed language there is merely appetite and brute sensation. The narrator's "I can tell you" adds mock-conspiratorial relish, inviting the reader to share the pleasure of the blow and the wolfish cry that follows it. Primary senses of howl
  • Verb (the card's core sense) — to utter a long, loud, doleful cry of pain, anger, grief or distress; of wolves, dogs, wind, and human beings.
  • Noun — such a cry itself; a long, wailing, open-mouthed sound (the howl of a wolf, of a gale, of a man in pain).
  • Of the wind and natural forces — to make a prolonged, wailing, mournful sound as it rushes or tears (the wind howled round the house).
  • Of things — to make a high-pitched, wailing noise: sirens, factory hooters, amplifiers and audio feedback all howl.
  • Of laughter and weeping — to laugh or cry loudly and uncontrollably (howl with laughter, howl with grief); a howl as an uproariously funny moment.
  • Derived nouns — howler: one who howls; also, from the 19th century, a glaring, absurd blunder; and howling, the sustained cry or noise.
Polysemy: how the senses developed
  • The core is purely imitative. The word descends from an Indo-European expressive base *kēu- / *ū- built directly on the sound of a long, open-mouthed cry — the same raw material as Latin ululāre, Greek ololyzein, Sanskrit ululīkāra, and Old English ūle "owl," named for its hollow hoot. The vowel is the sound; the word was born imitating its referent.
  • From the animal and wind cry, the human sense followed naturally: a person in extremity makes the same open-mouthed, wordless, prolonged sound, so from its first records the verb covered wolves, dogs, the wind, and grieving or anguished people together.
  • The extension to machines and things (sirens, storms, feedback) transfers the wind sense to any long, wailing, rising sound; the sound-pattern — a prolonged, rising, open vowel — is the constant.
  • The comic and emphatic senses (a howl of laughter, a howler as a blunder) grow from the idea of uncontrolled, noisy release: laughter that escapes control "howls" just as grief does, and a grotesque error is a "howler" because it makes one howl with laughter or despair.
Distinctions from near-synonyms Howl is best distinguished from its rivals. Scream and shriek are high-pitched, piercing and distinctly human or terrified; yell is loud and aggressive but still word-shaped; wail adds grief and weeping, plaintive and human; bellow and roar are deep, throaty and imposing; ululate is the technical or exotic word for ritual howling; cry is the general word for any emotional sound; and bay is the specific deep, prolonged cry of hounds and wolves in pursuit. Howl alone is the long, open-throated, wordless, doleful cry at the animal end of the scale — the sound that escapes control.
