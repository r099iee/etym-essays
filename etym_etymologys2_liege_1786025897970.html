Frankish *līthig (reconstructed)

"free, unencumbered" — Germanic adjective; compare
Middle Dutch ledich, Old High German ledig (free, idle);
the exact Frankish form behind Old French is not attested

↓

Old French lige / liege

"free, unconstrained, absolute" (11th–12th c.); in
feudal law: hommage lige "unqualified homage"

↓

Anglo-Norman lige / lyge

"entitled to full allegiance (of a lord); bound by
full allegiance (of a vassal)" (13th c.)

↓

Middle English lige / liege

"feudal lord entitled to fealty, or faithful vassal
bound by homage" (attested c. 1300)

↓

LIEGE

"sovereign lord entitled to absolute feudal allegiance;
loyal vassal; (adj.) paramount, bound by supreme fealty"

Note on the debate: the origin is disputed. The common view connects lige with the Germanic word for "free, unencumbered" (Middle Dutch ledich / Old High German ledig); an older theory derives it from Late Latin laeticus, from laeti, the Germanic settlers granted land in the Roman Empire; a third suggestion links it to Germanic *liud- "people." The exact Frankish form and the route into Old French are reconstructed, not attested.
