PIE *sak- 'to make sacred, to consecrate, to bind by ritual sanction' (traditional reconstruction, Pokorny; debated - see note) -> Latin sancire 'to consecrate, make sacred; to ratify, enact' (nasal-infix stem *sank- built on *sak-; attested from the 3rd c. BCE) -> Latin sanctus 'consecrated, holy, sacred' (past participle of sancire; also the noun 'saint') -> Latin sanctitas 'holiness, sacredness, inviolability' (abstract noun in -tas; 1st c. BCE) -> Old French saintete / learned sanctite 'holiness, sanctity' (via Late Latin and ecclesiastical Latin) -> Middle English sanctite 'holiness, sanctity' (14th c., c. 1380) -> English SANCTITY 'the state of being holy, sacred, or inviolable'.

Note on the debate: the step above Latin is disputed. The PIE root *sak- is the traditional link between sancire and sacer 'sacred', but de Vaan treats the pre-Latin history of the family as uncertain, and some scholars derive it from a root meaning 'to make binding, to establish an agreement' or even entertain a substrate origin. The chain from Latin sancire onward is solid; only the pre-Latin stage is open to question.
