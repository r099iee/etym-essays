- [Late 15th to 16th c. / Tudor Era] - Supernatural portents and celestial warnings: entered English via Late Latin (*monstrositas*) to describe unnatural biological births, conjoined twins, or strange animal mutations; perceived with religious dread as divine omens or celestial warnings of impending catastrophe or royal displeasure.
- [17th c. / Scientific Revolution] - Teratology and natural history: transformed into an object of empirical investigation in early medical and anatomical treatises; natural philosophers cataloged anatomical malformations not as demonic curses, but as fascinating lusus naturae (freaks of nature) testing the boundaries of species classification.
- [18th c. / Enlightenment] - Moral, rhetorical, and artistic condemnation: extended into literary criticism and moral philosophy; used by Enlightenment essayists like Swift and Pope to denounce grotesque moral depravities, chaotic rhetorical styles, or tyrannical political systems that defied classical harmony and reason.
- [19th c. / Victorian Era] - Industrial disquiet and architectural critique: applied with fervor to the sprawling, smoke-belching industrial machinery, Victorian circus 'freak shows', and bloated, eclectic architecture of the industrial metropolis, reflecting deep social anxiety over uncontrolled technological growth.
- [20th to 21st c. / Contemporary usage] - Architectural and cultural critique: widely established as a vivid, biting epithet for disastrous urban planning, colossal architectural eyesores, bureaucratic nightmares, or wildly miscalculated artistic endeavors, occasionally softened with ironic or humorous affection for absurdly complicated gadgets.
