Latin indulgēre

"to be kind to, forbear, yield to, grant, allow;
to give free play to a feeling or desire"
(attested from the 2nd c. BCE; Cicero, Caesar)

↓

Latin indulgentia

"kindness, forbearance, leniency; in the Empire,
an act of imperial clemency or remission of
taxes/penalties"
(attested 1st c. BCE – imperial period)

↓

Old French indulgence

"pardon, forgiveness, remission; favor"
(attested from the 12th–13th c.)

↓

Middle English indulgence

"remission of temporal punishment for sins,
granted by the Church"
(first recorded in English, late 14th c.)

↓

Early Modern English indulgence

"ecclesiastical remission; then also leniency,
yielding to desire, pampering"
(15th–17th c.; secular senses broaden)

↓

INDULGENCE

"yielding to desire or pleasure; leniency toward
others; ecclesiastical remission of temporal
punishment; a luxury treat"
(Modern English)

Note on the deeper origin: the derivation of Latin indulgēre
is UNKNOWN and debated. It is traditionally analyzed as
in- "in, toward" + a verbal base *dulgēre of uncertain origin.
Proposals include a link to a PIE root meaning "long"
(cf. Greek dolikhós "long", via a sense "to put off, be
patient") or to Old Irish dlongaid "to cleave" — none is
accepted. The medieval association with Latin dulcis
"sweet" is a folk etymology and is rejected. Because no
secure PIE etymon exists, the chain above begins at the
earliest firmly attested stage, Classical Latin.
