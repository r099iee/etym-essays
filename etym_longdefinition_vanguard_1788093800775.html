Vanguard names the foremost part of a moving military force - the division that marches or sails ahead of the main body - and, by a long and vigorous figurative extension, any person or group at the leading edge of a cause, movement, science, or fashion. It is one of the oldest and most productive of the military metaphors that English has naturalised from French.

1. The military sense.

  • Armies. In traditional march order an army advanced as vanguard (or van), main body, and rearguard: the vanguard - often the cavalry or light troops - moved first, scouting the road, seizing crossings, making first contact with the enemy, and covering the deployment of the rest. Command of the vanguard was prized in medieval and early modern warfare as the post of honour and of danger.
  • Fleets. The word was early extended to naval formations: the vanguard of a fleet is the squadron that sails in the van, meeting the enemy first in line of battle. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century admirals' instructions speak of the "vanguard" and "rearward" of a fleet as routinely as generals spoke of armies.

2. The figurative sense: the forefront of anything.

  • From the sixteenth century onward the word began to travel from camps to causes: reformers, scholars, prophets, and crusaders could be "the vanguard" of a movement for change. Today one speaks of the vanguard of science, of medicine, of technology, of public opinion, of fashion - the group furthest forward, whose position defines where the rest are heading.
  • Political vanguardism. In socialist theory the term took precise institutional form: Lenin's doctrine of the "vanguard party" (developed from 1902) held that a disciplined minority of professional revolutionaries must lead the working class. "Vanguardism" still describes theories of revolutionary leadership across the twentieth century's communist and national-liberation movements.
  • Artistic vanguard. In the nineteenth century the French phrase avant-garde was reapplied to artists and writers who led cultural change - radical, original, ahead of public taste. English re-borrowed the phrase as avant-garde in the later nineteenth century, creating a famous doublet: vanguard (the old military borrowing, now chiefly political, scientific, and technological) and avant-garde (the newer borrowing, chiefly artistic).

3. Position versus persons.

  • Vanguard is used both for the position (the front line of advance: "in the vanguard of progress") and for the people occupying it (the vanguard as a body of troops or a group of leaders). This easy oscillation between place and persons is the key to its figurative success: being "in the vanguard" is a place; the "vanguard of a generation" is a people.

4. Form and relatives.

  • Van is a seventeenth-century clipping of the same word ("to lead the van"), still common in set phrases and military usage; advance guard is the plainer modern synonym in drill manuals; avant-garde is the artistic doublet; and vanguard also survives as a brand name - most famously the investment firm The Vanguard Group, founded 1975, which chose the name precisely for its connotation of leading, disciplined, pioneering finance.
  • In the sentence of this card - the goblin host streaming into Dale in Tolkien's The Hobbit - the word bears its full military weight: "Ere long the vanguard swirled round the spur's end and came rushing into Dale": the leading wave of the enemy army outruns the main host and strikes first.

5. Semantic bridge in one line.

  • The whole modern life of the word rests on a single transfer: what is true of the front of an army - it arrives first, it meets danger first, and everyone else follows it - is true of the front of any human enterprise. To be in the vanguard is to be where the future is first encountered.
