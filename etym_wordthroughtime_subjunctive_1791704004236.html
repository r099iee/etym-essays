- Late 16th c. — Renaissance grammatical importation: adopted from Latin subiunctīvus into Early Modern English grammar treatises to describe synthetic dependent verb forms expressing doubt, condition, and contingency.
- 17th to 18th c. — Prescriptive pillar of correctness: celebrated by grammarians like Robert Lowth and Lindley Murray as an essential hallmark of disciplined, refined, and logical English prose, resisting the colloquial decay of distinct inflections.
- 19th c. — Stylistic marker of erudition & literary elevation: Victorian authors consciously preserved the were-subjunctive and mandative bare forms to signal intellectual gravity, even as spoken dialects increasingly substituted indicative forms.
- Early to Mid-20th c. — Transatlantic syntactic divergence: British English witnessed a pronounced retreat of the mandative subjunctive in favor of modal should or plain indicative, while American English vigorously preserved and revived the mandative subjunctive in formal and journalistic writing.
- Late 20th to 21st c. — Conceptual metaphor & philosophical trope: expanded beyond formal syntactic debates into literary theory, anthropology, and cognitive science to denote 'subjunctive spaces', counterfactual imagination, and alternative realities.
