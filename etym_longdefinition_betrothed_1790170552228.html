Betrothed means engaged to be married — formally promised to another person as a future husband or wife — used both as an adjective ("they are betrothed") and, very commonly, as a noun meaning the person to whom one is engaged ("his betrothed", "her betrothed"). Tuppy Glossop, Angela's __________, is Angela's fiancé: the young man publicly pledged to marry her, whose expected presence at the party follows from that recognised bond. The tone is formal, slightly old-fashioned, and respectable, exactly the diction of announcements, church registers, and Wodehouse's gently mocking upper-class narration.

Core sense — formal engagement: To be betrothed is to have exchanged a solemn promise of marriage, whether by private proposal accepted, by public announcement, by church betrothal rite, or historically by a legally binding contract. It is stronger than dating or going steady and more formal than being engaged: it implies troth-plighting, witnesses, families informed, and an expectation the wedding will follow. Example: "After Christmas they were betrothed" means the engagement is settled and socially recognised.

  • Betrothed as noun — the promised person: "his betrothed" = his future wife; "her betrothed" = her future husband. Unlike French fiancé / fiancée, English betrothed does not mark gender: context alone shows whether it is the man or the woman. Example: "He introduced his betrothed to his parents." Solemn, literary, journalistic register.
  • Betrothed as adjective / participle — the state: "the betrothed couple", "a betrothed virgin", "they remained betrothed for two years". Here it describes the condition of being bound by promise. Example: "Betrothed lovers in wartime wrote daily."
  • Religious and legal betrothal: in many churches (Orthodox, Catholic, Anglican) betrothal or espousal is a distinct liturgical blessing before the wedding; in older English law and in biblical law it created enforceable rights, so that a betrothed virgin was already treated as a wife in fidelity. Example: Mary's betrothal to Joseph in the Gospels. The bridge is that a vow before God and witnesses was itself quasi-marital.
  • Extended and comic use: by hyperbole, betrothed can describe anything solemnly wedded to another — "betrothed to his work", "the party betrothed to reform" — and Wodehouse uses it ironically for on-off comic engagements that never quite reach the altar. The humour comes from applying a grave sacramental word to feather-headed flappers and feckless drones.

Connotations, register, and boundaries: Betrothed is formal, dignified, and faintly archaic; everyday speech prefers engaged or fiancé(e). It suggests intention, fidelity, public commitment, and a waiting interval, not yet cohabitation or marriage. It does not mean merely in love, merely promised informally, or already married: the wedding is still future, but honour binds. To break a betrothal was historically disgraceful and sometimes actionable as breach of promise.
