- 8th to 11th Century (Old English): As a noun, *pæll* (derived from Latin *pallium*) designated fine, costly, royal or ecclesiastical purple silk cloth, used for regal robes, altar hangings, and ceremonial cloaks.
- 13th to 14th Century (Middle English): The noun specialized into a ceremonial funeral cloth draped over a coffin (*pall*). Simultaneously, the verb *apallen* (borrowed from Old French *apalir*, meaning to become pale, fade, or lose strength) underwent aphesis to become the Middle English verb *pallen*, meaning to lose flavor, become flat (especially of wine, ale, or cider), or lose vitality.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English): The verb expanded from physical beverages losing their effervescence to psychological, sensory, and social experiences—describing how pleasures, jokes, courtly amusements, and luxuries become tedious and tasteless upon repetition.
- 18th to 19th Century: The noun developed its ubiquitous figurative sense in English literature and journalism, denoting an oppressive, dark, suffocating emotional or physical blanket ("a pall of smoke", "a pall of silence", "cast a pall of gloom").
- 20th to 21st Century: Preserved in sophisticated literary prose, political journalism, and psychological commentary ("the novelty quickly began to pall", "the scandal cast a pall over the election").
