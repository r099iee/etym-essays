- 17th Century (France) - French *prude* (from Old French *prode*, "virtuous, wise woman") originally had an honorable meaning ("discreet, noble woman"), but in the 17th-century French court (Molière's *Le Misanthrope*, 1666), *pruderie* shifted into a satirical term for women who affected exaggerated moral squeamishness to conceal their own vices.
- Early 18th Century (Augustan England) - Borrowed into English (c. 1704–1710, Addison, Steele in *The Spectator*) as *prudery*, contrasting the "prude" with the "coquette" as two opposing caricatures of female social performance.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Solidified in social commentary to describe institutionalized, bourgeois anti-sensual censorship, euphemistic speech (avoiding the word "leg" for "limb"), and the sanitization of literature.
- 20th Century - Re-theorized by Freudian psychoanalysis and modern literary critics (Roland Barthes, Susan Sontag) as an ideological defense mechanism: the intellectual "prudery" that fears aesthetic ecstasy and bliss in texts.
- 21st Century - Applied in cultural critiques to describe both traditional religious modesty policing and contemporary secular hyper-sensitivities around speech, art, and bodily representation.
