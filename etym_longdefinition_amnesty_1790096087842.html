amnesty is a noun and occasionally a verb denoting an official, typically collective act by a sovereign authority that forgives offences, especially political offences, and treats them as legally forgotten. In its strictest sense it is not mere mercy toward one person but a general oblivion: a class of offenders is released from liability, punishment is remitted, and the state undertakes not to remember or prosecute the acts. Your context — The Council offers amnesty plus cash for documents — is classic: a political body trading oblivion for compliance.

The core legal sense must be distinguished from neighbouring concepts. A pardon forgives penalty but may leave guilt on record; amnesty buries both penalty and memory of guilt. Clemency is the virtue; amnesty is its institutional form. Expungement is judicial; amnesty is legislative or executive and usually collective. In international humanitarian law, amnesty for participation in hostilities is encouraged at end of non-international armed conflict, but amnesty for war crimes and crimes against humanity is now widely held impermissible.

  • General political amnesty: A law or decree covering rebellion, sedition, desertion, illegal assembly, or conscientious dissent, e.g. post-civil-war amnesty, amnesty for draft evaders. It restores civil rights and often allows return from exile.
  • Human-rights amnesty: Release of prisoners of conscience, as campaigned for by Amnesty International. Here amnesty means freedom for those who should never have been criminalised.
  • Fiscal / administrative amnesty: Tax amnesty, immigration amnesty, library amnesty — a limited window where penalties are waived if the underlying obligation is met. This is metaphorical extension preserving the structure of oblivion in exchange for compliance.

Secondary and figurative uses are productive. As a verb, to amnesty means to grant amnesty to, e.g. they were amnestied in 1990. As an attributive, amnesty law, amnesty deal, amnesty programme. In ordinary speech, give me amnesty can mean overlook my fault, grant me a fresh start, as in amnesty for late homework. The word always carries formality and downward grace from authority; one does not amnesty equals.

  • Countability and collocation: Usually countable — an amnesty, amnesties — but also uncountable in grant amnesty, under amnesty. Strong collocations: declare / proclaim / offer / grant amnesty; general / unconditional / blanket / partial amnesty; amnesty for rebels, for deserters, for illegal migrants.
  • Register and connotation: Formal, legal-political, solemn and hopeful when granted to victims of oppression, cynical or controversial when granted to perpetrators. Blanket amnesty often pejorative, implying impunity; conditional amnesty implies truth-telling in return, as in South Africa.
  • Semantic bridge: All senses descend from Greek amnestia, forgetfulness. The bridge is institutionalised forgetting: just as personal amnesia loses memory, political amnesty orders the law to lose memory. Tax and library uses keep the bargain structure — pay / return now and we forget lateness — showing how oblivion became forgiveness became fresh start.

Do not confuse with amnesia. Amnesia is involuntary medical loss of memory; amnesty is voluntary legal ordering of oblivion. The similarity is etymological, not accidental, and makes a perfect mnemonic, but they are not interchangeable. Amnesty International's name exploits the moral glow of the word while reminding that prisoners of conscience need oblivion of unjust laws, not treatment.
