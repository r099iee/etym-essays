The participle snivelling drips from one of the noisiest corners of the Germanic lexicon: the imitative sn- cluster that mimics every sound the nose makes. Sniff, snuff, snort, sneeze, snot, and snivel all turn on the same snarl of consonants, formed not by grand sound-laws but by speakers copying the body. Such words are notoriously hard to trace to single Indo-European ancestors, since every language invents its own nose-noises afresh, and honest etymology treats them as a family resemblance rather than a pedigree.

Middle English mucus and tears: by the fourteenth century snivelen or snevelen was established for running at the nose, whether from winter cold or from weeping, and the two causes were never separated because the symptom is identical. Medieval medicine, which read character in humours and rheums, found the snivelling nose eloquent: a cold proved phlegm, and phlegm proved a weak watery nature. When moralists needed a verb for puling repentance that might be mere drip, snivel lay ready, and sermons warned against snivelling devotion that wept on Sundays and cheated on Mondays.

Candles that wept: one of the word's oddest lodgings was the chandlery. From the sixteenth century a candle whose wick guttered and spilled hot tallow was said to snivel, weeping wax as eyes weep tears, and housekeepers complained of snivelling candles that spoiled cloths and tempers. The technical sense died with tallow and gaslight, but it left a fossil record proving how naturally English mapped nose to flame whenever anything dripped where it should burn clearly.

  • The hypocrite's badge (17th c.): Restoration satirists fixed snivelling onto canting sectaries and new converts, whose piety allegedly flowed with their rheum. The stereotype, unfair as caricature always is, gave the participle its permanent moral stain: to snivel is not just to drip but to perform.
  • Schoolroom and workhouse (19th c.): Victorian school fiction made the snivelling sneak the lowest rung of boy society, while Dickens dealt out snivelling clerks and beadles by the dozen, sometimes for pity and sometimes for the rod. The classroom sealed the contempt that sermons had begun.
  • Gollum's apotheosis (20th c.): Tolkien distilled centuries of disgust into a single balanced phrase, much snivelling and snarling, the worm and the wolf alternating. No later use can quite escape that pairing, which remains the locus classicus taught in essays on the character.

Kin and echoes: Dutch snuffelen and snotteren, German schnüffeln and Schnupfen, all sniff around the same barnyard of nose-words, each language stirring its own spelling of the sniff. English sniffle itself is a frequentative younger cousin, the repeated small sniffs to snivel's continuous drip, and the two have divided the labour ever since: sniffles for colds, snivels for contempt.

Modern standing: today snivelling keeps one clinical foot and one jeering foot: the pharmacist sympathises with your snivelling cold while the columnist denounces snivelling excuses. Spelling keeps the double l in British English against American sniveling, a small transatlantic sniff of difference. Wherever tears are thought strategic rather than sincere, the old nose-word waits, ready to convict.
