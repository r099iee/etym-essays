The verb reap is one of the few English words still smelling of the straw it was born in. Reconstructed Proto-Germanic *rīpan-, to cut grain, gave Old English rīpan in the south and reopan in Mercia, the everyday verb of the harvest months when survival depended on cutting corn dry and carrying it safe. It belonged to a tight native family of ripeness: Old English rīpe, mature and ready for harvest, describes exactly the state that calls the reaper to the field, and scholars have long suspected the two share one harvest root, though no deeper Indo-European source commands agreement, and honest dictionaries print the family tree with its topmost branch fading into mist.

Through Middle English repen the word worked the feudal calendar: villeins owed their lords boon-days of reaping, harvest-home feasts closed the labour with ale and song, and the sickle remained the universal tool — a technology unchanged since Rome. The Reformation gave the verb its second, moral life: Tyndale and then the King James translators rendered Saint Paul as whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap, planting the harvest metaphor of consequences so deep in English that four centuries of speakers use it who never saw a sheaf. Parallel with this ran the great allegory of death as harvest: medieval wall paintings showed the scythe sweeping kings and ploughmen alike, and by the seventeenth century the Reaper, later fixed as the Grim Reaper, needed no explanation.

Sound change barely touched so useful a word: the long vowel glided from ree-pan toward modern reep in the Great Vowel Shift, the spelling steadied early, and the strong-verb past reop vanished in favour of regular reaped. What changed was the field itself. Cyrus McCormick's mechanical reaper of the 1830s, then the binder, then the combine harvester, took the sickle gangs — the twelve hay-cutters of the source sentence with their rakes behind — off the land within a lifetime, leaving reap literal work to machines and its figurative work to everyone.

Shifts and survivals:

  • Act to consequence: the founding metaphor turns farming into ethics — sowing and reaping become the Bible's accounting system for good and evil, quoted in courts, classrooms, and cartoons.
  • Harvest to death: the Reaper allegory converts the cornfield into the churchyard, the scythe harvesting souls with terrible even-handedness.
  • Sheaf to dividend: commercial English reaps profits, benefits, and synergies, the boardroom borrowing the barn's vocabulary for bloodless gains.
  • Sickle to machine: inventions from horse-drawn reapers to satellite-guided combines kept the verb while abolishing the labour it named, so modern children reap only metaphorically.
  • Spell constancy: four centuries of reap spelled identically shows a word too central to respell, its four letters carrying harvest, morality, and mortality at once.

From Ruth gleaning after the reapers to the wonder-tale meadow where twelve cutters swing as one, reap has watched English civilisation eat by its favour. Hosea's whirlwind proverb still warns, headline writers still promise rich harvests, and every August the combines roll — the ancient verb steering new iron through old wheat.
