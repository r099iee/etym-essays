Pièce de résistance is a French borrowing meaning the best, most important, or most remarkable part of something — originally and literally the main dish of a meal (the roast, the substantial joint after the soups and entrées), and by extension the showpiece, masterpiece, or crowning event of any series: the pièce de résistance of a collection, an exhibition, a career, a menu. In the source passage it caps a catalogue of primitive delicacies: shellfish through reptiles’ eggs, any one of which “was somewhere a delicacy, or even a __________,” i.e. the pride of some tribe’s table. The phrase lends mock-ceremonious grandeur to lice and locusts — Durant’s joke depends on the reader hearing fine-dining French applied to grubs.

Pronunciation in English hovers between near-French (/pjɛs də rezistans/, say pee-ESS duh ray-zeess-TONSS) and fully anglicised forms; italics are customary but optional in modern style (the phrase is naturalised enough for roman type in journalism). It is invariable in the French manner: the plural, when needed, is pièces de résistance, though English writers usually dodge the plural entirely (“the showpieces”). Grammatically it behaves as a singular noun phrase taking ordinary determiners: “the pièce de résistance,” “her pièce de résistance.”

  • Culinary core: the substantial principal dish — historically the roast joint, today any signature main (“the chef’s pièce de résistance is the duck”). Note the false friend: it is NOT the starter, the dessert, or the amuse-bouche; it is the weight-bearing centre of the meal.
  • Figurative extension (dominant in English): the outstanding item in any set — “the pièce de résistance of the auction was the lost Vermeer”; “his pièce de résistance was the final bridge scene.” Slightly formal, faintly playful; signals connoisseurship.
  • Ironic/burlesque use (the passage’s use): conferring grand status on something low or absurd — “lice were their pièce de résistance.” The irony only works because the phrase’s prestige is intact.
  • Confusables: pièce de résistance (main thing) vs. pièce d’occasion (occasional piece, minor work) vs. tour de force (feat of skill — stresses performance, not centrality) vs. chef-d’œuvre/masterpiece (stresses making, not serving). A Vermeer can be all three at once; a roast cannot be a tour de force.
  • Register and attitude: literate-middle to high; food writing, art criticism, auction catalogues, wedding speeches. Used straight it flatters (“your garden is the pièce de résistance of the street”); used archly it teases. Overuse in menus (“our famous pièce de résistance burger”) tips into parody — the phrase polices its own dignity.
  • Polysemy bridge: meal → any ordered series conceived AS a meal (exhibition as courses, career as courses, the “main course” metaphor does the work). English keeps both the literal restaurant sense and the transferred sense alive side by side, which is why the ironic use lands: the reader tastes the roast even when the subject is lice.

Orthography: pièce with grave accent, résistance with acute; English texts increasingly drop the accents (piece de resistance) without penalty, but accented Capitals of respect cost nothing. Never write “resistence”; the French noun keeps its -a-. And resist the folk-etymology that it means “piece of resistance” in the political sense — the résistance here is staying power: the dish that ‘holds its ground’ as the meal’s centre, not a partisan uprising.
