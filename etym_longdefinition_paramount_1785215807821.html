PARAMOUNT (adjective, also noun) means more important than anything else; supreme, dominant, or preeminent in rank, authority, or importance. The core idea is hierarchical priority: something paramount stands above all other considerations and governs them. Three related senses must be distinguished: (1) of greatest importance or priority (the modern dominant sense) — "safety is of paramount importance," "the paramount objective," "a paramount concern"; (2) supreme in rank or authority (the older, more explicitly hierarchical sense) — "the paramount power," "the paramount chief," "paramount authority"; and (3) the legal-historical sense — in feudal law, a "lord paramount" was the supreme lord of a tenancy, the tenant-in-chief who held land directly from the Crown, above all sub-tenants. From this legal usage derive the modern noun paramountcy, "supreme authority or predominance," used in constitutional law of the supremacy of one legislature, law, or interest over others, and the adjective use in fixed collocations of administrative and political English.

In the source context — Mill's Autobiography, where "the only actual revolution which has ever taken place in my modes of thinking was already complete" before another person's influence "became paramount" — the word registers the exact point at which an influence ceases to be merely important and becomes supreme, the governing force to which every other consideration yields. The nuance is one of hierarchical priority: paramount does not mean simply "great" but "above all others in rank or authority," so Mill claims that Harriet Taylor's influence occupied the very apex of the forces that shaped his intellectual life, outranking even the completed revolution in his thought. The tone is measured, retrospective, and quietly complimentary — the formal language of a Victorian intellectual assigning the first place among influences.

Full polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Of overriding importance (dominant modern sense): first in importance, more important than anything else; "of paramount importance," "paramount concern," "paramount duty," "paramount interest." Purely evaluative and logical, with no necessary relation to power.
  • Supreme in rank or authority: highest in position or power within a hierarchy; "paramount authority," "paramount power," "the paramount chief." Retains the feudal image of the apex of a ladder.
  • Feudal legal sense (historical): "lord paramount," the supreme tenant-in-chief holding land directly from the Crown; "seigneur paramount" in Anglo-Norman law. The historical source of the whole word.
  • Constitutional sense: paramountcy, the supremacy of one law, legislature, or interest over another; e.g. "the paramountcy of Parliament," "paramount legislation."
  • Extended / commercial uses: the proprietary name and institutions of Paramount Pictures, and the mountain-gate logo that trades on the idea of being "above all," deliberately echo the word's meaning.

Derived forms and collocations: the noun paramountcy (supreme authority; in constitutional law, the primacy of one jurisdiction over another, especially in colonial and dominion history), the rare noun paramountness, the adverb paramountly. Fixed collocations: "of paramount importance," "paramount concern," "paramount duty," "paramount interest," "paramount authority," "paramount objective," "lord paramount."

Register: the word is formal, institutional, and administrative — the language of law, government, military command, and careful intellectual prose. It is distinguished from supreme, preeminent, dominant, and sovereign by its focus on governing priority: a matter can be paramount in importance without being supreme in power, dominant in action, or sovereign in law.
