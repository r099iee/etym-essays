Pallid is an adjective meaning "very pale, wan, lacking colour" — noticeably, often strikingly white or washed-out in complexion, in light, or in quality. It describes faces drained of blood by fear, illness, shock or exhaustion; light that is weak, dim and colourless; and, by extension, anything feeble, faint or lacking vitality — a performance, an argument, a style, an imitation. In the source passage, a mysterious light "grew to a little globe of pallid light": a weak, colourless, eerie glow, pale to the point of being sickly.

Primary senses

  • Of a person or face — unusually pale: "a pallid face", "she looked pallid after the long illness". This implies more than ordinary paleness of skin: it names a bloodless, unhealthy whiteness that a doctor would notice and a friend would worry about.
  • Of light or colour — weak and dim: "pallid light", "a pallid sun", "a pallid blue". Light or colour so faint it seems washed-out, greyish and drained of its natural intensity — not bright white but lifeless white.
  • Of writing, art, ideas or people — lacking force: "a pallid performance", "pallid prose", "a pallid imitation". The figurative sense: feeble, dull, bland, lacking vigour, interest or intensity. To call something pallid in this sense is always a criticism.

Secondary senses and nuances

  • Clinical: Pallor is the medical term for abnormal paleness of the skin — often a sign of anaemia, shock, cold, fear or poor circulation. Pallid is the everyday adjectival partner of pallor, and keeps a faintly clinical exactness.
  • Emotional register: A pallid face tells a story — fear, grief, illness, exhaustion, terror. Writers exploit this: pallid characters are frightened, haunted or unwell, their whiteness an outward sign of inner collapse.
  • Art criticism: In writing about painting, photography or cinema, pallid describes weak, desaturated, low-contrast tones; a work can be "pallid" for lacking depth of colour and light.
  • Pejorative figurative force: Applied to work, ideas, arguments or performances, pallid means uninspired, timid, bloodless, without conviction — the intellectual cousin of "bland" and "feeble", but with the built-in image of washed-out paleness.

Register and nuance

  • Formality: Pallid is more elevated and literary than "pale"; it belongs to written prose, literary description, criticism and clinical language, and carries a precise, Latinate feel.
  • Degree: Pallid means paler than pale — it marks a striking, noticeable, often unhealthy lack of colour, not a fair or light complexion.
  • Opposites: flushed, rosy, ruddy, vivid, vibrant, glowing, luminous, brilliant.

Word family and relatives

  • From Latin pallere "to be pale": pallor (noun, "paleness"), pallid (adjective), and the rarer pallidly (adverb) and pallidness (noun).
  • Doublet: the word "pale" itself comes (via Old French) from the same Latin pallidus; so pallid and pale are doublets — the same Latin adjective borrowed twice by different routes.
  • Distant relatives from the same Indo-European root: Greek polios "grey, hoary", Old English fealu giving "fallow" (pale brown/yellow), and Sanskrit palita- "grey".
  • Do not confuse with the coffin cloth "pall" (from Latin pallium, "cloak"), nor with the verb "to pall" (to become dull) — although that verb, from Latin pallere via the sense of losing colour, is indeed a cousin.
