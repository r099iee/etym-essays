STUFFY (adjective) primarily describes a room, space, or atmosphere that is lacking in fresh air — ill-ventilated, close, oppressive, stale, and uncomfortably warm or thick with the smell of enclosure. A stuffy room feels "stopped up": the air does not move, so it becomes heavy, humid, or odorous, and breathing it is mildly unpleasant. The word's literal, historical core is the sense of being stuffed, crammed, or plugged — an enclosed space so filled or sealed that fresh air cannot circulate. This physical sense is the root of every later use.

In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 4, the word does concentrated atmospheric work: "the way went down and down, and it was most horribly STUFFY." The captured company is being carried into the goblins' tunnels beneath the Misty Mountains, and "most horribly" loads the adjective with revulsion — this is not mere discomfort but a foretaste of suffocating, reeking, underground darkness, cut off from the wind and sun of the world above. Tolkien's "stuffy" is the homely, childlike word that makes the horror physical and immediate: the reader feels the dead air on their own skin, and the ordinary domestic word makes the nightmare more vivid, not less.

  • Polysemy 1 — ill-ventilated, close (air of a room or place): the core modern sense, recorded from the early 19th century; a stuffy room, attic, or hall is one whose air is stale and oppressive.
  • Polysemy 2 — congested nasal passages: a "stuffy nose" or "stuffy head" is blocked and congested, as in a cold or allergy. The bridge is the same image of plugging: the sinuses are "stopped up" exactly as a room's air is "stopped up."
  • Polysemy 3 — prim, formal, rigidly conventional (of persons, institutions, manners): since the mid-19th century, a "stuffy person" is pompous, strait-laced, old-fashioned, or narrow-minded — someone stiff, formal, and resistant to change; "a stuffy institution" is tradition-bound and humorless. The bridge is metaphorical: the person is mentally and socially "closed up," sealed against fresh ideas the way a room is sealed against fresh air.
  • Polysemy 4 — dull, boring, full of an unpleasantly heavy seriousness: "stuffy" meetings, lectures, or events are tedious and over-formal; this is a close relative of sense 3, extending the "closed, heavy, stale" image from persons to occasions and discourse.
  • Polysemy 5 — historical literal sense (now obsolete): in the 16th century, stuffy meant simply "full of stuff, substantial, material" — a neutral or positive word (e.g., a stuffy meal, a well-stuffed cushion); only later did the "crammed so as to be sealed" implication take over. The modern senses rest on this older literal base.
  • Polysemy 6 — dialectal extensions: in some English dialects stuffy has meant angry, sulky, or out of sorts (a "stuffy temper"), again by the image of being closed and pent-up; and in dialect it has meant drunk or "full" in a coarse physical way.

Why the secondary meanings developed: the entire polysemy of stuffy radiates from the single image of stuffing — cramming and plugging until nothing can get in or out. A room stuffed with stale air is airless; a nose stuffed with congestion is blocked; a person stuffed with self-importance is pompous and closed to fresh ideas; a mind stuffed with convention is narrow. Every modern sense is the same physical picture transferred to a different domain, which is why the word remains so consistent and vivid.

Distinctions from near-synonyms: close is the older, subtler word for dead, unventilated air; airless simply states the absence of circulation; stifling and suffocating are a degree stronger, suggesting actual difficulty in breathing; sultry and muggy add heat and humidity; musty and fusty add the odor of damp, mould, and age; oppressive widens the discomfort to moral or emotional weight; and for the person-sense, prim, strait-laced, pompous, and stiff all point at the formality without the airless metaphor that stuffy carries.
