The evolution of apex is unusually conservative: a Latin word for a priest's pointed cap enters English almost unchanged in form and climbs from ritual headgear to geometry to empire to ecology without ever losing its point. Its obscurity of deeper origin only heightened its prestige — an unanalysable Latin remnant that sounds sharp because it is sharp.

  • Latin apex: from cap to tip to eminence. Classical Latin apex, genitive apicis, meant the pointed tip or extremity of anything, but specifically the conical tufted cap worn by flamines and Salian priests, made of olive wood with wool thread wound to a point. By metonymy apex came to mean any pointed prominence, crest, summit, and figuratively the highest honour or crown of a thing. Virgil writes of apices of flames, Cicero of apex of dignity. Old Latin form may have been *apets; etymology is disputed. De Vaan marks apex as of unknown origin, possibly from *h₂ep- to take, seize via sense of projection that is grasped, or Etruscan loan connected with priestly apparel; no PIE root can be securely assigned. That obscurity matters: unlike capere-words with clear pedigrees, apex stood isolated, a ritual object turned abstraction.
  • Borrowing into English (late 16th–early 17th c.). English antiquaries and translators rendering Latin histories needed apex for both the flamen's cap and the summit sense. First English attestations c. 1601–1611 denote top, tip, point of a cone or pyramid. Pronunciation anglicized the Latin long a to diphthong /ˈeɪpɛks/, plural learned as apices /ˈeɪpɪsiːz/ on Latin model. Spelling stayed apex; the x advertised Latinity as z or ks never could. Early use is scientific and geometric: apex of a parabola, apex of the heart in anatomical Latin carried over intact.
  • Enlightenment generalization: summit of glory (18th c.). Augustan writers seize the figurative Latin sense for moral and imperial climax: apex of virtue, apex of power, apex of human reason. Edward Gibbon can write of empire at its apex without explaining the metaphor because readers know pyramids. Dictionaries define apex as top, height, tip, acme. The triangular-continent use belongs to this settled geometric sense popularized by travel narratives and imaginary voyages: continents, islands and fortresses figured as triangles, diamonds, crescents, each with an apex where treasure or city lies.
  • 19th-century science: tooth-roots, leaves, light, sun. Botany systematizes apex of leaf, stem, root and apical growth; dentistry apex of fang; astronomy Sir William Herschel's solar apex c. 1783–1805 names the point toward which the sun moves through stars in Hercules, later refined to apex of solar motion. Phonetics begins apical for tongue-tip sounds. Each coinage exploits a different facet — plant apex grows, tooth apex anchors, solar apex beckons — but all keep pointedness as orienting function. The word becomes indispensable precisely because tip, top and point are each too vague alone.
  • 20th–21st century: hierarchy, ecology, brand. Sociology and management speak of apex of hierarchy, apex court for supreme court, apex organization. Ecology after 1950s trophic-pyramid diagrams creates apex predator, now a household phrase for sharks, wolves, lions — and metaphorically for corporations and athletes. Physics retains apex of trajectory. Commerce discovers Apex as trademark for tools, hotels, games, suggesting nothing higher. Pronunciation splits only in plural: careful /ˈeɪpɪsiːz/ versus regularized /ˈeɪpɛksɪz/. The source sentence's triangular continent with city at its apex thus sounds at once archaic-voyage and modern-strategy: a geometric point that is also a seat of power.

Phonetically apex barely moved in four centuries; semantically it only rose. From wool wound to a point on a priest's head to the vertex that orients a continent and the predator that crowns a food web, it retained the same logic: where lines converge to a point, power converges too. To call something the apex remains to say it is both sharp and supreme — the place where shape ends and dominion begins.
