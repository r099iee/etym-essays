The phrase BY THE WAY is built from three independent root words, BY, THE, and WAY, each with its own chain, followed by the coalescence of the phrase. Chain 1 — BY: 1. Proto-Indo-European *ambʰi, "around, on both sides, about" (also reconstructed as *h₂embʰi), a prepositional/adverbial root; it survives in Latin ambi- "both, around" (whence English ambient, ambition) and in Greek amphí, "on both sides." 2. Proto-Germanic *bi, "by, near, around," a weakly stressed by-form of *umbi "around, about" (cf. Old English ymbe "around, about," still visible in the place-name element), formed by reduction of the initial vowel of the same PIE root. 3. Old English be, bī, "by, near, alongside, at" (attested from the earliest Old English texts, c. 8th–10th c.), the preposition used with the dative for position beside something. 4. Middle English bi, by, "by, beside, near" (12th–15th c.). 5. Modern English by, "by, beside, along," unchanged; the final element of the phrase BY THE WAY. Chain 2 — THE: 1. Proto-Indo-European *so / *to- (neuter *tód), "that," the demonstrative pronoun stem. 2. Proto-Germanic *sa (masculine), *þat (neuter), "that," the demonstrative; the þ-forms (from PIE *to-) supply the modern article. 3. Old English sē (masculine), sēo (feminine), þæt (neuter), "the, that"; by the late Old English period the þ-forms of the paradigm (þæt, þām, þǣre) are increasingly used for the article regardless of the older s-forms. 4. Middle English þe, the, "the" (12th–15th c.), the definite article, with the þ-forms generalized. 5. Modern English the (spelled th-), the definite article; the element "the" of BY THE WAY. Chain 3 — WAY: 1. Proto-Indo-European *weǵʰ-, "to travel, go, convey in a vehicle" (also *u̯eǵʰ-), a verbal root of motion; it is the source of Latin vehō "I carry, convey" (English vehicle, vector) and Greek ókhos, "wagon, carriage." 2. Proto-Germanic *wegaz, "way, path, road" (attested in all branches), a nominal formation from the verbal root *weǵ- with the sense "a course travelled." 3. Old English weg, "way, path, road, journey" (c. 8th–10th c.), masculine noun. 4. Middle English wei, way, "way, road, course" (12th–15th c.). 5. Modern English way, "way, path, method"; the head noun of BY THE WAY. Phrase chain — coalescence: 1. Old English "be þæm wege" (also "be wege"), "along the path, by the way, en route" (attested in Old English prose; dative wege of weg with the article þæm). 2. Middle English "bi the weye," "by the way, along the road" (c. 1200, Layamon's Brut and later texts), literal spatial sense; already used of remarks made while travelling. 3. Early Modern English "by the way," "incidentally, in passing" (figurative transfer attested by the 16th c., e.g. in dialogue of the period), the "beside the road" image applied to discourse. 4. Modern English BY THE WAY (17th c. onward), a fully grammaticalized discourse marker for digressions and asides; the literal sense survives only in literary and archaizing use. Note on the debated stage: the reduction of PIE *ambʰi to Germanic *bi (rather than *umbi) is the standard account (Kroonen, Pokorny) but the weak-grade by-form *bi is occasionally reconstructed as an independent PIE *bhi; the overall trajectory from a PIE prepositional stem to modern by is not in doubt. The coalescence of the phrase into a discourse marker is a matter of recorded English literary history, not reconstruction.
