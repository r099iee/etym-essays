• wandering: Similar because both mean "moving without a fixed course"; differs because "wandering" is neutral, pastoral, and physical, while "errant" implies a deviation from a proper course or boundary that was supposed to be kept.
  • roving: Similar because both mean "travelling about freely"; differs because "roving" is deliberately adventurous or restless without moral weight, while "errant" can carry the sense of straying or going wrong.
  • stray: Similar because both mean "having left the proper place or course"; differs because "stray" suggests accident or the pathetic (a stray dog, a stray bullet), while "errant" can suggest a more forceful or roguish deviation, and even adventure.
  • wayward: Similar because both mean "turning from what is right or expected"; differs because "wayward" implies deliberate, stubborn, perverse defiance, while "errant" may be merely spatial or accidental.
  • deviant: Similar because both mean "departing from a norm"; differs because "deviant" is clinical, sociological, or moral in a heavy, disapproving way, while "errant" ranges from admiring (knight-errant) to neutral (errant arrow) to judgmental.
  • drifting: Similar because both mean "moving without control"; differs because "drifting" suggests passive, aimless movement on a current, while "errant" suggests an active or notable departure from an intended path.
  • astray: Similar because both mean "off the proper course"; differs because "astray" is usually an adverb or predicate ("led astray") describing a state, while "errant" is an adjective qualifying a person or thing.
  • erroneous: Similar because both come from the same Latin root and involve "going wrong"; differs because "erroneous" is strictly about error, falsehood, and mistaken belief, while "errant" is about physical wandering and moral straying, not facts.
  • erratic: Similar because both mean "irregular, unpredictable, off-course"; differs because "erratic" describes behaviour, movement, or performance that is inconsistent and unpredictable, while "errant" describes a single departure from a course or standard.
  • aberrant: Similar because both mean "deviating from the normal"; differs because "aberrant" is formal-scientific (aberrant genes, aberrant behaviour) and strongly abnormal, while "errant" is lighter and includes adventurous wandering.
  • vagrant: Similar because both mean "wandering, without settled place"; differs because "vagrant" is applied to persons, poverty, and homelessness with legal overtones, while "errant" applies to knights, arrows, particles, and behaviour.
