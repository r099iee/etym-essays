The biography of divested begins with the oldest Indo-European word for getting dressed. The Proto-Indo-European root *wes- means "to clothe, to dress, to be clothed in," and it is one of the most widely attested roots in the family, surviving from one end of the Indo-European world to the other. In Anatolia, Hittite waššiya- means "to clothe oneself"; in India, Vedic Sanskrit vaste (वस्ते) means "he wears, he is clothed in," and Sanskrit vastra (वस्त्र) is the ordinary word for cloth or garment, descending into Hindi as the learned vastra and into Odia as bastra; in Greece, Homeric heímata (εἵματα) names the garments of heroes, and Attic hesthēs (ἐσθής) the clothing of daily life; and in the Germanic north, Proto-Germanic *wazjaną gives Gothic wasjan "to clothe," Old English werian, and thus modern English wear, wore, worn. The root's deep association of the self with its covering is visible in these early reflexes: to wear is to present oneself, and clothing is the second skin in which identity is made visible. The whole family is built on the intuition that what covers a person is not incidental to them but constitutive of how they are seen.

Latin builds the wardrobe

Latin took the root through vestis, "garment," and vestīre, "to clothe," and on this pair Latin built a small derivational empire. Vestimentum gave English vestment, the ceremonial clothing of the clergy. Investīre, "to clothe in, to put on," acquired the figurative sense "to clothe with office, insignia, or authority," and English invest (16th century) took the whole package, later adding the financial sense in the 17th century — to invest money is to dress one's capital in the garments of enterprise, and an investment is wealth put into clothes that will yield a return. The English word vest itself, borrowed in the 17th century as a noun for a garment (and in the 20th century as a verb of giving rights: "to vest" authority in a body), completes the wardrobe: vest, invest, divest are three English words from one Latin root, all built on the image of putting clothes on — and, in divest's case, taking them off.

The negative prefix does its work

The opposite of investīre is dēvestīre, "to unclothe," built with the prefix dē-, "off, away from." Latin dēvestīre reached English by way of Old French desvestir, "to undress, strip," borrowed in the 16th century as devest and respelled divest in the 17th, under the influence of medieval Latin divestīre and the pull of the established invest. From the beginning the word split into its two great territories: the literal (to undress, to take off clothing) and the legal (to strip a person of property, rights, or office). In 17th–18th-century legal and political prose divest swelled: a monarch divested of power, an heir divested of the estate, a clergyman divested of his benefice. The reflexive form, to divest oneself, gained a flavour of deliberate, almost ritual renunciation — the subject chooses to shed what clothes him, item by item.

From the robe to the balance sheet

In the 20th century the word completed its largest leap, from the courtroom to the boardroom. Corporate finance appropriated divest as the term for selling off an asset, subsidiary, or holding, with divestment and divestiture (the legal noun) becoming standard equipment of mergers, antitrust law, and business strategy. In the 21st century the ethical investment movement gave the word a moral charge: universities, funds, and governments "divest" from fossil fuels, tobacco, or arms — withdrawing capital in a deliberate act that is strikingly faithful to the root, for the money is literally stripped out of the enterprise as clothes are stripped from a body. The clothing image has proved extraordinarily durable across three millennia.

The literary resonance

In literary prose, meanwhile, divest keeps its eerie neutrality and its full ceremonial weight. To be divested is to be relieved, item by item, of everything that clothed one — name, origin, family, history, identity — until only the bare, unclaimed self remains. The passage on this card — "Only now is the child finally divested of all that he has been" — sits at the end of this long history, gathering the literal undressing, the legal dispossession, and the figurative unmaking into one hushed sentence. The ancient Indo-European equation of self with clothing is still fully active: the boy is described not as destroyed but as undressed, and the removal, however total, leaves a body — naked, new, and unclaimed — which is precisely the point. Divested is a word of endings that is also, quietly, a word of beginnings: when everything has been taken off, what remains is whatever was underneath all along.
