Milled is the past tense and past participle of the verb mill. In the source passage it belongs to the phrasal verb mill about or mill around, which describes a crowd moving in a loose, restless, aimless way within a confined space: “most of the graduate students were unwilling to leave Cicero as they milled around the entrance to Annie Mae Swift Hall.” The picture is of people who have not dispersed, who drift and turn and re-form into small groups, waiting for something or simply reluctant to go. There is no destination in the movement and no order in it.

That sense is only one of several that the same form carries. Milled can mean ground in a mill (milled flour, milled pepper), stamped by a coining press (a milled edge), shaped by a machine tool (a milled slot), or thickened by fulling (milled cloth). All of these descend from the same noun, and the crowd sense is the youngest of them.

The crowd-movement sense

  • Core meaning. Of a number of people or animals: to move around in a disordered, circular, or undirected way, usually within a limited area and usually while waiting. The verb is intransitive and almost always takes about or around; mill alone in this sense is rare.
  • Implied qualities. Collective but uncoordinated motion; low-grade agitation; a suspension of purpose. A crowd that mills is not yet a crowd that acts — it has not been given a direction, an entrance, or a decision.
  • Typical subjects. Students, shoppers, delegates, journalists, fans, cattle, sheep. The usage began with livestock and was extended to people, which is part of why it can sound faintly depreciative.
  • Typical settings. Doorways, lobbies, foyers, corridors, stadium concourses, town squares — anywhere a group is held briefly between one place and another.
  • In the source. The students have been reading or discussing Cicero; they are unwilling to leave, so they hover at the entrance. “Milled around” catches the combination of lingering affection and physical restlessness better than stood or waited, both of which would imply stillness and patience.

Grinding: the oldest sense

To mill grain is to grind it between stones or rollers, and milled in this sense means reduced to flour or meal: milled wheat, freshly milled pepper, milled rice (rice with the husk and bran removed). The word extends to anything reduced by mechanical crushing — milled ore, milled limestone, milled bark. In industrial contexts milled often implies a controlled particle size rather than simple destruction, which distinguishes it from crushed or pulverised.

Coins: the technical numismatic sense

A milled coin is one struck by machinery rather than by hand with a hammer, and especially one with a raised rim and a patterned or reeded edge. The milled edge was a security device: it made clipping — shaving silver from the rim — immediately visible. England moved to milled coinage in 1662 under Charles II, using screw presses introduced by the engineer Peter Blondeau, and the contrast between hammered and milled coinage is fundamental to numismatics. Modern British coins still have milled edges, and the word survives in the phrase milled edge even where the machinery has changed completely.

Machining: the industrial sense

In engineering, milled describes a surface or part shaped by a milling machine, in which a rotating multi-toothed cutter is fed against the work. A milled slot, a milled face, a CNC-milled component. The sense dates from the early nineteenth century, when machine tools were developed that could cut metal with the precision previously reserved for hand filing, and it is now the most common technical use of the form.

Textiles and other manufactures

  • Milled cloth is fulled: woven wool is agitated in water so that the fibres mat and the fabric shrinks and thickens. Milling in this sense is one of the oldest finishing processes in Europe.
  • Milled soap has been passed through a roller mill to remove air and make it dense and smooth; “triple-milled” is a marketing phrase built on the same process.
  • Milled timber is sawn, planed, or moulded to profile in a sawmill or planing mill.
  • Milled leather has been run through a milling machine to soften and grain it.

Related noun senses

The noun mill itself has branched in parallel. It means a building with machinery for grinding grain; then any factory with powered machinery (a cotton mill, a paper mill, a steel mill); then, by the nineteenth century, the industrial system itself, as in “the mill owners” or “mill towns.” Figurative idioms preserve older senses: run of the mill (ordinary, from ungraded mill output), grist to the mill (something useful to one's purpose), a millstone round one's neck, and put through the mill (subjected to a gruelling process).

Informal and slang uses

In informal British and Commonwealth usage, to mill can mean to beat someone up or defeat them soundly, and milled can mean thrashed or heavily defeated. The usage is slang, regionally variable, and not recorded in the source passage; it should not be confused with the crowd sense, which is standard English.

Why the crowd sense makes sense

Two images lie behind mill about. The first is the mill wheel or millstone, turning endlessly in place and producing nothing visible in the turning itself. The second is the behaviour of cattle driven around a mill to power it, or penned and circling restlessly. Either way the metaphor captures motion without progress, which is exactly what a lingering crowd does. The word is therefore not merely descriptive of movement but evaluative of its pointlessness.

Grammar and form

Mill is a regular verb: mill, mills, milled, milling. The past participle is identical to the past tense. In the crowd sense it is intransitive; in the grinding, coining, machining, and textile senses it is usually transitive, and the participle functions as a passive adjective. The form milled is therefore grammatically ambiguous and is disambiguated only by context — which is why the source sentence needs “around” to signal the crowd meaning.
