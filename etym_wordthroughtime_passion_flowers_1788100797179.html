- 1610: Italian friar-scholar Jacomo Bosio publishes the symbolism — the “flower of the Passion” becomes a devotional object across Catholic Europe; Spanish missionaries had already read the emblem in New World plants.
- 1640s: “passion-flower” is first recorded in English; botanical writers copy the allegorical reading — every part of the flower is interpreted as an instrument of the Crucifixion.
- 1753: Linnaeus formalises the genus as “Passiflora” in Species Plantarum; the emblematic frame survives inside respectable Enlightenment botany.
- 19th century: the Victorian conservatory craze — passion-flowers as exotic garden ornaments; floriography gives the flower meanings of faith and religious ardour; “passion fruit” enters English (late 19th century) from the same symbolism.
- 20th century: horticulture and pharmacology modernise the plant — “passionflower” becomes a sedative supplement; the religious frame quietly fades from public speech.
- Present: for most speakers “passion flower” is a beautiful climber whose name suggests intense love — a folk re-etymology; the Crucifixion symbolism survives in botanical lore, Catholic tradition and the plant’s scientific name.
