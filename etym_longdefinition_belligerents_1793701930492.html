The term "belligerents" refers primarily to nations, states, or other organized political entities that are formally engaged in a war or armed conflict. In the context of international law, a belligerent state is one that is recognized as being in a state of war, thereby acquiring certain rights and obligations under the laws of armed conflict (also known as international humanitarian law or the law of war). These rights include the ability to blockade enemy ports, capture enemy property, and treat captured enemy combatants as prisoners of war. Corresponding obligations include adherence to principles such as distinction (between combatants and civilians), proportionality, and humanity in the conduct of hostilities, as codified in treaties like the Geneva Conventions.

Beyond formal state-on-state warfare, the term can also extend to non-state armed groups, such as insurgent forces, rebel movements, or liberation fronts, when they achieve a certain level of organization, control territory, and conduct sustained military operations against a recognized state. The recognition of such a group as a "belligerent" by other states is a complex political and legal act, often carrying significant implications for the conflict's international status and the rights of the parties involved. For instance, if an insurgent group is recognized as a belligerent, its members might be entitled to prisoner-of-war status if captured, rather than being treated as mere criminals or terrorists, which significantly alters their legal protections.

In a broader, less formal sense, "belligerents" can refer to any individuals, groups, or parties involved in a hostile confrontation, argument, or dispute, characterized by aggression, readiness to fight, or a confrontational attitude. This usage extends beyond armed conflict to describe parties in a heated debate, a legal battle, or even a personal quarrel where both sides exhibit aggressive or uncooperative behavior. When used in this general context, the term often carries a connotation of active, sometimes even eager, participation in conflict.

It is crucial to distinguish "belligerents" from related terms. While all belligerents have combatants, a "combatant" is an individual directly participating in hostilities, whereas a "belligerent" is the larger entity (state, group) that fields these combatants. The term "aggressor" implies a party that initiated an unjust war or conflict, carrying a moral judgment; "belligerent," however, is neutral in this regard, simply denoting participation in hostilities, regardless of the justice of their cause. An "enemy" is a general term for an opponent, but "belligerent" specifically situates that opposition within the framework of armed conflict or intense dispute. The adjectival form, "belligerent," describes a person or country that is hostile and aggressive, or a state of being engaged in war, further reinforcing the core meaning of active, often aggressive, involvement in conflict.

Historically, the concept of belligerency has been central to the development of international law, particularly concerning neutrality. A "neutral state" is one that does not take part in a war between belligerent states, and international law defines the rights and duties of both belligerents and neutrals towards each other. For example, belligerents are generally prohibited from conducting military operations on neutral territory, and neutral states are obligated not to provide military assistance to any belligerent. The evolution of this term reflects the changing nature of warfare and international relations, from formal declarations of war between sovereign states to more complex conflicts involving non-state actors and asymmetric warfare, continually challenging the legal frameworks that define who or what constitutes a belligerent.
