- Antiquity–early Middle Ages — The sea has no fixed name in classical geography: Tacitus calls it Mare Suebicum, Pomponius Mela Sinus Codanus, and later writers Mare Sarmaticum; “Baltic” is not yet in use.
- 11th–13th centuries — Mare Balticum appears in Latin chronicles (Adam of Bremen, c. 1075), and the name spreads with Hanseatic trade; it is a navigational and commercial label.
- 16th–18th centuries — “Baltic” attaches to the provinces and to the German-speaking nobility of the coast; it connotes trade, grain, timber and imperial rivalry.
- 19th century — Philologists extend the name to the language family (Old Prussian, Lithuanian, Latvian); “Baltic” becomes a scholarly classification as well as a geographical adjective.
- 1918–1940 — The three new republics are called the Baltic states; the word acquires a political, anti-imperial and modernising flavour.
- 1940–1991 — Under Soviet annexation the name is used mostly by émigrés and Western diplomats; inside the USSR the republics are referred to individually, and “Baltic” becomes a marker of non-recognition.
- 1989–2004 — The Singing Revolution and the Baltic Way make “Baltic” a byword for peaceful resistance; after 2004 it also signals EU and NATO membership.
- 2000s–present — The word is routine in security policy, marine ecology and finance (Baltic Dry Index), while “Baltic” as a style label evokes amber, linen and cold northern light.
