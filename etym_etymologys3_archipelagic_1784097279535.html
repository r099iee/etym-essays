ARCHIPELAGIC is a nineteenth-century English adjective, but to tell its story properly one must tell the stories of its two Greek elements — arkhi-, "chief," and pélagos, "sea" — and then the long Mediterranean journey by which the two fused into the name of a sea and then into a universal geographical term. Each element brings its own evolution; they meet in the Aegean and travel together through Italian into English and, finally, into international law.

Essay on arkhi-, "chief, principal" — the first element.

An obscure and debated beginning. The history of arkhi- begins in an uncertainty that every honest etymologist must record. The Greek verb árkhō, "to be first, to begin, to rule," has no securely established Proto-Indo-European etymology. The most authoritative modern reference, Robert Beekes's Etymological Dictionary of Greek (2010), judges its origin unclear, and treats the word group as possibly of Pre-Greek origin — a substrate word adopted by Greek speakers before the fully historical period. Older handbooks connected the root to a reconstructed PIE *h₂erǵ-, "to be white, shining," the root of Latin argentum, "silver," imagining the "chief" as the "shining one" at the head of a tribe; this romantic reconstruction is now rejected by most specialists. A second, more temperate comparison links arkhein to Latin arceō, "to shut in, to protect," and arx, "citadel," the idea being that the ruler is the one who encloses and defends; but even this is debated, and the forms do not align as cleanly as one would wish. The honest verdict is that the origin of arkhi- is genuinely unknown, and that nothing in the word's prehistory can be safely reconstructed.

The documented Greek career. What is fully documented is the career of árkhō from the beginnings of Greek literature. In Homer (8th c. BCE) árkhō already means "to be first, to rule," and the derived nouns arkhḗ, "beginning, rule, office," and arkhós, "leader, chief," are in place. The word family became political bedrock: the Athenian magistrates were the árkhontes, the "rulers" or "archons," and the annual term of office was the arkhē. The same stem supplied the abstract noun for "beginning" that the pre-Socratic philosophers used for the first principle of the universe — Anaximander's arkhē is "the source from which all things arise." And as a combining form, arkhi-, "chief, principal," Greek built a vast array of compounds — arkhiereús, "high priest"; arkhēgós, "leader, founder" — that later poured into the languages of Europe.

The Latin, church, and English inheritance of arkhi-. The prefix reached English through several channels. Through Latinised Greek it became archi-/arch-, and through the vocabulary of the church it entered Christian terminology: archangelos, "archangel, chief angel"; archiepiskopos, "archbishop, chief bishop." Through the vocabulary of secular rule came monarchos, "sole ruler," from mónos, "alone," + árkhō, "to rule"; oligarchia, "rule by the few"; hierarchia, "sacred rule," originally of the angelic orders ranked in hierarchy; and anarchia, "without a ruler," whence anarchy. English compounds of the modern period — archduke, archenemy — show the same "chief" sense alive and productive. It is precisely this "chief, principal" arkhi- that enters the sea-compound: the arkhipélagos is the "chief sea," the sea that stands at the head of all seas in the Greek world's self-conception — the Aegean, studded with the Cyclades and Sporades, the great waterway at the centre of Hellenic civilisation.

From chief sea to any island group. The Greek compound arkhipélagos appears in medieval Greek, and it was through the sailors and merchants of the Mediterranean — especially of Venice, the great maritime republic whose ships plied the Aegean — that the name travelled westward as Italian arcipelago, "the Aegean, the chief sea." English borrowed the noun about 1500, and for centuries archipelago in English meant specifically the Aegean: travellers to the Levant wrote of "the Archipelago" as a proper name. The generalisation came gradually as navigators applied the word to other island-dotted seas and then, by metonymy, to the island groups themselves. The archipelagic adjective, formed in the nineteenth century on the model of Greek adjectives in -ikos, was born into this generalised geography. The whole arc of the first element runs from an unknowable origin, through the Greek political vocabulary of rule and beginning, into the "chief sea" of the Aegean, and from there into universal modern geography.

Essay on pelagos, "sea" — the second element.

A sea of uncertain parentage. The second element, pélagos, "the open sea," is as old as Greek literature and equally debated in its origins. The traditional account derives it from the PIE root *pleh₂- (earlier written *plā-), "flat, broad, spread out" — the same root that produced Greek platús, "broad"; Sanskrit pṛthú, "broad"; Latin plānus, "flat," ancestor of English plain and plane; and, through Italian, the musical piano (literally "soft, flat"). On this account the pélagos is "the broad expanse," the wide open water as opposed to the narrow straits and the coast-hugging shallows — a sea-word built on the idea of breadth. The account is attractive, but Beekes and others have noted that a "broad" root is a somewhat generic basis for a sea-word, and have suggested that pélagos may instead be a Pre-Greek substrate term — a word Greek took over from the older inhabitants of the Aegean world, whose non-Indo-European seafaring vocabulary Greek absorbed. The question is genuinely open, and the honest reconstruction treats pélagos as either a *pleh₂- derivative or a substrate word, with the two possibilities plainly flagged.

The Greek career of pelagos. What is certain is the Greek word's meaning and standing. From Homer onward, pélagos names the open, high sea — the broad deep water on which ships sail far from land — distinguished from póntos, "the path of the sea," the navigable sea as a route, and from thálassa, "the sea" as a whole. The pélagos is thus the wide maritime plain, the "flat field" of water: Homer's heroes cross the pélagos to Troy; the pélagos is the arena of the Odyssey's wanderings. The word's association with breadth and openness is exactly what made it the natural second element of a name for the Aegean — a sea, but a wide and open one, distinct from the enclosed gulfs and inland waters that ring the Greek coast.

Two seas meet in one name. When Greek joined arkhi-, "chief," to pélagos, "sea," the compound arkhipélagos, "the chief sea," named the sea that Greeks regarded as the principal water of their world — the Aegean, ringed and studded with the islands (the Cyclades, the Sporades, Crete, Rhodes, Lesbos) that made it both a highway and a home. It has been suggested that the name may owe something to a reanalysis of the older Aígaion pélagos, "the Aegean Sea" (literally "the Goat Sea," from aíx, "goat," a sea-name of obscure origin), re-shaped as though it were arkhi- + pélagos; the suggestion is plausible but not proven. Whatever the exact route, the compound became, through Italian arcipelago (14th century) and English archipelago (c. 1500), first a proper name for the Aegean and then — as navigators applied it to the West Indies, the Indonesian islands, and every other island-dotted sea — a common noun for any group of islands and the sea containing them.

From geography to law: the modern archipelagic. The nineteenth-century adjective archipelagic entered this mature geography, and the twentieth century gave it its most important new meaning. The Law of the Sea had long treated islands as the limit of territorial waters; the island states of the mid-twentieth century — Indonesia, the Philippines, Fiji — sought to have the waters between their islands recognised as their own, and the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea codified the result. An "archipelagic State" is a State made up wholly of one or more archipelagos, which may draw straight baselines joining the outermost islands and thereby enclose "archipelagic waters" — a special regime, neither internal waters nor territorial sea nor exclusive economic zone, through which foreign ships enjoy "archipelagic sea-lanes passage." Cape Verde, the ten volcanic islands of the card's context, is an archipelagic State, and its geography — nine inhabited islands strung through the central Atlantic — is the textbook illustration of the term. The word that began as a Greek name for a sea dense with islands has become a precise category of international law, an instrument by which scattered island peoples claim the waters that make them a single nation. From arkhi- and pélagos to the UNCLOS chamber, the archipelagic adjective has travelled farther than the ships of its first sailors ever did.
