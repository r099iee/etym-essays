1. The Latin base. English loin comes from Old French loigne / loyne, from (Vulgar) Latin *lumbea or *lumbeus, an adjectival use of classical Latin lumbus, "loin, hip, lower back". Latin lumbus is attested from early texts (it supplies Pliny and the medical writers with the lumbar region) and survives in Romance: Italian lombo, Spanish lomo, Portuguese lombo, French lombe. The deeper pre-Latin history of lumbus is uncertain: it is frequently connected with an Indo-European *lendh- formation meaning "loin" (with possible cognates in Old Church Slavonic and, some hold, in English lend "loin, flank" from Old Norse lend), but no completely secure PIE reconstruction commands assent, and serious etymologists (e.g. de Vaan) advise caution about the root's shape; the safest statement is that lumbus belongs to a Western Indo-European word-family for the lower back whose precise ancestor is not recoverably attested.

2. The Gallo-Romance development. In the spoken Latin of Gaul the noun shifted to the adjectival form *lumbea ("that of the loin") — a frequent pattern for body-part nouns (cf. pulmo → "the lung thing"). The sequence -mb- before a palatalised vowel produced the Old French loigne: the labial consonant weakened and vanished, the vowel raised, and the -gn- palatal glide (the same sound written -gn- in French vigne from Latin vinea) marked the result. Old French also had loyn/loing; the form entered insular French and then English with the Norman lexicon of meat, hunting, and the body.

3. Middle English. English borrowed the word in the thirteenth century (loyne, lion, loyn), overwhelmingly in the plural from the first: the pair of loins naturally made a plural noun, like bowels or hips. The word appears in the fourteenth-century romances, in Chaucer's descriptions of the athletic body, and in the agricultural and culinary vocabulary of the manor (a loyne of pork). From the beginning English treated singular and plural almost interchangeably for the region, with the plural dominant for "the two sides of the lower back".

4. The Bible and the permanent idiom. The English Bible made loins a literary fixture. Wycliffe, Tyndale, and above all the Authorized Version (1611) used it where the Hebrew and Greek have "hips" or "kidneys", giving English the standing phrases: "gird up thy loins" (to hitch up the robe and prepare), "loins girt about with truth" (Ephesians 6:14), "out of his loins" and "the fruit of thy loins" for offspring, and "strength is in his loins" (Job 40:16). Because the KJV was the most-quoted book in English for three centuries, loins became a word of solemn, bodily, and genealogical resonance — strong, modest, and archaic at once. Its plural form was fixed in these contexts, which is why the card's form is loins rather than the anatomical or culinary singular.

5. The culinary line. Meanwhile the singular continued its practical career in the kitchen: loin of veal, pork loin, and the Old French derivative sirloin (literally "over the loin", sur "above" + loigne, the upper, choicest part) — the false legend that a king knighted a "Sir Loin" is a folk etymology of the sort the word's real history easily provokes. In the nineteenth century tenderloin (the tender strip under the loin) gave American English the "Tenderloin" nickname for a flourishing (and vice-ridden) city district, a curious urban offshoot of the butcher's counter.

6. Anatomy and medicine. Medical English retained the learned adjective lumbar (the lumbar vertebrae, the lumbar puncture) directly from lumbus, while "loins" continued in veterinary and rural usage. The lay sense narrowed: by the twentieth century, "loin" for most speakers meant the meat, or the region of pain in "a stitch in the loin", while the plural belonged to the Bible, to poetry, and to the euphemistic prudery of the Victorians (who used "the loins" where earlier writers said "the genitals").

7. The word today.Loins is now a word with three lives: anatomical in medical and veterinary English, culinary in the singular, and scriptural-literary in the plural. Its survival in ordinary speech hangs on one phrase — "gird up your loins" — which is itself invariably a conscious allusion. It is a body-word that has grown a soul: from butcher's anatomy to the figure for readiness and descent, the history of loins shows a common noun quietly promoted, by a national translation of the Bible, into the language of strength and lineage.
