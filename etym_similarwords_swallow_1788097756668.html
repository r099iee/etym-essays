• Martin (house martin, sand martin): Similar as close relatives of the swallow within Hirundinidae, sharing aerial insect-feeding and mud nesting; differs because martins have shorter, less deeply forked tails and colonial cliff-eave nesting, while “swallow” strictly names the barn-type bird.
  • Swift: Similar as an aerial insect-hunter that looks like a swallow; differs because swifts are an entirely different family (Apodidae) — stiff-winged, sooty dark, never perching, often larger — the classic pair for teaching convergent evolution.
  • Chimney swift: Similar as a North American aerial insectivore with a chimney-roosting habit; differs because, in spite of the name, it is a swift, not a swallow.
  • Purple martin: Similar as a large American hirundine; differs because it is a martin of the genus Progne — bigger, colonial, cavity-nesting — while the swallow nests in the open.
  • Tern: Similar in white, graceful, forked-tailed flight; differs because terns are marine birds (family Laridae) that plunge-dive for fish, wholly unrelated to hirundines.
  • Flycatcher: Similar in eating insects; differs because flycatchers perch and sally out rather than feeding in continuous flight, and belong to several unrelated passerine families.
  • Nightjar: Similar as a dusk-feeding insect-eater of the air; differs because nightjars are nocturnal, cryptically plumaged, and in a different order (Caprimulgiformes).
  • Hirundine: Similar as the technical name for the swallow family; differs because hirundine is ornithological jargon (from Latin hirundo), never an everyday word.
  • Gulp / Ingest (homograph note): Similar only by spelling and pronunciation to the verb swallow “to gulp down”; differs because these are wholly separate words with a different ancestry — listed here only to prevent confusion between the bird and its homograph.
