Pinch in this line is the noun of crisis: a tight spot, emergency, or critical moment when resources, space, or nerve are squeezed — "as fierce as a dragon in a pinch." The physical image is the root of everything: a small part of something compressed sharply between finger and thumb. From that concrete act of squeezing, the word has radiated an extraordinary family of senses — the tiny measure, the economic squeeze, the moment of need, the theft, the arrest — and the idiom in a pinch ("when it really matters, in a difficulty") freezes the situational sense that this card requires. Primary senses and their historical order.
  • To squeeze tightly between finger and thumb (verb): the concrete root sense — "pinch someone's cheek," "pinch a candle out" — a sharp, small compressive grip.
  • The act or grip of squeezing (noun): "he gave her a pinch" — the finger-action as a noun.
  • A tiny gripped measure: "a pinch of salt" — the amount held between thumb and forefinger, a culinary idiom in English from the sixteenth century.
  • Scarcity, pressure, want: "to feel the pinch" — the economic squeeze, when money or resources become tight.
  • A critical situation, emergency: "in a pinch" (and British "at a pinch") — a tight spot that tests you; the exact sense of this card.
  • To steal (thieves' slang): "he pinched my wallet" — to lift something with a quick, sharp grasp; nineteenth-century underworld slang.
  • An arrest (slang): "a pinch" — the police "pinch" a criminal; to be caught in the squeeze of the law.
  • Stinginess: "to pinch pennies" — to squeeze every coin, and the adjective pinch-penny for a miser.
Semantic bridges. Every sense grows from the image of compression between finger and thumb. The small measure is simply what the fingers can hold. Scarcity is the feeling of being squeezed — of one's resources compressed to the limit. The critical situation is the moment when circumstances press in on you like a grip. Theft is a quick, sharp taking with the fingers. Arrest is being seized — caught in the grip of the law. The word's genius is that the same small act of pinching generates both the everyday (a pinch of salt) and the desperate (a pinch, an emergency), and the idiom in a pinch rides on the physical metaphor of a tight squeeze. Register and usage. Pinch is ordinary, vivid English, equally at home in the kitchen, the marketplace, and the police station. In this card it must remain the noun inside the idiom in a pinch — the crisis-squeeze that proves Bilbo's value — not be reduced to the kitchen verb of pinching spice. The context in full. The sentence — "Gets funny queer fits, but he is one of the best, one of the best — as fierce as a dragon in a pinch." — is a dwarf's practical character reference for Bilbo. The speaker concedes the hobbit's odd spells, insists twice on his excellence, and then locates his worth precisely: he is fierce when the pressure comes. The idiom in a pinch supplies the testing condition — the emergency, the tight spot, the moment when circumstance squeezes nerve and resource alike — and the dragon comparison gives the fierceness its scale. The noun is not decorative; it names the whole situation that makes courage visible. Without the pinch there is no proof, and the dwarves are hiring a burglar precisely because a pinch is coming. Word families and formation. The modern word is both noun and verb with identical spelling. The noun derives from the verb, and its senses have stacked historically: the act of pinching, the amount pinched (a pinch of salt), the condition of being pinched (the pinch of poverty), the tight situation (in a pinch), and, in slang, the theft and the arrest. Adjectival and participial forms include pinched ("thin, strained, drawn" — the look of want) and the compounds pinch-penny (a miser) and pinchbeck (a gold-colored alloy, hence anything showy and false). The noun has also given the set phrases at a pinch (British), to feel the pinch, and pinch-hit (to substitute, from baseball), all of which preserve the image of stepping in when the situation squeezes.
