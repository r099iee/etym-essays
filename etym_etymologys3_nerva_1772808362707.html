The name NERVA is one of the few Roman imperial names that can be traced back, with confidence, to a Proto-Indo-European root — and the root itself is a vivid physical image: *sneh₁- / *sneu-, "to twist, spin, wind." In the Indo-European imagination, the sinews and tendons of the body were conceived as twisted cords, the "strings" that bind muscle to bone and give the body its strength. Descendants of this root are scattered across the family: Greek neûron "sinew, tendon" (later the root of English "neuron" and "neurology"), Indo-Iranian forms meaning sinew, and the Germanic family that gave English "sinew" and "snood." Latin inherited the same idea in the noun nervus, "sinew, tendon, nerve, bowstring." The phonetic path from the PIE root to Latin nervus is well understood in outline though debated in detail. The root *sneh₁- begins with s plus n; Latin generally retained such clusters, and some handbooks reconstruct an intermediate form involving a vocalic or laryngeal element (often written *sneh₁w-), which then developed into the sequence seen in nervus. Because Latin had no separate phoneme for the glide in this position in some analyses, the form stabilized as nervus. An alternative hypothesis, suggested by some scholars, is that the word entered Latin through Sabine or other Italic dialect influence, which could explain a slightly unexpected vocalism. These are genuine technical debates about the precise phonetic mechanism, but the semantic core is not in doubt: sinew, tendon, the twisted cord of the body. In Latin the noun immediately developed figurative richness. The "sinews" of a speech (nervi orationis) were its strength, force, and argumentative vigour; the "sinews" of the state were its military and economic power; a man of nervi was a man of nerve, courage, or bodily strength. The concept of vigour, in other words, was metaphorically attached to the literal cord of the body from the earliest Latin literature. It is from this vigour-sense that the cognomen Nerva arose. Roman cognomina very often froze a physical or moral characteristic into a family label — names like Calvus ("bald"), Flaccus ("flabby"), Cato ("wise") show the habit — and Nerva, "sinewy," "vigorous," belongs exactly to this class. It was borne by several families; the gens Cocceia, a distinguished Italian clan of equestrian and senatorial rank, used it, and the future emperor Marcus Cocceius Nerva was born into precisely that family, probably in the late 20s or early 30s AD. Nerva's own life explains the name's historical destiny. He pursued a senatorial career under Nero, Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian, holding the consulship in AD 90 (and perhaps earlier). As an elderly, respected, and moderate senator — connected to the imperial house, associated with legal culture, and untainted by the worst excesses of Domitian's court — he was the natural compromise candidate when the tyrant was assassinated in AD 96. The Senate acclaimed him emperor, and his short reign was devoted to repairing the damage of Domitian's terror: he swore not to put senators to death, restored confiscated property, recalled exiles, and instituted the alimenta, a state system supporting poor children. His most consequential act was the adoption of the popular general Trajan in AD 97, which secured a capable, non-hereditary succession and founded the era of the adoptive emperors. When Nerva died in early AD 98, he had ruled barely sixteen months, but he had set the precedent that made the "happy period" of the Antonines possible. Linguistically, the name passed straight from the Latin cognomen into modern European usage as the fixed designation of this emperor: English Nerva, French Nerva, Italian Nerva, Spanish Nerva, all preserving the Latin form with minimal change because it is a proper name of historical reference rather than an everyday word subject to sound-law evolution. The common noun, meanwhile, followed a separate path: Latin nervus gave French nerf, Italian nervo, Spanish nervio, and English "nerve," with the famous English semantic drift from "sinew, tendon" to the fibrous structures of the nervous system and then to "courage" (nerve) and to the plural "nerves" for anxiety. The emperor's name and the modern English "nerve" are thus genuine cousins, both descending from the same PIE root for a twisted cord; but while "nerve" underwent centuries of ordinary linguistic change and meaning-shift, Nerva remained a frozen proper noun, a fossilized cognomen meaning, at bottom, "sinewy" — an oddly fitting label for the man who restored the nerve, the vigour, of Roman government after Domitian's terror. In Gibbon's sentence, Nerva functions exactly as this etymological background suggests: the first name in the list, the restorer, the sinew of the restored constitution. The eighteenth-century historian uses the proper name as a milestone of the "happy period," and the reader who knows the etymology can feel the old vigour-sense resonate under the surface. The word's history thus spans the whole Indo-European career of an idea — from the twist of a sinew to the strength of the body to the vigour of the state, and finally to the name of the emperor who restored that vigour to Rome.
