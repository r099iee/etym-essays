Biely (predominantly spelled Bely in contemporary Slavic scholarship; Russian: Белый, meaning "White") is the celebrated literary pseudonym of Boris Nikolaevich Bugaev (1880–1934), the towering Russian Symbolist novelist, poet, literary theorist, and prosodist. Within world literature, comparative aesthetics, and modernist narrative theory, the name designates one of the foremost pioneers of avant-garde 20th-century fiction, whose 1913 masterpiece Petersburg (Петербург) revolutionized novelistic architecture through rhythmic prose, musical motifs, urban mythology, and hallucinatory psychological polyphony.

The historical significance of Biely in world literature is indelibly captured in Vladimir Nabokov's famous canon of twentieth-century masterpieces. In a celebrated 1965 television interview, Nabokov was asked to name the greatest prose works of the century; he famously declared: "My greatest masterpieces of twentieth-century prose are, in this order: Joyce's Ulysses, Kafka's Transformation [The Metamorphosis], Biely's Petersburg, and the first half of Proust's fairy tale In Search of Lost Time." By placing Biely directly alongside James Joyce, Franz Kafka, and Marcel Proust, Nabokov rescued Bely from provincial obscurity in the West, establishing him as the vital fourth pillar of high European modernism—a writer whose linguistic daring, syntactic experimentation, and apocalyptic urban vision rivaled Joyce's Dublin and Proust's Paris.

The name Biely encompasses extensive literary, philosophical, onomastic, and historical dimensions:

  • The Author of Petersburg (1913/1916/1922): As an authorial signifier, Biely stands for the visionary creator of Petersburg, universally acclaimed as the supreme city-novel of Russian literature. Set against the atmospheric fog, neoclassical facades, and apocalyptic unrest of the 1905 Russian Revolution, the novel dramatizes the generational conflict between a rigid, cerebral Tsarist bureaucrat (Senator Ableukhov) and his decadent son (Nikolai), who has promised a radical terrorist cell that he will detonate a bomb concealed inside an ordinary sardine tin to assassinate his own father. Biely transforms this Oedipal and political drama into a cosmic meditation on the clash between Western rationalism and Eastern mysticism, dominated by the bronze ghost of Peter the Great (the Bronze Horseman).
  • Symbolist Poetics, Anthroposophy, and the "Symphonies": In literary theory and poetics, Biely represents a revolutionary marriage between music, mathematical formalism, and occult mysticism. The son of a renowned Moscow University mathematics professor, Bely pioneered a new experimental genre between 1902 and 1908 called "Dramatic Symphonies" (four prose suites organized strictly according to classical musical sonata form, leitmotifs, and counterpoint). A devoted follower of Rudolf Steiner's Anthroposophy, Biely believed that the sounds of language—vowels, consonants, and rhythmic feet—possessed occult spiritual vibrations capable of transfiguring human consciousness. His pioneering treatise Symbolism (1910) laid the foundational quantitative groundwork for modern Russian verse analysis and Russian Formalism.
  • Rhythmic and Phonic Prose Innovation: In stylistic analysis, Biely is synonymous with rhythmic prose (орнаментальная проза, ornamental prose). Rather than utilizing conventional naturalistic narrative, Biely wrote prose structured around strict dactylic and anapestic meters, internal rhymes, alliteration, and typographic spacing, creating a hypnotic, incantatory musicality that shattered 19th-century realist prose and paved the way for subsequent modernist innovators.
  • Onomastic Meaning of the Pseudonym (The Color White): As a common Russian word, белый (bely) is the adjective meaning "white." Boris Bugaev adopted the pseudonym Andrei Bely in 1901 at the age of twenty-one upon the advice of his mentor Mikhail Solovyov (brother of the philosopher Vladimir Solovyov). The name was chosen to shield his father's academic dignity while symbolizing apocalyptic purity, spiritual radiance, the white light of Divine Sophia (Wisdom), and mystical transfiguration against the decadent gloom of the fin-de-siècle.
  • Orthographic and Transliteration Conventions: The spelling Biely represents the older French- and German-influenced romanization of Cyrillic (where soft Cyrillic е following a consonant was rendered as ie rather than e). This orthography was consistently utilized by Vladimir Nabokov, early Western Slavic scholars, and John Cournos in his pioneering 1959 English translation of Petersburg, whereas contemporary international library standards (such as Library of Congress) generally prefer the transliteration Bely.

The semantic and cultural trajectory of Biely—from an ancient Indo-European root signifying radiant light, through the spiritual crises of Russian Symbolism, to Nabokov's modernist canon—embodies the explosive revolutionary energy of early 20th-century world literature.
