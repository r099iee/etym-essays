Solemnised is the British past tense of solemnise / solemnize, "to perform a ceremony with formal rites," most often a marriage. Its story starts with the Roman calendar, not with seriousness.

Latin sollemnis (older spelling sollennis, solennis) meant "annual, recurring every year, festive." Roman antiquarians derived it transparently from sollus "whole, entire" (from PIE *sol- "whole," also seen in solidus, salus) + annus "year" (from PIE *h₂et-no-). A sollemne was that which was performed toto anno — in full yearly cycle — hence a regular religious festival. Only secondarily did it gain the sense "formal, awe-inspiring, grave," because annual rites were performed with full pomp. This is the classic semantic path: calendrical → ritual → emotional tone.

  • Classical Latin: Cicero and Livy use sollemne for sacrifices, games, marriages, funerals — anything done rite, duly. Sollemne matrimonium is already "a marriage duly celebrated." The adverb sollemniter means "with due form."
  • Late Latin / Christian: The Church adopts sollemnis for liturgy: sollemnis missa, solemn vows. Seriousness now dominates; annualness fades.
  • Old French: Solemne, solempne (12th c.) means "celebrated, magnificent, grave." English borrows it after the Norman Conquest as Middle English solemne (c. 1300), describing masses, oaths, feasts, and demeanour: "a solemne man," "solemne vow."
  • Verb formation: English loves -ize (from Greek -izein via Latin -izare and French -iser) to make causatives: solemn + -ize = to make solemn. First recorded c. 1590s-1610s in the senses "to render grave" and "to celebrate with rites." Shakespeare uses solemnize for feasts and marriages.

Phonology is straightforward: Latin sollemnis /solˈlem.nis/ lost gemination in French, gained an unstressed English second syllable /ˈsɒl.əm/, and the verbal suffix -ize /-aɪz/ was added. Past tense -ed /d/ after a voiced consonant gives /ˈsɒl.əm.naɪzd/. British spelling keeps French -ise; American Noah Webster regularised to -ize. Dickens, writing in 1843 London, naturally uses solemnised.

Dickens's use is deliciously ironic and requires context. "He was an excellent man of business on the very day of the funeral, and solemnised it with an undoubted bargain." Funerals are normally solemnised — performed with dignity. Scrooge solemnises Marley's funeral not with tears but with a bargain struck that same day, probably haggling over coffin, fees, or a business deal sealed while mourners wept. The verb's ritual gravity collides with commercial cheapness. Modern readers miss the joke if they think solemnised means "felt sad"; it means "marked, celebrated, performed." He performed the funeral by doing business.

  • Legal specialisation: In modern British law, solemnize marriage is a term of art under the Marriage Acts: only authorised persons in registered buildings may solemnize marriage. American law uses solemnize identically. The funeral sense survives but marriage dominates.
  • Semantic drift: From "annual" to "ritual" to "grave" to "to make grave" to "to perform duly" — a textbook widening then narrowing. Contemporary solemn stresses mood; solemnise stresses action.
  • Debate note: The sollus + annus etymology of sollemnis is ancient (Festus) and accepted by de Vaan with a caution: phonological details of the compounding are irregular, and an Etruscan intermediary cannot be wholly excluded, but no serious alternative competes. The PIE roots cited are standard.

Thus solemnised carries two millennia of festivals, masses, weddings, and funerals: to do a thing as the calendar and custom demand — which for Scrooge meant doing a deal.
