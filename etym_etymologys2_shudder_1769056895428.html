Derivation chain for shudder — a single linear chain from the Indo-European root to the present English form: 1. Proto-Indo-European *(s)keud- (also written *(s)kewd-) "to shake, throw, dart, drive, hasten" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — a root of violent projected motion; probable cognates include Vedic Sanskrit skundáti "to hasten, spring forward" and Greek σκυτάλη (skytálē) "a staff, a club" (the latter uncertain). 2. Proto-Germanic *skudaną "to shake" (the *skud- zero-grade variant; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) — attested indirectly through Middle Dutch schudden and Old High German scutten "to shake." 3. Proto-Germanic *skudrōn "to shake again and again, to tremble" (a frequentative formation adding -r- to *skud-; same period) — the direct ancestor of the English word. 4. Middle English schoderen "to tremble" (attested c. 1300) — the immediate English ancestor; its exact pre-English route is debated (a native frequentative formation versus a borrowing of Middle Low German schōderen or Middle High German schüderen "to tremble, be terrified"). 5. English shudder (verb); the noun "a shudder" (the form used in "felt a slight ________") is a nominalisation of the verb. Note on debate and nuance: OED flags the origin as "of uncertain origin," plausibly a frequentative formation; modern German schaudern "to shudder, be appalled, shiver with horror" and Middle Low German schōderen are close Continental relatives of the same frequentative formation. English "shock" is a false friend — it descends via Old French from Frankish *skok- (root *skakan "to shake"), a different Germanic base, and must not be merged with *(s)keud-.
