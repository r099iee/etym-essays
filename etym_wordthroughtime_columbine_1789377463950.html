- Classical Antiquity to Late Latin (c. 1st c. BCE – 6th c. CE) - Latin adjective *columbīnus* ("of or pertaining to a dove, dove-like") derives from *columba* ("dove, pigeon"); later applied in herbal glosses (*herba columbina*) to plants with dove-like parts.
- 13th to 14th Century (Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French *colombine* as *columbine* / *columbyne*, rapidly established in monastic herb gardens and Christian devotional art as a sacred symbol of the Holy Spirit and the Seven Gifts.
- 16th Century (Renaissance & Elizabethan Era) - Popularized in secular literature and drama (Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, where Ophelia dispenses columbines symbolizing ingratitude); simultaneously in Italy, the commedia dell'arte creates the nimble female stock character *Colombina* ("little dove").
- 17th to 18th Century - The theatrical Columbine becomes the central female figure of British Harlequinade pantomimes; botanically, double and spurred cultivars multiply in English cottage gardens.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Celebrated in Victorian floriography, pastoral poetry, and children's allegorical tales (Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*) as the hallmark of timeless English cottage gardens.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as an essential perennial in shade and pollinator gardens worldwide; the Rocky Mountain columbine (*Aquilegia coerulea*) is adopted as the state flower of Colorado.
