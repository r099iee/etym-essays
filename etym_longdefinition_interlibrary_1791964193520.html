interlibrary is an English compound adjective built from the prefix inter- (“between, among”) and the noun library. It is attributive only: it stands before a noun, almost always in the fixed phrase interlibrary loan (abbreviated ILL), and is essentially never used predicatively or as a noun on its own. Its core meaning is relational — “existing, occurring, or arranged between two or more libraries”.

The source passage uses precisely this professional sense. A bibliographer is said to have “severely limited the citation of works which could not readily be discovered by a simple interlibrary loan”; that is, he declined to cite scholarship that an ordinary reader could not track down through the routine borrowing arrangements that operate between libraries, and he further excluded works written in languages other than Latin, French, German, Italian and English. “A simple interlibrary loan” supplies the baseline of easy access against which his selectivity is measured: a modest, everyday library service standing in for “reasonably obtainable”.

Primary sense — the interlibrary loan

  • Definition. A loan of library material — a book, a periodical volume, a thesis, a microform, or, more often now, a scanned article — made by the library that owns it (the supplying or lending library) to another library (the borrowing or requesting library) acting for one of its own readers.
  • The transaction. The reader submits an interlibrary request; the borrowing library checks its own holdings and catalogue first, identifies a prospective supplier through a union catalogue or a consortium, and transmits the request; the supplying library issues the item for a fixed loan period, often with restrictions such as “library use only” or “no renewal”.
  • The modern infrastructure. In practice the phrase is inseparable from the systems that carry it: national union catalogues, the OCLC WorldShare network, regional consortia, dedicated ILL management software and electronic document delivery. The abbreviation ILL is standard professional shorthand on both sides of the Atlantic.
  • The legal frame. Because an interlibrary copy is frequently a reproduction rather than the original, the service is hedged about with copyright declarations and guidelines — in the United States, for instance, the CONTU guidelines of 1978 on photocopying for interlibrary purposes.

Extended senses inside librarianship

  • interlibrary lending / interlending — the supply side of the same traffic, seen from the lender’s desk; interlending is the term favoured by IFLA and by British and Commonwealth practice.
  • interlibrary cooperation — the wider policy of sharing acquisitions, cataloguing and storage between institutions; here the word no longer refers to a single loan but to a standing relationship.
  • interlibrary network / consortium — the organised body through which the loans flow, typically with reciprocal borrowing rights and a shared catalogue.
  • interlibrary delivery — the physical or electronic transport of the item, a term that has grown with courier services and scan-to-PDF workflows.
  • interlibrary request / interlibrary transaction — the administrative unit counted in library statistics, which is why the word appears so often in annual reports.

What it does not mean

  • It does not describe a physical passage, corridor or door between two buildings; a connecting walkway would be called a link or a corridor, not an interlibrary anything.
  • It has nothing to do with multilingualism or with “international” libraries, despite the resemblance of inter- to the inter of international.
  • It is not a strict synonym of interlending: interlibrary is the attributive modifier, interlending the activity noun.

Register and connotation

The word is technical but unmarked: it belongs to the neutral register of institutional administration and carries no emotional colour of its own. Its connotations are supplied by context — efficiency, delay, paperwork, cooperation, cost. In the source sentence the collocation “a simple interlibrary loan” is quietly ironic, since anyone who has used the service knows it is rarely simple: the adjective promises ease and the noun delivers bureaucracy. British usage often hyphenates the older form inter-library; American usage prefers the solid interlibrary, and both are current.

Semantic reasoning

Inter- marks a relation across a boundary, and here the boundary is institutional rather than geographical: the two libraries may stand on the same campus. The compound therefore presupposes a plurality of independent libraries with separate collections, budgets and jurisdictions — a state of affairs that only became general in the nineteenth century. The word is thus a small fossil of library history: it could not have been coined before libraries were numerous, distinct and connected.
