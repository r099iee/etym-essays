• hanging / wall-hanging: Similar because it is fabric suspended on a wall for decoration; differs because hanging is the plain, generic term that says nothing about how the fabric was made or whether it pictures anything, while a tapestry is specifically a dense, pictorial, woven textile—a narrative, not just drapery.
  • arras: Similar because it denotes a large pictorial hanging tapestry, and for centuries the two words were near-synonyms; differs because arras originally meant specifically a tapestry made in the town of Arras in the Low Countries and is now archaic and poetic—Polonius dies behind the arras in Hamlet—while tapestry remains the living, everyday word.
  • drapery: Similar because it is cloth hung about a room; differs because drapery names the soft, loose folds of curtain fabric and is about ornament through folds and falling cloth, not about woven pictures; you drape a window, you hang a tapestry.
  • embroidery: Similar because it is decorative needle-and-thread work that can depict a scene; differs because embroidery is applied onto an already woven ground—its image is stitched on the surface with a needle—while a tapestry is woven into the fabric itself, the design built in as the threads go in.
  • brocade: Similar because it is a rich, patterned woven textile; differs because brocade is a clothing and upholstery fabric with a raised, often gold-thread pattern, made to be cut and sewn into garments, while a tapestry is hung as a wall picture, often the size of a room.
  • carpet / rug: Similar because the whole word family began with the floor (Greek τάπης, "carpet"), and a tapestry is structurally a floor-weave stood on end; differs because a carpet lies on the floor and is walked on, while a tapestry hangs on the wall and is read like a book—the two are the same craft turned from horizontal to vertical.
  • needlepoint / tapestry-work: Similar because it is sold and worked as "tapestry" in craft shops and on stamped canvas; differs because needlepoint is true embroidery worked stitch by stitch over an already woven canvas, and is not a woven textile at all—the craft trade's "tapestry" is a misnomer for a picture in stitchery.
