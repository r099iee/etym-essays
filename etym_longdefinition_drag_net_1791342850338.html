The compound noun drag-net denotes, in its primary physical sense, a heavy, weighted fishing net designed to be drawn, towed, or trailed along the bottom of a river, pond, lake, or seabed to capture bottom-dwelling fish, shellfish, or sunken debris; and in its widespread figurative sense, a comprehensive, systematic, and wide-ranging search operation or cordon conducted by authorities to apprehend fugitives, gather suspects, or uncover illicit evidence. In the source passage (“And some ten minutes later, having failed to find scent in the house, I started to throw out the __________ through the grounds, and flushed him in the rose garden”), Bertie Wooster employs the term with comic narrative flair. He playfully intertwines the forensic terminology of a police dragnet with the imagery of foxhunting and game shooting (“find scent,” “throw out the drag-net,” “flushed him”) to describe searching the country-house gardens for an elusive acquaintance.

The transition from maritime fishing gear to forensic police methodology is one of the most natural semantic progressions in the language. A fishing drag-net operates indiscriminately: weighted with lead sinkers and pulled by boats or shore teams, it scrapes the muddy bed of a watercourse, scooping up everything in its path—marketable fish, weeds, river stones, and lost artifacts alike. When modern metropolitan police forces emerged in the 19th century, detectives adopted the term to describe cordoning off entire neighborhoods or combing districts where every potential suspect was swept into custody for interrogation. By the mid-20th century, the term had become so synonymous with systematic police investigation that it titled one of the most influential procedural radio and television franchises in broadcasting history.

  • Physical Maritime and Freshwater Fishing Gear: A specialized trawl net, dredge, or seine equipped with heavy sinkers along the bottom lead-line and buoyant corks or floats along the top cork-line. Hauled along lakebeds, estuaries, or coastal waters to ensnare demersal fish (such as flounder, cod, or carp) and benthic invertebrates (such as oysters or crabs).
  • Forensic Salvage and Recovery Operations: In municipal and maritime rescue work, a drag-net fitted with iron grappling hooks or heavy mesh is dragged across river bottoms, canals, or harbor basins by police and rescue divers to recover submerged evidence, discarded weapons, sunken wreckage, or drowning victims.
  • Law Enforcement Sweeps and Manhunts: A coordinated, large-scale search operation where police forces seal off perimeter exits across a city quarter, county, or transit network to trap fleeing criminals, escapees, or gang members (e.g., “a nationwide police drag-net captured the armed robber at a highway checkpoint”).
  • Mass Surveillance and Digital Intelligence: In modern technological and geopolitical contexts, the term “digital dragnet” or “dragnet surveillance” refers to the bulk, indiscriminate collection and algorithmic filtering of vast quantities of electronic communications, telecommunication metadata, and internet traffic by state intelligence agencies (such as the NSA or GCHQ), sweeping up innocent citizens alongside genuine targets.
  • Ecological and Conservation Significance: In marine environmental science and fisheries regulation, commercial drag-nets (specifically heavy bottom-trawl nets) are the subject of intense international debate due to the severe physical damage they inflict on fragile seabed ecosystems, deep-water coral reefs, and the catastrophic levels of marine bycatch they generate.
  • Metaphorical Talent Searches and Academic Sweeps: Extended to organizational recruitment, where corporate headhunters, sports scouts, or university admissions boards “cast a wide drag-net” across global institutions to identify top-tier candidates, research fellows, or athletic prospects.
  • Biblical and Allegorical Imagery: In Christian scripture (notably the Parable of the Dragnet in Matthew 13:47–50), the drag-net cast into the sea gathers fish of every kind, which are subsequently sorted on shore into the good (kept in vessels) and the bad (thrown away)—functioning as a powerful theological symbol for the final judgment of humanity.
  • Polysemy Bridge: The unifying conceptual bridge connecting all senses is the mechanics of an unescapable, indiscriminate sweep. Whether scraping physical mud for fish, sealing streets for suspects, scouring internet servers for metadata, or hunting through rose gardens for a friend, the actor casts an extensive net across a bounded domain to ensure that nothing concealed can slip away.

In orthography, the word appears in modern English both with a hyphen (drag-net, traditional in British literary prose and the source text) and as a solid closed compound (dragnet, standard in contemporary American journalism). Phonetically pronounced in Received Pronunciation as /ˈdræɡ.net/, it functions grammatically as a countable noun, frequently combining with operational verbs: to throw out the drag-net, to cast a drag-net, to slip through the drag-net, to be caught in the drag-net.
