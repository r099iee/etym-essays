- 11th c. — The name belongs to an obscure hamlet and its fields south-west of Paris; it means a place, not a palace.
- 1623–1682 — Louis XIII builds a hunting lodge, Louis XIV enlarges it into a palace, and in 1682 the court moves there; “Versailles” begins to mean the royal court as much as the building.
- 1682–1789 — The name stands for the whole system of court etiquette, patronage and absolute government; to be “at Versailles” is to be at the centre of power.
- 1789 — The October Days bring the royal family back to Paris; the palace empties, and “Versailles” becomes in revolutionary speech a byword for the old regime’s waste and secrecy.
- 1837 — Louis-Philippe turns the palace into a museum of French history; the name acquires a heritage and tourist sense.
- 1871 and 1919 — The Hall of Mirrors hosts the proclamation of the German Empire and the signing of the peace treaty; “Versailles” enters diplomacy and, in Germany, becomes a contested political symbol.
- 20th–21st c. — The palace is a UNESCO site and a global tourist destination; the name is a byword for opulence, and modern French uses “ce n’est pas Versailles” as an ironic comment on modest surroundings.
