- 16th c. — wheels write on roads: rut names the wheel-track and rutted the lane that bears it, a plain countryman word.
- 17th–18th c. — travel literature loves it: coach diaries complain of rutted highways, and the adjective becomes shorthand for jolting discomfort.
- 19th c. — the metaphor escapes: to be in a rut now means stuck in routine, and rutted describes minds and marriages as well as lanes.
- Early 20th c. — war fixes the image: rutted Flanders mud and tank-torn tracks make the word emblematic of armies bogged in their own grooves.
- Late 20th c. — tarmac pushes back: metalled roads banish most ruts, so rutted turns nostalgic, marking bridleways, farms, and fiction rather than highways.
- Today — lane and life together: walkers still curse rutted paths after rain while columnists mock rutted routines, the wheel-groove serving road and soul alike.
