Plaited belongs to one of the oldest and most productive "fabrication" roots of Indo-European. The reconstructed root *pleḱ- meant "to plait, to twist, to fold, to weave," and at its core is a manual gesture: the crossing of strands over and under one another. Almost every branch of the family kept some form of that gesture. In Hellenic, Greek πλέκω (plékō), attested from Homer, means "to plait, to weave, to twine," and it gave the language πλοκή (plokḗ), "a braid or web," and the strikingly apt Homeric πλοχμός (plokhmós), "a tress, a braid of hair" - an uncanny foreshadowing of the very context in which this card's word appears. In the Balto-Slavic branch the root became Proto-Slavic *plestì, "to plait, to weave," the ancestor of Russian плести (plesti) and Polish pleść, still used today both of braiding hair and of weaving words together. In Germanic it produced *flehtan-, "to braid," the source of German flechten, Old Norse flétta, Swedish fläta, and the Old English verb fleohtan, "to plait, to weave," whose noun family survived in flax, the fibre that exists precisely to be woven. In the Italic branch the root split into two specializations that have given English two enormous vocabularies. Latin plicāre, "to fold, to bend, to turn," emphasized the folding gesture and produced ply and its compounds apply, imply, reply, and employ, together with implicit, explicit, complicate, multiplicate, supplicate (literally "to fold the knees"), and duplicate (literally "to fold twofold"). Latin plectere, "to plait, to weave, to twine," kept the weaving sense and gave English plexus, complex, and perplex - a "complex" thing being, etymologically, something woven together from many strands. Both lines descend independently from *pleḱ-, which makes them sisters, not parent and child; this is the branch structure that the linear chain for plait must thread through plicāre, never through plectere. The specific history of the English word plait is a story of French importation. From the Latin past participle plicātum the Romance vernaculars formed a noun meaning "a fold": Old French pleit or ploit. When the Normans brought their legal and administrative language to England, Anglo-French carried this noun across the Channel, and by the late fourteenth century Middle English had pleit / plait, "a fold, a plait," used first of cloth. At this stage the word is purely a fold word: a plait of cloth, a plait of a garment, the ancestor of the modern pleat. It is from this fold sense, not from any new borrowing, that the braid sense grew. Hair is a material that can be folded and interwoven, and by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries plait and plaiting are firmly attached to hair and to interwoven strands of straw, leather, and rope; the participle plaited is attested from the seventeenth century in exactly the sense this card's context uses - hair dressed and interwoven with silk. Several forces shaped the word's modern profile. First, the spelling split of the nineteenth century: when printers and lexicographers began to regularize, the cloth sense was respelled pleat (following the French-derived pleat spelling and reinforcing the distinction), leaving plait to the hair and strand senses. Second, the competition with braid: both words mean the same act of interweaving, but braid, from Old English bregdan "to move quickly, weave," became the dominant everyday term in American English, pushing plait toward a distinctly British and somewhat old-fashioned register. Third, the technical afterlife: plaiting survives as a precise craft term in ropework, basketry, and electrical engineering, where the over-under interlacing is the operative method. The sound changes along the line are light. The Latin plicāre loses its middle syllable on the way to the French noun (compare plicātum -> Old French pleit), and English adapts the French pleit to its own vowel system as plait, pronounced to rhyme with "flat" in conservative usage and increasingly with "plate" in modern British speech. The semantic drift, by contrast, is rich: fold of cloth -> fold of hair -> braid of hair -> any interwoven strand-work -> craft technique, with the parallel modern drift of pleat back toward pure dressmaking. Literature keeps the word alive in its oldest texture: from the plaited hair of fairy-tale heroines to the "plaited" ropes of nautical fiction and the plaiting of straw in rural verse, the participle never loses its manual, tactile character - it is a word of hands working strands, the very gesture that opens its Indo-European biography.
