• Elijah: Similar because both are biblical Hebrew theophoric masculine names sharing the divine root *ʾĒl* ('God') and celebrating monotheistic devotion; differs because Elijah (Hebrew *ʾĒliyyāhū*, 'My God is Yahweh') explicitly incorporates the tetragrammaton (*Yah*), whereas Elihu (*ʾĔlīhū*, 'My God is He') employs the third-person personal pronoun (*hūʾ*), creating a distinct theological emphasis.
  • Elisha: Similar because both are classical Old Testament prophet names of Hebrew origin beginning with *ʾĒl*; differs because Elisha (*ʾĔlīšaʿ*) translates as 'God is my salvation' or 'God is abundance,' emphasizing divine deliverance rather than the pronoun-focused monotheistic confession of Elihu.
  • Joel: Similar because both are Semitic theophoric personal names declaring the identity of God; differs because Joel (*Yōʾēl*) inverts the components to mean 'Yahweh is God,' functioning as the mirror image of names beginning with *ʾĒl*.
  • Theophoric name: Similar because Elihu belongs to the taxonomic category of theophoric names (personal names containing the name or title of a deity); differs because theophoric name is an abstract onomastic classification, whereas Elihu is an individual proper noun and historical moniker.
  • Puritan namesake: Similar because Elihu was frequently bestowed by seventeenth-century English Puritans as an emblem of pious biblical heritage; differs because Puritan namesake is an onomastic cultural category encompassing hundreds of Old Testament names (e.g., Increase, Cotton, Obadiah, Ebenezer).
  • Patronymic: Similar because historical names like Elihu frequently evolved into family patronymics or institutional identifiers; differs because a patronymic specifically denotes a surname derived from the father's given name, whereas Elihu functions primarily as an independent given name.
  • Eli: Similar because Eli (*ʿĒlī*) shares the identical English phonetic opening and serves as the universal collegiate nickname for Yale University students and alumni ('an Eli'); differs etymologically because the biblical name Eli (1 Samuel) derives from the distinct Hebrew root *ʿ-l-h* meaning 'elevated, high, ascended' (with the initial guttural letter *ʿAyin*), whereas the first element of Elihu is *ʾēl* ('God', with an initial *ʾAleph*).
