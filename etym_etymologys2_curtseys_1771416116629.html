Stage 1 — PIE *gher- "to grasp, enclose, surround" (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE). The root splits into two faces: the grasping hand that seizes and the encircling wall that holds a space in; the "enclosing" face feeds the whole court-family of words. Stage 2 — PIE *ghor-to- "that which encloses, enclosure" (zero-grade derivative of *gher-) → Latin hortus, horti "garden, enclosed plot" (attested from the 2nd–1st c. BCE; e.g. Varro, De Re Rustica). Stage 3 — Latin cohors, cohortis "enclosed yard; farmyard; company, retinue, troop" — formed from com- "together" + hortus "enclosure" (Caesar, Gallic War, 1st c. BCE); later also the military cohort of the legion. Stage 4 — (Vulgar Latin) *curtis / *cortem "enclosed yard; manorial court, courtyard" (Late Latin, c. 4th–6th c. CE). Stage 5 — Old French cort (later cour) "court; the lord's enclosed residence and seat of rule and justice" (10th–11th c.) → Old French cortois "courtly, of the court" (11th c.) → Old French cortoisie "courtliness, courteous behaviour" (12th c.). Stage 6 — Middle English courtesy / curteisie "courteous conduct, courtly virtue" (borrowed via Anglo-Norman in the 13th c.). Stage 7 — English courtesy → curtsey/curtsy "the woman's knee-bending gesture of greeting" (specialized in the 16th c.; "to make a courtesy" is the first recorded form; spelling curtsey established 16th–17th c.). Stage 8 — English CURTSEYS (plural noun, 16th c.; this card's word). NOTE ON DEBATE: the traditional link of Sanskrit griha- "house" (Hindi ghar) to *gher- is not universally accepted, though the link of Greek khortos "enclosed pasture" (Homer) and Latin hortus is secure. The exact phonetics of the shift from Late Latin *curtis to Old French cort follow the regular loss of unstressed syllables in Proto-Romance and Gallo-Romance; the modern French spelling cour reflects a later scribal reform.
