- [Old English / c. 8th-11th c.] - ġeþrang - [crushing pressure, martial turmoil, oppression, distress, and the violent press of warriors in the shield-wall (scyldburh)]
- [Middle English / 12th-15th c.] - thrang / throng - [broadened from military combat and oppressive distress to encompass dense market gatherings, civic assemblies, and the physical act of crowding together]
- [Early Modern English / 16th-17th c.] - throng - [established in Renaissance drama (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton) for royal court assemblies, public festive gatherings, and figurative cognitive overcrowding of thoughts]
- [18th-19th c. / Industrial & Victorian Eras] - throng - [evoked the swelling populations of industrialised cities, bustling metropolitan thoroughfares, and elegant ballroom society (as in Oscar Wilde's prose)]
- [20th-21st c. / Modern Era] - throng - [retained in high journalism, literature, and formal prose to describe enthusiastic festival crowds, political rallies, and cultural assemblies]
