- Late 16th c. – Coined in Elizabethan naval jargon (from *broad* + *side*) to designate the physical flank of an armed warship and the simultaneous discharge of its artillery.
- 17th c. – Adopted into the printing trade for single-sheet publications, royal proclamations, and popular street *broadside ballads*.
- 18th–19th c. – Enters mainstream literary, journalistic, and political rhetoric as a vivid metaphor for a blistering intellectual assault or polemical offensive.
- 20th c. – Expands into automotive and traffic reporting to describe side-impact collisions (*broadsided*), while retaining its military and rhetorical senses.
- 21st c. – Frequently used in political journalism and media commentary to describe sweeping, aggressive public denunciations.
