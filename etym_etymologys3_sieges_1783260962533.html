SIEGES — Sitting Down Before the Walls Sieges belongs to one of the most securely attested roots in all of Indo-European: the verb *sed-, "to sit," a root so well documented that its reconstruction is beyond reasonable doubt. It surfaces across the family in Sanskrit sad-, "to sit" (with sádas, "seat, assembly"), Greek hédra, "seat," which through kathédra and Latin cathedra gave English cathedral and, via French, chair, and throughout the Germanic world in English sit, set, settle, saddle, and even nest, which is etymologically the "sitting-down place." This is a root of stillness and establishment — to sit is to take a place, to remain, to settle — and nearly every one of its descendants preserves that core of fixed position. The Latin branch, sedēre, "to sit," was extraordinarily productive because Roman public life was conducted by people who were literally sitting: sessiō, "a sitting" of an assembly, gives session; assidēre, "to sit beside," gives assess and assessor, the judge's companions; praesidēre, "to sit before," gives preside and president; residēre, "to sit back," gives reside; possidēre, "to sit as master over," gives possess; subsidēre, "to sink down," gives subside and subsidy; and īnsidiae, "ambushes," from those who "sit in wait," gives insidious. For our word the crucial member is obsidēre, "to sit before or against" — ob-, "against," plus sedēre — which the Romans used for an army that took its position in front of a town and blockaded it, the ancestor of the military sense and of the rare English adjective obsidional, "pertaining to a siege." The direct path to English runs through the spoken Latin of the provinces. From sedēre, Late Latin formed the noun *sedicum, "a seat, a resting-place, a position" — a word reconstructed from its Romance descendants — and from it Old French produced sege and siege, "a seat, a throne." The semantic leap from "seat" to "military siege" happened in medieval France through a chain of images: a king on his throne occupied his "seat of power"; an army that settled before a town "seated itself" there in a fixed encampment; and the verb assiéger, "to besiege," literally means "to seat oneself before." What began as a throne ended as a blockade because both are the same act of taking and holding a position — the besieger, in the oldest metaphor, is simply an army that sits down in front of the walls and refuses to leave. Middle English took sege in the thirteenth century, and for centuries the word retained both meanings at once: Chaucer and his contemporaries speak of "sieges" that are thrones and "sieges" that are blockades, and the seat-sense lingered into the Renaissance in phrases such as "the siege of majesty." As warfare changed — as castles and walled towns dominated medieval strategy, and as gunpowder made the reduction of fortresses the central art of war in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries — the military sense wholly eclipsed the throne, and siege became the epic set-piece of military history: Vienna, La Rochelle, Derry, Sebastopol, Leningrad. By Macaulay's time, "battles and sieges" was already the shorthand for old-fashioned chronicle history, the very stuff he disclaims in the sentence that frames this card. The modern career of the word is entirely metaphorical. From the seventeenth century onward, siege came to describe any prolonged, encircling pressure: a "siege" of illness, a city "under siege" by disease, a public figure "under siege" from reporters. Today the word operates in journalism, in law-enforcement language (a "hostage siege," a "siege mentality"), in medicine and public health, in sport, and even in computing, where a network can be "under siege" from hackers. Through every one of these extensions the ancient image holds: to besiege is to sit down before something and wait it out. The PIE root *sed- has given English everything from "sitting" to "president," but its most dramatic descendant is this patient, implacable word — an army's seated persistence, hardened into one of the great nouns of war.
