• sarcotesta: Similar because both are fleshy, edible structures surrounding a seed; differs because a sarcotesta develops directly as the fleshy outer layer of the seed coat (testa) itself (as in pomegranate or ginkgo), whereas a true aril originates as an outgrowth from the seed stalk (funiculus) or hilum.
  • arillode: Similar because both form fleshy envelopes around seeds; differs because an arillode (or false aril) arises specifically from the tissue surrounding the micropyle rather than from the funicular stalk.
  • caruncle: Similar because both are seed outgrowths; differs because a caruncle is a small, localized, non-enveloping outgrowth near the micropyle (common in the spurge family, *Euphorbiaceae*), often functioning as a lipid-rich elaiosome for ants.
  • mace: Similar because mace is literally an aril; differs because mace is the specific culinary and commercial name for the dried, ground scarlet aril of the nutmeg tree (*Myristica fragrans*).
  • seed coat: Similar because both enclose the seed; differs because the seed coat (testa) is the primary protective outer covering derived from the ovule's integuments, whereas the aril is an accessory exterior outgrowth.
  • integument: Similar as an envelope of the ovule; differs because the integument is the embryonic cellular layer that later matures into the seed coat, rather than a post-fertilization fleshy structure.
  • pericarp: Similar because both can be fleshy and edible; differs because the pericarp is the true fruit wall derived from the ovary (comprising exocarp, mesocarp, and endocarp), whereas the aril arises directly from seed tissues.
  • endocarp: Similar as a layer adjacent to the seed; differs because the endocarp is the innermost layer of the fruit wall (such as the hard stone of a peach or the membrane of an apple core), not a seed appendage.
  • mesocarp: Similar because both frequently form sweet, juicy fruit flesh; differs because the mesocarp is the middle layer of the fruit wall (like the flesh of a plum or mango), developing from the ovary rather than the seed stalk.
  • elaiosome: Similar because both are fleshy seed appendages rewarding animal dispersers; differs because an elaiosome is specifically rich in lipids and proteins, adapted exclusively for ant dispersal (myrmecochory).
  • tunic: Similar in providing an outer wrapping; differs because a tunic is a dry, papery covering found around bulbs and corms (such as onions), entirely unrelated to seed dissemination.
