Uppish is a transparent English formation — up + -ish — whose bluntness is the point. To call someone uppish is to accuse them of going up beyond their proper level. Its etymology is therefore twofold: the ancient directional adverb up and the young evaluative suffix -ish that turns it into character judgment. Both strands are well attested.

The base up descends without break from PIE *upo, meaning from below, up, above, a particle that also yields Greek hypo- under, Latin sub under, Sanskrit upa near. In Proto-Germanic it becomes adverbial *uppa / *upp, up, upwards, preserved in Old English upp, Old Saxon up, Old Norse upp. Old and Middle English use it purely spatially: go up, look up, up the hill. No arrogance yet. The moralization of vertical space — HIGH is proud, LOW is humble — is pan-European Christian metaphor, but English lexicalizes it late with uppish, uppity, upstart, high-hat, stuck-up.

  • PIE *upo to Proto-Germanic *uppa: Regular loss of final vowel, gemination. Meaning stays directional. Cognates across Indo-European confirm antiquity.
  • Old English upp to Middle English up: Phonetic simplification of double consonant, spelling up stabilized after Norman Conquest. Still purely adverb/preposition: up and down, up to London.
  • Suffix -ish: From PIE adjectival *-iskos via Proto-Germanic *-iskaz (as in English, Danish meaning belonging to), to Old English -isc (Englisc), to Middle English -ish after palatalization. By Early Modern English it gains a second, dismissive sense: childish, foolish, snobbish — having the bad character of. Adding -ish to an adverb to make an adjective of behaviour — uppish, stuffy-ish, la-di-da-ish — is colloquial 18th-century wordplay.
  • 1728 birth: OED first records uppish in 1728 meaning pert, self-assertive, arrogant. Context is class: servants, tradesmen, juniors accused of forgetting their place. Jonathan Swift's world of footmen and masters is the habitat. The double p preserves the stem upp- before vowel suffix, as in upping.
  • 19th c. class weapon: Victorian masters complain servants are getting uppish; colonial administrators complain natives are getting uppish; men complain women are getting uppish. The word does ideological work: upward mobility framed as rudeness. American South creates parallel uppity (1880s) with same function, infamously racialized.
  • 20th c. general arrogance: In Wodehouse, Waugh and Orwell, uppish loosens from service to any snobbery: public-school boys, parvenus, officials. "The best of them get uppish" if you grovel — i.e., deference inflates ego. Meaning now: conceited, haughty, putting on airs, regardless of origin.

Phonology is uneventful: /ʌp/ plus /ɪʃ/ yields /ˈʌpɪʃ/ with stress on stem. Spelling with double p follows English doubling before suffix. The near-twin uppity (up + -ity? or up + -y + -ty) overtakes it in American English, while uppish remains chiefly British, slightly comic, slightly dated — exactly why Wodehouse favours it.

Culturally uppish encodes British anxiety about hierarchy. Unlike neutral arrogant from Latin, which condemns character universally, uppish condemns movement: you have risen. It implies the speaker's right to set the ceiling. Hence its decline as deference declines, and its survival as deliberate period flavour or ironic self-deprecation: "I was feeling a bit uppish." To call a wine, restaurant or cat uppish today is to joke with hierarchy while disavowing it.

Modern dictionaries label it informal, chiefly British, often with note "disapproving." That disapproval is the fossil of 1728. The PIE particle that once merely pointed skyward now points a finger.
