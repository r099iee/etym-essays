pavenders
- 1952 - coinage: C. S. Lewis invents pavender in The Voyage of the Dawn Treader to name a fictional rainbow-colored fish of the Eastern Sea of Narnia; from birth it is engineered to pass as an old, established species name - two syllables, -er final, sitting comfortably beside gudgeon, pilchard, prawn, and bream.
- Golden-age freight - within the book's own world the fish is bound to the lost splendour of Cair Paravel: "which they all remembered eating in the old days," so the word carries nostalgia from its first page; catching and eating a pavender is a small revival of Narnia's golden age.
- Post-publication afterlife - the word lives only in the Narnia corpus, one of Lewis's naturalistic coinages alongside the Dufflepuds and the Marsh-wiggles; its rainbow colouring invites a speculative echo of pavonine ("peacock-coloured," from Latin pāvo), though that is inspiration, not inheritance.
- Contemporary - a knowingly allusive word: any modern use is a fan's wink at the Dawn Treader's feast; it has no register outside the book, and its charm is precisely that a made-up name can sound old, ordinary, and delicious.
