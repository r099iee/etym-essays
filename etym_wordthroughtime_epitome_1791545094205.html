- 16th c. – Humanist literary abridgment: Introduced into English from Greek via Latin as a scholarly term for a concise digest, summary, or condensed compendium of a long treatise or historical chronicle.
- Early 17th c. – Microcosmic and miniature representation: Extended philosophically to refer to anything that contains in miniature the essential elements of a greater whole (such as humanity viewed as an "epitome of the universe").
- 18th c. – Personification and embodiment: Shifted from textual abridgment toward social and moral representation, used to describe people who concentrated all the virtues, vices, or characteristics of an age or social class.
- 19th c. – Quintessential exemplar: Became established in Victorian prose as the standard idiom for the ultimate archetype or consummate specimen of an aesthetic, style, or temperament ("the epitome of elegance").
- 20th c. to Present – Universal cultural archetype: Cemented as a ubiquitous colloquial and journalistic praise-word for ideal models across fashion, politics, sports, and ethics, while its original textual meaning ("summary") receded into historical and philological contexts.
