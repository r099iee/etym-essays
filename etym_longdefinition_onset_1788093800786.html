Onset is a noun with two historically ordered senses. (1) The older, now literary sense: an attack, assault or vigorous charge — the act of setting upon an enemy. (2) The modern default sense: the beginning or start of something, especially of something unwelcome, inevitable or beyond one's control — the onset of winter, of illness, of a storm. Tolkien's sentence, "as the valley widened his onset grew ever slower", works in the older sense: the charge of the goblin host loses momentum as the ground opens out, so the very word carries the military image of forces being "set on" the foe.

  • Sense 1 — the attack. Entering English as a military word (noun, 16th c., from the compound verb onsetten, "to set on, assail", which continues Old English onsett(an)), "onset" meant the violent opening of a fight: "the onset of battle", "a furious onset". In this sense it runs parallel to "assault", "charge" and "onslaught", and it survives in historical prose, battle narrative and deliberately epic register.
  • Sense 2 — the beginning. By the 1560s the same word had slid to "the coming-on of a period or condition" — the onset of winter, of old age, of night. The semantic bridge is the verb's own picture: an attack is something that "comes on" you, unasked; so any arrival — a season, a sickness, a crisis — has its onset. Once the slide happens the aggressive colour fades, and the word becomes the neutral-to-ominous name of an arrival over which one has little control.
  • Medicine and epidemiology. Modern clinical writing uses "onset" as a precise timestamp: acute versus insidious onset, sudden onset, age at onset, early-onset and late-onset disease, "the onset of symptoms" — a standard phrase in case descriptions and public-health reporting, where the time of onset is epidemiology's primary measurement.
  • Linguistics. In phonology (20th century) the "onset" of a syllable is its initial consonant or consonant cluster, the first member of the descriptive triad onset–nucleus–coda that analyses every syllable; the term was consciously chosen from the "beginning" sense, and onset–rhyme theory is now a standard table of undergraduate linguistics.
  • Other technical domains. Meteorology and climatology speak of the "onset of the monsoon"; psychology of the onset of depression or schizophrenia; pharmacology of slow or rapid onset of action (how fast a drug takes effect); music information technology uses "onset detection" for the instant a note or percussive event begins. In every case the sense is the arrival of an event, not the violence of an attacker.

Current profile. In daily English the aggressive sense has withered — speakers say "attack", "assault" or "onslaught" (a different word, from Old Norse, which kept the violence), while "onset" is reserved for beginnings: "at the onset" is wholly benign, "the onset of war" ominous. The word also sits in the family of set-compounds — onset, outset, offset, upset, upshot, set-to — each a fossil of a different "placing" figure, and its noun stress on the first syllable (/ˈɒn.set/) distinguishes it from the verb phrase "set on". Tolkien's usage is a deliberate archaism: a writer of the 1930s reaching for the old military word gives the modern reader the word's whole biography in a single line.
