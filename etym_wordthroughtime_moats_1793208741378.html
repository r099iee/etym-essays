- c. 1300 — the castle ditch: Middle English mote names the trench around walls and keeps, wet or dry, twin of the drawbridge and the portcullis.
- Late medieval — towns take it up: walled cities ring themselves with water, and the moat becomes civic as well as baronial.
- 17th–18th c. — from defence to ornament: artillery makes moats militarily obsolete, so great houses keep them as picturesque water features, and poets make the moated grange an emblem of seclusion.
- 19th c. — the tourist moat: drained and grassed moats like the Tower's become promenades, and the word turns romantic and antiquarian.
- 1990s — the business metaphor: the economic moat carries the castle into the stock market, and the financial press adopts it wholesale.
- Today — heritage and finance share it: visitors photograph Bodiam's still waters while analysts rate companies by the width of their moats.
