- Antiquity (c. 1500 BCE – 5th Century CE) - Known in Babylon, Egypt, Greece, and Rome under terms like gnomon, sciothericon, or hemicyclium, serving as the paramount scientific device for public and civil timekeeping - Sacred astronomical and civic instrument.
- Middle Ages (6th–14th Century) - Maintained across Islamic observatories and carved onto south-facing walls of Christian monasteries (canonical sundials or scratch dials) to regulate monastic prayer hours (matins, terce, sext, none) - Religious and liturgical timekeeper.
- 15th–17th Century - Compounded in Middle and Early Modern English as 'sun-dial' (distinguishing it from the dial plate of newly invented mechanical escapement clocks); widely installed in public plazas and private estates - Scientific triumph and mathematical prestige.
- 18th–19th Century - Became a central aesthetic centerpiece of formal English gardens and a poignant literary symbol in Romantic and Victorian poetry (e.g. Oscar Wilde, John Keats) for the passage of time, mortality, and sunlit serenity - Romantic, pastoral, and philosophical emblem.
- 20th–21st Century - Preserved primarily as historical monuments, garden decorative art, and educational astronomical instruments demonstrating apparent solar time and celestial mechanics - Horological heritage and ornamental antiquity.
