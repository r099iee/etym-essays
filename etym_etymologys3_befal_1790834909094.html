The verb befal (in modern standard orthography befall) is an ancient, purely Germanic strong verb whose developmental lineage reaches directly back to the Proto-Indo-European root *pōl- (or *pʰol-), expressing the physical action of falling, collapsing, or dropping downward under gravity. In the Germanic linguistic family, this root generated the fundamental Class VII strong verb *fallaną, which formed the nucleus of numerous prepositional compounds.

Proto-Germanic Morphological Synthesis:

  • In Proto-Germanic, the verbal prefix *bi- (an unaccented proclitic form of the preposition *umbi / *bi, meaning "around, about, upon, by", ultimately from PIE *h₂m̥bʰi) was compounded with the root verb *fallaną ("to fall").
  • This yielded the Proto-Germanic strong verb *bifallaną. The prefix functioned both transitivizingly and applicatively, shifting the intransitive motion of "falling" into a transitive action of "falling upon", "descending around", or "besetting" a specific target.
  • Cognates developed across the Germanic branches: Old Saxon bifallan, Old High German bifallan (whence Modern German befallen, "to attack, infest, affect"), and Middle Dutch bevallen ("to please, happen to, give birth").

Old English Period (c. 450–1150 CE):

In Old English, the verb was attested as befeallan (past tense befeoll, past participle befeallen). In Anglo-Saxon poetry and prose, befeallan exhibited a dynamic spectrum of meanings:

  • It functioned concretely to describe physical falling or crashing down ("the building fell upon them").
  • It denoted sudden deprivation or loss when used with an instrumental or genitive construction (e.g., to be "befallen of life" or "befallen of friends", meaning to be bereft).
  • Crucially, it began its metaphorical abstraction to denote the arrival of events, fortunes, or doom upon individuals: in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Old English homilies, disasters, divine judgements, and royal deaths are described as having befeallen upon the land.

Middle English Flourishing and Narrative Formulas (c. 1150–1500 CE):

  • Following the phonological erosion of inflections in Early Middle English, the prefix normalized to be- / bi-, and the verb emerged as befallen / bifallen (past: bifel / befel, participle: bifalle / befallen).
  • During the 14th century, bifallen became one of the quintessential narrative framing verbs in Middle English literature. Geoffrey Chaucer famously opens the General Prologue of The Canterbury Tales with this exact verb: "Bifil that in that seson on a day..." ("It befell that in that season on a day...").
  • The verb functioned as the supreme bridge between chance, providence, and storytelling, capturing the medieval worldview that events do not merely happen mechanically, but "befall" humans according to the hidden designs of God or Fortune's wheel.

Early Modern English Orthography and Semantic Narrowing (c. 1500–1800 CE):

During the Early Modern English period, spelling conventions for monosyllabic and disyllabic words with final /l/ were highly fluid. Writers and printers frequently utilized single-l forms such as befal, recal, instal, and fulfil alongside double-l variants.

  • In 17th- and 18th-century prose—exemplified by John Milton's Paradise Lost, Joseph Addison's The Spectator, and Jonathan Swift's essays—the variant befal was widely used.
  • Semantically, as everyday spoken English increasingly favored simpler Romance borrowings (happen, from Old Norse happ, and occur), befal became restricted to higher-order moral, poetic, and ominous discourses. It became the verb par excellence for grave adversities, dramatic twists of fate, and moral warnings.
  • Phonologically, the root vowel underwent regular development: the Old English short diphthong in feallan smoothed to Middle English /a/, which lengthened and retracted before /l/ during the Early Modern period, producing the rounded back vowel /ɔː/ seen in modern RP /bɪˈfɔːl/.

Modern Status:

Today, while standard modern dictionaries list befall with double-l, the historical text form befal remains an evocative monument to classical English prose, carrying the weight of ancient Germanic fatalism into modern philosophical reflections on destiny.
