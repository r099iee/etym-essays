The biography of CONTENTEDLY begins with a root of stretching. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch," one of the most productive verbal roots of the whole family: Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches," Greek teinein "to stretch" (whence tendon, tone, and the tennis of the racket's taut strings), and Latin tendere "to stretch" all descend from it. From tendere, Latin derived tenere, "to hold, to keep," and the step from stretching to holding is a physical one — the hand that stretches out and grasps keeps its object; the taut rope that is stretched and then secured holds its tension. Roman compound-forming pressed tenere into service everywhere: with ad- "toward" it made attinere "to pertain," with ob- "against" obtinere "to obtain," with re- "back" retinere "to retain," and with con- "together" continere, "to hold together, to contain." The decisive semantic moment in the word's history came with the past participle contentus, literally "held together, contained." In Roman moral vocabulary the participle shifted from describing a thing that is physically held within bounds to describing a person whose desires are held within bounds — the contentus man is the self-contained man, the one who wants nothing more because everything he needs is already within him. This is the great historical bridge of the word: containment of object became containment of appetite, and containment of appetite became satisfaction. The famous Stoic ideal of autarkeia, self-sufficiency, was rendered into Latin with exactly this vocabulary; the contented man was a philosophical ideal long before he was a kitchen word. From Latin the word travelled into the Romance world. Old French took the adjective content "satisfied, pleased," and English borrowed it around 1400, at first of persons ("content with one's lot"). From the adjective English formed a back-formation verb to content, "to satisfy," and from that verb's past participle came the adjective contented in the sixteenth century, which carried the stronger, more abiding sense of a settled inner state rather than a momentary satisfaction. The adverb contentedly was then built with the native suffix -ly, from Old English -lice, itself from Proto-Germanic *-liko, "in the manner or form of," so that contentedly means literally "in the manner of one who is contented." The sound changes along the chain are modest but real. The PIE root *ten- appears in Latin with the vowel e retained (tendere, tenere); the compound continere shows the regular Latin prefix form con- before t, with assimilation of the nasal; and the participle contentus preserves the -nt- cluster of the present stem rather than the past-participle -t- of the base, a typically Latin formation. Across the French and English stages the word lost its classical inflections: the Latin case endings of contentus vanished into the bare French content, and English content and contented show the typical Middle English loss of final unstressed syllables and the shift of stress to the first syllable in the noun-adjective. The only fully English innovation is the adverbial -ly, applied natively. Semantically, the word has never wavered from its core image: to be content is to be contained. What has changed is the register and the company it keeps. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries contented was a moral and religious word, the blessed state of the man who does not covet; the Prayer Book's "contentedness with our estate" belongs to this older world. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word had become domestic and sentimental, the homely virtue of the hearth, the "contented mind" of the moral essayists. In modern English the adverb is far rarer than the adjective, and it survives mainly in precisely the registers Tolkien exploits — the cozy, the comic, and the gently ironic. In The Hobbit, Bilbo's "sleep contentedly" is the adverb at full power: after the terrors of the goblin tunnels, the word declares that everything he wants is contained, and the "though" that follows — "he would have liked a loaf and butter better" — is the hobbit's comic confession that even the most perfectly contained desires may leak a little.
  • PIE *ten- (stretch) — Sanskrit tanoti, Greek teinein, Latin tendere: the root of extension and tension.
  • Latin tenere (to hold) — the stretched hand becomes the holding hand; *ten- powers the whole Latin family of keeping.
  • Latin continere (to hold together) — con- + tenere, the compound at the heart of the English word.
  • Latin contentus (held in check) — the past participle that became the moral ideal: desires contained, hence satisfied.
  • Old French content — the participle loses its inflections and becomes a bare adjective of satisfaction.
  • English content/contented/contentedly — the adjective (c. 1400), the verb, the participle-adjective (16th c.), and the adverb with native -ly (c. 1600).
The word's literary life is richest exactly where its etymology predicts. The Stoic and Christian traditions make contentment the highest attainable calm; the English moralists make it a private virtue; and Tolkien makes it the characteristic inwardness of the hobbit, a being defined by sufficiency rather than desire. Contentedly is thus a word with a long moral memory: it began as a term of physical containment in Roman engineering vocabulary, became a Stoic ideal of self-sufficiency, and now does its quiet work in the nursery, the hearth, and the fantasy novel — the adverb of a full stomach, a safe fire, and a mind that, for a moment, wants nothing more than what it has.
