• Barrage: Similar because both denote a heavy discharge of weapons or words; differs because a barrage is an unbroken, sustained, and prolonged artillery bombardment designed to create a continuous wall of fire, whereas a volley is a single synchronized, simultaneous discharge of multiple weapons at once.
  • Salvo: Similar because both describe the simultaneous discharge of firearms; differs because a salvo (from Italian salva) is typically associated with naval artillery or heavy guns fired together as a broadside or formal salute, whereas volley applies to small arms, arrows, tennis strokes, and soccer kicks.
  • Fusillade: Similar because both involve the firing of firearms; differs because a fusillade (from French fusil) denotes a rapid, continuous, and often ragged outburst of multiple rifle shots in quick succession rather than a single disciplined, simultaneous snap.
  • Broadside: Similar because both describe simultaneous firing; differs because a broadside specifically denotes all the artillery pieces on one entire side of a warship firing simultaneously, or an all-out published written denunciation, while volley is broader across sports, archery, and rhetoric.
  • Hail: Similar because both describe a shower of missiles or words descending on a target; differs because a hail suggests an uncontrolled natural precipitation of countless chaotic objects, whereas a volley suggests intentional, coordinated military or athletic action.
  • Discharge: Similar because both refer to the firing of a weapon; differs because discharge is a generic, technical term for the release of any firearm or missile (even a single shot), while volley requires multiple missiles fired together in coordinated flight.
  • Battery: Similar because both are associated with military artillery; differs because a battery refers to the physical tactical unit of artillery guns or their fortified emplacement, whereas a volley is the actual shot fired by those guns.
  • Burst: Similar because both signify a sudden release of energy or fire; differs because a burst refers to a brief, continuous cycle of automatic gunfire from a single weapon, while a volley involves multiple distinct weapons firing in unison.
