BY AND LARGE is a fixed idiomatic phrase meaning “on the whole, taking everything into consideration, in most respects, generally speaking.” It is used to qualify a general statement so that it asserts a broad truth while quietly allowing for exceptions. In the context of the card — a scholarly monograph on English metrics in which the author announces that “I have had to limit my scope to a single remarkably homogeneous body of poetry: the main tradition of regular accentual-syllabic verse in Middle and Modern English” — the phrase performs the classic rhetorical work of the hedged generalization: it concedes that other verse traditions exist and have been deliberately set aside, while affirming that, exceptions apart, the chosen corpus is the relevant field of study. The author does not claim that his limitation is absolute, only that it is the right general decision.

Meaning and nuance. The phrase has three parts of semantic interest. First, it is inclusive rather than exclusive: unlike “altogether,” “entirely,” or “completely,” which admit no exceptions, “by and large” asserts that the statement holds across the great run of cases while leaving the door open for anomalies. Second, it is a sentence or clause adverbial — it modifies the whole assertion and typically stands at the head of the clause (“By and large, the results were consistent”), though it can appear mid-clause. Third, it is a frozen, irreversible binomial: one cannot say “large and by,” and the two elements cannot be separated or reordered, a fact that marks the phrase as a single idiomatic unit rather than a freely assembled prepositional phrase. Its register is polite and slightly old-fashioned but entirely standard; it is equally at home in casual conversation and in the cautious prose of scholarship, where it licenses a responsible generalization.

Polysemy and historical senses.

  • Modern idiomatic sense (the card’s sense): “on the whole, everything considered, generally speaking” — the only sense in ordinary modern use.
  • Literal nautical sense (17th century, now obsolete as a technical phrase): “close-hauled (sailing ‘by’ the wind) and running with the wind free (‘large’)” — i.e. handling a ship in all conditions of wind; a vessel that sailed “by and large” performed creditably in every condition.
  • Interim sense (early 18th century): “in all respects, everywhere, taking everything together” — the step between the nautical literal sense and the modern hedged one, attested in writers such as Defoe and Swift.
  • Modern clausal use with concessive force: in academic and journalistic prose the phrase often carries an implicit concession — “by and large X is true (although I admit exceptions)” — which is exactly its force in the card’s sentence.

The historical bridge. The phrase was born at sea. In seventeenth-century seamanship, a ship “sailed by” when she was close-hauled, lying as near to the wind as her rig would allow, with the sails sheeted in hard; she “sailed large” when the wind was free, coming from abaft the beam, with the sails eased out. A ship that handled “by and large” was one that behaved well in both conditions — beating into the wind and running with it — and the phrase passed into the language of sailors as shorthand for “under every circumstance of wind and sea.” The generalization is the natural next step: a ship that can be trusted in all conditions is a ship that can be summed up, and by the early eighteenth century the phrase meant “in all respects, on the whole.” The modern sense is simply the final, fully abstracted form of that nautical verdict, with only a faintly salty flavour remaining to recall the forecastle.

Grammar, register, and collocations. The phrase functions as a sentence adverbial, most often at the start of a clause (“By and large, the theory holds”), occasionally parenthetically in mid-sentence (“The plan, by and large, worked”), and rarely at the end. It combines with statements of tendency and majority (“by and large, most voters…”), and it is frequently paired with an acknowledging clause that follows or precedes the main assertion. Its register is informal-to-formal neutral: unlike “all things considered,” it does not invite explicit weighing of counter-arguments, and unlike “for the most part” it does not quantify a proportion; it simply sums a broad impression while leaving room for the exceptions that careful speakers and writers never forget.
