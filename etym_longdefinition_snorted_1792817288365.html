Snorted is the past tense and past participle of the verb snort: to drive air suddenly and audibly outward through the nostrils, producing the harsh trumpeting puff that humans make when scorn, amusement, or indignation escapes before words can catch it, and that horses, bulls, and other great beasts make when alarmed, exerted, or clearing their airways.

In the source sentence — Well-preserved indeed!’ he snorted — the word does the work of an entire stage direction. It is a dialogue tag of pure derision: the speaker does not merely say the words but blasts them through the nose, turning agreement inside out so that well-preserved indeed lands as its opposite, a jeer at someone who has plainly not worn well at all. This is the classic literary office of snorted: the narrator needs the reader to hear the contempt, and the nose supplies it.

Core meaning in detail: Physiologically a snort is a forced nasal exhalation — the diaphragm and chest drive air up while the soft palate shunts it through the nasal passages, whose narrow, mucus-lined chambers turn the stream turbulent and loud. Humans can snort voluntarily as a signal or involuntarily in laughter, weeping, disgust, or sudden effort. Animals snort for plainer reasons: the horse clears dust and flies and advertises alarm to the herd, the bull gathers itself before a charge, the pig roots and grumbles through its snout all day long. The human social snort borrows the animal blast and aims it.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Derisive or contemptuous exhalation (primary modern sense): to snort with contempt, snort at a suggestion — the blast that says absurd without spending a syllable. The semantic bridge is bodily: disgust and rejection live in the nose (we wrinkle it at bad smells), so expelling air through it becomes the sound of rejection itself.
  • A burst of sudden or stifled laughter (very common): to snort with laughter, to snort into one's tea — the laugh that escapes through the nose because the mouth was shut against it. The bridge is mechanical: suppressed laughter seeks any outlet, and the nose is the emergency valve, so the snort becomes the emblem of amusement barely contained.
  • The animal blast — horses, bulls, boars (oldest sense, from Middle English): the horse snorted and pawed the ground. The bridge is the word's origin: before humans snorted scornfully in novels, beasts snorted in fields, and every human use is a metaphor borrowed back from the stable and the sty.
  • To inhale a drug through the nose (slang, 20th century): to snort cocaine, to snort a line. The bridge runs the airflow in reverse: the same nasal passage that blasts air out can suck powder in, and the street verb keeps the plumbing while flipping the direction — exhalation's word conscripted for inhalation.
  • The noun sense — a single blast or a small drink (secondary): a snort of derision, and in older slang a snort of whisky for a quick nip. The bridge is metonymy twice over: the sound names one explosive puff, then any short sharp dose — of scorn or of spirits — that hits like one.
  • Snoring kin — heavy nasal breathing in sleep or exertion: older writers use snort where modern ones write snore, as of sleepers and winded runners. The bridge is family resemblance: snort and snore are imitative twins, and for centuries they swapped shifts describing every loud noise the nose makes.

Register and connotation: snorted is blunt, vivid, faintly coarse — the novelist's shortcut for a character who will not trouble with politeness. Heroes snort when provoked; villains snort by habit; comic characters snort tea through their noses. It almost never flatters: to report that someone snorted a reply is to report that the reply was rude, amused at your expense, or both. Grammatically it is usually intransitive with a prepositional tail — snorted at, snorted with, snorted in derision — though fiction loves the transitive reporting clause, he snorted, standing in for said with the contempt included free of charge.
