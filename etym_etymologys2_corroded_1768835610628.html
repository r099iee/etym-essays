PIE *red- "to gnaw, to scrape, to chew" (the root of the gnawing animals; earliest reconstructable stage) to Sanskrit radati "he bites, he gnaws" (Vedic; cognate, not the direct source of the Latin word), with rada "tooth" - the gnawing instrument to Latin rōdere "to gnaw" (classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) to Latin corrōdere "to gnaw away entirely" (com- intensive "wholly" + rōdere; classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) to Old French corroder "to gnaw away" (14th c.) to Middle English corroden "to eat away, to corrode" (14th c.) to English corrode (14th-15th c.) to English corroded "eaten away by chemical action" (past participle, used as adjective; the rootWord). Note: the same root produced Latin rōdēns, rōdentis "gnawing" (whence the zoological order Rodentia, "the gnawers", 1820s, and English rodent), and Latin ērōdere "to gnaw out" (whence erosion). English rat (Old English ræt) has traditionally been explained as "the gnawer" from the same root; the etymology is still widely cited but modern scholarship regards the origin of rat as uncertain - a probable but disputed branch. Hindi कुतरना kutarnā "to gnaw" belongs to a different root and is not a cognate.
