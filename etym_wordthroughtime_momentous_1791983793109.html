- 1650s — Coined in learned English as “of moment, important”, preserving the old sense of moment as weight and consequence; at first a bookish synonym for weighty.
- 18th c. — Settles into public and historical prose: elections, battles, treaties and discoveries are called momentous, and the word acquires its characteristic gravity.
- 19th c. — Becomes a staple of historiography and imperial rhetoric; “a momentous era”, “momentous consequences” are standard in Victorian historical writing.
- 20th c. — Widened by journalism and broadcasting to almost any noteworthy occasion, and consequently weakened by inflation; “momentous occasion” now often means merely ceremonial.
- Contemporary — Still the default elevated adjective for turning points, but increasingly used with irony or hype; the older “of moment” sense survives mainly in the fixed phrases of moment and of no moment.
