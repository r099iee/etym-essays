- [12th–13th c.] — Borrowed from Old French valour / Late Latin valor into Middle English, initially denoting general personal worth, value, high moral virtue, and spiritual or physical efficacy.
- [14th–16th c.] — Specialized during the height of medieval chivalric romance and feudal warfare into exceptional military prowess, noble gallantry, and fearlessness on the battlefield.
- [17th–18th c.] — Retained in classical literature and epic poetry as an exalted aristocratic virtue, contrasted sharply with common brawling or reckless rashness.
- [19th c. Victorian Era] — Institutionalized into formal military medals and state decorations (e.g., Britain's Victoria Cross in 1856 inscribed simply "For Valour"), cementing its status as the highest recognized standard of martial gallantry.
- [20th–21st c.] — Maintained almost exclusively in formal military citations, solemn rhetoric, and high-register prose, expanding metaphorically to describe exceptional civic, investigative, or moral courage in the face of overwhelming odds.
