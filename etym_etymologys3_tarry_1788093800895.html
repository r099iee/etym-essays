Introduction. Tarry is a word whose entire documented history is a story of decline from workaday commonness to dignified archaism — and whose origin is so obscure that even its own dictionary entries hedge. It arrived in English from the Anglo-French dialects of post-Conquest England in the later Middle Ages, flourished as an ordinary verb of waiting and staying for four centuries, was consecrated by the King James Bible, and has since outlived its usefulness in speech while gaining an almost liturgical glow in writing.

Phase 1 — the medieval French background (12th century).

  • Anglo-Norman and Old French tarier (12th c.) meant “to delay, retard, hinder.” Some sources also record a homonymous French verb tarier “to provoke, vex, tease,” which survived into modern French as tarier “to bore, weary” — and scholars disagree whether the “delay” word and the “vex” word are one verb (with “delay” developing from “to hold someone up, provoke them to stay”) or two.
  • Medieval Latin in England had tariāre “to delay,” attested in Anglo-Latin documents, and a frequently repeated theory connects these forms to Latin tardāre “to delay, retard,” from tardus “slow” (the ancestor of tardy). The OED records this link as plausible but unproved; the expected sound development from Latin into French is irregular, which is why the dictionary refuses to commit.

Phase 2 — Middle English arrival (13th–14th centuries).

  • English took the verb as tarien/tarrien, recorded from the early 14th century in the senses “to delay, to wait, to remain, to stay.”
  • Medieval usage spans love poetry, chronicles, and everyday prose: lovers tarry for meetings, hosts tarry guests, travellers tarry in towns. The transitive “to hold (someone) back” sense was still alive, as in romances where a companion is “tarried” by a fight.

Phase 3 — early modern English and the Bible (1500–1700).

  • By Shakespeare’s time tarry was a plain, high-frequency verb of waiting; Shakespeare uses it dozens of times across comedies and histories as an ordinary promise to stay.
  • The 1611 King James Version gave tarry its most famous platform, using it scores of times: “Tarry ye here, and watch with me” (Matthew 26:38), “If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee?” (John 21:22), “tarry all night” (Judges 19), among many others. The biblical register — solemn, patient, gently admonitory — began to coat the word itself.
  • Style manuals and sermon rhetoric of the 1600s–1700s treated tarry as a perfectly dignified verb, and it appeared in law, letters, and philosophy alike.

Phase 4 — retreat from speech (1700–1900).

  • Through the eighteenth century, wait, stay, and remain steadily displaced tarry in colloquial use; by the nineteenth, tarried and tarrying sounded consciously literary or rustic.
  • Novelists used it for period colour and rural speech — dialect fiction made “tarry a bit” a signature of country characters, and historical novels revived it for medieval scenes. Its modern perfume of archaism was solidified in this period.
  • Meanwhile the adjective tarry (“tar-covered”, from tar) created a permanent homograph; the two are unrelated, but their spelling collision periodically confuses readers and has kept the verb’s distinctiveness dim.

Phase 5 — twentieth century and Tolkien.

  • In the twentieth century tarry survived in hymns, Scripture reading, and literary prose. J.R.R. Tolkien, a master of archaic register, used it to give Elvish and ancient speakers gravitas: in The Hobbit, the Elvenking’s “Long will I tarry, ere I begin this war for gold” makes patient, deliberate delay the voice of ancient royalty.
  • Quotations and memes of the King James Bible keep the word alive for millions who never use it in speech; dictionaries continue to label it “archaic or literary.”

Phase 6 — today.

  • Spontaneous speech has effectively abandoned tarry except in set-phrase fossils; writing can still employ it for dignity, irony, or period texture.
  • Its semantic core — patient, voluntary waiting — is intact after seven centuries; what has changed is entirely social: a once-neutral verb of everyday waiting became a deliberately weighted one, a marker of elevated register whose meaning readers still grasp instantly from its biblical and literary echoes.

Summary. From a shadowy Anglo-French verb of delay, through four centuries of everyday English use, a biblical canonisation, a nineteenth-century slide into quaintness, and a twentieth-century afterlife in fantasy and homage, tarry illustrates how a word’s fortune can be decided less by its sound or meaning than by the company it keeps in great books.
