satchel: a small bag, typically made of soft leather, cloth, or canvas, with a single shoulder strap (or short handles) and a top flap or zip, used for carrying books, papers, and personal items. The word names a portable container that hangs at the hip and is held close against the body, and its long history runs from the coarse sackcloth of the ancient Near East, through the medieval scholar's and peddler's pouch, to the schoolchild's book-bag and the fashion accessory of today.

PRIMARY SENSE — a small bag with a shoulder strap

In its everyday modern sense, a satchel is a soft-sided bag with a flat or gusseted base, an opening at the top or from a front flap, and usually a long strap worn over one shoulder so that the bag rests at the hip. It is the classic carrier of books and papers — the schoolchild's bag, the student's or commuter's bag, the messenger's and mail-carrier's bag. The defining features that separate it from a backpack are that it hangs from a single shoulder, sits low at the side of the body, and keeps its contents close to the hip rather than distributing weight across both shoulders.

  • School satchel — the leather or cloth bag carried by schoolchildren (especially in Britain) for books and exercise books; the canonical image of education from the 19th century onward, still a standard meaning in dictionaries.
  • Postal / mail satchel — the canvas or leather shoulder bag of the postman or mail-carrier, in which letters and small parcels are sorted and delivered.
  • Messenger bag — the modern descendant: a large one-strap satchel used by bicycle couriers and commuters to carry documents and laptops.
  • Doctor's / parson's satchel — the leather bag carried by doctors making house calls or by clergy; the "black bag" of the old general practitioner is a satchel.
  • Travel satchel — a small piece of hand-baggage used for overnight travel, distinct from the larger suitcase or trunk.

SECONDARY SENSES AND SPECIALIST USES

  • Satchel charge (military) — a demolition charge carried in a canvas bag, typically a bundle of explosives with a handle, thrown or placed against fortifications; the term dates from the Second World War and is still current military and historical usage, showing the satchel as the soldier's portable carrier of dangerous cargo.
  • Satchel (equestrian) — a strap fitted around the muzzle of a horse to support the bit, also called a "satchel strap"; a narrow technical sense.
  • Satchel handbag / satchel bag (fashion) — in the contemporary fashion industry the word names a structured handbag with a top handle, sometimes with an optional shoulder strap, echoing the classic school satchel; "satchel" thus functions as a marketing and design category for a retro style of leather bag.
  • Proper name — "Satchel" is the nickname of the American baseball pitcher Leroy "Satchel" Paige (1906–1982), said to come from his large feet resembling satchels; the proper-name use is a lively reminder of the word's everydayness.

METAPHORIC AND SYMBOLIC SHIFTS

Because a satchel is worn against the body and holds a person's intimate necessities, it has become a literary and psychological emblem of the portable self — a "satchel of secrets," an archive of survival. In the context card, Shi-yin snatches the satchel from the madman's shoulder, and the bag becomes a vessel of hidden interiority: the sum of a wandering life carried close to the flesh. The semantic bridge runs from the physical fact (a bag that hangs at the hip, guarded by the arm) to the symbolic reading (a person's gathered belongings as their identity). A related shift makes the satchel the emblem of the travelling archive: scholars, couriers, peddlers, and postmen carried the culture's letters and learning, so the satchel historically implied literacy, errand, and communication as much as luggage.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES

  • vs backpack / knapsack — larger, worn on both shoulders, built for load-bearing travel and weight distribution; the satchel is softer, single-strapped, and carried at the hip.
  • vs purse / wallet — far smaller, for money and cosmetics; the satchel has the volume for books, papers, and tools.
  • vs briefcase — rigid, formal, hand-carried business case; the satchel is soft, informal, and slung over the shoulder.
  • vs haversack — the rugged canvas provision-bag of the soldier or hiker; the satchel retains a scholarly, civilian, day-to-day quality.
  • vs pouch — a small drawstring or flap bag; the satchel is large enough to carry documents.

HISTORICAL BRIDGES

The chain of senses maps neatly onto history. The earliest ancestors, Hebrew śaq and Greek sákkos, named the coarse goat-hair sack used for grain storage or worn as sackcloth in mourning — bulk, communal storage. Latin shrank this to sacculus, the "little sack" for coins and herbs, the personal purse. Medieval French sachel specialised it as the travelling scholar's and peddler's small bag. From the 18th–19th century the satchel became the schoolchild's book-bag and the postman's pouch. In the 20th century it added the military "satchel charge," the messenger bag, and finally the fashion satchel. Every stage preserves the core idea — a small, portable container worn close to the body — while the register shifts from coarse utility to scholarly intimacy to retro chic.
