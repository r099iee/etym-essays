REPROBATING — the biography of the failed test. The word is the latest English layer of a Latin compound whose two halves come from two separate and ancient Indo-European roots, and the whole story turns on the idea of testing. The first element descends from Proto-Indo-European *pro-, "before, in front, forth", itself an extension of *per-, "to pass through", the root of Greek pro, Latin pro, Sanskrit pra, and Germanic "fore" — a family that gave English fore, forward, first, prologue, prophet, and proceed. The second element descends from *bheu-, "to be, to become, to grow", the root of English be and being, of Greek physis "nature" (whence physics), of Latin futurus "about to be" (whence future), and of Sanskrit bhū "to be". In prehistoric Italic these two roots were fused into *pro-bhwo-, "being before, standing in front", and because what stands before others is foremost and excellent, the compound came to mean "good". Latin inherited it as probus, "good, upright, honest" — a word that already contained the idea of worth. From probus Latin formed the crucial verb probare, "to test, to approve, to deem good": to probare a thing is to put it to the proof and to hold it good if it passes. Around this verb grew one of the most fruitful families of judgment in the language. Probatio, "a testing, a proof", passed through Old French prove into English proof, and directly into probation and probate; probabilis, "provable", gave probable and probability; probitas, "uprightness", gave probity; proba, "a test", gave probe. Most important, the same verb produced the two opposite verdicts of a single test: approbare, "to approve" (ad-, "toward"), and the negative compounds that carry the family in the other direction.
  • Approbate / approbation: to approve, to pass the test.
  • Improbus, "not good, wicked" (in-, "not"): the failed test as a moral state, the ancestor of the reprobate as a depraved person.
  • Reprobare (re-, "back, against"): to test against, to prove a thing's failure, to reject.
Latin reprobare entered Christian Latin as the verb of divine rejection, and the theology sealed the word's fate. In the sixteenth century, the theology of John Calvin made reprobation a fixed doctrine: as God's decree of election passes over the wicked, so his reprobation rejects them eternally, and the participle and noun reprobate came to name both the act of divine rejection and the person rejected. The English word therefore carried a double heritage — the Latin jurist's tested rejection and the Calvinist's eternal decree — and that is why reprobating even in a secular context feels judicial, solemn, and final. In England the word arrived through two routes. Old French reprover gave the milder reprove, "to scold or blame", recorded from the fourteenth century, while reprobate itself entered Middle English from Latin as an adjective and noun meaning "rejected, cast off, depraved", and the verb to reprobate appeared in the sixteenth century with the sense of formal condemnation. The kinship with prove is worth pausing over: prove, approve, disprove, reprove, and reprobate are all children of probare, and the negative prefix re- is what turns "to prove" into "to prove against". English thus owns the whole courtroom of testing in one small family. The literary record shows the word at work in the highest registers. Shakespeare uses reprobate as a noun for a wicked man and as an adjective of moral ruin; the Authorized Version of the Bible uses reprobate for what is rejected and disallowed — "reprobate silver shall men call them, because the Lord hath rejected them" (Jeremiah 6:30) — and the vocabulary of the pulpit carried the word into every later century. In the nineteenth century John Ruskin, writing Modern Painters against the critics of Turner, chose the participle for his own pamphlet: it was "intended to be a short pamphlet, reprobating the manner and style of those critiques". The choice dignifies his quarrel: he does not merely attack the critics, he tests their manner and style against the standard of truth and pronounces them wanting, in the measured language of a verdict.
  • Semantic drift: from "test against" (probare with re-) to "reject after testing" to "condemn formally" to "declare depraved".
  • Register: always formal; judicial, theological, and literary rather than colloquial; the participle reprobating is the severe, verdict-like form.
  • Modern state: the verb is rare; the noun and adjective reprobate survive as words for a depraved person, often jocular; the participle keeps the word's oldest gravity in elevated prose.
The word's evolution is a study in how a technology of judgment — testing, proving, weighing evidence — became a moral verdict and then a theological decree, and how that long history survives in a single English participle.
