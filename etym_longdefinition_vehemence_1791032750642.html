vehemence is a noun denoting the quality of being vehement: forceful intensity, passionate ardour, vigorous energy in speech, feeling, action, or movement. To speak with rising vehemence, as Tuppy does in the Wodehouse source, is to speak with mounting heat, emphasis, volume, and emotional pressure — indignation swelling sentence by sentence. The core entailment is impetuous force that sweeps restraint aside: vehement protest, denial, applause, condemnation, desire, wind, or attack.

The word straddles emotion and manner. Subjectively it names inner passion — fervour, zeal, fury, earnestness; objectively it names outward manner — loudness, rapidity, sharp gestures, insistent repetition, uncompromising wording. A vehement opponent is both deeply convinced and loudly insistent. This double aspect lets the word cover righteous and unrighteous heat alike: vehement love and vehement hatred share force but differ in valence. Context decides approval: vehemence of grief invites sympathy, vehemence of denunciation may invite admiration or alarm.

Wodehouse's comic use shows an important secondary colouring. Tuppy's rising vehemence over a synthetic shark is pompously disproportionate — upper-class bluster defending honour over trifles. Here vehemence shades into bluster, bombast, and comedic over-earnestness. The narrator invites us to hear both genuine indignation and performative self-justification. That ironic potential is common: describing someone's vehemence often hedges between reporting passion and gently mocking excess.

  • Primary sense — passionate intensity of expression: vehemence of speech, protest, denial, argument, testimony. E.g. she denied the rumour with vehemence; he argued with increasing vehemence. Bridge: Latin vehemens meant rushing, impetuous, eager — mind carried away.
  • Emotional sense: vehemence of feeling — love, hatred, anger, jealousy, enthusiasm, religious zeal. The force is inward before it is uttered.
  • Physical / dynamic sense: vehemence of wind, fire, torrent, assault, collision. Older and literary: the vehemence of the storm, of the charge. Shares the idea of violent impetus.
  • Tertiary / ironic sense: comedic or blustering over-earnestness — Tuppy's vehemence, bureaucratic vehemence, etiquette vehemence. Force perceived as disproportionate to cause.
  • Domain usages: law and politics (vehemence of cross-examination, debate, campaign); journalism (with characteristic vehemence); religion (prophetic vehemence); sport commentary (vehemence of the tackle, celebration); literary criticism (Augustan vehemence of satire, e.g. Swift, Pope).
  • Collocations and register: formal, Latinate, slightly literary. Verbs: speak, protest, denounce, deny, argue, condemn, applaud with vehemence; adjectives: rising, growing, considerable, surprising, characteristic, passionate, righteous. Often with vehemence or vehemence of. More elevated than heat, passion, force; less violent than fury, violence.
  • Grammar and relations: uncountable abstract noun; adjective vehement, adverb vehemently. Opposite pole: mildness, calm, moderation, apathy. Intensity without direction — vehemence needs an object or cause to be intelligible.

To grasp the word fully, hear its embodied metaphors: voice rising in pitch and speed, face flushing, gestures sharpening, words hammered out. Vehemence is passion made audible and visible. Whether admired as conviction or dismissed as loss of control depends on whether the hearer shares the speaker's cause — which is why the same speech can be praised as vehement defence and blamed as vehement outburst.
