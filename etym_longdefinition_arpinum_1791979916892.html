Arpinum (Latin Arpinum, Greek Ἄρπινον) was a town of the Volsci in the valley of the Liris, in what the Romans called Latium adiectum. It is the modern Arpino, a hill town in the province of Frosinone in Lazio, roughly sixty miles, or about one hundred kilometres, east-southeast of Rome. In the present reading it is named as Cicero's birthplace: he was born on 3 January 106 BCE "near Arpinum," on his family's estate in the town's territory.

Location and physical setting

  • The town stood on a steep hill above the river Liris (modern Liri), controlling the fertile valley and the routes between Latium, the Volscian hills and the upper Volturno.
  • Its acropolis retains extensive walls of polygonal masonry, characteristic of Volscian and other Italic strongholds of the fifth to fourth centuries BCE.
  • The territory produced grain, olives and wine, and the surrounding countryside was dotted with the villas of Roman senators, including Cicero's family estate.
  • The site's defensibility explains its long importance out of proportion to its size: Arpinum was never a great city, but it commanded a strategic corridor.

Roman history

  • Arpinum was a Volscian town and appears in the Roman narrative of the Volscian wars; Rome captured it in 305 BCE, during the final subjugation of the Volsci.
  • In 303 BCE it received civitas sine suffragio, Roman citizenship without the vote — a status that bound the town to Rome while leaving its own institutions intact.
  • In 188 BCE Arpinum, with Formiae and Fundi, was granted full Roman citizenship; thereafter it was a municipium whose leading families could enter the Roman Senate.
  • The town supplied soldiers and magistrates to Rome, and by the late Republic its local elite were thoroughly Roman in culture while retaining pride in their Volscian ancestry.
  • Its most famous sons were Gaius Marius, born at Cereatae near Arpinum around 157 BCE, and Marcus Tullius Cicero, born in 106 BCE — two novi homines from the same small town, a coincidence Romans themselves found remarkable.

Marius and Cicero

  • Marius, a man of modest Italian stock from the Arpinate countryside, rose to seven consulships and saved Rome from the Cimbri and Teutones; his career proved that talent from an Italian municipium could reach the highest office.
  • Cicero used Marius as a precedent when defending his own candidacy for the consulship, and his enemies used Arpinum against him: Arpinas ("the man from Arpinum") was a snarl in the mouths of aristocratic rivals such as Clodius.
  • In De Legibus Cicero sets the dialogue at his Arpinate estate, among the woods and springs associated with Marius, and reflects on having two fatherlands — the town of his birth and Rome, the commonwealth of his citizenship.
  • He also praises the district's landscape and antiquity, using Arpinum to argue that Italian municipal towns were the seedbed of Roman virtue, not backward villages.
  • Cicero's brother Quintus and their friend Atticus are the other speakers in De Legibus, so Arpinum becomes the setting for a philosophical conversation about law, nature and Roman tradition.

The name

  • Arpinum is a pre-Latin toponym, almost certainly Volscian or at least Italic; no securely attested Volscian form survives, and its derivation is unknown.
  • Ancient antiquarians liked to supply eponymous founders for obscure towns, but any such founder story for Arpinum is genealogical invention, not evidence.
  • The adjective and ethnonym Arpinas ("of Arpinum") is common in Cicero's own writings and in later invective, and it survives in the modern Italian name of the inhabitants, arpinesi.
  • Modern Italian Arpino continues the ancient name by regular phonetic development; English Arpinum is a humanist re-borrowing of the Latin form, used for the ancient town.

Modern Arpino and why the place matters here

  • Arpino remains a small hill town, notable for its polygonal walls, medieval churches, a famous pointed arch, and its views over the Liri valley.
  • It is also the birthplace of the painter Giuseppe Cesari, called the Cavalier d'Arpino (1568–1640), and it trades culturally on its Roman memory.
  • For readers of De Oratore, Arpinum explains Cicero's angle of vision: he was a provincial novus homo writing about an art dominated by the Roman nobility, and his speakers include the aristocrat Crassus and the equally Italian Antonius.
  • The birthplace also sharpens the passage's theme: the power of eloquence could lift a man from a Volscian hill town to the consulship, which is precisely why its social importance was worth debating.
