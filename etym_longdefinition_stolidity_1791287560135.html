Stolidity is the noun for the quality of being stolid: calm, impassive, almost immovable composure that shows little or no emotion, especially under discomfort, provocation, or surprise, often with a suggestion of dullness, heaviness, or bovine solemnity.

Wodehouses phrase — the quiet stolidity of a stuffed moose — captures the visual core perfectly: a face that is a mask, giving nothing away, eyes steady, mouth closed, body still, as if stuffed and mounted. In moments of discomfort the stolid person does not fidget, complain, blush, or grin; he preserves decorum through inertia. The adjective quiet adds approval: this is not aggressive sullenness but dignified, almost butler-like self-containment.

Historically stolidity meant outright dullness or stupidity, and that overtone has never fully vanished. To call someone stolid can still imply slow-wittedness, lack of imagination, or emotional thickness, not just admirable calm. Context decides whether it is praise for steadiness or criticism for obtuseness: a surgeons stolidity under pressure is a virtue, a students stolidity in discussion may be a defect.

Polysemy and contemporary senses include:
  • Impassive calm under pressure: the primary modern sense — unflappable composure, keeping a straight face, not betraying feeling. The semantic bridge is from Latin stolidus as insensible to pain toward English stoicism as insensible to panic.
  • Dullness, obtuseness, lack of sensitivity: the older, still live sense — slow to understand, emotionally thick, unresponsive to humour or suffering. The bridge is direct continuity from Latin dull-brutish.
  • Bovine, mask-like demeanour: the Wodehouse visual sense — resembling a cow, moose, or statue in immobility and unreadability. The bridge is metaphorical extension from temperament to physiognomy.
  • Philosophical / cultural steadfastness: in post-colonial and class discourse, stolidity can denote working-class or peasant endurance — patient, uncomplaining, rooted. The bridge is Victorian romanticisation of the labourer and soldier as nobly impassive.
  • Clinical flatness (extended): in popular psychology, stolidity shades into flat affect or alexithymia — inability to express emotion. The bridge is medicalisation of a character trait into a symptom.
Collocates reveal attitude: quiet, bovine, mask-like, monumental, dull, dependable stolidity. It pairs naturally with nouns of large, slow animals and statues, and with verbs like preserve, maintain, wear, betray no break in. Unlike serenity, which is luminous and aware, stolidity is opaque and heavy; unlike apathy, which has given up, stolidity endures.

In use, stolidity governs a distinctive set of verbs and modifiers that reveal its embodied character: one maintains, preserves, affects, or hides behind stolidity, and one breaks through or ruffles it only with effort. It is described as bovine, monumental, impassive, granite-like, or mask-like, never as sparkling or agile. Unlike patience, which waits hopefully, stolidity simply stands; unlike dignity, which commands respect, stolidity withholds information. That withholding is its power in comedy and in confrontation alike: the stolid face gives the observer nothing to work with, forcing jokes, insults, or questions to fall flat against it like stones against a wall.
