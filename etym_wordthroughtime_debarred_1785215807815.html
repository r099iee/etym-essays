- 15th century legal exclusion - enters English (from French desbarrer) as a formal verb of shutting out: to debar is to bar the way, to prevent someone from a right, office, or place by authority.
- 16th-18th century institutional use - settles into law, church, and civic procedure: debarred from holding office, from the sacraments, from practising a trade; the word is administrative and sober.
- 18th-19th century figurative force - extends to mental and moral exclusions, as in Mill "debarred from thinking so" by his own principles; the barrier is now internal, a conviction acting as a bar across the doorway of a thought.
- Contemporary use - still formal and active in legal and professional registers (debarred from practising), but in everyday speech replaced by barred, excluded, or prevented.
