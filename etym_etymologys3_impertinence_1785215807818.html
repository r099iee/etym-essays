Evolution of "impertinence." The noun IMPERTINENCE is a compound formation built on two Proto-Indo-European roots: *per-, meaning "forward, through, or forth," and *ten-, meaning "to stretch." Their combination yields the idea of stretching through to something — reaching, touching, or belonging to a given matter. Latin realized this compound as pertinere ("to stretch out to, to extend to, to belong to, to concern"), a verb that produced the present participle pertinens ("pertaining, relevant, belonging") and, with the negative prefix in-, impertinens ("not relevant, not belonging, not proper"). The entire history of the English word — from logical irrelevance to social rudeness — is already latent in this Latin contrast between pertaining and not pertaining, belonging and not belonging.

1. The Latin foundation. In Latin, pertinere was a spatial verb: to stretch out to a point, to reach, and by extension to belong to or concern a thing. What pertains to a matter is what reaches it, what is connected to it, what has a rightful share in it. The negative impertinens and the abstract noun impertinentia therefore meant, in the language of Roman rhetoric and logic, "not pertaining to the matter" — irrelevance, unsuitableness, the introduction of what does not belong to the subject under discussion. This was a scholarly, analytical vocabulary, used by grammarians and philosophers to mark the boundaries of a topic.

2. From Latin to French and English (the logical sense). From impertinens, Late Latin formed the abstract noun impertinentia, "irrelevance or inappropriateness," and this word passed into Old French as impertinence. When English borrowed it in the fourteenth century, its meaning was strictly logical: impertinence denoted something that was beside the point, an absurd unsuitability, or a statement that failed to pertain to the matter at hand. An impertinent remark, in this early sense, was simply one that was irrelevant to the discussion — not necessarily a rude one. The word belonged to the vocabulary of debate, scholarship, and legal pleading, the registers in which French-derived abstraction was entering English after the Norman Conquest.

3. The shift from logic to society (16th–17th century). During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word's center of gravity shifted from logic to social conduct. The bridge is the concept of one's own sphere. A person who refuses to confine himself to what pertains to his business — meddling in affairs, presuming to a place, or intruding on what is not his — was seen as overstepping propriety; and so the logical idea of "not pertaining to the matter" slid into the social idea of "not pertaining to one's own business," and from there into "presumptuous, improper, rude." By the seventeenth century impertinence had come to mean rudeness, impudence, and presumptuous intrusion into matters where one has no right to interfere. The word kept the buried image of the boundary: the impertinent person is one who crosses the line that defines what belongs to him. This is the sense in which the sentence preserved in this card deplores the attempt "to lift the veil" on a private mystery as something that "savours almost of impertinence": the very act of prying into a guarded personal life is treated as an improper, intrusive overstep, an entry into what does not belong to the public.

4. The modern word. Modern impertinence preserves this social meaning almost exclusively. It collocates with verbs of transgression and reproof — show impertinence, tolerate impertinence, stand no impertinence, a piece of impertinence, verging on impertinence — and with the adjective impertinent and adverb impertinently it forms a small family of censure. In casual modern speech it is somewhat dated, replaced by cheek, nerve, or rudeness, but in formal and literary English it remains the precise word for the delicate offence of prying into what is not one's own. The logical sense survives only faintly: a merely "impertinent" detail is still, in older or technical usage, one that does not belong to the matter. The word thus holds, compressed in its long career, the whole journey from the scholar's scruple about relevance to the moralist's censure of intrusion.

  • PIE *per- "forward, through" + *ten- "to stretch" → Latin pertinere "to stretch out to, to belong to, to concern" → pertinens "pertaining, relevant."
  • Late Latin impertinens "not relevant, not proper" → impertinentia "irrelevance" → Old French impertinence → Middle English impertinence (14th c.).
  • Early English sense: logical irrelevance — what does not pertain to the matter at hand.
  • 16th–17th century shift: from "not pertaining to the subject" to "not pertaining to one's own business" — meddling, presumption, intrusion — and hence to rudeness and insolence.
  • Modern: formal/literary censure of improper intrusion (the present passage); cheek, nerve, rudeness in casual speech; the logical sense survives only marginally in technical usage.
