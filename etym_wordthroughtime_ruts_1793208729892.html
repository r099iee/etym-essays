- Medieval — the road-word arrives: Old French route, a beaten way, gives Middle English the notion of the travelled track, and worn grooves take the name rut.
- 16th–17th c. — the farm lane vocabulary: ruts mean muddy cart-grooves, the curse of carriers and the measure of winter; Shakespeare age knows them well.
- 18th c. — antiquaries admire them: sunken lanes and Roman wheel-ruts become picturesque evidence, history legible in the ground.
- 19th c. — the metaphor escapes: in a rut and the beaten rut describe minds and marriages, not just roads; the groove becomes psychology.
- 20th c. — engineers and therapists divide it: highway labs measure rutting in millimetres while self-help urges readers to leave their ruts.
- Today — twin lives: literal ruts steer tractors, bikes, and off-roaders, while figurative ruts headline every essay on habit, burnout, and midlife change.
