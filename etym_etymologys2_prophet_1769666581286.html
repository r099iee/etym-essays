1. Proto-Indo-European *bheh₂- / *bhā- "to speak, say, declare" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE; cf. Sanskrit bhāṣati "speaks", Latin fārī "to speak").
2. Ancient Greek phēmí "I say, I speak" (attested from the 8th c. BCE, Homer); agent noun phētēs "speaker, one who speaks."
3. Ancient Greek prophētēs "one who speaks before/for another; spokesman and interpreter of an oracle" (attested from c. 6th–5th c. BCE — Pindar, Aeschylus). DEBATE/NUANCE: the second element is the agent noun phētēs of phēmí; older analyses sometimes invoked the aorist stem phan- "to speak," but the present-stem analysis (phē- + -tēs) is the standard one.
4. Septuagint Greek (3rd–2nd c. BCE): prophētēs used to translate Hebrew nāḇī "spokesman of God, prophet," shifting the sense to "one who foretells and proclaims the divine will."
5. Latin prophēta "prophet, interpreter of God's word" (2nd c. BCE onward; Vulgate, 4th–5th c. CE).
6. Old French prophete "prophet" (c. 11th c.).
7. Middle English prophete "prophet" (c. 13th c., e.g. Ancrene Wisse).
8. English PROPHET (modern) — final node. NOTE: the modern pronunciation and spelling are unchanged since Middle English; only the vowel of the first syllable has shortened in unstressed-connected use, and the ph digraph still reflects the Greek phi.
