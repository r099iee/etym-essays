The biography of incrassation is a study in how a neutral idea of growth darkened into a clinical and moral image of stagnation. The word is built on Latin crassus, 'thick, fat, dense, solid', and crassus is traditionally derived from the same Proto-Indo-European root that produced Latin creare 'to create' and crescere 'to grow': *ker-, 'to grow, nourish, increase'. If that derivation is right, the semantic logic runs as follows: to grow is to add substance, to accumulate mass; a thing that has grown very much is thick; and a thickened thing is dense, heavy, and, in the wrong place, obstructive. The positive energy of growth — crops rising, children growing, fortune increasing — curdled into the heavy physicality of crassus, 'thick, coarse, gross'. It is worth noting explicitly that the etymology of crassus is debated: some scholars prefer to link it to *ker- 'to strengthen, make firm' rather than 'to grow', and a fully certain PIE etymon has not been established. The 'growth' account is the traditional one given in the handbooks and is followed here with that caveat. The same Latin family spread in two directions. Through crescere and creare it gave English the vocabulary of positive growth: create, creature, increase, decrease, crescent (the growing moon), crescendo (music growing louder), and concrete (grown together, hence compacted). Through crassus itself it gave a cluster of words for density and grossness. Latin crassus yielded English 'crass', whose meaning moved from 'thick, coarse in texture' to 'grossly ignorant, unrefined' — the intellectual thickness that makes a person insensible. In Vulgar Latin the feminine crassia meant 'fat, grease', which passed through Old French craisse into English as 'grease' and 'greasy'. And in technical Latin, the adjective built a verb: incrassare, 'to make thick', formed with the prefix in-, here intensive or directive ('into thickness'), and from the verb a noun of action: incrassatio, 'a making thick, a thickening'. The word's great age was the era of humoral medicine. From Hippocrates and Galen onward, Western medicine explained health as the balance of four fluids — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile — whose perfect state was a mobile, well-mixed liquidity. Disease was understood as a disturbance of that balance, and one of the most feared disturbances was incrassation: the thickening, inspissation, or coagulation of the humours. A thickened humour could no longer circulate; it blocked the channels of the body, stagnated, and decayed, and the whole organism slowed with it. Medical writers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries — the age when the word entered English — used incrassation as a diagnostic term for fevers, melancholia, 'obstructions', and the general loss of vitality that physicians attributed to sluggish, viscous blood. The prompt sentence belongs to exactly this world: a long period of neglect leads to the gradual incrassation of the vital humours and a consequent loss of vitality. The word also had a parallel, more neutral career in alchemy and early chemistry. Alchemists 'incrassated' solutions — boiled them down, thickened them, inspissated essences — as a routine laboratory step, with no sense of disease. Natural historians and botanists used incrassation for the thickening of plant parts: leaves that grow fleshy to store water, seed coats that harden, membranes that thicken in maturity. In these technical contexts the word is purely descriptive, the nominal counterpart of the adjective 'incrassate', 'thickened in form'. By the twentieth century, humoral medicine had collapsed, alchemy had become chemistry, and incrassation had lost its clinical work. It survives now as an archaic, learned, slightly eerie word. Writers reach for it when they want the atmosphere of old medical books, or when they want a mock-scientific grandeur for any slow congealing — of fluids, of systems, of organisations. Its near-twin 'inspissation' (from Latin inspissare, 'to make thick') is the only serious rival, and both words now live chiefly in dictionaries, historical essays, and literary pastiche. What makes incrassation distinctive is that it never lost the moral shadow it acquired in humoral medicine: it is not just thickening, it is thickening as decay — the slow turning of what was fluid and alive into something dense, unyielding, and suffocating. It is the noun of a body losing its flow.
