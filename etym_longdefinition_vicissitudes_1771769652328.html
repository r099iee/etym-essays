In the literary context of Gibbon and the Roman Empire, vicissitudes names the relentless, often harsh alternations of fortune that shape imperial history. The word does not merely mean "change"; it carries a tone of weathered inevitability — the ups and downs, rises and collapses, and grinding turns of power that no polity can permanently escape. When a writer speaks of "the vicissitudes of empire," the phrase invites the reader to see people and events as subject to a larger pattern of instability: glory followed by decline, security followed by crisis. The plural form itself matters: it suggests not a single reversal but a continuing series of turns — the cumulative drama of fortune over long stretches of time.

Primary sense. Vicissitude (countable, usually plural) denotes a change of circumstances or fortune, especially one that is unwelcome or unpleasant. The singular can mean either a single change or the abstract condition of change itself; the plural vicissitudes almost always points to the successive hardships, reverses, and turns that a life, career, or polity must endure.

Extended and metaphorical senses.

  • Historical and political (the Gibbonian core): "the vicissitudes of empire" — the alternating rise and fall of states, the wheel of fortune as applied to nations; a standard phrase of historiography.
  • Personal and biographical: "the vicissitudes of life / of a career" — the tests and turns of an individual's fortunes, from illness to prosperity to setback.
  • Economic and commercial: "the vicissitudes of the market / of the global economy" — booms and busts, regulatory turns, and unpredictable swings; common in financial journalism.
  • Natural and environmental: "the vicissitudes of climate / of the seasons" — alternations of weather and natural variability; preserves the older, neutral sense of mere succession.
  • Abstract-philosophical: vicissitude as mutability itself — changeability as a principle of the universe; close to the Latin source meaning "alternation, succession."
  • Scholarly-editorial: "the vicissitudes of the text" — the corruptions, revisions, and accidents a manuscript suffers through history; a technical extension in textual criticism.

Semantic bridge. Latin vicissitūdō derived from vicis, "a turn, change, succession." The idea is of things taking turns — one thing giving way to another in rotation. English first used the word for neutral alternation (day and night, seasons, ups and downs) and only later, especially in the plural and in moral-historical prose, loaded it with the notion of misfortune: the "turns" became the turns of fortune's wheel, and the word darkened toward "hardship." The modern default use is plural, formal, and sober or elegiac.

Collocations and register. The vicissitudes of life / fortune / history / war / politics / empire; weather the vicissitudes; survive the vicissitudes of; subject to the vicissitudes of. Register is elevated and literary — common in historiography, biography, and formal prose, rare in casual speech, where "ups and downs" or "twists and turns" take over. The plural form dominates modern usage by a wide margin.
