Veddahs is an older English plural form of Veddah or Vedda, denoting the Vedda people, the Indigenous hunter-gatherer community of Sri Lanka, formerly called Ceylon. In modern standard spelling the form is usually Veddas or Vedda, but 19th- and early 20th-century books, including histories of civilisation and architecture, regularly write Veddahs of Ceylon to mean members of this community collectively. The term is a proper ethnonym and is capitalised.

Anthropologically the Veddas are an Australoid-Veddoid population of the Sri Lankan dry zone, traditionally living by bow-hunting, honey-collecting, fishing and shifting cultivation, sheltering in caves, rock overhangs and simple thatch rather than permanent houses. Their language, Vedda language, is a creolised or heavily influenced form of Sinhala with a distinct hunter-gatherer lexicon and ritual dialects, now highly endangered. Culturally they are known for the bow and arrow, the cult of dead ancestors called Nae Yakka, and intimate forest knowledge.

  • Ethnic collective sense: the Veddahs as a people, e.g. the Veddahs of Ceylon had no dwellings, meaning the community as an ethnographic example of shelterless life.
  • Individual plural sense: several Vedda persons, e.g. two Veddahs guided the party, though modern style would say two Vedda men or two Veddas.
  • Historical-anthropological type sense: in Victorian evolutionism Veddahs served as a type-specimen of primitive hunter life, grouped with Bushmen, Tasmanians and Australian Aboriginal peoples in stadial histories.
  • Extended pejorative sense in Sinhala source: väddā can mean a wild, uncouth or rustic person generally, but this extension is not normally carried into English Veddahs.
  • Modern preferred forms: Vedda as singular, Veddas or Vedda people as plural, Wanniyala-Aetto as self-designation meaning forest-dwellers, used in rights discourse.

Polysemy is therefore largely a matter of number and register rather than distinct lexical senses. The bridge from occupational hunter in Sanskrit vyādha to ethnic Vedda in Sinhala is narrowing by association: because the autochthones lived pre-eminently by hunting, the generic hunter word became their name. The bridge from singular Vedda to plural Veddahs is regular English -s pluralisation applied to a borrowed ethnonym, with the -h- an English orthographic accretion to mark a breathy or long final vowel heard by early travellers.

Domain usages reflect this history. In anthropology Veddahs appears in discussions of kinship, totemism and relict languages. In Sri Lankan law and activism Vedda denotes a recognised Indigenous group with claims to forest access in Maduru Oya and Dambana. In travel and older popular writing Veddahs evokes romantic primitivism, forest freedom and contentment with earth and sky, as in the source passage contrasting them with cave-dwellers and shelter-builders. Contemporary scholars avoid primitivist framing, stressing centuries of interaction, trade, Sinhalisation and marginalisation rather than isolation.

In the source context, like the Veddahs of Ceylon, had no dwellings at all, the word functions as an ethnographic illustration of a shelterless stage in a history of dwelling. The connotation is not contempt but stadial illustration, using a living people as proxy for a prehistoric condition. Modern readers should recognise both the factual kernel, seasonal mobility and reliance on natural shelter, and the outdated evolutionist frame that presents contemporary Indigenous life as a past stage of European history.
