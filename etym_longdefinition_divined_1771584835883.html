DIVINED is the past tense and past participle of divine, the verb: “to discover or understand by intuition, insight, or supernatural means; to foresee or foretell; to guess correctly by penetrating perception.” Immediately after Bao-yu’s baffling question—“Have you got a jade?”—Hawkes tells us that “the rest of the company were puzzled, but Dai-yu at once divined that he was asking her if she too had a jade like the one he was born with.” The verb divined is chosen with precision. To divine something is to arrive at knowledge not by weighing evidence but by a sudden, penetrating apprehension, as though a veil had been lifted; the word descends from Latin dīvīnāre, “to foretell by supernatural agency, to act as a soothsayer”, and so it lends Dai-yu’s act a quasi-oracular character. Everyone else in the room needs Bao-yu’s words explained; Dai-yu alone reads him, because their bond—in the novel’s mythic frame, the pre-incarnate kinship of two spiritual beings—lets her hear the question beneath the question. The tone is delicate and faintly magical: a flash of insight that feels less like reasoning than like recognition, as if the two share a private frequency. In a scene otherwise full of noise and puzzlement, the word quietly crowns Dai-yu as the only true reader of Bao-yu’s heart. Polysemy and the full range of senses:
  • To discover by intuition or acute perception—the modern, psychological sense and the sense at work in the context sentence: Dai-yu at once divines what Bao-yu is really asking.
  • To foretell or foresee, especially by supernatural agency—the original sense: a prophet, soothsayer, or augur divines the future; “to divine the outcome of battle”.
  • To conjecture or guess correctly—a weaker, colloquial sense (“she divined his true intentions despite his polite words”), often now lightly ironic or playful.
  • To search for by divination—the dowser’s art: “to divine for water”, the “divining-rod”; the water-witch in the example sentence divines the hidden well with her hazel rod.
  • Technical and religious jargon—divination as a discipline: augury, haruspicy, soothsaying, the reading of omens; the diviner as a professional seer; related nouns divination and divining.
The synonym family divides sharply. Guessed is the ordinary, humble member: a guess may be a wild shot in the dark, carries no prestige, and requires no insight—Dai-yu’s instant certainty is far above guessing. Deduced and inferred are the logical members: they name conclusions drawn step by step from premises and evidence, and they would be comically wrong here, since Dai-yu has almost no evidence to reason from. Surmised comes closest in dictionary glosses—“conjectured on slight evidence”—but surmise keeps a cool, tentative, intellectual cast, and even the strongest surmise remains a considered opinion, whereas divined reports a certainty that arrives already formed. Intuited matches the immediacy, but intuition is personal and psychological, an inner feeling, whereas divining adds a penetrating, almost supernatural character. Sensed is faint and passive, detected requires observation of clues, anticipated is reasoned expectation, suspected is negative and doubtful—none captures the sudden, oracular certainty of divined. Register and idiom: divined is literary, delicate, and faintly magical; even in its most secular modern use it carries a ghost of the sacred—to divine is to know as a god knows, suddenly and without evidence. Related formations include divination (the art), diviner (the practitioner), divining-rod (the dowser’s instrument), and the Romance verbs that eroded the same root into the plain word for “guess” (Italian indovinare, Spanish adivinar).
