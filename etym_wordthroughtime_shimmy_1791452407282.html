- Early 19th century - A dialectal American pronunciation of chemise, naming a woman's slip or undergarment; the word is domestic, unremarkable and confined to speech.
- 1918 to 1920 - The jazz dance explodes into fashion, named for the shaking of the chemise; the word becomes a byword for modern, slightly scandalous youth culture.
- 1920s - The verb appears: to shimmy is to dance, and by extension to shake the body; clergymen and columnists denounce it, and the notoriety only sharpens its glamour.
- 1930s to 1950s - Engineers borrow the word for the violent wobble of car and aircraft wheels; "shimmy damper" becomes a technical term, and the word acquires a second, sober life.
- Late 20th century - The dance fades into nostalgia, but the verb generalises: to shimmy into clothes, to shimmy along a ledge, to shimmy out of a coat.
- Contemporary - A lively, slightly comic word for wriggling or vibrating, still faintly tinged with jazz-age naughtiness and still used by mechanics for wheel oscillation.
