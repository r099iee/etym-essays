justly (adverb) — In a manner that is morally or legally right, fair, and deserved; in accordance with justice, equity, or the merits of the case. When a prose writer says that something "has been justly said", the adverb means "rightly, with good reason, and as is only fair to acknowledge": it certifies that the statement is not merely true but merited, that it carries the authority of what is just.

Primary sense — fairness and rightness of action. As the adverbial form of just, the core meaning is "in a just manner": a judge acts justly when he gives every party what the law and the evidence require; a punishment is justly inflicted when it is proportionate and deserved; praise is justly bestowed when it matches achievement. In this first sense the word binds the moral to the legal: what is done justly obeys conscience and rule at once.

Sense 2 — deservedly, with good reason (epistemic justification). In literary and critical idiom, justly more often modifies a judgment than a deed: justly famous, justly criticised, justly described, justly celebrated. Here the sense has shifted from the fairness of an action to the correctness of a belief: the semantic bridge is that a true and well-grounded opinion is itself "fair" to the object it appraises, giving it its due. This is exactly the sense in the reading context, where Pater records that a certain aim of criticism "has been justly said" — rightly and to the point, with the quiet implication that the writer agrees.

Sense 3 — precisely, exactly (older English). Earlier usage also employed justly where modern English says just or exactly ("justly so", "justly proportioned"). The bridge is ancient: Latin iūstus "upright, exact" carried a sense of strict conformity to rule, from which "conforming precisely" developed; the modern adverb just ("just now", "just enough") preserves the exactness, while justly has narrowed to fairness and rightness.

  • In legal discourse — justly means "in conformity with the law and its merits": "the contract was justly enforced".
  • In moral philosophy — of a justly ordered society; of distributive and corrective fairness between persons.
  • In rhetoric and criticism — justly remarked, justly dreaded: the measured intensifier of praise or blame, signalling even-handedness.
  • Period style — the old "precisely" sense survives chiefly in set phrases and older texts, so encountering it is a marker of historical prose.

Register and scale.Justly is formal and measured, gravitating to written English; in speech it tends to yield to rightly (rightly said) or to fairly. It keeps full force wherever fairness, deservedness, and due proportion matter — courts, criticism, history, biography, and everyday complaint ("justly annoyed") all draw on it, and it remains the one crisp adverb that covers the whole moral–legal territory of just. Its lexical family — justice, justifiable, justification, unjustly, adjust (via Old French ajuster) — shows how deeply the root has spread through law, ethics, and everyday talk.
