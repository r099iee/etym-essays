- Middle English: borrowed from Old French "unreson" — lack of reason, an unreasonable act, and also a wrong or injustice. - 14th–16th century: in allegorical and legal usage, set against "reason" as the principle of right; Unreason meant the contrary of justice and rational order. - 18th–19th century: became more abstract, meaning irrationality, folly, or the loss of sane judgment. - Contemporary sense: a literary and philosophical term (often capitalized, as in Wells's The Time Machine) for the decay or suppression of rational thought, carrying a dramatic, cosmic register.
