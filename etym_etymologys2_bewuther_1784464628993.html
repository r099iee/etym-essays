BEWUTHER is a compound of the Germanic prefix be- and the northern dialectal verb wuther; each element has its own history, so a complete linear chain is given for each root word in isolation. Chain for the prefix be-: PIE *h₁ópi 'at, on, near' (also reconstructed *h₁epi; the family is first attested in the fourth millennium BCE) → Proto-Germanic *bi 'by, near, around' (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE) → Old English be-/bi-, prefix 'about, around, all around,' forming transitive and intensive verbs (8th–11th c.) → Middle English be- (12th–15th c.) → Modern English be-, intensifying prefix, as in BEWUTHER. Note on the debate: the PIE starting point is sometimes given instead as *ambhi 'around, on both sides' (whence Greek amphí, Latin amb-) with a reduced form *bhi; the two proposals differ in detail, but both agree that the Germanic prefix is a weakening of a PIE word meaning 'around, by, about.' Chain for wuther (second element of the compound): Wuther has no confidently reconstructible PIE etymology. It is best treated as an imitative (onomatopoeic) dialect word that mimics the dull roaring of wind, part of a family of northern English weather-words; a Scandinavian origin has been suggested (Old Norse weather-vocabulary) but is not established. The attested sequence is short: Middle English and northern dialect wind-vocabulary of the wuther/wother type (medieval period onward) → Modern northern English dialect wuther 'to blow with a dull roaring sound' (attested in Yorkshire and related speech; also the wuthering of Brontë's 1847 title) → with the intensive prefix be-, Modern literary English BEWUTHER (J. R. R. Tolkien, The Hobbit, 1937). Note on the unknown: because the root is sound-symbolic rather than a regular Indo-European lexeme, no PIE root can be stated; scholars describe the family as imitative and leave the deeper origin open.
