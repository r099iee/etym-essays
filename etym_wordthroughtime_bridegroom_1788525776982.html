- [Pre-8th c. OE] - brȳdguma denotes "the bride's man", combining brȳd (bride) and guma (man, warrior, hero) - [connotation: high tribal and heroic dignity in Anglo-Saxon matrimonial alliances]
- [12th–14th c. ME] - As brīd-gome, the word persists throughout Middle English romances and Wycliffite biblical translations - [connotation: central liturgical figure in Christian matrimonial rites and scriptural parables]
- [15th–16th c. Early Modern] - As gome becomes obsolete, speakers reshape the second element to grome / groom via folk etymology - [connotation: fully standardized spelling bridegroom in the Book of Common Prayer and Elizabethan poetry]
- [17th–19th c.] - Retains elevated romantic, devotional, and poetic stature in writers from Milton and Shakespeare to the Victorian poets - [connotation: noble, impassioned, solemn symbol of lifelong fidelity and sacred union]
- [20th–21st c.] - While frequently clipped to groom in colloquial speech, bridegroom endures in formal invitations, religious liturgy, legal documents, and literature - [connotation: formal, timeless, dignified ceremonial designation]
