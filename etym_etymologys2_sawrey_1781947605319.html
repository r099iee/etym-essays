SAWREY is an Old Norse compound placename, so it requires two derivation chains — one for the element Saur- 'sour, muddy' and one for the element -ey 'island'. The two chains meet in the compound Saur-ey and then pass together into English. CHAIN 1 — element SAUR- ('sour, muddy ground'): 1. PIE *sūro- 'sour, salty, bitter, damp' (reconstructed; Pokorny *sūro-; the oldest sense 'salty' is preserved in Baltic: Lithuanian sūras 'salty', Latvian sūrs 'bitter'). 2. Proto-Germanic *sūraz 'sour, acidic, damp' (the ordinary Germanic adjective; attested by Old English sūr, Old High German sūr, Old Norse saurr). 3. Old Norse saurr 'mud, dirt, filth, sour boggy ground' (Viking Age, 8th–11th c. CE) — the damp, fermenting earth sense specialized from 'sour' to 'filth'. Note: the modern English adjective sour is a sister reflex of the same root, not an ancestor of this placename element. 4. Old Norse compound Saur-ey 'muddy island' (9th–10th c. CE, the period of Norse settlement of Cumbria); recorded in English documents as Saurey (13th c.), Saureye (14th c.). 5. English placename Sawrey (Near Sawrey and Far Sawrey, Cumbria), the modern reflex of Saur-ey. Debated nuance: the shift -aur- > -aw- and the smoothing of the compound show ordinary Middle English spelling change; some authorities also connect the same saurr element to the Yorkshire placename Sowerby ('Sauri's or muddy farmstead'), which supports the reading 'sour/muddy' for Saur-. CHAIN 2 — element -EY ('island'): 1. PIE *h₂ekʷeh₂ 'water' (reconstructed; the same root gives Latin aqua 'water'). 2. Proto-Germanic *ahwō 'water, river' (attested by Gothic ahwa 'river, water', Old High German aha 'water, river'). 3. Proto-Germanic *awjō 'watery land, flood-meadow, island' (a derivative of *ahwō; cf. Old English īeg, ēg 'island', Old Norse ey 'island'). 4. Old Norse ey 'island' (Viking Age) — the second element of the compound. 5. Old Norse compound Saur-ey 'muddy island' (9th–10th c. CE) → English records Saurey (13th c.), Saureye (14th c.) → modern Sawrey. Debated nuance: the derivation of PGmc *awjō from the water-word *ahwō is widely accepted, but the morphological detail is debated (some scholars derive it via a suffix *-jō from a vṛddhi grade of the water-root); the broad picture — 'island' as 'watery/flood land' — is not in doubt. The same element appears in Alderney (Old Norse Aldern-ey) and in Old English Selsey 'seal-island'.
