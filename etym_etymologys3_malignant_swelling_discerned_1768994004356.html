Malignant: a bad seed.

The word malignant is a bad seed. It was assembled in Latin as malignus — from male, "badly," and -gnus, the adjectival suffix of gignere, "to beget, to give birth" — and its first meaning was literal: one "ill-born," of a bad nature, as opposed to one who merely acts badly. The root *gen-, "to give birth," is one of the great Indo-European stems: Sanskrit janas, "race, offspring," and jāti, "birth" (whence Hindi jāti, "caste"), Greek génesis, "origin," Latin genus, "kind," and English generate, genius, genus, and genocide all descend from it. By the time Latin fused malus with gignere, the compound had already softened from "born evil" to "ill-disposed, spiteful" — the sense in which Renaissance English borrowed it through Middle French malin, maligne. English took malignant in the sixteenth century, first of persons and dispositions, then, decisively, of diseases: a "malignant fever" resisted treatment and spread, and by the late seventeenth century the word was fixed in medicine as the opposite of benign, naming growths that invade surrounding tissue and recur. That medical sense is the one Burroughs exploits: the "malignant column" of the zoorth is not just an evil wind but a growth of the air itself — tumorous, spreading, born bad, intent on consuming the travelers. The etymology survives in the word's full form: malignancy is not a choice but a nature, something begotten, bred in the bone of the world.
Swelling: from the root *swel- to the boil of the sky.

The story of swelling begins in the PIE root *swel-, "to swell, to rise," a root that must be kept carefully apart from its twin homophone *sāwel-, "to burn, to shine" — the ancestor of Latin sōl, "sun," Sanskrit svàr, "sun, light," and the whole family of solar words. The two roots are identical in shape and have been confused by lexicographers for centuries; only the "swell" root is the ancestor of our word. In Proto-Germanic it produced *swellan, "to become larger," and in Old English swellan, used from the earliest records of rivers rising, wounds inflaming, and sails filling with wind. From the very first, swelling was an internal, intransitive growth of substance from within under pressure — the opposite of being built up from outside. In Middle English the participle became an adjective for anything in the act of inflating, and the noun names the act or the result: the rising of a river, the lump of a tumor, the lift of a wave. In the nineteenth century the word took a social turn: a "swell" is a person puffed up with fashion and importance, and "swell" as an adjective means "excellent, fashionable" — the oldest logic showing through, that to swell is to become more than one was. In contemporary English the word serves tides, floods, tumors, storms, and swelling crowds, chords, and numbers. In the passage it catches the zoorth mid-inflation: "twisting and swelling toward them" — a boil of the sky filling itself as it rolls across the plain, the participle giving atmospheric violence a body, a growth from within under pressure, toward rupture.
Discerned: sifting signal from dust.

Discerned is a word whose whole meaning is the gesture of sifting. Its ancestor is the PIE root *krei-, "to sieve, separate, discriminate," allied to the great *sker-, "to cut," family. In Greek the root produced krī́nein, "to separate, judge," and with it the decisive noun krísis, "judgment, decision," the ancestor of English crisis, critic, and criterion — so that our words for the turning points of history and for the judge of art both descend from the image of the sieve. In Latin the root built cernere, "to sift, distinguish, decide," whose compounds are a map of Latin culture: discernere, "to sift apart" (dis-, "apart"), gave English discern; secernere, "to set aside," gave secret; excernere, "to sift out," gave excrement; and the past participle crētus, "decided, settled," gave certus, "certain," and English certain, certify, certificate. English took discern in the fourteenth century from Old French discerner, itself from Latin discernere. Its original force is active separation by close examination, and it settled into the language of perception in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries: to distinguish one thing from another, to separate the true from the false by judgment. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it became the refined word for perceptive effort — to discern a faint thing through distance, clutter, or darkness — which is precisely the labor of the earthmen in the passage, scanning a lifeless land for the first sign of animation. Discerned is the word of active sifting, and its whole family history is the history of the sieve: you discern when you separate signal from noise, motion from stillness, one grain of life from the dead dust.
