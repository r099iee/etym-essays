- [Late 13th–14th c. Middle English] — Borrowed from Old French cheance (ultimately from Vulgar Latin *cadentia, "the falling of dice"), referring to gambling, the cast of dice, and personal fortune or destiny — [perception: an unpredictable, dangerous gamble or the mysterious decrees of Lady Fortune's wheel]
- [15th–16th c. Late Medieval & Tudor Eras] — Broadened to describe unexpected occurrences, accidents, or mishaps in daily life, while also developing the positive sense of an "opportunity" or "opening" for advancement — [perception: an unforeseen turn of events that can either ruin an enterprise or provide an opening for a bold adventurer]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment Era] — Transformed by mathematicians (Pascal, Fermat, Bernoulli) from superstitious "fortune" into the scientific "Doctrine of Chances," creating the mathematical foundation of probability theory — [perception: a calculable, quantifiable branch of natural philosophy and mathematics rather than supernatural whim]
- [19th c. Victorian & Romantic Eras] — Celebrated in Romantic poetry for improvised, natural simplicity (as in Wordsworth's "chance equipment"), while concurrently serving as the foundational engine of Darwinian natural variation in evolutionary biology — [perception: poetic freedom and serendipity in literature, juxtaposed with the foundational, blind mechanic of biological evolution]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Ingrained in quantum physics as an objective property of the universe, institutionalized in risk management, and integrated into avant-garde aleatory aesthetics — [perception: fundamental scientific reality of universal indeterminacy, alongside ubiquitous everyday idioms of opportunity and risk]
