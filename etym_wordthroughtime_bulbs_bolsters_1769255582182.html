- The kitchen-garden swelling — 'Bulb' (from Latin bulbus, the onion) has always meant the round, fleshy underground plant-storage organ — onion, tulip, daffodil, lily — a plain, earthy word of kitchen garden and field, and by ready extension any rounded swelling (a bulbous nose, the bulb of an eye or thermometer).
- The bulb gives light — In the nineteenth century 'bulb' leaps from garden to drawing room: the round glass globe that glows is called a bulb, and for the first time the word becomes a daily electric-age word while preserving its old sense of roundness swollen from within — the light bulb is the onion given a job.
- The homely bolster — Meanwhile 'bolster', a word of Old English, names the long, firm, full-width bed pillow on which a tired head rests; from this propping pillow grows the verb 'to bolster' — to prop up from beneath — which begins to carry abstract weight (bolstering confidence, morale, an argument).
- Contemporary pair — Modern English keeps both alive in harness: 'bulb' is at once the spring-planting onion and the household light, while 'bolster' survives as an old-fashioned bed noun and a vigorous abstract verb; joined as 'Bulbs and bolsters!' they form a Narnian household oath of earthly roundness and homely support.
