moored is the past tense and past participle of the verb moor, whose primary nautical sense is "to make fast (a boat, ship, or vessel) by attaching it with cables, chains, or ropes to a fixed object" — the shore, a dock, a buoy, a bollard, an anchor, or, in the Narnia passage, a tree. In The Voyage of the Dawn Treader, "they ran the boat up into a little creek and moored her to a tree" closes a small nautical sequence: mooring is the seaman's way of ending a voyage, and the moment carries a calm, homely relief after the perils of open sea. Lewis's "her" for the boat and "to a tree" for the fastening show how matter-of-fact the act is in this world; the moored boat signals rest, provision, and the beginning of a shore adventure.

Secondary and extended senses:

  • Figurative / metaphorical — "to moor" means to fix, fasten, or hold steady anything that might drift: a person, a life, a belief, a nation ("a life moored in habit," "moored to the past," "firmly moored in evidence"). The rope that holds a boat in a creek became the image of anything held fast against drift.
  • The noun mooring (c. 1490) — "a place where a ship is moored; the act of mooring; the ropes, chains, and anchors used," with the collocations "mooring rope," "mooring buoy," "mooring post," and the idiom "to slip one's moorings" (to break free, to leave the safe point).
  • Unmooring — the natural opposite: "to cast off, to loosen the mooring." To be moored is to be secured, not imprisoned; departure remains as easy as casting off, which is part of the word's reassuring sense of provisional rest.
  • Grammatical use as a predicate adjective — "moored in/at/to": the past participle freely modifies ships ("a moored vessel"), and by metaphor people and ideas ("moored to tradition," "moored in memory").

Synonym boundaries. anchored is the stricter, more technical act of letting down an anchor to the seabed, the vessel held by weight and grip rather than by a rope to a fixed shore point. docked and berthed mean brought alongside a pier or into an assigned harbour station, implying port infrastructure. tied up is the informal, landside equivalent. tethered suggests a rope restraining an animal (or, figuratively, a person), and hitched a quick, temporary fastening. moored is the general nautical verb for securing a vessel by cables, chains, or ropes to a fixed object — a tree, a bollard, a buoy, the shore — and it is the only word of the set unambiguously at home on the water while still permitting attachment to a natural object like a tree. Moreover, to moor is to make fast in a way that anticipates unmooring.

Important homonym note: the verb moor must be kept strictly separate from moor the noun, "a tract of open, waterlogged wasteland" (from Old English mōr), and from Moor, "a member of the Muslim peoples of North Africa" (from Latin Maurus). All three words are historically unrelated; only the nautical verb yields moored.
