PIE *kleng- "to bend, turn, be flexible" (reconstructed root; attested securely only in Germanic — Indo-Iranian, Greek, and Latin show no clear cognate) -> Proto-Germanic *hlanka- "side, flank, loin; that which bends" (the flexible, bendable part of the body; from the root's "bend, turn" sense) -> (West Germanic) Frankish *hlanka "side, flank, hip" (the loan core, c. 5th–8th c.; borrowed by the Romance dialects of Gaul) -> Old French flanc "hip, side, flank" (12th c.; the regular conversion of Germanic hl- to Romance fl-, cf. French flèche "arrow" beside English lance) -> (English) late Old English / Middle English flanc "fleshy part of the side" (borrowed from Old French, c. 11th–14th c.) -> English flank (noun) "side of an animal or army; fleshy part of the side between ribs and hip" (14th c.) -> English flank (verb) "to be situated at the side of; to guard or cover the flank" (16th c., military register) -> English FLANKED (past participle, 16th c.) Note on the root: the reconstruction of PIE *kleng- rests on the Germanic evidence alone, and some handbooks keep the root as a Germanic-level reconstruction without committing to a PIE vowel shape. The parallel English words from the same root — lank (OE hlanc "loose, slender"), link (from *hlenka- "the bending part", via Old Norse hlekkr "a link of a chain"), and flinch (via Old French flenchir "to bend, give way") — confirm the "bend, turn" core. The route of flank itself is a Germanic-to-Romance loan and then a Romance-to-English re-borrowing: the Franks gave Old French the word, and English took it back from French. The military sense of the verb is first recorded in English in the 16th century.
