• Outdated: Similar because it also means no longer current or in general use; differs because it is more neutral and flexible — data, information, and fashions are “outdated,” whereas “antiquated” adds a hint of being visibly old and left behind.
  • Old-fashioned: Similar because both mean belonging to an earlier era; differs because “old-fashioned” is broader and can be affectionate or positive (charming old-fashioned courtesy), while “antiquated” is usually mildly critical.
  • Obsolete: Similar because both describe things no longer in current use; differs because “obsolete” is stronger — it implies something has fallen completely out of use or is unsupported to the point of uselessness, whereas something “antiquated” may still function.
  • Obsolescent: Similar because it describes a thing becoming out of date; differs because it points to an ongoing process (“becoming obsolete”) rather than an accomplished state.
  • Archaic: Similar because both mean old and no longer current; differs because “archaic” is a more technical term, used especially of words and grammatical forms surviving only in specialised, literary, or liturgical use (“thee,” “thou”).
  • Dated: Similar because both signal that something belongs to a past period; differs because “dated” is lighter and usually about style or fashion — something can be “dated” but still perfectly functional.
  • Outmoded: Similar because both mean no longer in fashion or accepted; differs because “outmoded” emphasises falling out of fashion or favour rather than falling out of use or efficiency.
  • Passé: Similar because both mean out of fashion; differs because it is a French loanword used almost exclusively of styles and trends, with a lighter, more dismissive register.
  • Anachronistic: Similar because both suggest something out of place in time; differs because “anachronistic” describes an object or idea appearing in the wrong historical period (often deliberately, in fiction) rather than simply being old.
  • Antique (adj.): Similar because both relate to old age; differs because “antique” values old age positively — an antique object is prized for its age and craftsmanship, while “antiquated” is pejorative.
  • Ancient: Similar because both mean very old; differs because “ancient” is neutral or grand, pointing to great (often thousands of years) age, with no implication of being rejected by the present.
  • Old: Similar because both denote age; differs because “old” is entirely neutral and needs context, while “antiquated” already contains the judgment “too old for its context.”
