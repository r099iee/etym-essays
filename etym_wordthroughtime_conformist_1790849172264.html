- c. 1610–1689: Coined in post-Reformation England within religious politics to designate an adherent of the established Church of England who "conformed" to the Book of Common Prayer and episcopal governance (contrasted with *Nonconformists* or *Dissenters*).
- c. 1689–1850: Maintained strong ecclesiastical and legal significance under the Toleration Act and Test Acts, defining who had access to universities and public office based on religious conformity.
- c. 1850–1920: Broadened from religious orthodoxy to general social and moral adherence to Victorian bourgeois conventions, middle-class etiquette, and institutional norms.
- c. 1920–1970: Shifted dramatically into sociology, psychology, and countercultural critique (e.g., David Riesman's *The Lonely Crowd*, Frankfurt School critiques of authoritarianism, and Beat literature) as a sharply pejorative term for uncritical herd mentality, docility, and suppression of individuality.
- c. 1970–Present: Universally used across political theory, literary criticism, sociology, and everyday discourse to critique unquestioning compliance, linguistic standardization, and institutional groupthink.
