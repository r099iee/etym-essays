Flatfish is the common name for any member of the order Pleuronectiformes, a large group of ray-finned marine fishes whose adults have a dramatically compressed, sideways-flattened body and, uniquely among vertebrates, both eyes on one side of the head.

Zoologically, flatfish comprise roughly 800 species in families including Pleuronectidae (righteye flounders), Bothidae (lefteye flounders), Soleidae (soles), Cynoglossidae (tonguefishes), Scophthalmidae (turbots), and Paralichthyidae. Familiar food species are plaice, dab, flounder, sole, Dover sole, halibut, turbot, and brill. All share a remarkable life history: larvae hatch symmetrical and pelagic like normal fish, then during metamorphosis one eye migrates across the skull, the body flattens laterally, pigmentation becomes countershaded — dark eyed-side up, pale blind-side down — and the fish settles to a benthic, bottom-dwelling life, often buried in sand or mud with only eyes protruding.

Culinarily and economically, flatfish are among the most valued whitefish. Dover sole, turbot, and halibut command premium prices for their delicate, firm, low-fat flesh; plaice and dab are staple frying fish in Britain; sole meunière, fish and chips, and sashimi hirame all depend on flatfish. Fisheries management therefore uses flatfish as a category for quotas, minimum sizes, and stock assessments, and the word appears constantly in markets, menus, and marine policy.

Beyond biology, flatfish carries secondary and figurative senses. Anglers and naturalists may call any flat-bodied fish a flatfish loosely, though scientists restrict it to Pleuronectiformes, excluding skates, rays, and other dorsoventrally flattened groups. In Australian slang, a shark is sometimes euphemistically called a flatfish or the joke “probably a flatfish” minimizes a danger, as likely in the Wodehouse line where a shark scare is deflated. Metaphorically, flatfish can evoke drab camouflage, bottom-dwelling passivity, or a pancake-flat shape.

  • Taxonomic sense: member of Pleuronectiformes with migrating eye and asymmetrical adult; e.g., Atlantic halibut is the largest flatfish.
  • Commercial-culinary sense: whitefish food category; e.g., sole and plaice sold as flatfish at the market.
  • Loose popular sense: any flattened bottom fish; sometimes misapplied to rays or skates by non-specialists.
  • Humorous-euphemistic sense: minimizing label for an unseen marine menace; e.g., “probably a flatfish” to calm bathers after a shark scare.
  • Adjectival extension: flatfish-like camouflage, flatfish stage of larval development, flatfish fishery.

The polysemy bridge is shape plus habitat: flat + fish transparently describes a fish that is flat. Scientific discovery then loaded that simple compound with extraordinary developmental biology — eye migration, asymmetry, metamorphosis — making flatfish a textbook example of evolution, adaptation, and ontogeny. From market stall to Darwinian debate, the word moves effortlessly because everyone can picture a flat fish lying on the sand, yet biologists can unpack a marvel of transformation inside it.

Usage is countable: one flatfish, several flatfishes or flatfish. Register is neutral, both popular and scientific, unlike Latinate pleuronectid. Collocations include flatfish stock, flatfish fillet, flatfish nursery grounds, left-eyed flatfish, and the dismissive probably just a flatfish.
