Stud is a "stand" word. Its whole biography — post, boss, fastener, breeding-horse — is the story of things that stand upright, project outward, or constitute a standing stock, and the different modern senses branch from those three images at different periods.

Stage one: the PIE root of standing. The deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *steh₂-, "to stand, to set upright" — the root of English stand, stool, stall, state, and Latin stāre "to stand", Sanskrit tiṣṭhati "stands", and Greek hístēmi "I set". From this root, with the reduced grade and various formants, Germanic built a family of words for upright timbers and posts.

Stage two: Proto-Germanic and Old English. A Proto-Germanic noun of the type *stud- / *stuþ-, "post, prop, pillar", underlies the West Germanic branch; it is related to the North Germanic support-word seen in Old Norse stoð "prop, support" (modern Icelandic stoð). In Old English the word is studu, attested with the meaning "post, pillar, prop" — the timber that stands and bears weight in a building. From the start the word is about an upright member of a structure.

Stage three: Middle English — from post to knob. Middle English stode / stud keeps the structural sense but also develops the image of any short projecting piece: a nail-head, a boss, a raised ornament on leather, metalwork, or wood. This is the semantic pivot. A stud is now something that stands proud of a surface — a small upright projection — and from that image the clothing fastener is a natural step.

  • OE — studu "post, pillar" (structural).
  • ME — stode / stud "post; projecting knob; ornament".
  • 18th–19th c. — shirt / collar studs as formal-wear fasteners; jewelled heads as gifts.
  • Modern — wall-studs, ear-studs, snow-studs; the verb to stud.

Stage four: the clothing fastener. The removable shirt or collar stud — a head that stands proud of the fabric with a shank passing through an eyelet and a back-piece that locks — is attested in formal dresswear from the 18th century onward and became standard gentleman's kit in the 19th. The visible head could be plain metal, engraved, or set with gemstones; diamond-faced studs turned practical fasteners into jewellery and gifts. The plural studs names a set or pair, exactly as in Tolkien's "a pair of magic diamond studs".

Stage five: the breeding-horse stud — same family, separate branch. The "stud" that means a stallion kept for breeding, or the establishment that keeps breeding stock, has its own history: Old English stōd meant "a herd of horses", literally a "standing stock" — animals kept standing, i.e. kept at hand, in the same "stand" family (compare steed, related to the same complex). Dictionaries differ on whether this is one word or a homonym pair; the safest statement is that both "post / knob" stud and "breeding" stud belong to the same Indo-European "stand" complex but developed as separate lexical branches within Germanic. The slang stud for an attractive, virile man is a 20th-century metaphorical extension of the breeding-stallion sense.

Sound and spelling. The phonetic path is short: studu → stode / stud → stud, with regular Middle English loss of the unstressed final vowel. The spelling stud is stable from the 16th century. The word's interest is semantic — an upright post that gradually became a protruding knob, a fastener, and a firework-painted memory in a wizard's story.

The Tolkien context. "Magic diamond studs that fastened themselves and never came undone till ordered" takes ordinary dress hardware and enchants it: they fasten themselves, obey a command to undo, and carry diamonds — three magical upgrades of a mundane fastener. Bilbo's memory of Old Took's gift is both social (a fine present) and wondrous (true wizard-work), and the card keeps the plural of that remembered pair — the exact inflection of the recognition speech that re-identifies Gandalf.
