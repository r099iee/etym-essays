- [7th–11th c. / Anglo-Saxon Era] - coined as the Old English legal compound *bōcland* (*bōc* "written charter" + *land*), defining privileged, alienable land granted by royal book - [perception: revered as the ultimate form of secure, elite property ownership, exempt from customary tribal dues]
- [1086 / Domesday Book era] - recorded across dozens of counties as *Bocheland*, identifying specific agricultural manors held by Saxon thegns and newly installed Norman lords - [perception: codified in royal administrative records as valuable agrarian estates and fiscal units]
- [13th–16th c. / Middle English toponymic consolidation] - phonetic contraction from *Bocland* / *Bokeland* to *Buckland*, transforming from an active legal status into a permanent local toponym and hereditary family surname - [perception: recognized as rural hamlets, villages, and prominent ecclesiastical holdings across England]
- [18th–19th c. / Industrial & Victorian Era] - the rural hamlet of Buckland in Hampshire was swallowed by the massive urban and industrial expansion of Portsmouth's Royal Navy dockyards, housing thousands of dock workers, naval artisans, and scholars like Arberry - [perception: viewed as a dense, gritty, vibrant working-class urban enclave supporting Britain's global maritime empire]
- [20th–21st c. / Contemporary Era] - recognized as an inner-city residential ward and parish in Portsmouth, while preserving in historical scholarship its prestige as the archetype of ancient English charter land - [perception: understood locally as an urban neighborhood and globally by historians as a living monument to Anglo-Saxon legal culture]
