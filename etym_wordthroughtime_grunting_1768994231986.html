- 16th-c. literal animal sense - "to grunt" (a word that sounds like its own meaning) names the short, low, guttural throat-noise of pigs; purely animal, purely bodily.
- 16th–17th-c. extension to human effort - the word is applied to people straining and labouring — porters, rowers, wrestlers — a noise of brute effort rather than of meaning, consciously placed below speech.
- 18th–19th-c. menial register - the noun gains use for the involuntary sounds of work and pain (to grunt with exertion, grunting under a weight); the word settles a deliberate step down the vocal ladder from "groan" toward "snort."
- 19th-c. zoological naming - "grunt" is fixed as the name of a family of fish that croak when hauled from the water; the word's animal core is made literal.
- 20th-c. U.S. military slang - a "grunt" is a low-ranking infantryman (Vietnam-era), and "grunt work" comes to mean menial labour; the association of low bodily effort hardens into social rank.
- Contemporary dismissive usage - "grunting" as a description of inarticulate or foreign speech carries an animalistic, dehumanizing contempt: sound without mind, the body's noise standing where language should be.
