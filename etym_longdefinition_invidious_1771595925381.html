invidious: likely to arouse resentment or ill-will in others, and therefore felt to be unfairly or offensively preferential; that which singles out a person or thing for special treatment in a way that rankles; hence, in extension, offensively or unjustly discriminating. In the preface to Modern Painters from which this sentence is drawn, Ruskin is anticipating a hostile reception: he concedes that certain judgments in his book may strike readers as invidious — that is, as woundingly partial, as singling out one artist (Turner) and disparaging others in a way that feels unjust — while insisting that any such appearance flows not from the spitefulness or bias of his design ("the tenour of the work") but simply from the work's incompleteness, which leaves his reasons partially exposed. The tone is wary, forensic, and self-aware: the critic knows exactly how his words will be felt, and he is pre-emptively absolving himself of bad faith.

Primary senses

  • Resentment-arousing / invidious comparisons: describing an act, remark, or distinction that provokes envy or ill-will in the onlooker, because it appears to give one party an unearned or offensive advantage. "An invidious comparison" ranks people or things in a way that stirs the rankled sense of being singled out and found wanting.
  • Unfairly discriminating / offensively preferential: a judgment, law, or practice that discriminates between persons in a way felt to be unjust — singling out an individual or group for adverse or privileged treatment. This is the sense that fed the modern legal phrase "invidious distinction" (a classification that imposes burdens or benefits unequally and without reasonable basis).
  • Hateful, detestable, odious (older and now rare sense): in earlier English, invidious could mean "hateful, offensive, exciting hatred" without any necessary idea of envy — the sense survives in the archaic phrase "an invidious office," a duty that is thankless and likely to make enemies.
  • The obsolete envious sense: very early and now obsolete uses could mean "envious, full of envy" (the meaning of the Latin source invidiōsus in one of its faces), so that the word has at different dates pointed at both the feeler and the provoker of envy.

The semantic bridge

The word descends from Latin invidia, "envy, grudge, ill-will," which itself comes from invidiōsus, formed on invidiōsus ... that is, from invīdēre, "to look at with ill-will, to envy, to cast an evil eye," literally "to look upon." The root is vidēre, "to see," with the prefix in-, "upon": the envious person is one who looks upon another's good with a poisoned gaze. The adjective carries a built-in double face — "envious" toward the subject who feels, "hateful, exciting hatred" toward the object that provokes — and English inherited both, though the provoking sense came to dominate. The connection between envy and the eye is ancient and worldwide: the evil eye, the envious look, the sense that being looked at enviously brings harm. Invidious things are those that draw that look.

Near-synonyms and boundaries

  • Odious: similar in provoking strong negative feeling; differs because odious describes what is hated for its intrinsic repulsiveness or detestableness, whereas invidious stirs resentment because it appears unjustly preferential — the sting is in the inequality, not in the object's badness.
  • Objectionable: similar in being displeasing; differs because objectionable is far milder — merely open to objection — while invidious carries the wound of unfair discrimination and the envious look it draws.
  • Discriminatory: similar in treating persons unequally; differs because discriminatory denotes systematically prejudicial treatment in modern legal usage, while invidious names the emotional consequence — the rankled resentment — that such treatment stirs, and is the adjective in the legal collocation "invidious distinction."
  • Envious: similar in sharing the same Latin root family; differs because envious describes the person who feels envy, whereas invidious describes the act or remark that causes resentment: one is the emotion, the other the provocation.
  • Unfair / partial: similar in naming an unjust judgment; differs because unfair and partial describe the quality of the judgment itself, without the emotional consequence of the rankled onlooker that invidious carries inside it.
  • Galling / rankling: similar in describing something that irritates and offends; differs because galling and rankling describe the feeling caused, whereas invidious names the provoking quality of the act itself — its power to single out and sting.
  • Offensive: similar in giving displeasure or resentment; differs because offensive is much broader — anything that affronts — while invidious is specific to the resentment aroused by apparently unjust preference or discrimination.

The word here is not "unpleasant" in a merely trivial sense; it names the special discomfort of a judgment that seems to discriminate unfairly between persons. In modern usage invidious is almost always relational: it attaches to comparisons, distinctions, classifications, and offices that create winners and losers in the eyes of the onlooker, and its core remains the envious look it provokes.
