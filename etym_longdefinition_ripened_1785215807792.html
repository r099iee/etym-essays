ripened is the past tense and past participle of the verb ripen, meaning matured, grew full, or developed progressively into a state of completeness or perfection, as fruit reaches the moment of harvest-readiness. The word's root sense is agricultural: to ripen is for a crop to come to its natural fullness — grain standing yellow, fruit softening and sweetening — so that it is ready to be reaped or gathered. From the fields the image was extended to anything that develops to its full state: a plan that ripens into action, a talent that ripens with practice, a person who ripens into maturity, cheese and wine that ripen in the cellar, and conditions that ripen for a change. The past participle ripened can therefore describe either the literal maturation of produce or the figurative coming-to-fullness of a relationship, an idea, a skill, or a set of circumstances.

In the literary context of this card the word is the hinge of a dense organic metaphor: 'The acquaintance rapidly ripened into intimacy and the intimacy into friendship.' Mill and Harriet Taylor's attachment is described as though it were a crop or a fruit — something that grew of its own nature, in its own season, to a natural fullness, without contrivance or forcing. The adverb 'rapidly' is what makes the metaphor pointed: the growth was organic but uncannily quick, an August heat ripening in weeks what should have taken a season. The word thus smuggles an air of inevitability and wholesomeness into what was, in fact, a swift and scandal-adjacent Victorian attachment; to say that the acquaintance ripened is to claim that its development was natural, healthy, and the fulfilment of an inner potential rather than a deliberate choice.

The senses and uses may be catalogued:

  • Literal agricultural sense (the core sense): of fruit, grain, vegetables — came to maturity and readiness for harvest ('the warm sun ripened the wheat').
  • Of manufactured and fermented goods: cheese, wine, cigars, wood — matured by ageing to a developed flavour or quality ('the wine had ripened for a decade in oak').
  • Of people and character: matured emotionally or intellectually, gained wisdom and fullness with age or experience ('a ripened judgment', 'ripened by suffering').
  • Of relationships and feelings: grew gradually into something fuller — the sense at work in 'ripened into intimacy and friendship'; implies naturalness, season, and inevitability.
  • Of plans, conditions, and historical moments: came to the point of ripeness for action or change ('the crisis ripened', 'conditions ripened for reform').
  • Figurative and literary register: anything brought to completion or perfection by a process of gradual development; the participle lends a wholesome, organic, almost biblical air to whatever it describes.

ripened must be distinguished from its companions. Matured is the closest, but it is blander: it means grew to full development, whether of a person, a plan, or a debt, and it lacks the specifically agricultural image of readiness for harvest. Developed is neutral and technical, describing an unfolding process without any flavour of ripeness or sweetness. Flourished and blossomed stress thriving, vigorous growth and often brightness or beauty, but they do not imply the completed end-state that ripening does — a thing can flourish and still be green. Mellowed names the softening that comes with ripeness (of fruit, or of character with age), but it emphasizes gentleness and sweetness rather than fullness and readiness, and it would be wrong for a passion that ripened into intimacy. Evolved describes gradual structural change, far too clinical here. What is distinctive about ripened is the whole organic image: season, naturalness, sweetness, and the completion of an inner potential.
