- [16th-century verb embody] - Formed from body: to give a body to a spirit, to incorporate, to unite into one body (a corps, a corporate body) — originally physical and religious (a soul embodied).

- [17th–18th century: the abstraction] - Embody extends from flesh to form: principles, ideas, and qualities are "embodied" in actions, institutions, and persons — made concrete and visible to the senses.

- [19th-century noun embodiment (c. 1820s)] - The noun is coined and becomes the standard way to say that a person or thing makes an abstraction tangible — "the embodiment of kindness," of a nation, of a theory — a fixture of praise and portraiture.

- [Modern register] - Fully ordinary, unshowy, positive-to-neutral: embodiment describes an idea given sensory reality, with the religious charge ceded to incarnation and the confinement to a human person left to personification.
