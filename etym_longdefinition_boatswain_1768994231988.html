boatswain — pronounced "bosun," a ship's warrant or petty officer in charge of the hull, rigging, sails, anchors, and deck crew — is in this passage the identification of the child's assassin: "a Maltese boatswain shoots him in the back with a small pistol." In this literary context the word's nuance is cold professionalism. The title does the work of a dossier entry: it classifies the killer by trade, presenting the attack as an occupational fact rather than a personal crime. A boatswain is a practical, hardened seaman — the man who keeps the vessel's gear in working order, who disciplines the deck crew, who is more at home with rigging and belaying pins than with moral questions — and to name the assassin thus is to make the violence feel matter-of-fact, technical, almost administrative. The word also carries a sinister contrast between scale and deed: the boatswain's domain is the huge machinery of a ship, yet he shoots "in the back" with "a small pistol," a weapon as petty as the betrayal — the small gun is the moral size of the act, while the office lends it weight. There is a cold irony that the one who keeps the ship's gear sound uses his competence for an ambush.

Primary sense: the boatswain is the senior warrant/petty officer of a ship's deck, responsible for the hull, rigging, sails, anchors, cables, and the supervision and discipline of the deck crew; his badge of office is the boatswain's call, a small whistle used to pipe orders, and historically he carried the rope's-end ("starter") with which he enforced discipline.

  • Nautical-rank sense: in the age of sail the boatswain was a warrant officer between the master's mates and the common seamen — the technical foreman of the deck, keeping the ship's gear fit and the hands at work.
  • Pronounced "bosun": the spelling boatswain is an etymological spelling of a word the sailors themselves said as one clipped syllable — hence the variant spellings bosun, bos'n, bo's'n, bo'sun; the modern U.S. Navy writes boatswain's mate and still says "bosun."
  • Modern technical sense: in the U.S. Navy a "boatswain's mate" is a petty officer rating responsible for deck seamanship, small boats, and ground tackle; the boatswain's call remains the ceremonial emblem of the rating.
  • Literary/stock-character sense: in sea fiction the boatswain is a stock figure — profane, weather-beaten, competent, the ship's disciplinarian — and the word thus carries a flavour of the age of sail even in modern prose; here that flavour makes the assassin a nameless professional of the deck rather than a personal enemy.
  • Folk-etymology note: because the office "commands" the boat, the word is sometimes misread as "boat's-owner"; in fact the second element swain is the old word for "boy, servant, attendant" — the boatswain is the boat's servant, the man who tends the boat and its gear, which is exactly the root meaning.

Boundary words clarify the rank. Bosun is merely the spelling of the standard nautical pronunciation and names the same office. Petty officer is the general naval rank to which a boatswain belongs but carries no maritime-gear specialization. Mate (a ship's officer, esp. the first mate, the captain's second) commands navigation and crew duties rather than the deck tackle, and has a more respectable, quasi-officer ring. Coxswain — the same "-swain" suffix — steers a boat or rowing crew and commands a ship's small boat, a much humbler and more specialized post than the boatswain's charge of the whole deck. Deckhand is the unskilled labourer beneath the boatswain, the hand he sets to work. Captain and master stand at the top of command, responsible for the voyage and the ship, far above the gear-and-men province of the boatswain. In collocation the word is strongly nautical: "the boatswain's call," "boatswain's chair," "boatswain's mate," "to pipe the boatswain," "bosun's pipe" — and the modern pronunciation is always compressed, "bosun." The word in this sentence, then, does exactly what the story needs: it names a killer by his trade, and the trade itself — practical, deck-hardened, unglamorous — makes the murder feel like routine ship's business.
