Unlike most of the vocabulary of The Hobbit, booby did not come into English from an ancient Indo-European root but from the baby-talk of the Romance-speaking world, where reduplicated syllables of the shape ba-ba and bo-bo have for millennia imitated the stammering and babbling of infants and of the tongue-tied. The same imitative habit lies behind Latin balbus, “stammering, stuttering,” with its verb balbutiō, “I stammer” (whence the rare English balbutient), and it survives across the modern Romance languages in words for the foolish and the naive. In the Iberian peninsula this childish sound-base hardened into a regular adjective-noun: Spanish bobo and Portuguese bobo, meaning “foolish, simple, naive” and, as a noun, “a fool or a jester.” The semantic link is transparent: the person who cannot speak plainly is presumed not to think clearly, and the babbler becomes the simpleton. It was the sea that carried the word into English. In the sixteenth century Spanish and Portuguese mariners applied pájaro bobo, “the foolish bird,” to the great gannet-like seabirds of the family Sulidae, which nest fearlessly on remote coasts and islands and allowed themselves to be clubbed or caught by hand; their clumsy waddling on land and their apparent indifference to danger struck sailors as the very height of stupidity. English sailors of the same period took over booby for both the bird and for a dull-witted shipmate or landsman — “a dunce, a simpleton” — and the word is recorded in English in this double sense from around 1600. The word thus entered the language twice at once, on the same wave: a word of the forecastle for the bird that the sailors clubbed for supper, and a word of the quarterdeck for the fool who could not tell a rope from a reef. From the forecastle and the schoolroom the word then bred its modern compounds. The booby-trap is first recorded in the seventeenth century as a practical joke played upon a blunderer or dullard — the trap is set for a booby, and the booby springs it upon himself. In the twentieth century the same word was taken over by the military for a concealed explosive device, so that the jest of tripping a fool became the device that kills the unwary soldier; the semantic core — a trap for the careless — never changed. The booby prize followed in the late nineteenth century: the mock award given to the worst competitor in a race or game, the “prize” for the greatest fool, institutionalised in school sports days and village fêtes. Through all this the word has kept one foot in the seabird’s nest and one in the schoolroom. Its tone has remained consistently comic and light: booby is a mock-insult, not a clinical diagnosis; it names laughable dullness rather than madness, which is why it can be used with affection as well as contempt. In The Hobbit (1937) Tolkien gives it to the trolls — “‘You’re a booby,’ said William” — and the word’s childish, schoolyard register is exactly right for creatures whose quarrels are as petty as their wits are slow. The reader can hear, in a single insult, the nursery origin of the word and the nursery level of the trolls’ intelligence, while the hidden Gandalf, the real dupe-maker, stands outside the joke and orchestrates it. The history of booby illustrates three characteristic facts about word origins. First, not every English word has an ancient PIE pedigree: many, like booby, arise from imitative baby-talk and travel by trade and travel. Second, a word can enter a language carrying two meanings at once — the seabird and the simpleton arrived together from the same Spanish phrase. Third, words breed compounds in the direction of their register: a comic word produced a comic practical joke (the booby-trap) and a comic prize (the booby prize), and even the military’s lethal booby-trap kept the original idea of the trap set for the unwary fool. From the babbling of infants to the battlefields of the twentieth century is a long road, but booby has walked it in a straight line.
