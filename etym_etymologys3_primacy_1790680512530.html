The story of primacy begins with the Indo-European root PIE *per- (extended *preh₂-, *perh₃-) “forward, through, before, in front” — one of the language family’s great spatial organizers, yielding English fore, first, former, foremost, prime, prince, principal, prior, private and Latin’s entire pro-/prae-/pri- system. In Italic the root produced a comparative stem *prais- / *prī- (“before”) and then a superlative *prīsmos / *prīmos “foremost, firstmost,” which Latin regularized as prīmus “first” — first in time, first in rank, first in quality. From primus Latin derived a fertile family: prior (“former, superior”), princeps (“first-taker, chief, emperor”), principium (“beginning”), primitiae (“first fruits”), primas, primatis (“a chief man, principal, leading bishop”).

Late and Medieval Latin turned the adjective for a leading bishop into an abstract office: prīmātia / prīmātiō “the dignity and jurisdiction of a primas,” recorded in canon law for metropolitans who outranked ordinary archbishops — above all the claims of Rome, Canterbury, Armagh, and Constantinople to headship. Old French borrowed this as primacie / primatie (12th–13th centuries) in chronicles and conciliar records, and Middle English took it in the 14th century as primacie almost exclusively in the ecclesiastical sense: Caxton, Wycliffite tracts, and conciliar rolls dispute “the primacy of the Pope” versus national churches. The spelling with -acy (rather than French -atie) reflects the productive Middle English abstract suffix from Latin -atia, shared with supremacy, papacy, prelacy.

  • Ecclesiastical stratum (1300s–1600s): primacy as church jurisdiction — who outranks whom among bishops; Reformation polemic makes it explosive (“papal primacy” as usurpation or divine right).
  • Political generalization (1600s–1800s): from bishops to states and principles — “the primacy of Parliament,” “the primacy of reason” — via Enlightenment treatises that needed a noun for structural firstness.
  • Imperial-economic stratum (1800s–1945): naval primacy, industrial primacy, British primacy; the word becomes the standard counter in great-power rivalry and tariff debate.
  • American-hegemonic stratum (1945–2000s): “American primacy” as political-science term of art for unipolar dominance; “dollar primacy,” “air primacy” (doctrine), “English primacy” in linguistics.
  • Cognitive-psychological stratum (1900s–present): Ebbinghaus and successors’ primacy effect — first-presented items recalled best — giving the ancient root a laboratory sense.

Sound change is textbook: Latin long ī in prīmus diphthongized in the Great Vowel Shift to Modern English /aɪ/ (prime, primacy /ˈpraɪ-/), while the suffix -atia → -acy reduced to /əsi/; medieval three-syllable pri-ma-CY-e settled into RP /ˈpraɪməsi/. Spelling wobbled (primacie, primacye, primacy) until Johnson’s Dictionary (1755) and 19th-century schooling fixed primacy. No rival form survived, unlike primer / primary / prime which split into separate niches (schoolbook, adjective, chief / first quality).

Literary attestations mark each widening: Hooker and Laud on church primacy; Hume and Gibbon transferring it to Rome’s imperial primacy; Bagehot and Bryce on parliamentary primacy; Mahan on sea-power primacy; postwar American strategists (Huntington, Ikenberry) debating whether primacy is benign, transient, or already lost. The source sentence’s “monument to the primacy of practical purpose” belongs to essayistic irony in the Wolfe–Didion tradition: it grants the utilitarian doctrine its full monumental dignity — steel plants, freeways, suburbs — precisely so the counter-evidence (fins, chrome, souped-up personalization, social climbing from Chevy to Cadillac) can topple the monument from within. Mastering primacy thus means hearing both its trumpet (firstness proudly proclaimed) and its question-mark (first by whose measure, and for how long).
