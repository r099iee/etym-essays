Arpinum is a place-name of the obscure, pre-Latin stratum of central Italy, preserved by Roman literature because two of Rome's most consequential new men were born there. Its history is therefore both topographical and literary: a Volscian hill town that became a Roman municipium, and a municipal birthplace that became an argument about talent, class and eloquence.

The Volscian background

  • The Volsci were an Italic people of the southern Latian hills, speakers of an Italic language related to Latin and Umbrian, and long-standing enemies of Rome in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE.
  • Arpinum lay in the Liris valley, a corridor between Latium and the lands of the Samnites and Marsi; its hilltop site and polygonal walls show the usual Italic concern for defensible ground.
  • Rome's conquest of the Volsci was gradual. Arpinum was taken in 305 BCE, in the same campaign that reduced the neighbouring towns, and Roman control of the valley was secured by roads and colonies.
  • The town received civitas sine suffragio in 303 BCE, a status that imposed Roman obligations and granted Roman private law without political rights — a halfway house typical of Rome's piecemeal incorporation of Italy.

Becoming Roman

  • Full citizenship followed in 188 BCE, when Arpinum, Formiae and Fundi were enfranchised; the town then became a municipium with Roman citizens enrolled in a tribe and local magistrates modelled on Rome's.
  • Municipal life produced a local aristocracy of equestrian and senatorial ambition; Cicero's family belonged to this class, wealthy, well connected, but without a consular ancestor.
  • The town's territory included Cereatae, the birthplace of Gaius Marius around 157 BCE. Marius's rise to seven consulships became the template for the Italian novus homo.
  • Cicero was born in 106 BCE, half a century after Marius, and deliberately linked his own career to the earlier Arpinate: in speeches and dialogues he presents Arpinum as a source of sturdy Italian virtue rather than a provincial embarrassment.

The name and its obscurity

  • Arpinum is attested in Latin literature and inscriptions, and its Greek form Ἄρπινον appears in Greek writers on Italy; but no Volscian text preserves the town's own name for itself.
  • The suffix -inum is common in Italic toponyms, but the stem arp- has no secure etymology. Comparisons with the Apulian town of Arpi and with Italic words for a height or a rough place are speculative and cannot be demonstrated.
  • Ancient writers accounted for such names by inventing founders, but any founder story for Arpinum belongs to the same antiquarian imagination that gave Rome a Trojan ancestry.
  • The derived adjective Arpinas is securely attested and supplies the English noun Arpinate, used by historians for Cicero and his fellow townsmen; Italian preserves the same stem in arpinese.
  • The modern Italian Arpino shows the regular loss of the final -um and the normal Italian development of the ending; English re-borrowed the Latin form in the Renaissance for the ancient town.

Literary and historical afterlife

  • Cicero's De Legibus opens at his Arpinate estate, with a walk among the woods and the oak associated with Marius; the setting allows him to meditate on law, nature and the two fatherlands.
  • The phrase Arpinas was also a weapon: Cicero's enemies used it to suggest that a man from a Volscian hill town had no business leading the Roman state, and he answered by turning municipal origins into a proof of merit.
  • Plutarch and other biographers record the birthplace and the family's long residence there, and the town's name became inseparable from Cicero's biography in the Renaissance and Enlightenment lives of the statesman.
  • Modern Arpino preserves the ancient acropolis, polygonal walls and a medieval urban fabric; it is also the birthplace of the painter Giuseppe Cesari, the Cavalier d'Arpino, and lives partly on Ciceronian tourism.

Why it matters in this reading: the sentence identifies the birthplace of the author of De Oratore. That detail is not decorative. Cicero's origins outside the Roman nobility shaped his view of eloquence as a force that could confer standing on talent, and shaped the suspicion with which aristocratic speakers such as Crassus regarded professional rhetoric. Arpinum is thus the concrete ground of the dialogue's argument about who may speak, and with what authority, in the Roman state.
