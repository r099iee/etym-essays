The compound noun ninepins unites an ancient Proto-Indo-European cardinal numeral (nine) with a Latin-derived Germanic noun for a pointed peg or skittle (pin), producing a name that chronicles the evolution of bowling from medieval religious rituals to modern global sports and vivid English idioms.

PART I: EVOLUTION OF THE FIRST ROOT — NINE

  • 1. Proto-Indo-European Number System (*h₁néwn̥):

    The first element, nine, descends directly from the Proto-Indo-European cardinal numeral *h₁néwn̥. In Indo-European numerology and myth, nine was an exceptionally sacred and mystical number, representing three sets of three (the triad multiplied by itself). The root is universally conserved across the entire Indo-European family, appearing in Sanskrit náva, Ancient Greek ennéa, Latin novem (yielding November), Old Church Slavonic devętĭ, and Old Irish noí.

  • 2. Proto-Germanic and Old English Development:

    In Proto-Germanic, the initial nasal was preserved in *newun. In Old English, this developed into nigon (c. 8th century), with the typical intervocalic velar glide. By Middle English (c. 1200), the word regularized as nigon → nyen → nyne / nine, as the velar glide vocalized into the long vowel /iː/, subsequently shifting to the modern diphthong /aɪ/ in the Great Vowel Shift.

PART II: EVOLUTION OF THE SECOND ROOT — PIN (PLURAL PINS)

  • 1. Proto-Indo-European and Classical Latin 'Pinna':

    The second element, pin, originates from the Latin noun pinna (a variant of penna, from PIE *pet-no- / *pet- "to fly, feather, wing"). In Latin, pinna originally denoted a feather or wing, but by physical metaphor extended to mean a "projecting point, battlements, turret, or peak" (the source of pinnacle).

  • 2. Early Germanic Borrowing and Old English 'Pinn':

    Late Vulgar Latin and Gallo-Roman dialects used pinna to describe wooden pegs, sharp spikes, and fasteners. Late Old English borrowed the word as pinn (c. 1000) to designate a small wooden bolt, peg, or fastener. In Middle English, pinne or pin was applied to the large wooden pegs or skittles set upright in tavern bowling games.

PART III: THE COMPOUND — NINEPINS AND ITS CULTURAL HISTORY

  • 1. Medieval Origins in German Monasteries (Kegeln):

    The game of ninepins originated in medieval Germany (recorded as early as the 4th century by church chroniclers). Monks placed wooden clubs (Kegel, representing the devil or pagan sins) at the end of a courtyard and rolled stones to knock them down as an act of spiritual purification. Over time, secular gamblers standardized the game with exactly nine pins placed in a 3 × 3 diamond grid.

  • 2. Early Modern English Emergence (c. 1570):

    The compound ninepins (spelled nyne pynnes) was formally established in Elizabethan England during the 16th century. It quickly rivaled bowls and quoits in popularity across village greens and public houses. Shakespeare and contemporary playwrights frequently referenced ninepins and skittle-alleys as lively centers of working-class entertainment.

  • 3. The American Gambling Prohibition and Ten-Pin Bowling:

    When Dutch and English settlers brought ninepins to the American colonies (celebrated in Washington Irving's 1819 tale Rip Van Winkle), bowling alleys became hubs for organized gambling. In the 1840s, states like Connecticut and New York enacted strict legislation outlawing "the game of ninepins." In response, enterprising alley owners added a tenth pin, arranged the pins into an equilateral triangle, and created ten-pin bowling—bypassing the letter of the law while transforming the sport into a modern global phenomenon.

  • 4. Idiomatic Immortality:

    The dramatic visual spectacle of rolling balls toppling all nine pins gave birth to the enduring idiom "to fall like ninepins" (recorded c. 1820), forever embedding the medieval game in the English figurative landscape.
