Sumner is an English family name that has hardened into a cultural keyword. In present-day English it functions almost exclusively as a proper noun, and its dominant reference is William Graham Sumner (1840–1910), the American sociologist, economist and essayist who taught at Yale from 1872 to 1909. The word has no general common-noun sense; when it appears in ordinary prose it points either to a bearer of the surname, to a place named after one, or to the body of ideas associated with the Yale professor. Its interest for the lexicographer lies precisely in that narrowing: a medieval occupational byname for a minor court official became, through the accidents of American intellectual history, a label for a whole style of social thought.

Primary sense: the person and his program. The unmarked modern meaning of Sumner is the man. He was born in Paterson, New Jersey, graduated from Yale in 1863, studied theology and economics in Europe, served briefly as an Episcopal clergyman, and in 1872 became professor of political and social science at Yale. From that chair he became the most conspicuous American defender of laissez-faire, free trade, the gold standard and individual contract, and the most systematic American populariser of the vocabulary of struggle and selection in social life.

  • Sumner the social Darwinist — the textbook label. It names his conviction that economic competition is a form of the struggle for existence, that interference with it breeds dependency, and that “the fittest” in society are the industrious, thrifty and temperate. The label was applied to him posthumously and is now used more often as a reproach than as a description.
  • Sumner the sociologist of custom — the author of Folkways (1906), who argued that usages arise from instinct and necessity, become habitual, then coercive, and finally appear to their bearers as self-evident morality. This is the sense in which modern sociology still cites him, usually for the triad folkways–mores–ethnocentrism.
  • Sumner the essayist and controversialist — the writer of “The Forgotten Man” (1883), What Social Classes Owe to Each Other (1883) and “The Conquest of the United States by Spain” (1898), a fierce anti-imperialist tract.
  • Sumner the teacher — the lecturer whose course on the science of society, completed after his death by Albert Galloway Keller as The Science of Society (1927–28), trained a generation of American sociologists.

Secondary sense: the intellectual package. Because Sumner's name is attached to a cluster of doctrines, it has acquired a transferred, almost adjectival force. To call a policy “Sumnerian” is to say that it trusts market selection, distrusts paternal legislation, and expects moral habits to change slowly. The phrase “Sumner's forgotten man” refers not to the poor but to the anonymous, self-supporting citizen who is taxed and conscripted to pay for schemes designed for others; the irony is that the phrase is now often misremembered as a reference to the destitute. “Sumnerian ethnocentrism” names the tendency of every group to measure strangers by its own folkways. These are not separate words but stable collocations, and they carry the surname well beyond its original referent.

Tertiary sense: places and other bearers. The surname also survives in toponyms, and each commemorates a different Sumner. Sumner County, Tennessee, honours the Revolutionary general Jethro Sumner; Sumner County, Kansas, and the city of Sumner, Washington, honour Senator Charles Sumner; Fort Sumner, New Mexico, honours General Edwin Vose Sumner; Sumner, Maine, honours Governor Increase Sumner. Charles Sumner (1811–1874), the Massachusetts abolitionist and Radical Republican, is the second most famous bearer; Edwin Vose Sumner was a Union corps commander; Sumner Redstone was a modern media proprietor; and the singer Sting was born Gordon Matthew Thomas Sumner. In each case the word is a bare label, but the associations it brings differ sharply — abolitionist martyr, frontier post, media billionaire, pop star.

Modern connotations and semantic bridges. Today Sumner is academically respectable and politically contested. In sociology textbooks he is a founder whose categories outlived his politics; in political argument his name is shorthand for a hard-hearted “survival of the fittest” creed, even though specialist scholarship since the 1970s has stressed his anti-militarism, his distinction between the struggle against nature and competition among people, and his genuine sociological relativism. The bridge from the medieval name to the modern one runs through the occupation: a summoner was an apparitor who cited offenders before an ecclesiastical court, a figure Chaucer made venal and ridiculous. The surname was therefore never neutral in its earliest English life; it named an official whom ordinary people feared and despised. That faint residue of officiousness may even have made it a convenient handle for a thinker later accused of cold administrative logic.

Why the word matters. Sumner is a case study in how proper names become arguments. It denotes a man, but it connotes a method: observe custom without sentiment, trust competition, distrust reform. Anyone who uses the name today is therefore doing more than identifying a nineteenth-century professor; they are taking a position on how much a society can consciously redesign itself.
