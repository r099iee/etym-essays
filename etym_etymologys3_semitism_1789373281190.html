The etymology of Semitism reflects one of the most consequential and tragic semantic transformations in the history of ideas. Originating as an ancient Hebrew proper name associated with biblical genealogy, it was transformed into a pioneering taxonomic category of Enlightenment comparative linguistics, subsequently subverted into a pseudo-scientific racial theory in the nineteenth century, and ultimately embedded in modern political discourse as the morphological foundation of the term anti-Semitism.

The earliest known root of the word is the Biblical Hebrew noun Šēm (שֵׁם), which historically carried the concrete and abstract meanings of "name," "reputation," "renown," or "monument" (derived from the Proto-Semitic root *šim-, meaning "name," with direct cognates in Arabic ism and Akkadian šumu). In the Book of Genesis (Genesis 5:32; 10:21–31), Shem is identified as the eldest of Noah's three sons (along with Ham and Japheth). In the biblical "Table of Nations," Shem's descendants are portrayed as the genealogical forebears of the Abrahamic, Arabian, Aramean, and Mesopotamian peoples (Elam, Asshur, Arpachshad, Lud, and Aram).

During the Hellenistic and late antique periods, the Hebrew name entered the Mediterranean literary sphere:

  • Greek Septuagint (3rd c. BCE): Translators in Alexandria rendered the Hebrew Šēm into Greek as Sēm (Σήμ).
  • Latin Vulgate (4th c. CE): Saint Jerome preserved this spelling in Christian Europe as Sem. Throughout the medieval and early modern periods, the word remained strictly a biblical proper name, functioning exclusively in theological commentaries regarding sacred history and the post-diluvian repopulation of the earth.

The critical transition from theological genealogy to modern philological science occurred in the late eighteenth century within the vibrant intellectual circle of the University of Göttingen. In 1781, the German historian and philologist August Ludwig von Schlözer published an essay in Johann Gottfried Eichhorn's Repertorium für biblische und morgenländische Litteratur, in which he proposed the term semitische Sprachen ("Semitic languages"). Schlözer recognized that Hebrew, Arabic, Aramaic, Syriac, and Ethiopic (Ge'ez) shared profound structural, phonological, and lexical affinities that proved they belonged to a common ancestral language family. By adopting the biblical name Sem and attaching the Greek adjectival suffix -itēs (-ίτης, "descendant of") and the abstract noun suffix -ismus (-ισμός), Schlözer provided a neutral, scientific label for this linguistic family, mirroring the emerging classification of "Indo-European" (or "Indo-Germanic") languages.

In the mid-nineteenth century, however, comparative philology was catastrophically colonized by biological and racial theories. French orientalist Ernest Renan, in his influential 1855 work Histoire générale et système comparé des langues sémitiques, made the fatal intellectual leap of equating linguistic grammar with innate racial psychology. Renan asserted that while "Indo-European" languages reflected a philosophical, scientific, and mythological genius, the "Semitic" spirit was rigid, strictly monotheistic, legalistic, and incapable of true philosophical abstraction or artistic variety. Arthur de Gobineau's Essai sur l'inégalité des races humaines (1853–1855) further weaponized these linguistic categories, constructing a rigid biological hierarchy.

This pseudo-scientific racialization culminated in 1879 in Berlin, when the German radical nationalist Wilhelm Marr founded the Antisemiten-Liga ("League of Anti-Semites") and popularized the term Antisemitismus. Marr intentionally selected this Latinized, scholarly term to replace the traditional Christian religious phrase Judenhass ("Jew-hatred"). By styling anti-Jewish hatred as an opposition to "Semitism," Marr and his followers sought to present their animus as a legitimate, modern, secular, and biological struggle between competing races rather than religious intolerance.

During the 1890s, when the Dreyfus Affair erupted in France, Édouard Drumont and the anti-Dreyfusard press seized upon "anti-Semitism" as their supreme ideological banner. In response, writers like Marcel Proust and Émile Zola found themselves defending the universal ideals of justice against an irrational political hatred stirred up in the name of resisting "Semitism." Today, the standalone word Semitism survives as a respected technical term in historical linguistics, while in everyday speech it remains inextricably bound to the history of the racial hatred that sought its eradication.
