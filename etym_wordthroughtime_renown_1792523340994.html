- [13th–14th c. Chivalric Middle English] — Borrowed from Anglo-Norman renoun, designating heroic martial glory, aristocratic nobility, and the knightly reputation celebrated by minstrels and heralds - [perception: the supreme chivalric ideal of an unblemished name and martial immortality]
- [15th–16th c. Renaissance & Tudor Literature] — Broadened beyond battlefield prowess to encompass humanistic scholarship, courtly eloquence, poetic mastery, and sovereign statesmanship - [perception: noble secular fame celebrated as the antidote to mortal oblivion]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Augustan Prose] — Employed in philosophical biographies and classical histories to distinguish enduring historical merit from ephemeral court favor or common popularity - [perception: dignified, virtuous public honor earned through civic service or intellectual brilliance]
- [19th c. Victorian Romanticism & Imperial Prose] — Central motif in historical romances, colonial travel accounts, and epic verse (e.g., Tennyson, Scott), evoking legendary deeds and celebrated lineages - [perception: venerable, timeless aura of historic distinction and moral weight]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Retained as an elevated, prestigious noun across journalism, academic honors, and cultural commentary (often in the phrasal idiom "of world renown" or "achieve renown"), sharply distinguished from shallow viral celebrity - [perception: enduring, universally respected acclaim based on genuine substantive achievement]
