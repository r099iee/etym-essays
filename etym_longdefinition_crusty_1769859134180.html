crusty: (1) having or resembling a hard, crisp outer layer or shell; (2) of a person, gruff, irritable, short-tempered, or bluntly abrupt in manner, usually with a warm heart hidden beneath the rough exterior; (3) figurative uses naming anything encrusted, weathered, outdated, or rough-edged. Sense in this literary context. In the passage "He was a wise old thing, and all sorts of strange folk came to see him; for he was an excellent magician, and very kindly into the bargain, if a bit crusty on the surface," crusty describes the old magician's manner. The tone is warm, affectionate, and reassuringly paradoxical: the wizard is a "wise old thing," "excellent" at his craft, and "very kindly into the bargain," but his surface is "a bit crusty." The word here is a near-euphemism for abrupt, terse, or mildly prickly speech — the gruffness that age and independence often bring — with the explicit reassurance that this sharpness is only skin-deep. It is the classic literary figure of the stern-but-tender mentor: the rough outer shell that shields a genuinely kind interior. Crucially, the word does not imply real malice; it is almost endearing, and the writer deliberately juxtaposes "crusty" with "kindly" so that the two qualities coexist like rind and flesh on a loaf. Primary literal sense — a hard outer crust. At its base, crusty means "having a crust": crusty bread with a hard, crackling outer layer and a soft crumb; a crusty pie; a crusty old loaf. The noun crust itself refers to any hard outer coating — the baked surface of bread, the dry rind of cheese, the outer shell of the Earth, the ice on a frozen pond — and the adjective simply states possession of such a surface. Secondary human sense — gruff, irritable, short-tempered. When applied to people, crusty means easily irritated, surly, blunt, or abrupt. A "crusty old sea captain" or a "crusty teacher" is sharp, impatient, and given to brusque replies, but the word usually implies a lovable, well-meaning gruffness beneath the surface rather than genuine hostility or cruelty. This is the sense at work in the test passage. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • Having or resembling a hard, crisp outer layer (crusty bread, crusty snow, crusty soil) — literal, modern.
  • Of a person, gruff, short-tempered, or bluntly irritable; the rough-exterior sense — modern and common.
  • Encrusted, coated, or caked with something hard (crusty pots, crusty boots, crusty old paint) — literal extension, modern.
  • Weather-beaten or aged (a crusty old sailor) — extension of the human sense, affectionate.
  • Technical geology/planetary sense: "crusty" surfaces, i.e. rock, ice, or planetary crusts — scientific register.
  • British informal: out of sorts, irritable, or physically unwell — colloquial, low-frequency.
  • Slang (1980s, mainly British): "crusty" as an adjective for unwashed, grimy, or alternative/punk subcultures (a "crusty" look; "crust punks") — marginal modern usage.
  • Obsolete/archaic poetic: "crusty" applied to the skin meaning hard, dry, or scaly.
The semantic bridge. The whole family rests on one image: the hard shell that forms on the outside of something soft. Bread bakes a crust over a tender crumb; frost builds a crust of ice over a pond; skin hardens into a scab. When the metaphor crossed from baked goods to temperament in the 16th century, the same logic applied: a hard, brittle manner covers a soft interior. A person who is "crusty" is someone whose outer manner is baked hard by age, habit, or self-sufficiency, so that warmth must be looked for underneath. The modern affectionate softening — "a crusty old charmer" — completes the journey from mere surliness to a kind of endearing rigor. Register, collocation, and confusables.
  • "Crusty bread/loaf/roll" — literal, standard, positive (praise for a good crust).
  • "A crusty old man / crusty reply / crusty manner" — human sense, mildly affectionate-to-negative.
  • "Crusty" vs. "encrusted": encrusted means covered over with a deposit (jewels encrusted on a crown), while crusty states the resulting hard layer itself.
  • Root relatives: crust (noun), encrust, crustacean (an animal with a shell), custard (from a dish with a baked crust).
In the test sentence the word is used in its human, temperament sense: the magician's gruffness is a surface phenomenon only, and the reader is invited to love the rough rind for the kindly crumb beneath it.
