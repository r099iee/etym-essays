An honest derivation chain for disposition must begin with a caveat. The verb ponere, "to put, place, set", lies at the core of the word, and its own ancestry is debated: Latin grammarians derived it from an earlier *posinō, a compound of po- ("away, off") and sinō ("to let, leave, set down"), while a popular but rejected tradition attaches it to the PIE root *dheh₁- "to put, set" (the root of Sanskrit dadāti, Greek tithēmi, English do). Modern handbooks (de Vaan) reject that *dheh₁- link as folk etymology, so the genuinely attested chain begins at the Latin verb and flags the pre-Latin stage as unknown. Stage 1 — Latin *posinō > pōnō "to put, place, set" (Classical Latin, 2nd-1st c. BCE): formed from po- ("away, off") + sinō "to let, leave, set down"; deeper Indo-European origin UNKNOWN and debated (the PIE *dheh₁- link is rejected as folk etymology). Stage 2 — Latin positus "placed" (participle, Classical Latin), giving the noun positiō "a placing, position, arrangement" (Cicero, 1st c. BCE). Stage 3 — Latin dispositiō "a placing apart, arrangement, order" (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE): dis- "apart, in different directions" + positiō; used by Cicero for the arrangement of words in a speech and by military writers for the disposition of troops. Stage 4 — Late Latin / Medieval Latin dispositiō "arrangement of a person's temperament" (4th-6th c. CE): the humoral-medicine extension that equated the ordering of bodily qualities with the ordering of character. Stage 5 — Old French disposicion "arrangement, regulation, order" (12th-13th c.): passed from Latin into French with the full range of spatial and administrative senses. Stage 6 — Middle English disposicioun "arrangement, regulation, order; temperament" (14th c., c. 1387 in Chaucer): borrowed from Old French. Stage 7 — Modern English DISPOSITION "settled temperament; arrangement; legal disposal" (16th c. onward; the temperament sense dominant from the 17th c.). Note on the debate: the claimed descent of ponere from PIE *dheh₁- (the root of do, facere, dadāti) is a long-standing popular etymology rejected by the standard handbooks (e.g. de Vaan); the honest chain therefore begins at the attested Latin verb and labels the stage before it as unknown.
