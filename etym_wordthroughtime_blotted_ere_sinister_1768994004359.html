blotted:
- 14th c. - the noun blot enters Middle English as a spot or dark discoloration, especially one made by ink - a mark on a page or on a surface.
- Late medieval - the verb to blot grows from the noun: to smear, to stain, and figuratively to stain a reputation - a blot upon one's name.
- 15th-16th c. - to blot out develops its sense of total erasure: lines struck from a page, names and memories effaced, the mark of something gone.
- 16th-19th c. - the figurative sense deepens in literature and scripture: sins, memories, even the sun are "blotted out"; the word's perception shifts from a mere spot to a permanent, staining obliteration.
- Contemporary - blotted lingers in ink-and-paper imagery and in the phrase "blot out"; public feeling associates it with a messy, absorbed erasure - not mere covering but the smear left behind.

ere:
- Old English ǣr - the everyday adverb, preposition, and conjunction for "before, previously," a common word of daily speech built on the morning-root meaning "earlier."
- Middle English er - still the plain word for "before," used freely in narrative and common talk.
- 16th-17th c. - ere recedes from the spoken language; it survives in elevated, poetic, and scriptural registers, where it lends sentences a solemn, archaic cadence.
- 17th c. onward - a deliberate archaism: the word of prophecy, epic, and the King James Bible; public perception marks it as old-fashioned and grand.
- Contemporary - essentially fossilised in everyday speech, ere is kept alive only for archaic flavour, lending a slow, biblical gravity wherever it appears.

sinister:
- Classical Latin - sinister means "left, on the left side," with secondary senses of awkwardness, perversity, and ill luck; the left hand is the unhandy, unlucky hand.
- Roman augury - the left quarter of the sky is the quarter of omens, baleful or favourable depending on the soothsayer's orientation; "left" becomes tied to portent and misfortune.
- c. 1400 - English borrows sinister directly from Latin, at first simply "left," then "unlucky, evil, threatening."
- 15th-17th c. - the moral half takes over; in heraldry a bend sinister, the diagonal band toward the left, becomes the mark of illegitimacy.
- Modern English - sinister means menacing, evil-omened, and wrong; the physical "left" sense has vanished entirely, leaving only the residue of ill omen.
