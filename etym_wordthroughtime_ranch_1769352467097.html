- [Entering American English (mid-19th c.)] - Adopted from Mexican Spanish "rancho" to name the vast cattle- and sheep-raising estates of the Western frontier; the word arrives already loaded with associations of open range, horses, cowboys, and rough, self-reliant outdoor life.

- [Cattle-era consolidation (late 19th–early 20th c.)] - With the growth of the beef industry, the ranch becomes an American institution and cultural icon; its public image is romanticized in dime novels and rodeo culture as a place of masculine independence, hard physical labor, and wide, unenclosed land.

- [20th c. expansion & dilution] - The term spreads far beyond cattle: dude ranches for vacationers, ranch-style houses, and ranch dressing make "ranch" a wholesome, homespun, nostalgic Americana word, while working ranches grow more commercial and industrialized.

- [Contemporary sense] - Still a working term for a large Western livestock or agricultural operation, yet saturated with nostalgic, frontier-romantic connotations; in everyday speech it now evokes imagery of cowboy culture and open skies as much as the modern business of raising cattle.
