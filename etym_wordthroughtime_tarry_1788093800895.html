- (early 1300s) - Enters English from Anglo-Norman (tarien): “to delay, hinder, remain” — a plain, ordinary verb of everyday waiting.
- (1300s–1500s) - Central in romance and chronicle: lovers and travellers tarry; hosts tarry guests; no special dignity attaches yet.
- (1600s) - Shakespeare uses it as an everyday promise to stay, and the King James Bible consecrates it: “Tarry ye here, and watch with me” — a scriptural, patient colour begins to coat the word.
- (1700s–1800s) - Spoken English retreats to wait/stay/delay; tarry survives in literature, hymns, and rural dialect colour.
- (1900s) - Becomes consciously archaic and literary; fantasy and historical prose adopt it for ancient dignity — The Hobbit’s Elvenking: “Long will I tarry, ere I begin this war for gold.”
- (today) - Perceived as quaint, weighty, gently admonitory; understood instantly but used almost only in elevated prose.
