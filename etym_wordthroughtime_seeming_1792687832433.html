- [Late 12th–13th c. Early Middle English] — Derived from Old Norse *sœma* ("to befit, suit"), *semen* originally meant "to be suitable, proper, or becoming" (*it seems a king to be merciful*) — [perception: associated with decorum, social propriety, and fitting conduct in courtly society]
- [14th–15th c. Middle English & Chaucerian Era] — Underwent a major semantic shift from "fitting" to "presenting an impression or outward appearance"; the participial noun and adjective *seeming* emerged to denote visible look or outward show — [perception: viewed as sensory evidence of reality, though early skeptics began noting appearances could be deceptive]
- [16th–17th c. Elizabethan & Jacobean Drama] — Exploded into theatrical and philosophical prominence in Shakespeare (e.g., Hamlet's rage against "seems" and *Othello*'s "men should be what they seem"), representing hypocrisy, courtly masking, and metaphysical anxiety — [perception: treated with intense suspicion as the deceptive mask of hypocrites, traitors, and Machiavellian actors]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Romantic Poetry] — In philosophical empiricism (Locke, Hume), explored as the boundary of human perception; Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Coleridge) softened the term to describe poetic wonder and imaginative impressions (*"rare, or at least so seeming"*) — [perception: celebrated as the subjective, impressionistic charm through which human consciousness beautifies the ordinary world]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Philosophy & Cognitive Science] — Adopted as a technical term in epistemology (*phenomenal seemings*, *intellectual seemings*) referring to non-inferential experiential impressions that provide prima facie justification for beliefs — [perception: analytically examined as the foundational starting point of human cognition and perception]
