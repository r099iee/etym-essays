The story of fettered begins with the foot. Its deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *ped-, meaning foot, one of the most stable and widespread roots in the family: Sanskrit pád-, Greek poús (genitive podós), Latin pēs (genitive pedis, whence English pedal, pedestrian, expedition), all marching from the same prehistoric sole. In the Germanic branch, Grimm's Law turned the initial p into f, giving Proto-Germanic *fōts, the direct ancestor of English foot, German Fuß, and Dutch voet.

From this foot-word Germanic derived a tool-word: *fetero, literally that which goes on the feet, the iron shackle joining ankle to ankle. The semantic logic is brutally practical: the foot is the organ of escape, so the chain that masters the foot masters the man. Old English fetor inherits this exactly, appearing in law-codes and glosses for the bonds of thieves, captives, and the damned, always worn on the feet, always riveted or locked against slipping.

Stages of the change:

  • Prehistoric foot: PIE *ped- names the foot across the Indo-European world, from India to Italy, grounding the whole family in the body's lowest limb.
  • Germanic hardware: *fōts becomes foot while *fetero becomes the foot's iron counterpart, with cognates like Old Saxon fetar, Old High German fezzera, and Old Norse fjötrr (the gods bind the wolf Fenrir with a fjötrr in Norse myth), proving the fetter's place in every Germanic dungeon.
  • Old and Middle English: fetor the noun breeds feteren the verb, to chain by the feet, and the participle fettered enters chronicles of rebellions, where defeated rebels are led home fettered ankle to ankle.
  • Figurative liberation (17th–19th c.): preachers and pamphleteers free the word from the dungeon: Milton writes of minds, abolitionists of nations, economists of trade, all fettered by invisible iron, until the metaphor rivals the hardware.
  • The triumph of unfettered: in modern English the negated form dominates, from unfettered capitalism to unfettered joy, defining freedom itself as life without the foot-chain.

Phonetically the word has barely moved in fifteen centuries: the f-t-r skeleton survives from *fetero to fetter nearly unchanged, as if the iron resisted sound-change too. Culturally it has travelled from the Anglo-Saxon prison to the Russian fairy tale of the source sentence, where twelve riveted chains on a hanging giant show the fetter's final office: not merely to hold the body but to advertise, by sheer multiplication of iron, that what hangs there must never walk free. Even the word's grammar remembers the dungeon: fettered almost always arrives with its agent attached, fettered by or fettered to, as if no chain were complete without naming both the iron and the hand that locked it.
