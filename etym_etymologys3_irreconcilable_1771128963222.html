Irreconcilable carries within its syllables the oldest machinery of human politics. Its root is the Proto-Indo-European *kel-, 'to call, to summon, to shout out'. In a world without print, writing, or mass media, public life ran on the human voice: to summon a council, announce a law, declare war, or fix the calendar, an authority had to stand in an open space and literally call out. The Latin verb calare preserved exactly this act - 'to call out, to proclaim solemnly' - and from it came the calendae, the first day of the Roman month, when the pontiffs 'called out' the new moon to the assembled people. From the same root descend a host of 'calling' words across the family, from Greek kaleo 'to call, summon' to English claim and clamour.

The crucial step was the Latin noun concilium, formed from com- ('together') + calare: a 'calling together', an assembly of citizens convened by a voice. From this civic noun came the verb conciliare, 'to bring together in a council, to unite, to win over' - the ordinary diplomatic act of making people agree. And from conciliare, the Romans formed reconciliare, with the prefix re- ('again'): to call together again people who had been estranged, to restore broken peace. For Cicero, reconciliatio was the restoration of friendship after a quarrel. The metaphor is profoundly acoustic: reconciliation is re-convocation, the summoning of the separated back into one audible assembly, one room, one law.

From the verb the Latin language formed the adjective reconciliabilis, 'capable of being reconciled', and then, by simple negation, irreconciliabilis, 'incapable of being reconciled'. This negative form flourished in the theological and canonical Latin of the medieval church, where writers needed to say that some sins, errors, or schismatics could never be restored to communion. Middle French took it as irreconciliable in the fifteenth century, and English borrowed it in the decades around 1600 - at first in the heat of religious and political polemic, where one's theological enemies were pronounced irreconcilable with true doctrine.

The subsequent career of the word tracks the transfer of its absoluteness from religion to politics to private life. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries it was a word of faction and party: irreconcilable enemies, irreconcilable parties, irreconcilable kings and parliaments. In the nineteenth it became a familiar word of philosophy and psychology, describing contradictions that no system could absorb. In the twentieth, 'irreconcilable differences' entered the law of divorce, giving millions of couples a legal formula for the discovery that their marriage could not be brought back into harmony - an application that has arguably made the word far more common in the mouths of ordinary people than it had ever been before.

  • - c. 4000-3000 BCE: PIE *kel- 'to call, shout' - the acoustic root of assembly.
  • - Classical Latin: calare 'to call out, proclaim'; calendae 'the calends'; concilium 'a calling together, council'; conciliare 'to unite, win over'; reconciliare 'to bring back together'.
  • - Late / medieval Latin: reconciliabilis and irreconciliabilis in theology and canon law.
  • - 15th c.: Middle French irreconciliable.
  • - c. 1600: English irreconcilable, first in religious and political controversy.
  • - 20th c.: 'irreconcilable differences' as a legal ground of divorce.

Semantic coda: irreconcilable is a word about the failure of the very institution its root built. It presupposes that reconciliation - the calling back together of the separated - is the normal business of human life, and it names the exceptional case in which no call can ever be answered. Four thousand years after the root first named the summoning voice of the assembly, the word still stands, in its Latin form, for the moment when the assembly itself is split beyond recall. In the supplied sentence, two theories are pronounced irreconcilable: the voice that once called citizens together has found a division it cannot bridge, and the word that grew from public summons has become the seal of permanent intellectual war.
