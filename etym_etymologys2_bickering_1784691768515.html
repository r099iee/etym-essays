Origin DEBATED between Germanic and Romance sources; both converge on a root meaning “to strike, peck, hack”: 1. (a) Middle Dutch bikken “to hack, peck, cut” / Middle Low German bicken “to cut, strike” (West Germanic, medieval) — proposed source of the English verb; or (b) Old French bequer “to peck with the beak” (from bec “beak”; 12th c.) — proposed parallel or source. Both are reconstructed from the same sound-base *bik- “to strike, thrust, peck” and the exact route into English is uncertain. 2. Middle English bikkeren “to skirmish, exchange repeated light blows” — frequentative in -eren (first recorded late 14th century, c. 1380–1400) 3. English bicker (14th c.) “to skirmish, fight in small encounters” → (15th–16th c.) “to exchange sharp, petty retorts; to quarrel over trifles” 4. English bicker (imitative strand) “to flicker, ripple, patter” (of flame, water, rain) — in use by the 16th–17th century, literary in the 19th century (Southey, Tennyson, Dickens) 5. Modern English bickering (present participle/gerund: “petty, noisy, repetitive quarrelling”; also of flickering, rippling, pattering) Debate note: the OED favours a connection with Middle Dutch bikken and Old French bequer (“beak”), while some scholars derive the word from an English frequentative of an unattested native *biken “to peck”; the shared root idea is repeated light striking, whether by blades, beaks, or words. The sound-imitative “flicker” sense appears to be a native English development.
