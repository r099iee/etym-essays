PART ONE — The Indo-European root of "part".

The history of departed is the history of a euphemism forming itself out of an ordinary verb of parting. At the root of the whole family stands a Proto-Indo-European verb *per-, reconstructed with the meaning "to hand over, allot, distribute, sell, exchange" — a root of dealing out and sharing, quite different from the homophonous *per- "forward, through" that built English for and fore. From *per- in its "deal out" sense came Latin pars, "a part, share, portion" — literally "that which is apportioned" — with its genitive partis, the basis of English part, partial, particle, partition, and partner.

From the noun the Romans made a verb, partīre, "to share, divide, distribute", and then, with the prefix de- ("from, away"), prefixed formations of separation. The semantic field of the family is separation: to "part" something is to cut it into shares; to "part from" someone is to separate oneself. The Romans already used the language of dividing for the ordinary facts of leaving and separating, and this is the inheritance Old French received.

PART TWO — Old French: from dividing to departing.

In Old French the family underwent its decisive semantic shift. The verb partir, inherited from Latin partīre, kept the sense "to divide, share" (and supplies English part, "to separate", as in "to part ways"). But the prefixed form departir — de- "away" plus partir "to part" — moved from "to divide, separate" to the specific sense "to go away, to leave": to depart from someone is to part away from them, to separate oneself from a place or company. By the twelfth century the Old French poets and chroniclers use departir regularly for "to leave, to set off", and the noun départ, "departure", is fixed in the language. The metaphor at work is the oldest in travel: leaving is a parting, a breaking of the connection with the place left behind.

Middle English borrowed the word directly. Departen appears in English from the thirteenth century with both strands of the French sense: "to separate, divide" (still literary and archaic) and "to go away, leave". The transitive "to part, divide" sense remained literary; the intransitive "to leave" became the ordinary one. Chaucer, Gower, and the fourteenth-century chronicles use departen for leaving and for separation, and the word's career in English was set: an ordinary verb of going away, with a faint but ever-present overtone of division and parting.

PART THREE — The euphemism: death as a journey.

The decisive step came in the sixteenth century, when English began to press the participle departed into the vocabulary of death. The older word for the dead was simply dead; the church had long spoken of the "deceased", but the Renaissance taste for gentleness and dignity found a new instrument. To depart had long been a poetic way of saying "to die" — an image already old in Greek and Latin (the soul "going away", "setting out on the journey") and in the Psalms, where "the days of my life" depart. In the Book of Common Prayer and in funeral sermons, the phrase "the departed" hardened into the standard reverential term: the dead had not been destroyed but had departed, gone away on the last journey, left this world for another. The word's genius is precisely its indirectness: it names the act of leaving rather than the fact of death, and in doing so it comforts the living.

The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries confirmed the usage. "The faithful departed" became a fixed phrase of the prayers for the dead; obituaries and epitaphs adopted "departed" for its dignity; and the adjective passed into common condolence ("my dear departed husband"). Meanwhile the verb's older senses continued to live alongside: "departed from" kept the meaning "deviated from, abandoned" (as a man "departs from" duty or from the truth), and the spatial past tense ("the ship departed") remained the everyday use. The result is a single word with three distinct modern lives — an ordinary verb of leaving, a formal term for deviation, and the solemn euphemism for the dead.

PART FOUR — Sound and sense in English.

  • Latin to Old French: Latin partīre → Old French partir — the sense "divide, share" passes into Romance with the everyday meaning "to leave".
  • Old French to Middle English: departir → departen — the borrowed verb settles into English from the 13th century with both "separate" and "leave" senses.
  • Sixteenth century: the participle departed becomes a noun and adjective for the dead — death reframed as departure.
  • Liturgical fix: the "faithful departed" of the prayer-books makes the euphemism official and permanent.
  • Modern English: departed lives in three registers at once — spatial (the departed train), formal (departed from protocol), and funerary (the departed).

PART FIVE — The euphemism made literal.

On this card, Gibbon pushes the euphemism to its mythological root. "The spirits of the departed were ferried over from the land of the Franks at midnight" turns the dead into literal travellers — souls crossing water in a boat, in the dark, from the land of the living to "this desolate region". The image of the ferry of the dead is older than Christianity (Charon's oar, the Viking funeral ship, the ferryman of Slavic and Germanic legend), and it is exactly what the word departed promises: the dead have not vanished, they have merely gone away. The word thus closes a full circle — from the Indo-European root of allotting and sharing, through Latin and Old French verbs of parting, to an English participle that first meant "gone away" and finally meant, gently and indelibly, "dead, but travelling". DEPARTED is the euphemism that became a myth.
