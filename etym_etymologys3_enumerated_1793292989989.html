The story of enumerated begins with allotting shares: PIE *nem-, to assign, apportion, or take, is the root that gives Greek nemein, to distribute or pasture, and Germanic words for taking — the ancient economy of dividing common goods into counted portions. Latin drew on this root for numerus, number itself: originally the measured portion, the rank in a formation, the rhythmic measure of verse, and only then the abstract figure. Roman soldiers stood in numeri, regiments; poets scanned numeri, rhythms; accountants kept numeri, sums — one word for every place where wholes divide into countable heads.

Prefixing ex-, out or thoroughly, to numerare, to count, produced enumerare: to count out, to reckon up fully, and by natural extension to recount in narrative — Cicero uses it for running through arguments head by head, and imperial clerks for totalling stores. The semantic slide from arithmetic to rhetoric is the whole future of the word in miniature: numbering things and telling them over are one act when each item must be named aloud.

Into French and English:

  • Medieval transmission: scholastic Latin keeps enumerare alive for syllogisms and distinctions, while Old and Middle French shape enumerer for inventories, wills, and chronicles.
  • English borrowing, 1640s: the verb enumerate enters learned prose alongside the statistical age — parish registers, trade tables, and Royal Society reports all enumerate specimens and souls.
  • Constitutional consecration: the American founding fixes enumerated powers and later unenumerated rights in legal bedrock, making enumeration a doctrine: what the list names is granted, what it omits is contested ground.
  • Scientific census: Victorian naturalists and statisticians enumerate strata, species, and slums, and the census enumerator becomes a familiar figure knocking on doors with a numbered schedule.
  • Computing formalisation: twentieth-century programming languages canonise the enum type, where permitted values must be enumerated exhaustively before the program will compile — Cicero enforced by compiler.
  • Pulpit and platform: preachers and lecturers still enumerate heads of doctrine — firstly, secondly, thirdly — because numbered order lets hearers follow, remember, and repeat, keeping alive the oral-rhetorical root of the verb inside literate culture.

Sound shifts: remarkably little changed across two millennia: the Latin vowels and the nu-mer skeleton survive intact through French into English, with only the stress shifting to the second syllable and the participial -ated marking completion in the modern form. Literary attestations run from Ciceronian perorations through Locke and Blackstone to modern judgments weighing enumerated against unenumerated rights. The source sentence stands in that long rhetorical line, using the most bureaucratic of verbs to confess the inexhaustibility of its subject: some human things can be counted, and the rest can only be contemplated.
