In Gibbon's self-portrait as a classical scholar, well-flogged is a grimly comic metaphor for the brutal thoroughness of traditional Latin-and-Greek schooling. He laments that his own "desultory" Greek left him without "the scrupulous ear of the well-flogged critic": the adjective does not merely mean "beaten," but "trained by years of corporal discipline until precision became second nature." The tone is ironical and self-deprecating rather than sadistic; Gibbon grants that the public-school victim of the birch may possess a finer ear for quantity and idiom than the self-taught amateur, even as he flinches at the method. The compound thus fuses physical punishment with intellectual accreditation: a "well-flogged" critic is one whose knowledge has been literally and figuratively driven into the body.

Core meaning: literally, thoroughly beaten with a whip, rod, or birch; by figurative extension, highly disciplined or rigorously trained, especially by harsh methods that produce thorough mastery. The compound combines the degree adverb well- ("thoroughly, completely") with the past participle flogged ("beaten with a whip or stick").

Primary senses:
  • Literal corporal punishment — thoroughly beaten, whipped, or birched: a well-flogged schoolboy, a well-flogged seaman or soldier flogged for desertion.
  • Figurative rigorous training — trained with such severity and repetition that skill becomes ingrained: "the scrupulous ear of the well-flogged critic."
  • Figurative over-use of an idea or argument — a "well-flogged" topic is one beaten to death, overworked, exhausted by repetition (e.g. "a well-flogged argument").

Secondary and extended uses:
  • Naval and military register — in the age of sail, flogging was routine naval discipline; "well-flogged" describes a man punished with the cat-o'-nine-tails, carrying the period connotations of harsh service discipline.
  • Schoolroom register (18th–19th c.) — the classical grammar school used the birch as an instrument of Latinity as much as of order; "well-flogged" is the period-flavoured phrase for a boy whose grammar was literally beaten into him.
  • Ironical self-deprecation — in memoir and biography the phrase is used half-humorously to acknowledge that one's competence was purchased at the cost of suffering, as in Gibbon's lament over his own "desultory" (un-flogged) Greek training.
  • Modern metaphorical echo — in contemporary prose the literal force has faded, and "well-flogged" may be used of any thoroughly drilled or disciplined performer, though it retains its archaic-humorous flavour.

Historical semantic bridge: the literal sense "thoroughly beaten" (flogged + intensive well) developed the figurative sense "rigorously trained" because in the 18th- and 19th-century schoolroom, rigorous training and corporal punishment were the same practice: the same birch that punished also drilled. The bridge from punishment to accreditation is therefore direct and historically grounded, and it is exactly what Gibbon's ironic compliment exploits.

Collocations and register: a well-flogged schoolboy / crew / seaman / soldier; well-flogged for desertion; the scrupulous ear of the well-flogged critic; a well-flogged argument / topic / horse. Register is literary-historical or deliberately archaic-humorous; the compound is rare in everyday prose and would sound jarring in modern non-ironic speech. Antonymic or contrasting phrases include self-taught, desultory, amateur, and lightly schooled — the very contrast Gibbon draws between his own casual Greek and the birch-disciplined precision of the professional classicist.
