PANTHEISTIC: of, relating to, or characteristic of pantheism — the doctrine that God and the universe (or nature) are identical, so that the divine is not a being separate from the world but the world itself, seen as a single whole. A pantheistic view identifies God with nature, rejects a personal creator standing outside creation, and locates the sacred in the totality of things rather than in a transcendent being. The word is the adjective of pantheism and pantheist, coined in the early 18th century, and today it ranges over formal philosophy, comparative religion, Romantic poetry, and ecological spirituality — from Spinoza's single substance to a poet's reverence for a forest to a modern environmental vision of nature as "an ambient, gentle awareness", exactly as the source context describes.

Core senses and current polysemy:
  • Philosophical doctrine (the core sense): in metaphysics, pantheism asserts that "God is everything and everything is God" — one single reality with no distinction between creator and creature. Spinoza's Ethics (1677) is the classic statement: God, or Nature (Deus sive Natura), is a single infinite substance of which all finite things are modes. A pantheistic metaphysics is therefore a monism, denying any separate transcendent God while still affirming that the whole is divine.
  • Religious and comparative-religion use: a pantheistic worldview is contrasted with monotheism (one personal God), with polytheism (many gods), with deism (a God who creates and withdraws), and with atheism (no god at all). Traditions described as pantheistic include strands of Hindu Vedanta (Brahman and the world), Stoic theology (the cosmos as divine reason), the Sufi monism of Ibn Arabi, and various mystical currents in which the soul and the divine are ultimately one.
  • Romantic and literary use: in 19th-century literature a "pantheistic" mood is a religious awe felt in the presence of nature — Wordsworth's "sense sublime / Of something far more deeply interfused", Shelley's animated universe, Goethe's nature-mysticism, Coleridge's fascinated and frightened flirtation with Spinoza. Here the word describes an emotional and aesthetic stance rather than a doctrine: the poet need not argue that God and nature are identical; it is enough that the forest or the mountain is felt as divine.
  • Eco-philosophy and modern spirituality: in contemporary environmental thought a "pantheistic" vision regards the living cosmos as sacred and worthy of reverence without any personified deity — nature as the ambient, gentle presence of the source card. Writers in deep ecology, New Age spirituality, and "nature religion" use the word for a spirituality of immanence, in which the divine is present in every leaf and current rather than above the world. The adjective has thus shifted from cold metaphysics to warm environmental ethic.
  • Adjacent technical terms: panentheistic (God is in the world and also extends beyond it — distinct from pantheistic, which denies any beyond), pandeistic (a rare blend of pantheism and deism), and the now obsolete "pantheistical" (18th–19th centuries, meaning the same as pantheistic).

The historical bridge: The Greek roots are pan- "all" and theos "god", already joined in Greek pantheos "of all the gods" (an adjective used of temples such as the Pantheon, the "temple of all the gods"). The English philosopher John Toland coined the noun pantheist in 1705 to describe — and to advocate — the view that God and the universe are one, and pantheistic followed within a few decades. The concept then travelled from Toland's pamphlets through 18th-century rationalism and the rehabilitation of Spinoza by the German Idealists, into the nature-worship of the Romantics, and finally into modern ecological spirituality. At every stage the same root image — "the all is god" — is retained, while the force of the word moves from formal metaphysics (what is God?) to poetic mood (how nature feels) to environmental ethic (how we should treat the world).

Register: In philosophy and theology the word is precise and technical, one of a family of "-theistic" positions (monotheistic, polytheistic, deistic, panentheistic, atheistic) that must be kept carefully apart. In literary and popular writing it is looser, meaning little more than "finding the divine in nature". It is sometimes carelessly used where "animistic" or "pagan" would be more accurate — the distinctions are set out in similarWords — but its proper force remains that of a philosophical claim: the world and God are one, and a pantheistic vision is one in which nature itself is holy.
