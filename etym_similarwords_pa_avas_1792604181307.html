• mṛdaṅgas: Similar because both are ancient, highly prestigious Indian percussion membranophones played in classical music and sacred ceremonies; differs because mṛdaṅgas are large, heavy, double-headed horizontal barrel drums with clay or wooden bodies famous for deep bass tones, whereas paṇavas are smaller hourglass or tapered tabors prized for sharper, piercing treble strokes.
  • dundubhis: Similar because both resound in epic battles and royal ceremonies to produce solemn acoustic majesty; differs because dundubhis are massive, resonant war kettledrums beaten with heavy sticks to project thunderous low-frequency booms over leagues, whereas paṇavas are handheld or arm-braced tabors played with agile hand or stick techniques.
  • bherīs: Similar because both function as ancient martial drums on heroic battlefields; differs because bherīs are large metal or wooden battle kettledrums designed exclusively for deafening martial alarms and sonic intimidation, while paṇavas possess refined musical tuning suited for theatrical and chamber concerts.
  • ḍamarus: Similar because both share an hourglass or pinched-waist organological silhouette; differs because ḍamarus are small ritual rattle-drums sounded by twisting the wrist so knotted strings strike the heads (closely identified with Shiva's iconography), whereas paṇavas are larger, braced musical drums struck directly by the fingers or beaters.
  • darduras: Similar because both formed part of the canonical classical tripuṣkara theatrical ensemble of ancient India; differs because darduras are bell-shaped or hollow pottery vessel-drums resembling inverted water pots (ghatam), whereas paṇavas are wooden frame-braced membranophones.
  • tabors: Similar because both denote small, portable snare- or tension-drums utilized historically to accompany dancers and civic pageants; differs because tabors are specific to medieval and Renaissance European fife-and-drum traditions, whereas paṇavas belong to classical Indo-Aryan musicology and complex Indian rhythmic talas.
  • kettledrums: Similar because both generate dramatic percussive punctuation in orchestral and martial settings; differs because kettledrums feature a vessel-shaped hemispherical metal bowl with a single upper head tuned to definite orchestral pitches, whereas paṇavas are tubular or hourglass double-headed drums.
  • tom-toms: Similar because both are cylindrical or hourglass non-snare drums used to establish primal acoustic rhythms; differs because tom-toms is an informal onomatopoeic term adopted into Western drum kits and Native American ethnography, lacking the ancient codified classical treatise status of the paṇavas.
  • dholaks: Similar because both are popular South Asian two-headed hand drums; differs because dholaks are folk instruments with simple rope or ring turnings used predominantly in modern folk and film music, whereas paṇavas were elite classical instruments of antiquity codified in Sanskrit dramatic theory.
  • pakhawajs: Similar because both descend from the ancient tradition of north Indian membranophones; differs because the pakhawaj is the direct modern asymmetric descendant of the classical mṛdaṅga used exclusively in solemn Dhrupad singing, whereas the ancient paṇava was an hourglass-shaped companion drum.
  • bodhráns: Similar because both are traditional historic drums played with vigorous hand and stick movements; differs because the bodhrán is an Irish single-headed round frame drum held from the inside, whereas paṇavas are Indian double-headed braced drums.
  • cymbals: Similar because in later medieval regional terminology some lexical sources broadened paṇava to include rhythmic timekeepers; differs because cymbals are metallic idiophones producing shimmering clashes, whereas the primary canonical paṇavas are leather-headed membranophones.
