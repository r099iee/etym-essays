PIE *ghel- "to shine; be bright, green, yellow" (Pokorny; the colour/shine root that named bile by its hue) — earliest reconstructable stage → (Germanic branch) Proto-Germanic *gallōn "bile, gall" — the yellow-green secretion, so named from the root's colour sense → Old English gealla "bile, gall" — recorded in early glosses and medical texts (ninth–tenth century) → Middle English galle "bile; bitter humour" (twelfth century); the noun then takes the sense "sore spot caused by chafing" (fourteenth century) → gall (verb) "to chafe, make sore" (denominal from the noun, 1550s) → GALLED (past participle) "made raw by rubbing; vexed, irritated" (sixteenth century onward) Note on debated/nuanced points: The modern "sore" sense is generally agreed to have grown from the bile-word because abraded flesh was believed to fill with a yellow, bilious discharge; a minority view treats the English sore-sense as an independent development from the idea of hard, bitter lumps (cf. plant-galls), but the mainstream etymology (OED, Etymonline) treats "gall = bile" and "gall = sore" as one semantic family. The same PIE root *ghel- also gave Greek cholē "bile" (whence choler, melancholy), Latin fēl "bile, gall," and Sanskrit hari- "yellowish, tawny" / harita- "green." No alternative PIE root is seriously proposed for the bile-family.
