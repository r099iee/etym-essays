Pelew (proper noun, historical toponym) is the archaic, historically prevalent eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English colonial name for the archipelago and sovereign nation now known as Palau (endonym: Belau), located in the western Pacific Ocean within the Caroline Islands of western Micronesia. In Western historical, ethnological, and literary literature, the term carries enormous cultural resonance, representing one of the Enlightenment’s most celebrated real-world encounters with an egalitarian, matrilineal Pacific civilization.

Geographical & Ethnological Identity:

  • Archipelagic Territory: The Pelew Islands comprise an archipelago of over 340 coral and volcanic islands situated approximately 500 miles (800 km) east of the Philippines. Geographically and culturally positioned within Micronesia, the islands have been continuously inhabited by indigenous Austronesian Palauan populations for over three millennia.
  • Matrilineal Clan Governance & Female Councils: In Will Durant’s historical exposition, the Pelew Islands are specifically cited to illustrate the institutional power of women in traditional matrilineal societies. Traditional Palauan society is organized into matrilineal clans (kebliil). While executive titles and village council chiefs (the klobak) were held by men, political legitimacy, title bestowal, clan land tenure, and ultimate veto authority resided in the hands of the council of senior clan women, known as the ourrot. A high chief (such as the Ibedul of Koror) could undertake no consequential political, diplomatic, or military action without the explicit sanction and counsel of these elder matriarchs.

Historical Significance, Literary Romanticization & Polysemic Context:

  • The Shipwreck of the Antelope (1783): The name Pelew erupted into global prominence following the August 1783 shipwreck of the British East India Company packet ship Antelope, commanded by Captain Henry Wilson, upon the reefs of Ulong Island. The British crew was received with extraordinary generosity and hospitality by the High Chief of Koror (the Ibedul). The crew spent three months constructing a replacement schooner, forging a deep cross-cultural friendship.
  • Prince Lee Boo and European Enlightenment Reception: Upon Wilson's departure in 1784, the Ibedul entrusted his second son, Prince Lee Boo (Lebuu), to accompany Captain Wilson to London to learn Western knowledge. Lee Boo was hailed in British society as the ideal embodiment of the "virtuous natural gentleman," captivating London high society before his tragic death from smallpox in December 1784. In 1788, author George Keate published An Account of the Pelew Islands, compiled from Wilson's journals. The book became an international bestseller, translated into French, German, and Spanish, deeply influencing Romantic poets (such as Samuel Taylor Coleridge and William Lisle Bowles) and Enlightenment philosophers who cited the "Pelew Islanders" as proof of innate human morality and societal harmony without European artifice.
  • Cartographic & Colonial Succession: Throughout the nineteenth century, British admiralty charts, imperial treaties, and anthropological works universally labeled the archipelago as the Pelew Islands. Following the Spanish-American War of 1898, Spain sold the islands to the German Empire (1899), which introduced the spelling Palau; control subsequently passed to Japan (1914–1944) and the United States (1944–1994 as part of the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands), before Palau achieved sovereign independence in 1994. In modern English, Pelew survives exclusively as a historical toponym in archival, literary, and ethnological contexts.
