- Early 16th to 17th Century (Scholastic Theological Inception) - Coined in Late Scholastic Latin and borrowed into Early Modern English theological treatises; used strictly in Catholic and Protestant dogmatics to describe the divine attribute of being incapable of sinning (impeccabilitas), attributed uniquely to God, Christ, or the elect in glory.
- Mid-17th to 18th Century (Moral Broadening and Secularization) - Expands in philosophical writing and moral essays (Milton, Dryden, Locke) to qualify spotless human virtue, uncorrupted conscience, and ethical integrity that withstands the temptations of vice.
- Late 18th to 19th Century (Aesthetic and Neoclassical Polish) - Undergoes widespread secularization across literary reviews, neoclassical art critique, and Victorian etiquette; used to describe flawless poetic meter, impeccable taste, elegant dress, and immaculate high-society manners.
- 20th Century (Scholarly, Philological, and Technical Benchmark) - Solidifies in modern intellectual prose and scientific criticism as the premier high-register adjective for flawless transcription, forensic precision, musical virtuosity, and institutional governance.
- 21st Century (Ubiquitous Contemporary Hallmark) - Retains an aura of refined, prestigious excellence across global literature, academic peer review, gastronomy, and cultural commentary, designating an execution so consummate that criticism is silenced.
