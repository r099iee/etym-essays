- Late 14th Century (Middle English Inception) - Entered the English lexicon via Anglo-Norman ecclesiastical French (*malediccioun*) and the Latin Vulgate (*maledictio*); functioned primarily in liturgical, scriptural, and canonical contexts as the formal antithesis to *benediction*, designating a solemn curse, excommunication, or divine condemnation of sinners.
- 15th to Early 16th Century (Late Medieval Devotional Drama) - Deeply embedded in biblical mystery plays, homiletic treatises, and early Bible translations (Wycliffe, Tyndale); perceived by society as a terrifying, spiritually efficacious formula capable of unloosing real supernatural vengeance and physical affliction upon oath-breakers.
- Late 16th to 17th Century (Jacobean Drama & Secularization) - Dramatized and secularized by Renaissance playwrights (Shakespeare, Webster, Marlowe) and epic poets (Milton); expanded from strictly sacerdotal rites into powerful theatrical climaxes, where dispossessed kings, abandoned lovers, and tragic protagonists spat bitter maledictions against betrayal and tyranny.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment Rationalism & Rhetorical Invective) - With the decline of widespread belief in literal witchcraft and magical curses, Enlightenment writers (Swift, Pope, Johnson, Gibbon) redeployed *malediction* with stylistic irony and elevated formality to describe vicious political pamphlets, parliamentary vituperation, and virulent sectarian polemics.
- 19th Century (Gothic Revival & Romantic Historiography) - Flourished anew in Gothic fiction (Mary Shelley, Maturin, Poe) and Romantic historical novels (Walter Scott), where ancient ancestral maledictions served as dramatic narrative machinery haunting castles and doomed aristocratic bloodlines across generations.
- 20th Century to Contemporary Usage - Virtually absent from casual spoken English, the word is preserved as an elevated, evocative literary noun employed by historians, novelists, and essayists to denote ornate curses, dramatic imprecations, or intense, sustained verbal hostility hurled at prominent figures.
