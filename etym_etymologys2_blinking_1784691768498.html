=== ROOT WORD: BLINK (single root, Germanic) === Stage 1 — PIE *bʰel- "to shine, flash, burn" (c. 4000–3500 BCE; the productive root of blaze, blank, bleach, blue, blond; also behind Latin flamma "flame" and flagrare "to burn") Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *blīkaną "to shine, gleam" (reconstructed) and the nasalized variant *blinkaną "to gleam, sparkle, blink" (reconstructed; the -n- infix creates the blink-form, cf. the pair blick / blink) Stage 3 — Middle Low German blinken / Middle Dutch blinken "to shine, glitter, sparkle" (attested 13th c.) and Middle English blinken "to shine, sparkle; to glance; to blink" (c. 1300 — whether native from the same Germanic base or borrowed from the continental forms is debated; both point to the same Germanic family) Stage 4 — Early Modern English blink "to close and open the eyes quickly; of a light, to flicker" (16th c.) Stage 5 — Modern English blink + present-participial suffix -ing → blinking "blinking; (colloquial intensive) confounded, bloody-like" (16th c. as participle; 19th c. as the British colloquial intensive) => blinking (modern English participle / participial adjective) DEBATE / NUANCE: The Middle English verb blinken "to shine" may be native (built on an English base related to Old English blīcan "to shine, gleam") or borrowed from Middle Low German / Middle Dutch blinken; scholarship cannot decide with certainty, but all candidates belong to the same Germanic *blīk- / *blink- family under the PIE *bʰel- "shine" root. The development of the "eye" sense from "shine/flicker" (because the eye glints as it opens and closes) is secure. The colloquial intensive sense is purely English and later (19th c.), born of the Victorian euphemism for bloody; it is unrelated to the physiology except through the metaphor of a mild shock making one blink.
