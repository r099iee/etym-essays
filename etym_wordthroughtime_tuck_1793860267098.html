- Old English / Early Middle English (ca. 12th–13th c.) - Attested in the sense of pulling roughly, snatching, jerking, or tugging (cognate with tug); carried connotations of rough handling, twitching, or physical discipline.
- Middle English (14th c.) - Specialised within the textile guilds for the fulling and finishing of woven woollen cloth; 'tucking' involved cleansing, beating, and stretching cloth on tenterframes (giving rise to the English occupational surname Tucker).
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - Shifted decisively into apparel and domestic life; meant drawing up long skirts, folding garments into a belt or girdle, or stowing edges neatly away out of the mud; simultaneously acquired an independent homophonic noun sense as a rapier or thrusting sword (via Old French estoc / Italian stocco).
- 18th to 19th c. - Standardized across familial domesticity for putting children to bed ('tucking in' with blankets); British boarding school slang adopted 'tuck' as a noun for sweetmeats, pastries, and tuck-shop delicacies, and as a colloquial verb for eating greedily ('tuck in').
- 20th to 21st c. - Broadened into biomechanics and sports for tightly curled body postures (diving, gymnastics, skiing); clinical medicine adopted 'tummy tuck' (abdominoplasty); LGBTQ+ and drag communities developed 'tucking' as a specialized practice of concealing genital contours; and modern interface design speaks of 'tucking away' menus and sidebars.
