historiographer in the source passage — "Could a historiographer drive on his history, as a muleteer drives on his mule,——straight forward ." — appears in Laurence Sterne's landmark meta-fictional novel The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman (1759; Book I, Chapter XIV). Sterne uses the grand, formal title historiographer with profound irony to describe the chronicler or biographer who attempts to construct a coherent, uninterrupted historical narrative of a human life. Sterne's narrator explains that a true historiographer faces an impossible challenge: unlike a muleteer who can drive his mule straight ahead along a road without distraction, a writer of history cannot advance a single step without being overwhelmed by a thousand collateral facts, ancestral anecdotes, legal technicalities, and philosophical reflections that demand immediate explanation, forever shattering the illusion of linear narrative progress.

Historically, a historiographer (Greek ἱστοριογράφος, Latin historiographus, French historiographe) is an official scholar or author appointed by a royal court, sovereign monarch, civic republic, or religious order specifically commissioned to write the official annals, chronicles, and authorized historical records of the realm. Unlike a general amateur historian or chronicler, an official Historiographer Royal (such as those appointed to the courts of England, Scotland, or France, including literary luminaries like John Dryden, Thomas Shadwell, Jean Racine, Nicolas Boileau, and Voltaire) occupied an esteemed state office tasked with celebrating the achievements of the regime, recording monumental state events, and curating historical memory for posterity.

  • Official Court Chronicler and State Historian (The Primary Historical Sense): An authorized, salaried state official appointed to write the official history of a reigning dynasty, sovereign nation, or government institution (e.g. "John Dryden was appointed Historiographer Royal by King Charles II in 1670"; "Voltaire served as Historiographe de France, chronicling the reign of Louis XIV"). The bridge to classical antiquity is direct: Greek city-states and Hellenistic kings appointed official historiographoi to record military victories and diplomatic treaties.
  • Writer or Chronicler of History (The General Literary Sense): In classical, Renaissance, and eighteenth-century prose, used as a formal, dignified synonym for any serious author who writes historical narratives, chronicles, or biographies (e.g. "the ancient historiographers preserved vital accounts of the Peloponnesian War"). This is the broader literary sense invoked in Sterne's humorous comparison.
  • Scholar of Historiography (The Modern Academic Sense): In contemporary university humanities, a specialist who studies historiography — the history, theory, methodology, ideological biases, and narrative techniques of historical writing over time (e.g. "the historiographer examined how Victorian colonial assumptions shaped Edward Gibbon's portrayal of Rome").
  • The Self-Conscious Meta-Narrator: In literary theory and post-modern fiction, a narrator who constantly reflects upon the limitations, artifices, and structural impossibility of writing history objectively without personal bias or narrative distortion.
  • Compound Morphological Structure: The word is an elaborate compound built from Ancient Greek elements: historia (inquiry, knowledge obtained by investigation), the verbal root graphein (to write, scratch, record), the Greek combining suffix -graphos, and the naturalized English agent suffix -er (doubling the agency of the classical title).
  • Tone and Register: historiographer is an archaic, formal, and scholarly noun. While modern scholarship generally prefers historian for someone who studies the past, historiographer is deliberately preserved when referring to official court appointments, ancient Greek and Roman chroniclers, or theoretical studies of narrative methodology.

Stylistically, historiographer evokes the dignified, velvet-robed grandeur of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century European court culture and scholarly academies. It contrasts sharply with the humble, everyday vernacular of the working road, making Sterne's juxtaposition with the muleteer endlessly brilliant and comic.
