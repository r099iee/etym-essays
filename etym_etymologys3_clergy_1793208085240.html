The history of clergy opens with the rattle of dice. The Greek noun klēros first meant a small object cast to decide — a pebble, a potsherd, a marked stick — the lot by which soldiers divided spoil and sailors chose the guilty; Jonah own shipmates cast klēroi to find him. From the act of casting, the word slid to whatever the cast assigned: the allotted share, the apportioned field, the inheritance. Athenian colonists received their klēroi, plots of colonial land; the word is thus cousin to cleruchy, the Athenian colonial system. Nothing could seem further from priests — until the early Christians, reading their Greek Scriptures, found the bridge. Matthias is chosen by lot to replace Judas; the Levites are called the Lord portion; Peter warns pastors against lording it over the klēros, the allotted flock. Within two generations the Christians were calling their ministers themselves the klēros — God lot, God inheritance, the portion set apart for sacred service — and the gambler pebble had become the priest.

Latin Christendom took the word as clērus, and the third-century Church gave it institutional iron. Cyprian and Tertullian already oppose clerus to laicus, the people; the Councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon legislate for clerics dress, marriage, and morals; and by the time of Gregory the Great the clergy are a parallel state with their own courts, lands, and taxes. The phonology of the borrowing is straightforward — Greek kappa gives Latin c, the long ē survives — but the sociology is revolutionary: an empire that once persecuted the clerus now exempts and endows it. Jerome and Augustine both pause over the etymology, noting with satisfaction that ministers are called God inheritance precisely so that God may be theirs — may the Lord himself be their klēros — a piece of patristic wordplay that preachers have never tired of repeating.

Old French clergie carried the word into the feudal world with a fateful double meaning: the clerical order AND learning itself. Since only churchmen could read, clergie meant scholarship, science, even magic — the clerk was the wise man, and the clerc who copied charters shaded into the clerk who kept shop accounts. Middle English inherited the whole bundle: Chaucer Clerk of Oxenford embodies hollow-cheeked learning; Langland clergie debates with the dreamer; and the legal fossil benefit of clergy let any felon who could read the neck-verse escape the gallows, until literacy spread so far that the privilege became absurd and was abolished in the 1820s.

  • Lots to Levites (antiquity to 100): Greek klēros, cast lot and allotted share, is adopted by the infant Church for the ministry as God allotted portion.
  • Order and empire (100–600): clērus hardens into the ordained estate opposed to the laity, endowed by Christian emperors with courts, lands, and immunities.
  • The learned monopoly (600–1400): through Old French clergie the word absorbs learning itself; clerks read, write, copy, and account for a largely illiterate Europe.
  • Reformation and division (1500s–1600s): reformers retain ordained ministry while attacking clerical privilege; clergy versus ministry becomes a confessional marker, and the clerk drifts toward the counting-house.
  • Establishment and attack (1700s–1830s): the Hanoverian clergy, tithes, pluralities, and bishops in the Lords draw radical fire — the source sentence anger at a clergy sanctifying repression is the age purest expression.
  • Reform to the present (1830s to now): tithe commutation, disestablishment debates, the ordination of women, and ecumenical ministry remake the clergy as a profession of pastoral service rather than an estate of power, while the word travels by analogy to the functionaries of every world faith.

Literary attestation runs from Chaucer sly Friar and worldly Monk through Shakespeare ambitious bishops, Milton blind fury at hireling shepherds in Lycidas, the Reformation martyrologies on both sides, Trollope infinitely observant Barchester clergy, and the modern novel of clerical doubt from The Power and the Glory to Diary of a Country Priest. Through it all the buried metaphor of the lot persists: the clergy remain, in the word own secret history, those on whom the lot has fallen — chosen, allotted, set apart — whether their neighbours regard the allotment as a privilege to be envied, a power to be checked, or a burden to be pitied.
