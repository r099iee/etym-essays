Inventio (Latin, literally "a finding, a coming upon") is the technical name for the first of the five canons of classical rhetoric: the discovery of the arguments and materials that a speech needs. It is not the invention of new facts but the finding of the available means of persuasion in a given case. In the source the capital I marks it as a term of art — Antony's remarks in Book II of Cicero's De Oratore on inventio, the first department of the orator's work.

The canon and its place in rhetoric

  • The five canons: inventio (finding the arguments), dispositio (arranging them), elocutio (wording them), memoria (memorising the speech) and pronuntiatio or actio (delivery). The list is standard in Latin handbooks from the Rhetorica ad Herennium and Cicero's De Inventione onward.
  • Cicero's definition: "Inventio est excogitatio rerum verarum aut veri similium quae causam probabilem reddant" — invention is the working out of things that are true or resemble the truth, such as to make the case probable (De Inventione I.7.9). The definition is forensic: invention serves plausibility in a court, not fantasy.
  • The Greek background: Aristotle's Rhetoric analyses the pisteis, the means of persuasion — ethical, emotional and logical — and distinguishes artistic proofs the speaker constructs from non-artistic proofs such as witnesses and contracts. Later Greek rhetoric, especially Hermagoras of Temnos, developed stasis theory to determine what is actually in dispute.
  • Topics: invention proceeds through topoi (Latin loci), "places" of argument — definition, comparison, cause and effect, circumstance, testimony — from which a speaker searches for suitable material. The metaphor is spatial: one hunts for arguments in known locations.
  • Not creation from nothing: the classical orator does not invent the facts; he finds the arguments latent in them. This is the crucial difference from the modern English sense of "invention."

Inventio in De Oratore

  • The dialogue divides the art among its speakers. Crassus, in Book I and Book III, treats the ideal of the orator and the resources of style; Antonius, in Book II, expounds invention, arrangement, memory, delivery and humour.
  • Antonius's treatment is practical rather than philosophical: he shows how a speaker searches out the strong points of a case, anticipates the opponent's arguments, and shapes the narrative to support the probable.
  • The source passage observes that Antony's comments on Inventio "are certainly endorsed by the author," even where other speeches in the dialogue are ambiguous. The technical discussion is thus one of the places where Cicero's own doctrine can be read with most confidence.
  • This matters because De Oratore is not a textbook but a dialogue with competing voices. The question of which speaker represents Cicero is central to its interpretation, and the treatment of invention is a test case.

Inventio and English "invention"

  • English invention descends from the same Latin noun, through Old French invencion. Its earliest English sense is "discovery, finding out," which still survives in phrases such as "the invention of the true cross" or "the Invention of relics."
  • From the sixteenth century the word shifts toward "something newly devised" — a machine, a process, a contrivance — and then toward "fabrication, made-up story." The modern default is creation of something new, not discovery of something already there.
  • Patent law fixes the modern sense: an invention must be new, useful and non-obvious. The legal word is a direct descendant of the rhetorical term, but its criterion of novelty would have puzzled Cicero, for whom inventio was the recovery of arguments available in the case.
  • Music: Bach's Inventionen (1723) gave the word a genre sense — a short contrapuntal piece built on a single motive — which survives in the titles of works by later composers.
  • Rhetorical study: modern composition theory revived "invention" in the twentieth century to name the pre-writing work of finding something to say, often with explicit reference back to the classical canon.

Register and use today

  • Inventio is a technical Latin term, usually italicised in English prose and often capitalised when it names the canon or a heading, as in the source.
  • It belongs to classical rhetoric, medieval and Renaissance education, and modern rhetorical theory; outside those fields it is opaque, and English "invention" or "invention of arguments" is used instead.
  • The word carries no pejorative force in its technical sense, though its English descendant can mean a lie ("a convenient invention") — a drift that illustrates how far the modern word has travelled from the classical canon.
