The etymological trajectory of the word lichen represents a fascinating journey of semantic transference spanning over three millennia, beginning with a physical action of the tongue, shifting to a distressing medical pathology, and culminating in one of the most transformative biological discoveries in the history of science.

The ultimate ancestor of the term is the Proto-Indo-European root *leyǵʰ- (alternatively reconstructed as *leigh-), an onomatopoeic verbal root signifying "to lick" or "to lap with the tongue." This root produced a wide family of cognates across the Indo-European spectrum, including Sanskrit rihati ("he licks"), Latin lingere ("to lick") and lingua ("tongue"), Old Church Slavonic lizati ("to lick"), Old Irish ligim ("I lick"), Old English liccian (modern English lick), and Ancient Greek leíkhein (λείχειν, "to lick").

In Ancient Greek, the nominal derivative λειχήν (leikhēn) emerged through semantic extension. The imagery arose from the way certain creeping skin diseases—such as scabby eruptions, ringworm, eczema, or psoriasis—appear to "lick" or slowly lap across the surface of the human body, spreading gradually like a tongue sweeping over skin. In Hippocratic medical treatises from the 5th and 4th centuries BCE, leikhēn designated a chronic, rough, scaly skin eruption or "tetter."

The crucial metaphorical bridge from human dermatology to botany occurred during the classical Greek period. In his foundational botanical treatise Historia Plantarum (Enquiry into Plants), Theophrastus (c. 371–c. 287 BCE), often termed the father of botany, observed that certain dry, scabby, crust-like plant growths clinging tenaciously to the bark of olive and oak trees bore an uncanny visual resemblance to the scabby cutaneous plaques of human leikhēn. Theophrastus therefore transferred the medical noun to these arboreal encrustations, creating a botanical category that coexisted alongside its original pathological meaning.

This dual semantic cargo was subsequently adopted into Classical Latin as līchēn (genitive līchēnis). In Roman natural history and medicine, Pliny the Elder (23–79 CE) in his monumental Naturalis Historia utilized lichen in both senses: describing the parasitic plant-like growths on trees and rocks (from which Roman dye-makers extracted valuable pigments) and detailing medicinal recipes to cure human facial eruptions (which Roman physicians also called lichen or mentagra).

Following the decline of the Western Roman Empire, the term was preserved in medieval medical and monastic herbals. During the Renaissance, as Greek and Latin medical texts were rediscovered and translated into European vernaculars, lichen entered Middle French and Early Modern English around the turn of the 17th century. Initially, English medical writers (such as Philemon Holland in his 1601 translation of Pliny) used lichen primarily to describe scaly tetters and ringworms. Concurrently, natural philosophers such as John Ray (1686) and later Carl Linnaeus in his Species Plantarum (1753) formalized Lichen as a genus under the class Cryptogamia (plants with hidden reproductive organs).

The definitive conceptual revolution occurred in 1869, when Swiss botanist Simon Schwendener published his "dual hypothesis" of lichens. Schwendener demonstrated that lichens were not autonomous primitive plants, but rather compound dual organisms consisting of a fungal master and an algal captive—a revolutionary thesis that established the modern scientific paradigm of mutualistic symbiosis. In the late 20th and 21st centuries, molecular genetics further revealed that many lichens also incorporate specific basidiomycete yeasts and bacterial communities within their cortex, cementing lichen as an emblem of biological complexity and ecological interconnectedness.
