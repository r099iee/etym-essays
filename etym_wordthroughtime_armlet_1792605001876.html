- [Late 16th c. (Elizabethan Era)] - coined in Early Modern English as a morphological diminutive of arm (modeled on ringlet and bracelet), describing ornate metal jewelry and spiral bands worn on the upper arm by nobility or classical figures - [perception: admired as an exotic, opulent ornament of classical and eastern antiquity]
- [17th c. (Stuart & Restoration Era)] - broadened by physical metaphor from "an arm of the sea" to denote a small branching inlet, tidal creek, or river tributary - [perception: recognized in cartography and maritime travel as a precise geographical descriptor]
- [18th–19th c. (Victorian & Colonial Archaeology)] - heavily utilized in classical scholarship, archaeological reports (excavations of Anglo-Saxon barrows, Viking hoards, and Egyptian tombs), and translations of Indian epics (Sanskrit keyūra) - [perception: viewed as a romantic, heroic symbol of warrior kingship, aristocratic wealth, and ancient chivalry]
- [Mid-20th c. (Post-War Era)] - adopted in British English and Commonwealth nations as the standard commercial and regulatory term for inflatable vinyl swimming aids worn by children learning to swim - [perception: acquired a common, domestic, utilitarian meaning alongside its historical jewelry sense]
- [21st c. (Contemporary Era)] - maintained in high fashion and bohemian jewellery design (bicep cuffs, spiral cuffs), while enduring as an indispensable term in museum curation and physical hydrology - [perception: regarded as a distinctive, elegant term bridging ancient royal jewelry and modern design]
