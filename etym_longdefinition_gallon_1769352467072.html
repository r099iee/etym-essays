gallon (noun) — a unit of liquid capacity; in the United States, the customary gallon equals 231 cubic inches, or 3.785 liters; in Britain and the Commonwealth, the Imperial gallon equals 4.546 liters, or the volume of 10 pounds of distilled water. It is divided into four quarts, eight pints, or thirty-two gills. In everyday life the gallon is the standard bulk measure for milk, fuel, paint, and other liquids, and in the source passage from Of Mice and Men it appears in George's bitter fantasy of freedom: "I could go get a job an' work... I could eat any place I want... Get a gallon of whisky, or set in a pool room and play cards." Here the gallon is not an industrial measure but a symbol of excess — a gallon of whisky is an impossible, extravagant quantity for a man who cannot even hold a job, and the word does double duty as both a precise unit and a loose figure of abundance. Primary senses:
  • A unit of liquid capacity — "a gallon of milk," "ten gallons of fuel," "the tank holds fifty gallons."
  • Specifically, in US usage, the 231-cubic-inch wine gallon of 3.785 liters; in UK/Imperial usage, the 4.546-liter gallon, the volume of ten pounds of distilled water at a specified temperature.
  • Figuratively, in the plural, an immense or lavish quantity — "gallons of paint," "gallons of tears," "gallons of coffee" — the loose rhetorical use that turns a precise measure into an idiom of abundance.
Secondary and technical senses — the polysemy in full:
  • Historical measures: the gallon has not always been one thing. Medieval England knew several overlapping gallons — the wine gallon, the ale gallon, the corn gallon, and the dry gallon — differing by commodity and region until Parliament fixed the standards. The modern split is the fossil of that history: the US kept the old English wine gallon of 231 cubic inches, while Britain redefined the gallon in 1824 as the Imperial gallon of 4.546 liters.
  • Legal and fiscal usage: the gallon has been a unit of taxation and regulation since the Middle Ages, the basis of excise duties on wine, beer, spirits, and later petrol; "gallonage" is a technical term for the amount measured in gallons and for taxes levied per gallon.
  • Engineering and metrology: in the US customary system the gallon is defined from the inch (231 cubic inches); in the Imperial system it is defined from the pound and water density. The two gallons differ by about 20%, which is a standing trap in engineering and trade — "the gallon gap."
  • Colloquial exaggeration: "a gallon of" anything means a great deal of it — "a gallon of coffee" (a very large pot), "gallons of rain," "gallons of homework" — the precise measure stretched into pure hyperbole, exactly as Steinbeck stretches it in the whisky passage.
  • Related derivatives: "gallon" appears in compounds and derived terms — "gallonage" (capacity or tax in gallons), "half-gallon," "gallon-size," and in older use a "gallon" of wine or ale as a container itself, the jug or jar of that capacity.
Historical semantic bridge. The word begins as the name of a liquid container, not a fixed measure. Medieval Latin galo and galeta named a jar, jug, or bowl for wine and other liquids, probably descending from a Celtic word for a vessel. When trade demanded a standard amount, the container's name was transferred to the quantity it held, and royal statute fixed that quantity — the gallon became the volume that had to fill the king's gallon-measure. The modern word therefore still holds two lives in balance: the precise, legally defined unit of the market, and the loose, generous "gallon of" of everyday speech — a measure that is simultaneously exact and, in the right hands, a wild exaggeration.
