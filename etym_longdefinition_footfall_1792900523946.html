Footfall names the most ghostly part of walking: the sound a foot makes when it meets the ground. It is a word for listeners rather than walkers — for those who sit up at night because they heard one on the stair, for hunters reading the wood, for Tolkien’s Elves who can pass, when they wish, without any. Where footstep marks the print left behind, footfall marks the instant of striking: the fall of the foot, caught by the ear and gone.

In the source sentence the word is defined by its absence: Elves ‘could walk when they wished without sound or ______.’ The pairing of sound with footfall is almost redundant, and deliberately so — it insists that elvish silence is total, neither the noise in general nor the particular tap of sole on soil. Against hobbits, who at least rustle, and Men, who clump, the Elves move like shadows and faint lights, and footfall is the measure of their grace.

Core meaning in detail: A footfall is a single audible tread — soft or heavy, near or far — and by extension the pattern of such treads: light footfalls on gravel, the heavy footfall of the night-watchman. Writers love it because it is both concrete (a noise with a cause) and atmospheric (a noise with a meaning): a footfall in an empty house is never just acoustics. The plural, footfalls, gathers steps into a sequence, the audible signature of someone approaching or withdrawing.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The sound of a footstep (primary, from early 17th c.): we heard footfalls in the corridor. The bridge is compositional: foot + fall in the old sense of a falling blow or stroke — the foot’s descent made audible.
  • The step or tread itself (extended): to follow someone’s footfalls, with stealthy footfall. The bridge shifts attention from the sound to the movement producing it, so the word covers silent steps too — exactly Tolkien’s use.
  • Manner of walking, tread (literary): she knew him by his footfall. The bridge individualises the sound: as voices differ, so do treads, and the footfall becomes identity made audible.
  • Number of passers-by, pedestrian traffic (modern British, 20th c.): shop footfall fell by a tenth, high-street footfall. The bridge aggregates: countless single footfalls blur into a statistic, and retailers count footfalls the way website owners count clicks — feet as commercial weather.
  • Pressure or impact of treading (technical, rare): soil scientists and farriers speak of footfall pressure on turf. The bridge strips away the sound entirely, keeping only the physics: the fall of weight through the foot.

Register and connotation: Footfall is quiet, watchful, and faintly gothic — corridors, staircases, night woods, empty houses. It implies a listener holding their breath and a walker who may not wish to be heard. The retail statistician’s footfall is its daylight twin, brisk and bloodless, counting crowds where the novelist counts heartbeats. Between them the word spans dread and data: the same syllables that chill a ghost story now fill a shopping-centre report, and both depend on feet that keep arriving.
