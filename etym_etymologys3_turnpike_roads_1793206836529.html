The compound turnpike-roads welds three histories — a turning, a spike, and a riding — into a single institution that paved Georgian England. Its first element, turn, descends from Greek tornos, the lathe on which wood is rounded by rotation, through Latin tornare, to turn on that lathe, and Old French torner, whence Middle English turnen inherits the whole circle of senses from carpentry to dancing to changing one's mind. Its second element, pike, is the infantryman's sharp staff: Old French pique, from popular Latin *piccare, to prick or pierce, a word of uncertain, possibly imitative origin that also gives the fish that darts like a spear. Set a row of such pikes in a revolving frame and you have the turnpike of the fifteenth century — a gate that turns, bristling, to admit friends and wound intruders.

Its third element, road, began as an action, not a place. Old English rad meant a riding, a journey on horseback, from ridan, to ride; only in the thirteenth century did rode settle into the strip of ground the riding uses. The hyphenated Georgian plural turnpike-roads therefore reads, with full literalness, as ridings barred by turning spikes — journeys you may only make by paying at the gate.

How the institution grew:

  • Medieval barrier (15th–16th c.): turnpikes block roads, bridges, and breaches against enemies and cattle-plague alike; the word belongs to soldiers and engineers, not to travellers.
  • First toll act (1663): Parliament lets the Great North Road charge users for repairs — the revolutionary principle that the traveller, not the parish, pays — and the pike-gate becomes a cash register.
  • Trust boom (1706–1770s): hundreds of turnpike trusts borrow, gravel, ditch, and straighten; engineers such as Metcalf, Telford, and McAdam turn mud into metalling, and the stage-coach becomes the fastest machine on earth.
  • Golden gridiron (1780s–1830s): some twenty thousand miles of turnpike-road web the kingdom; tariff boards price every horse, wheel, and bullock, and guidebooks measure England in tolls.
  • Cobbett's revolt (1820s–1830s): the Rural Rides denounce the system from the saddle — tolls taxing the labourer's loaf, trusts fattening fundholders — and the lane becomes the moral alternative to the highway.
  • Rebecca and the railway (1839–1850s): Welsh farmers in women's dress smash the hated gates while locomotives drain the traffic; trust after trust defaults on its bonds.
  • Municipal ending (1870s–1895): county councils and highway boards absorb the roads, the last tolls are extinguished, and keepers' cottages become tea-rooms and museums.
  • American afterlife (1940–today): the Pennsylvania Turnpike borrows the venerable name for the superhighway, and millions who never saw a pike pay at plazas named for gates their grandfathers tore down.

Cobbett's hyphen matters: turnpike-roads as one double-barrelled noun shows how completely gate and highway had fused in the mind, so that to name the road was to name the toll. His scorn is economic as well as aesthetic — every penny at the gate raised the price of bread — and his remedy, foot and horseback, is the traveller's declaration of independence. The pikes have long since been melted down, but the compound keeps their turning motion inside it, a small revolution preserved in every letter.
