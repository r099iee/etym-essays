The history of heap begins with a Proto-Indo-European root whose original meaning was probably not "pile" at all but something curved and swelling. Scholars reconstruct *keup- (with the variant *kewp-) as meaning "to bend, arch, swell," and the family of words that descends from it spans both vaulted containers and raised masses: Latin cūpa is a tub or cask built from curved staves, Lithuanian kaupas is a heap or mound, and the Germanic word that reached English is a raised, heaped-up mass. The exact relation of these branches is debated — the Dutch etymologist Michiel de Vaan treats the Latin "cask" word with caution, and some scholars suspect that the "cup/cask" words and the "heap" words grew up independently from a shared idea of curving — but the Germanic-Baltic pair, Old English hēap and Lithuanian kaupas, is securely cognate.

The journey from PIE to English runs through the regular sound changes that define the Germanic branch. By Grimm's Law the inherited *k- became the voiceless fricative *h-, so *keup- took the shape *haup-; the diphthong *eu also shifted to *au, giving Proto-Germanic *haupaz, "a heap, a crowd." In Old English the diphthong *au broke and lengthened to ēa, producing the attested hēap (long vowels and the lost final -z of the nominative are typical of the age), and by Middle English the ēa monophthongised to a long eː, spelled heep or hepe. The modern pronunciation /hiːp/ then arrived with the Great Vowel Shift, which raised the long eː to /iː/. In other words, between Beowulf and the present day the word has been continuously present in English, changing only its vowels, never its shape or its meaning of a massed accumulation.

Semantically the word has always been generous, and its development can be followed era by era.

  • Old English (8th–11th c.): hēap means both a physical pile and, strikingly, a group of people — a troop, a band, a multitude. In Beowulf the retainer band is a hēap, and the same collective sense appears throughout the prose of Alfred's age. The idea that a crowd is a "heap" of people shows how naturally the word could move from matter to humanity.
  • Middle English (13th–15th c.): the collective "troop" sense dies out; heep settles into its modern profile as the word for an untidy pile of objects, and the language begins to build idioms around it.
  • Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): the quantitative sense blossoms — "a heap of money," "heaps of time" — turning the concrete pile into a general intensifier. The biblical idiom "to heap coals of fire on one's head" (from Proverbs 25:22 and Romans 12:20) enters English during this period and remains alive.
  • 18th–19th c.: the social and moral metaphors solidify: "the bottom of the heap," "to heap scorn on someone." The word also enters the vocabulary of industry as the "scrap heap," "slag heap," and "rubbish heap."
  • 20th–21st c.: computing adopts the word twice over. The dynamic memory region is called the heap because blocks of data are dumped into it without order, in contrast to the disciplined stack; and the binary heap data structure — a near-complete binary tree used for priority queues and the heapsort algorithm (published by J. W. J. Williams in 1964) — borrows the same homely name for a rigorously ordered structure. Meanwhile the slang "old heap" for a broken-down car keeps the word firmly in everyday speech.

Socio-culturally, heap is a word of the plain people. It never moved into courtly or learned registers; it stayed in the fields, the kitchens, and the workshops of English, which is why it carries so much colloquial weight today — from the Australian "heaps good" to the programmer's "heap allocation." In Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, the context of this card, the word is at its most elemental: Lennie gathers dried leaves and twigs and "threw them in a heap on the old ash pile," a crude mound of kindling resting on ancient ash. It is the raw material of survival — mass gathered against the night, waiting for the spark. Across a thousand years the word has meant exactly this: things thrown together, in a mound, without ceremony, ready to be put to use.
