Dryads (plural of dryad) are, in Greek mythology, nymphs who live in and are bound to trees — most especially the oak — functioning as the tree's own spirit or soul, its life and voice made person. The singular dryad (Greek dryás, plural dryádes) literally means "she of the oak." The plural here names a whole class of woodland deities, and in the passage from the Narnia cycle that generated this card, "Dryads" appear in a litany of old-Narnia wonders: "Naiads and Dryads they were called." The word's tone is wistful, mythic, and defamiliarizing: where a modern child would say "tree-spirits," the text reaches for the classical name, and that name drags with it the whole lost world being evoked — an older Narnia where animals talked and "there were nice people who lived in the streams and the trees." A Dryad is not a spirit that happens to live in a tree; she is the tree, in the ancient view, its life and voice made personal. The word therefore does two things in the passage: it certifies the reality of the pre-human enchanted world the speaker yearns for, and it signals that this world is classical and unrecoverable — the Dryads, like the Fauns and Dwarfs named alongside them, belong to the fauna of a vanished Golden Age.

Principal senses (polysemy).

  • Classical mythology (primary sense): a nymph whose life is bound to a tree, especially an oak; the tree's spirit personified. In Greek religion the dryas was one of the nymphs — young female nature-deities, daughters of Zeus and lesser gods of springs, trees, and mountains — and her death was tied to the death of her tree. The most famous dryads are the hamadryads, whose lives were absolutely bound to a single tree, so that when the tree fell, the nymph died with it.
  • Folkloric and literary use (secondary sense): by extension, any tree-spirit, wood-nymph, or nature-spirit of the woods, especially in poetry and fantasy; the word became a general English term for female tree-divinities and for the spirit of the forest itself. Poets from the sixteenth century onward used dryads as emblems of woodland beauty and of nature's animate life.
  • Modern fantasy and genre usage (tertiary sense): in modern fantasy literature, games, and popular culture, dryads are a staple of magical-forest settings — nature-spirits that appear as beautiful women bound to or inhabiting trees, guardians of the woods. This modern sense has broadened the word to mean simply "tree-spirit" in any enchanted forest, and it is now as much a genre term as a classical one.
  • Scientific and technical transfers: the word appears in zoology (the "dryad" is a butterfly of the genus Minois, so named from its oak-wood habitat) and in botany (dryad's saddle, a bracket fungus; Dryas, the mountain avens genus, from a Greek dryad-name), where it keeps the oak-and-wood association in taxonomic nomenclature.
  • Figurative modern use: in ecology and environmental writing, "dryad" is sometimes used figuratively for the spirit or genius of a forest, and in aesthetics for any figure who embodies the life of trees — the word has become a standing image of nature's hidden, animate presence.

Distinctions within the nymph-world.

  • Hamadryads are the deeper sub-class: Dryads proper were often regarded as mobile forest-nymphs, while a hamadryad's life was literally bound to one tree, so that it died when the tree fell; the difference is one of attachment.
  • Naiads are the closest companions but are water-nymphs (Greek naein, "to flow"); the boundary is habitat — fresh water for the Naiad, tree for the Dryad.
  • Nymphs is the general term for all young female nature-deities (Greek nymphē, "bride, young woman"), including Oreads (mountains), Nereids and Oceanids (seas); a Dryad is a species of Nymph.
  • Fauns and Satyrs are goat-footed male woodland gods, quite different in gender and bodily form.
  • Tree-spirit / wood-elf / tree-elf are the modern, flattened translations that lose the classical gravity and the oak-specificity of Dryad.

What "Dryad" alone carries is the specifically Greek marriage of divinity with a particular tree — not mere woodland magic, but a soul rooted in the oak, which is exactly the note of old, deep, unrecoverable enchantment Lewis sounds in the passage.
