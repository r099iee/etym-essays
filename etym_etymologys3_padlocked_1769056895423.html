“Padlocked” is really two life-stories joined by a hyphen, and they must be told separately. The second half, “lock,” is ancient. Its root is the Proto-Indo-European *leug-, “to bend, twist, turn” — a root that in Greek produced “lygízō,” “to make pliant,” and the pliant withy used for binding, and in Lithuanian gave “lenkti” (“to bend”) and “lankas” (“a bow, arch”). The idea behind the Germanic word is that a door is closed by bending a bar into place: Proto-Germanic *lūkaną meant “to close, shut,” and its noun *luką meant “a bolt, fastening.” Old English carried both down as the strong verb “lūcan” (“to lock, shut”) and the noun “loc” (“a fastening, a lock”), and the noun then branched richly — “locksmith,” “lockup,” “unlock,” “interlock” — while Norse and Continental relatives preserved the same closing sense: Old Norse “lúka”/“lok,” Dutch “luiken”/“luik” (“shutter”), German “Loch” (“hole,” originally an enclosed opening), and Gothic “galūkan,” “to shut.” Through the Franks the Germanic word even crossed into Romance, where Old French “loquet,” “a latch,” later gave English “locket.”

The first half, “pad-,” is a mystery. From the very first record — Middle English “padlok,” about 1275 — the word has been “pad-” + “lock,” but no one has proved what “pad-” was. The OED calls the first element of obscure origin. The favourite hypothesis is an Old English *padd, a basket or wicker hamper: the earliest padlocks secured chests, hampers, and panniers, and Scots “pad” still means a wicker basket or pannier. A second guess sees a small travelling “pad” (a bag or pouch) in which the lock was carried; a third, now rejected as fanciful, fancied a resemblance between a padlock’s iron body and a toad’s or bear’s padded foot. Because the element is untraceable, it has no cognates anywhere — no Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, or Slavic relative — and the other European languages built their padlock words independently: German “Vorhängeschloss” (“hanging lock”), French “cadenas” (from Arabic “qadamūs”), Italian “lucchetto.” The word is therefore a purely English compound, and its very obscurity tells us something: the object is old, homely, and native, a piece of travelling iron that the English speaking world simply named for itself.

The object’s history reinforces the word’s meaning. The padlock is among the oldest lock forms — Roman padlocks survive in the archaeological record — and its defining virtue is portability: it is a lock you can carry, clap onto any chest, gate, or chain, and remove again. The medieval record shows it securing chests, hampers, and panniers on the road; the modern record shows it everywhere security needs to travel — from the “heavily padlocked” oak chests of Cair Paravel in the Narnia passage, to the padlock icon of an encrypted website, to the love locks clipped to bridges in Paris. Each usage keeps the two core ideas alive: layered, portable defence, and the audible snap of finality. In the passage, “strengthened with iron bars and heavily padlocked,” the bars stop force and the padlocks stop tampering — the portable lock adds discretion to the barrier.

Timeline:
  • pre-3rd millennium BCE — PIE *leug- “to bend, twist, turn” (for the “lock” element).
  • c. 500 BCE–500 CE — Proto-Germanic *lūkaną “to close, shut” and *luką “bolt, fastening”; Roman padlocks already in use.
  • before c. 1000 CE — Old English lūcan/loc; the native lock stock.
  • c. 1275 — Middle English padlok first recorded; “pad-” of obscure origin (basket? pouch? — unknown).
  • 14th–17th c. — the word settles into plain, practical hardware register; the padlock secures chests, gates, and shops.
  • 19th c. — “padlocked” hardens as an adjective/participle for a finished state of security.
  • 20th–21st c. — metaphorical widening: padlocked doors and lips, the HTTPS padlock icon, and love locks on bridges.
