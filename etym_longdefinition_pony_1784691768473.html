Pony is a noun denoting a small horse of any of several small breeds, conventionally standing less than about 14 hands (approximately 56 inches, or 142 centimetres) at the withers. Unlike foals or undersized individuals of large breeds, ponies form a distinct category in equine husbandry: they are hardy, sure-footed, thick-maned, and proportionately strong for their size, with a docile temperament and a taste for rough forage, and for centuries they have carried children, hauled packs over moors and mountains, and worked underground as pit ponies in the mines. Breeds such as the Shetland, Welsh, Dartmoor, Exmoor, and New Forest pony each embody the type in a local form, selected for endurance on poor pasture rather than for height or speed. The full range of senses and extensions:
  • The animal (primary sense). A small horse of a small breed, under about 14 hands; the hardy, sure-footed pack-animal and children's mount, distinct in kind (not merely in age or size) from the larger horse.
  • The small horse of a particular breed. Shetland, Welsh, Dartmoor, Exmoor, New Forest, Connemara, Highland, and other regional breeds, each a local type of the small, hardy equine.
  • Pit pony. The small pony formerly worked underground hauling coal tubs in the mines — a poignant historical sense of the word's utility in hard labour.
  • Ponytail. The hair style in which the hair is gathered and tied at the back of the head so as to hang like a horse's tail — a transferred sense from the animal to the human coiffure, current since the 20th century (and the sense that supplies many mnemonics for the word).
  • Pony up (US). To pay up, settle, produce the money — a verb phrase from the late 19th / early 20th century, probably from the idea of a small payment, or of "showing your pony" in a card game.
  • Pony Express (US, 1860). The mail service that carried letters across the American plains by relays of small, fast horses — the romantic American lease of life the word enjoyed in the 19th century.
  • A pony = £25 (UK slang, since the 19th century). In British slang a "pony" is twenty-five pounds, from the amount 25 that the animals were once wagered or sold for, in the number slang of the turf and the East End.
  • A small glass of beer or measure (US and Australian slang). A "pony" or "pony glass" is a small measure of liquor or beer — again the smallness of the animal transferred to a small serving.
  • A crib, a literal translation (US academic slang). A "pony" is a trot, a key, or a translation used surreptitiously to cheat in study — the small horse that carries you through an exam.
  • Pony car (US, 1964). The sporty, affordable small car class inaugurated by the Ford Mustang — the automotive sense, from the horse to the horsepower.
In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2 ("Roast Mutton"), the word carries a deliberately humble, workaday nuance: the company "were on ponies, and each pony was slung about with all kinds of baggages, packages, parcels, and paraphernalia." The ponies are not heroic chargers or the white steeds of romance; they are the unpretentious pack-animals of practical travel, plodding beneath a mountain of luggage through hobbit-lands. Tolkien chose ponies precisely because their smallness of stature and sturdy endurance mirror the travellers themselves — folk of modest height setting out on a great adventure with homely, disorderly baggage. The sentence quietly establishes that this is an expedition of provisions and parcels, not of banners, and that the journey is comic as well as perilous. The word should be distinguished from its near-synonyms. A horse is the large animal of any breed, standing over about 14 hands; pony names a size and a breed type, not a young animal. A foal is a young horse of any breed and any eventual height, and a colt or filly is a young male or female horse respectively; a pony may be old or young, and is simply small. A nag is a colloquial, faintly disparaging term for an old or inferior horse; a steed is high style for a grand, spirited mount; a palfrey (archaic) is a saddle-horse; and a hack is a horse for ordinary riding, especially one let out for hire. Pony alone is the affectionate, humble, workaday word for the small, serviceable equine.
