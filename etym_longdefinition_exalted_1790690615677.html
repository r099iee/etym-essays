Exalted is an adjective meaning raised to a high, noble, or lofty position — literally elevated, figuratively eminent in rank, character, mood, or style. “On a less exalted level” means on a humbler, more ordinary or practical plane, as opposed to a lofty, ideal, or mystical one.

Principal senses today:

  • High in rank, office, or dignity (core formal sense): an exalted position, office, title, or personage. Kings, judges, Nobel laureates occupy exalted stations. Implies honour earned or conferred, public recognition, and distance above the everyday. “She rose to the exalted post of Secretary-General.” Formal and respectful; opposite is lowly, humble, menial.
  • Noble or lofty in character, ideals, or aims: exalted principles, motives, thoughts. “An exalted view of human nature.” Praises moral elevation: selfless, idealistic, untainted by pettiness. Can shade into naïve if the ideals seem unreachable, but the default is admiration.
  • Elevated or sublime in style, language, or art: exalted prose, poetry, rhetoric, music. Diction that is lofty, grand, or solemn — Miltonic rather than conversational. In criticism often double-edged: genuinely sublime versus pompous or overblown, depending on success. “The speech lapsed into exalted bombast.”
  • Elated or euphoric in mood (emotional): an exalted mood, state, feeling — uplifted, rapturous, transported with joy or religious ecstasy. Psychiatry uses it for pathologically elevated mood in mania (“exalted affect”). Everyday use is positive (“we felt exalted by the mountains”) unless irony intrudes.
  • Inflated in self-estimation (ironic): an exalted opinion of oneself, exalted self-image. Here loftiness is presumptuous. “He has an exalted idea of his own importance.” The criticism borrows the height image to accuse someone of climbing too high.

Usage distinctions:

  • Exalted vs. elevated: near-synonyms, but elevated is more neutral-technical (elevated platform, elevated levels, elevated diction as stylistic term), while exalted is warmer, grander, and more personal — it praises or teases. One speaks of elevated train tracks but exalted ideals.
  • Exalted vs. lofty: lofty covers both physical height and haughtiness (“lofty tower,” “lofty disdain”); exalted stresses having been raised (by promotion, praise, or emotion) and retains a ceremonial glow. Lofty can be cold; exalted is rarely cold.
  • Exalted vs. sublime / noble: sublime is aesthetic-philosophical (awe mixed with terror); noble is ethical-hereditary; exalted combines rank + rapture + rhetoric, moving freely among office, feeling, and style — which is why it fits the source sentence’s ladder of “levels.”
  • Register and collocation: formal-literary, seldom colloquial. Typical partners: position, rank, office, status, title, ideals, principles, vision, thoughts, language, prose, style, mood, state, opinion. In religion, the Exalted capitalised is a divine title. Lowering the level (“less exalted”) is itself a gentle bathos, stepping from cosmology to common sense.

In short, exalted marks the top of a vertical scale of worth. Whether the height is a throne, a thought, a sentence, or a feeling, to call it exalted is to say it has been lifted — and to speak, as the source does, of a “less exalted level” is to acknowledge the ladder while choosing, for the moment, a lower rung.
