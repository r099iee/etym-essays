- 1908: coined by German scholars Sieg and Siegling as a label for newly deciphered manuscripts from the Tarim Basin; purely a philological term.
- Early 20th century: two languages, A and B, are distinguished, and “Tocharian” is debated as a misnomer because the manuscripts were not found in ancient Tokharistan.
- Mid-20th century: the branch becomes central to Indo-European studies because of its archaic features and its centum-like phonology in a satem area.
- Late 20th century: “Tocharian” extends from the languages to the people and culture of the Silk Road oases, and appears in debates about the Tarim mummies.
- 21st century: the term is standard in linguistics and archaeology, while its ethnic accuracy remains contested; the variant “Tokharian” fades.
