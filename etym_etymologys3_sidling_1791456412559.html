Sidling is a word made by mistake — or rather by a productive misreading. Its parent, sidle, was created in the late seventeenth century by speakers who heard the old adverb sideling and assumed it was the present participle of a verb. That kind of back-formation is one of the quiet engines of English word growth, and the history of sidling shows how a directional adverb became a verb of furtive social movement.

Side: the long flank. Old English sīde “side, flank” descends from Proto-Germanic *sīdǫ, a noun built on the adjective *sīdaz “long, extended.” The original sense was probably “the long part” of the body — the flank that runs from armpit to hip — and from there the word extended to the edge or surface of anything, to regions and districts, and to parties in a dispute. The Proto-Indo-European root is usually given as *seh₁- “long, lasting,” though some scholars prefer *sēy- “to send, throw, drop”; the connexion is old but not entirely secure.

The -ling suffix. Middle English formed adverbs with -ling or -lings, as in darkling “in the dark,” headlong “head first,” sidelong, and sideling. The suffix originally had a sense of “in the manner or direction of,” and it produced a whole family of vivid directional words. Sideling therefore meant “in a sidelong direction, sideways, obliquely,” and it is recorded in Middle English as sidlyng and sydelyng from the fourteenth century.

From adverb to verb. By the sixteenth century the form sidelong had arisen, probably under the influence of the unrelated long. English speakers now had both sideling and sidelong, and the spelling sidling looked exactly like a present participle. In the 1690s someone made the obvious inference and coined the verb sidle. The new verb was instantly useful: it named the action that the adverb had always described, and it allowed tenses, infinitives, and figurative extensions. This is a classic back-formation, like burgle from burglar or edit from editor.

Semantic colouring. The verb did not stay neutral. From its earliest uses sidle carried a suggestion of caution or stealth: to move sideways is to move without presenting oneself frontally, and that physical obliquity readily became social obliquity. By the eighteenth century the word was established in fiction for shy, guilty, or embarrassed approach, and the phrase sidle up to had begun its career as a description of ingratiating oneself.

Literary and social contexts. The word is a novelist’s tool. It appears in scenes of courtship, where a suitor sidles towards the beloved; in scenes of guilt, where a character sidles out of a room; and in scenes of social manoeuvre, where an ambitious figure sidles up to power. P. G. Wodehouse uses it for comic entrances — a character “came sidling out from behind a tree” — and the participle does the work of a stage direction, telling us that the arrival is sheepish, tentative, and slightly ridiculous.

Phonetic and morphological summary.

  • Phonetic: Old English sīde [ˈsiːde] → Middle English side → sidlyng, with the suffix weakened to [lɪŋ] and later [lɪŋ].
  • Morphological: side + -ling → adverb sideling → reanalysed as a participle → back-formed verb sidle → participle sidling.
  • Semantic: “long flank” → “side” → “sideways” → “obliquely, furtively” → “ingratiating approach.”
  • Register: standard but slightly literary; especially at home in fiction, description, and social commentary.

Modern extensions. Contemporary English uses sidling for physical movement, for social approach, and for anything that enters gradually and unnoticed. A policy can sidle into law; a doubt can sidle into the mind; a habit can sidle into a life. The metaphor remains fresh because the underlying image — the body turning sideways to slip past — is so concrete.

Why it endures. Sidling endures because it is both precise and suggestive. It names a particular physical action and, in the same breath, a whole range of social emotions: shyness, guilt, discretion, ambition. Very few words manage to be so specific about the body and so eloquent about the mind.
