Relented, the past tense and participle of relent, means softened, eased, or gave way: a hard will grew merciful, a cruel pressure slackened, a stern course yielded to pity or weariness.

In the source sentence the travellers feel the Forest itself soften: it seemed to them that the Forest had relented, and was going to let them pass unhindered. The great wood is personified as a jailer reconsidering, its malice melting into indifference or mercy. Whether the relief is real or a trap of the trees, the word carries the exhale of the hunted: the pressure lifts, the heart beats better, the feet quicken.

Core meaning in detail: To relent is to grow less severe after severity, whether in feeling or in force. A pursuer relents and calls off the hounds; the weather relents and the wind drops; a creditor relents and forgives the debt; a fever relents and the forehead cools. The verb always looks backward at hardness: nothing relents that was not first unyielding, and the softening is felt as an event, a turning, never a mere state.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Grew merciful, softened in feeling toward an offender or sufferer (primary personal sense, from the 16th c.): the tyrant relented and spared the city; she relented and forgave him. The bridge from melting is inward: as ice softens to water, so the hard heart softens to pity.
  • Became less severe, of weather, pain, pressure, or hostile forces (primary impersonal sense, as in the source): the storm relented, the Forest relented, the pace relented. The bridge carries the softening outward: whatever pressed hard now presses gently, and sufferers breathe again.
  • Yielded after resistance, gave way in a contest of wills: the committee relented and granted the appeal. The bridge is dialectical: hardness meets endurance until one side melts, and melting is surrender.
  • Melted or dissolved, physically (original Middle English sense, now archaic): snow relents into slush, salt relents in water. This is the root sense from which all others grew, and though modern ears hear only mercy in it, the thaw is still audible underneath.
  • Repented of a judgement, of God or fate (biblical and literary): as in the Lord relented from the disaster, the old translations' phrase for divine reconsideration. The bridge is theological: even heaven's sentence can melt before pity or prayer.

Register and connotation: relented is graver than eased and warmer than subsided: it implies a will behind the severity, even when the subject is weather or woodland, and therefore a kind of moral drama in the softening. To say the Forest relented is to grant the trees intention; to say a creditor relented is to grant him a heart. The word is never trivial: small comforts ease, but only great pressures relent. Its music is part of its meaning, the slow lengthening of re-lent-ed itself enacting the slackening it names.
