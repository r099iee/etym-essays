- 5th–8th centuries (Frankish Europe): *halsberg — the throat-guard of the warrior's armour; the root sense is about protecting the neck, and the word is war-band Germanic, part of the equipment of the comitatus.
- 11th–12th centuries (Old French): hauberc — in the Chanson de Roland the hauberk is already the full knee-length coat of mail put on over the head; perception shifts from "neck-guard" to "body armour", the emblem of the mounted knight.
- 13th–15th centuries (England): hauberk/haubergeon in records and romances; the age of the great helm — the word is inventory-crisp but also heroic, worn by everybody from hired crossbowmen to Arthurian heroes.
- 16th–19th centuries: gunpowder displaces mail; hauberk becomes a museum word — antiquarian and romantic (Scott and the medieval revival), a relic of vanished chivalry.
- 20th–21st centuries: fantasy literature, games and film lift it back into living use — Tolkien's "hauberks of steel mail", Dungeons & Dragons armour tables, film armourers — making HAUBERK the standard heroic word for a mail coat.
