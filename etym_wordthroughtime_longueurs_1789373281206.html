- [12th–16th c. Old/Middle French] - Originally an objective physical measurement (*longor* / *longueur*) denoting spatial length, linear extension, or temporal duration - [connotation: neutral, factual measurement of distance or time]
- [17th c. Classical French] - Adopted into French theatrical and literary criticism during the reign of Louis XIV (Boileau, Corneille, Molière) to censure scenes that breached the dramatic unities by dragging out the action - [connotation: stylistic defect, dramatic stalling, violation of audience engagement and decorum]
- [Late 18th–19th c.] - Naturalized into English high criticism as an untranslated Gallicism, predominantly in the plural (*longueurs*), to review sprawling Victorian multi-volume novels, grand operas, and travel accounts - [connotation: urbane, cosmopolitan critical refinement; polite acknowledgment that even monumental masterpieces contain tedious stretches]
- [20th c.–Present] - Firmly established in Anglo-American literary, theatrical, and film criticism; frequently interrogated in modernist and avant-garde aesthetic debates (e.g. regarding Marcel Proust, James Joyce, Michelangelo Antonioni, or Chantal Akerman), where conventional narrative longueurs are re-evaluated as intentional phenomenology of lived duration - [connotation: critical critique of pacing versus avant-garde valorization of temporal contemplation]
