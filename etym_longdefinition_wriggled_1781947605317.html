In the breathless chase scene of Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), the preterite verb wriggled carries the whole escape: "MR. McGREGOR came up with a sieve, which he intended to pop upon the top of Peter; but Peter wriggled out just in time, leaving his jacket behind him." The word is a frequentative — its -le suffix encodes quick, repeated, twisting motions — and that grammatical shape is precisely the drama: where Mr. McGregor's gesture is a single decisive "pop," Peter's response is a rapid, continuous squirming that slips the trap by a hair. "Just in time" frames the action as a narrow margin, and the fact that the jacket is left behind shows the violence of the contortion: Peter does not remove his garment; he writhes out of it, abandoning it as a decoy. Potter's tone is comic suspense — the reader feels the split-second scramble — but the word also softens Peter's fright into something wriggly and childlike rather than truly panicked. A chased child squirms; a captured adult thrashes. The verb keeps the scene mischievous and warm, true to a tale meant to be read aloud to small children who know exactly what it feels like to wriggle out of a jumper or a tight lap.

Core senses and polysemy:

  • Intransitive physical sense — to twist and turn the body with quick, small, writhing movements, usually with the aim of freeing oneself or passing through a narrow gap: the worm wriggled in the damp soil, she wriggled into the tight dress, the child wriggled out of his mother's arms. This is the sense in the rabbit scene.
  • Transitive movement — to cause something to move by twisting, or to make one's way by squirming: to wriggle one's toes, to wriggle through a gap, to wriggle free.
  • Figurative sense — to extricate oneself from a difficulty, obligation, or blame by twisting words or manoeuvring: to wriggle out of a promise, to wriggle out of an embarrassing position. Here the physical squirming is transferred to verbal or social evasion.
  • Of animals — worms, snakes, eels, insects, and fish are said to wriggle; the word is the archetypal verb of the worm, whose whole locomotion is a wriggle.
  • Noun — a wriggle is one such twisting movement (a wriggle of discomfort), and figuratively a small manoeuvre or loophole (wriggle-room, chiefly British: a margin for manoeuvre).
  • Derived forms — wriggly (inclined to wriggle), wriggler (one who wriggles, and dialectally a worm or eel), wriggle-work (in embroidery, interlacing patterns), and the nursery adjective in a wriggly toddler.
  • Register nuance — wriggled is homely, light, and slightly comic; it implies quickness and elasticity rather than strength, which is why it suits a small animal, a child, or a slippery excuse better than the heavier thrashed or writhed.

Near-synonyms and their boundaries:

  • Writhe: similar in twisting the body; differs because writhing is slower, larger, and often implies pain, anguish, or a violent struggle, whereas wriggling is quick, small, and often playful or purposeful.
  • Squirm: similar in twisting movements; differs because squirming adds discomfort, embarrassment, or unease — one squirms when embarrassed, but one wriggles to escape or through a gap.
  • Wiggle: similar in small quick side-to-side motions; differs because wiggling is lighter and more casual, often of a single part (hips, fingers, toes), while wriggling is a whole-body, effortful, goal-directed contortion.
  • Twist: similar in turning movement; differs because twist is the general verb for any rotation or wrenching, lacking the repeated, squirming, escape-seeking quality of wriggle.
  • Worm (one's way): similar in making progress by slow sideways effort; differs because worming is slow, stealthy, and ingratiating, whereas wriggling is quick and squirming.
  • Slither: similar in smooth writhing motion; differs because slithering is the gliding motion of snakes over a surface, smooth and continuous, not the rapid struggling of a small body trying to free itself.
  • Snake (one's way): similar in twisting progress; differs because to snake is to follow a winding course over distance, not to squirm in place.
  • Wangle (out of): similar in the figurative sense of escaping an obligation; differs because wangling implies crafty persuasion or scheming, while wriggling out implies squirming, evasion, or slippage rather than clever talk.

The word thus runs from the worm in the soil to the child slipping out of a lap to the politician slipping out of a commitment — a single image of quick, repeated, twisting escape that stays vivid and comic at every level.
