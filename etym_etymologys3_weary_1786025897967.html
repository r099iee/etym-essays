WEARY is a native English word of extraordinary longevity: its line of descent runs unbroken from the earliest Old English poetry to the present day, and across that thousand-year span its meaning has migrated steadily inward, from the tired body to the tired mind to the tired soul. It is also, etymologically, a word of honest uncertainty at its very root.

1. The Disputed Indo-European Root

The recorded history of the word begins in Germanic. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic adjective *wōrigaz, "tired, worn out, exhausted," is the certain ancestor of the Old English wērig, and cognate forms appear across the Germanic world — Old Saxon wōrig and Old High German wuorag ("burdened, exhausted") among them. But the ancestry of *wōrigaz itself is debated. The traditional explanation links it to an Indo-European root meaning "to tread down, wear out, injure," reconstructed variously as *wer- or *wōr-, so that weariness is conceived as the result of being worn down underfoot — the sense of someone exhausted by long walking or hard marching. This is a plausible and attractive etymology, but it is not secure: Kroonen and other modern comparativists treat the West Germanic form as of unclear Indo-European origin, and no attested cognate outside Germanic settles the question. The honest account is that weary is certainly Germanic but only probably Indo-European in its ultimate root.

2. Old English: The Wērig Body (8th-11th c.)

In Old English, wērig is attested from the earliest period, in the heroic and elegiac poetry that forms the bedrock of the language. In Beowulf, the poet describes warriors exhausted after battle; in The Wanderer and The Seafarer, the exile and the sailor are wērig — worn down by wandering, cold, and loss. The word was productive in compounds: mōd-wērig, "weary in spirit"; hōd-wērig, "weary of heart"; flōd-wērig, weary from the sea. These compounds reveal something important: from the very beginning wērig described both the tired body and the heavy heart. The Anglo-Saxon world — with its long winter voyages, its foot-travel, its border warfare, and its melancholy Christian poetics of exile and mortality — gave the word a sombre, spiritual resonance that it has never entirely lost.

3. Middle English: The Wery Mind and Soul (12th-15th c.)

In Middle English, spelled wery or weri, the word broadened decisively from the physical to the psychological and moral. Religious lyrics and devotional prose described the soul wery of sin and the flesh wearying of prayer; courtly literature described the lover sick with longing, weary with desire unsatisfied. The phrase "weary of" began its long career: one could be weary of a place, of a person, of life itself. Chaucer uses the word throughout his work, for tired travellers and for the weary voices of grief. By the end of the Middle English period the word covered the full modern range — bodily fatigue, mental boredom, spiritual desolation, and a prickling impatience with what will not end.

4. Early Modern English: The Verb and the Judgement (16th-17th c.)

The Early Modern period added the transitive verb — "to weary" someone, to exhaust them — attested from around 1600, together with the passive construction and the participle wearying. Shakespeare exploits the whole range: characters are weary in body, weary of life, and wearisome to others; the verb "to weary" enemies is a standard tactic in his histories. The Reformation and the flowering of English prose also fixed "weary of" as an idiom of moral and political surfeit — one grows weary of controversy, of superstition, of a tyrant. In this period the word's centre of gravity moved from describing a state to expressing a judgement: "I am weary of this" is not a report of fatigue but a verdict.

5. Modern English: The World-Weary Register (18th-21st c.)

In modern English the psychological sense has overtaken the physical. The word remains common for tired bodies, but its distinctive power is now existential and political: world-weary, battle-weary, war-weary, weary of promises, a weary shrug. The rise of this register is connected to modernity's own sense of exhaustion — the literature of disillusionment, the rhetoric of publics tired of endless crises, the cliché of the weary idealist.

  • Sound change: Old English [ˈweː.rij] became Middle English [ˈweː.ri]; the Middle English long vowel was later raised and smoothed during the Great Vowel Shift, and the final weak syllable lost its vowel, giving modern [ˈwɪə.ri]. The spelling weary has been stable since Middle English.
  • Derivative family: wearily, weariness, wearisome grow directly out of the Old English root, and the word formed compounds from the earliest period (mōd-wērig) to the present (world-weary).
  • Key literary attestations: Beowulf, The Wanderer, The Seafarer, Chaucer, and Shakespeare all deploy the word at the emotional centre of their most sombre passages.
  • Summary: Over a thousand years, the word has done what very few words do — held its ground as the plainest, strongest English word for the exhaustion that no sleep cures.
