TITHE literally means “a tenth part,” and the word carries a double historical register: at once a pure, precise fraction and a sacred, fiscal institution. In the couplet from the verse that introduces Dai-yu in The Story of the Stone, the word is pressed into service as a precise, quasi-legal unit of measurement: “She had more chambers in her heart than the martyred Bi Gan; And suffered a tithe more pain in it than the beautiful Xi Shi.” Bi Gan was the Shang-dynasty minister whose heart, so the legend ran, was cut out at the tyrant Zhou’s command, and Xi Shi was the most beautiful woman of the ancient world, famed for clutching her heart in pain—so that both allusions already scale emotion to extremes. Onto this scale the couplet adds the tithe: Dai-yu suffers a full tenth more pain than even Xi Shi. The word imports into the tally of grief a register that is at once churchly and fiscal—the tithe is the tenth part of income paid to God—so that sorrow is quantified, apportioned, “taxed” upon a legendary beauty’s suffering. Its tone is formal, archaic, and bleakly witty: the narrator measures heartache as a collector measures dues, and the precise, almost mocking fraction only heightens the tragedy, because it suggests that grief is countable at all. Polysemy and the full range of senses:
  • The literal fraction—any tenth part of anything; the neutral, numerical sense preserved in the couplet, where it is pressed into precise quasi-legal service.
  • The religious levy—the tenth of one’s produce or income paid to the church or clergy; historically a legal obligation enforced by canon and parish law, later a voluntary devotional practice.
  • The Old Testament institution—the tithe of the Law of Moses, a tenth of the harvest, flock, or herd given to the Levites, the poor, and the sanctuary (Genesis, Leviticus, Numbers, Malachi).
  • The medieval fiscal tax—from Domesday Book onward, “tithes” were assessed on crops, livestock, and sometimes industrial produce, enforced by the church courts and stored in the parish tithe barn.
  • The verb sense—to tithe: to give or pay a tenth; also, historically, to exact a tenth from someone.
  • The figurative “small exact portion”—in modern English, “a tithe of” usually means a small but precisely measured part (“not a tithe of the former glory”); the fraction still reads as exact, though often ironically diminished.
The semantic boundaries against near relations sharpen the word. Tenth is the neutral, purely numerical word for the ordinal fraction; a tithe is a tenth with institutional weight. Share and portion are allotments of a whole with no fixed size (a generous share); a tithe is fixed at exactly one tenth. Fraction is the modern mathematical term for any part of a whole, wholly neutral. Mite is a tiny but sacrificial amount (the widow’s mite); modicum is a small but sufficient quantity; iota, from the Greek letter, is the least possible amount—all differ in size and in carrying no fixed proportion. Tax, levy, and due are general exactions of any size by any authority; a tithe is specifically the tenth-part ecclesiastical levy. Register, tone, and historical colour: tithe is archaic, formal, and churchly, but it is also a survivor of the ordinary decimal system—the same root as ten, tenth, and dime. Its fiscal gravity makes it an ideal literary instrument for measuring grief as a collector measures dues, which is exactly the bleakly witty use to which the couplet puts it. In contemporary usage it survives chiefly in voluntary religious practice (“giving a tithe of one’s income”) and in literary metaphor for an exact, exalted fraction of something; its tone is that of an old, heavy, sacred coin.
