overall (noun): a loose protective garment worn over ordinary clothes to shield them from dirt, paint, grease, or dust — in British English characteristically a one-piece working suit covering the trunk and limbs and fastened down the front (worn by factory hands, mechanics, house-painters, and caretakers), and in American English far more often a bib-and-brace garment, a pair of heavy trousers with a protective front bib held up by shoulder straps, called overalls in the plural. In the sentence from Mervyn Peake's Titus Groan the word works with conspicuous exactness. Rottcodd, the curator of the Hall of the Bright Carvings, “envelopes himself” in his overall as though in a second skin: the garment is not mere clothing but a ritual integument, a shapeless grey membrane that seals the keeper inside his office and keeps the dust of the hall from the world outside the wing. Peake chooses overall rather than uniform or robe because the word carries a threefold force: (1) the physical layering of one fabric over another, (2) the total, “over-all” absorption of the wearer's identity into his function, and (3) the humility of a servant's livery, so unremarkable that it seems to merge with the dust it polishes away. The tone is at once droll and ceremonial: the feather duster raised like a weapon, the overall as the protective armour of a man whose entire kingdom is a hall of carvings.

The word is far more than a garment, and any complete account of it must confront its extraordinary polysemy, because the noun is historically the youngest and narrowest of its senses. Consider the full modern range of meanings that cluster under the single spelling:
  • Adjective (the dominant modern use): “total, including everything; general, not particularised” — overall length, overall impression, overall winner, overall responsibility. Here the compounding of over and all yields the idea of a single comprehensive sweep that takes in every part of a whole.
  • Adverb (the earliest English sense, 14th century): “over the whole extent; everywhere; altogether, in total” — overall, the project was a success. The adverb keeps the original spatial force of the Middle English phrase overal, “over the whole extent, everywhere”.
  • Noun (British, 19th–20th century): the one-piece protective working suit covering the trunk and limbs — the shop-floor livery of the labourer, the servant, the housewife's protective smock; also any loose outer garment worn over one's clothes for protection.
  • Noun (American, from 1759): heavy bib-and-brace trousers, overalls, worn by farmers, railwaymen, and construction workers; in the plural the term can also mean the full one-piece suit, the American coveralls.
  • Technical and figurative extensions: in statistics and management “overall” performs the wholesale verdict (“overall performance”, “overall rating”); in multi-stage sport an “overall winner” is the general-classification champion across all stages; in engineering an “overall dimension” is the extreme measurement that encloses the whole object — the word of total assessment in every discipline that needs to hold a whole in a single glance.

The historical bridge that connects these senses is elegant and complete. The Old English phrase ofer eall, “over all, across the whole extent”, described a net cast over a field or an eye passing over a page; by the 14th century the phrase had fused into a single adverb meaning “everywhere, altogether”, then it became an adjective meaning “total” (14th–16th centuries); and only in the 18th century did it jump into the wardrobe — the garment that is worn over all the others, protecting the whole person at once. The noun is thus a late materialisation of an abstract word of totality: the same “over-all” sweep that lets a manager measure a whole business lets a worker cover his whole body. In the Peake passage the two registers collide to perfect effect — the humble garment carries within its own name the totalising abstraction of “over-all” governance, so Rottcodd, dressed in his overall, is literally clothed in completeness, the one man in whom the entire Hall of the Bright Carvings is summed up.
