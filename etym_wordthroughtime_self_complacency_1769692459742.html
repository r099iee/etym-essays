- Early sense (17th-18th c.) - A peaceful, legitimate satisfaction with one's own conduct; a serene and morally neutral self-contentment, closer to ease of conscience than to vanity.
- 19th-century moral register - Becomes a target of satire and moral censure: self-complacency denotes a smug, uncritical self-satisfaction that blinds one to one's own defects, as in Austen's portrait of Mary Bennet.
- Contemporary sense - Retains a quietly pejorative, reproachful tone; used for the inert, self-congratulating complacency that resists self-improvement, less showy and outward than smugness or vanity.
