- Spine and back (13th c.): 'ridge' (ME rigge) first means the human or animal back — the body's raised midline, carried over from OE hrycg
- Shape-metaphor to landscape and roof (13th–14th c.): the back becomes the crest of a hill and the ridge of a roof — the roof is the house's back
- The ploughman's ridge (14th–16th c.): ridge-and-furrow field systems make the word a daily agricultural term for raised strips of soil
- The surveyor's ridge (16th–18th c.): exploration and map-making fix the topographic sense; the living-body sense begins to fade from ordinary speech
- The scientist's ridge (19th–20th c.): mid-ocean ridges, alveolar ridges, ridges of high pressure, ridged coins and corduroy — the word multiplies across geology, anatomy, meteorology and industry
- Today: purely descriptive and technical — a word that once named a living spine now names lines of land, metal and cloth; 'back' has entirely taken over the body sense
