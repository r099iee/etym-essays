Necromancer is a noun naming a practitioner of necromancy: magic or divination that claims to conjure, consult, or command the spirits of the dead. Historically the word is technical — divination by the dead, one specific branch of the ancient art of divination — but in popular and modern fantasy use it has broadened into a general name for a dark sorcerer whose power is bound up with death, corpses, shadows, and forbidden lore. The word is a Greek compound: nekro- "corpse, dead body" plus -mancer "diviner, seer," so at its root a necromancer is literally a "dead-diviner," one who gains knowledge by consulting the dead. In Gandalf's grim report from The Hobbit — "I found him a prisoner in the dungeons of the Necromancer" — the title is a proper dread-name for the hidden power at Dol Guldur, later revealed in the wider legendarium as Sauron in disguise. It is not a village fortune-teller's label but the name of an enemy, capitalized in many editions to mark a known, feared power. The dwarves' shudder answers the word before any further gloss: the narrator does not need to explain what a necromancer does, because the word itself, with its Greek corpse and its medieval "black art" associations, carries the horror. Tone: theological-political dread inside adventure prose; a title that names the worst kind of wizard. Core senses.
  • Divination by the dead (historical primary): one who practises the art of calling up and questioning the spirits of the dead to learn hidden or future things — the sense of Greek nekromanteia and of medieval learned usage.
  • Conjurer of spirits and shades (expanded historical): one who summons, raises, or commands the dead — the practitioner of the rite rather than merely the inquirer, shading into magic proper.
  • Dark sorcerer, evil wizard (popular and modern): any sinister magician whose power is associated with death and the undead — the dominant sense in modern fantasy, gaming, and horror.
  • Agent noun formation: the -er/-mancer shape, now productive in playful coinages (e.g. "chronomancer," "necromancer" in games), meaning "one who practises X-magic."
Polysemy: secondary and specialized senses.
  • Occult and spiritualist usage: in modern occult literature, necromancy is divination through mediums, séances, and contact with the deceased; the necromancer is the medium-magician of that tradition.
  • Fantasy worldbuilding and gaming: in role-playing games, fiction, and film, the necromancer is a class or archetype — one who raises armies of the undead, commands zombies and skeletons, and draws power from death and decay.
  • Figurative and jocular: "necromancer" is applied metaphorically to anyone who revives something dead — a scholar who resurrects a forgotten language, a musician who revives a dead genre — the "raising the dead" image transferred to cultural revival.
  • Historical distortion — "nigromancy": medieval English and French spellings (nigromancie) were reshaped by folk etymology after Latin niger "black," so that the word was understood as "black magic," a reinterpretation that permanently darkened its popular sense.
Historical and semantic bridges. The bridge from "diviner of the dead" to "evil wizard" is medieval and moral. Greek nekromanteia named a specific, though already disturbing, technique of divination — consulting the dead at the nekyia, the summoning of shades. Latin and Medieval Latin transmit the word; but in Old French the shape nigromancie invites the association with niger "black," and the art is classed with forbidden "black magic," condemned by the Church as commerce with demons. The modern word therefore arrives in English with two layers already fused: the learned divination sense and the popular black-art sense. Modern fantasy completes the drift by making the necromancer not an inquirer but a commander of the dead — a dark wizard who raises and deploys the undead. The agent noun necromancer, formed on the ordinary English -er pattern (with the Greek/Latin -mancer element), names the practitioner at every stage. Differentiation. A wizard or sorcerer is a general magic-user; a warlock is often the popular word for a male witch; a magician may be a stage performer or a scholar of magic; a diviner foretells the future without necessarily using the dead; a medium communicates with spirits in spiritualist practice; a shaman is a tribal spirit-intermediary; a necromancer specifically ties power to the dead or to death-magic. In Tolkien's early Hobbit tone the name is ominous and half-explained — enough to freeze the company. The card preserves the singular title form of the text: the enemy's name as function.
