The phrasal verb cut along yokes two words of wildly different pedigree: one a medieval mystery with no known parents, the other an ancient line stretching back to the Stone Age steppe. Taking them in order honours both. Cut first appears in Middle English around the 1200s as cutten, kitten, and kuttin, already meaning to sever with a sharp edge, and etymologists have been puzzled ever since. No solid Germanic cognate set explains it, no Romance donor offers itself convincingly, and the Celtic candidates proposed from time to time refuse to line up in sound. The honest handbook verdict, repeated from Skeat to the OED, is that the deeper origin is unknown: a blunt, useful monosyllable that walked out of some workshop or kitchen and into the language without leaving a birth certificate. Its very obscurity fits its meaning, a quick street word rather than a pedigreed one.

Along, by contrast, is aristocracy of the oldest kind. Old English andlang joined and-, against or opposite, to lang, long, picturing something extended at full length beside something else: the road running alongside the river, the rank drawn out along the wall. Behind lang stands PIE *dlonghos, long in space and time, a root so stable that its descendants — Latin longus, Greek dolichos, Sanskrit dirgha, English long — still recognise one another after five thousand years. Middle English wore andlang down to along, keeping both the spatial sense, down the length of the lane, and the temporal one, all along, through the whole length of the time.

The marriage of blade and line began when cut learned to travel. Tudor sailors wrote of ships cutting the sea, surveyors of roads cutting the moor, walkers of cutting across the meadow, each use turning the knife's path into a journey's path: to move decisively is to slice the distance. From cutting across, taking the diagonal shortcut, it was a short step to cutting along, taking the route at speed, with the verb donating suddenness and the particle donating direction. By the 1700s the imperative cut along was set in the mouths of authority — officers to recruits, masters to errand-boys, elder brothers to juniors — meaning be off this instant and keep moving.

Victorian and Edwardian children's literature gave the phrase its happiest home. Ballantyne's sailors, Stevenson's lads, and a whole lineage of scouts and schoolchildren cut along cliffs, towpaths, and lanes on honourable urgent errands, and the Row that Sam cuts along belongs to this lineage: a village lane, an evening errand, a rendezvous beyond the meadows. The slightly archaic flavour it carries today is the perfume of those books, which is exactly why Tolkien, who grew up on them, puts it in Frodo's mouth. Frodo speaks as the head boy of the conspiracy, brisk, kind, and in command.

Landmarks of the journey:

  • Unknown blade: Middle English cutten appears c. 1200 with no certain ancestors, the orphan at the heart of the phrase.
  • Ancient line: PIE *dlonghos stretches through Latin, Greek, and Sanskrit into Old English andlang.
  • Tudor travel: ships and roads learn to cut seas and moors, motion becoming an edge.
  • Shortcut logic: cut across fathers cut along, suddenness wedded to direction.
  • Barrack imperative: 18th-century authority fixes cut along as the brisk order to be off.
  • Adventure adoption: Victorian story-books make it the errand-verb of plucky youths.
  • Shire echo: Frodo's order to Sam carries the school-story snap into Middle-earth.

Two histories, one hurry: an orphaned knife-word and a five-millennium line combine to send Sam flying down the Row, which is precisely what phrasal verbs do, welding unlike pasts into a single present dash.
