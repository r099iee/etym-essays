- Old English — the enclosure itself: hecg names the planted thorn barrier fencing the homestead and fields — living wood as law and wall.
- Medieval — parish boundaries: hedges mark who owns what across open-field England; grubbing one up is theft, and hedgerow trees become landmarks in charters.
- Enclosure era — hedges as revolution: between 1600 and 1850 millions of new quickset hedges carve commons into private farms, and the hedge becomes the visible signature of English farming.
- 16th–17th c. — the verb escapes: to hedge now means to dodge, equivocate, and to secure a wager — sheltering behind words and counter-bets as behind thorns.
- 20th c. — finance adopts it: hedge funds and hedging strategies carry the farmer’s barrier into the stock market, while gardeners clip yew into architecture.
- Today — ecology redeems it: ancient hedges are protected as wildlife corridors and heritage, even as hedging in speech and finance means cautious self-protection in an uncertain world.
