Arrest functions both as a transitive/intransitive verb and as a noun, signifying the act of stopping, holding back, or checking a physical motion, process, or development, as well as the formal seizure and custody of an individual under legal authority. Its semantic breadth extends from the mechanics of law enforcement to clinical medicine, mechanical engineering, and aesthetic perception.

The primary, secondary, and specialized senses of arrest include:

  • Physical Cessation and Interruption: To bring an active motion, progression, or spreading force to an immediate or gradual halt. In physical descriptions and literary prose (such as halting the course of celestial bodies or checking a running torrent), to arrest an object is to neutralize its momentum completely.
  • Legal Seizure and Custody: To seize, hold, and formally detain a person by virtue of legal warrant or lawful authority, typically on suspicion of having committed a criminal offense. The individual arrested is deprived of personal freedom to answer before a magistrate or tribunal.
  • Perceptual and Cognitive Capture: To seize, attract, and command the attention, gaze, or imagination of an observer. Something described as arresting (such as a striking painting, an extraordinary face, or a sudden discordant sound) forcefully interrupts the observer’s train of thought, compelling immediate visual or mental focus.
  • Clinical and Biological Cessation: In medical pathology, the sudden or complete stoppage of a physiological function or bodily organ. Cardiac arrest denotes the cessation of effective mechanical pumping by the heart; respiratory arrest denotes the total failure of breathing; arrested development describes the permanent or temporary halt in the physical, cognitive, or emotional maturation of an organism.
  • Mechanical and Engineering Systems: To physically constrain or absorb kinetic energy in moving machinery. An arrestor gear or arresting wire on the flight deck of an aircraft carrier rapidly dissipates the momentum of landing aircraft; a flame arrester prevents the transmission of ignited gas through a piping system.

Polysemic Evolution and Historical Semantic Bridges: The development of arrest represents a classic example of causative transitivization. The base Proto-Indo-European root *steh₂- ("to stand") yielded Latin stāre ("to stand") and its compound restāre ("to remain behind, hold back"). In Late Latin, prefixing the directional preposition ad- created *arrestāre, converting the static state of "standing firm" into the active transitive exertion of "forcing another to stand firm." When the term entered Old French (arester) and subsequently Anglo-Norman law, it was adopted by the royal courts to denote an officer of the crown laying hands upon a subject to enforce obedience. From this physical act of seizing, the word branched metaphorically into medicine (the heart "stopping in its tracks"), aesthetics (beauty "stopping the gaze"), and industrial engineering (mechanically capturing kinetic force).
