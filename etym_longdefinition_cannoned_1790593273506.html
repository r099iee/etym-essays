cannoned is the past tense and past participle of the verb cannon, meaning to strike something with great force and rebound or ricochet, as a cannonball does after firing.

In its most central modern sense, it describes uncontrolled, heavy bodily impact: a person, animal, vehicle or object hurtles forward and crashes into another person or obstacle, often with comic or chaotic consequences. Wodehouse’s shark that “cannoned into” a surfboard perfectly captures this: great momentum, little steering, and a violent transfer of energy that flings the victim aside.

A second, historically earlier verbal sense belongs to billiards and snooker: to make a cannon or carom, where the cue-ball strikes two other balls in succession. From the 19th century, billiard manuals speak of cannoning skilfully, cannoning off the cushion, or cannoning into the red. This sporting sense preserves the idea of elastic collision and calculated rebound, whereas the general sense emphasizes clumsiness and accident.

A third, now rarer sense is military: to attack or batter with cannon, to fire cannon at, or to discharge like a cannon. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century texts speak of towns cannoned, ships cannoned, or balls cannoned forth. Though largely superseded by bombard and shell, it survives in historical fiction and in extended metaphors such as words cannoned forth or cannoned accusations.

  • Violent collision and rebound: to rush into and bounce off; e.g., he cannoned into me in the corridor, the car cannoned off the barrier. This is the dominant contemporary use, always implying speed, weight, and loss of control.
  • Billiards carom: to strike two balls successively with the cue-ball; e.g., she cannoned neatly to score. The subject is the player or the ball itself.
  • Artillery action: to batter with cannon-fire or to shoot out forcefully; e.g., cannonballs cannoned across the field. Archaic but still intelligible, linking the verb transparently to the noun cannon.
  • Figurative hurtling: to move recklessly through events or time; e.g., he cannoned through life, cannoned from job to job, cannoned into adulthood. Here physical impact becomes social or biographical turbulence.

Polysemy is bridged by the image-schema of the cannonball: massive, fast, loud, and non-deflecting except by violent rebound. From artillery tube to projectile to trajectory to impact, English gradually shifted focus from the weapon to the manner of motion. Thus anything that moves like a discharged ball — heavy, fast, straight, and careless of obstacles — can be said to cannon. The past form cannoned typically marks a completed, punctual crash, often followed by into, against, off or through, and it carries a slightly comic, old-fashioned flavour much loved by comic novelists like P. G. Wodehouse.

Grammatically, cannoned functions as simple past and as participial adjective: a cannoned ball, though rare. It is intransitive with prepositional complement in the collision sense, transitive in the billiards and artillery senses. Register is informal-literary rather than technical; physicists would say collided or rebounded, journalists say crashed, but storytellers say cannoned to add noise, weight and farce.
