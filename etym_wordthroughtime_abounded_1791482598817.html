- Latin, 1st c. BCE - abundare is a water verb: to overflow, to rise in waves over the banks; the image is physical and agricultural
- Middle English, c. 1300 - borrowed as abounden, it still means "to overflow" but quickly shifts to "to be present in plenty"; the liquid image begins to fade
- 14th–16th c. - biblical translation gives the word a strong religious charge: grace, consolation and sin abound; the tone becomes elevated and moral
- 17th–18th c. - the literal overflow is lost; abound in/with becomes the standard construction in learned and polite prose; the word is formal and slightly bookish
- 19th c. - travel and natural-history writing make "abounds in" a guidebook formula for wildlife, minerals and curiosities; the tone is descriptive and confident
- 20th–21st c. - the impersonal "rumours abound / evidence abounds / opportunities abound" dominates journalism and institutional prose; the word is now a formal, slightly evasive way of saying "there is a lot of it about"
