Imagistic is an adjective with two tightly linked senses, both turning on the power of the image. In general use it means characterized by vivid imagery, appealing to mental pictures, expressing thought and feeling through concrete sensory pictures rather than abstract statement, as in imagistic clarity, imagistic prose, imagistic language. A passage is imagistic when it makes the reader see, hear, smell, or touch, when pear trees, rust, river-light, or tired hands do the work that explanation could not. In this sense imagistic is a stylistic compliment, suggesting precision, economy, and visual immediacy.

In literary history imagistic means pertaining to Imagism, the Anglo-American poetic movement of 1912 to 1917 led by Ezra Pound, H.D., Richard Aldington, and Amy Lowell, and to its doctrines of direct treatment of the thing, economy of words, and musical rhythm rather than metronome. To call a poem imagistic in this narrower sense is to place it in the lineage of Pound's few-lines manifestos, of haiku-like superposition, of red wheelbarrows and faces in metro crowds, where clarity means hard, dry, sculptural presentation without Victorian verbiage. The source sentence, in a world of words it strove for imagistic clarity, invokes both senses at once, general vividness and Poundian discipline, suggesting prose that seeks the condensation and luminosity of the image amid verbal abundance.

Morphologically imagistic joins image with the adjectival suffix -istic, as in artistic, realistic, stylistic, marking pertaining to a practice or doctrine. It is therefore slightly more programmatic than imagistic rival image-laden or imaginal, implying not just containing images but committed to image as method. Critics contrast imagistic with discursive, expository, and abstract, where discursive argues, imagistic shows, and with impressionistic, where impressionistic blurs, imagistic hardens. Psychologists and educators use a parallel sense for imagistic thinking, thought in pictures rather than words, and for imagistic memory, recall via visual snapshot.

  • General stylistic sense: full of sharp sensory pictures, e.g. imagistic description, imagistic clarity. Polysemy bridge: from noun image as mental picture to adjective for style that produces such pictures, transferring object to effect, what creates images is called imagistic.
  • Imagism-movement sense: of or in the manner of Imagist poets, e.g. imagistic brevity, imagistic superposition. Polysemy bridge: via proper-name derivation where a school lends its name to a technique, so that Pound's doctrine hardens into a portable label for any hard, clear, juxtaposed picture-writing.
  • Cognitive-psychological sense: thinking or remembering in images, e.g. imagistic coding, imagistic vs verbal learners. Polysemy bridge: via scientific extension where literary image is redefined as mental representation, and imagistic marks the visual channel of cognition versus verbal channel.
  • Religious-visionary nuance: occasionally pertaining to sacred icons or inner visions, e.g. imagistic devotion. Polysemy bridge: via older imago as holy likeness and effigy, where image carries numinous presence, and imagistic devotion means worship through pictures rather than word alone.

Collocationally imagistic favours abstract nouns of style, clarity, density, economy, power, language, prose, poetry, and nouns of mind, thinking, memory, representation. It is gradable, more imagistic, highly imagistic, and it carries positive evaluation in modern criticism, where to be imagistic is to be concrete, honest, and memorable rather than vague. To strive for imagistic clarity in a world of words, as here, is thus to confess both temptation and discipline, surrounded by floods of words, the writer seeks the few hard pictures that can still be seen clearly and hold feeling without preaching.
