The story of unreason is the story of a reckoning gone dark. The word's second element, reason, descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *reh₁- (or *rē-), which originally meant "to count, reckon, calculate, and arrange in order". In the archaic Indo-European world, thinking was not an abstract, disembodied act: it was concrete reckoning — tallying flocks, measuring grain, balancing accounts. The root produced Greek arithmos, "number", the ancestor of arithmetic, and in the Germanic north it formed *rēdaną, "to counsel, interpret", from which English gets read and riddle: to read, like to reason, was to correctly tally the signs of the world.

In Rome the root became the verb rērī, "to think, calculate", and its great noun ratiō — an account book, a business calculation, a logical procedure, and finally reason itself. When Roman merchants and lawyers spoke of ratio they meant balanced ledgers and structured arguments; the word straddled the counting house and the senate. As Latin dissolved into the Romance languages, ratiōnem weakened phonetically into Old French raison, meaning speech, cause, and intellect at once. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman scholars carried raison into Middle English as resoun. Now came the prefix. English had kept the Germanic privative prefix un- (from PIE *n̥-, "not", the same source as Greek a- and Latin in-), and by the 14th century it was being joined to resoun to make unreson — "lack of reason", but also, in the legal-moral idiom of the age, "a wrong", "an injustice": to do unreason was to violate the order of right dealing that reason guaranteed.

For the medieval and Renaissance mind, reason was not optional: it was the faculty that made humans like God, the "law of reason" by which justice and right order stood. Its negation therefore carried moral weight — unreason was sin, injustice, and chaos, not merely a cognitive slip. This moral colouring explains why "unreason" survived the centuries as a grave, formal word while plainer synonyms like "nonsense" stayed light. In the Enlightenment, reason was elevated to the supreme standard of truth and progress, and its shadow side became a topic of serious philosophy: writers began to ask what happens to the mind, and to society, when reason is withdrawn or suppressed. The 19th and 20th centuries made Unreason (with a capital) a figure in its own right — a personified antagonist in philosophical and literary discourse on madness, mysticism, and the limits of rationality. Michel Foucault's Histoire de la folie (1961, translated as Madness and Civilization) examined the historical "dialogue between reason and unreason" that classical civilization had silenced — the madman as the embodiment of unreason locked away so that reason could define itself against him.

Science fiction found its own use for the word. In the test sentence — "Unreason darkens that gap of time bridged by our lightspeed ships, and in the darkness uncertainty and disproportion grow like weeds" — the capitalized noun is given full agency. It does not merely denote a lack; it darkens, it creates a space where error multiplies. The image inherits the whole history: the reckoning root (a world that should add up), the moral weight of the medieval injustice, the Enlightenment fear of darkness returning, and the modern philosophical figure of reason's opposite as an active force. When interstellar distances and long gaps of time sever communication, the account books of knowledge stop balancing — and unreason grows in the void.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *reh₁- → Latin rērī / ratiō → Late Latin ratiōnem → Old French raison → Middle English resoun → English reason; prefix from PIE *n̥- → Proto-Germanic *un- → Old English un-; compound unreson (14th c.) → unreason [ʌnˈriːzən].
  • Semantic drift: "to count, reckon" → "calculation, account" (Latin ratio) → "reason, cause, speech" (Old French) → "the rational faculty" (English) → with un-: "lack of reason" → "a wrong, an injustice" (medieval) → "irrationality, the collapse of rational order" (modern) → "a personified cosmic force" (literary-philosophical).
  • Socio-cultural factors: the medieval equation of reason with justice and order gave unreason moral weight; the Enlightenment's cult of reason made its negation a philosophical problem; modern philosophy (Foucault) and speculative fiction turned Unreason into an active, capitalized antagonist.
  • Literary attestations: Middle English legal and moral texts (unreson as injustice); Renaissance and Enlightenment discourse on the limits of reason; 19th–20th-century literature and philosophy (including Foucault's dialogue of reason and unreason); and science fiction of the 20th century, where the capitalized figure darkens interstellar distances.
  • Modern situation: unreason is a formal, literary-philosophical word; capitalized it names the personified force opposing reason — as in the science-fiction sentence where it grows uncertainty and disproportion "like weeds" in the dark gaps between worlds.

Every use of unreason is thus a small ledger of all the meanings reason itself has carried — number, account, cause, justice, truth — and a reminder that the word was coined when a society believed that to lose reason was to commit a wrong against the order of the world.
