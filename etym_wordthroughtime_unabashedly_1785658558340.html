- [Late 16th century] - Abashed (from Old French esbair, "to astound, confound") means "disconcerted, put to shame"; unabashed is coined by simple negation — "not put to confusion," said of those who keep their composure under scrutiny.
- [17th–18th century] - Unabashed stays a fairly plain negative: "not disconcerted or ashamed," used of blushing maidens and brash youths alike; morally neutral, no special praise or blame.
- [19th century] - The adverb unabashedly appears (c. 1840s) and begins attaching to the very qualities a refined person would be expected to hide — sentiment, ambition, enthusiasm; it takes on an approving charge: candor and authenticity worn without apology, rather than mere nerve.
- [Modern register] - Mid-formal, journalistic-to-literary, mildly approving: "unabashedly proud, sentimental, commercial, honest" — acting as one whose eyes have never once been forced to the floor, even before a skeptical crowd.
