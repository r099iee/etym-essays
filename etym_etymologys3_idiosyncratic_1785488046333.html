The word idiosyncratic is a compound of three separate Indo-European roots, each with a long independent history before they met in a single Greek coinage. The first, *swé, was the reflexive pronoun of the proto-language — the word for “self, oneself”. Greek reshaped it through *swed-yós into ídios, “one’s own, private”, and from that single stem grew a remarkable family: idiṓtēs, “a private citizen, a layman without public office”, whose meaning decayed through “unskilled person” to the modern idiot; and idíōma, “a peculiarity of one’s own language”, source of idiom. Its Indo-European sisters kept the same possessive sense across the whole family — Latin suus (whence suicide), Sanskrit sva, Gothic swēs, Old Church Slavonic svoi — so that “one’s own” was one of the most stable words in the entire language family, surviving into modern Hindi svayaṁ and Russian svoj.
  • *sem-, “one, together”: the second root, *sem-, “one, together”, gave Greek sýn, the prefix of synthesis, sympathy, and synopsis; its Sanskrit sister sám lies at the very heart of Indian culture in saṃskṛta, the name of Sanskrit itself — “the well-put-together language”. In Greek, sýn became the standard prefix for union and combination, and it joined the third element to make synkrasis, “a blending together”.
  • *ḱerh₂-, “to mix”: the third root, *ḱerh₂-, “to mix”, produced the Greek verb keránnumi, the symposium’s act of mingling wine with water in a kratḗr, and its noun krâsis, “a blending”. The physicians seized on krâsis as the technical name for the body’s own mixture — the balance of the four humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) that constituted a person’s temperament, with eukrasía for the healthy balance and dyskrasía for the poisoned imbalance; Sanskrit śrīṇāti, “he mixes, cooks”, is a distant cognate of the same blending verb.
  • The compound is born in Greek medicine: in the Hippocratic medical writers the three elements fused: idiosynkrasía — literally “a mixing that belongs to oneself alone”, one’s own private blend — named the particular bodily temperament that made each patient unique and susceptible to his own diseases. This is the oldest sense of the word and the source of all later meanings.
English took the word in two stages. First, in the early 17th century, it borrowed idiosyncrasy from Greek idiosynkrasía (via Latin idiosyncrasia) as a clinical term for a person’s physical constitution and special susceptibility to disease or stimuli — the medical doctrine of the humours still governed the diagnosis. Then, in the 18th century, the word widened from the body to the mind: idiosyncrasy came to name a person’s characteristic temperament, mode of thought, and behaviour — their private blend of qualities. In the 19th century it settled into the neutral descriptive use we know today: a habit or trait peculiar to a single person, keeping a mildly medical, diagnostic flavour; and the adjective idiosyncratic followed, describing whatever bears the stamp of that private blend. By the 20th and 21st centuries idiosyncratic labels any personal quirk or distinctive stylistic stamp — comparative and descriptive rather than judgemental, capable of a wry, amused tone when the quirk is comically overdone, exactly as in this chapter’s spectrum of scholarly and critical ardour “ranging from the comically idiosyncratic to the laboriously obvious”. The word has come a long way from the Hippocratic consulting-room, but it has never lost the root image of its coinage: each person’s blend is one-of-a-kind, and the critic who meets it, as this chapter does, both recognises the fingerprint and smiles at the excesses of the mixture.
