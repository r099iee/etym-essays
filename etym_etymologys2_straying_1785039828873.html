Linear derivation chain for STRAYING (single line of descent; a rival theory is noted below): 1) Proto-Indo-European *sterh₃- (also *stere-) "to spread out, extend, scatter, strew" — reconstructed; in Sanskrit stṛṇāti "to scatter, strew," Greek strōnnýnai "to spread, strew," and Germanic *strawjan "to scatter" (English strew). 2) Latin sternere "to spread out, lay flat, pave" — classical verb built on the same root. 3) Latin strata "spread out, paved" — feminine past participle of sternere; in Late Latin via strata "paved road" becomes the ordinary word for a made road. 4) Old French estree "route, highway" — from via strata; the same source gives English street through Old English strǣt. 5) Old French estraier "to roam, run loose, wander about the streets" (of a horse without a master) — 12th c.; a verb built on estree, literally "to be on the highway," hence to wander. 6) Middle English straien "to wander from an enclosure or herd" — c. 1300, an aphetic shortening (initial e- dropped) of Old French estraier. 7) Middle English straiyng "straying" — the participial and verbal-noun form. 8) Modern English STRAYING "wandering off course or out of bounds." Debated note: the OED also records a rival derivation of Old French estraier from Vulgar Latin *estragare, a contraction of *extravagare — Latin extra "outside" + vagārī "to wander" — the family of extravagant, vague, and vagabond. Both etymologies plausibly fed the medieval image of a wandering beast, and scholars note that the two routes may have reinforced one another: a stray is both a "street-roamer" and an "outside-wanderer." The paved-road chain via via strata is the traditional and better-supported account.
