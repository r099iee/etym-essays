- Mid-16th c. — the rank enters English from French "caporal" (via Italian "caporale," "head of a body"): a low-ranking non-commissioned officer commanding a small body of soldiers, already set below the sergeant.
- 17th–18th c. — the military sense spreads with the professional armies of the age; the spelling shifts toward "corporal" under the pull of the older English adjective "corporal" ("of the body," from Latin corporalis, as in "corporal punishment") — two different words merging on the page.
- 19th c. — "corporal" is institutionalized as the lowest NCO rank in national armies, the first step of authority above the private; the word takes on its flat, administrative, bureaucratic flavor — a rank, not a character.
- Contemporary — "corporal" remains the soldier who leads a small squad; the old collision with the bodily adjective still works quietly beneath the surface, for the corporal is, etymologically, the head of a "body" of men — a congruence the institutional sense keeps half-alive.
