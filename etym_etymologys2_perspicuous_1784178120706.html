Proto-Indo-European root *spek- (also *spēk-, "to observe, look at, watch") — reconstructed, no attested date. One of the most productive PIE roots for seeing; it underlies Sanskrit páśyati "he sees," Greek skopós "watcher" and sképtesthai "to examine," Latin specere "to look," and Germanic *spehōn "to spy" (source of English spy). Latin speciō / specere "to look at, to observe" — attested from the classical period (3rd–1st c. BCE). From this verb Latin built an enormous family: speculum "mirror," spectator "looker-on," species "appearance, kind," and the prefixed verbs aspect-, conspect-, inspect-, prospect-, respect-, and perspic-. Latin perspiciō, perspicere "to look through, to see through, to perceive clearly" — attested 1st c. BCE (Cicero, Lucretius, etc.). Compound of per- "through" + specere "to look." Latin perspicuus "transparent, clear to the eye; that can be seen through" — attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE (Lucretius, Cicero, Pliny). Adjective built on the stem perspic- with the suffix -uus ("able to be..."). Latin perspicuitās, perspicuitātis f. "transparency, clearness, perspicuity" — attested in the classical period; the abstract noun used by Latin rhetoricians for clarity of style. English perspicuity (noun) "clarity, transparency; clearness of expression" — first attested c. 15th century, borrowed through Old French perspicuité and Latin perspicuitas. The noun entered English before the adjective. English PERSPICUOUS (adjective) "transparent; clearly expressed, lucid" — first attested in the 1590s, borrowed directly from Latin perspicuus. ◄ TARGET Note on the chain: every stage from PIE *spek- through Latin specere, perspicere, perspicuus, perspicuitas to the English borrowings is secure and well attested; there are no reconstructed or debated intermediate links in the documented history. The only PIE-level point to flag is that *spek- is itself a reconstructed proto-form (inferred from the daughter languages), but its existence is among the most certain reconstructions in Indo-European studies, and it is accepted by all standard etymological authorities (Pokorny, de Vaan, Beekes).
