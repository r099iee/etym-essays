Wilde's young playwright has beside his tumbler "a bunch of withered violets" — the plural of violet, one of the best-loved wild flowers of European spring. A violet is a small, low-growing perennial of the genus Viola (family Violaceae), with heart-shaped leaves and nodding, five-petalled blooms, usually violet-blue or purple, often fragrant, rising from a tuft of leaf-stalks. The archetype is the sweet violet (Viola odorata), native to Europe and western Asia, whose scent has been bottled for centuries; the dog violet (Viola riviniana), scentless but ubiquitous in woods; and the pansy (Viola tricolor, the heart's-ease), the wild ancestor of the great-faced garden hybrids.


Botany and uses. Violets seed themselves along hedgerows, banks and wood-margins, flowering early — in the northern hemisphere from March. Almost every part of the traditional violet is useful: the flowers are candied whole for cake decoration; the leaves are edible, rich in vitamin C and eaten young in salads; and the flowers yield an essence that flavours violet syrup, violet liqueurs (crème de violette, parfait amour) and the confectionery of Parma, which made the violet its emblem. In perfumery the flower is a paradox: its scent is carried by ionone, a molecule that dulls the nose within seconds, so true violet perfumes are usually built from other materials imitating the note — a small, well-known irony of perfumery. Herbal tradition used the plant for coughs, sore throats and skin troubles, and the leaves were laid on wounds in folk medicine.


Symbolism and the language of flowers. From the plant's habit of half-hiding its head among its leaves, the violet became the flower of modesty, shyness and faithful quiet love — hence the idiom "a shrinking violet" for a painfully shy person. In Victorian floriography, a gift of violets said "I return your love" or "I am faithful"; in Christian use the colour provides the liturgical hue of Advent, Lent and mourning — the violet vestments of penance and waiting. Napoleon gave the flower a political season: the Bonapartists wore violets as their emblem, and when the Emperor returned from Elba in March 1815 — "with the violets", as his supporters said — the modest flower became a badge of loyalty and hope, and Napoleon himself was nicknamed "Corporal Violet".


The colour sense. Since the fifteenth century the word has also named the colour: violet is the bluish-purple hue at the short-wavelength end of the visible spectrum (roughly 380–450 nanometres), the deepest band of the rainbow as Newton fixed it. The distinction with "purple" is precise: violet is a true spectral colour with its own wavelengths, while purple is any perceptual mixture of red and blue light and appears nowhere in the spectrum. From the colour came "ultraviolet" — the invisible radiation just beyond the violet band, discovered by Johann Wilhelm Ritter in 1801 — and the early twentieth-century health fad of "violet rays" (ultraviolet lamp treatment), which gave the flower's name to a chapter of medical history.


Literary career. The violet is a standing figure of English poetry: Laertes in Hamlet speaks of a character "a violet in the youth of primy nature, forward, not permanent, sweet, not lasting" (1.3), and the flower of modesty runs through the lyrics from Herrick to Housman — "the violets of early spring", the flower that "dies upon her breast" in the ballad tradition. In Wilde's scene the bunch of withered violets on the playwright's desk pairs the flower of love and delicacy with the cold and hunger of the garret — the young man's poverty underlined by the fading of the season's most graceful flower.


Sense summary. (1) The plant: any of the small flowering plants of the genus Viola, plural violets; (2) the colour: bluish-purple, the spectral extreme; (3) figurative: modesty, shyness (a shrinking violet), fidelity; (4) perfumery and confectionery: the scent and flavour; (5) in compounds: ultraviolet radiation, violet rays. The plural form is the one Wilde uses — a bunch, the flower in quantity, the season gathered in the hand.


  • Sense 1: the plant — sweet violet, dog violet, pansy; family Violaceae.
  • Sense 2: the colour — bluish-purple, the shortest visible wavelengths; source of "ultraviolet".
  • Sense 3: symbolism — modesty, shyness, faithfulness; "shrinking violet"; the Napoleonic violet.
  • Sense 4: perfume, syrup, candied flowers; the ionone paradox.
