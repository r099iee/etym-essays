Derivation chain for flurry: 1. Earliest attested form: dialectal verb flurr (mid-16th c.) 'to scatter, sprinkle, fly up, flutter' - attested of grain being winnowed, of feathers, of snow, and of a startled flock bursting into the air; of unknown origin, almost certainly onomatopoeic / echoic of a spattering or fluttering sound. No PIE root can be established, and no Germanic or Romance etymon is attested. 2. flurry (verb, late 16th-17th c.) 'to agitate, to move in a quick shaking manner, to drive in a scattered whirl' - built from flurr with the suffix -y; later also 'to confuse, alarm, excite'. 3. flurry (noun, 1680s) 'a gust of wind; a sudden brief squall' - the first recorded nominal sense, meteorological, derived from the verb. 4. Extended nominal senses: 'a brief swirling shower of fine snow or rain' (early 19th c.), then 'a sudden commotion or burst of activity' (mid-19th c.) - the metaphorical leap from weather to human agitation. 5. flurry (Modern English) - both noun ('a sudden brief commotion; a light fall of snow or rain') and verb ('to agitate, confuse, alarm'); the modern form has been unchanged since the 18th century. Note on the debate: the origin is unknown. Some dictionaries connect flurr to Old Norse or to Dutch flodderen 'to flutter, slop about', and others derive it from a blend of flutter and hurry; none of these etymologies is secure, and the OED classifies the word as of obscure, probably imitative origin. No PIE root is reconstructible, and the chain must therefore begin with the earliest attested English form.
