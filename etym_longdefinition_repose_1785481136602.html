REPOSE (noun and verb) denotes a state of rest, ease, or peaceful inactivity — being undisturbed and at rest. In the passage on the history of literary criticism, the word carries its quietest, most literal sense: the undisturbed resting of books on library shelves. The tone is gently ironic: the "problematic" examples demand an explanation, but most books simply slumber unchallenged, gathering nothing but dust. The word suggests a state of peace that is also a state of neglect — rest, yes, but the rest of things no one bothers to disturb. This is the "absence of motion or agitation" sense applied to inanimate objects, and it sits in deliberate contrast with the idea of works that are actively read, debated, or found wanting.

Polysemy and the full range of senses.
  • Physical rest (core sense): the cessation of activity or motion; lying at rest; sleep in its dignified register. "To repose in one's bed," "a night of repose."
  • Of inanimate objects: the undisturbed resting of a thing in its place — books "in repose" on shelves, a lake "lying in perfect repose," a statue "at repose." The word suggests a sculptural, almost composed stillness.
  • Tranquillity of mind: inner calm, freedom from agitation or anxiety — "repose of mind," "perfect repose of spirit." By the 16th century the word extended from body to mind.
  • Euphemism for death: "final repose," "eternal repose," the dignified resting of the dead. Closely tied to "repose" in funeral and memorial language.
  • Art, music, and aesthetics: visual or formal harmony; the quality of a composition that is calm and balanced; in music, a cadence or sustained calm passage; the "repose" of a pose or arrangement. Transferred from bodily rest to aesthetic harmony.
  • Verb senses: (1) intransitive "to lie at rest" — "she reposed on the couch"; (2) transitive (archaic) "to lay down, to place" — "to repose one's head"; (3) with "in" — "to repose one's trust/confidence in" — this last is a DIFFERENT verb, from Latin reponere "to place back," a homonym that must not be merged with the rest-word.
  • As a quality of character / composure: dignity and self-possession, a composed stillness of manner — "a face full of repose," "the repose of his bearing."

Semantic bridges. The word enters English from Old French reposer/repos, ultimately from Late Latin repausare "to cause to rest thoroughly" — pausare "to pause, halt, rest" itself from Latin pausa "a pause," borrowed from Greek pausis "a stopping." The bridge from physical to psychological and aesthetic rest is one of the classic metaphors of the language: to stop moving becomes to stop worrying, and a stopped, balanced composition becomes "reposeful." The funeral sense is a dignified specialization of physical rest. Register: the noun is now markedly elevated and literary; "rest" is the plain everyday word, while "repose" adds gravity, stillness, and composure — and, applied to objects or the dead, a quiet ceremonial weight. Collocations: "in repose," "undisturbed repose," "repose of mind," "final/eternal repose," "perfect repose," "a position of repose," "to repose confidence in." The word's dignity is precisely its point in the card's sentence: the books are not merely resting, they are reposing, undisturbed, and their undisturbed repose on the shelves is, the author says with a wry shrug, not unmerited.
