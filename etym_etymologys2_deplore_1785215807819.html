PIE *pleu- / *plew- "to flow, to run, to wash" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the root of moving water, seen in Greek πλέω (pléō) "to sail," πλύνω (plýnō) "to wash," and the Germanic family of flow and flood.
│
└──▶ Latin plōrāre "to cry out, to weep aloud, to lament" (attested from the 2nd c. BCE) — a verb of the voice built on the root of flowing; the precise derivation is debated (see note).
────▶ Latin dēplōrāre "to bewail, to weep bitterly over, to lament greatly" (attested from the 1st c. BCE) — intensive prefix dē- ("thoroughly, utterly") + plōrāre.
────────▶ Old French deplorer "to lament, to bewail, to express grief" (11th–13th c.) — inherited into the vernacular from Vulgar Latin.
────────────▶ Middle English deploren "to lament, to mourn" (first attested c. 15th c.) — borrowed from French.
────────────────▶ Modern English DEPLORE "to lament" (16th c.), then "to feel and express strong disapproval of" (modern dominant sense, established across the 17th–18th c.).

Note on debate: the origin of Latin plōrāre itself is uncertain. The traditional derivation links it to PIE *pleu- "to flow" (the voice "flowing" with tears, or the face streaming); alternative proposals connect it to an expressive, onomatopoeic base imitative of weeping, or to a reduplicated formation. Pokorny and de Vaan favour the link to *pleu- but flag it as probable rather than proven. The stages from plōrāre onward are fully attested and uncontroversial.
