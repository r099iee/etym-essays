- 16th to early 18th century: Toponym of an East Indian Trading Emporium - In European maritime trade, *Bantam* referred to the renowned Sultanate of Banten in western Java, a bustling pepper emporium; European sailors returning from the East Indies introduced diminutive, colorful chickens known as *Bantam fowls*.
- Mid-18th to early 19th century: Aristocratic Avicultural Fancy - Shortened in English to the standalone noun *bantam*; selectively bred across British country estates as an ornamental miniature chicken celebrated for its compact size, vivid plumage, and fiery, combative temperament.
- 1820s to 1880s: Colloquial Personification of Scrappiness - Transferred metaphorically into Victorian slang and sporting journalism to describe small, exceptionally spirited, aggressive, or combative men who refused to back down from larger opponents.
- 1880s to 1920s: Formalized Boxing Division & World War I Battalions - Codified as the official 'bantamweight' division in professional prize-fighting (upper limit of 118 pounds); during WWI, adopted by the British Army to designate legendary 'Bantam battalions' composed of undersized but fiercely determined infantry recruits.
- 1930s to Present: Compact Industrial Engineering & General Metaphor - Associated with subcompact industrial machinery (such as the American Bantam Car Company, designers of the original World War II military Jeep prototype); permanently established in modern English as an affectionate and admiring metaphor for pint-sized scrappiness and resilience.
