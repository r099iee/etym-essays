The morphology and intellectual history of signifiers encapsulate one of the deepest transformations in Western semiotics, linguistic philosophy, and literary theory, evolving from archaic Indo-European roots of physical marking and carving into the cornerstone of modern structuralism, psychoanalysis, and deconstruction.

The word's genealogical roots stem from two primary Proto-Indo-European linguistic elements:

  • *sek- (or alternatively *sekʷ-): An ancient verbal root signifying "to cut," "to incise," "to sever," or "to follow." In Proto-Italic, this root developed into the nominal form *seknom, which became the direct ancestor of Classical Latin signum. Originally denoting a distinguishing mark carved, branded, or pressed into a physical surface, signum broadened across Roman military and civic life to designate a military standard, an identification token, a seal, an omen, or a constellation in the heavens.
  • *dʰeh₁-: The ubiquitous Indo-European verbal root meaning "to put," "to place," "to do," or "to make," which produced the Latin verb facere ("to make, to do, to produce").

In Classical Latin, these elements merged to form the compound verb significāre, meaning literally "to make a mark," "to show by signs," "to indicate," "to portend," or "to intimate." From this verbal stem arose the present participle and agent nominal forms denoting that which performs the act of indicating or expressing meaning. Entering Old French as signifier in the 12th century, the word was borrowed into Middle English during the late 14th century (attested in the works of Geoffrey Chaucer and John Wycliffe) as both a verb ("to mean, signify, or portend") and an agent noun ("one who signifies or delivers an omen").

For centuries throughout the late medieval and early modern periods, signifier remained a modest, general term denoting an omen, a messenger, an emblem, or a visible symptom. However, between 1906 and 1911, the Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure delivered his revolutionary series of lectures at the University of Geneva. In what would be compiled posthumously by his students in 1916 as the Cours de linguistique générale (Course in General Linguistics), Saussure created a rigorous scientific taxonomy of human communication. Rejecting the ancient, naive belief that words are simply names attached to pre-existing objects in the world, Saussure introduced the concept of the signe (linguistic sign) as a two-sided psychological entity composed of:

  • Le signifiant (the signifier): The acoustic "sound-image" (image acoustique), physical graphic mark, or perceptible sensory pattern.
  • Le signifié (the signified): The abstract mental concept (concept) evoked by that sensory image.

Saussure established two fundamental principles that redefined modern linguistics: the arbitrary nature of the sign (there is no natural, intrinsic link between the sound-pattern and the mental concept) and the differential nature of language (signifiers only possess meaning through their negative contrast with other signifiers within a closed system). When Saussure's work was translated into English by Wade Baskin in 1959, signifiant was rendered as signifier (plural: signifiers).

During the mid-20th-century intellectual ferment of Parisian structuralism, psychoanalysis, and post-structuralism, the term underwent an explosive expansion. Psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan declared that "the unconscious is structured like a language" and famously inverted Saussure's diagram to place the Signifier over the Signified ($S/s$), arguing that human subjecthood and desire slip continuously along an endless chain of signifiers. Shortly thereafter, Jacques Derrida, Michel Foucault, Julia Kristeva, and Roland Barthes expanded signifiers to literature, film, fashion, and cultural mythology. In Barthes's textual analysis, signifiers are celebrated in their joyful, unmoored materiality—inviting readers into a state of blissful plenitude where language revels in its own generative play.
