SINGLE ROOT WORD (remonstrate) — a Latin formation ultimately from the PIE mental root *men-. PIE *men- 'to think, to be mindful, to have in mind' (c. 4000–3000 BCE, reconstructed; cognates Sanskrit manas 'mind', Greek ménos 'spirit, force', Germanic *mun- > mind) → Latin monēre 'to warn, advise, put in mind' (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; a causative/advisory verb from the *men- root, originally 'to make think of') → Latin monstrum 'a portent, divine sign, warning' (attested; literally 'that which is shown', built on the monēre / monstrāre nexus) → Latin monstrāre 'to show, point out, make visible' (attested 1st c. BCE) → Late / Medieval Latin remonstrāre 'to demonstrate again, to show back, to point out in return' (re- 'back, again' + monstrāre; documented in medieval Latin usage) → English remonstrate 'to make a forceful, reasoned protest; to expostulate' (borrowed early 17th c., c. 1620s, from the Latin past participle remonstrātus or directly from remonstrāre) → REMONSTRATE. Debate / nuance note: The derivation of monstrāre from monstrum ('that which is shown') is traditional, though some scholars derive it from a frequentative formation on monēre; both paths keep the same semantic idea of 'making visible to the mind'. English remonstrate may have been reinforced by Italian rimostrare or French remontrer in the same 'show back' sense; French remontrer and Spanish remostrar show the same re- + 'show' formation across Romance. The English verb is thus a single learned Latin chain, not a Germanic word.
