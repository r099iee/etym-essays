The biography of balled is a biography of swelling. It begins with the PIE root *bʰel- "to swell, to blow up, to inflate," one of the family's oldest words for roundness — a root that conceived a sphere not as a shape drawn on paper but as something pushed out from within, a bulge, a blow-up, a thing made round by pressure. From this root the Germanic branch built *balluz, "a ball, a round object," and English got its word chiefly through Old Norse bǫllr in the Middle Ages, a legacy of the Scandinavian settlement of northern England. The sound shape is compact and stable: PIE *bʰ- (an aspirated voiced stop) became plain Germanic *b-, and the vowel *e rounded and lengthened through the Germanic and Middle English stages to modern English /ɔːl/ in "ball," with the participle "balled" showing the ordinary weak-verb ending -ed.

The Germanic form also traveled south, and here the story widens into the whole European family. Frankish *palla carried it into French as balle and ballon; the Langobards — the same Lombards of the "lumber" story — carried it into Italian as palla and pallone, whence French ballon and English balloon (the "big ball" that rises by inflation — the root's oldest image returning). The Italian ballotta, "a small ball," gave English ballot, the voting ball dropped into a box in the Venetian elections — democracy itself, etymologically, a matter of small balls. Meanwhile Latin kept its own twin of the root: follis, "a leather bag, a bellows, an inflated ball," which English inherited as bellows and as boll, the rounded seed-pod. Every one of these words — ball, balloon, ballot, bale, bellows, boll — is the same PIE "swelling" taking different shapes in different mouths.

The verb is a later English development. From the noun ball came the verb to ball, "to form into a ball," and its past participle balled describes the completed act: a hand balled is a hand gathered into a sphere, the fingers and palm compressed until the flesh swells into a fist. The step is a simple English verbification — the noun turned to a verb by conversion, without any suffix — but it carries the whole etymological burden: to clench a fist is to perform, in miniature, the ancient act of swelling, the muscle rounding, the knuckles rising, the hand becoming a dense, bulging weapon. The idiom "ball up" extends the image into confusion (to be "balled up" is to be tangled into a knot), and the physical senses survive in "the ball of the foot" and "the ball of the thumb," the rounded, padded, swollen parts of the body.

  • PIE *bʰel- "to swell" — the root of roundness; a sphere as something blown up from within.
  • PGmc *balluz — the Germanic "swelling," the round object; loaned south to Frankish (French balle, ballon) and Langobardic Italian (palla, pallone, ballotta → balloon, ballot).
  • Old Norse bǫllr — the Viking form through which English got the word.
  • Middle English bal — the round object, the sphere of play.
  • 16th–17th c. — the verb "to ball," to form into a round mass; the phrasal "ball up."
  • 20th c. — the participle "balled" in narrative prose: balled fists, hands balled in pockets, the roundness of the fist read as tension made visible; figuratively, "balled up" = tangled or confused.

Two guardrails keep the story honest. First, the dance "ball" is a separate word: it comes from Greek ballízein "to dance" through Latin ballāre and Old French bal, and has nothing to do with the swelling root — the two balls are pure homophones that English happens to spell alike. Second, Greek phallós "phallus" is sometimes compared with *bʰel- as a "swollen thing," and Sanskrit bāla "strength" has been linked to a *bel- root, but neither is accepted as a secure cognate of "ball" in the handbooks, and no Hindi or Odia word descends from the swelling root — so none is forced into the chain. The word on this card, balled, is thus a plain English participle of a Norse noun of a Germanic root of a PIE idea: the hand made round, the fist as a small swelling, anger stored and rounded and ready.
