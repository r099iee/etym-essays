- 14th c. — the name denotes a distant island of classical legend, known from Roman history and from the story of the Athenian disaster.
- 16th–17th c. — humanist travel and the Grand Tour make Sicily a required stop for antiquarians, and the name takes on a learned, classical air.
- 18th c. — the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies and the age’s excavations give the name a flavour of ruins, heat and Spanish ceremony.
- 19th c. — unification with Italy in 1860, then rural poverty, banditry and mass emigration give “Sicily” a new and darker association with backwardness and crime.
- 1943–1946 — the Allied invasion and the grant of regional autonomy turn the name into a political as well as a geographical term.
- 21st c. — tourism, archaeology and television crime fiction (Montalbano, The White Lotus) restore a glamorous image, while the older stereotypes persist in news coverage.
