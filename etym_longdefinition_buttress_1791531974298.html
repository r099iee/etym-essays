Buttress is a word of stone and thrust. As a noun it names the mass of masonry built against a wall to keep it from bulging or falling outward; as a verb it names the act of giving such support, and by extension the act of strengthening any claim, institution or argument. The physical and the figurative senses are unusually close: a buttress does not decorate a wall, it resists a force, and that is exactly what a “buttressed” argument is supposed to do.

Architecture: the primary sense

In building, a buttress is a projecting pier or mass of masonry built against a wall to counteract the lateral thrust of vaults, arches or roofs. The thrust is real and directional: a stone vault pushes outward as well as downward, and without an opposing mass the wall will lean, crack and eventually collapse. The buttress supplies that mass, transmitting the load down to a wider foundation. Related terms mark slight differences: a counterfort is a buttress concealed in an earth bank or retaining wall; an abutment takes the thrust of a bridge arch; a pier is a vertical support between openings; a pilaster is a shallow, often decorative buttress engaged with the wall.

The flying buttress

The flying buttress (arc-boutant) is the Gothic solution to the same problem. Instead of a solid mass against the wall, an arch springs from a detached outer pier and carries the thrust over a gap to the upper wall, leaving the intervening space open. This allowed the tall clerestories, thin walls and vast windows of Chartres, Notre-Dame de Paris, Amiens and Beauvais. The system was not merely aesthetic: the collapse of Beauvais cathedral’s vault in 1284 is a reminder of how finely the forces were balanced. Renaissance and Baroque architects continued to use buttresses, often masked by pilasters or volutes, and the device remains standard in masonry construction.

Engineering extensions

  • Buttress dam — a dam whose upstream face is supported by a series of buttresses on the downstream side; the Ambursen and multiple-arch types are common, and the form is efficient in concrete because it concentrates material where the load is.
  • Buttress thread — an asymmetric screw thread with a nearly vertical load-bearing flank, designed to take heavy thrust in one direction; it appears in jacks, presses and pipeline couplings.
  • Buttress root — the plank-like flare at the base of many tropical trees, such as figs and ceibas, which braces a shallow-rooted trunk against wind and uprooting.
  • Buttressing in geology and glaciology — the support a mass of rock or ice gives to a slope, cliff or glacier; removing the buttress can trigger failure or accelerate flow.

The verb: literal and figurative

The verb is recorded from the sixteenth century. Literally it means “to support with a buttress”: “the wall was buttressed with brick piers.” Figuratively it means “to strengthen, reinforce, prop up”: “to buttress an argument with evidence,” “to buttress the economy with subsidies,” “a claim buttressed by statistics.” The figurative use keeps the direction of the physical one — support is applied from outside, against a pressure — and it usually implies that the thing supported is under strain.

The figurative noun

Less common but still current is the noun used abstractly: “a buttress of the constitution,” “the buttresses of society,” “the family as a buttress of the state.” Here the image is institutional and enduring: a buttress is permanent, external and load-bearing, unlike a temporary prop. That is why the metaphor suits traditions, alliances and constitutional safeguards better than it suits mere help.

Semantic bridges

  • Physical prop → structural reinforcement: the architectural sense, where the word is precise and technical.
  • Structural reinforcement → evidential reinforcement: the early modern transfer from walls to arguments and institutions.
  • Architecture → engineering: dams, threads and roots extend the shape and function to new materials.
  • Permanent external support → political and social metaphor: “buttress” suggests something added from outside to hold a burden-bearing structure upright.

Literary and rhetorical use

Architectural metaphors have long been used to describe institutions. Ruskin’s The Stones of Venice (1851–53) taught Victorian readers to see buttresses as moral as well as structural facts, and Walter Scott’s medieval romances made the word part of the picturesque vocabulary of abbeys and castles. In modern prose the verb is the more active form: writers buttress claims, reputations and currencies, and the word lends a faintly conservative, engineering solidity to the sentence.
