- 14th c. — Coined in Medieval Latin anatomical texts as occipitalis by Italian dissection pioneers (Mondino de Luzzi) to demarcate the dorsal cranial bone and posterior lambdoid suture.
- 16th c. — Introduced into Tudor English medical treatises (Thomas Gale, 1563) as an osteological adjective distinguishing the posterior cranium from the forehead and sinciput.
- 17th c. — Adopted into Early Modern neurology (Thomas Willis's Cerebri Anatome, 1664) to describe the posterior brain tissue, occipital sinuses, and emergent cervical nerve branches.
- Late 19th c. — Underwent a dramatic conceptual transformation in neurophysiology (Munk, Ferrier, Inouye), shifting from a passive skeletal landmark into the active cortical center of visual perception.
- 20th–21st c. — Universal clinical standard across neuroscience, neuroimaging (fMRI, PET), and cognitive psychology, defining retinotopic coordinates and visual processing streams.
