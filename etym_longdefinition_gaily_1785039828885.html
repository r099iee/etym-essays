GAILY is the adverb of gay, meaning in a cheerful, lighthearted, and carefree manner — with mirth, brightness, or jaunty unconcern. In The Hobbit, ch. iv ("Over Hill and Under Hill"), the adverb is charged with narrative irony: when the dwarves said goodbye to Elrond at Rivendell "in the high hope of a midsummer morning," they "had spoken gaily of the passage of the mountains, and of riding swift across the lands beyond." Their cheerful talk now rings hollow, for the company is stranded in freezing wind and lashing rain among the high passes, "thinking equally gloomy thoughts." The word's tone is therefore one of naive, untested optimism — the bright, showy confidence of travellers who have not yet met the storm — and Tolkien lets its glitter betray them. The brightness is literally misplaced: what was gaily promised at midsummer has become a grim, drenched reality. Senses of the word (exhaustive)
  • Cheerfully, merrily, lightheartedly (primary): doing something with mirth and high spirits, as in "the children sang gaily."
  • Brightly, showily, with fine or gay colours (secondary, historically strong): gaily dressed, gaily coloured, gaily decked — the adverb of the adjective's sense "bright, finely attired, showy"; dated today but still used of colour and ornament.
  • Blithely, heedlessly, without care for consequences (extension): "she gaily ignored the warning" — cheerful unconcern shading into irresponsibility, a common modern use that carries an ironic edge.
  • Jauntily, sprightly (bearing): describing a smart, self-confident manner of walking or dressing, overlapping the sense of gay as "fashionable, fine."
Semantic bridges The two poles — cheer and show — are one word because the Middle English adjective gay meant both "joyful" and "bright-coloured, finely dressed"; both senses descend from the Old French gai (joyful, pleasant, bright), and both flowed into the adverb gaily from the 14th century. For centuries "gaily dressed" and "gaily singing" were equally ordinary. The 20th century broke this unity from outside: as gay shifted in its noun and adjective use toward homosexual identity, gaily (which had always meant "in a gay manner") became capable of that meaning too, and in ordinary speech it retreated to dated or ironic use. The modern writer who uses gaily does so knowing it sounds old-fashioned — which is precisely what lets Tolkien deploy it: the archaic, showy optimism of gaily is the point, the bright confidence that the storm will betray. Grammar, register, and near-synonyms Gaily is an adverb of manner, formed from gay + -ly, and it modifies verbs of speech, movement, dress, and play. Its register today is literary and slightly dated; in modern prose it is often ironic or deliberately nostalgic. Cheerfully and happily describe a settled inward good humour; merrily points to laughter and festivity; blithely stresses heedless unconcern, usually with an edge of irresponsibility; jauntily describes a smart, self-confident manner in bearing or dress; breezily suggests casual, confident ease; joyfully expresses deeper, more serious gladness. Gaily alone links cheer to appearance — its historic senses of bright colour and fine dress still glint through the adverb — so that "gaily" carries both the smile and the glitter at once.
