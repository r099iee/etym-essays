- Old English (8th–11th c.): Distinct noun *willa* (desire, wish, delight, purpose) and athematic verb *willan* (to wish, desire, want), expressing subjective yearning rather than mere future time.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.): *Wille* acquires formal legal meaning as a written testamentary instrument under ecclesiastical probate courts; auxiliary *will* begins competing with *shall* for future tense marking.
- Early Modern English (16th–18th c.): Intense philosophical and theological debates over 'free will' versus predestination; Defoe uses 'against the will' to illustrate filial defiance against parental authority.
- 19th Century: German idealist philosophy (Schopenhauer's *The World as Will and Representation*, Nietzsche's *Will to Power*) elevates 'will' into a cosmic metaphysical force.
- 20th–21st Century: Psychological science focuses on 'willpower', cognitive self-regulation, and executive function, alongside universal daily modal and legal uses.
