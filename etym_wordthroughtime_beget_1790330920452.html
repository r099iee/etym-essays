- Old English (c. 850–1100) - *begietan* functioned primarily as a general verb of acquisition, meaning 'to obtain, acquire, seize, grasp, or achieve', without any inherent biological or procreative specialization.
- Early Middle English (c. 1150–1350) - Developed a specialized procreative meaning ('to get by generation', 'to sire/father offspring'), shifting from simple material acquisition to the generation of progeny.
- Late Middle English to Early Modern English (c. 1380–1611) - Cemented into the highest sacred and literary register through the Wycliffite Bible and the King James Version ('Abraham begat Isaac'), establishing an enduring aura of antiquity, patriarchal authority, and biblical solemnity.
- 17th to 19th Century - Broadened into moral philosophy, political discourse, and legal prose to describe inexorable causal sequences ('tyranny begets rebellion', 'ignorance begets superstition'), serving as the quintessential verb of organic causation.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retained in contemporary English primarily as a literary, rhetorical, and aphoristic verb denoting self-perpetuating cycles of cause and effect (e.g., 'violence begets violence'), with its literal biological use perceived as deliberate archaism.
