- Parricide enters English in the 16th c. straight from Roman law, where the killing of a parent — and by extension of the state, the 'fatherland' — was the gravest crime, punished with a terror reserved for no other.
- Through the 17th–18th c. the word broadens in ordinary legal and moral use to the killing of any near relative, and its formality makes it sound like a sentence passed even when read aloud.
- In the 19th–20th c., as companion -cide words sharpen the distinctions, 'parricide' stays the broadest and most solemn term, freighted with the oldest domestic taboo; it remains a word of moral horror, not neutral law.
- In modern usage 'parricide' keeps its grim, archaic, absolute tone, while its narrower relative 'patricide' does most of the everyday work for father-killing specifically.
- Hamlet runs a separate track: entering English in the 14th c. as a small village, a cluster of houses without a church, it gains a second, unshakeable life in 1600 as Shakespeare's Danish prince.
- In the present, 'hamlet' remains the smallest of settlements in administrative speech, while the literary ear still hears the ghost of Elsinore behind it.
