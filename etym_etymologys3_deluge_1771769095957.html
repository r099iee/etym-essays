THE EVOLUTION ESSAY OF DELUGE 1. THE WASHING ROOT The story of DELUGE begins in a remarkably humble place: the Proto-Indo-European root *lewH- (also reconstructed *leu-, or *(s)leuH-), which simply meant 'to wash.' It is a root of water and cleaning, of the daily offices of bathing and laundering, and its reflexes are scattered across the Indo-European world. Greek preserved it in loúō, 'I wash, bathe,' and loutrón, 'bath,' the word behind the loutrophoros, the vase that carried bath-water at Greek weddings and funerals. In Latin the root produced lavere and lavāre, 'to wash, bathe,' a verb whose descendants still wash the modern world: Italian lavare, the source of the geological term lava (the molten rock that flows, as Vesuvius's washing flood of stone was described in the eighteenth century); Old French lavandier, whence English launder and laundry; Latin lavātōrium, the washing place that became our lavatory; Latin lōtiō, 'a washing,' our lotion; and the contracted lātrīna, 'a place of washing,' our latrine. Even today the family of *lewH- is a small domestic empire of soap and water, and it is from this bath-time root that one of the most catastrophic words in the language was born. 2. WASHING AWAY: DILUERE AND THE ROMAN PREFIXES Latin multiplied the root with prefixes to create a family of verbs meaning 'to wash away, to dissolve.' Abluere, 'to wash away,' gave English ablution. Diluere, built with dis-, 'apart, away,' and luere, 'to wash,' meant 'to wash away, to dissolve, to weaken by mixing' — the sense that survives in English dilute, from the participle dīlūtus. The Romans used diluere for dissolving salt in water, for watering down wine, for washing away stains, and for dissipating or weakening an argument or an emotion. The prepositional prefix dis- is the key: it splits the action, so that diluere means to wash something apart, to undo its coherence by water. From this verb of undoing Latin formed the noun dīlūvium, 'a flood, an inundation': literally a great washing-away, water unleashed to sweep things apart and dissolve them. A parallel feminine form, dīlūvies, also existed. The word carried from the start a sense of violent, disintegrating water — not the gentle washing of the bath but the water that takes everything with it and separates it into ruin. 3. FROM LATIN TO OLD FRENCH AND THE BIBLICAL FLOOD It was this dīlūvium that passed into Old French as deluge, and thence, in the fourteenth century, into English — at first exclusively as the name of the biblical Flood, the Deluge of Noah, the greatest washing-away ever imagined. In Christian tradition the Flood was divine punishment, the water of God's wrath dissolving a corrupt world so that a clean one could begin again. The Latin Bible's word for the Flood had been dīluvium in the Vulgate's usage, and the French and English word inherited that whole theological weight: for three centuries after it entered English, 'deluge' was primarily the name of Noah's Flood, capitalized as the Deluge, and secondarily any flood imagined on that apocalyptic scale. The word thus entered the language already pregnant with judgment: a deluge was not merely a flood but a providential catastrophe, the sea of God's displeasure rising over a sinful world. 4. THE SEVENTEENTH-CENTURY GENERALIZATION In the seventeenth century the word generalized from scripture to any devastating inundation. 'A deluge' came to mean any overwhelming flood of water — the sea-burst, the dam-burst, the river in spate — while retaining the biblical scale and the sense of total, world-remaking destruction. It is in this period that the word begins to move into figurative territory: a 'deluge' of words, of petitions, of letters, of war, of evil. The metaphorical extension was natural because the Flood was the archetype of totality — it covered everything without exception — so any overwhelming quantity could be figured as a deluge. The word kept its grandeur even in metaphor: a 'deluge of letters' is not a heavy post-bag but an inundation, something that submerges the recipient. 5. THE EIGHTEENTH CENTURY AND GIBBON'S IRONIC USE By the eighteenth century deluge had both its literal and its figurative life in full, and it is in this climate that Gibbon deploys it in the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire. In his account of the earthquakes of the late fourth century, Gibbon writes that 'the most sagacious divines could distinguish, according to the colour of their respective prejudices, that the establishment of heresy tended to produce an earthquake, or that a deluge was the inevitable consequence of the progress of sin and error.' The word is doing several things at once. It invokes the whole biblical history of the word — the divines reason exactly as the interpreters of Genesis reasoned, reading the world's waters as the instrument of God's displeasure. It carries the scale of an apocalyptic catastrophe, which makes the absurdity of the causal claim ('the progress of sin and error' producing a deluge) all the more visible. And it does so in the solemn, Latinate register of the believers themselves, so that the irony is gentle, deflating, and devastating all at once: the historian does not mock the word, he lets the word mock the divines. Gibbon's deluge is the real object of superstitious fear — the water that might sweep away the world — and, at the same moment, the emblem of how far the religious imagination will go to find moral causes for natural events. 6. THE NINETEENTH CENTURY: GEOLOGY AND THE 'FLOOD' VOCABULARY In the nineteenth century the word family acquired a learned scientific life. The geological term diluvium — the deposits of gravel, sand, and drift that early geologists attributed to the action of the Flood — entered English from scientific Latin, along with the adjectives diluvial and diluvian, 'of or relating to the Deluge,' and the celebrated antediluvian, 'before the Flood.' As geology outgrew the flood hypothesis, antediluvian survived in the language as a vivid word for anything hopelessly old-fashioned, and the family's scientific chapter is a reminder of how deeply the word's biblical associations had been woven into the Western understanding of the earth itself. Meanwhile the ordinary word deluge continued in its double life, literal and figurative. 7. THE MODERN WORD: OVERLOAD AND JUDGMENT In the twentieth century and the present, deluge has become overwhelmingly figurative in ordinary use. We speak of a deluge of emails, of data, of information, of applications, of abuse, of criticism — a great quantity of anything arriving at the same time and overwhelming the recipient. The catastrophic grandeur of the biblical word has softened into modern overload, yet the biblical echo of judgment remains: to be deluged is to be submerged, to lose one's feet, to be swept along by a force beyond one's control. The word has also become a verb — 'to deluge' someone with something — and survives in the famous cynical phrase 'after me, the deluge' (French après moi, le déluge), attributed to Louis XV or to Madame de Pompadour, which dismisses all concern for the world that will be left behind. In every one of these modern uses the word carries, however faintly, the memory of its entire career: the bath of the Indo-Europeans, the washing-away of the Roman diluere, the Flood of Noah that drowned the world, and the Gibbonian irony that set the divine machinery of punishment against the facts of nature. A word that began as the homely verb 'to wash' has become, across nearly four thousand years, the language's grandest name for the water that overwhelms everything, and for everything that overwhelms us like water.
