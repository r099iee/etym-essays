Proto-Indo-European *kens- "to speak solemnly, proclaim, announce authoritatively" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; posited on the basis of Latin censēre and Vedic Sanskrit śaṃs- "to recite, praise")
→ Proto-Italic *kens-e- "to assess, estimate, pass judgment solemnly, express a formal opinion" (reconstructed)
→ Latin censēre "to assess, value, estimate, pass judgment, express a formal opinion" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; in Plautus and Cicero)
→ Latin censor "the Roman magistrate who registered citizens, took the census, and oversaw public morals" (the office was created in 443 BCE; the word is attested from the 3rd c. BCE)
→ Latin censūra "the office of censor, assessment, judicial judgment, severe moral criticism" (attested 1st c. BCE; e.g. in Cicero)
→ Old French censure "judgment, condemnation, ecclesiastical penalty" (13th c.; used in church law for excommunication and spiritual punishment)
→ Middle English censure "judgment, opinion, official rebuke" (recorded late 14th c., e.g. in the works of Chaucer and Wycliffe)
→ Modern English censure "to express severe formal disapproval", whose gerund and present participle is censuring (the -ing form is native English morphology, first regularized in the 16th c.).

Note on the debate: The PIE root *kens- is reconstructed largely from the Itali-Celtic and Indo-Iranian comparanda (Latin censēre, Vedic śaṃs-); its exact shape and the precise semantic bridge "speak solemnly" > "assess formally" are debated by specialists, with some preferring to keep the two families apart. The later chain from Latin to English is firmly attested and uncontroversial.
