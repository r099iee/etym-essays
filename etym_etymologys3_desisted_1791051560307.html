Desisted continues Latin desistere, itself a compound with a very old pedigree. The base is Proto-Indo-European *steh- to stand, one of the most prolific roots in the family, yielding English stand, stay, state, status, station, stable, establish, insist, resist, persist and Latin stare to stand. Latin formed a reduplicated causative sistere (si-st-ere) to set, place, stop, whence prefixed verbs assistere, consistere, insistere, resistere, persistere and desistere de- away + sistere — literally to stand away, hence to leave off, give over.

In classical Latin desistere governed ablative or infinitive: desistere bello, desistere incepto to leave off war or an undertaking. Cicero, Livy and Tacitus use it for armies desisting from siege, speakers desisting from pleading, emperors desisting from projects. It was dignified but ordinary, implying voluntary standing-off rather than defeat. Late Latin and medieval canon law kept it for abandoning suits and claims, which passed into Old French desister, deshister to give up a legal demand, attested from the 14th century in Norman and Parisian records.

  • Phonetics: Latin /deːˈsistere/ → Old French /desister/ with loss of quantity → Middle English /dɛˈzɪst/ with voicing of s between vowels in Anglo-French; modern RP /dɪˈsɪst/, past /dɪˈsɪstɪd/ with regular -ed /ɪd/ after /t/.
  • Morphology: de- separative + sist- stand + -ere infinitive; English adds Germanic weak past -ed, present participle desisting, noun desistance, desistence.
  • First English: 15th-c. Scots law — desyst, desiste — then Caxton and Tudor statutes: desist from further proceedings.

Early Modern English briefly allowed transitive desist — desist thy clamour — on Latin analogy, but by 1700 the construction hardened to intransitive desist from + gerund/noun, mirroring refrain and abstain. The Authorized Version, Shakespeare and Milton use desist sparingly for solemn stops; Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines To Desist: to cease from any thing; to stop. The 19th century made cease and desist a doublet formula in injunctions, trademark and nuisance law, freezing desisted in legal boilerplate while conversation moved to stop and quit. Anthropology and history, as in the source sentence, retained desisted for ironic elevation: savages who were once civilized and desisted from it as a nuisance — civilization as a lawsuit one drops.

Today desisted survives in three niches: law (has desisted, shall desist), formal minutes (the committee desisted from further discussion), and literary irony. Corpus frequency is low (Tier 4-5 for the inflected form), but recognition is high because cease-and-desist letters are culturally salient. The evolution shows specialization: from general stand-away to marked formal stop, ceding everyday ground to Germanic stop while keeping Latin dignity for official stops.

Corpus comparison confirms the register split: stop and quit dominate speech by orders of magnitude, while desist clusters in judgments, injunctions and formal minutes, preserving its Latin dignity exactly where authority must sound impersonal and final.
