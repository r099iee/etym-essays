• Rushes: Similar because both are tall, slender wetland plants growing in or near water; differs because rushes have solid, round, soft, pith-filled stems with no joints, while reeds have hollow, jointed stems (and rushes are usually softer and more grass-like in the eye of the botanist).
  • Sedges: Similar because both are wetland grasses-like plants; differs because sedges typically have solid, triangular stems (as in "sedges have edges"), while reeds are round and hollow.
  • Cattails: Similar because both are tall wetland plants; differs because cattails (bulrushes, reed mace) have a distinctive cylindrical brown flower head and flat leaf blades, while reeds carry feathery plumes and hollow stems.
  • Bulrushes: Similar because both name tall marsh plants; differs because bulrushes are usually the cattail or clubrush plants with solid/soft stems, while reeds are true hollow-stemmed grasses.
  • Canes: Similar because both have long, stiff, hollow-ish stems used for support; differs because canes (e.g., bamboo, sugar cane) are thicker, woodier, and often tropical, while reeds are slender, flexible, temperate marsh grasses.
  • Grasses: Similar because both are narrow-leaved, jointed monocots; differs because grasses is the broad botanical family, while reeds specifically denotes the tall, hollow, water-loving members — grasses are to reeds as family is to member.
  • Stalks: Similar because both are long, upright plant stems; differs because a stalk is any plant stem and is a part of the plant, while a reed is a whole plant with a characteristic habitat and structure.
  • Rods: Similar because both are long, thin, straight plant stems; differs because a rod suggests a stiff, cut, straightened stem (a fishing rod, a measuring rod), while a reed is the living, flexible, hollow plant.
  • Wands: Similar because both are long, thin, straight flexible stems; differs because a wand is a cut, trimmed, often magical or ceremonial stick, while a reed is a living wetland plant and its material.
  • Papyrus: Similar because both are tall hollow-stemmed marsh plants used for writing material; differs because papyrus is a specific African sedge (Cyperus papyrus) and the ancient writing surface made from it, while reed is the broader class of hollow-stemmed grasses.
