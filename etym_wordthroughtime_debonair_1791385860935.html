- c. 1200–1400s: 'gentle, meek, mild, courteous' - Borrowed from Old French into Middle English romance and devotional literature; praised humble gentleness, moral docility, and Christian meekness in knights and holy figures.
- 1500s–mid 1600s: 'gracious, pleasant, complaisant' - Transitioned toward secular social ease during the English Renaissance (Spenser, Milton); denoted pleasant manners and gracious sociability, though gradually becoming an archaism.
- Late 1600s–1700s: 'jaunty, cheerful, lively' - Revived in Restoration comedy with sparkling wit; John Dryden and playwrights applied it to high-spirited courtiers, stripping the word of medieval religious humility.
- 19th c.: 'dashing, light-hearted, gallant' - Victorian literature (Scott, Thackeray) consolidated the modern spelling and linked it to aristocratic poise, jaunty confidence, and stylish bearing.
- 20th c.–Present: 'suave, smooth, impeccably stylish' - Solidified around charismatic, tuxedo-clad icons (e.g., Cary Grant, James Bond); denotes effortless cosmopolitan charm, easy confidence, and stylish composure under pressure.
