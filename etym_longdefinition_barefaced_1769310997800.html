barefaced (adjective): shameless, undisguised, brazen, or audaciously open — said of a lie, falsehood, action, or assertion that is carried out with complete, unblushing frankness and without the slightest attempt at concealment, excuse, or disguise. The word also retains, especially in historical or literary register, its literal meaning: having the face uncovered, unmasked, unvarnished, or beardless. Primary sense (modern core)
  • Brazen, flagrant, undisguised — the overwhelmingly dominant modern sense. A "barefaced lie" is a lie told so boldly and openly that the liar does not even bother to craft a cover story or show shame; it is a falsehood so naked in its falsehood that it insults the intelligence of the hearer. The word colours the noun it modifies with moral outrage: a barefaced lie, a barefaced robbery, a barefaced attempt at bribery.
  • Audaciously open about wrongdoing — beyond lying, the adjective can modify any shameless act: "barefaced cheek," "barefaced impudence," "barefaced effrontery." Here the emphasis is less on the act being hidden and more on the agent refusing to disguise or apologise for it, displaying a kind of insolent transparency.
Secondary / literal-historical senses
  • Having the face uncovered — the original, now chiefly archaic sense. In sixteenth-century English a barefaced person was simply one who went about with the face exposed: without a mask, a visor, a protective veil, or (for men) a beard. A "barefaced" soldier fought with the visor of his helmet raised; a "barefaced" actor performed in the open rather than behind a theatrical mask.
  • Beardless, youthful — because a smooth, exposed chin distinguished the young or the clean-shaven from the bearded adult, barefaced could mean "beardless" or "without a moustache," a physical observation with no moral colour at all.
Polysemy and the semantic bridge
  • The historical bridge from literal to moral is the social symbolism of the face and the mask. In early modern Europe, modesty, discretion, and honour were conceived of as a "veil" or "mask" that a decent person wore over their intentions; shame caused the face to "blush" or "fall." To go barefaced — to act with the face naked and the blush absent — was therefore to discard the natural and social covering that signals conscience. A person who could lie barefaced was one in whom the internal mask of shame had been stripped away entirely. The transfer is complete by the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, when "barefaced" is applied to lies, villainies, and audacious impostures.
  • In the theatre this was literalised: Shakespeare's players could act "barefaced" rather than behind masks, and the public stage's open, unapologetic frankness fed the word's slide toward "impudent." By the eighteenth century the moral sense entirely overwhelms the physical one, and "barefaced lie" becomes a fixed collocation of English.
Register, style, and usage notes
  • The word is emphatic and censorious: to call something barefaced is to condemn it while expressing disbelief at its openness. It is most at home in literary prose, journalism, and rhetoric rather than neutral speech.
  • In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice the Bennet daughters assail their father with "barefaced questions" — questions so undisguisedly curious and mercenary that they violate the delicate indirections of polite society. Here the word still means "unashamedly open," bridging the physical and moral senses.
  • It is almost exclusively negative in the modern language; the neutral sense of "with an uncovered face" survives mainly in historical fiction, dressage, or technical description of optics (a "bare-faced" or exposed surface).
