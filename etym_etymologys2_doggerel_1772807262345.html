DOGGEREL has no reconstructable PIE (Proto-Indo-European) root: the word is an English-internal coinage, and its component parts must be traced separately. The attested chain is as follows. - Old English "docga" "dog" (first attested c. 1050 in the Leiden glosses; origin unknown and debated, possibly a diminutive or expressive formation with no secure PIE etymon; it displaces the older native word "hund" in many functions) to Middle English "dogge" "dog" (attested from c. 1150) to Modern English "dog" "domestic canine; also, from the 16th c., a term of contempt for a person or a marker of base/inferior status in compounds such as dog Latin, dog cheap" (c. 16th c.). - Anglo-French / Old French pejorative-diminutive suffix "-erel", "-arel" "small, despicable thing" (attested in Old French from the 12th c.; cf. Old French "couardel" "a little coward") borrowed into Middle English as "-rel", "-erel" (attested in "mongrel", "wastrel", from the 14th c.). - English "doggerel" "comic or irregular verse" (first attested 1630 in the phrase "dogrel" / "dogerel", and in the 1630s-1640s as "doggerel verse"; formed within English as "dog" + "-rel"). The semantics are transparent: verse "worthy of a dog", i.e. base, cur-like, inferior rhyme. to DOGGEREL (modern standard English form, 17th c. to the present). DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The formation from "dog" + "-rel" is the received explanation, but it is not fully certain. Some scholars have connected the word to "dogger" (a wooden-legged person, hence "lame" verse) or to the verb "to dog" (to follow like a dog, hence "dogged"). No PIE root can be invoked for the coinage itself; "docga" itself is of unknown (possibly non-Germanic, possibly expressive) origin, and the earliest attestation of "doggerel" in 1630 leaves a gap in the documented chain that no source can fill with certainty.
