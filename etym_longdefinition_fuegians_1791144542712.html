Fuegians is the collective English ethnonym for the Indigenous peoples of Tierra del Fuego — the windswept archipelago divided today between Chile and Argentina at the foot of South America. In classic 19th- and early-20th-century usage (the sense live in the source passage, drawn from a history of civilisation), it covers principally the maritime canoe peoples, the Yaghan (Yámana) of the southern channels and the Kawésqar (Alacaluf/Alakaluf) of the western straits, and often by extension the terrestrial hunter peoples, the Selk’nam (Ona) of the main island’s steppe and the Haus (Mannekenk) of the far south-east. The passage’s details — eating fish raw from head to tail, killing it with a bite behind the gills, omnivorous foraging — describe the canoe cultures as reported by early voyagers and missionaries.

The term is a straightforward formation: Fuego (from Spanish Tierra del Fuego, “Land of Fire”) + the English ethnic suffix -ian. It is always plural in this use (“the Fuegians”), singular Fuegian for one person or as an adjective (“Fuegian canoes, Fuegian vocabulary”). Capitalisation is standard for an ethnonym. In modern anthropology the blanket term has receded in favour of the peoples’ own names — Yaghan, Kawésqar, Selk’nam, Haush — partly for precision (their languages and lifeways differed sharply: maritime vs. terrestrial) and partly because Victorian writers, including Darwin in The Voyage of the Beagle (1839), used “Fuegian” inside a hierarchy that ranked these peoples as the ‘lowest’ savages, a framing now rejected.

  • Who the word covers: Yaghan/Yámana (Beagle Channel, Cape Horn islands); Kawésqar/Alacaluf (Strait of Magellan, western archipelago); Selk’nam/Ona (Isla Grande steppe, guanaco hunters); Haush/Mannekenk (Mitla peninsula). Early writers lumped; modern scholarship splits.
  • Lifeway behind the passage: the canoe Fuegians lived almost naked in a sub-Antarctic climate, keeping fires (the fuegos Magellan saw) burning even in bark canoes; diet of fish, shellfish, sea mammals, seabirds, fungi, and beached whale — hence the “feast for a week on a whale” and the raw-fish detail. Missionary Thomas Bridges’ Yaghan dictionary (some 32,000 entries) later proved the richness of a culture once dismissed as destitute.
  • Connotation history: 16th–18th c. Spanish fueguinos were distant exotics of travellers’ tales; 1830s–1900s English “Fuegians” became the textbook type-specimen of ‘primitive man’ (Darwin’s famous shock at their ‘wretched’ state); 20th c. the word turns neutral-historical, then semi-obsolete as descendant communities and scholars prefer self-names. Using “Fuegian” today is not a slur, but it marks older literature.
  • Grammar and neighbours: compare Tasmanians, Patagonians, Eskimo/Inuit — the same colonial lumping problem. Adjectival use (“Fuegian ethnography”) survives better than the noun. The singular Fuegian also names the region’s dog? No — the famous “Fuegian dog” (a domesticated culpeo) is usually called just that; the Fuegian snipe and Fuegian steamer-duck keep the adjective alive in ornithology.
  • Why the singular/plural matters: “Fuegian” as adjective (a Fuegian word-list) vs. “the Fuegians” as peoples. The source’s “Though the __________ can cook” needs the plural noun: a whole people, not a language or a duck.
  • Polysemy note: the word has no real secondary senses beyond ethnonym/adjective; its ‘polysemy’ is really referential breadth (which peoples? how many?) plus the shifting moral frame (exotic → ‘lowest savage’ → victims of genocide → revived heritage cultures). The Selk’nam were effectively exterminated in the sheep-farming genocide of the 1880s–1910s; Yaghan and Kawésqar descendant communities persist in Villa Ukika, Puerto Williams, and Puerto Edén.

For the Anki learner, anchor Fuegians as “the Fire-Land people”: Fuego = fire (Magellan’s shore-fires, 1520), -ians = people of. When you meet the word in older histories (Buckle, Darwin, Spencer, Durant), translate it mentally to “Yaghan/Kawésqar/Selk’nam as 19th-century writers lumped them,” and expect raw fish, bark canoes, beachcombed whales, and the lice-soup petite marmite of the very same chapter.
