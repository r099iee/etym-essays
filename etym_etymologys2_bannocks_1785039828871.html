Linear derivation chain for BANNOCKS (single line of descent; a debated origin is noted below): 1) Proto-Celtic *bannā "peak, top, rounded mound" — reconstructed; the semantic metaphor is the swelling, rounded shape of a lump of baked dough rising on the hearth. 2) Scottish Gaelic bonnach "cake, bannock" — attested in Gaelic; a sister formation appears in Old Irish bairgen "bread, loaf," and the dialectal Gaelic form bannach "a cake" is the one closest to the English. 3) Old English bannuc "a bit, a piece, a cake" — recorded in Northumbrian dialect glosses around 1000 AD; generally agreed to be a loan from a Celtic source. 4) Middle English bannok "hearth-baked flat loaf" — 14th c. 5) Scots bannock — the Scots and Northern English form that carried the word into the modern period. 6) Modern English bannock "flat round griddle or hearth cake of coarse meal" — plural BANNOCKS. Debated note: an older rival theory, recorded by early lexicographers, derives the Gaelic word from Latin pānicium "bread-cake" (a derivative of pānis "bread," from PIE *pa- "to protect, feed"). On that account the chain would instead run: PIE *pa- "to protect, feed" → Latin pānis "bread" → Latin pānicium "bread-cake" → (loan) Gaelic bannach "a cake" → Old English bannuc. Both theories converge on the same Old English form; modern scholarship inclines toward a native Celtic word, but the Latin-loan route cannot be excluded.
