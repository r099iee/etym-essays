- 14th c. (Middle English) - Borrowed via Anglo-Norman from Latin *adversarius*; heavily freighted with theological gravity, famously designating Satan (The Adversary / Ha-Satan) and spiritual foes of the soul.
- 15th–16th c. (Early Modern English) - Broadened to legal disputants in English common law and martial opponents on battlefield campaigns, retaining a formal and formidable register.
- 17th–18th c. (Enlightenment & Rhetoric) - Adopted into political philosophy and parliamentary discourse to denote ideological, diplomatic, and polemical antagonists without necessarily implying demonic evil.
- 19th c. (Victorian Literature & Warfare) - Emphasized moral and strategic parity in great geopolitical contests (the Great Game) and epic literary struggles.
- 20th–21st c. (Modern & Contemporary English) - Standardized in international relations (state adversaries), cybersecurity ('adversary simulation'), jurisprudence ('adversarial legal system'), and elite competitive sports.
