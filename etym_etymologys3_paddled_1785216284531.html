PADDLED: from a Greek dinner-plate to a goblin's lake THE UNHISTORIC DISH The biography of paddled begins in the kitchen, not on the water, and it begins in uncertainty. The oldest securely attested stage on the main line is the Greek noun πατάνη (patánē), "a shallow dish or plate," the kind of broad, flat vessel that stood on ancient Mediterranean tables. Its own ancestry is doubtful: the Dutch linguist Robert Beekes argued that patánē belongs to the Pre-Greek substrate — words that Greek took from the older, non-Indo-European population of the Aegean — in which case no Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for the line at all, and none should be forced. This matters, because every later stage of the word's history is a story of shapes: the paddle is the descendant, not of an Indo-European verb of propelling, but of a dish. From Greek the word entered Latin as patina, "a broad shallow pan," the standard cooking vessel of Roman kitchens from the first century BCE onward. One dish, and a whole scattered family: the church plate of the English paten (13th c.) is literally a patina; the kneecap in anatomy is the patella, Latin for "small dish," named in the 1690s for its dish-like shape; the aged green-brown colour of old bronze is patina; the Italian frying pan is still la padella; and the dish-shaped limpet shell is the French patelle. All of these are descendants of a shallow Roman pan — proof of how prolific a single utensil-word can be. THE MEDIEVAL TURN: FROM PAN TO SPADE The decisive step for our word came in Medieval Latin, where the diminutive padella, literally "a small pan," slid from cooking to digging. The bridge was entirely visual: a small spade's blade is a broad, flat, pan-like shape, so padella came to name "a small spade or paddle-shaped tool." This is a textbook shape-transfer — a word moves from one tool to another because the two look alike. English borrowed the tool-word in the fifteenth century as padell, "a small spade," and within the same century the implement, flattened and lengthened, was adapted for water: a padell — now spelled paddle — was also "a short, broad-bladed oar," the flat pan-shaped blade now stroking the surface of a river instead of a field of earth. The semantic sequence dish → spade → oar is a parade example of how tools migrate by shape, and the OED, with its usual caution, adds that the borrowing may instead have come from Latin palla or Old French pale, "spade, shovel," words of obscure origin — so the etymology is honestly recorded as uncertain. THE VERB AND THE STEAM AGE From the noun, English formed the verb: to paddle, "to propel with a paddle," is attested from the seventeenth century, a regular weak verb whose past tense, paddled, is made with the ordinary -ed suffix and rhymes with handle, paddle, saddle. The compounds of the Victorian age then made the word loud, mechanical, and industrial: paddle-wheel (1688), paddle-boat (1787), and paddle-steamer (1843), the flat boards on a rotating wheel that paddle the water as the great side- or stern-wheel turns. Yet the same image of short, repeated, dipping strokes also produced the word's quiet, playful offshoots: children paddle in the sea (the wading sense, 17th c.), a dog paddle is a swimming stroke (1850s), and the American maxim paddle your own canoe (1840s) moralized the motion into a creed of self-reliance. In the same centuries the flat instrument lent its name to punishment: to paddle someone, chiefly in American schools and homes, is to beat them with a flat board — the harshest register the word has ever known, born of nothing more than the shape of the implement. SOUND AND FORM The sound changes along the chain are modest but instructive. The Greek aspirated t in patánē passed into Latin as plain t in patina; the voiced plosive d in the Romance diminutive padella is the regular outcome of intervocalic Latin t (cf. Latin vita > Italian vita with a /v/, and the same voicing that gives French cuiller from Latin coclearia). English took the word with its Romance d and its short a, and the fifteenth-century spelling paddle fixed a short /æ/ in the first syllable, a schwa-laxed second vowel in the modern RP pronunciation /pˈadəld/, and a final -ed pronounced /d/ after the voiced l. No dramatic sound law is involved; the interesting history is entirely lexical and semantic. IN THE HOBBIT When Tolkien writes that Gollum "paddled it quite quietly about the lake," the word carries all of this history at once: the small, primitive, blade-worked craft of the sense's oldest boat usage; the practised silence that the verb has implied since it first named free strokes rather than fixed oars; and, faintly, the childlike wading and duck-like paddling senses, which make Gollum's motion at once innocent and sinister. The dish that became a spade that became an oar finally became the quiet, creeping stroke of a creature in the dark — and the word still means, as it has meant for four centuries, propulsion by short, dipping, unbroken strokes, "never a ripple." The full derivation chain from the uncertain Greek dish to the modern past tense runs: Greek patánē "shallow dish" → Latin patina "pan" → Medieval Latin padella "small pan" → "small spade" → Middle English padell "small spade" (15th c.) → English paddle "short broad-bladed oar" (15th c.) → English paddle "propel with a paddle" (verb, 17th c.) → English paddled (past tense with -ed). Every stage is a real, attested form, and the one genuine gap — the word's origin before Greek — is a gap the evidence asks us to leave open.
