vellum — fine writing material made from the prepared skin of a calf (in strict usage), the term being loosely extended to high-quality parchment from lamb or goat. The hide is soaked in lime, de-haired, stretched taut on a frame, scraped with a crescent knife to an even, smooth, creamy-white surface, and dried under tension; the result is a dense, durable, almost indestructible writing surface that can be scraped and reused, and on which ink sits with a crispness that paper has rarely matched. In the passage, the Earl throws down to the chosen men the traditional scrolls of vellum whose writings permit them to walk the battlements above their cantonment at the full moon of each alternate month — the skin of the law, a material so old in the ceremonial life of Gormenghast that the decree itself seems cut from the animal and handed down intact from an earlier age. The tone is that of immemorial written authority: where paper is disposable, vellum is dynastic — each scroll is a physical membrane of tradition, carrying the Earl's word in the same substance that carried the law codes, charters, and illuminated books of medieval Europe, its pale surface the parchment of command. In modern usage the word has broadened: "vellum" also names fine heavy paper that imitates the skin's surface (vellum paper), tracing vellum in drafting, and the premium animal-skin coverings of deluxe bindings.
  • Vellum senses: (1) strictly, calfskin parchment; (2) loosely, any fine animal-skin writing material; (3) modern, imitation parchment paper and drafting film; (4) bookbinding material for deluxe volumes.
battlements — the notched parapet or defensive wall-top of a castle, fortification, or tower, consisting of a low wall (the parapet) in which alternating solid sections called merlons are separated by open gaps called crenels (or embrasures), through which defenders could shoot at attackers below while remaining protected. The word descends from the vocabulary of fighting: battlements are literally the wall for battle, the fortification built for combat. In the passage the battlements are the walkway above the cantonment along which the chosen carvers are permitted to pace at the full moon of each alternate month — the moonlit skyline of the castle, the liminal place between the inner world of the Earl and the outer world of the carvers, where the honour they covet becomes visible. The word's modern force is almost wholly romantic and medieval: battlements signal "castle," the notched silhouette against the sky, the archer's station of the age of siege; but the same word has spread by extension to any notched or toothlike architectural feature, and to decorative crenellation reproduced on Victorian villas, railway stations, and civic buildings.
  • Battlement senses: (1) the crenellated parapet of a fortress; (2) by extension, any notched or embrasured parapet, decorative or defensive; (3) figurative, the embattled skyline of a castle or stronghold.
cantonment — a permanent or temporary military lodging, station, or quarter; a district or area in which troops are lodged, especially, in the British colonial world, a permanent military station outside a town, with its own barracks, parade ground, and officers' quarters. The word descends from canton, "a corner, a district": a cantonment is literally the corner of the map allotted to the troops, their quartering in their own district. In the passage the cantonment is the settlement of the outer carvers at the foot of the castle wall — the quarter of those excluded from the inner world, whose whole existence is bounded by the wall above which, once a month, they are permitted to walk. The word carries the social geography of the passage: a cantonment is a place of stationing, a permanent camp with its own rules and its own place in the hierarchy — the corner of the world to which a people is assigned.
  • Cantonment senses: (1) the quartering of troops in a district; (2) the permanent military station itself; (3) in the Indian subcontinent, a designated military station or cantonment town (as in the "Cantonment" districts of cities such as Bangalore and Kanpur); (4) by extension, any assigned quarter or lodging.
