King Harold is the conventional English name for Harold II (Old English Harold Godwinson, c. 1022 – 14 October 1066), the last crowned Anglo-Saxon king of England. He reigned for less than ten months, from 6 January to 14 October 1066, and his defeat and death at the Battle of Hastings ended the Anglo-Saxon monarchy and inaugurated the Norman conquest. The phrase “King Harold” is thus both a personal name and a historical hinge: it names a man, a dynasty, a battle and the end of an era.

The man and the family

Harold was the second son of Godwin, Earl of Wessex, and Gytha Thorkelsdóttir, a Danish noblewoman. The Godwinsons were the most powerful family in England after the crown: Godwin had risen under Cnut and survived the turmoil of 1051–52, and his daughter Edith married King Edward the Confessor. Harold became Earl of East Anglia in 1045, succeeded his father as Earl of Wessex in 1053, and by the 1060s was the effective leader of the English aristocracy.

  • Earldoms — Wessex and Herefordshire; after the revolt against his brother Tostig in 1065 he exercised wide authority in the north through his alliance with Edwin of Mercia and Morcar of Northumbria.
  • Military reputation — his campaign against Gruffydd ap Llywelyn of Wales in 1063, alongside Tostig, was a striking success and made him the obvious war-leader of the kingdom.
  • Family — his handfast wife Edith the Fair (Edith Swanneck) bore him several sons; his sister Edith of Wessex was Edward the Confessor’s queen.

1064: the oath

According to Norman sources, Harold was shipwrecked in Ponthieu in 1064 and handed over to Duke William, with whom he stayed for some months. He is said to have sworn an oath on relics to support William’s claim to the English throne. English sources are silent, and the Bayeux Tapestry famously shows the oath being taken on a reliquary. Whether Harold was a free guest, a hostage or a diplomatic envoy is disputed; what is certain is that the story became the legal foundation of William’s case that Harold was a perjurer.

Accession, January 1066

Edward the Confessor died on 5 January 1066. The witan chose Harold, and he was crowned at Westminster on 6 January, probably by Archbishop Ealdred of York, since Stigand of Canterbury was not in full papal favour. His claim rested on his position as the leading magnate, on Edward’s deathbed designation in some accounts, and on the fact that the royal house was exhausted: Edgar the Ætheling was a boy, and Harold was the only adult with the means to defend the kingdom.

1066: three invasions

Harold faced threats from three directions at once:

  • Norway — Harald Hardrada, supported by Harold’s exiled brother Tostig, invaded the north in September. They defeated the northern earls at Gate Fulford on 20 September.
  • Normandy — William landed at Pevensey on 28 September with a fleet and an army drawn from Normandy, Brittany, Flanders and beyond.
  • Denmark and the north — a standing threat of Scandinavian intervention, which is why the northern campaign could not be ignored.

Harold marched north in extraordinary time, surprised the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, and killed both Hardrada and Tostig. Three days later William was ashore in Sussex. Harold force-marched south, gathered what troops he could around London, and offered battle near the coast.

Hastings, 14 October 1066

The English army took position on Senlac Hill, a ridge about seven miles north-west of Hastings. Harold’s housecarls formed a shield wall; the battle lasted all day and was decided by the exhaustion of the English line and by feigned Norman retreats that drew individual defenders downhill. Harold was killed in the final fighting. The Bayeux Tapestry shows a figure with an arrow near the eye, long read as Harold’s death-wound; the identification is plausible but debated, and the earliest literary accounts simply say he was killed.

Death, burial and the arrow

The story that Harold was struck in the eye by an arrow appears in twelfth-century chronicles — Henry of Huntingdon and, in a poetic version, Baudri of Bourgueil — and became canonical through the tapestry and later art. Later tradition adds that the body was so mutilated that it could be recognised only by Edith Swanneck, who identified it by marks known to her. Harold’s body was eventually buried at Waltham Abbey in Essex, a house he had founded and enriched; the exact site was lost at the Dissolution.

The legend of survival

Because no tomb survived and because the defeat was so complete, a folklore grew that Harold had not died but had escaped, lived as a hermit and died in obscurity. Versions place him in Chester, in Devon or in Germany. There is no contemporary evidence for any of them, but the legend is revealing: it expresses the difficulty of accepting that a crowned king could simply vanish into a Norman battlefield grave.

Historiography and memory

Norman writers presented Harold as a perjurer whose defeat was a divine judgement; English writers from the twelfth century onward treated him as a legitimate but doomed king. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (D and E versions) records the battle with restraint and laments the loss of “the best men of England.” Victorian historians made Harold a national hero, and the 900th anniversary in 1966 turned “King Harold” into a heritage brand. The phrase now appears in pub names, battle re-enactments, school textbooks and the title of every book about 1066 that wants a human face for the catastrophe.

What “King Harold” means today

  • The historical man — Harold Godwinson, the last crowned Anglo-Saxon king, a capable ruler destroyed by an unlucky conjunction of invasions.
  • The type of the lost king — in literature and popular history, the phrase evokes a doomed last stand, the end of an old England and the arrival of a new ruling class.
  • The name — Harold remains an ordinary English given name, and “King Harold” is a fixed collocation that distinguishes the king from the many other Harolds of history.
  • The national symbol — in the older patriotic narrative, Harold stands for English courage; in the newer one, he stands for the contingency of 1066, a battle that could have gone otherwise.

Semantic bridges

The phrase began as a title plus a personal name, became a chronicle formula (“Harold king”, “King Harold”), and then a shorthand for the whole catastrophe. The development is typical of royal names: once a reign ends badly, the name ceases to be merely a label and becomes an argument about legitimacy, courage and providence. To say “King Harold” is already to take a position on whether 1066 was a tragedy, a judgement or a conquest.
