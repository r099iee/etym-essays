The story of GLINT begins with the North Germanic languages rather than with a securely reconstructable Indo-European root, and the honest account of its pre-history must say so. The word enters English as a loanword from Old Norse, part of the great wave of Scandinavian vocabulary that washed into the northern and eastern dialects of England after the Viking settlements of the ninth and tenth centuries. That wave carried everyday words (sky, window, egg, knife) and a smaller set of vividly physical and sensory terms, and glinta — "to gleam, to sparkle, to shine dimly" — belongs to the latter group: a quick, glancing, half-casual light, the kind of light that catches the eye for an instant and is gone.
  • Germanic background: Old Norse glinta sits in a cluster of Germanic light-verbs built on a base *gl- meaning "to shine", which also gives gleam, glow, glimmer, glitter, glisten, and glossy across the West Germanic languages. The vowel variant glinten/glenten in Middle English suggests the Norse base itself carried an ablaut alternation (*glint-/*glent-), a feature typical of the second- and third-class Germanic verb series. Beyond that cluster, however, the trail goes cold: a PIE root such as *ghel- "to shine" (the source of gold, glass, yellow) is often invoked to unify the Germanic *gl- words, but the derivational bridge from *ghel- to a nasalized *glint- is not attested and remains speculation.
  • Entry into English: the earliest English records are late thirteenth and early fourteenth century. The Cursor Mundi (c. 1300), a northern verse life of Christ, uses the verb glent, and northern texts keep the word alive through the Middle English period. The borrowing is characteristically Norse in its phonology and in its northern first attestation; by the fifteenth century it is fully naturalized, and the noun "a glint" begins to appear in the sixteenth century.
  • Sound changes: the loan is phonologically light — Norse glinta with its initial stop+liquid cluster and short nasal vowel passes into English with only the loss of the final -a and a settling of the vowel (glint /ɡlɪnt/). There is no dramatic shift; the word's history is more semantic than phonological, which is typical of words borrowed with a ready-made sensory meaning.
  • Semantic drift: the original sense was purely "to gleam or sparkle" of light on an object, but by Middle English a second sense had attached itself: "to cast a quick sidelong glance." This link between light and sight is ancient (compare glance itself, from Old French, originally "to strike off a blow" and then "to shoot a look"); in glint the two senses — a darting light and a darting look — run side by side for centuries, and it is this old verb sense that underwrites the modern figurative "a glint in the eye." In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word settles into the register of poetry and of hawk-and-steel imagery: Shakespeare and his contemporaries use glint (and the noun glint) for small hard flashes of light on armour, water, and eyes.
  • Literary attestations: after a period of colloquial and dialectal use, the word is revived and sharpened by the nineteenth-century Romantics and by later writers of fantasy and adventure. It appears in Scott's verse and prose, in Victorian sensation fiction for the glint of a knife or a madman's eye, and it becomes a stock word of the popular adventure register — the glint of the moon on steel — precisely the collocation Tolkien deploys in the passage from The Hobbit. Tolkien, a professional philologist, chooses the word with deliberate archaism and precision: the glint is the one thing the darkness cannot hide.
  • Modern development: in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the noun has widened into figurative and technical uses. "A glint in one's eye" is a fixed idiom for sudden intent; "a glint of hope" or "of humour" generalizes the metaphor to any faint, momentary sign; and in optics, astronomy, and military surveillance the word has been adopted as a technical term for specular reflection seen as a momentary bright point — the glint of a satellite, the glint of an aircraft, the glint off a body of water in remote sensing imagery. Computer graphics has likewise borrowed the term for the small specular highlights that read as wetness or metal.
  • Socio-cultural valence: throughout, the word carries coolness and hardness. It is the light of steel, ice, glass, and watchful eyes — never of warmth or of festivity. Its briefness makes it the perfect sign of danger and of betrayal by light in darkness, which is why it recurs in thriller, horror, and heroic fantasy alike: a glint is what the darkness fails to conceal.
The overall arc, then, is a Norse sensory verb, borrowed into northern English in the fourteenth century, keeping its light sense and adding an eye sense, settling as a precise noun in the sixteenth century, revived and hardened by the Romantics and their successors, and finally widened in our own time into figurative idiom and technical jargon — while always remaining, at its core, the small hard flash that gives a hidden thing away.
