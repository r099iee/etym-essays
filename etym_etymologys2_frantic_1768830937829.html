PIE *gʷʰren- “mind, thought, seat of feeling” (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE; attested branch almost confined to Greek) → Greek φρήν (phrēn) “diaphragm, midriff; mind, heart, seat of feeling” (Homeric, c. 8th c. BCE) → Greek φρενῖτις (phrenitis) “inflammation of the mind, brain-fever, delirium” (Hippocratic medicine, c. 5th–4th c. BCE) → Greek φρενητικός (phrenētikos) “mad, suffering from inflammation of the mind; frenzied” (classical period) → Latin phreneticus “insane, frenzied” (loanword, c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) → Old French frenetique / frentik “mad, raving” (c. 12th c.) → Middle English frentik / frenetik “insane, mad, raving” (14th c.) → English frantic “wildly excited with fear, anxiety, or grief; frenzied in haste” (the a-variant settles in by the 15th c.) ◄ TARGET Note on the PIE stage: the root *gʷʰren- is reconstructed chiefly on the evidence of Greek; modern scholars caution that the family may have no certain cognates outside Greek, and the deep PIE link is therefore debated (accepted in Pokorny’s comparative dictionary, treated warily by more recent work). Note on the doublet: English frenetic preserves the older spelling and descends from the same chain; the two forms split the sense-field, frantic carrying the common emotional senses and frenetic the learned, activity-centred ones.
