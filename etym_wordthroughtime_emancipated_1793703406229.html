- Late 16th Century (Legal/Roman Law) - Primarily referred to the legal act of freeing a son from paternal authority (patria potestas) or a slave from servitude. Connotation: Formal, legal, specific to Roman civil law, denoting a change in legal status from dependent to independent.
- 17th-18th Century (Broader Legal/Social) - Extended to the general act of freeing from legal, political, or social restraint, though still often within a legal framework. Connotation: Still formal, but gaining broader application beyond Roman law, often in philosophical discussions of liberty.
- 19th Century (Social Justice/Abolition) - Became a central term in movements for social justice, most notably the abolition of slavery (e.g., Emancipation Proclamation) and the granting of rights to religious minorities (e.g., Catholic Emancipation). Connotation: Powerful, revolutionary, associated with fundamental human rights, liberation from systemic oppression, and a shift towards equality.
- Late 19th - 20th Century (Women's Rights/Intellectual Freedom) - Applied extensively to the women's suffrage movement and broader feminist movements, signifying freedom from patriarchal norms and traditional gender roles. Also used for intellectual liberation from dogma or superstition. Connotation: Progressive, modern, challenging societal norms, self-reliant, often associated with strong, independent women.
- 21st Century (Contemporary Usage) - Continues to be used in all previous senses, particularly in discussions of social equality, individual autonomy, and breaking free from restrictive traditions. Connotation: Positive, empowering, signifying personal and collective freedom, often with a nuance of having overcome significant barriers.
