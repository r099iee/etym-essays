The history of abroad is the history of an English doorway opening wider and wider. It begins as a small domestic compound: the preposition a-, worn down from Old English an and on, fused to the noun broad in its old sense of an open expanse. To be abrood around the year 1300 was to be out in the broad — outside the house, under no roof, exposed to weather and neighbours. Charters and romances use it for anyone who has stepped past the threshold: travellers benighted, lovers meeting, labourers in the fields.

The great widening came with England’s widening world. As merchants pushed into the Baltic and the Levant, as pilgrims sailed for Compostela and Jerusalem, outside silently redrew its boundary from the parish to the nation. By the mid-15th century the foreign sense is firmly attested: abroad now means across the sea, in strange countries, among foreign tongues. The Wars of the Roses exiles, the Tudor ambassadors, the Elizabethan sea-dogs — all of them go abroad, and the word absorbs their glamour and their danger at once.

Stages of the journey:

  • Old English seedbed (before 1100): the two halves live apart — on as preposition and brād as adjective meaning wide and flat. No compound yet, but the collocation on brād, out in the open, is the natural ancestor.
  • Early Middle English fusion (c. 1300): abrood and abrod appear meaning out of doors and away from home. The vowel spellings wobble for two centuries before settling.
  • Late medieval widening (15th c.): the foreign sense arrives; Caxton-era prose can send a merchant or an army abroad, and readers understand another country, not merely the garden.
  • Early modern flowering (16th–17th c.): the Authorised Version fixes the circulatory sense for all time — rumours, voices, and flames spread abroad — while to walk abroad becomes the poets’ favourite periphrasis for taking the air, from Shakespeare to Milton.
  • Imperial heyday (18th–19th c.): the Grand Tour makes going abroad a gentleman’s education; the empire makes it a career. Travel writing, from Smollett to Stevenson, carries the word to every corner it names.
  • Modern democracy of distance (20th c. onward): aviation and mass tourism put abroad within everyone’s reach — cheap flights abroad, studying abroad — while sport adds the away-fixture sense and politics adds the suspicious money from abroad.

Phonetically the word barely moved: the long oa of brood shortened and settled into the modern abroad, rhyming with road and toad, its stress fixed on the second syllable. Semantically it travelled further than almost any compound of its size — from the farmyard to the foreign office — yet every modern sense still answers to the original picture: something that has left its enclosure and is now out in the broad world, whether that something is a traveller, a rumour, or a latecomer arriving after dark.
