The biography of colophon rises from one of the most basic images of Indo-European speech: height. The reconstructed root *kel- meant “to rise, to be prominent, to be tall,” and it left a rich scatter of descendants across the family. In the Germanic branch it produced the word for hill — Old English hyll, Dutch heuvel, German Hügel, our English hill. In Latin it generated celsus, “high, lofty,” collis, “hill,” and the pair columen / culmen, “top, summit, pillar”: from culmen English gets culminate (“to reach the top”), from columen come column and columna (the pillar that supports the roof), and from excellere, “to rise above” (built on the same base with the prefix ex-), come excel, excellent, and excellence. In Greek the same root produced kolṓnē, “hill, mound,” and, from it, kolophṓn, “summit, peak, top.” Already in Greek the word was moving from geography to rhetoric: to “set the colophon on a matter” (epitheinai kolophōna) was to put the finishing touch on it, the capstone that completes a work, just as the last stone completes a tower.

• The earliest attested sense of kolophṓn is topographical, “summit, peak”; the phrase with epitithénai (“to put on”) is the pivot on which the whole later history turns, because it converts a word of mountains into a word of completion. Aristotle’s idiom “the colophon of the war” and similar expressions show that the transfer was already natural in classical Greek.
• Latin borrowed the word as colophōn, “a finishing stroke, a conclusion,” and this sense governed the word’s long history in the world of books; no significant sound change reshaped the Greek form, so the word’s drama is entirely semantic rather than phonetic.

In the age of handwritten manuscripts, long before printed title pages existed, a scribe would close his work with a brief inscription at the very end of the codex recording the title of the text, the name of the scribe, the place and date of copying, and often a prayer of gratitude — Laus Deo, “praise to God,” a formula that survives in the pious closures of old books. This closing note was naturally called the colophon, the “finishing stroke” of the work, and it was the only formal identification a manuscript carried. When printing arrived in the fifteenth century, the early printers inherited the custom: the first printed books — including the Gutenberg Bible and the Mainz Psalter — ended with a colophon naming the printer, the place, and the date of the edition. It is in this exact sense that English adopted the word: the Oxford English Dictionary records colophon from 1774, when antiquarian scholars of incunabula needed a precise term for the printer’s closing note.

The great cultural shift came with the rise of the title page in the sixteenth century. As publishers began to identify their books at the front rather than the back, the colophon’s identifying function was displaced — but not lost. It migrated from the end of the book to the beginning, and from a sentence to an image: the printer’s device, the emblematic mark (Aldus Manutius’s dolphin-and-anchor, Plantin’s golden compass, the Elzevir owl) that stood for the press and its values. By the twentieth century the device had become the publisher’s emblem, a registered trademark like the Modern Library’s torchbearer, and the word colophon had acquired its modern sense — a pictorial badge of the imprint on the title page or spine.

Two further strands deserve note. First, the “colophon page”: the modern bibliographic note, usually at the end of the book, recording the typeface, paper, and design of the edition — a direct, unbroken descendant of the scribe’s signed farewell, and a favourite of fine presses. Second, with characteristic bookish humour, the word has been borrowed into computing for the boot-time identification banner of a computer or console: the machine’s own little colophon, printed when it “comes to life.” The chain is one of the cleanest in the language: a hill in Greek becomes a summit, a summit becomes a finishing touch, a finishing touch becomes a scribe’s closing note, a closing note becomes a printer’s emblem, and an emblem becomes a trademark and a computer banner — five thousand years from mountain to microprocessor, with the idea of “crowning” intact at every step.
