prosaic in the source passage — "Falstaff is likewise only Falstaff; the classical typology to which his sort once belonged has been swallowed up in a prosaic theory of the world, along with all thoughts of hierarchic grandeur." — denotes a disenchanted, secularized, and stubbornly realistic worldview. In this literary-critical context (drawing on Hegelian aesthetics and theories of the novel such as those of Georg Lukács and Mikhail Bakhtin), the "prosaic theory of the world" refers to the emergence of modern bourgeois realism, where transcendent cosmological hierarchies, epic heroism, and mythic archetypes dissolve into the messy, contingent, and earthbound realities of everyday material existence. Falstaff is no longer a symbolic Vice or mythic clown from a grand cosmic hierarchy; he is reduced to a flesh-and-blood, workaday mortal living in an unheroic, practical, and prosaic reality.

In standard usage, prosaic functions as an evocative adjective describing anything that lacks poetic flair, imaginative elevation, emotional splendor, or aesthetic inspiration. It designates that which is straightforwardly ordinary, dull, matter-of-fact, mundane, or pedestrian. While it originally arose as a strictly neutral technical term meaning "composed in or pertaining to prose" (as opposed to verse), the cultural elevation of poetry during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries caused prosaic to undergo a decisive pejorative and stylistic shift, coming to mean everything that prose was accused of being: uninspired, literal-minded, plodding, routine, and devoid of spiritual or romantic grandeur.

  • Literal-Minded and Commonplace (The Primary Modern Sense): Describing everyday thoughts, objects, events, or routines that are thoroughly ordinary, unglamorous, and unadorned (e.g. "the poet struggled with the prosaic details of paying monthly utility bills"; "beneath the candidate's lofty rhetoric lay a prosaic policy agenda"). The bridge to prose is direct: just as prose follows the linear, unmetered syntax of daily conversation, prosaic reality is flat, factual, and devoid of lyrical heights.
  • Literary, Aesthetic, and Philosophical Realism (The Disenchanted World): In aesthetic philosophy, literary criticism, and sociology, prosaic describes the secularized, post-mythic condition of modern life (Hegel's "Prosa der Verhältnisse" or "prose of relations"). Here, grand epic destiny gives way to the novel's domain of domestic drama, social negotiation, economic calculations, and psychological complexity.
  • Unimaginative and Pedestrian Style: In literary and rhetorical criticism, applied directly to writing, speech, or artistic composition that is dry, plodding, mechanically literal, and completely devoid of metaphoric richness or emotional resonance (e.g. "a prosaic summary of a deeply passionate romantic affair").
  • Pragmatic and Utilitarian Focus: In psychology, governance, and daily life, describing a mindset or temperament that is strictly practical, commonsensical, and unconcerned with visionary ideals, artistic refinement, or spiritual aspirations (e.g. "she approached the architectural design with a prosaic focus on cost per square foot rather than aesthetic harmony").
  • Technical and Historic Literary Sense (Pertaining to Prose): In classical philology and early modern literary scholarship, used neutrally to classify non-metrical texts or prose compositions (e.g. "the manuscript contains both poetic fragments and lengthy prosaic chronicles"). While mostly historical today, this sense remains active in formal stylistic analysis.
  • Nuanced Registers and Collocations: prosaic frequently collocates with nouns emphasizing reality, details, and function: prosaic reality, prosaic details, prosaic concerns, prosaic existence, prosaic explanation, and prosaic tasks. It contrasts sharply with lyrical, poetic, romantic, sublime, and transcendent.

Stylistically, prosaic is an educated, elegant Latinate adjective. It provides a more refined, philosophically resonant critique of dullness than crude synonyms like boring or dull. It diagnoses a specific kind of flatness: the flatness that comes from being entirely confined to the factual, practical, and earthbound dimensions of reality.
