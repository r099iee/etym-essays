1. The imitative wind-root. Wuther, the heart of the compound, belongs to the oldest stratum of northern English weather-vocabulary: words that do not descend from a documented classical root but that mimic the sound of the wind itself. In dialect speech a 'wuthering' wind is one that roars, buffets, and seems to shake the world; the very shape of the word — the voiceless w, the breathy -uth-, the final rhotic rattle — behaves like an onomatopoeia, and etymologists accordingly treat the family as imitative or sound-symbolic rather than as a regular Indo-European lexeme with a single reconstructible PIE ancestor. 2. The northern dialect habitat. The word has always been local. Its recorded life runs through the dialect speech of Yorkshire and the Pennine dales, where exposed valleys funnel the wind and make 'wuther' a practical, everyday verb. Such words survive in dialect glossaries, in dialect novels, and in the mouth of the moorland landscape itself. It is precisely this regional texture that made wuther attractive to two writers, a century apart, who cared about the English of particular places. 3. Brontë and the national leap (1847). Emily Brontë's choice of Wuthering Heights as her title carried the dialect weather-sense into national literature. The 'wuthering' of the heights is not a mere mood-word; it is a precise meteorological description of a house standing exposed to fierce winds, and the word brings with it the whole economy of northern exposedness — harsh weather, harsh manners, energies that cannot be kept indoors. In Brontë the word still means weather; but its cultural temperature rises, and it begins to connote wildness, extremity, and the sublime. 4. Tolkien's philology and the coinage (1937). A century later J. R. R. Tolkien, professor of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford and a West Midland philologist steeped in dialect, pressed the old prefix be- onto wuther to form bewuther: 'to put someone thoroughly into a wuthered state.' The prefix is the same intensifier that gives English befuddle, bewilder, beset, and belabour; it is of PIE descent (through *h₁ópi or *ambhi to Germanic *bi and Old English be-) and in Old English formed verbs of encompassing action. In The Hobbit, Bilbo — swept into 'the most awkward Wednesday he ever remembered,' apologizing to a dozen angry dwarves — is described as 'altogether bewildered and __________ed.' Whether Tolkien was reviving an obscure attested word or coining afresh on the model of dialect, the formation is transparent English morphology on a regional wind-word, and it is deliberately chosen as the partner of bewildered. 5. Semantic drift: from weather to the mind. The evolution of bewuther exemplifies a classic drift from the physical to the psychological:
  • Stage one (weather): wind blows with a dull roaring sound — a 'wuthering' wind or 'wuthering' heights.
  • Stage two (body): a person caught in such wind is buffeted, breathless, and deafened — physically wuthered.
  • Stage three (mind): the same buffeting is transferred metaphorically to the mind — the person is bewuthered, i.e., flustered, scattered, and discomposed by a 'storm' of activity.
  • Stage four (character): the word becomes a name for a comic-vivid condition of being overwhelmed, at home in Tolkien's domestic-and-heroic register.
This trajectory mirrors the older history of bewilder, which moved from 'to lose in a wilderness' to 'to confuse.' Where bewilder travels from geography to the mind, bewuther travels from meteorology to the body-and-nerves; one is a map without bearings, the other a gale in the face. 6. Sound and structure. The compound preserves the sound-shape of its parts: the weak-stressed intensifying prefix be- is followed by the heavy, roaring wuth- syllable, and the final -er is the ordinary English agent/infinitive ending. In the participle bewuthered the stress falls on the root syllable, keeping the word close to its spoken dialect life. Tolkien, a connoisseur of such phonetic textures, chose a word that sounds like what it means. 7. Socio-cultural factors and afterlife. The word's survival is a small monument to two forces in English literary history: the nineteenth-century romantic valorization of northern landscape and dialect, and the twentieth-century philological revival in which writers like Tolkien treated regional vocabulary as a living resource rather than an embarrassment. Bewuthered is today a rare, treasured, essentially Tolkienian word, cited by readers of The Hobbit and by historians of English lexicography as proof that a dialect wind-word, given the right literary setting, can blow clean across the national language. The pairing with bewildered in the awkward-Wednesday sentence is deliberate craft: Bilbo is both mapless and winded, lost in a mental wilderness and blasted by a domestic gale of dwarves, bells, and anger — and the word's etymological honesty (dialect wind, not fake PIE) is part of its fitness, for it feels spoken, local, and weather-true inside a book that loves the English of particular places.
