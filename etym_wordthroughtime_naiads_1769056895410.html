Naiads
- 16th century - English first takes up naiad from Renaissance Latin and French as a learned, classicising ornament: a fresh-water nymph of Greek religion, catalogued in mythographies and translations, one localised species of nymph set against the Nereids, Oceanids, and Dryads.
- 17th-18th centuries - the word remains a scholarly and poetic term for the classical guardians of springs, fountains, wells, streams, and pools; a proper name, usually capitalised, dwelling in the fixed pairings "Nymphs and Naiads" and "Naiads and Dryads."
- Romantic era - Milton, Keats, and Shelley press the Naiads into pastoral and elegiac verse as figures of luminous, innocent nature; the classical deity softens into a wistful symbol of enchanted, prelapsarian landscape - water as a presence you could love and be loved by.
- 19th-20th centuries - the name migrates into science: entomologists call the aquatic nymph stage of dragonflies and damselflies the naiad stage, malacologists loosely call the fresh-water mussels of the family Unionidae "naiads," and in 1989 astronomers name a small inner moon of Neptune Naiad.
- Contemporary - in Narnia (1952) the Naiads return as gentle, personable "nice people" of the streams, a symbol of a lost golden age of talking beasts and kind waters; today the plural still signals nostalgia for enchanted nature more than a catalogue entry for a classical deity.
