- Late 17th c. (1680s–1700s): 'at the middle of a vessel' - Coined during the expansion of the Royal Navy and nautical manuals; described steering positions, cargo stowage, and gun deck layout between stem and stern.
- 18th c.: 'tactical naval target' - Rose to prominence in combat narratives; aiming cannons 'amidships' sought to breach the enemy's waterline, destroy masts, or detonate powder magazines.
- 19th c.: 'helm command & mechanical center' - Standardized in steam and sail navigation ('helm amidships'); engine rooms and heavy boilers were placed amidships for stability.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c.: 'anatomical and comic slang' - Jocularly extended into civilian British and American English (Dickens, Wodehouse, naval veterans) to mean hitting someone squarely in the stomach, waistline, or solar plexus.
- Mid-20th c.–Present: 'general metaphor for central vulnerability' - Preserved in modern maritime operations and naval architecture, while colloquially used for catastrophic T-bone vehicle collisions or damaging blows to an organization's central core.
