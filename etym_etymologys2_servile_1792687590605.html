Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*ser-w-
"to watch over, protect, guard"
(root of Latin servare 'to preserve, keep')
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*serwo-
"guardian, keeper; captive, slave"
↓
Old / Classical Latin (c. 3rd–1st c. BCE)
servus
"slave, bondman, servant"
(traditionally glossed as captive whose life was spared)
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
servīlis
"of a slave; slavish, groveling, servile"
(formed from servus + suffix -īlis 'pertaining to')
↓
Old French (14th c.)
servil
"subservient, slavish; subject to bondage"
↓
Middle English (late 14th c.)
servile
"pertaining to serfdom; slavishly submissive"
(attested in Trevisa and Wycliffite writings)
↓
Modern English (16th c.–present)
servile
"abjectly submissive, fawning; slavish"

Note on historical debate: Roman jurists, including Justinian in the Institutes and Florentinus in the Digesta, derived servus from servare ('to preserve, keep alive'), asserting that victorious military commanders sold war captives into bondage, thereby preserving (*servare*) rather than slaughtering them (*servi ex eo appellati sunt, quod imperatores captivos vendere iubent ac per hoc servare nec occidere solent*). Modern Indo-European philologists (such as Ernout-Meillet and Michiel de Vaan) note that while servus and servare both descend from the PIE root *\*ser-w-* ('to watch over, protect'), the ancient Roman juridical explanation was an ingenious post-facto folk etymology and moral rationalization intended to justify the violent institution of chattel slavery.
