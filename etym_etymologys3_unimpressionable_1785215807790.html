The word unimpressionable is a modern English construction: a negative prefix fixed to the adjective impressionable, which is itself a nineteenth-century formation built on the noun impression. The underlying image is ancient and physical. Latin premere meant 'to press,' and from it the Romans formed imprimere, 'to press into, to stamp,' combining the prefix in- 'into' with the verb of pressing; the past participle was impressus. The literal act was the pressing of a seal or stamp into a soft material — wax, clay, lead — so as to leave a mark, and Latin impressio named both the act of stamping and the mark left behind. Old French carried the word into English as impression in the fourteenth century, still fully physical: an impression was a mark made by pressure, exactly as a coin is 'struck' or a page is 'printed.' The related Latin imprimere in its printing sense lies behind imprimatur, 'let it be printed,' and behind the whole vocabulary of the press.

The decisive semantic turn came with the empiricist philosophy of the seventeenth century. For John Locke, the newborn mind was a tabula rasa, a blank tablet or sheet of wax, on which experience wrote; ideas entered as 'impressions' made upon the senses, and the whole contents of the mind were such impressed marks. Aristotle had already used the same metaphor — the mind receiving forms 'as a seal-ring is impressed upon wax' (De Anima ii.12) — but it was Locke and his eighteenth-century successors who made the wax-and-seal model the foundation of psychology, and David Hume who built his entire epistemology on 'impressions' and their faded copies, 'ideas.'

The family that grew from the metaphor is large:

  • Impress (verb, 'to mark by pressure; to affect deeply') and imprint (from Old French emprent) both keep the physical act; press itself descends from Latin pressare and Old French presser.
  • Impression developed the psychological sense — 'the effect produced on the mind' — from the Lockean seal, and by the nineteenth century an 'impression' of a person or performance was a matter of effect rather than of stamped marks.
  • Impressionable is recorded in English in 1836 (with French impressionnable running in parallel): a person whose mind is like soft wax, easily stamped by the emotions, opinions, and fashions of the surrounding world.
  • The negative unimpressionable was formed at once on this model in the early nineteenth century — the very decades of James Mill's associationist psychology and of John Stuart Mill's autobiography — and it is the word that the biographical tradition applies to the father.

The nineteenth-century context gives the word its sharpest point. James Mill was a convinced associationist: he taught, in the Analysis of the Phenomena of the Human Mind, that all mental life is built up from impressions and their associations, so that education and environment literally form the mind. To call such a man unimpressionable is a deliberate paradox — the psychologist of impressions is himself the man on whom no impression sticks. The biographer's word turns the doctrine back on its author: the wax that he described as receiving every stamp is, in his own case, already hard. The sense has held steady ever since: in psychology, in literary biography, and in aesthetic criticism the word names a character, mind, or audience that resists being shaped by feeling or opinion, and the image of the wax that will not take the seal keeps the word precise. Its register is formal and slightly dated; in contemporary speech 'thick-skinned', 'unmoved', or 'hardened' have largely taken its place, which is why the word, when it does appear, carries a deliberate, almost clinical dignity.
