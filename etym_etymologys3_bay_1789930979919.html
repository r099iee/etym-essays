Bay in the horse-colour sense descends from Latin badius meaning chestnut-brown, said especially of horses, first securely attested in the 4th-century agricultural writer Palladius (Opus agriculturae), who recommends equus badius among approved colours. De Vaan (Etymological Dictionary of Latin) lists badius as of unknown origin with no secure Proto-Indo-European etymology; Pokorny offers no convincing root, and suggestions of an Oscan or other Italic substrate remain conjectural. The OED and Etymonline agree: the deeper ancestry is obscure and must be stated as unknown rather than invented. What is certain is the Romance transmission: Latin badius survives in Italian baio, Spanish bayo, Portuguese baio and Old French bai (masculine) / baie, baye (feminine), all meaning bay-coloured (of horses).

  • Latin badius to Old French bai / baie: stable technical term. In late antique and medieval horse trade, badius is a dealer’s adjective, not poetic colour. Sound change is regular Gallo-Romance: intervocalic -d- weakens and falls (badius > *baju > bai), final -us lost, feminine formed with -e. Anglo-Norman charters and farriery treatises use bai, bay for horses owed as renders. Meaning stays narrowly equine; Old French does not call cloth or hair bai except by extension from horses.
  • Middle English borrowing c.1300: bai, baye, bay. English horsemen borrowing Norman stable vocabulary take bai alongside sorel, liard (grey), morele (black). First English attestations (c.1300–1350, in romances and the Ayenbite of Inwyt) already mean a reddish-brown horse with dark extremities. Spelling wavers bai, baye, beye before settling on bay in the 15th century under the influence of the other homograph bay (inlet). Pronunciation follows the Great Vowel Shift: Middle English /bai/ becomes modern /beɪ/.
  • Early modern standardisation: stud books and colour discipline. From Elizabethan cavalry musters through 18th-century racing calendars (Weatherby’s General Stud Book from 1791) bay becomes a controlled term opposed to brown, black, chestnut, chesnut-bay, dun, grey, roan. Farriers’ manuals define it by the black-points test still taught today: if mane, tail and legs below the knee are black, the horse is bay; if they are red, it is chestnut. Shakespeare uses it familiarly (“my bay courser”), and dialect keeps compounds like bayard (a bay horse, then a proper name for any horse, as in Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales).
  • 19th-century literary naturalism: Tolstoy’s gnedaya rendered as bay. Russian гнедой denotes exactly the agouti bay phenotype and occupies the same basic-colour slot as English bay. Constance Garnett, Louise Maude and later translators therefore render гнедая кобыла as bay mare without gloss. In Strider the colour individualises a suffering animal among a depersonalised herd, a standard Tolstoyan device: plain stable lexis carries moral weight.
  • Modern genetics confirms the folk category. 20th-century coat-colour genetics shows bay = black base (E_) + dominant agouti (A_) restricting eumelanin to points. DNA testing now distinguishes A/A, A/a, a/a (bay vs black) and modifiers (cream, dun, silver) that produce buckskin, bay dun, chocolate. Yet breed registries retain bay as the phenotypic label, proving the Latin dealer’s eye described a real Mendelian class fifteen centuries before Mendel.
  • Homonym disclaimer (zero guesswork). Three other modern bay words are etymologically unrelated and must not be merged into this chain: (a) bay inlet from Old French baie < Late Latin baia (harbour, after Baiae), of unknown deeper origin; (b) bay recess/compartment from Old French baee opening < baer to gape < Late Latin *batare to yawn; (c) bay laurel/berry from Old French baie berry < Latin baca; (d) bay to bark from Old French abaier, of imitative origin. Dictionaries number them bay1–bay5; only bay1 (horse) belongs to the badius chain above.

The result is a rare case of a Latin stable-yard adjective surviving almost unchanged in form and meaning for sixteen centuries, because horse dealers in every century needed exactly one word for a red-brown horse with black points, and no synonym ever displaced it.
