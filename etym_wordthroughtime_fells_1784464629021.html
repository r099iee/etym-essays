“Fell” as a landscape word names the high, barren hills and rocky uplands of northern England — wild, treeless, windswept heights that were for centuries marginal grazing land for sheep and hardy hill folk.
“Fell” in the 18th–19th centuries, through the Lake District poets, turned these same uplands into scenery of sublime beauty, and the word gained a romantic, reverent register as a name for beloved northern landscape.
“Fell” in the 20th century, through fell-walking and fell-running, became an outdoor, recreational word — the hillwalker’s terrain — while keeping its northern, topographical precision.
“Fells” today still names specifically those barren high uplands, never a mere poetic synonym for any hill, with a rugged ballad-clear flavour.
