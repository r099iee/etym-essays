Tariff begins not in Europe but in the administrative Arabic of the medieval Islamic world. Its root is ʿ-r-f, "to know"; the basic verb ʿarafa means "he knew", and the intensive or factitive form ʿarrafa means "he made known, informed, notified". From that verb comes the noun taʿrīfa, "a making known, a notification, an inventory" — and, in the ledgers of merchants and customs officials, "a list of prices or charges". The semantic bridge is exact and rather beautiful: a tariff is knowledge made public. To publish a rate is to tell everyone, in advance, what a thing will cost.

English did not borrow the word directly from Arabic. It arrived through the commercial Mediterranean, where Arabic administrative vocabulary had long circulated in the ports of Italy and the crown of Aragon. Italian tariffa is recorded in the fourteenth century in the sense of a schedule of prices and customs dues; Medieval Latin tarifa follows. French took the word as tarif in the sixteenth century, dropping the final syllable, and English acquired it from French in the 1590s. The loss of the Arabic definite article al- is diagnostic: words transmitted through Spanish often keep it (alcohol, algebra, alcove), whereas tariff came through Italian and French and therefore begins bare.

From table to tax

  • 1590s: English tariff means an arithmetical table or ready-reckoner — a printed grid that saves a merchant the labour of calculation. This is the word's first foothold and the ancestor of every later sense.
  • 17th century: The sense narrows and hardens into the official schedule of customs duties. England's Book of Rates, the crown's customs tariff, is the institutional background: a printed book in which every commodity had its charge. By the 1660s "tariff" routinely means this fiscal instrument.
  • 18th century: Political economy adopts the word. Mercantilist writers defend high tariffs as a tool of national power; Adam Smith attacks restraints on trade in The Wealth of Nations (1776). Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures (1791) gives the infant-industry argument its classic American form.
  • 19th century: The tariff becomes the central issue of Anglo-American politics. The Tariff of Abominations (1828) provokes the nullification crisis; the Cobden–Chevalier treaty (1860) marks a high point of free trade; the McKinley Tariff (1890) and the Dingley Tariff (1897) carry American protectionism to new heights. "Tariff reform" becomes a party slogan in Britain.
  • 20th century: Smoot–Hawley (1930) raises American duties amid depression and is widely blamed for deepening the slump; the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act (1934), GATT (1947) and the WTO (1995) build an architecture of tariff reduction. Tariff schedules become technical documents governed by the Harmonized System of commodity classification (from 1988).
  • 21st century: Tariffs return to the centre of geopolitics, imposed not only for economic protection but for national security and as instruments of retaliation. "Tariff war" and "tariff barrier" are again daily newspaper phrases.

Beyond the border

In parallel, the older "schedule of charges" sense never died. Railways, gasworks and telephone companies published tariffs; regulators still speak of "tariff structures" and "tariff orders". British telecom usage turned tariff into the ordinary word for a mobile price plan, while Indian English uses it for electricity rates set by a regulatory commission. In criminal law, the "sentencing tariff" is the conventional range of penalties for an offence — a direct descendant of the Hammurabi-style scale that the reading describes, where each injury has its published price.

Connotation today

In trade, tariff is technical but never neutral: it evokes protection, sovereignty, consumer prices and retaliation. In domestic regulation it is dry and bureaucratic, associated with bills and rate schedules. The word's journey — from "notification" to "price list" to "tax on imports" to "political weapon" — is a case study in how a metaphor of published knowledge can be captured by institutions and turned into an instrument of power.
