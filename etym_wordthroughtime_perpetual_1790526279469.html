- 1380s–1500s: Middle English theological and legal permanence - Borrowed from Old French to designate eternal salvation, unending damnation, and legal covenants or chantries established in perpetuity.
- 1500s–1650s: Institutional titles and sovereign treaties - Applied to civic offices, lifelong appointments (e.g. perpetual curate), and treaties of "perpetual peace" between monarchies.
- 1650s–1800s: Natural philosophy and mechanical thermodynamics - Codified in physics and early engineering through debates over the physical impossibility of "perpetual motion" (perpetuum mobile).
- 1800s–1950s: Colloquial exaggeration and literary realism - Widely adopted into everyday speech as an expressive hyperbole for incessant, nagging, or relentlessly repeated daily occurrences.
- Contemporary usage: Finance, law, and figurative discourse - Retained in high finance (perpetual bonds), property law (rule against perpetuities), and general prose denoting unending conditions.
