The story of SWOOP begins in the shared Germanic inheritance of the words we still use for broad, fast, sweeping motion: sweep, swipe, swoop, and even swoon. All of them go back to a Proto-Germanic base *swīp-/swaip- expressing a single gesture — a driving, curving stroke that carries something along with it — and the family's English descendants split along that vowel alternation: sweep and swipe continue the long-vowel grade, swoop the rounded, short-o development. It is a family of verbs of violent elegance, and it is from that family that the hawk's verb is finally carved out.
  • Germanic foundations and Old English: the Old English strong verb swāpan (past sweop) meant "to sweep, to rush, to sweep along"; Beowulf uses the past form of it for a fire sweeping through a hall, and it is applied to wind, flame, and armed hosts rushing onward. The sense is already one of irresistible, engulfing motion, but it is not yet the hunter's dive: an Anglo-Saxon flame sweeps, it does not swoop. The word carried no predatory specialization and no connection to height.
  • Middle English and the curve: in Middle English the verb swopen/soupen continued as "to sweep, to swing, to move in a curve", and, crucially, it began to take the preposition upon: "to soupen upon" someone is to rush down on them. This is the pivot. The general Germanic verb of sweeping motion is being narrowed toward an act of attack, and the curve that had always been latent in the word-family (the stroke that bends around its target) is now read as the curve of a descent.
  • Sound change: the development from Old English swāpan to modern swoop passes through the regular lengthening and rounding of the Middle English diphthong; by early Modern English the form settles as swoop with the long rounded vowel /uː/, and the past tense is levelled to the regular swooped, replacing the older strong forms. The modern spelling keeps the sw- cluster that ties the word visibly to sweep and swipe.
  • The sixteenth-century specialization: the decisive semantic event occurs in the sixteenth century, when swoop becomes the falconer's verb for the raptor's stoop — the descent of a hawk or eagle from a height, folding its wings and dropping in a curve onto its quarry. Here every element of the Old English word is re-read: the sweeping motion becomes the curved dive; the rushing strength becomes the merciless speed; and the height, implicit in the bird's soaring, becomes part of the word's picture. It is this sixteenth-century sense that Shakespeare canonizes in Macbeth (1605–1606), where the line "in one fell swoop" describes the single sudden stroke that carries off a whole family at once — and the idiom stamps the sense of devastating totality onto the word for ever.
  • Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries: from falconry the word spreads into ordinary, and then literary, use. The noun appears beside the verb; "a swoop" is the act or motion of swooping, and by the Romantic period poets and novelists use it for eagles, rooks, gulls, and for the moral image of fate or ruin descending at one blow. The word acquires its modern feel — vivid, dynamic, morally weighted — a movement that is at once graceful and merciless, an attack that also reads as an intervention.
  • Literary attestations: after Shakespeare, the word runs through the adventure and nature-writing traditions. Nineteenth-century writers of sensation fiction use it for birds and for sudden calamities; twentieth-century writers of heroic fantasy, above all Tolkien, draw on it for eagles and for the sudden rescue from above. In The Hobbit the past tense swooped is chosen with philological care: the word's Germanic ancestry (it is cognate with sweep and swipe, not a Romance loan) gives the eagles' action a native, archaic, saga-like dignity, and its curve matches the circling flight of the great birds as they come down upon the goblins.
  • Modern figurative and technical widening: in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word is extended far beyond birds. Police "swoop" on criminals, tax authorities "swoop" on evaders, investors "swoop" on bargains, and companies "swoop in" to buy distressed rivals — the image of the watchful predator descending at the chosen moment is preserved as an abstract business and legal metaphor. The phrase "in one fell swoop" remains an everyday idiom for doing something all at once. In military and police reporting "swoop" denotes a coordinated surprise raid; in ornithology it is a standard description of raptor hunting; and in popular culture the word has kept its dramatic charge, appearing constantly in headlines about sudden raids and sudden rescues.
  • Socio-cultural valence: the word's career shows a consistent emotional colouring: a swoop is never clumsy, never slow, never neutral. It is the motion of the powerful descending upon the powerless, whether for good (the eagles' rescue) or for ill (the police raid, the corporate takeover). That is why it does so much work in heroic fantasy: it carries at once the grace of the hawk and the finality of judgment.
The whole arc, then, runs from a broad Germanic verb of sweeping, rushing motion, through a Middle English "rush down upon", to a sixteenth-century falconer's term for the raptor's dive, and then outward again — fixed forever by Shakespeare, revived for eagles and rescues by the fantasy tradition, and now extended to every sudden, decisive, predatory descent that the modern world can stage.
