brimming in the source passage — Anna Pavlovna Scherer, in spite of her forty years, was on the contrary brimming over with excitement and impulsiveness (Leo Tolstoy, War and Peace, Book 1, Chapter 1, depicting the social hostess of the Petersburg imperial court) — describes being filled to overflowing capacity with intense emotion, lively animation, and spontaneous energy. It serves as a deliberate characterological contrast to Prince Vassily’s languid detachment: whereas the courtier is drained of genuine feeling, Anna Pavlovna is an emotional vessel filled right up to the lip, where her political enthusiasm and salon gossip constantly threaten to spill over in vibrant, dramatic bursts.

In general and literary English, brimming is a present participle and descriptive adjective indicating that a receptacle, space, surface, natural feature, or person is completely filled to its uppermost edge (the brim), so that its contents reach the extreme margin or overflow. Metaphorically, it expresses an abundance of emotional, psychological, or creative intensity — feelings so voluminous and lively that the container of the mind, body, or expression cannot fully restrain them.

  • Physical and hydraulic sense (containers, vessels, and waterways): Filled with liquid to the very upper rim or edge, often forming a delicate convex meniscus. For example: a brimming goblet of spiced wine; the rain barrel was brimming after the midnight thunderstorm; the mountain stream was brimming with snowmelt. In this primary physical sense, the word evokes visual fullness right at the threshold of spilling.
  • Emotional, psychological, and spiritual overflow (source sense): Experiencing or projecting an intense, radiant plenitude of feeling, enthusiasm, joy, confidence, or tears. For example: she entered the hall brimming with confidence; his heart was brimming with gratitude; her eyes were brimming with unshed tears. The semantic bridge is the metaphor of the human soul or face as a vessel that fills with passion until it overflows into gesture and tears.
  • Biological, ecological, and spatial abundance: Describing environments, ecosystems, markets, or vessels teeming and crowded with life, activity, or resources. For example: the tidal pool was brimming with tiny crustaceans; the harbor was brimming with merchant vessels from across the Baltic; a notebook brimming with inventive sketches. Here, it functions as a dynamic synonym for teeming or abounding.
  • Facial and ocular expressions (tears and gaze): Specifically describing eyes welling with moisture or shining with suppressed emotion. For example: her brimming eyes caught the candlelight; he listened with brimming gaze as his daughter began her recital. This ocular usage is one of the most frequent literary collocations in English poetry and 19th-century fiction.
  • Grammatical constructions and collocations: Frequently appears in the prepositional constructions brimming with (joy, tears, ideas, life) and brimming over (with excitement, enthusiasm, pride). It acts both as a participial adjective (a brimming cup) and as an intransitive progressive verb (the river is brimming over its banks). Register is vivid, expressive, and warmly evocative across both formal literature and refined conversation.

The core image connecting every application of brimming is fullness at the boundary: whether water meeting the lip of a crystal goblet, tears touching the eyelid, or enthusiasm radiating across a drawing room, the word captures the vibrant tension of a vessel loaded to its absolute limit.
