capitale is a Latin neuter noun (from the adjective capitalis) meaning property, stock, principal wealth counted by heads of cattle, the direct ancestor of English capital, cattle and chattel.

In Classical and Late Latin capitale (genitive capitalis) denotes what stands at the head: the principal sum as opposed to interest, the main stock as opposed to increase, livestock as capital. Roman farmers and jurists reckoned substance in capita — by heads — so capitale is literally head-wealth. From this barnyard accounting come the great modern abstractions: capital (wealth that breeds wealth), capitalism, capitalise.

The form in the source sentence is cited as the etymon: French capital, chatel, cattle go back to Latin capitale, which in turn derives from caput (head). It is a learned citation form rather than an everyday English word, but its derivatives dominate economics.

  • Core Latin sense — principal property, livestock-stock: Capitale as substantive means the head-count stock: cattle, goods, capital fund. Varro and Columella discuss capitale in farm management; jurists oppose capitale (principal) to usura, fructus, incrementum (interest, fruit, increase). The logic is reproductive: heads beget heads, stock begets increase, principal begets interest — the biological metaphor inside finance.
  • Adjectival base — capitalis: The adjective capitalis means pertaining to the head or life: poena capitalis (capital punishment, loss of head/life), crimen capitale (capital crime), hostis capitalis (mortal enemy), urbs capitalis (chief city, hence capital city), littera capitalis (capital letter, standing at the head of the line). Capitale the noun is simply the neuter used absolutely: the chief thing, the head thing.
  • Medieval development — capitale as wealth, chapter, capital: In Medieval Latin capitale diversifies: (1) wealth, funds, stock-in-trade of merchants; (2) head, chapter of a cathedral (capitulum diminutive, but confused); (3) capital city. Merchant Latin capitale is the direct source of Italian capitale, Old French capital, chatel/catel, Middle English capital, catel/chatel. The commercial sense — money put to breed money — becomes dominant with urban trade and later industrial capitalism.
  • English derivatives and polysemy inherited: Modern capital preserves every Latin branch: (a) wealth/principal (capital investment, venture capital, capital gains); (b) chief city (capital of France); (c) chief letter (capital A); (d) punishable by death (capital offence); (e) excellent (capital idea! — archaic colloquial); (f) architecture (capital of a column — the head of the pillar, from same root via capitellum). Cattle and chattel preserve the livestock-property branch split by Norman/Picard dialect. To know capitale is to hold the single knot from which all these threads diverge.
  • Technical economic senses today: Economists distinguish fixed vs. circulating capital, human capital, social capital, cultural capital, capital stock, capital account. All metaphors extend head-count stock: value that, like a herd, can grow, be bred, be counted per head, be decapitated (decapitalised). Marx, Smith and Piketty all theorise capital without needing to gloss that they are theorising cattle-heads.
  • Usage note: Capitale itself is not used in modern English except as a Latin citation or Italian word (il capitale); English uses capital. Students meet capitale in etymology dictionaries and in sentences like the source, where it functions as a stepping-stone display: English ← French ← Latin capitale ← Latin caput.

The semantic bridge throughout is headship: the head is top, chief, countable unit and seat of life. Count wealth by heads, call the count capitale, lend the count to breed increase — and you have simultaneously cattle, capital punishment, capital cities, capital letters and capitalism, all decapitable from one head-word.
