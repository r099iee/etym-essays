Brash describes behaviour, speech, or style that is aggressively self-assertive: loud, tactless, impudent, and unapologetic, crashing into a situation without regard for subtlety or for the feelings of others. It also denotes rashness — hasty, reckless, impetuous action taken on impulse and without due thought for consequences. In the passage from the Dream of the Red Chamber, the phrase “brash and unmannerly” delivers a double condemnation of the newly arrived young woman: the first word supplies the aggressive, intrusive energy, the second the failure of courtesy, and together they place her in stark opposition to the hushed, ceremonious, heavily regulated atmosphere of the Jia household, where quiet, composed dignity is the expected currency. Yet the context also hints at the magnetism of brashness: the “beautiful young woman” who enters surrounded by attendants — unmistakably Wang Xi-feng — owes much of her commanding presence precisely to the ungoverned force that propriety deplores.

Polysemy and secondary senses. Brash is richer than its everyday use suggests, with a family of related senses spread across personality, style, the physical world, and technical jargon:

  • Of persons and behaviour: aggressively self-assertive, tactless, loud, and forward — the dominant modern sense; a “brash young man” is one who pushes into company, interrupts, and speaks without deference.
  • Of action: rash, hasty, impetuous, reckless — the earlier nineteenth-century sense, still alive in “a brash decision” or “brash haste”, which marks action taken suddenly, on impulse, and without weighing risks.
  • Of appearance and style: garish, showily bright, vulgarly striking — “brash colours”, “a brash neon sign”, “brash décor” — where the metaphor of a loud personality is transferred to loud, clashing visual stimuli that cannot be ignored.
  • Of noise: raucous, harsh, and jarring — “a brash trumpet blast”, “brash laughter” — the sound equivalent of visual garishness.
  • Physical senses (technical and dialectal): “brash” names brittle, crumbling rock or timber (“brashy” in mining and building); “brash ice” is the mass of small broken fragments floating in water, from the breaking-up of floes; in Scots and northern dialect a “brash” is a sudden shower of rain or a sudden short bout of illness; in forestry, “brash” is the lopped branches and tops left after felling.
  • Metaphoric bridge: all these senses share the image of sudden, violent breaking or bursting — a squall, a fit of illness, a fragmenting mass, a voice or colour that shatters decorum — and the modern personality sense is simply the transfer of that shattering energy from weather, matter, and sound to human temperament.

Register and collocation. Brash belongs to informal-to-semi-formal speech and writing, where it functions as a pointed criticism. Typical collocations: “brash young man”, “brash confidence”, “brash demeanour”, “brash tone”, “brash behaviour”, “brash colours”. Its boundaries against near-synonyms are sharp: audacious implies admirable risk-taking on a grand scale; impudent centres on insolent disrespect toward authority; rash stresses foolish haste and disregard of consequences; bold is generally a positive word for courage; forward is milder and chiefly social; brazen adds shamelessness; tactless names the social clumsiness rather than the aggression. Brash uniquely combines volume, impatience, and an unpolished, unguarded force that is felt as an intrusion — which is why, in the society of the novel, it is the precise antonym of the composed, formal distinction the aristocracy demands of itself.
