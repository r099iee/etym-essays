dampness:
- 16th c. - the word 'damp' (borrowed from Low German 'damp', vapour) first names a choking gas: the noxious damp of mines and closed rooms, and 'dampness' is the state of being filled with that suffocating vapour
- 16th-18th c. - the sense softens from gas to moisture: dampness becomes the cool, absorbed wetness of cellars, walls, and marsh air, and carries a medical shadow - the age fears damp air as the carrier of fevers and consumption
- 19th c. - the word settles into the everyday climate of northern Europe: the dampness of English weather, of soil and stone, a national complaint of chill and rheumatism; still faintly unwholesome, a quality to be kept out of the bones
- Contemporary - 'dampness' is the plain, clinical member of the wet family: a neutral physical index of water absorbed into air and surface, felt rather than seen - which is exactly how Maspic uses it, as a cool sensory deduction rather than a complaint
