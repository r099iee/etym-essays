Quaint is an adjective describing something old-fashioned in an attractive or amusing way. A quaint village, a quaint custom, a quaint turn of phrase: the word approves of the age of a thing while gently noting that it is out of date. It is nearly always mildly positive, but the approval is often patronising, since the speaker is usually an outsider looking at someone else's home or habits.

In the source passage quaint modifies “language.” The writer has examined every English translation of a book and concluded that the Watson edition remains the best complete version “despite the often quaint language.” Here the word means archaic and odd — full of constructions and expressions that belong to an earlier period, charming to some readers and tiresome to others. It is a criticism softened by affection: the language is dated, but the datedness is part of the edition's character rather than a fatal flaw.

The dominant modern sense

  • Attractively old-fashioned. Of buildings, streets, villages, costumes, ceremonies, or turns of speech: a quaint cottage, a quaint fishing harbour, quaint local customs. The thing described is old but not merely old; it has survived in a form that pleases the modern eye.
  • Pleasingly odd or curious. A quaint idea or a quaint superstition is eccentric in a way that entertains rather than threatens.
  • Of language or style: archaic, old-world, slightly strange. Quaint spelling, a quaint expression, the quaint diction of a seventeenth-century translation. This is the sense used in the source passage.
  • Faintly condescending. To call a place or a people quaint is to put them at a distance, to treat their way of life as a picture rather than a present reality. The word has been criticised for this reason in post-colonial and tourist-studies writing.

Older senses, now obsolete or literary

  • Wise, clever, skilful. The earliest Middle English sense, from Old French: a quaint man was shrewd or ingenious. This survives only in historical writing.
  • Cunning, crafty. A darker development of the same idea, close to sly, and now entirely obsolete.
  • Fine, elegant, fashionable. From the thirteenth to the sixteenth century, quaint could praise dress, manners, or appearance as refined and stylish — the opposite of rustic.
  • Proud, haughty. A courtly sense recorded in Old French and Middle English, implying a fine opinion of oneself.
  • Ingenious, fanciful, elaborate. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, quaint conceits, quaint devices, and quaint embroidery described invention and ornament, especially in literature and design.
  • The noun quaint. An obsolete noun, identical in form, denoted the female genitals. It is the same Middle English word, and it survives chiefly as a footnote to Chaucer, whose Miller's Tale puns on it.

The semantic journey, and why it matters

The history of quaint is one of the most striking semantic slides in English. It begins in Latin cognitus, “known,” the past participle of cognoscere, “to get to know.” Something known is familiar; a person who knows is clever; a clever person in courtly society is refined; what is refined is elegant; what is elegant and rare is unusual; what is unusual and old is quaint. Each step is small and each depends on the one before, yet the endpoints could hardly be further apart: the word has travelled from “known” to “charmingly unfamiliar.” The modern sense is therefore a kind of etymological irony, since quaint now describes precisely what is not known to the speaker's own world.

The critical turn is the step from “elegant, fine” to “strange, unusual.” A refined object is select and uncommon, and what is uncommon can be viewed as odd. Once “odd” is in place, the word is free to attach itself to anything old and unfamiliar, and from the eighteenth century it settles into its modern meaning of old-fashioned charm.

Register and collocations

  • Mildly positive but not enthusiastic: quaint is fainter praise than charming and warmer than antiquated.
  • Frequent partners: village, cottage, custom, tradition, ceremony, expression, spelling, charm, little, old, rather, utterly.
  • Derivatives: quaintly (adverb), quaintness (noun). The comparative and superlative are regular: quainter, quaintest.
  • The word is common in travel writing, heritage brochures, and nostalgic memoir, and it is avoided in contexts where respect for a living culture is at stake.

Quaint, antiquated, archaic

The three words form a scale of approval. Quaint likes what it sees; antiquated disapproves of it as out of date and inefficient; archaic is neutral and technical, applied by linguists to forms that have dropped out of ordinary use. A quaint custom is a pleasure, an antiquated custom is an obstacle, and an archaic custom is a datum. Choosing among them is a way of taking a position on the past.

In this passage

“The often quaint language” is a measured judgement. The writer does not say the translation is bad; the word quaint concedes oddity while withholding condemnation, and “often” suggests that the oddity is frequent enough to notice but not constant. The sentence therefore balances editorial loyalty against stylistic honesty, which is exactly the work that quaint is good at.
