Wedges adds regular plural -s to wedge, a word whose form has barely changed in 1200 years because its referent - a sharpened V of hard material driven to split, lift, or grip - cannot be improved. Old English wecg (pronounced /wedʒ/) already means exactly what modern wedge means. It descends from Proto-Germanic *wagjaz, itself from a PIE mobile root connected to movement and driving, often cited as *weg- or extended *wegh- "to move, drive". The semantic logic is that the wedge is "the mover" - the thing driven in to move apart what was joined.

Cognates confirm deep Germanic roots: Old Saxon weggi, Old Norse veggr (in compounds), Old High German weggi, wecki, Dutch wig. All show geminate -gg- from West Germanic consonant doubling before -j-. Old English palatalises g before front vowels, giving the modern /dʒ/ sound spelled dg. The vowel e remains short and stable through the Great Vowel Shift because short vowels in closed syllables largely escape it.

  • Old English stability (800-1100): Wecg appears in Ælfric's Colloquy for splitting wood and in Beowulf-adjacent smithing contexts for cleaving. Spelling wecg with cg denotes /ddʒ/. Plural wecgas with -as.
  • Middle English normalisation (1100-1500): Ormulum and Chaucer show wegge with French-influenced -gge spelling to mark the affricate. Final -e silences, giving wedge. Plural shifts from -en to -es, yielding wedges /ˈwɛdʒɪz/ with epenthetic vowel before /z/.
  • Early Modern fixing (1500-1700): Printing fixes wedge. The verb to wedge (to split with, to force in, to pack tight) grows alongside. Shakespeare uses wedge for splitting and for ingots (wedge of gold) showing shape-based extension.

Technologically the wedge is one of the six classical simple machines identified by Renaissance engineers after Archimedes and Hero: the inclined plane in motion. Its Stone Age form - a flint or antler sliver to split wood or bone - appears in the source list among hammers and levers. Bronze and iron wedges for quarrying, felling (felling wedges), and shoeing horses follow. By the 19th century splitting wedges, door wedges, and engineering shims diversify the family while keeping the V profile.

  • Shape extensions: Because the profile is so distinctive, wedge extends to anything V-shaped: a wedge of cheese or cake, a wedge of land (wedge issue in politics), a wedge heel in shoes, a wedge haircut, a pitching wedge or sand wedge in golf (lofted clubs that wedge under the ball), a wedge formation in football or battle (flying wedge), and a wedge filter in optics.
  • Figurative politics: To drive a wedge between allies, the thin end of the wedge as the seemingly small first step that splits wide - 19th-century temperance and abolition rhetoric made this proverbial. The connotation turns divisive and cautionary.
  • Science and sport: Optics uses wedge prisms; geology uses wedge faults; cricket uses wedge fielders; darts and surfing use wedge ramps. Each preserves push-apart or tapering logic.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare's "wedge of gold" in Tombs (illicit ingot), Milton's artillery wedges, Dickens's door wedges in damp counting-houses, modern golf commentary obsessing over 56-degree wedges.

From PIE mover to Saxon splitter to golf bag saviour, wedges shows radical formal conservatism paired with exuberant metaphorical spread: the shape stayed, the uses multiplied.
