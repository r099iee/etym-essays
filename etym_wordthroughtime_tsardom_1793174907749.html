- 1547 — the word becomes possible: Ivan IV takes the title tsar, and his realm is henceforth describable as a tsardom, the gathered Russian lands under one autocrat.
- 16th–17th c. — the Muscovite reality: streltsy, prikazes, and Siberian yasak-tribute; the tsardom expands to the Pacific while Europe barely learns its name.
- 1721 — the renaming: Peter proclaims an empire, and tsardom slides from chancery style into history books, kept warm by historians of the pre-Petrine age.
- 19th c. — the folklorists' elsewhere: Afanasyev's tales give every enchanted bridegroom his own Hawk's-like tsardom beyond the thrice-nine lands, and English translators import the word with them.
- 20th c. — the journalist's shorthand: tsardom becomes colour-word for Russian autocracy old and new, from Nicholas II to Soviet centralism described as tsardom in red paint.
- Today — history and metaphor: scholars date the Tsardom 1547–1721, while columnists crown every petty office tyrant of his own little tsardom.
