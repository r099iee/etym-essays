• Alcyone: Similar because both are the central royal spouses in the Ovidian metamorphosis legend who share a tragic fate and simultaneous transformation into seabirds; differs because Ceyx is the king whose sea voyage and drowning instigate the catastrophe, whereas Alcyone is the grieving queen whose boundless mourning stirs the gods to divine mercy.
  • Eosphorus: Similar because of direct mythological kinship (Eosphorus, the Morning Star or bringer of dawn, is the divine father of Ceyx); differs because Eosphorus is an immortal astral deity and celestial titan, whereas Ceyx was born a mortal monarch subject to the perils of sea voyaging and physical death.
  • Halcyon: Similar because both names are inextricably intertwined with the classical kingfisher transformation myth; differs because halcyon historically represents the female bird (Alcyone) and the resulting peaceful midwinter days, whereas Ceyx was originally associated with the male diving seabird and now denotes a specific taxonomic genus of dwarf kingfishers.
  • Morpheus: Similar because both appear in the dramatic climax of the Ceyx narrative; differs because Morpheus is the god of dreams and son of Somnus (Sleep) who merely assumes the dripping, pale likeness of the drowned Ceyx to reveal the tragic shipwreck to Alcyone in her sleep.
  • Alcedo: Similar because both serve as primary taxonomic genera within the kingfisher family (Alcedinidae); differs because Alcedo comprises typical Old World river kingfishers (such as the common European kingfisher), while Ceyx designates the miniature, three-toed or four-toed dwarf river kingfishers of the Indo-Pacific.
  • Trachinian: Similar because Ceyx was the sovereign ruler of Trachis, making him the foremost Trachinian monarch; differs because Trachinian is a broad geographic and demonymic adjective applying to any inhabitant, event, or play (such as Sophocles' Trachiniae) associated with that region of Thessaly.
