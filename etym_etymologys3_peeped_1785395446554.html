Peeped is a word born of a squeak, and its biography must be told in two movements, because modern English peep is really a pair of homographs that happen to rhyme. English dictionaries classify the first peep, the verb of chirping, as of obscure, probably imitative origin: the base *pī- echoes a small, sharp, repeated sound, and the same sound-symbolic pattern appears independently all over Europe — Dutch piepen "to cheep, squeak" (and, strikingly, also "to look slyly"), German piepen "to cheep", and Latin pīpāre, the chirping of birds, which survives in French pépier and in Latin pīpiō, the young bird. English itself had Middle English pīpen "to chirp", kin to pipe the instrument, and the specific verb pepen, "to cheep or squeak", is recorded from about 1400; by extension it produced the noun peep for the thin cry of a nestling and, in the eighteenth century, the phrase the peep of day, the moment when light first "chirps" over the horizon. None of this has an Indo-European root to speak of: the whole family is expressive, born again and again from the noise itself. This sound-sense shows the word's first recorded register — nursery, barnyard, and birdsong — tiny and high, the vocabulary of nestlings rather than of men.

The second peep, the verb of looking, is younger and stranger. It appears in English around 1470 meaning "to look out quickly and furtively", and scholars generally treat it as an alteration of peek, which is first recorded in the late fourteenth century and which itself comes from the North Sea look-word family — Middle Low German kīken, Dutch kijken "to look", Middle Dutch kieken — a family that also gave Scots its surviving keek "to glance" and colloquial German its gucken / kucken "to look, peep". The bridge between the two homographs was metonymy of scale: a peep is to a stare what a squeak is to a shout. The word moved from the sound of a small creature to the small, sharp, momentary act of looking out from concealment, and the two tiny words, both monosyllables with a long front vowel, reinforced each other in the mouths of fifteenth-century speakers until they fused into a single idea — the furtive, bird-like glance.

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries gave the look-verb its classic social habitat. To peep was to spy through keyholes, to look through curtains and chinks, to steal a view of what was hidden — and the word acquired a settled geometry of prepositions: one peeps through an aperture, out from cover, over a fence, round a corner. The eighteenth century then turned the small verb into a small industry. The phrase peeping Tom (attested 1796) fixed the word forever to voyeurism, taking its name from the legendary tailor who, alone in Coventry, dared to look at Lady Godiva as she rode naked through the town; the Victorian peep-show made the illicit glimpse a purchasable spectacle; and the nursery game peekaboo (also of the eighteenth century) domesticated the same act of hide-and-seek vision for infants. In the same century the old chirp-sense contributed the idiom not a peep — utter silence — and the peep of day, the dawn's first thin line of light.

Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word stayed small and unpretentious. It was the vocabulary of children's books and of narrative voice; it was the gesture of the cautious and the shy; it carried the comic tone of someone showing only their eyes. In The Hobbit (1937), Tolkien deploys precisely this register: Bilbo, hidden in the pine's boughs, is betrayed only by "his eyes gleaming in the moon as he peeped out". The word keeps the scale of the act — a peep is the smallest kind of look — and its furtive warmth; it is simultaneously the most childish and the most precarious gesture in the scene, and the past-tense form -ed stamps it as a single completed moment in the moonlight.

Two sound changes matter in the story. First, the regular weak past-tense formation: peep takes its -ed like any normal verb, so that peeped, the form of the card, is purely a grammatical development, the past of a verb that never changed its stem. Second, the expressive vowel itself: the long ī of both homographs (the ē of the look-sense before the Great Vowel Shift) is exactly the kind of sharp, high, small sound the word names — the phonology of the word, its single bright vowel, is an icon of its meaning. Socioculturally, the word has drifted from a noise (a chirp) to a glance (a peep) to an entire culture of curiosity — the Peeping Tom, the peep-show, the prying glance — while retaining, even in its darkest uses, a residue of playfulness and scale. It remains a word of smallness: small creatures, small sounds, small sights, small secrets.

  • Chronology of the two strands: chirp-sense in English c. 1400 (Middle English pepen); look-sense c. 1470 (alteration of peek, late 14th c., from the North Sea *kīk- family).
  • Key literary attestations: Middle English pepen (c. 1400); Shakespeare's era uses of peep as the furtive glance; Peeping Tom (1796); the Victorian peep-show; Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937), the gleaming eyes in the moon.
  • Semantic drift summary: sound (chirp) → scale-metaphor (tiny look) → voyeurism and spectacle (peeping Tom, peep-show) → intimate narrative voice in modern fiction.
  • Socio-cultural factors: nursery language and children's literature kept the word playful; Victorian urban culture attached it to voyeurism and the commercial glance; modern prose values it for its comic, confidential smallness.
