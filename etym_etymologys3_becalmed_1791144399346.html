Becalmed is a transparent Modern English formation be- + calm + -ed, but its story depends on the strange Mediterranean history of calm itself, from Greek burning midday heat to sailors' windless stillness to a verb meaning to deprive of motion and then to soothe.

The ultimate root is usually given as PIE *keh₂- to burn, glow, seen in Sanskrit śā-? and Greek kaiein to burn, kindle. From the verb Greek formed kauma burning heat, especially the scorching midday heat when work stops and air shimmers. In the hot Mediterranean, midday heat coincides with a glassy sea and breathless air at noon. Late Latin borrowed cauma in the senses heat, midday rest, and by sailors' metonymy the flat calm of the sea during that heat. This shift from temperature to stillness is the pivotal semantic leap.

From Late Latin cauma, Italian calma and Old French calme generalized to any stillness of sea or air, then to tranquillity of weather, mind and politics. First attestations: Italian calma 13th century for sea-calm, French calme 14th century, English calme c. 1380s via French in Chaucer-era texts for windless weather and peaceful disposition. Phonetics are regular: Latin au > Romance al before m in this word through dissimilation or analogy, debated but attested across Western Romance.

  • Prefix be-: From Old English be-, bi- meaning about, around, thoroughly, from PIE *bʰi- by, around. Highly productive in verbs like befoul, bedeck, becalm with causative or covering force: to becalm is to bring calm upon, to envelop in calm.
  • Verb becalm formation (16th–17th c.): First as nautical transitive: wind becalms a ship, i.e., deprives it of wind and leaves it helplessly motionless. Shakespeare's contemporaries write of ships becalmed in the doldrums. Past participle becalmed describes the ship's state. Later reflexive and figurative: to be becalmed in life is to be stuck.
  • Intransitive and psychological extension: By analogy with calm as soothe, becalm briefly meant to make calm, to soothe, though calm down won. Modern dictionaries list both to deprive of wind and archaically to calm.
  • Source-context use: Tribes remained permanently becalmed in the transition means arrested, stuck midway between hunting and farming, like a ship without wind unable to advance. This is figurative becalmed as developmentally stalled, with faintly patronizing 19th-century evolutionist overtones.
  • Literary attestations: Defoe, Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner with its painted ship upon a painted ocean, Melville and Conrad on becalmed vessels, Darwin on becalmed seas during the Beagle voyage. Figurative use in Victorian anthropology for arrested cultures.

The debated point is whether calm truly comes from cauma. Some older dictionaries suggested Greek kalamos reed, sheltered water, or a pre-Indo-European Mediterranean substrate. Modern Romance philology, following von Wartburg and the FEW, accepts cauma > calma via regular Romance au > al before labials in this lexical item and semantic pathway heat-rest > sea-calm, citing Spanish calma, Portuguese calma and medieval Latin calma. The be- prefixation and -ed inflection are fully regular English morphology and undisputed. Thus becalmed unites ancient Greek heat, medieval sailors' dread, and modern figurative stuckness in one participle.
