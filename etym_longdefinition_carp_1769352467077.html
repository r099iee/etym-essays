carp (noun and verb) — the word belongs to two entirely separate words that happen to share a spelling. The noun carp, which matches the card's definition and context, is a large freshwater fish of the minnow family (Cyprinidae), most famously the common carp (Cyprinus carpio), a heavy-bodied, long-lived fish of slow, warm, muddy waters, native to the Danube and Black Sea basins and spread by human fish-farming across the whole world. In the reading context of this card, taken from C. S. Lewis's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, "a big carp rose to the surface of the pool, gulped air and then sank mysteriously into the dark water again": the word names the large, silent denizen of the forest pool, surfacing for a moment and vanishing into the dark — a creature at home in the deep, still water of Narnia's wood. The verb carp, by contrast, is a homonym with an unrelated history: it means to complain or find fault continually about trivial matters.

Primary senses — the fish
  • The common carp — Cyprinus carpio: a large freshwater fish of the minnow family (Cyprinidae), with a deep body, small mouth, thick scales, and barbels (whisker-like feelers); it lives in warm, slow, muddy rivers, lakes, and ponds, feeds on the bottom, and can grow very large and live for decades.
  • Close relatives — the family includes the crucian carp, goldfish (Carassius auratus, a domesticated carp relative), grass carp, silver carp, bighead carp, and the ornamental koi of Japan.
  • The fish in human life — carp have been farmed for food for over two thousand years (Chinese aquaculture dates to around 2000 years ago; European monastic fish-ponds date to the Middle Ages), are a major coarse (non-game) angling fish in Britain, and are culturally significant as koi in Japan and as symbols of perseverance in Chinese tradition.

Primary senses — the verb
  • To complain — "he's always carping about something": to express dissatisfaction persistently.
  • To find fault continually — "she carped at every detail of the plan": to make petty, trivial, persistent criticisms.
  • Related forms — "carping" (adjective and noun: petty fault-finding, as in "carping criticism"), "carper" (a person who carps).

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings
  • Coarse angling jargon — in British fishing, "carp" is the archetypal coarse fish; "carp fishing", "carp rod", "carp bait" form a rich technical vocabulary, and the carp's shyness and strength make it the most prized of coarse fish to catch.
  • Ornamental koi — the Japanese koi is a selectively bred ornamental variety of the common carp, kept in garden ponds and valued for colour patterns; the word "koi" itself is simply Japanese for "carp."
  • Invasive-species jargon — silver carp, bighead carp, and grass carp are notorious invasive species in North American rivers, where "carp" in environmental news usually means an invasive pest.
  • Symbolic and cultural uses — in Chinese legend the carp that leaps over the Dragon Gate becomes a dragon, a symbol of perseverance and success; carp appear in art, proverbs, and festive food across East Asia.
  • The verb in modern usage — "carping" survives as a literary and journalistic word for petty, persistent, trivial criticism ("carping critics"), distinct from the blunter "complain" or "grumble."
  • Etymology reminder — the noun (fish) and verb (complain) are etymologically unrelated homonyms; the fish descends from a Late Latin substrate loanword, the verb from Old Norse, with possible reinforcement from Latin carpere.

Historical semantic bridges
  • The fish noun travelled as a loanword, not an inheritance: it moved from a pre-Indo-European Danubian substrate term into Late Latin, Old French, and then English with the spread of fish-farming, carrying its meaning unchanged.
  • The verb began in Old Norse karpa, "to brag, talk noisily," entered Middle English as carpen, "to speak, to talk," and only in the 16th century narrowed to "to find fault," possibly under the influence of Latin carpere, "to pluck, to pick at, to slander" — a semantic narrowing from "talk" to "talk critically."
  • The two words merged in English by pure homonymic accident; no historical connection links the fish to fault-finding.

Boundaries against near-synonyms
  • As a fish, carp is distinguished from trout and salmon (prized cold-water game fish) and from minnows (its small relatives); it is the archetypal coarse, bottom-feeding fish of warm, still waters.
  • As a verb, carp is sharper and more petty than complain (the general word), more persistent than grumble (low muttered discontent), and more general than nitpick (which focuses on tiny details).
