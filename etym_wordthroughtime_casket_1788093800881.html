- 15th c.: “a small box for jewels and valuables”, borrowed from French — a courtly, precious word from the start, the container of jewels, rings and love-tokens.
- 16th–17th c.: also a strong chest for relics, charters and money — and Shakespeare’s Portia’s three caskets (1596–98) make the word an allegory of appearance and worth.
- 18th c.: general ornamental treasure-box — the jewel-casket of romantic and biblical prose; stately, gleaming, slightly ceremonial.
- Mid-19th c. America: the funeral trade reaches for the most precious word it knows — casket becomes the euphemism for coffin, softened and dignified for a mass-death age (Civil War era).
- 20th c.: the American sense hardens into the standard, even technical word (open-casket funeral; casket vs tapered coffin); in Britain the jewel-box sense survives as the literary default.
- 21st c.: the Atlantic split is complete — a “casket” buried in Ohio is a coffin; a “casket” in a Tolkien novel is a strong, iron-bound chest of treasure; both senses shine in their own registers.
