• Trousers: Similar in being leg-covering garments; differs because trousers run to the ankle and are the modern general word, while breeches stop at or just below the knee.
  • Pants: Similar in being a general word for leg-covering clothing; differs because in British English pants means underpants while in American it means trousers — breeches is unambiguously the knee-length riding garment.
  • Knickerbockers: Similar in being knee-length divided garments; differs because knickerbockers are loose breeches gathered at the knee, whereas breeches are cut to fit the leg.
  • Culottes: Similar in being divided leg-garments; differs because culottes are skirt-like and typically worn by women, while breeches are the older fitted garment once the mark of the master of the house.
  • Britches: Similar as the dialectal and colloquial American form of the same word; differs only in spelling and register — britches is informal and regional, breeches the standard literary form.
  • Shorts: Similar in covering the legs only partway; differs because shorts are modern, casual, and thigh-length, while breeches are the historical knee-length garment fastened below the knee.
  • Trews: Similar in being close-fitting leg garments; differs because trews (from Irish/Gaelic triubhas) are the tight tartan trousers of the Scottish Highlanders, while breeches are the knee-length garment.
  • Plus-fours: Similar in being knicker-style knee garments; differs because plus-fours are the loose, baggy knickerbockers of golfers, worn four inches below the knee, while breeches are fitted.
  • Pantaloons: Similar in being leg-covering garments of history; differs because pantaloons are long tight trousers reaching to the ankle (from the Venetian character Pantalone), while breeches are knee-length.
  • Breech (singular): Similar as the same word specialized; differs because breech names the hind part of the body or the rear of a gun barrel, while breeches is the garment covering that part.
