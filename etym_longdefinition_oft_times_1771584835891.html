Oft-times (also written ofttimes) is the archaic and poetic form of "often" - "many times, frequently, on many occasions" - a compound adverb built from oft and the plural noun times, formed on the model of sometimes and betimes. In the couplet from David Hawkes's translation of The Dream of the Red Chamber, "Oft-times he sought out what would make him sad; / Sometimes an idiot seemed and sometimes mad", the word carries a deliberately antique, ballad-like gravity. Its tone is rhythmic and oracular: two heavy syllables open the line with a solemn, tolling beat, and the very archaism of the form lifts the verse out of ordinary prose into the register of prophecy and proverb. The word tells the reader that Bao-yu's self-tormenting melancholy is not a passing mood but a habit of existence, a recurring pattern repeated "oft-times" throughout his life; it frames his strange behaviour as a fate rather than a whim, the repeated choosing of sorrow that the second line then counterbalances with his alternating idiocy and madness. The contrast embedded in the couplet - oft-times versus sometimes - is itself a small study in the grammar of frequency: the first adverb insists on repetition, the second on alternation, and together they sketch a life ruled by recurrence. Etymologically the adverb is a compound of two elements: oft, the native Germanic adverb meaning "often, frequently", and the plural of time, used adverbially in the same way as in sometimes, betimes, and the older ofttimes and oftentime variants. The compound is old: forms such as "ofte tymes" are attested in Middle English, and the word became a fixture of verse, hymns, ballads, and proverbial diction, where its two syllables gave poets a ready-made rhythm and its antique ring lent dignity. The full set of senses and uses can be set out:
  • Adverbial frequency, archaic register: "on many occasions, frequently" - the core sense; now virtually confined to poetry, archaizing prose, hymns, and deliberate pastiche.
  • Poetic / rhetorical function: used to open lines and clauses for emphasis and metre, as in the couplet; the initial position gives it the weight of a refrain.
  • Variants and spellings: ofttimes (one word), oft-times (hyphenated), and the older ofte times / ofte-tymes; all mean the same, with the hyphenated form the one in the text.
  • Colloquial residue: the plain "oft" survives in fixed collocations such as oft-quoted, oft-told, and oft-repeated, while "often" itself is the standard modern successor; the compound "oft-times" has no everyday spoken use today.
  • Grammatical note: the -times element is an adverbial plural, not a count noun; "oft-times" is an adverb of indefinite frequency, equivalent to "on many occasions", and it does not take a following "a" or "the".
Register is everything here. Oft-times left ordinary prose by the eighteenth century and survives only in verse, in Biblical and hymnal archaism, and in deliberate pastiche; its sister forms sometimes and betimes kept the same -times structure alive in the modern language, which is why oft-times still sounds intelligible even as it sounds ancient. In the couplet, that double life is exactly the point: the word is familiar enough to be understood, archaic enough to be memorable, and rhythmic enough to make Bao-yu's habitual melancholy sound like the refrain of an old song - the repeated pursuit of sadness "oft-times", until the pattern itself becomes his fate. To say "he often sought out what would make him sad" would be a report; to say "oft-times he sought out what would make him sad" is to pronounce a doom.
