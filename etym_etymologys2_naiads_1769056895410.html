PIE *sneh₂- (also written *sneH₂-, zero-grade *snā-; c. 4500-3500 BCE) — "to swim, flow, bathe, drip"; the watery root of motion-in-liquid, reconstructed on the strength of Hellenic, Italic, and Indo-Iranian reflexes (Pokorny 971; Beekes). The initial cluster *sn- is retained in Indo-Aryan but loses its *s- in Greek and assimilates in Latin (nāre "to swim").\n\nSTAGE 2 — Ancient Greek νάω (nā́ō), infinitive νάειν (náein) (attested from the 8th c. BCE, Homeric and lyric) — "I flow, trickle, run" (of springs, rivers, tears); from *sneh₂-ye/o-, with regular loss of the initial *s- and the lengthened root vowel nā-.\n\nSTAGE 3 — Ancient Greek νᾶμα (nâma) (attested 5th c. BCE) — "a spring, fountain, flowing stream"; a denominative noun built on the verb-stem nā- with the result-suffix *-ma.\n\nSTAGE 4 — Ancient Greek Ναϊάς (Nāïás), plural Ναϊάδες (Nāïádes) (attested from the Homeric period, c. 8th c. BCE) — "the flowing one; she of the flowing waters; a spring-nymph"; a feminine cult-name derived from the same nā- base, literally "she who flows."\n\nSTAGE 5 — Latin Nāias, genitive Nāiados / Nāiadis (1st c. BCE - 1st c. CE) — "a naiad, a fresh-water nymph"; Roman poets and mythographers adopt the Greek nymph-name.\n\nSTAGE 6 — English naiad (16th c., via Renaissance Latin and French naiade) — "a fresh-water nymph"; a learned, classicising loan.\n\nSTAGE 7 — English NAIADS — the English plural (naiad + -s), the inflected form on this card.\n\nNOTE ON THE DEBATE: the exact laryngeal shape of the root is debated — *sneh₂- vs. *sneh₃- vs. a simple *sna- — and Pokorny's grouping of Latin nāre under *snā- is standard but rests on an s-less reflex whose exact route is not fully transparent; the relationship between Greek νάω "I flow" and νῆχω (nḗkhō) "I swim" (velar-extended) is agreed, but the precise morphological bridge is debated. None of these uncertainties affects the direct naiad line, which runs unbroken through Greek νάω → νᾶμα → Ναϊάς → Latin → English.
