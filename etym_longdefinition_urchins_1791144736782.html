The plural urchins in the source passage (“shellfish, sea __________, frogs, toads…”) means sea urchins: small, globular echinoderm marine animals in the class Echinoidea, whose calcareous shell (test) bristles with movable spines and whose orange roe (uni in Japanese cuisine) is eaten as a delicacy — which is exactly why they appear in a list of things “somewhere a delicacy.” But the bare word urchin carries one of English’s most delightful double lives, and the card must hold both: the sea urchin (zoology, food) and the street urchin (a ragged, mischievous, usually poor child, Dickensian and affectionate by turns).

The two senses are linked by the hedgehog. Urchin originally meant simply ‘hedgehog’ (Middle English urchon); the round spiny sea animal was named “sea urchin” — sea-hedgehog — for its likeness, and the prickly, ill-kempt guttersnipe was likewise an “urchin” by metaphor: small, defensive, hard to handle, faintly comical. The hedgehog sense itself is now obsolete (replaced by hedgehog), leaving the sea and street senses to divide the inheritance.

  • Sea urchin (the passage sense): class Echinoidea — urchins, sand dollars, heart urchins. Globular test of fused plates, spines for defence and locomotion, fivefold symmetry, Aristotle’s lantern (the chewing apparatus). Edible gonads (“roe”/uni) eaten raw in Japan, the Mediterranean (oursins, ricci), Chile. Gathered at low tide by shore peoples worldwide — hence their place in the omnivore catalogue. Adjective: echinoid.
  • Street urchin: “a ragged urchin begging at the corner”; “Dickens’s urchins” (Oliver, the Artful Dodger, Jo the crossing-sweeper). Connotation: poverty + mischief + resilience; often affectionate (“you little urchin!” to a muddy child). The American equivalent leans to gamin, ragamuffin, guttersnipe; the British literary default is urchin.
  • Obsolete hedgehog sense: “urchon” in medieval bestiaries and cookbooks; survives only inside “sea urchin” and in heraldry/folklore (urchins as witches’ familiars in Shakespeare: “urchins, ouphes” in The Tempest — there meaning goblins/hedgehog-spirits).
  • Sporadic extensions: urchin for any small prickly thing (botanical “burr-urchins” in older writing); capitalised Urchin as a nickname; zoological compounds (heart-urchin, cake-urchin, helmet-urchin).
  • Register and collocation: “sea urchin” is neutral-scientific and culinary; bare “urchin” for a child is literary-journalistic (“street urchins of Victorian London”) and jocular-affectionate in families. Menus say oursin, uni, ricci; newspapers say urchin; marine biologists say echinoid.
  • Polysemy bridge: hedgehog → sea-hedgehog (shape metaphor) and → prickly child (behaviour/appearance metaphor: spiky hair, ragged coat like spines, defensive cheek). Both metaphors were live by the 1500s; the parent sense then died, stranding two children who no longer resemble each other.

For review: if the word swims and stings, it is the echinoderm; if it runs shoeless through Dickens, it is the child. The passage’s “sea __________” wants the animal — one more spiny mouthful in the omnivore’s menu, cracked open on the rocks and eaten raw like the Fuegians’ fish.
