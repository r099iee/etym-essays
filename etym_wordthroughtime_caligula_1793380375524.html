- 1st c. CE (c. 14–41 CE) — from affectionate camp pet-name to hated moniker: coined as an affectionate nickname by Rhine legionaries for toddler Gaius wearing miniature soldier sandals (caligae); despised by Gaius himself as emperor, who punished its utterance as lese-majesty.
- 1st–2nd c. CE — codified as the quintessential imperial monster: Roman biographers Suetonius and Tacitus immortalize Caligula as the definitive historical paradigm of sudden moral degeneration, psychotic sadism, and despotic folly.
- Middle Ages & Renaissance — moral exemplum of pagan tyranny: cited by Christian moralists and dramatists alongside Nero and Herod to illustrate the divine retribution that awaits sinful sovereigns and the dangers of uncurbed power.
- 18th–19th c. — rhetorical antonomasia for capricious malice: adopted by polemicists and critics (including Ruskin) as literary shorthand for arbitrary, irrational malice, envious destruction, and the humiliating abuse of authority.
- 20th c. — existential archetype of absurdity: Albert Camus reinterprets Caligula (1944) as an existential rebel seeking impossible absolute freedom through deliberate, horrifying nihilism; modern popular culture permanently cements the name with decadent, grotesque excess.
- Today — journalistic and political metaphor: routinely invoked in educated political discourse to describe erratic, vindictive autocrats or reckless executives who govern through vindictive tantrums and personal whim.
