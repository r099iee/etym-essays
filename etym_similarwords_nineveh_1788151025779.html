• Babylon: similar because both were the supreme Mesopotamian capitals, both famous for empire and luxury and both biblical types of imperial downfall; differs because Babylon flourished longer, under different peoples, is linked to the Exile and the Hanging Gardens, and was never annihilated as abruptly as Nineveh.
  • Assur: similar because both were great Assyrian cities, and Assur was the ancient religious capital of Assyria; differs because Assur was the old cult center (of the god Ashur) and administrative seat of the earlier empire, while Nineveh was Sennacherib’s new showpiece capital on the Tigris.
  • Kalhu (Nimrud): similar because it was the immediate predecessor capital of the Assyrian Empire (Assurnasirpal II) and equally rich in palaces and sculpture; differs because Nineveh superseded it and is today the more famous of the two, and Nimrud was sacked by ISIL in 2015 alongside Nineveh’s walls.
  • Tyre: similar because Kipling pairs them (‘one with Nineveh and Tyre’) as once-great cities that fell; differs because Tyre was a maritime commercial city of the Phoenicians, survived sieges for centuries, and its site stayed inhabited, while Nineveh was abandoned after a single cataclysm.
  • Persepolis: similar because both were opulent imperial capitals sacked and burned (Persepolis by Alexander in 330 BCE), their ruins symbolizing lost splendor; differs because Persepolis was ritual and ceremonial rather than Nineveh’s war-machine capital, and Persia survived its burning while Assyria did not.
  • Palmyra: similar because both were great Near Eastern cities whose ruins stand for vanished glory and which were newly devastated in the 21st century; differs because Palmyra was a Roman-era caravan-trading city destroyed in 273 CE, never an empire’s capital.
  • Rome: similar because both were world-state capitals whose fall became proverbial (‘Roma’ and ‘Nineveh’ as imperial types); differs because Rome survived, was reborn, and became a spiritual center, whereas Nineveh was erased and only later disinterred.
  • Ur: similar because both are celebrated Mesopotamian cities with monumental ruins and rich burials; differs because Ur was an early Sumerian city of the third millennium, famed for its ziggurat and royal tombs, not a late imperial capital.
  • Carthage: similar because both were great ancient cities whose total destruction became proverbial, and Will Durant’s same book mourns both; differs because Carthage was a Phoenician commercial empire-city refounded and long-lived, while Nineveh was a land empire’s capital destroyed in a single coalition sack.
  • Hattusa: similar because both were great Near Eastern imperial capitals (the Hittites) destroyed and abandoned; differs because Hattusa is far less known and its story lacks the biblical and literary afterlife that made Nineveh proverbial.
