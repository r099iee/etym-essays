- Old English — a solemn message: ǣrende is a message or mission entrusted by a lord, carried by envoys and even angels — weighty, official, sometimes sacred.
- Middle English — missions great and small: knights, pilgrims, and servants alike ride on errands; the word covers anything from diplomacy to fetching wine.
- 16th c. — mockery enters: the fool’s errand turns the faithful messenger into a joke, sending innocents after nonexistent goods.
- 18th–19th c. — domestication: errands shrink to household rounds, and the errand boy becomes a fixture of shops and offices — youth, bicycles, and boundless running about.
- 20th c. — the plural takes over: running errands becomes the standard Saturday phrase, batched, listed, and motorised, while the solemn singular survives in proverbs and fantasy.
- Today — humble but honourable: errands mean everyday diligence and helpfulness, with the old messenger gravity echoing only in phrases like errand of mercy.
