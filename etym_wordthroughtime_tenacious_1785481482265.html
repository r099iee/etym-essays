- [Learned Latinism (early 1600s)] - Borrowed from Latin tenāx, "grasping, holding fast," tenacious entered English first in technical and scholarly prose, carrying the physical sense of a firm, clinging grip.
- [Extension from body to mind (17th–19th c.)] - The word spread from physical clinging to figurative hold — tenacious memories, tenacious weeds, tenacious opinions — preserving the tactile force of a thing that will not let go.
- [Modern register] - Today tenacious is admiring but also wary: it celebrates the sheer adhesive strength of a grip, but the same clinging persistence can shade into stubborn refusal to be dislodged — firmness of hold itself, for better and worse.
