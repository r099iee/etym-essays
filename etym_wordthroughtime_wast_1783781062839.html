Old English Two Past Forms - Wast descends from the Old English past of wesan ("to be"), which already kept two second-person forms, wǣre (inherited) and, later, the analogical form built on was with the -st ending of the strong verbs.
Middle English Analogical Growth - Middle English creates wast (was + -st) in the 13th century on the pattern of satst and didst, and it becomes a standard "thou"-form alongside wert for the second-person past of be.
Early Modern Biblical Usage - The King James Bible, Shakespeare, and Milton use wast for plain indicative existence ("Thou from the first Wast present"), reserving wert for the subjunctive — a solemn, intimate, hieratic form of address.
Contemporary Sense - Today wast is archaic, surviving in liturgy and poetry; modern English uses were (and was) for all persons, while the thou-forms live on only as deliberate archaism.
