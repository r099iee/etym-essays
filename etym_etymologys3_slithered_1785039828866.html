Slither is a frequentative verb — a verb built to repeat its action in small, grating, uncontrolled units — and 'slithered' is its regular past tense. Its history runs from a Proto-Indo-European root for slipping, through the strong verbs and frequentative habits of Old English and Middle English, down to the serpent, the mountain path and the modern language of unsteady motion, without ever losing the friction that is its signature. The root and the Germanic family: The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *sleydʰ- 'to slide, to slip,' reconstructed from a set of Germanic and other Indo-European forms and securely established as the ancestor of the English sliding-words. From it Proto-Germanic built the strong verb *slīþaną 'to slide, to slip, to glide,' which became Old English slīdan — a class I strong verb whose past forms (slād, sliden) are attested in the early English records. Slīdan is the ancestor of modern slide, and around it grew a whole Germanic family of slipping and slithering words: Old English slīma 'slime' (the slippery stuff of sliding things), the related slidrian 'to slide, to slip repeatedly,' and, far afield, German schlittern 'to slide,' Middle High German slīten, and the slithery set of modern dialect words. The root idea everywhere is loss of friction — the body failing to grip — and the earliest English senses are all about slipping on wet or smooth ground. The frequentative step: The crucial transformation happened in Old English and Middle English. English, like its Germanic relatives, built new verbs from old ones by adding a frequentative or iterative suffix that meant the action was repeated, prolonged or broken into small units: float gave flutter, and slide gave slideren. Middle English slideren (and the variant slitheren) meant 'to slide, to slip, to move with a sliding, grating, unsteady motion,' and it is attested from the 14th century. The word carried the sound and feel of its action: the repeated -er- made the sliding go on and on, and the verb sat thick with friction — the grating, scraping, semi-controlled motion of sliding under imperfect control. By the 16th century the -th- spelling of slither was established, and the modern verb was born with its long, slithering, friction-heavy sound — so onomatopoeic that speakers still choose it precisely to hear the scrape. The 16th-18th centuries and the doublet with slide: For two centuries slither lived as the dialectal and literary doublet of slide — a rustic, energetic cousin used in the north of England and Scotland and in prose that wanted vigour. Where slide was smooth, deliberate and under control, slither was grating, unsteady and repeated; where slide belonged to polished surfaces and skaters, slither belonged to mud, scree and loose ground. The word's dialectal life kept it warm: it survived in everyday northern English while standard 'slide' took the polite register. The snake and the 19th century: The 19th century fixed slither's most famous association. As natural history and Romantic writing turned their attention to reptiles, slither became the signature verb of the snake: the long, writhing, noiseless glide of the serpent, described with the verb's combination of friction and sinuousness. From this association the word gathered its modern colour: slithering is writhing, ungraceful, repetitive, slightly sinister — the motion of the snake and, by metaphor, of anything that moves evasively and without dignity. A slithery politician, a slithering rumour, a career that 'slithers' into decline all borrow the serpent's motion. The noun slither (a glide, a snake-like path) and the adjective slithery (slippery, evasive) grew from the same stem. Literary afterlife: In The Hobbit (1937) Tolkien uses the word at a threshold: 'Bilbo never forgot the way they slithered and slipped in the dusk down the steep zig-zag path into the secret valley of Rivendell.' The verb carries its full history — the Old English sliding, the Middle English frequentative grating, the 19th-century writhing — into a steep, loose, light-failing descent where the travellers neither walk nor fall but skid and catch and skid again. The pairing with 'slipped' sets the prolonged, repeated slide against the sudden, short one, and 'never forgot' charges the whole motion with danger and strangeness. In modern English, slithered remains the exact word for any long, writhing, friction-losing glide — of snakes, of travellers on loose stone, of anything that wants to grip and cannot. The word has drifted from 'slipping on wet ground' to 'the serpent's glide' to 'any unsteady, evasive progress,' yet its root — loss of friction, repeated and uncontrolled — has never changed in over a thousand years.
