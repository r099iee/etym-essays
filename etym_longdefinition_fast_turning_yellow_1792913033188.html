Fast turning yellow is a descriptive phrase for foliage that is rapidly losing its green colour and going golden, the classic early-autumn change in which chlorophyll drains away and yellow pigments stand revealed.

In the source passage the hobbits halt under an elm whose leaves, though fast turning yellow, are still thick, so that the tree still shelters them from lessening rain. The phrase marks a precise seasonal moment: past mid-day on a wet autumn walk, summer green already failing, gold spreading leaf by leaf, yet the canopy still whole enough to keep the travellers fairly dry while they laugh over Elvish drink.

Core meaning in detail: Green leaves are green because chlorophyll masks the yellow-orange carotenoids beneath. As days shorten and cold comes, the tree seals each leaf stalk, chlorophyll breaks down fast, and the hidden gold appears within days. Elm leaves do this unevenly, branch by branch, so that a single crown can be half green and half gold. Fast here measures the visible speed of the change across a wet week; turning names the gradual process rather than a finished state; yellow names the revealed pigment, from pale straw to deep honey gold.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Autumn foliage going gold (primary literal sense, the source use): leaves shifting from green to yellow as chlorophyll fades and carotenoids show through. The bridge is direct botany: growth pigment withdraws, reserve pigment remains, and the eye reads the swap as turning.
  • Any plant part yellowing quickly (horticultural extension): corn fast turning yellow in drought, a lawn yellowing after heat. The bridge generalises the elm to every green tissue under stress, where haste signals trouble rather than season.
  • Chlorosis and disease (technical plant pathology): leaves yellowing from nitrogen lack, virus, or waterlogging. The bridge runs through diagnosis: the gardener reads sudden yellow as hunger or sickness, the same colour with a different cause from autumn.
  • Ripening towards gold (food and harvest): wheat, pears, and cheese turning yellow as starches sweeten. The bridge is chemistry shared with leaves: pigments unmask or build as tissues mature, and yellow announces readiness.
  • Human yellowing by age or illness (figurative and medical): pages, teeth, skin, and eyes turning yellow with years, smoke, or jaundice. The bridge is analogy of fading freshness: what was white or green dulls toward yellow, and fast turning warns of decline rather than season.
  • Yellow as fading courage (idiom and slang): to turn yellow means to grow cowardly, and a yellow press sensationalises. The bridge is cultural symbolism laid over colour: medieval and modern English alike painted cowardice, sickness, and cheap sensation yellow, so that turning names a moral as well as a botanical change.

Register and connotation: fast turning yellow is plain nature-writing English, observational and unhurried even while it reports haste. It belongs to walkers, gardeners, and poets rather than laboratories, though botanists would recognise every step it describes. The tone is gently elegiac: beauty deepening even as life withdraws, gold arriving as green departs. In Tolkien the phrase warms a wet lunch-hour, the yellowing elm becoming a roof, a calendar, and a small promise that autumn gold can shelter travellers as surely as summer shade.
