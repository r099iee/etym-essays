- Old English / medieval: the verb rōwan is a plain working word for a practical task; the person who rows is an oarsman of ferry, fishing boat or warship — humble, functional, no sporting sense. - 16th–18th c.: "rower" stabilises as the everyday English word for any oarsman; Thames watermen and wherrymen ply for hire; rowers are ordinary working people on rivers and coasts. - 19th c.: the sporting revolution — regattas and boat races (Oxford–Cambridge 1829, Henley 1839); the rower acquires prestige as a gentleman-amateur athlete. - 20th c.: rowing joins the Olympics (1900); the word becomes fully gender-neutral and standard, while "oarsman" begins to fade; school and club rowing spread. - Modern: "rowers" means athletes, hobbyists and indoor rowing-machine users alike; the old galley-slave image survives only in historical novels.
