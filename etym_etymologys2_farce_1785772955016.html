FARCE has no securely reconstructible Proto-Indo-European etymon: Latin farcīre is widely treated as of unknown or uncertain origin, so the chain begins at the earliest securely attested stage and proceeds to modern English. Stage 1 — Latin farcīre 'to stuff, to cram, to fill full' (attested from the 2nd century BCE in Roman comedy and satire; fourth-conjugation verb, farciō, farcīre, farxī/farsī, fartum/farctum). Stage 2 — Gallo-Romance continuation: Latin farcīre develops into Old French farsir / farcir 'to stuff', and from the verb (or its participle) the noun farce is formed in Old French with the meaning 'stuffing, forcemeat — a seasoned mixture crammed into meat or fowl' (attested from the 12th century). Stage 3 — Old French farce 'stuffing, forcemeat' extends metaphorically into performance: comic interludes, clowning, and earthy sketches inserted into long religious mystery and morality plays are conceived as material 'stuffed into' the serious drama (13th–15th centuries). Stage 4 — Middle French farce 'a comic interlude; a short comic play' solidifies as an independent genre label (15th century; the genre flourishes in 16th-century French comedy). Stage 5 — English farce 'a comic play or interlude; specifically a broad, exaggerated comedy' (borrowed from French, first attested c. 1520s; the culinary sense 'stuffing' also entered English, later refashioned as forcemeat). Stage 6 — Modern English FARCE 'a light, absurd dramatic work of exaggerated comedy; and, by figurative extension, an absurd, ridiculous, or sham event or proceeding' (figurative use in steady evidence from the 18th century, dominant in modern journalism). Note on the derivation: the PIE ancestry of Latin farcīre is debated and not securely established; standard reference works (e.g., de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin) treat farcīre as of unknown origin, with no reliable Indo-European cognates, so no PIE root stage can be asserted without guesswork. The Romance and English stages above are securely attested.
