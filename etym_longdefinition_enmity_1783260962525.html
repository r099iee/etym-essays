enmity (noun) — the state or feeling of active opposition or hostility; the condition of being an enemy; settled, mutual ill-will between individuals, groups, or nations. In Macaulay's History of England the word appears in the compressed phrase "after ages of enmity," describing the centuries of Anglo-Scottish hostility that preceded the Union of 1707. The nuance is deliberately abstract and historical: enmity here does not name a single battle, a feud between individuals, or a passing emotion, but a settled, mutual, institutionalized condition of being enemies — sustained across generations by border warfare, dynastic rivalry, religious division, and recurrent invasion. It serves as the dark foil to the "indissoluble ties of interest and affection" that follow; the sentence turns on the starkness of the reversal. CORE SENSES
  • The state of being an enemy. The primary and oldest sense: the condition or fact of being enemies with someone — mutual, durable opposition, the natural antonym of friendship.
  • Feeling / hostility. The hostile feeling or attitude that accompanies that state: active ill-will, hatred, or opposition directed at a person, group, or cause.
  • Collective / national sense. Organized hostility between peoples, nations, factions, or states — the sense Macaulay exploits for the centuries of Anglo-Scottish conflict, and the sense common in diplomatic and historical prose.
  • Religious / cosmic sense (biblical). In scriptural and theological usage enmity can be total and even cosmic — the "enmity" between good and evil, between God and sin — a moral state of irreconcilable opposition.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED MEANINGS
  • Personal and familial. A long-standing feud or grudge between individuals or families (an old enmity), with the sense of recurrence and mutual hatred.
  • Political and factional. In modern political prose, enmity between parties, blocs, or nations, often institutionalized and distinct from mere disagreement or rivalry.
  • Abstract usage. As an uncountable abstraction, enmity can name hostility as a general phenomenon — "the enmity of the crowd," "personal enmity" — where the state rather than any single act is meant.
BOUNDARIES AGAINST NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • enmity vs hostility. hostility emphasizes active opposition and can be momentary or one-sided, and is often used of actions; enmity is the mutual, durable state of being enemies.
  • enmity vs animosity. animosity is passionate, energetic, often personal ill-feeling; it is hotter but less institutional than enmity.
  • enmity vs antagonism. antagonism is opposition in principle or structure — intellectual, impersonal, closer to rivalry than to hatred.
  • enmity vs antipathy. antipathy is instinctive dislike or aversion, a matter of temperament rather than of declared conflict.
  • enmity vs hatred. hatred is the strong emotional feeling; enmity is the state that may contain it — one can hate without declared enmity, and enmity can persist without intense hatred.
  • enmity vs feud. a feud is the concrete, ongoing quarrel — a sequence of vengeful acts; enmity is the underlying state that a feud expresses.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE AND HISTORY The word's anatomy is a negation: Latin inimīcitia, "the state of being an enemy," from inimīcus, "not-a-friend" (in- "not" + amīcus "friend"), and ultimately from amāre, "to love." The state of being enemies is thus etymologically the state of being not-friends — a negative image of friendship itself, which is why enmity and amity (from amīcitia, "friendship") are exact antonyms built from the same root. English received the word through Old French enemisté and Middle English enmite in the 13th–14th centuries. Its career has moved from the personal (an individual enemy) to the collective (the enmity of nations) to the cosmic (biblical enmity between good and evil), and in Macaulay's prose it carries the full historical weight of "ages of enmity" — the long mutual hostility of two nations, compressed into a single dignified abstraction and reversed in a single turn of the sentence.
