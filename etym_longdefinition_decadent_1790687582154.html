Decadent, as an adjective, most gravely means marked by moral, cultural, or artistic decline — falling away from a prior standard of vigour, virtue, or taste. A decadent society is imagined as overripe and enervated; decadent art as refined beyond health into artificiality; decadent manners as luxurious and self-indulgent at the expense of discipline. The historical prototype is late imperial Rome, and every later use borrows something of that tableau: splendour shadowed by rot. As a noun, a decadent names a person embodying such decline — or, in the specialised literary sense, an adherent of Decadent aesthetics.

In that specialised capital-D sense, Decadent names the late nineteenth-century movement in France and England — Baudelaire’s heirs, Verlaine, Huysmans, Wilde, Beardsley — that cultivated artifice, perversity, and exquisite decay as artistic virtues. Here the word’s moral charge is deliberately inverted: what respectable critics called sickness the poets called honesty, preferring the hothouse orchid to the oak. To write of ‘Decadent prose’ in this sense is to invoke jewelled surfaces, languorous rhythms, and the perfume of the forbidden, a style performing decline as triumph.

In contemporary everyday use the word has softened into playful hyperbole for luxurious self-indulgence. A decadent dessert, weekend, or hotel is extravagantly rich and pleasurable, with only a winking acknowledgment of guilt — the fall in question being from one’s diet or budget rather than from civilisation. Between these poles lies the continuing critical use: commentators diagnose decadent politics, decadent universities, or a decadent culture whenever they wish to allege that comfort has corroded purpose, a usage that always implies, and rarely defends, some lost golden age.

  • In decline (historical-moral): exhibiting the decay of a civilisation, institution, or art, e.g. ‘decadent late-imperial sculpture’; condemnatory, comparative, nostalgic for lost vigour.
  • Fin-de-siècle aesthetic (literary): belonging to Decadence as a movement — artificial, refined, transgressive, e.g. ‘decadent prose-poems’; can be descriptive, admiring, or scandalised depending on speaker.
  • Morally dissolute: given to luxury and sensual excess, e.g. ‘a decadent aristocracy’; the pulpit sense, fallenness as vice.
  • Luxuriously indulgent (colloquial): deliciously extravagant, e.g. ‘a decadent fudge sundae’; affectionate hyperbole, guilt worn lightly.
  • Overrefined (stylistic): of art or prose, so elaborate as to seem unhealthy, e.g. ‘decadent ornament’; bridges the aesthetic and moral senses.
  • A person of decline (noun): one living in or embodying decadence, e.g. ‘the weary decadents of the quarter’; from Roman senators in Gibbon to velvet-jacketed poets of the 1890s.

What unites these senses is the downward metaphor: health is up, decay is down, and the decadent slides. Whether the slide appals (the moralist), fascinates (the aesthete), or merely amuses (the diner before the chocolate cake) depends on how seriously the speaker takes the height fallen from — which is why denying that something is decadent, as the source sentence does, is a way of refusing the whole economy of fall and nostalgia.
