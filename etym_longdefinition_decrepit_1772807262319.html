Grandmother Jia’s dismissal of Nannie Wang as too old and decrepit is blunt household judgment, not medical diagnosis. In this aristocratic service world the word means worn past usefulness: creaking with age, short of energy, no longer able to supply the alert, tireless attendance that the fragile and high-status Dai-yu requires. Paired with Snowgoose’s opposite flaw (too young and irresponsible), decrepit marks the lower bound of fitness for intimate service. The tone is unsentimental and managerial; affection for an old nannie does not outweigh the household’s need for a capable body-servant, which is why Nightingale—Grandmother Jia’s own second-grade maid—is reassigned. Core meaning. Decrepit means weakened, worn out, and infirm with age or long use; of a person, elderly and enfeebled; of a building, vehicle, or object, dilapidated and near collapse. The word implies more than old age: it pictures something that has been “creaked” and cracked into near-uselessness, a machine or body broken down by time. Etymological and conceptual bridge. Decrepit comes from Latin dēcrepitus, “worn out, broken down, infirm from age,” formed from de- “down, thoroughly” and crepāre “to rattle, creak, crack.” The original image is acoustic and physical: a decrepit thing is one that has creaked and cracked its way to the end of its strength, like an old door or an old man’s joints. The Latin adjective was already specialized for extreme old age, and English borrowed the whole package in the late medieval to early modern period, later adding the abstract noun decrepitude. Polysemy and secondary senses (exhaustive):
  • Of persons: enfeebled and nearly spent with age; infirm, worn out; “a decrepit old man.”
  • Of animals: aged and broken-down, past useful service; a decrepit horse.
  • Of buildings and structures: fallen into disrepair, dilapidated, likely to collapse; “a decrepit building scheduled for demolition.”
  • Of objects and machines: rusted, broken, past repair; a decrepit car, chair, or bridge.
  • Of institutions, systems, or arguments (figurative): decayed, no longer functioning effectively; “a decrepit political system.”
  • Metaphoric transfer: anything whose strength has been “cracked out” by long use, whether body, fabric, or regime—the word’s unique image is the creak of collapse rather than mere age.
  • Note on mental state: decrepit describes physical and structural decay, not mental decline; it should not be confused with senile, which targets dementia.
Register and collocations. Standard-to-formal and often literary or clinical in tone; the word can be pitying or contemptuous depending on context. Common collocations: decrepit old man / woman, a decrepit building / car / chair / bridge, decrepit with age, and the abstract decrepitude (the state of being decrepit). In this passage the word does managerial work: it is the household’s cold assessment that Nannie Wang has passed the point of usefulness, and the similarWords list (frail, infirm, dilapidated, senile, worn-out, run-down) sets out the precise boundaries of that assessment.
