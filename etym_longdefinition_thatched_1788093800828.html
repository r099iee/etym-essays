Thatched is the past participle and participial adjective of the verb thatch, meaning "covered with a roof of straw, reeds, rushes, sedge, heather or similar plant material, laid in overlapping layers and fixed to a roof frame". In the source passage from Tolkien's The Hobbit, "fire leaped from thatched roofs and wooden beam-ends" as the dragon Smaug hurtles over Lake-town: the flammable straw roofing catches instantly, and the whole town blazes. The word's very first meaning in Old English was simply "roofed, covered" — for the ancestor word meant "roof" or "covering" in general — and only later narrowed to the straw-and-reed technique.

The word is a direct descendant of the Indo-European root *(s)teg- "to cover", which also produced Latin tegere "to cover" (whence English protect, detect, tegument and toga), Latin tegula "roof-tile" (whence English tile), and Greek stégē "roof". English thatch and tile are thus distant cousins — doublets from the same ancient root, one Germanic, one Latin.

1. Primary sense — the craft and the material

  • Thatched roof: a roof covered with bundles of dried vegetation — traditionally water reed (Phragmites australis), long wheat straw, combed wheat reed, sedge, rushes or heather — laid in thick overlapping courses, fixed with hazel or wire "sways", and finished with a ridged or rounded crest.
  • To thatch (v.): to cover a roof in this way; thatcher (n.): the craftsperson; thatching (n.): the craft itself, one of the oldest building crafts in Britain, practised continuously for millennia.
  • The material is waterproof by layering: each course overlaps the one below, so water runs off the slope rather than penetrating; a well-made thatch can last forty to sixty years (longer for water reed).
  • It is also a good insulator — thick and light — which is why it remained the vernacular roofing of choice in rural Britain for centuries.

2. Secondary senses — transferred uses

  • Thatch of hair (colloquial, 19th c.): a thick head of hair; a person with dense hair is jokingly "well thatched" — the hair is imagined as a roof of straw on the head.
  • Thatch palm / thatch plant: palms and grasses whose leaves are used for roofing in tropical regions; the word is used of roofing material generally.
  • Thatch ant (informal): a wood ant whose large mound-nest is built of a "thatched" heap of plant litter.
  • In conservation and heritage discourse, "thatched" describes listed buildings and historic districts whose roofs preserve the traditional craft.

3. The semantic bridge

  • Old English þæc meant "roof, covering" of any kind, and the verb þeccan meant simply "to cover" (cognate with Old Norse þak "roof", German Dach "roof", Dutch dak).
  • Because in early medieval England most roofs were made of straw and reeds, the general word "roof/cover" narrowed to mean specifically "roof of straw" — the default technology became the default meaning.
  • The narrowing parallels the opposite path of tile: when clay tiles and slates became the prestige roofing of towns, "thatched" came to mark the rural, the vernacular, the old-fashioned — and eventually the picturesque.
  • The hair metaphor then re-uses the visual similarity between a thick straw roof and a thick head of hair, a transfer typical of colloquial wordplay in the 19th century.

4. Cultural history and modern life

  • In medieval England thatching was regulated by manorial custom; after the Great Fire of London (1666) new building regulations effectively banned thatched roofs in the capital, and fire risk has shadowed the material ever since.
  • In the 18th–19th centuries the "thatched cottage" became an emblem of the rural idyll in poetry, painting and the picturesque movement; Thomas Hardy's Far from the Madding Crowd (1874) contains a celebrated description of the thatching of a rick.
  • Today thatching is a protected heritage craft; thatched buildings are treasured, insured at a premium, and protected by fire-safety regulations; the word "thatched" instantly evokes chocolate-box Englishness, though thatched roofs are common across Europe, Asia and Africa wherever reeds and straw abound.
  • In modern English the adjective is used almost always of roofs and (jokingly) of hair, and its ancient generic sense "covered" survives only in fossilized contexts.

5. Summary

Thatched is a word that carries its whole history on its surface: an Indo-European root meaning "cover", a Germanic word for "roof", a medieval default technology of straw and reed, and a modern heritage image — plus a joke about hair. In Tolkien's burning Lake-town it does what it has always done: it names the most flammable, most human, most ancient kind of roof there is.
