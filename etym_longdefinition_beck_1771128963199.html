Beck is a noun denoting a silent signal or summons, most commonly a movement of the head or hand by which one person commands another to approach, to act, or to wait. Unlike a spoken order, a beck needs no words: a slight inclination of the head, a quick motion of the fingers, a raised hand — the gesture is enough because the authority behind it is assumed. In the reading context supplied with this card — 'The servants were expected to be at the ______ and call of their master at all hours of the day and night' — the word appears inside the fixed idiom 'beck and call,' which names the state of total, unquestioning readiness to obey the slightest sign. The idiom has become so dominant that for most modern readers it is the only context in which the noun is met at all. The word is steeped in the imagery of hierarchy. A beck presupposes two parties locked in a relation of command and obedience: the one who signals is empowered to expect an instant response, and the one who receives the signal has surrendered the right to hesitate. This is why the idiom 'at one's beck and call' so often appears in descriptions of domestic servitude, courtly dependence, or clientelism — the master's merest twitch of muscle is sufficient to mobilise subordinates. The tone is of absolute authority on one side and reactive submission on the other, and the word itself, being silent, makes the power relation feel automatic and unnegotiable. The full polysemy of the noun can be set out exhaustively:
  • Sense 1 — the gesture itself: a nod or motion of the head or hand used to summon, call, or command. Historically the active, literal sense, still found in archaic and literary writing ('he gave her a beck to approach').
  • Sense 2 — the idiom 'beck and call' (also 'beck or call'): readiness to serve or obey instantly, on the merest sign; the sense in which the word survives for virtually all modern speakers. Note the idiom originally paired the silent gesture (beck) with the spoken summons (call), and the two together signified 'by word or sign, at any hour.'
  • Sense 3 — a broader signal or sign (historical): in older usage a beck could be any sign of intent, a hint, or a token, not necessarily a summons — the wide sense it inherited from its verb and from the Old English sign-words it grew out of.
  • Homonym — beck, 'a small stream': an entirely different word, from Old Norse bekkr 'brook, stream,' common in northern English and Scottish place-names and dialect. It shares no etymology with the gesture-noun and is distinguished purely by context.
Historically the noun grew out of the verb 'to beck' — to make a mute sign — which itself came down from the Old English verbs of sign-making, ultimately the same family as the word 'beacon.' The semantic logic is continuous: a beacon is a signal visible from afar; a beck is the same idea reduced to the intimate scale of the body, a sign meant for a single person in a single room. The word has therefore always carried a double connotation of visibility and of command, and its modern confinement to 'beck and call' has only sharpened the sense of helpless, total availability that makes the idiom so expressive of servitude.
