Cask in its primary sense is a stout barrel-shaped vessel, traditionally built of oak staves hooped with iron, used for storing, transporting, and ageing liquids — above all wine, beer, cider, rum, and whisky — and by extension the quantity such a vessel holds.

The source fragment It was a cask is only the opening of a longer description now cut away, but even this stub shows the word doing its plainest work: naming the object itself, the familiar bulging barrel of cellar, ship hold, and tavern. The indefinite article and the bare noun suggest a moment of bare identification, the narrator registering what the thing is before saying more.

Core meaning in detail: A classic wooden cask is a cooper's masterpiece: tapered oak staves held without nails by the pressure of iron or wooden hoops, the bulge called the bilge giving strength and allowing the barrel to be rolled and turned. Oak matters chemically as well as structurally, since the wood breathes, admits tiny doses of oxygen, and lends tannin, vanilla, and toast notes to the liquid within — which is why whisky law in Scotland demands oak casks and why wine writers speak of cask influence as a flavour in its own right. Standard sizes carry ancient names: the firkin, kilderkin, barrel, hogshead, butt, puncheon, and tun form a ladder of capacity from small to vast.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Wooden barrel for liquids (primary sense, from the 15th c.): a cask of wine, water casks on a ship, the cider cask in the farm cellar. The bridge is the object itself: the coopered vessel is the original and still dominant referent.
  • The contents or quantity held (metonymy): to broach a cask, to finish a cask of ale. The bridge is container for contents: naming the vessel names what is inside it, as with to drink a bottle.
  • Cask-conditioned ale (British beer jargon, 20th c.): real ale that undergoes secondary fermentation in the cask and is served unfiltered and unpasteurised. The bridge narrows the vessel to a method: the cask becomes not just packaging but a brewing stage, championed by CAMRA against keg beer.
  • Cask strength and cask finish (whisky jargon): whisky bottled undiluted from the cask, or finished in a second cask such as sherry or port wood. The bridge runs from vessel to prestige label: the cask authenticates the spirit, so naming it sells rarity and craft.
  • Australian cask wine (regional, late 20th c.): box wine in a bladder inside a cardboard box, confusingly still called a cask. The bridge is functional analogy: it stores and dispenses wine as a barrel once did, though no wood is involved.
  • Figurative powder-cask (by analogy with powder keg): a volatile situation ready to explode, as in the region is a powder cask. The bridge is the sealed vessel of force: like gunpowder in a barrel, tension packed tight awaits a spark, though powder keg is the more usual form.

Register and connotation: cask smells of oak, cellars, and craft — warmer and older than industrial drum or container, more technical than barrel in wine and whisky talk where it signals tradition and quality. A cask-aged whisky promises patience; a cask of water promises a voyage; a cask of wine promises a feast. The word never quite left the age of sail and the cooperage, and drinks marketing keeps it there on purpose.
