decree entered English in the 14th century from Old French decre, itself from Latin dēcrētum, the neuter past participle of dēcernere, “to decide, determine, decree.” The Latin verb is a compound of dē-, “down, off, away,” and cernere, “to sift, separate, distinguish, decide.” The original image is agricultural and judicial at once: to sift grain is to separate what belongs from what does not, and to decide a case is to separate the claims.

cernere and its family

Cernere is one of the most productive verbs in Latin. It yields discernere (to separate, distinguish), secernere (to set apart), concernere (to mix, involve, hence English concern), certus (settled, certain), crimen (a charge), and discrimen (a dividing line, crisis). The root is Proto-Indo-European *krey-, “to sieve, sift,” which also underlies Greek krinein, “to separate, judge,” and krisis, “judgment,” the source of English crisis and critic. A decree is thus, etymologically, a sifted decision: the outcome of separating the true from the false and the admissible from the inadmissible.

Roman usage

In the Republic, dēcrētum was the formal decision of a body; the senate's senatus consultum was a decree, and magistrates issued decrees in their edicts. Under the Empire the decreta of the emperor became a source of law: imperial constitutions included edicts, rescripts, mandates and decrees, and the decree was the emperor's judgment in a case, which could acquire general force. Justinian's Digest and Code preserve many such decrees, and the medieval revival of Roman law made decretum a technical term across Europe.

Medieval church and state

  • Canon law. Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) organised the scattered decrees of councils and popes into a coherent body; the Decretales of Gregory IX (1234) became the second part of the Corpus Iuris Canonici. In this world a decree is a binding ruling with spiritual authority.
  • Royal legislation. Medieval kings issued decrees, ordinances and statutes; the word competed with assise, constitution and edict.
  • English equity. From the 15th century the Chancellor's court issued decrees in equity, and “decree” became the ordinary name for its final order, as opposed to the common law's “judgment.”

Modern legal streamlining

The Judicature Acts of 1873–1875 fused law and equity in England and Wales and unified the courts; procedural vocabulary was standardised on “judgment,” and “decree” retreated to matrimonial causes and to ecclesiastical and Scottish usage. The matrimonial survival is the best known: a decree nisi followed by a decree absolute was the mechanism of divorce until the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 replaced it in England and Wales with a conditional order followed by a final order.

Political usage

The 20th century gave the word a new polemical life. Fascist and communist governments legislated by decree; emergency powers let presidents rule without parliaments; “governance by decree” became a standard indictment in political journalism. At the same time the United States developed the consent decree, a court-supervised settlement that became a major instrument of civil-rights and environmental enforcement. The same word thus names both arbitrary rule and a device of legal accountability — a reminder that its meaning depends on who issues it and who can challenge it.

Semantic drift

  • 14th c.: an ordinance of a sovereign or a church council.
  • 15th–17th c.: a final order in equity and canon law; also God's providential ordinance.
  • 18th–19th c.: legislation by absolute rulers; the word acquires a tyrannical flavour in liberal polemic.
  • 20th c.: executive orders, emergency rule and consent decrees; the noun becomes a term of political argument.
  • 21st c.: largely formal; in journalism a synonym for diktat, and in law a technical term in Scotland and in US consent decrees.

Style and connotation today

“Decree” sounds older and heavier than “order”; it suggests ceremony, seal and unanswerability. That is why it survives in religious and legal formulas long after the institutions that produced it have changed, and why a novelist reaching for the atmosphere of absolute power will choose it over any modern synonym.

Phonetics and orthography

Old French decre lost the Latin final syllable, and English restored a final e to mark the long vowel. The spelling with -ee is fixed by the 15th century, distinguishing the word from degree in writing even though both end in the same sound. The stress fell on the second syllable in French and has stayed there in English, so the noun and verb are homographs and homophones: to decree and a decree differ only in syntax.
