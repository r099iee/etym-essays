Note on historical nuance: The semantic descent of Grantha presents a classic example of codicological metonymy in historical linguistics. The Proto-Indo-European verbal root *grendʰ- ("to knot, tie, intertwine") entered Indo-Aryan as the verbal root granth- / grath- ("to bind, tie, string together"). In ancient India, manuscript folios made of dried palm leaves (*tāla-patra*) were perforated with holes, through which a cord was passed and secured with a firm knot (*granthi*). By semantic extension, the physical knotted bundle became known as a grantha ("book, treatise"). In the Tamil-speaking south, where the local Tamil script lacked characters for non-Dravidian Sanskrit phonemes (such as voiced and aspirated consonants), a specialized script was engineered to write these Sanskrit texts. Scribes and scholars referred to this script as Grantha-lipi—literally, "the script used for writing Sanskrit books and treatises." Comparative philologists (Mayrhofer EWAia I 507, Pokorny 405) connect the root to Germanic forms such as Middle High German grintil ("bolt, crossbar") and Baltic formations signifying twining and boring.
