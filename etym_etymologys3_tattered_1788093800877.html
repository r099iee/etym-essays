The family of tattered is small, Scandinavian and stubbornly obscure at its root — and precisely because its prehistory is unknown, its recorded history is unusually clear. The word enters English as a loan from Old Norse tǫturr "a torn piece, rag", one of the several hundred Scandinavian words that the Viking settlements of the Danelaw (ninth–eleventh centuries) seeded into northern English speech: everyday, physical words such as sky, skin, skirt, knife, loft and tatter. Old Norse tǫturr itself has no secure etymology — the Oxford English Dictionary pronounces its further origin unknown — and scholars note only that its shape (t-…-t-, with the nominative -r) has the flavour of an expressive, perhaps imitative formation, the kind of word that mimics the flapping or ripping of torn cloth. The Scandinavian cognates persist in the modern languages (Icelandic tötur "a rag", Swedish tatter "a scrap"), confirming the loan's home dialect.

Middle English: taters and clouts. English records the noun from the fourteenth century (tater, tatters), where it means both a single torn scrap and, in the plural, ragged clothing — the medieval equivalent of "in rags". The word belongs to the plain vocabulary of poverty: alongside clout "a patch of cloth" and rag, it describes what the poor wore and what servants mended. At this stage it is a noun only: there is as yet no English verb, and no adjective.

The sixteenth century: the adjective is born. The great change comes in the 1500s, when two things happen at once. First, the spelling stabilises as tatter, with the double -tt- that gives the word its modern look. Second, English develops a denominative verb to tatter "to tear into tatters", and from it the participial adjective tattered "torn into tatters, ragged", recorded from the mid-sixteenth century. The drama of the 1580s–1600s is the word's first golden age: the playwrights write tatter'd (the apostrophe spelling of the period) of beggars' coats, soldiers' ensigns, banners and decayed finery, and the adjective immediately acquires its characteristic emotional colouring — ruin, hardship and endurance. A tattered flag is not merely damaged; it is a survivor, and the word begins to carry that heroic-pathetic undertone.

Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: consolidation and first figures. The seventeenth century adds the mock-heroic noun tatterdemalion "a ragged, beggarly fellow" (recorded from the early 1600s), which cements the social picture of the tattered man: a poor soldier, a discharged veteran, a vagabond. In the eighteenth century the figurative extension begins in earnest — reputations, fortunes and enterprises are said to be "in tatters" or "tattered" — and Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) records the plain senses. The tattered Continental army of the American Revolutionary War (Washington's ragged troops at Valley Forge and beyond) and later the tattered armies of the Napoleonic and American Civil Wars gave the collocation "tattered soldiers" a permanent heroic resonance in history writing and poetry: the tattered uniform proves the soldier's ordeal.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries: romanticism, reportage, metaphor. Romantic and Gothic prose makes tattered cloaks, tattered manuscripts and tattered curtains part of its furniture of decay and mystery; the image of the tattered flag becomes a patriotic topos in nineteenth-century verse on both sides of the Atlantic. In the twentieth century the metaphorical life of the word takes over the bulk of everyday use: "tattered hopes", "tattered reputation", "tattered economy" and "tattered nerves" are journalistic staples, while the literal senses remain vigorous for flags, clothing, books and sails. War reporting kept the literal sense alive ("the tattered city" after a siege), and popular fiction — J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit among it — preserves the word's plain descriptive dignity.

The card's own sentence. "An old bundle of tattered oddments that he used as a pillow" shows how precisely the word still works: it names the condition of the cloth while quietly sounding the whole historical theme of poverty, survival and battered value. Bilbo's pillow is tattered — composite, worn, pieced together from odds and ends — and the adjective does its work of making the reader see the rags even as the Arkenstone gleams inside them. In its full career, tattered has moved from a Norse loanword for a scrap of cloth, through the Elizabethan description of ragged soldiers and banners, to a modern metaphor for anything broken and surviving: always the same image, the fabric pulled into shreds and still hanging on.
