An ONTOLOGICAL METAPHOR is a type of conceptual metaphor in which an abstract, non-physical, or unbounded experience — an emotion, an idea, time, the mind, a mental state, an institution, a process — is understood and structured as a physical entity, a substance, a discrete object, or a bounded container. The concept was systematized by George Lakoff and Mark Johnson in Metaphors We Live By (1980), where they argued that human beings cannot reason directly about abstractions and therefore project the properties of concrete physical things onto them: we say that "the mind is a container," that "anger is a heated fluid in a container," that "inflation is an entity." Once the abstraction is treated as a physical thing, we can quantify it ("a lot of anger"), locate it ("anger is inside me"), refer to it ("the idea is on the table"), and reason about its parts ("the argument fell apart").

Core mechanisms (polysemy of the term):

  • Entity / substance metaphors: an abstraction is treated as a discrete thing or as an uncountable substance, e.g., "the mind is a machine," "anger is a fluid," "inflation is an entity that can rise or fall."
  • Container metaphors: an abstraction is treated as a bounded region with an inside and an outside, e.g., "the mind is a container," "I am out of patience," "get into the flow of the argument."
  • Personification: an abstraction is treated as a human being with intentions and agency, e.g., "inflation ate my savings," "fate smiled on him" — a vivid subcategory of ontological metaphor in which the entity is specifically a person.
  • Purpose and event metaphors: events and actions are treated as objects or as journeys, e.g., "finish the race," "I am going nowhere with this project," allowing activities to be measured and evaluated as things.

Role in cognitive linguistics:

  • Ontological metaphors are one of the three basic types of conceptual metaphor in Lakoff and Johnson's system, alongside structural metaphors (which structure one concept in terms of another, e.g., "argument is war") and orientational metaphors (which organize concepts in spatial terms, e.g., "happy is up, sad is down").
  • Unlike structural metaphors, which map a rich system of correspondences between two complex domains, ontological metaphors supply the very first act of objectification: they let speakers pick out an abstraction as an entity at all, so that subsequent structuring can occur.
  • The distinction is a matter of emphasis rather than a hard boundary — a single expression may be simultaneously structural and ontological, and personification is itself a special case of the entity metaphor.

Why the concept matters: ontological metaphors are not decorative rhetoric; cognitive linguists argue they are constitutive of everyday reasoning. By treating time as money ("I spent three hours"), the mind as a container ("that idea stayed with me"), and the economy as a body ("the economy is sick"), speakers inherit systematic ways of calculating, planning, and evaluating that would otherwise be impossible for purely abstract domains. The choice of ontology is consequential: treating an illness as an enemy ("fight cancer") versus as a journey ("living with cancer") changes attitudes, treatment choices, and emotional experience. Critics of the framework note, however, that the three-way classification is partly a descriptive convenience and that many metaphors do not fall cleanly into one bucket.

Domain usage:

  • Linguistics and cognitive science: a standard analytic term used to classify metaphors in corpora, in cross-linguistic comparison, and in work on embodied cognition and conceptual blending.
  • Philosophy of language and metaphysics: connected to reification and hypostatization — the treatment of an abstraction as a thing — a topic debated since classical antiquity, and to the modern question of whether such objectification is harmless convenience or a systematic distortion of reality.
  • Rhetoric, law, and pedagogy: used to explain how figurative language works, how ideology is naturalized in media and politics, and how legal and medical framings shape public opinion and decision-making.

Near-synonym boundaries:

  • Structural metaphor structures one complex concept by another ("argument is war"); ontological metaphor confers entity/container/substance status on an abstraction ("the mind is a container").
  • Orientational metaphor organizes concepts by spatial axes ("happy is up"); ontological metaphor organizes them by thinghood or boundedness rather than by direction.
  • Personification is a subtype of ontological metaphor that treats an abstraction as a person with agency; ontological metaphor more broadly treats it as any kind of physical thing.
  • Conduit metaphor is a specific ontological metaphor family for communication (ideas are objects, words are containers, communication is sending); it is one species of the genus, not the whole category.
