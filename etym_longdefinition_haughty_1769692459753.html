haughty: arrogantly proud and disdainful; behaving, speaking, or looking as though one considers oneself clearly superior to other people. Core meaning. Haughty describes an attitude, manner, or appearance of cold, aloof superiority. A haughty person looks down on others — literally, the way one looks down from a height — and treats them as beneath notice. It is an adjective used of people, of the way they hold themselves, of their tone of voice, and of the expression in their eyes: a "haughty glance," a "haughty air," a "haughty manner." Unlike plain "proud," haughty always carries disapproval: it names pride that has curdled into contempt for everyone else. Primary sense — disdainful superiority. In modern English this is the only living sense: arrogantly superior, condescending, aloof. Examples: "She dismissed them with a haughty wave"; "his haughty tone made the staff resent him"; "the hotel's haughty doorman." The word is still frequent in literary and formal writing and in journalism, though less common in casual speech. Historical senses that have faded or vanished.
  • Physical height — "high, elevated, lofty." When the word entered English in the 16th century from Old French haut ("high"), it first meant literally raised or elevated, and could describe mountains, towers, or lofty walls. This literal sense is now obsolete; only the figurative sense of moral/social elevation survives.
  • "High" in rank or station — noble, exalted, of high degree. In 16th–17th-century English, "haughty" could describe a person of elevated social rank without necessarily implying arrogance. This sense is now archaic.
  • Of a person's nature — "high-minded, proud, spirited." Early uses were sometimes neutral or even admiring (pride of noble spirit); only later did the word acquire its exclusively negative coloring.
Modern secondary shades and collocations.
  • Attitude: "haughty disdain," "haughty contempt," "haughty superiority" — the object of the haughtiness is named by a following noun.
  • Appearance: "haughty beauty," "a haughty stare," "a haughty lift of the chin" — the word readily attaches to looks and bearing.
  • Behavior: "haughty and reserved," "haughty, cold, and imperious" — in Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Darcy is "haughty, reserved, and fastidious," a cluster that defines his uninviting manners.
  • Register: formal-to-literary; it is stronger and more severe than "proud," more aristocratic in flavor than "conceited," and close in tone to "supercilious" and "disdainful."
Derived words. haughtiness (noun, "arrogant pride"), haughtily (adverb, "in a haughty manner"). Both are formed on the adjective and follow its register. The word also has an obsolete sibling "haught" ("high, lofty, proud") that preceded the -y form. The semantic bridge. The entire modern meaning rests on a spatial metaphor. A person physically high up looks down on what is below; transferred to society, a person of "high" rank or self-elevated spirit looks down on others — and "haughty" names the contemptuous downward gaze of that imagined height. English's "high" has the same story in its sense "proud, overweening," but haughty preserved the Old French word for height and made it stand for arrogance alone. Polysemy summary (all senses, living and historical).
  • Arrogantly proud, disdainfully superior — modern, dominant, negative.
  • Of an expression or gesture: coldly proud, aloof, condescending — modern, literary.
  • Physically elevated, high, lofty — 16th-century, now obsolete.
  • Of high rank or noble station — 16th–17th-century, now archaic.
  • High-spirited, proudly noble — early, partially neutral, now obsolete.
In Jane Austen's sentence about Darcy, "haughty" works exactly in the dominant modern sense: Darcy's consciousness of his own superiority makes his manners "not inviting" and leads him to "continually giving offense." The word is the first member of a trio — haughty, reserved, fastidious — that paints the cold aristocrat whose pride keeps everyone at a distance.
