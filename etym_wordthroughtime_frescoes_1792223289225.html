- Late 16th c. (1590s–1600s) — Borrowed from Italian affresco / pittura a fresco ("painting on fresh plaster") by English travelers and connoisseurs admiring Renaissance architecture, treated as an exotic foreign technical phrase.
- 17th c. — Anglified into general art terminology; pluralized as frescoes (or frescos) to designate Italian Renaissance monumental wall paintings, contrasting with easel oil paintings.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment & Grand Tour) — Heavily discussed in aristocratic Grand Tour memoirs, architectural handbooks, and aesthetic treatises (such as Joshua Reynolds’s Discourses), celebrated as the most exalted and challenging medium of high classical art.
- 19th c. — Extended metaphorically into literary criticism and historiography to describe expansive, vivid literary depictions or panoramic historical narratives; also adopted during the Gothic and Romanesque revival in Victorian church decoration.
- 20th–21st c. — Reinvigorated by the Mexican Muralist movement (Rivera, Orozco, Siqueiros) as a public medium of political agitation and civic monumentality; in modern usage, denotes both the ancient fresco technique and large-scale public wall art.
