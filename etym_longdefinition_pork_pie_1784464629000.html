Pork-pie is a traditional British cold meat pie: chopped, seasoned pork (often with a filling of pink cured or un cured pork, sometimes with a core of ham or egg) enclosed in a firm, slightly crumbly hot-water crust pastry, sealed with a layer of savoury pork jelly or aspic, and eaten cold — usually in the hand — rather than as a hot dinner pie. The classic raised pork pie is cylindrical or slightly domed, hand-raised or moulded, with a characteristic crimped “frill” around the top and a pastry lid. It is the most famous member of the British raised pie family, the cold, jellied, hand-held pie of markets, pub counters, picnics, and cold collations. The word carries none of the sweet-spice festivity of the mince-pie: it is squarely savoury, robust, and democratic, a food of ordinary plenty rather than of celebration.

In Tolkien's scene Bombur's demand — “And PORK-PIE and salad” — pairs the heaviest, richest item on the dwarves' list with a token of freshness, and the juxtaposition is exactly right for both the character and the moment. The pork-pie stands for substantial, savoury, seriously carnivorous English fare, the kind of food a hobbit keeps in a well-stocked larder and a dwarf devours with relish; the salad is the comic gesture of a guest trying to sound moderate. The mention completes the progression of the unexpected party from sweet preserves and mince-pies to serious meat cookery, and it underscores both Bombur's legendary appetite and the sense that Bag End is being stripped of every comfort food before the adventure begins.

Polysemy must be surveyed carefully, because pork-pie has radiated well beyond the kitchen. First, the culinary sense just described: the cold, jellied, hot-water-crust meat pie, in its plain pork form and its regional varieties (the Melton Mowbray pork pie, a protected-geographical-indication product with pale, crumbling, unbaked-crust pastry and no artificial colourings, is the most famous). Second, by transparent metonymy, a pork pie names a single portion of the dish — a slice of pork pie, a whole pork pie — and the plural pork pies names the dish as a class. Third, in Cockney rhyming slang, pork pies rhymes with lies, giving the clipped form porkies: “tell porkies” means “tell lies”, a usage recorded from the twentieth century. Fourth, in twentieth-century millinery and fashion, the pork-pie hat is a felt hat with a flat-topped crown, a pinched or dimpled indent, and a small brim turned up all round, so named for its fancied resemblance to the raised pie — worn by jazz musicians, film noir figures, and ska and mod cultures, and carrying the word into an entirely non-culinary register. Fifth, figurative uses of the elements: pork as a metaphor for government spending (“pork barrel”, “discretionary pork”, American political slang) and for corrupt patronage, and pie in phrases such as easy as pie or pie in the sky — none of which directly involve the pastry but all of which lean on the same word stock.

Precision against its pastry neighbours is essential. A steak-and-ale pie is a hot, sauced, two-crust pie eaten from a plate with gravy, not a cold hand-held pie. A sausage roll uses flaky puff pastry, has no jelly, and its filling is a distinct sausage forcemeat rather than the seasoned chopped pork of a pie. A pasty — the Cornish pasty — is a crimped, semicircular hand-pie of beef and potato, a working miner's meal, while the pork pie belongs to the market town and the cold collation. The raised pie family (game pie, veal-and-ham pie, Melton Mowbray pork pie) shares the hot-water crust, and the pork pie is its most famous member. In short, a PORK-PIE is defined by its dense seasoned meat fill, its jellied sealing layer, and its sturdy, hand-raised or moulded, cold-served crust.

Register, collocation, and usage. The word is homely, English, and democratic: the food of markets, pub counters, picnics, and Boxing-Day buffets rather than of haute cuisine. It collocates with a slice of, a whole, cold, with pickles, Melton Mowbray, and in its slang and fashion senses with tell porkies and pork-pie hat. In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses PORK-PIE as character detail for Bombur and as atmospheric detail for hobbit hospitality — plump, practical, and thoroughly un-Elvish.

  • Primary sense — the cold meat pie: chopped seasoned pork and jellied stock in hot-water crust, eaten cold, in the hand; the archetypal British raised pie.
  • Portion and class: a pork pie = one pie; pork pies = the dish as a kind (Bombur's sense).
  • Regional protected form: the Melton Mowbray pork pie (PGI, 2008), pale crumbling crust, no artificial colours, unbaked-crust method.
  • Rhyming slang: Cockney pork pies = lies, clipped to porkies (20th c.).
  • Fashion: the pork-pie hat (1930s onward), flat-crowned felt hat likened to the pie.
  • Figurative pork: American political pork-barrel spending, patronage, graft — unrelated to the pie but same word stock.
