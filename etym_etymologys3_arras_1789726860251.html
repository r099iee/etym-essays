The history of ARRAS is not a sound-law story but a trade story: a town lending its name to its cloth. The town now called Arras in northern France was in Roman times Nemetacum or Nemetocenna Atrebatum, chief settlement of the Belgic tribe Atrebates, whose name also survives in Artois. After the fall of Rome the Atrebatum element prevailed, giving Old French Arras with double r and silent s. By the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries Arras, under Flemish and Burgundian rule, dominated luxury wool weaving alongside Tournai and Brussels. Its high-warp tapestries in wool, silk, and gold, depicting hunts, romances, and histories, furnished courts from Dijon to London. English royal accounts list purchases of arras as early as the 1300s.

Middle English borrowed the town name as a common noun by metonymy: arras meant cloth of Arras, then any rich figured hanging wherever woven. No phonetic reshaping occurred beyond anglicised pronunciation /ˈærəs/; the change is purely semantic from proper to common noun, parallel to damask from Damascus, calico from Calicut, milliner from Milan. Early attestations show the article shifting: an Arras to an arras. Shakespeare fixes the word culturally: Hamlet stabs through the arras killing Polonius, Falstaff hides, stage directions order arras doors. Eighteenth-century upholsterers still sold arras counterpanes and arras-work embroidery imitating true weave.

  • Trade to trope: as Brussels and Gobelins overtook Arras technically, arras in English narrowed to the archaic-poetic word for any old hanging, while tapestry became the neutral term. OED citations after 1700 are predominantly literary and antiquarian, signalling deliberate archaism.
  • From wall to grass: twentieth-century nature writers revived arras as metaphor for vegetation and weather precisely because of its woven, figured, pierceable associations. An arras of grass keeps warp-weft verticals, patterned light, and hidden-listener drama that curtain or mat would lose. Baker's thrush stabbing through grass arras thus re-enacts Hamlet in miniature.
  • Toponymic note: attempts to push Atrebates back to PIE *treb- dwelling are philologically debated and irrelevant to English; the English word starts with the medieval city, not the Iron Age tribe. This is stated explicitly to honour zero guesswork.

In sum, ARRAS travelled from Belgic hillfort to Burgundian loom to Elsinore wall to Essex ditch. Its form barely changed while its referent widened from one city's product to any veil that can hide a spy or a worm. To stab through an arras of grass is to let commerce, theatre, and ornithology meet in a single beak-thrust.

Museum survival confirms the semantic path. Hardwick Hall's Gideon arras, Hampton Court's Abraham series, and the Cloisters' Hunt of the Unicorn all show the heavy figured weave that could deaden sound, hold heat, and hide a listener. Stage history kept the hiding sense alive long after bedrooms replaced halls: Victorian Hamlets still dived behind stuffy velvet arrases, and radio drama kept the rustle as cue for murder. When a modern writer therefore reaches for arras of grass, the whole creaking stage machinery rustles behind the thrush, lending a ditch-bank killing the acoustics of a palace.
