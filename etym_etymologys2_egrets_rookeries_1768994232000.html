EGRETS — linear chain for egret (plural egrets):
1. Proto-Germanic *haigrô ~ *hraigrô "heron" (c. 500 BCE–CE 500; two metathesized twin forms, probably echoic of the bird's harsh cry). No secure PIE reconstruction is agreed upon; the OED marks the deeper origin uncertain.
2. Lombardic *haigaro "heron" (West Germanic speech carried into northern Italy by the Lombard settlement, 6th c. CE).
3. Italian garza / sgarza "heron" (medieval) → garzetta "little heron" (diminutive suffix -etta).
4. Old French aigrette "egret; heron's plume" (c. 11th–14th c., from the Italian chain).
5. Middle English egret (c. 1400) "a white heron".
6. English egret (TARGET) — the plural egrets on this card.
Semantic narrowing note: in the Germanic stock the word meant any heron; in the Romance chain it specialized to the white, plume-carrying members of the family — the shift that makes "white as candles" etymologically exact.

ROOKERIES — linear chain for rookery (plural rookeries):
1. PIE *ker- "to cry hoarsely, to caw" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the echoic root of the crow-and-raven family (cf. Greek korax "raven", Latin corvus "raven"). The handbooks treat it as a sound-imitating root; its reconstruction is secure only at the level of the family resemblance.
2. Proto-Germanic *hrōkaz "rook" (c. 500 BCE–CE 500; o-grade of *ker- with a -k- suffix that seems to catch the cry itself).
3. Old English hrōc "rook (Corvus frugilegus)" (attested from c. 8th–11th c.).
4. Middle English roke "rook" → English rook (n.), the bird.
5. English rook + -ery (the place-suffix from Old French -erie) → rookery (1725) "a breeding colony of rooks"; extended 18th c. to any gregarious bird's colony and 19th c. to crowded slum tenements.
6. English rookery (TARGET) — the plural rookeries on this card.
Debate/nuance note: the Germanic branch made two birds from *ker- — *hrabnaz "raven" (Old English hræfn) and *hrōkaz "rook" — and some handbooks keep the -k- suffixed rook separate from the plainer raven reflexes, treating the rook-word as an independent, late, echoic formation. The *ker- root itself is widely but not universally reconstructed; the correspondence is strong, yet no attested intermediate forms survive between PIE and Germanic.
