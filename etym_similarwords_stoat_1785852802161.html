• Weasel: Similar because both are small, slender, carnivorous mustelids of similar shape; differs because the least weasel is smaller, lacks the black-tipped tail, and "weasel" carries the metaphor of a deceitful person, whereas "stoat" is usually literal.
  • Ermine: Similar because it is literally the same species, Mustela erminea; differs because "ermine" denotes the stoat in its white winter coat or the harvested fur, while "stoat" is the year-round name for the living animal.
  • Ferret: Similar because it is a slender-bodied mustelid kept for hunting; differs because the ferret is the domesticated descendant of the European polecat, bred to bolt rabbits and kept as a pet, unlike the wild stoat.
  • Polecat: Similar in being a fierce wild mustelid predator; differs because the polecat is heavier-set, has a dark facial mask and light body markings, and is the wild ancestor of the ferret.
  • Marten: Similar in being a member of the same family; differs because martens are larger, largely tree-dwelling forest animals with bushier tails and prominent throat patches.
  • Mink: Similar in being a semiaquatic mustelid with valuable fur; differs because the mink has year-round dark brown waterproof fur, partly webbed feet, and lives beside water.
  • Otter: Similar in being a mustelid; differs because the otter is a large, semiaquatic fish-hunting animal with a thick flat tail, wholly unlike the stoat's land-hunting form.
  • Short-tailed weasel: Similar because this is the North American name for the very same species; differs in register and geography — it is the standard American field term, while "stoat" is the British and Commonwealth word.
  • Badger: Similar in belonging to the mustelid family; differs because the badger is large, stocky, black-and-white-faced, and a digging omnivore, at the far opposite of the stoat's lithe hunting build.
