- Old English slingan — “to wind, twist, turn” (strong verb): the word enters English as pure physical coiling and twisting, with nothing thrown and nothing bandaged.
- c. 1200–1300 — the noun slynge appears for the missile-throwing strap (Bible translations and devotional texts: David and Goliath); the verb shifts to “to hurl with a sling” — the weapon sense fixes the word in heroic and religious narrative.
- 16th century — Shakespeare fixes “the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” (Hamlet, c. 1600): slings become the type of hidden, long-range attack, and the verb broadens to any vigorous throwing.
- 17th–18th centuries — military and nautical extensions: muskets are ‘slung’ on straps; cargo ‘slings’ (rope and chain cradles) hoist goods; the hanging-strap sense generalises — and by the 18th century a cloth support for an injured arm, suspended from the neck, is a ‘sling’: “his arm in a sling” enters everyday speech.
- 19th century — the verb goes colloquial (sling mud at a rival; sling one's hook = decamp, UK slang); the American slingshot (forked elastic catapult) is named; and the American drink ‘sling’ appears (from c. 1807), of obscure origin, soon to include the Singapore Sling (1915).
- 20th–21st centuries — baby slings become mainstream parenting gear; surgical mesh slings support pelvic organs; helicopters carry underslung ‘sling loads’; the arm-sling remains the everyday, quiet medical sense — and the old hurling core still powers every figurative throw of mud, insults or luck.
