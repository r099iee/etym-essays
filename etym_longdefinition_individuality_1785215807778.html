INDIVIDUALITY (noun) — at its plainest core At its most basic level, individuality names the quality or fact of being a distinct, self-determining person: the sum of inner traits, tastes, dispositions, convictions, and habits that set one human being apart from every other, together with the right and capacity to hold and express those traits even against external pressure. The word is an abstract noun built from individual ("a single human being; one who cannot be divided") with the suffix -ity (Latin -itas, "state, condition, quality"), so that literally it means "the condition of being indivisible, one, a single self." Its natural opposite is conformity — the surrender of one's own traits to the expectations of a group, a role, or a machine. The sense in the Mill context In this sentence from the Autobiography of John Stuart Mill, the word carries a poignant, almost tragic nuance. The boy's individuality is declared "unimportant" because his father, James Mill, had designed him from infancy as a living instrument to carry on a philosophical mission: drilled in Greek at three, in Latin at eight, driven through logic, political economy, and the utilitarian programme as a machine for advancing an idea. The blank therefore registers not the neutral absence of a personality but an active suppression — the deliberate sacrifice of a child's unique selfhood to a paternal "idea." The architectonic contrast of the sentence, between "he was designed" and "the individuality of the boy was unimportant," makes the noun the moral pivot of the passage: what every human being naturally possesses and cherishes was treated as disposable raw material in a vast educational experiment. Mill himself, looking back in maturity, would write in On Liberty (1859) that individuality is "one of the principal ingredients of human happiness" and "a necessary condition and instrument of improvement" — the very doctrine his own boyhood denied. Primary senses
  • Distinct selfhood: the sum of qualities — temperament, tastes, opinions, style — that makes a person recognizably one-of-a-kind and not interchangeable with anyone else.
  • Self-determination: the capacity to live by one's own convictions and choices rather than by borrowed or imposed ones; the "right of individuality" that Mill defends against the tyranny of majority opinion and the despotism of custom.
  • Indivisible oneness: the oldest sense, inherited from Scholastic Latin individuum, "that which cannot be divided"; the mere fact of being a single, separate, undivided entity.
  • Particularity of things: the quality of being an individual specimen or instance — "the individuality of each grain of sand," each data point, each object.
Polysemy: secondary, technical, and metaphoric senses
  • Philosophy (Scholasticism and early modern metaphysics): individuum was coined in late Latin as a rendering of the Greek átomos, "that which cannot be cut" — the ultimate, undivided member of a species. "The principle of individuation" (principium individuationis) asks what makes a thing this thing rather than another; it runs from Aristotle through Thomas Aquinas to Leibniz and Schopenhauer. This is the historical bridge: the philosophical "indivisibility" of the individual is precisely what Romantic and Victorian thought later filled with psychological content.
  • Romantic aesthetics (18th–19th c.): individuality becomes the hallmark of genius and of the organic. For the Romantics the living organism — and the living artist — is an expressive whole that cannot be produced by rules, imitation, or reproduction; hence "individuality of style," "individuality of character," the uniqueness of the work of art. Goethe, Coleridge, and Emerson all build on this.
  • Liberal political theory (19th c.): in Mill's On Liberty, individuality is a moral and political ideal — the free development of the self against custom; the source of "experiments of living" and of human progress. Mill lists "perception, judgment, discriminative feeling, mental activity, and even moral preference" as the elements of individuality.
  • Biology and ecology: the property of being a single organism or of parts being integrated into one whole — the "individuality" of a coral colony or a bee hive, contrasted with a mere aggregate; also the individuality of a species' members as distinct specimens.
  • Statistics, law, and logic: each case, data point, or person is an individual instance; legal individuality means personhood with rights and responsibilities.
  • Everyday modern sense: personal distinctness of style, taste, or personality, often softened from Mill's moral force into mere quirk or uniqueness — "she has a lot of individuality," "the apartment lacks individuality," "celebrate your individuality."
How to use the word precisely The most important discrimination is between individuality and identity. Identity answers "who am I?" — membership in categories such as nation, religion, gender, profession — and can be held without any developed inner life. Individuality answers "what am I distinctively, from within?" — and implies self-expression, originality, and the courage of one's own traits. A person may have a perfectly secure identity and still lack individuality; conversely, individuality can flourish where identity is contested or denied. In the Mill sentence the boy is denied precisely this inner distinctness: his identity (heir of a mission) is fully supplied by his father, while his individuality is pronounced unimportant. The word therefore belongs to the moral vocabulary of freedom and personhood, and its force is always relative to pressure: it names what must not be dissolved into a group, a role, or a machine.
