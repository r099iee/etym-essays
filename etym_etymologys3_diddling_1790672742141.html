diddle and its gerund diddling belong to the large, murky family of English imitative, nursery-adjacent words whose origins resist neat pedigrees — and lexicographers say so openly. The Oxford English Dictionary records no certain etymology: the word appears first in dialectal senses meaning to totter or jiggle, perhaps imitative of unsteady movement, perhaps connected to nursery rhymes like Diddle, diddle, dumpling. There is no established Proto-Indo-European root, no Latin or French ancestor, and no sound-law chain to display. What we have instead is a brilliantly documented social career: from nursery jigging to stage slang to general informal English in under a generation.

  • Nursery and dialect (pre-1800): Diddle is attested in northern and Scots dialect for to shake, to jog, to dandle a child on the knee. Grose's Provincial Glossary and later dialect dictionaries record diddle as to totter like a child learning to walk. The reduplicative sound di-di belongs with bibble-babble, fiddle-faddle, piddle-paddle — sound-symbolism for small, repeated, unserious motion.
  • Jeremy Diddler (1803): The turning point is theatrical. Kenney's farce Raising the Wind introduced Jeremy Diddler, a needy, plausible, endlessly borrowing adventurer who never repays. The character was an instant catchphrase, like Mrs Malaprop or Scrooge after him. Within a few years to diddle meant to do what Jeremy did: to borrow without return, to cheat by plausible small tricks, to live by one's wits. Byron, Scott, and sporting newspapers all use diddled in this roguish sense by the 1810s.
  • Generalisation (19th century): From cheating, the word spread back toward mere idleness. To diddle about, to diddle away time, to diddle with things — all recorded by mid-Victorian slang dictionaries — blend the ideas of fiddling and wasting. The semantic bridge is clear: the petty cheat fiddles with accounts, the idler fiddles with time; both are unserious handling of what should be handled seriously.

Phonetically diddle /ˈdɪdl/ is a textbook expressive formation: voiced alveolar stop + high short vowel + syllabic lateral, repeated. The geminate -ddle spelling marks the preceding vowel as short and the lateral as syllabic, exactly as in fiddle, riddle, piddle. The gerund diddling /ˈdɪdlɪŋ/ adds the regular -ing with no change. The word's comedy is inseparable from its phonetics; it could not sound grave if it tried.

In the source sentence, harmless antiquarian diddling draws on the idleness strand while letting the cheating strand colour it faintly: antiquaries are not accused of fraud, but of a scholarship that cheats its own purpose by substituting looking at for looking through. The phrase belongs to a long humanist polemic against pedantry — from Swift's spider versus bee to Housman's sneers at mere collation — in which the critic grants harmlessness precisely to sharpen the charge of uselessness. To call something harmless diddling is to damn with faint affection.

Modern usage keeps both strands alive but separates them by collocation. British conversation still says to feel diddled for petty consumer grievance, while teachers say stop diddling about for classroom fidgeting. American use favours the time-wasting sense and the nursery echo (to diddle around), with the fraud sense yielding to rip off. The vulgar sexual sense, a late 20th-century extension, has not displaced these core uses in respectable registers and is contextually unmistakable when it occurs. Diddling thus remains what it was in 1803: small, comic, slightly disreputable — and instantly understood.
