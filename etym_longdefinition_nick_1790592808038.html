Nick as used in Wodehouse's phrase nick him for the paltriest sum is a British colloquial verb meaning to ask for, demand, or extract money from someone, especially by a sudden touch, small loan, or opportunistic request that the victim would rather refuse. To nick a rich uncle for a fiver is to hit him up, to tap him, to put the bite on him—not to rob him violently but to cadge with cheerful effrontery, relying on his inability to say no. The tone is roguish rather than criminal, implying youthful impecuniosity exploiting indulgent oofiness.

This financial sense sits within a much larger polysemy of nick that must be distinguished. The primary general sense historically is to make a small notch, cut, or chip in a surface: to nick a table with a knife, to nick one's chin shaving. A secondary sporting and gambling sense means to hit precisely, to score, or to catch at the right moment: to nick the ball, to nick time. A third, criminal slang sense means to steal, pilfer, or shoplift: to nick a bike, to nick sweets, and by extension in police slang to arrest—to get nicked meaning to get caught. A fourth sense means to cheat or overcharge. The Wodehousian nick for bridges stealing and asking: it takes without quite stealing, obtains by importunity what could be called a forced gift.

Tertiary nuances include timing and opportunism. To nick someone often implies catching them unawares or at a vulnerable moment, as in to nick in before doors close. In Australian and British school slang, to nick off means to sneak away. The financial nick for construction—nick him for ten quid—frames the sum as penalty or levy, humorously treating friendship as taxation. The subject is typically a impecunious young man, the object a rich, indulgent, slightly resentful elder, and the amount characteristically paltry, making refusal embarrassing and compliance inevitable.

  • Cadge and touch: Nick for money belongs with touch, tap, bite, beg, borrow; implies informal loan unlikely to be repaid promptly, softened by charm.
  • Theft and arrest: To nick meaning to steal and to be nicked meaning to be arrested form a crime-punishment pair central to modern British police procedurals; the Wodehouse sense is milder and non-violent.
  • Notch and cut: Original craft sense of small incision; survives in nick of time (the precise notch on a tally) and shaving nicks.
  • Sport and precision: To nick a catch or nick a win suggests narrow, timely success; shares with financial nick the idea of seizing opportunity.
  • Proper name distinction: Capitalized Nick as short for Nicholas (victory-people) is unrelated etymologically to verb nick; context disambiguates, but Wodehouse exploits lower-class verb against upper-class name for comic bathos.

Semantic bridges all involve a small sharp action with disproportionate effect. A tiny cut draws blood; a timely interception wins a game; a petty theft costs a shopkeeper; a cheeky request for a small sum causes a loud squawk audible at Land's End. Smallness of means versus largeness of reaction is the comic engine. Unlike borrow, which promises return, nick cheekily assumes entitlement; unlike steal, which is covert and criminal, nick for is overt and socially negotiated; unlike beg, which is humble, nick is jaunty and confident, in the gay spirit of an only child. Mastering this nick means hearing class, age, and insouciance in a single syllable: the Drones Club art of living on others without quite dishonour.

In modern British English the financial nick persists in nick a fiver off mum alongside dominant steal/arrest senses, while American English prefers hit up or mooch. Retaining root casing as Nick in the card preserves Wodehouse's sentence-initial capitalization, but function is unambiguously verbal, not onomastic.
