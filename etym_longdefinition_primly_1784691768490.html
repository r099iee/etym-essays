Primly is the adverb of prim, and it describes action done in a stiffly formal, precise, demure, and self-consciously neat manner: with exact posture, careful dress, restrained gestures, and a faint air of disapproval of anything untidy or improper. To do something primly is to do it with exaggerated correctness, as though conscious of being observed and anxious to give no offence to propriety. The word belongs to the vocabulary of manners rather than of morals: a person may be primly dressed, primly seated, primly silent, or primly offended, and in every case the emphasis falls on outward neatness and constraint rather than inward virtue. There is almost always a hint of comedy or mild criticism in the word, since prim behaviour tends to be a little too tidy, a little too controlled, to be wholly natural — it is the deportment of someone who is trying to be proper and knows it.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The adverb itself is essentially single in meaning, but it inherits the full spectrum of the adjective prim, and in different collocations different facets come to the foreground:

  • Neatness and tidiness — arranged, folded, or trimmed with meticulous care: “napkins folded primly,” “a primly kept garden,” “primly parted hair.” Here the emphasis falls on exact order.
  • Formality and stiffness of manner — rigid, ceremonious correctness of behaviour: “she sat primly on the edge of the chair,” “primly polite,” “primly formal.” Here the emphasis falls on constraint and self-control.
  • Demureness and modesty — a restrained, decorous, even prudish refusal of anything loose or free: “primly silent,” “primly shocked.” Here the emphasis falls on propriety and the suppression of spontaneity.
  • Comic or critical exaggeration — the same behaviour rendered slightly ridiculous by excess: “primly disapproving,” “a primly raised eyebrow.” Here the emphasis falls on the visible gap between the performance and the natural.
  • Obsolete sense of the adjective carried forward — in Middle and early modern English prim could still mean “excellent, fine, handsome” (the sense inherited from Old French); at that date primly could therefore also mean “excellently, finely, handsomely,” a meaning now wholly lost. The modern sense results from a narrowing that replaced “excellent in quality” with “exact in manners.”

Historical bridge and formation. The adjective prim descends from Old French prim (also prims), “fine, delicate, slender, neat,” which in turn comes from Latin prīmus, “first, foremost, finest.” The semantic journey runs from priority in time (“first”) to excellence in quality (“finest”) to physical delicacy (“fine, slender”) and finally, in English of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, to neatness of behaviour (“formally exact, demure”). The adverb primly is formed with the native English adverbial suffix -ly, descended from Old English -līce (“in the manner or form of”), the same suffix that yields slowly, quickly, neatly, and properly. The adverb is attested from the late sixteenth century, alongside the noun primness and the now-obsolete verb to prim (“to make prim, to pose primly”).

The word in context (The Hobbit). In J. R. R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit, Chapter 2 (‘Roast Mutton’), the adverb works as a small masterpiece of comic characterisation. Bilbo has gone alone through the dark forest “towards the red light” of the trolls’ camp-fire, and the narrator observes that he walks “primly.” The single word does three things at once. It recalls the hobbit’s ordinary domestic self — the waistcoat, the pocket-handkerchief, the neat little feet, the respectability of Bag End — carried into a situation where none of it is of the slightest use. It sets up the contrast with the trolls and with the whole rough business of burglary, which requires above all silence and cunning: a prim walk is precisely the wrong way to approach a fire where trolls are sitting and talking. And it prepares the narrator’s joke that follows — that not even a weasel “would have stirred a whisker at it,” that is, that despite the comic primness of his gait, Bilbo’s hobbit-feet are so naturally light that he passes in utter silence. The primness is visible; the stealth is real; the comedy lies in the disjunction between them.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. Demurely stresses shy, modest restraint, especially of a woman; sedately stresses calm self-possession without the fuss; stiffly stresses rigidity of movement without the neatness; fussily stresses anxious over-arrangement without the self-satisfaction; properly and correctly are neutral descriptions of doing something the right way; affectedly stresses insincerity; prudishly stresses an exaggerated horror of anything improper; finickily stresses petty fastidiousness of taste. Primly uniquely combines neatness, formality, demureness, and a faint, knowing self-satisfaction in a single compact adverb whose -ly tail lets it glide onto almost any verb of behaviour, movement, or arrangement.
