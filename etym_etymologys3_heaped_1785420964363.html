The past participle HEAPED descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *keup- or *koup-, reconstructed with the sense "to swell, bend, or arch up into a mound." The root's concrete, tactile image was of something bulging upward — grain rising in a store, stones gathered in a cairn, earth pushed into a tumulus — and this swelled-mound image appears to be preserved across several branches of the family. In the Baltic languages, Lithuanian kaũpas, "heap, pile," and kupetà, "a small stack, especially of hay," retain the same "risen mass" semantics, and Greek kŷphos, "a bend or hump," has been proposed as a wider cognate built on the same rounded-bulge image. It must be said plainly that this deeper connection is debated: the semantic match is suggestive rather than exact, and some authorities treat the Germanic and Baltic words as independent expressive formations with no secure PIE ancestry. What is certain is the Germanic stage. The Germanic branch converted the root into Proto-Germanic *haupaz, "mound, heap, mass," which produced a wide band of cognates: Old Norse hópr, "crowd, troop" (and, by a curved-shape extension, a bay or inlet), Old High German houf, "heap, pile, crowd," which became modern German Haufen, "heap, crowd," Middle Dutch hoop, modern Dutch hoop, "heap, pile," Swedish hög, and Danish hob. The Old English form was hēap, a word that meant not only a physical pile of objects but also a "great number" — a crowd or troop of people, as in a "heap of men." Already in Old English, then, the word had begun to stretch from concrete mounds of matter toward the abstract idea of a large aggregate, and this double life — pile and multitude — has never left the word. By the Middle English period the noun heep coexisted with the verb hepen (from Old English hēapian), "to heap, to pile up, to gather into a mass," and from the verb came the past participle heaped.
  • Kitchen and measure. In cooking and domestic English, "a heaped spoonful" is a measure piled above the rim of the spoon — a precise, practical sense standard since the recipe tradition of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
  • Figurative abundance. From at least the sixteenth century, to heap praise, blame, honours, or gifts upon a person is to pour them out in overwhelming quantity; heaped criticism and heaped abuse are stock collocations. Here the geometry of the pile is forgotten and only the quantity remains.
  • Public exposure. Empson's "heaped up and published" (Seven Types of Ambiguity, preface) dramatizes the intellectual strategy of exposing every possible error at once, in public, in a pile, so that the real mistakes can later be identified — the heap as an instrument of honest experiment rather than of concealment.
  • Technical extensions. In computing, a "heap" is the region of dynamically allocated memory, so named precisely because allocations are scattered like a disorganized pile; in computer science a "binary heap" is a tree structure and "heapsort" the algorithm built on it. The semantic core — an unordered mass that can nonetheless be ordered and used — survives in every one of these uses.
Socio-culturally, HEAPED sits at the boundary between the homely and the monumental. It is a kitchen word and a nursery word — heaped plates, heaped apples — yet it is also the word of cairns and burial mounds, of grain stores and treasure hoards, where the piled mass carries dignity and power. In Empson's hands the word is deliberately lowered into the kitchen: errors are not elegantly arranged or discreetly filed but heaped up and published, with all the candid, slightly absurd generosity the participle implies. The phonology reinforces the sense: the short, aspirated /h/, the long front vowel /iː/, and the final dental /pt/ give heaped a compact, emphatic closure, a word that lands like the finished pile it names — gathered, complete, and slightly overfull.
