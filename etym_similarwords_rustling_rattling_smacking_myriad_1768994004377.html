• rustling — crackling: Similar because both are dry, light friction sounds; differs because crackling is the sharp popping of fire, while rustling is the soft, dry agitation of many small surfaces moving together — quiet, stealthy, approaching.
  • rustling — whispering: Similar because both are faint, hushed sounds; differs because whispering is vocal and human, the soft speaking of a voice, while rustling is the friction of surfaces — leaves, silk, paper — with no voice at all.
  • rustling — swishing: Similar because both name a soft rushing sound; differs because swishing is the movement of air or liquid (a swishing gown, a swishing tail), while rustling is the dry, sibilant agitation of small dry surfaces.
  • rustling — scraping: Similar because both involve surfaces in contact; differs because scraping is blunt, heavy contact of a point or edge, while rustling is light, multiple, and dry — many small touches rather than one dragged one.
  • rattling — clattering: Similar because both are rapid series of striking sounds; differs because clattering is heavier, the loose knock of plates or crockery, while rattling is the shorter, sharper, more irregular percussion of something shaken or dragged.
  • rattling — clanking: Similar because both name metallic impact; differs because clanking is the dull, heavy sound of metal on metal (chains, machinery), slower and weightier, while rattling is rapid, light, and irregular — a quick succession of small impacts.
  • rattling — clinking: Similar because both name a series of small sounds; differs because clinking is light and high-pitched, of glass or coins, while rattling is harder and more irregular, of things not fastened down being shaken.
  • rattling — jangling: Similar because both name a discordant ringing series; differs because jangling implies metallic ringing that irritates the ear, while rattling is the percussive clatter of collision, not necessarily ringing.
  • smacking — slapping: Similar because both name a sharp striking contact; differs because slapping is the flat blow of an open hand, while smacking is the quick wet strike of lips — appetite made audible in a single percussive syllable.
  • smacking — smacking one's lips / lip-smacking: Similar because both name the sound of lips meeting in relish; differs because the literal action is the perceptible sign of appetite, while the noun smacking isolates the sound itself, detachable from the eater — which is what makes it uncanny in the cave.
  • smacking — kissing / a smack (kiss): Similar because both involve lips making a sound; differs because a smack as a loud kiss is affectionate and human, while smacking as a repeated wet noise is neutral and mechanical, easily turned monstrous.
  • myriad — countless: Similar because both assert an enormous number; differs because countless is plain and neutral — merely uncounted — while myriad preserves its old precision of "ten thousand" softened into poetic hyperbole, and carries a formal, literary weight.
  • myriad — innumerable: Similar because both mean too many to count; differs because innumerable is the formal, objective claim of impossibility, while myriad is a rhetorical figure that asserts overwhelming scale with poetic colour.
  • myriad — legion: Similar because both name a vast multitude; differs because legion is metaphorical from a Roman military unit (a large body of troops), while myriad is a precise Greek numeral grown into hyperbole — a mathematical origin rather than a military one.
  • myriad — multitudinous: Similar because both describe a very large number; differs because multitudinous is the adjective of an uncounted mass, while myriad works as noun and adjective with the historical echo of "ten thousand" behind it.
