NOOKS — the biography of the sheltered angle.
The word surfaces in English around 1300 as Middle English nok or noke, meaning "a corner" — often a corner of a piece of ground, a triangular scrap of land where one field met another, or the angle where two walls or two coasts met. Its origin is genuinely unknown, and scholarship has never been able to pin it down: the OED, with characteristic caution, classifies the word's origin as obscure, and no modern etymology has displaced that verdict. This obscurity is itself part of the word's interest — the little sheltered corner arrived in the language, as it were, out of nowhere, with no recorded Old English ancestor and no agreed foreign parent.

Several hypotheses have been floated, each with phonetic plausibility and none with proof. Some scholars propose a Celtic source, comparing the Gaelic hill-word cnoc (whence the place-name element "knock") and a form niuc, "a small hill or corner of land"; others look to Middle Low German nōke or Middle Dutch noke "corner, angle," imagining a Low-Country loan into northern English; still others point to a Scandinavian word for a projecting peak or bend. The chronology argues against the continental loans, since the English word appears in the early fourteenth century, earlier than the German and Dutch parallels, and the Celtic forms differ in sense and shape enough that the comparison has never been accepted. A probable but unproven relative is notch, whose Middle English form oche came from Old French with an n- tacked on by rebracketing ("an oche" heard as "a no-che") — a fascinating cousin, but the connection to nook itself remains only possible, not certain.

From "a corner of ground" the meaning broadened through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries to "a recess, a secluded spot, a hidden place," and it is this later sense that literature adopted. The alliterative doublet nooks and crannies — with cranny from Old French cran "a notch, a crack" — fixed the word's most famous pairing by the early modern period, and "search every nook and cranny" became a standard idiom for exhaustive, loving thoroughness. Shakespeare uses the plural noun for intimate, secret places; the fairy-tale and romantic literature of the nineteenth century made the word's cosiness official, so that a "nook" in a garden, a wood, or a house became a small, warm, protective place in which a person — or a fugitive — could be safe.

In modern usage the word has split in two directions. One is cosy domesticity: the "reading nook," the "breakfast nook" of mid-century suburban architecture, the "snug nook" of a fireside, and the Barnes & Noble Nook e-reader of 2009, named precisely for its snug, bookish connotations. The other is the older, wilder sense of hidden refuge: "nooks and crannies" of a land, "nooks and wild places," the landscape's own pockets of secrecy where something hunted may still be alive in hiding. It is this older current that the passage deploys, and the word's dual history — cosy corner on one side, wild hiding place on the other — is exactly what makes it so well fitted to Narnia, a land that is at once domestic and enchanted.

The plural form in this passage adds a further shade: nooks, plural, become a geography rather than a single corner — all the little shelters of a country, scattered and waiting to be searched. The word's etymology may be lost, but its semantic career is clear: from a scrap of field-corner in the fourteenth century, through cosy domestic alcove in the modern house, to the wild, hopeful hiding places of the imagination.

  • c. 1300: Middle English nok / noke "a corner of a piece of ground" — origin unknown.
  • The hypotheses: Celtic cnoc / niuc, Middle Low German / Middle Dutch noke, Scandinavian — all unproven; the OED records "origin obscure."
  • The cousin: notch, from Old French oche with n- by rebracketing ("an oche" → "a no-che").
  • The idiom: nooks and crannies (cranny < Old French cran "notch, crack") = every conceivable hiding place.
  • Modern split: cosy domestic nook (reading nook, breakfast nook, the Nook e-reader, 2009) vs. wild hidden refuge (nooks and wild places).
  • The cross-linguistic echo: Hindi नुक्कड़ (nukkaṛ) "a tucked-away street corner" — the same sheltered angle.
