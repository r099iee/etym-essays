- 8th–10th c. — Attested in Old English (Beowulf) as the strong verb wrecan, meaning to drive out, banish, punish, or execute blood-feud vengeance for slain kin.
- 14th c. — Maintained in Middle English (Chaucer, Sir Gawain) as wreken (past participle wroken), signifying the avenging of insults, punishing of wrongdoers, and venting of righteous fury.
- 16th–17th c. — Transitioned from a strong verb to a regular weak verb (wreaked) in Early Modern English (Shakespeare, Milton, King James Bible), increasingly specializing in idioms like "wreak vengeance" and "wreak wrath."
- 19th c. — Formed its dominant modern collocation "wreak havoc" in journalistic, historical, and naval prose to describe battlefield slaughter and economic catastrophe.
- 20th–21st c. — Universal standard in global English for the catastrophic unleashing of natural disasters, computer viruses, and military onslaughts, alongside widespread orthographic confusion with "wreck."
