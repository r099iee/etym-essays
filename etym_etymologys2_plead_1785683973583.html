PLEAD descends, through Old French, from the Latin noun placitum, "a decree, a judgment, that which pleases," built on the verb placēre, "to please," from the PIE root *plāk-. The chain is traced in a single linear line with securely attested forms and dates. CHAIN 1 — the direct line to PLEAD: 1) PIE *plāk- "to be flat, broad, smooth, calm" (reconstructed; the root of Latin placēre "to please," Greek platýs "broad," and their families; the sense "to please" developed from "to be smooth, calm, favourable toward someone") — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin placēre "to please, give pleasure, be acceptable" (attested from the classical period; from "to be smooth and calm toward someone" → "to satisfy, gratify"). 3) Latin placitum "decree, judgment, that which pleases [the judge]" (neuter past participle of placēre used as a noun; attested in Roman legal and administrative usage — "what pleases the court, the agreed decision") — classical Latin. 4) Medieval Latin placitum "lawsuit, plea, judicial decision, court session" (the word acquires its legal sense in post-classical and medieval legal Latin, where a placitum is the matter agreed and adjudged in court) — c. 5th–10th c. 5) Old French plait / plaid "lawsuit, plea, debate, dispute" (from Latin placitum, with the regular sound change of the -ci- cluster to -i-; also the verb plaidier "to argue at law, plead") — c. 9th–12th c. 6) Middle English plēden "to plead, argue a case at law" (borrowed from Anglo-French plaider, alongside the noun plee, "a plea," from Old French plait) — c. 13th c. 7) Modern English plead — PLEAD (verb: "to argue a case before a court; to make an earnest appeal"; the past tense plead or pleaded; the procedural formulas "plead guilty" / "plead not guilty"). NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The derivation of Latin placēre from PIE *plāk- is standard and secure; the semantic bridge from "flat, smooth" to "pleasing" (what is smooth and calm to the senses is what pleases them) is widely accepted. (2) The specifically legal sense of placitum — "a judgment, a decree, that which pleases the court" — is a Roman and medieval legal development that is not in dispute; the striking semantic leap is that a "plea" is, etymologically, "what pleases," the agreement that satisfies the court. (3) The English forms plead and plea entered through the Anglo-French legal language of the thirteenth century, and the verb has always retained both the courtroom sense and, from the fourteenth century, the personal, supplicatory sense. The modern past tense pleaded is the standard form (American legal usage also admits plead as a past form, pronounced /pled/); the older past pload is obsolete.
