TRIFLING is a present-participial adjective and verbal form of the verb trifle, with two connected lives. In the source context (““You have been ___ with me,” he cried.”) it is the verbal sense: trifling with someone — toying with, mocking, or deceiving a person, treating their feelings or promises as a light thing. In ordinary modern use, however, it is the adjective sense that dominates: trifling = trivial, of little value, importance, or seriousness — a trifling sum, a trifling matter, a trifling error.

The verbal / participial sense

  • To trifle with: to treat lightly, to dally with, to deceive or delude; “you have been trifling with me” is a reproach of heartless play — the speaker has been played with, made sport of. The construction survives in formal and literary register (“do not trifle with me”, “trifling with the affections of others”).
  • To trifle (intransitive): to act idly or frivolously, to waste time, to jest; a trifler is an idler who makes a game of serious things, and “trifling” name the sin of the time-waster in moral writing from Addison to the Victorians.

The adjectival sense

  • Trivial, unimportant: a trifling matter, trifling objections, trifling differences — so small as to be beneath notice; the dominant modern sense, established from the 16th century.
  • Small in amount: a trifling sum, a trifling cost, a trifling delay — negligible in quantity, barely worth counting.
  • Light, idle, fanciful: trifling pleasures, trifling amusements — of no account, mere pastimes.
  • Contemptuous dismissal: “it is a trifling thing” — the adjective frequently carries a shrug of dismissal or condescension (polite letters: “a trifling favour”); and in 20th-century African-American Vernacular English, “trifling” acquired a sharper moral edge for a person who is shiftless, unreliable, good-for-nothing, or a cheat — “that man is trifling”.

The historical bridge: Middle English trifle (from Old French trufle, “mockery, deceit”) first meant “a false tale, a trick, idle talk”; the verb triflen meant “to deceive, mock” — so “to trifle with” is literally “to play a trick on”. By the 15th–16th centuries the noun had settled into “a small matter of little value”, and the participle trifling became the adjective “belonging to trifles” — trivial. The modern adjective is thus a softened descendant of an old word for mockery: what is trifling was once what was tricked, and the reproach of “to trifle with” preserves the ancient sting.

Related words worth noting: the dessert trifle — a layered dish of sponge, custard, fruit, and sherry, from late-16th-century cookery books — is named for the word's sense “a thing of little account”: the dish was a light, easy “nothing”. Shakespeare's “trifles light as air” (Othello) made the noun central to the poetry of jealousy, and the phrase “a mere trifle” remains current for a small gift or sum.

Register: the adjective is standard, with a slightly formal or literary tinge; the verbal construction “trifle with” is formal and reproachful; the AAVE sense is colloquial and slang-register; the noun is everyday.
