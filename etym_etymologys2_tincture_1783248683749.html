PIE *teng- 'to soak, wet, dip' (the root of wetting and of dyeing; securely attested only in the Hellenic and Italic branches) → Ancient Greek τέγγω (téngō) 'to wet, moisten, steep' (attested already in Homer, confirming the primitive 'moisten' sense behind 'dye') → Proto-Italic *tengō 'to dip, moisten' → Latin tingō / tinguō 'to wet, soak, steep, colour, dye' → supine stem tinc- (tinctum, 'to have been dipped, dyed') → Latin tinctūra 'a dyeing, the act of dyeing; a dye' (formed on the participial stem tinct- with the abstract suffix -ūra) → Old French teinture 'dye, stain, hue' → Middle English tincture 'dye, colouring' (14th century) → English tincture (figurative 'a slight admixture, a faint trace'; pharmaceutical 'an alcoholic solution of a drug'; heraldic 'a colour, metal, or fur of a coat of arms'). Note: a frequently cited Germanic cognate *tinkan is unfounded; the Germanic branch is left unclaimed for accuracy over symmetry. English also drew tinct (17th century), tint (18th century, an aphetic doublet), tinge (15th century), and taint (via Old French teint, 'dyed') from the same Latin stock.
