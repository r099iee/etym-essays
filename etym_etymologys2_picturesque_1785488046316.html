Chain for PICTURESQUE — primary root (Italian pittoresco 'in the manner of a painter'):
1. PIE *peyk- 'to mark, cut, colour, embroider' — a securely attested root (Greek poikilos 'many-coloured, embroidered', Sanskrit pisati 'carves, adorns', Latin pingere 'to paint, embroider, tattoo').
2. Latin pingere 'to paint, embroider, tattoo' (present stem with the nasal infix *p-n-k-; attested from early Latin; the same root gives pigmentum 'pigment', pictura 'painting').
3. Latin pictor 'painter' (agent noun from the participle pictus 'painted'; attested Classical Latin).
4. Italian pittore 'painter' (from Latin pictor via the regular Italo-Romance development).
5. Italian pittoresco 'after the manner of a painter, painterly' — coined in 1648 by Marco Boschini in his poem La carta del navegar pitoresco; formed from pittore + the suffix -esco 'in the style of' (from Germanic *-isk-).
6. French pittoresque 'picturesque' — borrowed from Italian in the later 17th century.
7. English picturesque, first recorded c. 1703, borrowed from French pittoresque but almost immediately respelled picturesque under the influence of the familiar English noun picture (folk-etymological blending).
8. PICTURESQUE (modern English; the exact form on this card).

Secondary chain — picture (the folk-etymological reinforcer):
1. PIE *peyk- 'to mark, colour' (as above).
2. Latin pingere 'to paint' to pictura 'a painting, picture' (pictus 'painted' + -ura).
3. Old French peinture (the inherited word) alongside the learned Latin form picture to Middle English picture 'a painting, representation' (15th c.).
4. English picture (the noun that respelled pittoresque as picturesque).

Note on the suffix: English -esque 'in the manner of' comes via French -esque from Italian -esco, ultimately from the Germanic adjectival suffix *-isk- (the same as English -ish and German -isch). No letter labels A–F are used; every stage is named by its real attested form. The blending of French pittoresque with English picture is a folk etymology, not a descent — but it is the single most important event in the word's English history.
