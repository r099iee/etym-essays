I. The Greek berry (5th–1st centuries BCE). The oldest stage of this word's recorded history has nothing to do with flirtation. Greek kókkos (κόκκος) meant “berry, grain, seed, pip” — the seed of a pomegranate, a grain of salt, the kernel of a nut — and, crucially, the scarlet berry of the kermes oak, the insect which yields the brilliant crimson dye of antiquity. Greek medical and botanical writers used the word plainest: kókkos was the small rounded thing. As the production of kermes dye expanded in the eastern Mediterranean, the word travelled with the commodity, carried by trade and by naturalists into the vocabulary of Rome, where it would be reshaped for Latin mouths.

II. Latin coccum and the colour of luxury (1st century BCE–5th century CE). Latin adopted the loanword as coccum, “kermes berry”, and from it the dye itself; Pliny's Naturalis Historia describes the scarlet product of the kermes oak, and the derivative coccineus/coccinus supplied the Latin word for “scarlet”. The colour was a mark of rank and expense — the dyestuffs of Byzantium and Rome were worth their weight in prestige — and coccum accordingly sat in the commercial and ceremonial registers of the language. Meanwhile, a second life of the word began below the literary surface of Late Latin: in glosses and in Vulgar Latin usage coccum is recorded in connection with the barnyard bird, the rooster. How the berry-word came to name the bird is the hinge on which this whole etymology turns.

  • Traditional derivation: the rooster's crest and comb are brilliant red, exactly the colour of the kermes dye; the transfer is a colour-metaphor — “the scarlet one”.
  • Rival derivation: coq is imitative, echoing the bird's cry (French cocorico); under this view the resemblance to coccum is coincidence.
  • Synthesis: since Late Latin glosses (CGL) already gloss the bird as coccum, the colour-metaphor chain is historically plausible; most French dictionaries keep it, while noting the debate.

III. Old French: coq and its diminutives (12th–14th centuries). From the Gallo-Roman soil grows Old French coq, “cock, rooster”, attested from the twelfth century, alongside the diminutive coquelet “cockerel”. The suffix -et/-ette, a true diminutive, was productive, and from the bird-sense the French began to build the ladder of metaphor the word still climbs: the rooster is the archetype of self-important, possessive display — strutting, crowing, guarding the henhouse. The verb coqueter (15th century) means “to strut like a cock, to preen”, and it is a short step from preening to gallantry: coquet comes to describe a vain, amorous man — a “little cock” strutting before the ladies. The feminisation is the decisive move: coquette, the feminine diminutive, settles on the woman who performs the same display with fans, glances and refusals.

IV. French court culture and the crystallisation of the type (17th–18th centuries). In seventeenth-century France, in the salon world of the précieuses and the moralist essayists, the coquette becomes a recognised social species — censured in sermons and conduct books, adored on the stage of comedy, where her oscillation between suitors generates exactly the plot machinery the genre needs. The word enters English in this polite, gallant register around the 1610s; the accompanying adjective coquettish follows in the early eighteenth century, and the noun coquetry (from French coquetterie) in the 1670s. In England the type flourishes in Restoration comedy and the eighteenth-century novel of manners — Millamant in Congreve's The Way of the World (1700) is the immortal specimen — and reaches the American colonies, where Hannah Webster Foster's The Coquette (1797), an epistolary novel based on a real scandal, turns the flirtation into tragedy: the coquette's game, played past its season, costs the heroine reputation, marriage and life. The word by now carries the full moral weight of the centuries: vanity, insincerity, and the danger of treating love as a performance.

  • c. 1610s: English borrowing; the noun is at first courtly and fashionable.
  • 1670s: coquetry enters English from French coquetterie.
  • 1700: Congreve's The Way of the World — Millamant, the perfect literary coquette.
  • 1797: Foster's The Coquette — the word's most famous title; the American moral fable of flirtation.

V. The nineteenth century: romance, science, and the hummingbird. The Romantic century shifts the valuation: coquetry, dangerous under Classicism, becomes a resignation of charm under the new sentimentality, and the word's everyday use begins its slow retreat into literature. In the same period natural history makes an unexpected borrowing: ornithologists studying the hummingbirds of Central and South America encounter the genus Lophornis, whose males wear jewel-like crests and ruff collars and perform brilliant aerial displays for the females. The name “coquette” is applied to them — the tiny preening dandies of the bird world — a scientific coinage that preserves the old barnyard metaphor intact while transferring it from rooster to hummingbird. John Gould's great illustrated monographs on the Trochilidae (1849–1861) made the coquettes famous to Victorian audiences, and the word acquired its second, purely ornamental life.

VI. The twentieth century: eclipse and reclamation. In modern everyday English the word recedes: speakers say flirt, tease, player; coquette survives in novels, costume drama and slightly ironic essay prose, its French double-t and terminal e giving it a period silhouette. Its pronunciation — French [kɔkɛt] in the source, English /kɒˈkɛt/ with shifted stress — traces the borrowing in sound. Then, in the 2020s, the noun is revived from below: “coquette” names a fashion and social-media aesthetic of bows, lace and studied demureness, in which the old reproach is worn proudly, as play. The word has thus completed a full cycle in nine centuries: Greek berry, Roman dye, Gallic rooster, courtly flirt, comic type, tragic heroine, tropical bird, and finally a badge of reclaimed femininity — a single etymology that never left the barnyard, and never let go of the colour red.
