Tocharian is the scholarly name for an extinct branch of the Indo-European family, attested in two closely related languages commonly labelled Tocharian A and Tocharian B, whose manuscripts were recovered from the Tarim Basin in what is now Xinjiang, China. The word is both a linguistic label (“the Tocharian languages”) and an ethnonym for the speakers reconstructed behind them.

Primary sense: the language branch

  • A separate Indo-European branch. Tocharian is not closely related to any other branch; it splits off near the root of the family and preserves a mixture of archaic and innovative features.
  • Two languages. Tocharian A (also called Agnean or Turfanian) and Tocharian B (Kuchean) are distinct enough to be treated as sister languages. A is known mainly from liturgical texts around Turfan; B is better attested and includes secular documents from Kucha.
  • Attestation. The texts date roughly from the sixth to the eighth centuries CE, written on wood, paper, and palm leaf in a north-Indian Brahmi script, and consist largely of Buddhist translations plus monastic and commercial records.

Secondary sense: the people and their culture

  • The speakers. “Tocharian” also denotes the population that spoke the languages, a Central Asian people of the Silk Road oasis cities who acted as intermediaries between Indian, Iranian, Chinese, and steppe worlds.
  • Material culture. The term is used of the Tarim Basin manuscripts, murals, and monastic sites associated with those communities.
  • Genetics and migration debates. Modern scholarship sometimes speaks of “Tocharian” populations in discussions of the Tarim mummies and Bronze Age migrations, though the equation of genes, culture, and language is contested.

Tertiary and technical usages

  • Tocharian A / B. Standard abbreviations in Indo-European linguistics; “Tocharian C” has been proposed for fragmentary material but is not generally accepted.
  • Tocharian loanwords. Sanskrit and Prakrit Buddhist vocabulary entered Tocharian, and Tocharian forms are cited in comparative dictionaries for their archaic phonology.
  • The “Tocharian problem.” A phrase for the puzzle of a centum-type language in a satem-type area, which complicated nineteenth-century models of Indo-European dispersion.
  • Tokharian. An older variant spelling, now less common but still found in older literature.

Semantic bridge: why a people’s name became a language label

The bridge is scholarly convention. When Sieg and Siegling deciphered the manuscripts in 1908, they needed a name for a language with no surviving native designation. They borrowed the name of the Tokharoi, a people mentioned by Greek and Latin geographers in Bactria, and applied it to the new finds. The identification was and remains disputed, but the label stuck, so an ancient ethnonym now names a linguistic branch rather than a securely identified population.

Usage notes

  • Capitalization. As a language and people name, Tocharian is capitalized; the derived adjective is also capitalized.
  • Register. Exclusively academic: Indo-European philology, Silk Road studies, Buddhist studies, and archaeology.
  • Precision. Careful writers distinguish the label from the speakers’ unknown self-designation and note that “Tocharian” is a modern scholarly construct.

In sum, Tocharian names a vanished Indo-European branch and the Silk Road culture behind it, while quietly preserving one of the great naming controversies of modern philology.
