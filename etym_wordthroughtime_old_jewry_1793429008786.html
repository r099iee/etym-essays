- Late 11th c. — Royal enclave: William the Conqueror invites Norman Jews from Rouen to settle in London, establishing the street as the financial and administrative heart of the English Jewry under direct crown protection.
- 1290 — Expulsion and renaming: King Edward I banishes all Jews from England; the street’s property is seized by the crown and redistributed, acquiring the retrospective name la Vielle Iuwerie (‘Old Jewry’) to distinguish the historic quarter.
- 16th–17th c. — Mercantile and professional respectability: Transformed into a street of wealthy merchants, guilds, and medical practitioners, where John Stow documents its antiquity and Jonathan Swift places Gulliver’s first surgical practice.
- 18th c. — Centre of radical Dissent: The Presbyterian Meeting House in Old Jewry becomes one of London’s intellectual epicentres, where Richard Price preaches his celebrated 1789 sermon on the French Revolution.
- 19th–20th c. — Civic policing and institutional finance: Number 26 Old Jewry becomes the official headquarters of the City of London Police (1839–2020), cementing its reputation as a hub of civic law enforcement and corporate finance.
- Today — Financial and corporate thoroughfare: A bustling modern City street lined with corporate offices, law firms, and investment funds, preserving in its name the medieval memory of England’s early Jewish community.
