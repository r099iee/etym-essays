PIE *sed- "to sit, be seated" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; one of the most securely attested PIE roots — source of Sanskrit sad-, Greek hezomai, Latin sedere, Germanic *sitjan) → Latin sedēre "to sit, be seated, remain" (attested 1st c. BCE; source of session, sedentary, sedate, assiduous) → Latin praesidēre "to sit before/in front of, to guard, protect, preside over" — prae- "before, in front" + sedēre "to sit" (attested 1st c. BCE; source of praeses "protector, governor" and praesidens "the one sitting before," present participle) → Old French présider "to preside, be at the head of" (attested 12th c. CE, from Latin praesidēre) → Middle French présider → English preside "to occupy the head seat of authority, to act as chairperson" (borrowed early 17th c. CE, first recorded c. 1610) → preside ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The chain is fully attested and uncontroversial. Two nuances are worth recording: (1) the prefix prae- "before" in praesidēre originally described physical position (sitting in front of a group), and the "guard/protect" sense (praeses "garrison commander, protector") grew from that position of watching before the assembled; (2) English borrowed the verb directly from Latin through French in the early 1600s — the noun president (from Latin praesidens) was borrowed much earlier (c. 1380s) and named the presiding officer, so the family entered English in two waves. PIE *sed- is one of the few roots with reflexes in nearly every Indo-European branch (Sanskrit sad-, Greek hed-, Latin sed-, Germanic *sit-), making the chain exceptionally secure.
