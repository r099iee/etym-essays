• Heath: Similar because heather is the characteristic plant of a heath and the words are historically the same stem; differs because a heath is the land itself (open, uncultivated, often sandy country) while heather is the low shrub that grows on it.
  • Bracken: Similar because it is a low, spreading plant of open upland country; differs because bracken is a large coarse fern with no flowers, while heather is a woody flowering shrub.
  • Gorse / Furze / Whin: Similar because it is a spiny shrub of heathland with yellow flowers; differs because gorse is thorny and evergreen, flowering brightly in gold, whereas heather is soft, bristly-but-unarmed, and blooms purple or pink.
  • Bilberry / Blueberry: Similar because it is a low moorland shrub of the same plant family (Ericaceae); differs because bilberry bears edible fruit and has simple green leaves, while heather is cultivated for its masses of tiny flowers rather than its fruit.
  • Ling: Similar because “ling” is the traditional dialect name for the common heather (Calluna vulgaris); differs because it is regional and older, used chiefly in northern England and Scotland, whereas “heather” is the standard word.
  • Moor: Similar because heather covers moors and the words co-occur constantly; differs because a moor is the open, often wet, uncultivated land, not the plant itself.
  • Shrub: Similar because heather is a shrub; differs because “shrub” is the generic category name for any woody bush, with no particular habitat, colour, or flower attached.
  • Furze (as a type of heathland shrub): Similar in covering the same wild landscape; differs because furze is the yellow-flowered thorny Ulex, quite unlike the soft purple Calluna.
  • Thistle: Similar because it is a rough wild plant of poor, northern soil and a national emblem; differs because the thistle is a tall prickly flowering herb (Scotland’s emblem), whereas heather is a low woody shrub.
  • Rose / wild rose: Similar because it is a familiar small flowering shrub; differs because the rose is cultivated, thorny, and showy in single blooms, with none of heather’s moorland habitat or massed haze of tiny flowers.
  • Rhododendron: Similar because both are flowering shrubs of the family Ericaceae; differs because rhododendron is a large, glossy-leaved, often garden-planted shrub with big flowers, not a low, wiry moorland plant.
  • Moss: Similar because it forms a soft, low, ground-covering mass in damp wild places; differs because moss is a flowerless non-vascular plant, whereas heather is a woody flowering shrub that stands above the ground.
