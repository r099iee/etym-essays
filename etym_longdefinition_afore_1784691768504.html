afore (prep., adv., and conj.) — an archaic and dialectal English word meaning "before": in time ("previously, before now") and in place ("in front of, ahead of"). It is a compound of the prefix a- (from Old English on or æt, "at, in") and fore, the ancient Germanic word for "before." Once a standard preposition and adverb in Middle and Early Modern English, it has retreated in the modern standard language to fixed legal and nautical phrases — aforementioned, aforethought, afore the mast — while surviving as an ordinary word in regional dialects and in storytelling that affects an old-fashioned rustic voice.

1. Core sense — before in time. In its temporal sense afore means "previously, before now, before some other time": "as I've said afore," "afore this evening." This is the sense used by Bert the troll in The Hobbit: "as I've said afore this evening." It is a perfectly ordinary way of saying "before now" in the dialects that retain the word, and it is the sense preserved in the legal phrase aforementioned ("the previously mentioned [thing]") and aforethought ("thought of beforehand," as in malice aforethought).

2. Secondary sense — before in place. In its spatial sense afore means "in front of, ahead of, before" in position: "afore the mast" — the traditional nautical phrase for the forecastle quarters of common sailors, the part of the ship before (in front of) the mainmast. In early records the spatial sense is actually the older one; the temporal sense grows out of it by the ancient and universal metaphor in which space stands for time (what is "before" the eyes comes first).

3. Conjunction and other uses. The word is also recorded as a conjunction meaning "before" with a following clause ("afore he comes") and, in some dialects, in the formula "afore long" for "before long, soon." These uses, once common in regional speech, survive chiefly in dialect literature and in deliberate rustic narration.

4. Register, style, and social history.

  • Archaic and dialectal: in standard modern English the word is obsolete except in fixed phrases; in northern English, Scottish, and some American dialects it is still a living everyday word.
  • Legal fossil: aforementioned, aforethought, and aforenamed survive in law and formal prose; malice aforethought is the classic legal formula for premeditated murder.
  • Nautical fossil: afore the mast (also before the mast) names the forecastle and the life of ordinary sailors.
  • Rustic and storytelling flavour: the word is a favourite of writers marking dialect speech or old-fashioned, fireside narration, and it is exactly this flavour that Tolkien exploits for Bert's troll speech.
  • Derived forms: aforetime ("in former times"), afore-mentioned, afore-said, and the dialect aforehand all occur; the modern standard substitute is before.

5. Historical bridge — how the word was built and lost. Afore is a transparent compound: the prefix a-, representing Old English on or æt ("at, in, on"), fused with fore, the Germanic word for "before." Old English formed the emphatic compounds ætforan and onforan, "at the front, before," which contracted in Middle English to afore (compare how on- gave a- in a-bed, a-hold, a-new). The word's decline began as before — itself a compound of be- + fore — won out in educated speech from the late Middle Ages onward; by the nineteenth century afore had become chiefly dialectal, preserved in the fixed phrases of law and the sea. It is thus a fossil of the older standard language, and its very presence in Bert's speech in The Hobbit instantly marks the trolls as rustic, unlettered, and old-fashioned.

6. Distinctions from near-synonyms. Before is the modern standard successor and is everywhere interchangeable in meaning; ere is an older, different word (Old English ǣr, "earlier") surviving only in poetic and archaic use; prior to and previously are formal and Latinate. Afore occupies a middle ground — not so old as ere, not so standard as before — the dialect survivor par excellence, at home in law, the sea, and rustic speech.
