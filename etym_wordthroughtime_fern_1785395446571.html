- [Old English 'fearn' - the feathery plant] - From its first records the word named the feathery, wing-like, flowerless plant (the name itself built on the idea of a feather or wing); it carried no other major sense, only the plant.
- [Medieval woodland commonplace] - In folklore and herbals the fern was the humble spore-bearing plant of damp woods and heaths: ordinary ground-cover associated with shade and shadow, whose 'seed' (actually spores) was credited with magical invisibility.
- [Victorian pteridomania (19th c.)] - The fern became a fashionable ornament: fern-collecting, glass ferneries, and fern-leaf patterns in wallpaper and ironwork swept Victorian England, briefly lifting a wild woodland plant into high-cultural status.
- [Antiquity and wildness (modern)] - Today the fern stands as an emblem of prehuman antiquity (it dominated the Carboniferous coal forests long before flowers), of wildness, and of damp low-maintenance greenery; the word itself is stable, its register shifting from mundane plant to symbol of geological age and untamed forest floor.
