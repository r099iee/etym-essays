Wilt carries two great lives inside one small spelling. In the source sentence it is the archaic second-person singular of will: try as oft as thou wilt means try as often as you will or wish, the fossil grammar of thou-thee-thy speech preserved in fairy tales, scripture, and poetry. Elsewhere in modern use it is the gardener's verb: plants wilt when they droop, fade, and lose their crisp freshness for lack of water, and by extension people and causes wilt when their energy fails.

The Koshchei sentence shows the modal sense in its natural habitat — a wonder tale told in deliberately ancient diction, where thou and wilt and overtake thee conspire to sound centuries old. The meaning is purely volitional: however often you may want or choose to try, the magic horse cannot be outrun. No drooping plant is involved; the spelling coincidence is one of English's favourite traps, and this card exists to spring it safely.

Core meanings in detail: The modal wilt (pronounced to rhyme with built) is formed exactly like shalt from shall: stem will plus the old second-person ending -t, used only with thou. It expresses volition, futurity, and habitual action — thou wilt go, thou wilt find — and survives today only in liturgy, Shakespeare, hymns, and pastiche. The plant verb wilt (same sound, separate history) describes loss of turgor: cells low on water go limp, leaves hang, stems bend, and the whole plant looks tired; gardeners distinguish temporary midday wilting, which watering cures, from permanent wilting, which kills.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Archaic thou-will, second person of will (modal, from Old English): thou wilt try, thou wilt see. The bridge is pure inflection: will plus -t marks the singular familiar addressee, the sense being wish, intent, or futurity directed at thee.
  • To droop for lack of water, of plants (primary modern sense, from the late 17th c.): the lettuces wilted in the heat. The bridge is observation: gardeners needed a verb for the visible collapse of thirsty greens, probably reshaped from dialect welk meaning to wither.
  • To cause to droop (causative, transitive): the noon sun wilted the roses, grief wilted her. The bridge extends the plant image outward: whatever takes the stiffness out — heat, sorrow, age — wilts its object.
  • To lose energy, courage, or support, of people and movements (figurative): his enthusiasm wilted under questioning, the campaign wilted for lack of funds. The bridge is the plant as emblem of flagging life: human vigour mapped onto vegetable freshness, a metaphor every gardener understands.
  • Plant disease name, as in Fusarium wilt (noun, agricultural, 20th c.): a fungal infection that blocks water vessels so crops collapse. The bridge nominalises the symptom: the visible wilt becomes the name of the killer, familiar in cotton, tomato, and banana farming.
  • To grow limp with heat or fatigue generally (extended physical): collars wilt, spirits wilt on a humid afternoon. The bridge generalises drooping beyond botany to anything — fabric, appetite, attention — that loses its starch.

Register and connotation: modal wilt is solemn, intimate, and antique — prayer, vows, and fairy-tale kings — never to be used with you, since you wilt is ungrammatical theatre. Plant wilt is plain practical English, tender rather than technical: gardeners say it with sympathy, reserving necrosis and senescence for the laboratory. The two wilts share nothing but spelling, and context always tells them apart: thou before it means will, soil and sun around it mean droop.
