Latin prōvincia is a single compound, but its two traditional elements are treated separately because the inner form is debated.

Element 1 (the prefix): PIE *pro- 'forward, ahead, before, on behalf of' (reconstructed) → Latin prō- 'before, on behalf of, forth' (attested in compounds from the 3rd c. BCE; also the source of English pro-).

Element 2 (the traditional, debated base): PIE *weyk- 'to conquer, overcome' (reconstruction varies by authority and is contested) → Latin vincere 'to conquer, defeat' (attested 3rd c. BCE; source of victory, convince, invincible) — the base invoked by the traditional analysis of prōvincia as 'land conquered for (Rome)'.

The chain: PIE *pro- + (debated) vincere → Latin prōvincia 'sphere of duty or command assigned to a magistrate; then the governed territory forming that charge' (attested 3rd-2nd c. BCE; original sense 'charge, commission', not yet purely 'conquered land') → plural Latin prōvinciae 'the provinces of the empire' → Old French province 'province, region under authority' (12th c.) → Middle English province (late 13th-14th c.) → English province, plural provinces (15th c.).

Note on the debate: The exact morphology of prōvincia is not transparent. The pro- + vincere analysis is ancient and traditional (and pedagogically useful), but modern critical dictionaries (e.g. de Vaan's Latin etymological dictionary) treat the inner formation as unclear; a non-Latin, possibly Italic or Etruscan, source cannot be excluded. What is secure is the historical semantic path from 'assigned charge' to 'governed territory'.
