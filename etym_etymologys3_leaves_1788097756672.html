The noun: a Germanic word of peeling. English leaf descends straight down the Germanic line. Proto-Germanic *laubą "leaf, foliage", attested in Gothic laufs, Old Norse lauf, Old High German loub and Modern German Laub "foliage", continues the Proto-Indo-European root *lewbʰ- "to peel off, strip, tear off", the family that also gives Greek lépō "to peel" and the idea of things that flake and come away. The semantic logic is ancient and observational: leaves are the parts of plants that peel, fall and come off, and early Germanic speakers named the whole foliage after its most characteristic behaviour. Old English had lēaf with both senses already in place — plant leaves, and, remarkably early, the sheet of parchment: Anglo-Saxon scribes wrote of the lēaf of a codex, so the "page" sense is as old in English as the plant sense itself.

The plural and its voice. The modern plural leaves is a phonological fossil. In Old English the plural and other inflected forms placed the stem-final consonant between vowels, where intervocalic f was voiced to [v], a sound the scribes kept writing as f; hence the Middle English plural leves, continuing to modern leaves /liːvz/, while the singular kept the voiceless final leaf. The same sound-law produced the famous f/v plural alternations of English — wolf/wolves, loaf/loaves, thief/thieves, knife/knives — and the spelling was fixed in the early modern period. The occasional non-standard "leafs" survives only in quirky fixed names, most famously the Toronto Maple Leafs (1927), whose owners deliberately chose the grammatically impossible form as a marketing gag; the hockey team remains a standing joke against the rule that half/halves and leaf/leaves obey.

From tree to table to book to metal. The sheet logic multiplied senses across Middle English and early modern English. A leaf as a single page stayed standard, and "to turn over a new leaf" appears in the sixteenth century with the moral-resurrection meaning the page-metaphor gives it. Drop-leaf tables and fold-down leaf mechanisms extend the word to movable flat boards from the seventeenth century. Thin metal sheets are called leaves from the late Middle Ages onward, as gold-beating produced "gold leaf" and "leaf gold" for gilding altars, statues and books — precisely the sense in the note's source sentence, where a gilded statue stands "covered with thin leaves of fine gold". The Authorized Version of the Bible (1611) gives the word its most quoted literary echo, "the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2), and Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855) made the plural itself a title of world literature. The image is always the same: something thin and flat, layered like pages, light enough to fall.

The verb: a different ancestry. The verb leave is not a relative of the noun. Old English lǣfan "to permit to remain, to bequeath, to go away from" descends from Proto-Germanic *laibijaną, the causative of *lībaną "to remain, stay", which continues the Indo-European root *leyp- "to stick, to remain attached" — the family of Greek lípos "fat, grease" — the semantic thread being that what sticks to a place remains there, and to make something remain is to leave it. (An older schoolbook link to Greek leípein "to leave" is phonologically impossible for the Germanic labial forms and is rejected by modern scholarship.) The verb's history overlaps the noun's only in spelling: the third-person singular leaves and the plural leaves are identical on paper though unrelated in origin, and the same intervocalic voicing gives the verb its v. A third homograph, the noun leave "permission" (Old English lēaf, as in "by your leave"), descends from the Germanic root for "pleasure, allowance" (compare German erlauben "to permit") and drifted into compound with lǣfan's sense of taking one's leave — three words, three roots, one spelling family on the surface of modern English.

Modern abstractions. The twentieth and twenty-first centuries gave the word its most abstract extensions. In graph theory and computer science, the terminal nodes of a tree are its leaves — the botanical image of root, branch and leaf now structures databases, filesystems, decision trees and phylogenetic trees, so that a programmer calmly says "leaf node" without imagining chlorophyll. In agriculture and nutrition, leaf vegetables, leaf protein and leafy greens formalise the plant sense; in tourism, "leaf-peeping" names the autumn foliage season of New England and Japan; in environmentalism the leaf is the universal green symbol; and in the classroom, leaves remains the textbook example of an irregular plural and of the homograph problem — a word that is two words, teaching children that English spelling records history, not logic.

  • Key stages (noun): PIE *lewbʰ- → Proto-Germanic *laubą → Old English lēaf (plant leaf; parchment page) → Middle English leef / leves → Modern English leaf / leaves.
  • Key stages (verb): PIE *leyp- → Proto-Germanic *lībaną "to remain" and *laibijaną "to cause to remain" → Old English lǣfan → Middle English lēven → Modern English leave, third-person singular leaves.
  • Debated points: the derivation of Germanic *līb- "to remain" from PIE *leyp- "to stick" is the standard view but rests on semantic inference; the f/v voicing account of the plural is uncontroversial; the origin of *lewbʰ- itself is transparent.
  • Attestation anchors: Old English lēaf (glosses and prose), Middle English leves (fourteenth-century manuscripts), Authorized Version (1611), gold-leaf usage in early modern gilding records, the phrase "turn over a new leaf" (16th century), Leaves of Grass (1855), Toronto Maple Leafs (1927).
