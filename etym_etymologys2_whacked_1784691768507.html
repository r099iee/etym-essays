The word is echoic (onomatopoeic), coined directly from the crisp crack of a solid blow; there is no Proto-Indo-European root, and none is reconstructed for the whack family. [The deepest stage is an imitative sound-pattern, not a reconstructed root.] → Early Modern English thwack (verb: to strike smartly with a stick or flat object; noun: such a blow and its sound), first attested in the early 16th century CE — the family's first recorded member → Early Modern English whack (verb and noun), first recorded c. 1712, formed from thwack by loss of the initial th- consonant cluster, or coined independently on the same imitative template (wh- + short vowel + -ack, imitating a sharp, resounding smack) → 18th century: whack (noun — "a sharp blow"; also "a share, a portion," as in a fair whack / top whack) → 18th–19th centuries: whack (noun — "an attempt," as in to have a whack at something) → late 19th century, American English: the idiom out of whack (out of order, misaligned), first used of machinery knocked out of line by a blow → 19th century: whacked (past tense and past participle of the verb — struck sharply, beaten) → 20th century, chiefly British, Australian, New Zealand: whacked (adjective — exhausted, utterly tired) → 20th century, American slang: whacked out (crazy, eccentric; under the influence of drugs) → Modern English WHACKED — the card's target form: the past tense and past participle of whack ("struck with a sharp, resounding blow") and the informal adjective "exhausted." NOTE on the debate: whether whack descends from thwack by consonant-cluster loss or was coined independently is not certain — both accounts are defended in the dictionaries, and they are not mutually exclusive (a re-coinage may have been reinforced by thwack). The related verbs whap and whop are further independent members of the same imitative wh- family.
