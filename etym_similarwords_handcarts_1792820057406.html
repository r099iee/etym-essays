• Barrows: Similar because the source sentence pairs them as fellow hand-pushed carriers in the same lane; differs because a barrow is the smaller one-wheeled garden tool tipped to empty, while a handcart is the larger two-wheeled road vehicle unloaded by hand.
  • Carts: Similar because a handcart is a kind of cart by definition; differs because cart is the broad family word (including horse-drawn and donkey carts), while handcart specifies human power only, no animal.
  • Trolleys: Similar because both are hand-pushed wheeled platforms for loads; differs because a trolley usually has four small wheels or castors for smooth floors, while a handcart has two big wheels and shafts for roads and platforms.
  • Dollies: Similar because warehouse dollies move heavy loads by hand like handcarts; differs because a dolly is a low flat four-wheeled platform for single heavy items (pianos, crates), while a handcart has high wheels, handles, and rails for mixed loads.
  • Wheelbarrows: Similar because both are hand-pushed single-person load carriers; differs because the wheelbarrow balances on one wheel and tips forward, while the handcart balances on two and must be unloaded piece by piece.
  • Wagons: Similar because both are four-sided load vehicles on wheels; differs because a wagon is large and usually horse-drawn or railed for bulk freight, while a handcart is human-scaled and street-nimble.
  • Pushcarts: Similar because pushcarts and handcarts are near-twins of the street market; differs mainly in dialect, pushcart being the American market word, handcart the broader British term covering stations and warehouses too.
  • Rickshaws: Similar because both are human-drawn passenger or goods vehicles; differs because a rickshaw carries people and is pulled at a run, while a handcart carries goods at a walk.
  • Trailers: Similar because both extend carrying capacity on wheels behind a puller; differs because a trailer is towed by a vehicle or bicycle with a hitch, while a handcart is pulled by hands on shafts directly.
