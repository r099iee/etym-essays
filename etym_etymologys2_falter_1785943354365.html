CHAIN: FROM A POSSIBLE PIE "FOLD" ROOT TO FALTER (4 attested/reconstructed stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *pel- "to fold" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE). The connection of falter to a PIE root meaning "fold" is a speculative proposal (Watkins): the sense would be of legs or knees "folding" and giving way. The root is only weakly anchored to this word and must be treated as a debated, not certain, starting point.
  2. Proto-Germanic *faltaną "to fold" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE). Reflexes include Old English fealdan "to fold" and Old High German faltan. If the Watkins link is right, the English verb's ancestor is a frequentative built on this fold-base with the idea of repeated "giving way."
  3. Old Norse faltrask "to be encumbered, to hesitate, to falter" (reflexive verb, Viking Age) and the related adjective föltr "awkward, encumbered, difficult to manage." This Scandinavian base is the generally accepted immediate source of the English verb, and it is where the "stumble / hesitate" meaning is already present.
  4. Middle English falteren "to stagger, totter, stumble in speech or gait" (first recorded in the 15th century; the -er is a frequentative suffix). The form continued unchanged into Modern English.

→ Modern English FALTER (the exact form of this card).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The Old Norse derivation is broadly accepted (OED, Etymonline), but the deeper PIE link to *pel- "to fold" is speculative and not universally endorsed.
  • Middle Low German valteren and Middle Dutch valteren "to stumble, totter" are very close in form; they may be cognates of the English verb or parallel independent formations, so the exact Germanic family is not fully settled.
  • The frequentative suffix -er in falteren is a standard Germanic device (compare wander from wend, hover from hove), but the precise formation history is inferred, not directly attested.
