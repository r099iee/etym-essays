Linear chain from the reconstructed Indo-European base to the modern word. 1. Proto-Indo-European *mer- 'to rub away, to wear down, to crush, to harm' (reconstructed; Pokorny), the base from which the Latin verb of biting is traditionally derived; some scholars refine the immediate shape as *merd- (see debate note below). 2. Latin mordēre 'to bite' (second-conjugation verb, Classical Latin, attested from Plautus, 3rd–2nd centuries BCE); its perfect participle morsus 'bitten' gives the noun morsus, -ūs 'a bite' — both the action of biting and the piece of food bitten off. 3. Late/Vulgar Latin *morsellum (a diminutive of morsum 'a bite'), literally 'a little bite'; this spoken-Latin form is reconstructed, not directly attested in classical texts. 4. Old French morsel / morcel 'a piece, mouthful, or small portion of food' (11th–12th century). 5. Anglo-Norman morsel 'a bite, a mouthful' (12th century). 6. Middle English morsel 'a small bite or piece of food' (attested from the 13th century). 7. Modern English MORSEL (the food sense and, figuratively, a small piece of anything). Notes on debate and nuance: The PIE base of Latin mordēre is debated. The traditional handbooks connect it with *mer- 'to rub away, crush, harm' (Pokorny), with a variant *merd- sometimes posited for the immediate stem; the exact root shape is not settled, and modern scholarship (de Vaan) treats the deeper connection as plausible but not fully certain. The Romance-to-English history, by contrast, is fully documented: Latin mordēre → morsus → spoken-Latin diminutive *morsellum → Old French morsel → Middle English morsel.
