- [Classical/Medieval Latin] surrepticius — "stolen; done by stealth; fraudulently obtained"; strong legal and moral stigma around concealment in procurement.
- [Late 15th–16th c. English] Adjective surreptitious enters learned English — applied to clandestine privileges, secret publications, underhand grants; perception: technical, condemnatory, Latinate.
- [17th–18th c.] Adverb surreptitiously generalizes — any action performed so as to escape notice; fiction and essays use it for quiet illicit or private gestures; still elevated diction.
- [19th–early 20th c.] Novelistic staple — lovers, spies, servants, schoolboys act surreptitiously; readers hear intrigue without slang coarseness; mild moral eyebrow-raise built into the word.
- [Late 20th–21st c.] Surveillance & digital age — photos, recordings, data collection done surreptitiously; privacy ethics debates; perception remains formal and slightly disapproving or thriller-tinged; everyday speech still prefers secretly/sneakily/on the sly.
