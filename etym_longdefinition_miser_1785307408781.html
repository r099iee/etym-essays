MISER is a noun meaning a person who hoards wealth and spends as little as possible, typically living in miserably frugal or squalid conditions for the sake of accumulating money that is never used. The sentence "The old miser counted his gold coins every night in a cold, unheated room, refusing to buy even a loaf of bread" sketches the classic literary type in a single tableau. Every detail is diagnostic: the gold coins counted by hand each night are money never to be spent, wealth treated as an end in itself rather than a means of living; the cold, unheated room shows that the miser will not spend even on his own comfort, denying himself the warmth he could easily afford; and the refusal of a loaf of bread completes the portrait — the miser's poverty is entirely self-inflicted, a voluntary wretchedness chosen so that the hoard can grow untouched. The tone is one of grim moral fable, and the word carries heavy judgment: greed, isolation, and a life drained of every use for the wealth that defines it.

The word's history gives it an irony worth noticing. Its Latin ancestor miser meant "wretched, unhappy, pitiable" — a word of compassion for the unfortunate, with no connection to money at all. The modern sense is a sixteenth- and seventeenth-century English development: in the expanding money economy of early modern England, observers noticed a new social type — the wealthy man who chose to live in squalor, who denied himself bread and fire while hoarding gold — and the language's answer was a metonymy. The man who lives like a miserable person because of money became, simply, a miser. The word's center of gravity slid from the victim of fate to the agent of his own wretchedness, from the object of pity to the object of contempt. That is why miser and miserable, though twins, have diverged so far: the first now names the grasping hoarder, the second the pitiable sufferer.

  • Sense 1 — the hoarder of money (modern, dominant): "a person who hoards wealth and spends as little as possible, especially one who lives in squalid or frugal conditions for the sake of accumulating money" — the sense fixed by about 1600 and universal today.
  • Sense 2 — historical, "a wretched person": the original English sense (15th–16th century), "a wretched, miserable, pitiable person, a victim of misfortune" — now obsolete, but the etymological meaning of the word.
  • Sense 3 — extended, "one who hoards anything": by transfer, a person who accumulates and clings to anything (time, affection, data, food) so compulsively that it is never used — "a miser with his praise", "a miser of information".
  • Sense 4 — figurative and adjectival uses: "miser" appears attributively in combinations (a "miser's hoard", "miser's gold") and feeds the adjective miserly and the abstract noun miserliness; the image of the miser is a standing figure in literature, moral fable, and satire.
  • Historical bridge for the secondary senses: the semantic shift from "wretched person" to "hoarder of wealth" is the key move of the word's history — a metonymy in which the self-inflicted wretchedness of the greedy man redefines the old word for victims. The extension to "hoarder of anything" then generalizes the trait beyond money: whatever is hoarded compulsively and never used is miserly.
  • Register and connotation: the word is strongly pejorative, moralistic, and literary; it implies greed, voluntary self-deprivation, isolation, and a life wasted on the accumulation of useless wealth. It sits at the head of a family of stinginess words and is the most morally charged of them.

Why miser rather than a milder word. The sentence's tableau — the cold room, the nightly counting, the refused loaf — requires a word that implies not mere reluctance to spend but a pathological devotion to hoarding. Miser alone carries that pathology, the voluntary wretchedness that makes the portrait both pitiable and contemptible, and it does so with a moral weight inherited from its long history in fable and satire.
