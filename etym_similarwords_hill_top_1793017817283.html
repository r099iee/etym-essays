• Summit: Similar because both name the highest point of raised ground; differs because summit is loftier and more formal (summits of mountains and of careers), while hill-top is homely and modest — a walk, not an expedition.
  • Peak: Similar because both denote the crown of an ascent; differs because a peak is typically pointed, rocky, and alpine, while a hill-top is rounded, grassy, and gentle.
  • Crest: Similar because both describe the topmost ridge-line where the slope breaks over; differs because crest stresses the narrow ridge itself (the crest of the wave, the hill's crest), while hill-top suggests a broader crown of ground to stand on.
  • Crown: Similar because both picture the head-like summit of a height; differs because crown is openly metaphorical and ceremonial (the crown of the hill), while hill-top is the plain geographical name.
  • Knoll: Similar because both are small rounded eminences of grass; differs because a knoll is the whole little hill itself, while the hill-top is only its summit.
  • Down: Similar because southern English chalk downs present the classic bare hill-top landscape; differs because down names the whole expanse of rolling chalk upland, while the hill-top is a single summit point.
  • Tor: Similar because both are bare rocky summits rising conspicuously; differs because a tor is specifically a granite outcrop pile (Dartmoor tors), while a hill-top may be smooth grass without a stone showing.
  • Heights: Similar because both mean raised ground commanding the lowland; differs because heights is plural and military in tone (seizing the heights), while hill-top is singular, peaceful, and scenic.
  • Plateau: Similar because both are elevated flat-ish ground with wide views; differs because a plateau is a vast high tableland (often miles across), while a hill-top is one hill's small crown.
