The biography of nicety is one of the most dramatic semantic odysseys in English, and it begins not with refinement but with ignorance. At the deepest recoverable layer lies the Proto-Indo-European root *sek-, 'to cut,' extended as *skei-, 'to cut, split, separate.' The cognitive metaphor is ancient and transparent: to know is to cut truth from falsehood, to split the world into distinguished parts. That metaphor yielded Latin scīre, 'to know,' and the large family of science-words—scientia, conscius, presciens—that English later borrowed. Sister branches preserve the physical sense of the root: Greek schizein 'to split' (schism, schizophrenia), Germanic sheath and shed as separation-vocabulary, and Indo-Aryan cutting roots in Sanskrit. The immediate parent of our word is the Latin compound nescīre, 'not to know,' formed from the negative particle ne- plus the stem of scīre; its participle nescius means literally 'not-knowing,' 'ignorant, unaware.' From nescius came Old French nice, 'foolish, simple, silly,' and through Old French the adjective entered Middle English, where from about 1290 onward it meant foolish, wanton, or simple-minded. The celebrated nineteenth-century scholar Richard Chevenix Trench, and later usage historians, traced the long chain of shifts: from foolish to shy or timid; from timid to over-particular and fastidious; from fussy to dainty and delicate; and finally, by the Early Modern period, to precise, exact, and refined in discrimination. Each stage is psychologically continuous: the 'nice' person who seems foolishly over-careful becomes the person whose care is valued as precision. Chaucer used nyce for 'foolish, wanton' in the 1380s; by Shakespeare's day nice could already mean 'fastidious' and 'precise.' The noun nicety (Middle English nicetee, nycetee) followed the same arc. Its earliest senses cluster around folly and foolishness—nicetee is the abstract noun of foolish niceness. From the sixteenth century it settles on the modern senses: a fine point, a subtle distinction, or the quality of exact discrimination. Fixed phrases capture the later stages: 'to a nicety,' 'exactly, with perfect precision,' is recorded from the seventeenth century, and 'the niceties of' a craft or code from the same era. Sound change played its part: Latin nescius passed through Gallo-Romance with palatalization and syncope to Old French nice, and English later levelled the vowels in nice and nicety. Literary attestations trace the arc precisely. Chaucer uses nyce for 'foolish, wanton' in the 1380s, and his nycetee belongs to the folly-sense; Wyclif and the mystery plays continue the 'ignorant, silly' line. In the sixteenth century, however, the tide turns: Spenser and Shakespeare already use nice for 'fastidious' and 'precise,' and the noun nicety begins to mean 'minute exactness.' Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century prose, from the Royal Society's language reformers to the connoisseurs of the Grand Tour, makes nicety a badge of refined judgment; Johnson's Dictionary (1755) records both the modern 'minute exactness' and the obsolete 'folly.' Meanwhile the everyday adjective nice drifted further toward bland pleasantness in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, especially in America, but the critical and technical senses of nicety retained the older, sharper idea of delicate exactness. Pater's use in The Renaissance belongs exactly here: great nicety is required to disengage aesthetic virtue from coarser admixture. Thus a word that began as 'not knowing' ends, in aesthetic prose, as the name for the most knowing kind of attention.
