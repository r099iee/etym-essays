• Stuttered: Similar because both name involuntary broken fluency; differs mainly by ocean — Americans say stutter where Britons say stammer — with stutter leaning slightly toward repeated syllables and stammer toward halting blocks.
  • Faltered: Similar because both describe unsteady, breaking delivery; differs because falter is loss of strength or confidence (a faltering voice, faltering steps), while stammer is specifically tripping over sounds and syllables.
  • Spluttered: Similar because both are explosive, inarticulate outbursts; differs because splutter sprays half-words in excitement or indignation, while stammer catches and repeats from nervousness — spray versus snag.
  • Hemmed and hawed: Similar because both are the sounds of reluctance buying time; differs because hemming and hawing is deliberate evasion (dodge the question), while stammering is involuntary (the tongue will not obey).
  • Mumbled: Similar because both deliver speech unclearly under pressure; differs because mumbling swallows words low and indistinct (the shy trainee), while stammering repeats and blocks them audibly.
  • Babbled: Similar because both are uncontrolled, tumbling speech; differs because babble rushes too fast (excitement, delirium, brooks), while stammer stumbles too slow — flood versus stumbling blocks.
  • Stumbled (over words): Similar because both picture speech tripping; differs because stumbling over one's words is the general metaphor (anyone, any cause), while stammered names the specific clinical pattern of repetition and blockage.
  • Floundered: Similar because both describe someone struggling helplessly mid-utterance; differs because flounder is thrashing without direction (lost in the argument), while stammer is stuck on the mechanics of sound itself.
  • Whimpered: Similar because both can voice fear in broken fragments; differs because a whimper is a thin frightened cry (the victim, the puppy), while a stammer is fractured articulation — sound-shape versus sound-content.
