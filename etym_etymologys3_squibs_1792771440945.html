1. Onomatopoeic Echoism and the Phonosemantic Cluster of /skw-/. The etymological origin of squib belongs to the expressive, echoic stratum of early Modern English vocabulary. Linguists and lexicographers (including the Oxford English Dictionary and Skeat) classify squib as an imitative formation arising in the early sixteenth century (first attested in literary print c. 1525–1534). It belongs to a prominent Germanic phonosemantic cluster beginning with the consonant blend /skw-/ (spelled squ- in English), which characteristically denotes sudden, violent, spurting, squeezing, or hissing physical actions:

  • squirt: Middle English squyrt, to shoot forth liquid in a thin, rapid stream.
  • squeak: Imitative of a thin, sharp, shrill cry.
  • squall: A sudden, violent gust of wind accompanied by hissing rain.
  • squish / squash: Imitative of crushing soft, wet matter with an accompanying wet sound.

The noun squib was coined to capture the exact auditory and kinetic sensation of a burning tube of black powder: the initial hissing "squi-" sound of escaping propellant gases followed by the abrupt, blunt "-b" stop of the casing rupturing.

2. Tudor Street Pyrotechnics and the Green Men. In sixteenth-century Tudor England, fireworks were an increasingly prominent feature of civic pageantry, Lord Mayor’s Shows, and Midsummer Eve celebrations. Pyrotechnic specialists known as "Green Men" or "fire-workers" dressed in ivy leaves and carried clubs that discharged wild sprays of sparks to clear crowds along procession routes. In these spectacles, squibs—small tubes of tough parchment or wrapped pasteboard filled with compacted serpentine powder (a mixture of saltpetre, charcoal, and brimstone)—were hurled by the hundreds. Contemporary Tudor accounts from the 1530s describe revelers tossing squibs through the streets of London, frightening horses and delighting apprentices.

3. The Elizabethan Satirical Metaphor: The Squib as Literary Missile. During the flourishing of Elizabethan drama and pamphleteering in the 1580s and 1590s, master stylists seized upon the word as a vibrant metaphor. London was gripped by vitriolic literary and theological feuds, notably the Martin Marprelate controversy, where anonymous satirists traded vicious pamphlets. Thomas Nashe, Robert Greene, and Gabriel Harvey hurled rapid-fire insults in print, which contemporary commentators christened "paper squibs."

Ben Jonson, in his epigrams and comedies (such as Every Man in His Humour and Timber, or Discoveries), frequently referred to light, ephemeral verse and satirical jabs as squibs. Like the physical firework, a literary squib was small, paper-made, ignited quickly, made a loud, amusing noise that stung anyone standing too close, and was quickly forgotten once its smoke cleared. By the mid-seventeenth century, the word had established itself firmly as a dual-purpose term for both fireworks and witty political lampoons.

4. Enlightenment Jurisprudence and the Famous "Squib Case". In the eighteenth century, the word entered permanent legal history. On the evening of October 28, 1770, in the market town of Milborne Port, Somerset, a young man named Shepherd threw a lighted gunpowder squib into a crowded, enclosed market building. The resulting chain of events culminated in the 1773 Court of King’s Bench ruling in Scott v. Shepherd.

Chief Justice William de Grey’s historic opinion established the foundational common law principle of transferred liability and chain of causation: although two other people had batted the squib before it blinded Scott, their instinctive reactions did not break the causal chain (novus actus interveniens). To this day, across every law school in the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, and Australia, Scott v. Shepherd is universally studied under the shorthand "The Squib Case."

5. 19th-Century Dampness and 20th-Century Cinematic Technology. In the nineteenth century, British newspapers gave birth to the idiom "a damp squib" to satirize political campaigns and theatrical openings that flopped miserably. Concurrently, coal mining operations adopted electrical squibs as reliable igniters for blasting powder cartridges underground. In the twentieth century, Hollywood technicians borrowed the mining squib to invent modern cinematic gunshot effects, securing the word a high-tech future. When J.R.R. Tolkien included squibs in Bilbo Baggins’s firework display in The Fellowship of the Ring, he drew upon this multifaceted etymological tapestry, blending rustic Elizabethan pyrotechnics with English nostalgia to create an unforgettable celebration of festive light.
