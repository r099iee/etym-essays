Brot is the German noun for bread: the staple food made by baking a dough of flour, water and usually yeast or sourdough. It is a neuter noun, das Brot, with the genitive des Brotes or des Brots, and the plural die Brote, which counts loaves or kinds of bread rather than slices. The word is one of the most culturally loaded items in the German lexicon: it names a food, a meal, a livelihood, a sacrament and a political slogan. Its resemblance to English bread — the point of the card's comparison — is a genuine cognate relationship, both words descending from the same Proto-Germanic noun.

Primary sense: the baked food. In its core sense Brot denotes the loaf produced by baking a flour dough. German distinguishes many kinds by grain, leavening and shape: Weizenbrot (wheat bread), Roggenbrot (rye bread), Schwarzbrot (dark rye bread), Weißbrot (white bread), Vollkornbrot (wholemeal bread), Sauerteigbrot (sourdough bread), Fladenbrot (flatbread), Knäckebrot (crispbread) and Pumpernickel, the dense Westphalian rye bread. The diminutive Brötchen (literally “little bread”) is the ordinary word for a bread roll and is regionally replaced by Semmel, Schrippe, Weck or Rundstück.

  • The loaf as object. Brotlaib and the simpler Laib name a whole loaf; Brotscheibe is a slice, Brotkante the crust end, Brotkrume the soft crumb, Brotkruste the crust. Brotmesser, Brotkasten, Brotdose and Brotkorb are the associated household objects.
  • The meal. Abendbrot (evening bread) is the ordinary German word for supper in many regions, and Brotzeit is the Bavarian and Austrian mid-morning or afternoon snack. Butterbrot is an open sandwich, and Brotaufstrich is any spread for bread.
  • Livelihood. Sein Brot verdienen means to earn one's living; Brotberuf is a bread-and-butter occupation, the job one does for income rather than vocation; Brotgeber is an employer and Brotneid is envy of another's livelihood. These uses rest on the ancient equation of bread with the means of subsistence.
  • Religion. In the Lord's Prayer, Unser täglich Brot gib uns heute (“give us this day our daily bread”), and in the Eucharist, Brot and Wein are the consecrated elements. Der Mensch lebt nicht vom Brot allein (“man shall not live by bread alone”) is a familiar biblical quotation.
  • Politics and culture. Brot und Spiele is the German rendering of Juvenal's panem et circenses, “bread and circuses”; Brot für die Welt is the name of a major German Protestant development agency.

Secondary sense: livelihood and money. The step from “bread” to “means of subsistence” is ancient and pan-European: one works for one's bread, and the staff of life stands for the whole economy of survival. German preserves this vividly in Brotberuf, Brotgeber and Brotneid. In colloquial speech Brot can also stand for earnings or money, though it is milder and less common than Kohle, Knete or Asche; the metaphor is the same, since money buys bread. The derived brotlos means “without a livelihood”, and to be brotlos is a formal way of saying unemployed — a reminder that the word's economic sense is not merely poetic.

Tertiary sense: figurative and technical extensions. Brot supplies a rich set of images. Brotbaum is the breadfruit tree; Brotgetreide is bread grain; Brotpreis belongs to the social history of subsistence, and Brotkorb is the basket in which bread is served or kept. Brotsuppe and Brotpudding are dishes of thrift, made from stale bread. In baking technology, Brotteig, Brotbacken and Brotführung are craft terms. The word also appears in the phrase Brot und Salz, the traditional gift of welcome and blessing at a housewarming, where the two staples symbolise hospitality and prosperity.

Semantic bridges. The literal loaf generates the other senses along three paths. Metonymy turns the food into the meal (Abendbrot) and into the means of living (Brotberuf). Biblical allusion turns it into a sacrament and a sign of divine provision. Social history turns it into a political symbol, above all in Brot und Spiele, where the gift of food is exposed as a technique of pacification. The German language is unusually explicit about this last bridge because bread really was, for centuries, the calorie base of the diet and therefore the first thing to become scarce in a famine or a war.

Register and connotation. Brot is warm, homely and fundamental. It appears in nursery rhymes, fairy tales and greetings; it is the first food a child is given and the last comfort of the poor. But the same word can be grim: Brot und Spiele is cynical, Brotneid is petty and envious, and brotlos is bureaucratic misfortune. In contemporary food culture the word has acquired a new prestige: artisanal Sauerteigbrot, regional grain varieties and the UNESCO listing of German bread culture have turned the ordinary loaf into heritage. At the same time, the gluten-free and low-carb market has made Brot a word of dietary anxiety as well as comfort.
