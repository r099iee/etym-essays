The biography of aeons is the biography of time itself — of how a word meaning "vital force" came to measure the age of a planet. Its Proto-Indo-European ancestor, *h₂eyu-, meant the living force and length of life of a creature: the breath, the days, the span allotted to a being. In Vedic Sanskrit this is the root's most primitive state preserved: āyus is "life, vital force," the treasure the hymns beg the gods to lengthen, and āyurveda — "the science of life" — still carries the ancient sense into modern medicine and wellness language. Hindi āyu, "age, lifetime," continues the same line; the Sanskrit idea of śatāyu, "a hundred lives," shows the root's core meaning of "lifetime." At this stage the word has not yet left the body: an aeon is what one organism lives.

The Greek branch is where the word begins its outward, cosmic expansion. Greek aiṓn starts at the same biological sense — "lifetime, age" (Homer already uses it so) — but the Greek philosophical and religious imagination stretched it to cover first a generation, then a long period, then an age of the world, and finally eternity itself. In Stoic and Platonist cosmology the aiōnes are the great cycles of world-time; in the New Testament the adjective aiṓnios, "eternal," attaches the word to the life of God and the age to come; and in Gnostic cosmology the "Aeons" — capitalized — are the eternal emanations that fill the divine Pleroma, personified ages often counted in sets of thirty. In late antiquity the expansion reached its limit: Aion was worshipped as a god, the personification of eternal, cyclic time, often shown as a lion-headed figure wound about with a serpent. Meanwhile the Latin branch ran a parallel course: aevum "age" gave English its everyday "age" (via Old French aage), and its derivatives "medieval" (the middle age), "coeval" (of the same age), "primeval" (of the first age), and "eternal" (from aeviternus, "of the age stretched to infinity"). The Germanic branch kept the root's insistence on duration without end: Proto-Germanic *aiwaz gave Old English ǣ and ā, and from these come "aye" and "ever" and, with the intensive prefix, "forever."

The English word itself is a direct borrowing: aeon entered the language in the 16th century from Greek aiṓn (through ecclesiastical and philosophical Latin aeon), at first as a grand, cosmic, religious word — the Gnostic "aeons," the eternal ages of the divine. In the 17th and 18th centuries it passed into general learned use for "an age or indefinite period," still carrying a religious or cosmic halo. Then, in the nineteenth century, geology gave the word a final, precise home: as "eon," it became the formal name of the largest division of the geologic time scale — a duration of half a billion years or more — while in ordinary prose the plural "aeons" became a rhetorical intensifier for "an unimaginably long time." The word thereby achieved a remarkable split personality: the strictest unit in the vocabulary of time and simultaneously the vaguest.

Bullet points on the trajectory:
  • Vedic period (c. 1500–1000 BCE) — Sanskrit āyus "life, vital force"; āyurveda "the science of life."
  • 8th c. BCE — Homeric Greek aiṓn "lifetime, age."
  • Classical and Hellenistic period — the sense expands: generation, age of the world, eternity.
  • 1st c. CE — New Testament aiṓnios "eternal"; late antique worship of the god Aion.
  • Gnosticism — the capitalized Aeons, emanations of the Pleroma.
  • 16th c. — English aeon borrowed via Late Latin; first a theological and cosmic word.
  • 17th–18th c. — general learned use for "an age, a long period."
  • 19th c. — geology formalizes eon as the largest time unit; the plural aeons becomes a literary intensifier.
  • 19th–21st c. — spelling split: aeons (classical, British-flavoured) vs eons (American, geological); the plural remains the standard literary form — "aeons ago," "for aeons," "aeons of erosion."
Sound and form notes: the Greek diphthong ai- (aiṓn) was kept in the Latin borrowing aeon and in the English aeons, and the American spelling eon dropped the initial a — a classic example of orthographic modernization rather than sound change; the pronunciation /ˈiːɒn/ is identical in both spellings. The word's final destination is visible in the source sentence: a Martian river-channel, measured in "earlier aeons," stands at the end of a three-thousand-year journey from the breath of a living thing to the age of the world.
