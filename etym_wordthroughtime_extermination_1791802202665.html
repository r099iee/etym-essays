- Mid-15th c. — the word enters English in theology, meaning the driving out of sin, vice or heresy from the soul; the object is a moral condition, and no physical killing is implied.
- 16th c. — “utter destruction” emerges and spreads to peoples, vermin and opinions; the older sense of banishment recedes but does not vanish.
- 17th c. — colonial warfare gives the word its modern object: the extermination of indigenous populations is openly discussed and defended in settler writing.
- 18th–19th c. — imperial expansion and racial theory make “extermination” a term of policy and public debate; pest control adopts it as a commercial commonplace.
- 1940s — the Holocaust fixes the word to industrialised mass murder; “extermination camps” and the Nuremberg proceedings give it an irreversible moral charge.
- 1998–2002 — the Rome Statute defines extermination as a crime against humanity distinct from genocide, codifying the lethal-conditions sense.
- Contemporary — used in atrocity discourse and human-rights law, but also still for rats and termites, and hyperbolically for institutions and high streets.
