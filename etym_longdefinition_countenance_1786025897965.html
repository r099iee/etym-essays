COUNTENANCE is a word with two distinct careers: a noun meaning "the face; facial expression; composure" and a verb meaning "to tolerate, approve, or permit." The link between the two is facial approval — the word for the face became the word for the expression of support shown on the face, and the expression of support became the act of allowing something to happen. The source passage plays exactly on this: "that you will countenance the suffering and death of thousands" means that you will permit it, look upon it without disapproval, let your face silently grant it leave. To countenance an evil is to give it your silent consent by refusing to look away or object.

The word entered English in the 13th century from Old French contenance, "bearing, demeanor, composure," which itself descends from Latin continentia, "self-restraint, moderation, holding together" — the abstract noun of continēre, "to hold together, contain, restrain." The historical bridge is elegant: composure is the holding-together of the self, visible in the calm face; the calm face is the countenance; the approval that shows in the face is countenance in the extended sense; and finally to countenance a thing is to let it stand by showing no contrary face.

Primary and Secondary Senses

  • 1. The face, or the look on it (noun, now literary or formal). The literal sense: a person's face and especially its expression — "her countenance fell at the news." In modern English this is the most poetic use of the noun, common in older literature and still alive in phrases like a stern countenance.
  • 2. Composure and self-control (noun, archaic and figurative). To keep one's countenance is to remain composed, to keep a straight face, not to laugh or show emotion. This sense survives chiefly in that idiom and in literary usage.
  • 3. Approval, support, or sanction (noun). The extended sense: favour shown toward a person or cause, especially moral or political backing — "he gave his countenance to the reform." This is the sense that bridges to the verb: support is literally the favourable expression you show on your face.
  • 4. To tolerate or permit (verb, the dominant modern sense). To allow something to happen, especially something objectionable, without protest or objection — "I will not countenance such behaviour." In modern English the verb is overwhelmingly used in the negative or with implied disapproval: one countenances something only by declining to forbid it.
  • 5. To approve or endorse (verb, now less common). A positive act of support or patronage — "the abbot countenanced the young scholar." This older positive sense has been largely absorbed by the tolerative use.

Metaphoric Shifts and Semantic Bridges

The whole modern range of countenance can be read as a single movement from the physical to the moral. Stage one: the face. Stage two: the expression as evidence of inner state, hence composure or its loss. Stage three: the favourable expression as a sign of support, hence patronage and approval. Stage four: approval as permission — since disapproval would be shown by a frown or refusal, the absence of a hostile face is treated as consent. Stage five: consent as tolerance — to countenance is finally to permit what you could have forbidden. In this final stage the word has almost detached from the face entirely: it is now a verb of moral allowance, which is why it is most at home in sentences about wrongdoing, cruelty, and complicity, as in the source passage about suffering and death.

Register and Tone

  • Register: Formal and literary in both parts of speech; the noun is often archaic or poetic, the verb belongs to serious, official, or moralising discourse.
  • Negation: The verb collocates strongly with negation — cannot, will not, refuse to, never — so that "to countenance X" almost always means "to refuse to allow X."
  • Idiom: in the countenance of is a rare formal idiom meaning "in the presence of"; the noun also appears with the face-sense throughout Shakespeare and the King James Bible.
  • Distinction: The noun has no direct synonym as precise as itself: face is the plain word, visage is archaic and grander, mien stresses bearing rather than the face itself, and expression names the feature rather than the whole face.
