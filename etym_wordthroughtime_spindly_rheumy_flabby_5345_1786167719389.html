- 17th century: "spindly" is coined from spindle + -y — "long and thin as a spindle," the tapering rod on which thread is spun — applied first to legs and limbs stretched out beyond strength.
- 18th-19th centuries: the word spreads from bodies to plants and furniture — anything grown too tall and weak for its own stem or frame — so that spindly now means thin to the point of mechanical fragility.
- 17th-18th centuries: "rheumy" (from rheum, Greek rheuma, "a flow") is a technical word of old medicine — the watery discharge of eyes and nose read as a leak in the body's humoral economy.
- 19th century to present: the medical theory dies but the word survives in literary use — rheumy eyes are the weeping, filmy, moisture-laden eyes of age, sickness, or feebleness, now a pitying rather than diagnostic description.
- Late 17th century: "flabby" (1690s) is formed from flab + -y, of the imitative family of flap — soft, loose flesh that yields under pressure and flops about.
- 18th-19th centuries: the sense stretches beyond flesh — flabby argument, flabby prose, flabby character — anything soft, lax, and lacking vigour or structure.
- Modern: the physical sense of flesh without muscle tone or resilience remains central — the tactile opposite of firm, taut, muscular — while the figurative sense of slackness persists.
