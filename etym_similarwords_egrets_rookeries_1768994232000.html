• Heron: Similar in being a long-legged wading bird of shallow water in the same family (Ardeidae); differs because "heron" is the general family name, often grey, brown, or blue, while "egret" names the typically white heron with ornamental breeding plumes.
  • Crane: Similar in being a tall wading bird of wetlands; differs because cranes belong to a different family (Gruidae), are heavier, fly with neck outstretched, and trumpet loudly, whereas egrets fold the neck back in flight and keep silent.
  • Bittern: Similar in haunting the same marsh; differs because the bittern is streaked, brown, secretive, and famous for a booming call — the deliberate opposite of the egret's white visibility and silence.
  • Aigrette: Similar in springing from the same etymon; differs because "aigrette" is the doublet that names the egret's plume itself and the hat-feather ornament, not the living bird.
  • Stork: Similar in being a large white long-legged wading bird; differs because storks (Ciconiidae) are unrelated to herons, nest on rooftops and chimneys in solitary pairs, and are famed for carrying babies in folklore, not for breeding plumes.
  • Ibis: Similar in being a long-legged wading bird with white forms; differs because ibises (Threskiornithidae) have down-curved bills and fly with neck outstretched, while the egret is a heron with a straight bill and a folded-neck flight profile.
  • Colony: Similar to rookery in naming a group of animals dwelling together; differs because "colony" is the general biological term for any associated group (ants, bees, penguins), while "rookery" specifies a dense breeding nesting site, chiefly of birds.
  • Heronry: Similar in being a bird nesting colony; differs because a "heronry" is a herons' nesting ground specifically, while a "rookery" is properly a rooks' colony, though both can be loosely extended to other gregarious birds.
  • Nest: Similar in being a place where birds breed; differs because a "nest" is a single constructed home, while a "rookery" is the whole communal cluster of many nests and their occupants.
  • Breeding ground: Similar in being a place of reproduction; differs because a "breeding ground" is a neutral, often non-structural site (a beach, a mudflat), while a "rookery" is a built, tree-top structure dense with nests.
  • Slum: Similar to the extended sense of rookery in naming crowded, poor urban quarters; differs because "slum" is the modern general term for such districts, while "rookery" preserves the old, vivid image of close-packed tenements "like nests" and is now archaic or historical.
  • Tenement: Similar in naming the crowded housing of the rookery-slum; differs because a "tenement" is the individual overcrowded building itself, while the "rookery" is the whole warren of such buildings packed together.
