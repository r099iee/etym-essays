- 14th–15th centuries — ME boistous means rough, crude, fierce, violent, stiff; applies to persons, forces, and strong things; tone often harsh or pejorative; deeper French origin already opaque to speakers.
- 16th century — form settles toward boisterous; frequent of winds, storms, and rough physical force; still close to ‘violent/turbulent’ more than ‘cheerfully noisy.’
- 17th century — literary English cements elemental uses (boisterous weather) beside rough human conduct; adverbial boisterously available for manner.
- 18th–19th centuries — major perception shift: boisterous laughter, boisterous play, boisterous mirth become common; word often describes loud high spirits without implying cruelty; children’s boisterousness becomes a social theme.
- 20th century — standard school, family, and sports vocabulary for noisy energy; weather sense survives mainly in literary or journalistic color writing.
- 21st century — public default hearing is ‘loudly, rowdily, full of unrestrained energy’ (usually human); can be fond or complaining; rarely heard as ‘savagely violent’ outside historical texts. The exact form boisterously is the neutral manner adverb of that modern sense-cluster.
