- [12th–14th c. Old French] — Existed across the English Channel as esquiper / esquipement, strictly designating the maritime act of fitting out, manning, and rigging a sailing vessel for an ocean voyage — [perception: maritime labor of naval provisioning, carpentry, and preparing wooden ships for stormy seas]
- [Early 18th c. Augustan Era] — Formally borrowed into English from French équipement (c. 1717), initially preserving its nautical and military focus, denoting the outfitting of soldiers, cavalry regiments, and warships with necessary supplies — [perception: military and aristocratic logistical term for martial outfitting, uniforms, and campaign supplies]
- [Late 18th–Early 19th c. Romantic Era] — Extended metaphorically into personal and domestic spheres to describe whatever clothing, provisions, or instruments a person had on hand, as seen in Wordsworth's The Prelude ("chance equipment of that hour") — [perception: versatile descriptor for personal traveler's gear, impromptu resources, and everyday apparel]
- [19th c. Victorian & Industrial Era] — Underwent massive semantic explosion driven by the Industrial Revolution, expanding to encompass heavy factory machinery, steam turbines, agricultural implements, and railway rolling stock — [perception: monumental symbol of industrial progress, mechanical power, and technological civilization]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Codified in corporate finance as a major capital asset category ("Property, Plant, and Equipment - PP&E"), standardized in occupational health protocols (PPE), and adapted into philosophy and literary theory (Kenneth Burke's "equipment for living") — [perception: universal, indispensable noun spanning heavy corporate capital assets, digital lab gear, and psychological frameworks for survival]
