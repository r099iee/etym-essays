• Snowdrop: Similar because both denote delicate, early-blooming white flowers associated with purity and known colloquially as "fair maids" (specifically the fair maids of February); differs because snowdrop refers precisely to *Galanthus nivalis* with nodding, bell-shaped green-tipped tepals that pierce winter snow, whereas fair-maids of France refers to double-flowered white ranunculus (*Ranunculus aconitifolius*) blooming in late spring.
  • Buttercup: Similar because both belong to the genus *Ranunculus* within the family Ranunculaceae; differs because common buttercups are typically single-flowered, glossy golden-yellow wildflowers (*Ranunculus acris* or *repens*), whereas fair-maids of France is an ornamental, double-flowered cultivar featuring dense rosettes of pure white petals.
  • Gillyflower: Similar because both are classic cottage garden perennials featured in historical English floral borders and literature; differs because gillyflower (historically *Dianthus caryophyllus* or *Matthiola incana*) possesses a spicy, clove-like scent and ruffled petals in shades of pink, crimson, or purple, whereas fair-maids is characterized by its scentless, pristine white rosettes.
  • Ladysmock: Similar because both are traditional spring-blooming English wildflowers associated with feminine folk names and pastoral poetry; differs because ladysmock (*Cardamine pratensis*, the cuckoo-flower) belongs to the mustard family (Brassicaceae) and features pale lilac or pink four-petaled cruciform blooms in wet meadows, whereas fair-maids is a cultivated double ranunculus of alpine origin.
  • Bachelor's-button: Similar because both are old-fashioned cottage garden plants characterized by compact, button-like rosette blossoms with folk names referencing marital status; differs because bachelor's-button usually designates the vibrant cornflower (*Centaurea cyanus*) or the double yellow meadow buttercup, carrying masculine, courting connotations, whereas fair-maids specifically celebrates feminine maidenhood and white purity.
  • Meadow Saxifrage: Similar because its double-flowered variety (*Saxifraga granulata* plena) was occasionally called "fair-maids of France" in northern British folk taxonomy due to its white rosettes; differs because true meadow saxifrage belongs to the family Saxifragaceae, grows from small subterranean bulbils, and bears distinct basal kidney-shaped leaves.
