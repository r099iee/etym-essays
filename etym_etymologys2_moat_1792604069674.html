Note on historical semantic debate: The etymological derivation of moat illustrates a classic historical phenomenon known as semantic metonymic inversion (or reciprocal transfer). The original Germanic and Gallo-Romance base *motta designated an elevated clump, mound, or hillock of earth—the precise artificial hill upon which early Norman castles were erected (a "motte-and-bailey"). Because constructing such an elevated mound required digging out a vast volume of surrounding earth, the resulting continuous circular ditch was intimately bound to the mound itself. By the early 14th century in Anglo-Norman and Middle English speech, the label mote transferred its primary referent from the raised mound to the surrounding excavated ditch, while French maintained motte for the earthen mound and adopted fossé (from Latin fossa, "ditch") for the watery moat.
