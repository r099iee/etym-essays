Tight-fisted is a transparent compound, but each half has a deep Germanic past. To understand it we must track tight and fist separately before they clench together around 1800.

PART 1 — TIGHT. Modern tight enters Middle English around 1400 as thight, tight meaning "dense, close-packed, watertight." It is a borrowing from Old Norse þéttr "watertight, close, dense," itself from Proto-Germanic *þinhta- "dense, solid." The ultimate PIE source is debated but commonly given as *tenk- "to draw, stretch tight, thicken, congeal" (also yielding thank? thick? taut?). Old Norse settlers in northern England supplied the word; southern Old English had used þiht, tyht sparingly and fast, heard more often. By 1500 tight meant firmly fixed, fitting closely, stretched, waterproof, and by 1700 also "stingy" in the phrase tight hand and "drunk" (tight as a drum). The semantic core is resistance to leakage or loosening — whether water, air, money, or self-control leaking out.

  • Norse to English: Viking-age contact (800-1100) plants þéttr in northern dialects. The Ormulum and later northern texts show thight for dense fabric, close joints, watertight ships.
  • Early Modern widening: Shakespeare has tight for well-laced, deft, sound. Sailors speak of a tight ship. Moralists speak of a tight hand that does not open in charity.
  • Parsing tight-fisted: Here tight is adverbial — clenched tightly — describing how the fist holds.

PART 2 — FIST, FISTED. Fist is ancient. Old English fȳst (c. 800) means "clenched hand," from Proto-Germanic *fustiz, from PIE *penkʷe- "five" via a stem *pn̥k-sti- "bundle of five (fingers)." Cognates include German Faust, Dutch vuist, and distantly Latin pugnus "fist" and pung- (pugnacious). The five-finger etymology is elegant: the fist is the five gathered. Old English already had idioms fyst-gemōt (fist-fight) and fyst-slege (blow). Middle English fist keeps the form, adding the sense "handwriting" (a scribe's fist) and grasp.

  • -ed suffix: -ed here is not past tense but the adjectival suffix meaning "having, provided with" (as in blue-eyed, three-legged, open-handed), from Old English -ede. Fisted alone (c. 1600) meant "having a fist of a specified kind" — big-fisted, horny-fisted for labourers.
  • Compounding: Close-fisted is recorded 1680s, hard-fisted and fast-fisted early 1700s, tight-fisted by the early 1800s (OED first clear use c. 1830s, but Dickens in 1843 popularises it). The image is physiological: a fist so tightly clenched that coins cannot slip out, the opposite of open-handed, free-handed, liberal.

The compound's triumph owes much to Dickens. "A tight-fisted hand at the grindstone" piles body metaphors: fist (grasping), hand (worker), grindstone (relentless labour). Victorian political economy debated miserliness versus thrift; tight-fisted let reviewers condemn Scrooge without praising waste. The hyphen marks it as a phrasal adjective before a noun; predicatively "he is tight fisted" often drops it.

  • Modern status: Fully colloquial, mildly pejorative, less severe than miserly or niggardly. American English prefers tight, cheap, stingy; British keeps tight-fisted, mean. Related forms: tight-fistedness, tight-fistedly.
  • Debate note: The PIE source of tight (*tenk-) is less secure than that of fist (*penkʷe-). Etymonline and OED flag *thinhta- as of unknown or disputed origin; *tenk- is the best hypothesis, linking tight to thick and taut, but not certain. The Norse-to-English borrowing itself is undisputed.

Together, two Iron-Age roots — drawing tight and bundling five fingers — meet in a Victorian counting-house to name a man who will not open his hand.
