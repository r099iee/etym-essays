• BRIDGES (Robert Bridges): Similar because Bridges is the card's own paired name — a Victorian/Edwardian master of learned, experimental prosody, whose metres are "culled" alongside Swinburne's by the schoolroom handbooks; differs because Bridges was the mainstream Poet Laureate and a systematic prosodic theorist, while Swinburne was the shocking, scandal-tinged outsider whose virtuosity came with daring themes.
  • TENNYSON: Similar because Tennyson is a major Victorian poet famed for musical prosody, and the two are the twin peaks of Victorian metrical art; differs because Tennyson's verse is decorous, laureate-approved, and technically sumptuous within classical decorum, whereas Swinburne courted daring themes and exotic Greek-inspired metres that shocked the public.
  • BYRON: Similar because Byron's reputation, like Swinburne's, was built on scandal and daring themes — the Byronic and the Swinburnian both connote rebellion; differs because Byron belongs to an earlier Romantic generation and is a narrative and satirical poet, not a metrical experimenter, so the name does not connote prosodic virtuosity at all.
  • ROSSETTI (Dante Gabriel Rossetti): Similar because Rossetti was Swinburne's close associate in the Pre-Raphaelite circle, sharing sensuous, medievalising and sometimes shocking subject matter; differs because Rossetti's fame rests on painting and on a smaller, more lapidary poetic corpus, whereas Swinburne's fame rests on sheer metrical fluency and volume.
  • SHELLEY: Similar because Shelley was Swinburne's own great predecessor in radical politics and lyrical audacity, and Swinburne consciously modelled his political verse on him; differs because Shelley belongs to the high Romantic generation and is celebrated for visionary lyric, not for recherché Greek metres or for scandalous erotic themes.
  • WHITMAN: Similar because Swinburne was one of Whitman's earliest and most ardent champions in Britain, and both names connote a freeing of prosody from conventional bondage; differs because Whitman's free verse abandons metrical structure altogether, while Swinburne's virtuosity consists precisely in mastering the most intricate classical metres.
