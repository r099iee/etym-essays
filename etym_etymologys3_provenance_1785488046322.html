The biography of PROVENANCE begins with one of the most ancient and productive roots in the Indo-European family: *gʷem-, "to go, to come, to step". Nearly every Indo-European branch forged its own verb of motion from it. In Germanic the root became *kwemaną, giving Old English cuman and modern English come, with its relatives became and overcome. In Greek it produced baínō, "to go, to step", the source of basis — "that on which one steps or stands" — and of basic and even diabetes, literally "that which passes through". In Indo-Iranian it yielded Sanskrit gámati, "he goes", and the noun gāti, "going, motion", preserved in modern Hindi and Odia as gati (गति / ଗତି), "speed, movement". A root meaning simply "to go" thus underlies words as different as a dictionary's basis and a diabetic's disease.

The decisive branch for this card is Latin. The Latin verb veniō, "to come", descended directly from *gʷem-yō, and it proved exceptionally productive. Prefixed forms gave English some of its most common words:

  • convenīre, "to come together" — convention, convent, and the adjective convenient.
  • advenīre, "to come to" — adventure, from the participle adventurus, "about to come".
  • inventīre, "to come upon, to find" — invention and inventory.
  • pervenīre, "to come through" — the Latin model behind pervade.
  • provenīre, "to come forth, to spring up" — formed from prō-, "forward, forth", and venīre, and used of things emerging from a source, such as crops springing from the earth or results issuing from a cause.

From provenīre Latin derived the noun proventus, "a coming forth, an issue, a produce, a crop" — the visible result of something having come out of its source. French inherited the verb provenir, "to originate or derive from", and in the eighteenth century French formed the noun provenance, "origin, the place from which something comes". The word passed into English in the 1780s, first as a neutral, bookish synonym for origin or source, with no special prestige — a word of naturalists and antiquaries describing where a plant, an object, or an idea had come from.

The modern career of the word began in the nineteenth century, when the art and antiquarian world took it up. A painting's provenance — the documented chain of owners, dealers, and exhibitions — became the evidence of its authenticity and the key to its value; a work with a full provenance could be trusted, priced, and insured, while a work with a gap in its history was suspect. In the twentieth century provenance became the indispensable term of museums and auction houses, and it acquired an ethical dimension: a work shown to have been looted or smuggled is said to lack proper provenance, and institutions are now expected to research the wartime and colonial histories of their holdings. The same logic spread to other fields:

  • Data provenance: the documented lineage of digital data from source to result, essential for audit, reproducibility, and trust in algorithms.
  • Sediment and mineral provenance: the source region of a rock or grain, reconstructed by geology.
  • Seed provenance: the geographic origin of forestry stock, which determines hardiness and survival.

In the sentence of this card the author's casual mention of "questions of provenance and textual detail" activates the whole scholarly and curatorial machinery: he is waving away the apparatus of source-criticism, attribution, and bibliography as beside the point for a study of rhythm. The word itself, born of a root meaning simply "to come", has become the record of everything that has come — and of every hand through which it has passed.
