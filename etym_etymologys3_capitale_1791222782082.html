Capitale travels from a severed-head adjective to the master-word of modern economics.

Its ultimate root is Proto-Indo-European *kaput- (head), giving Latin caput, capitis (head, top, life, person, mouth of a river, chief). Latin derives capitalis (of the head, chief, capital, deadly) by adding -alis, and substantivises the neuter as capitale, capitalis (chief thing, principal, stock, property). The formation is transparent: head → head-thing → chief wealth.

  • Classical Latin (3rd c. BCE – 3rd c. CE): Capitalis is common in law and rhetoric: causa capitalis, poena capitalis, facinus capitale (matters touching the head/life, hence capital trials). Capitale as noun means principal sum, stock, cattle-property. Agricultural writers (Cato, Varro, Columella) manage capitale as live inventory; moneylenders oppose capitale to usura (interest). Interest is figured as offspring: the principal calves. Phonologically caput + -alis > capitalis with regular vowel weakening (caput, capitis) and stress on -ta-: /ka.piˈtaː.lis/.
  • Late and Medieval Latin (4th–14th c.): With the collapse of central taxation and the rise of manorial head-counts (capitatio, capaticum — poll tax), capitale becomes the merchant's word for funds, stock-in-trade, and the villein's livestock held in capite. Vulgar Latin *capitale undergoes distinctive Gallo-Romance lenition and palatalisation: ca- > cha- in Central French, retained ca- in Norman; intervocalic -p- voices, -t- weakens, unstressed vowels syncopate. Hence Old French capital (learned, chief, principal), chatel (popular Central, goods, livestock), Norman catel (popular Northern, same). Italian keeps learned capitale (principal, capital city). The same Latin word thus yields three French shapes.
  • Middle English borrowing (13th–15th c.): After 1066 England receives Norman catel (property, livestock, money) and later Central chatel, capital. Early forms catel, chatel, capital overlap. By 1400–1500 senses sort: cattle → livestock; chattel → movable goods in law; capital (re-borrowed 13th c. as capital — head, chief, then 16th–17th c. as economic principal) → wealth-fund, chief city, chief letter. Early Modern merchants (Gresham, Mun) theorise capital/stock; 18th-c. economists (Turgot, Smith) make capital/stock technical; 19th-c. Marx (Das Kapital, 1867) and later sociology extend to human, social, cultural capital.
  • Sound and spelling: Classical /ka.piˈtaː.le/ → Vulgar */ka.piˈta.le/ → Old French /tʃaˈtel/ (Central) /kaˈtel/ (Norman) → Middle English /kaˈtel, tʃaˈtel/ → Modern cattle /kætəl/, chattel /tʃætəl/, learned re-borrowing capital /kæpɪtəl/ with restored -p- and Latin spelling. Italianate citation capitale in English etymology contexts is pronounced /ˌkæpɪˈtɑːleɪ/ after Italian.
  • Cultural stakes: The journey from head to capital encodes three revolutions: Roman law making the head the unit of liability and property; medieval pastoral-manorial economy counting wealth on the hoof; modern capitalism making wealth breed wealth like herds breed calves. When economists say capital accumulation, capital flight, decapitalisation, they unknowingly speak herdsman Latin. The source sentence stages this pedigree as a single genealogy lesson: our words ← French ← Latin capitale (property) ← caput (head of cattle).

Attestations are continuous: Plautus and Cicero for capitalis; Digest for capitale as principal; Old French Chanson de Roland and charters for chatel/catel; Chaucer, Langland, Paston Letters for Middle English catel; Smith Wealth of Nations (1776) for stock/capital; Marx for Kapital. No gap, no guess: each stage is documented, each palatalisation regular, each semantic step motivated by counting heads to counting interest.
