A WORD BUILT ON WEEPING The biography of implored begins not in a dictionary but in a cry. Its Latin verb plōrāre meant "to weep, to wail, to lament aloud," and Latin grammarians and modern linguists alike suspect that the word is expressive — an echoic formation that mimes the sound of open weeping, with no secure Indo-European ancestor behind it. Older attempts to attach it to a root connected with rain, Latin pluere, are now rejected; the word stands in the lexicon as a native, sound-imitative Latin coinage. To plorare was to abandon dignity in tears — the weeping of a child, a mourner, a defeated man — and Roman literature is full of it: Plautus' characters plorant and wail, and the word is the plain verb of crying aloud. THE INTENSIVE COMPOUND: TO CALL WITH TEARS On this weeping base Latin built a compound that fixed the word's destiny. The prefix in- (here "in, on, towards") turned "to weep" into implorare, "to call for help with tears" — the act of crying out to someone for aid while weeping. In classical Latin the verb is used exactly where English later uses implore: Cicero implores the help of the gods or of men, the suppliant calls with tears upon a power that can save him. The image in the word is precise: the petitioner does not merely ask, he calls out weeping. It is the verb of the dramatic suppliant, and it is this tearful intensity that implore carried into the languages of Europe. INTO ENGLISH: THE FOURTEENTH-CENTURY BORROWING From Latin the word passed to Old French as implorer, attested in the twelfth century, and from Old French into Middle English in the late fourteenth century — imploren appears in English records around 1387–1400, in the generation of Chaucer. English thus received the word through the French channel, keeping the full classical sense of earnest, tearful entreaty but quickly generalizing it: by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries implore simply means "beg earnestly," with the weeping present in the etymology and, in literary and religious use, in the register. The past participle and preterite implored follow the regular English verb pattern, the native suffix -ed marking past time; the spelling was fixed in the early modern period. LITERARY AND RELIGIOUS CAREER From the sixteenth century implore thrives in elevated narrative, prayer, and poetry. It is the verb of the suppliant in the King James tradition, in Shakespearean and Miltonic verse, and in the great Victorian novels, where characters implore one another for mercy, pardon, and aid. The word's register — formal, earnest, slightly grave — makes it the chosen verb whenever a writer wants the reader to feel the weight of an entreaty, and its fixed phrase "I implore you" still carries that gravity today. In modern English the tearful component has largely faded: to implore is to beg with great earnestness, but without the actual weeping the word once named. POTTER'S SPARROWS In The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902) Potter uses the participle implored at the crisis of the tale: the friendly sparrows, hearing Peter's sobs, fly to him "in great excitement" and implore him to exert himself. The choice is exact. The word is the most solemn available to a children's book, and its ancient core — calling upon a sufferer with tears — mirrors the scene: Peter weeps, and the sparrows' urgent plea answers his weeping. A word whose Latin ancestor meant "to wail" and whose compound meant "to call with tears" arrives, after two millennia and two crossings of the Channel, at the rescue of a small rabbit — a quiet instance of the deep literary history that lives inside the most ordinary narrative verbs.
