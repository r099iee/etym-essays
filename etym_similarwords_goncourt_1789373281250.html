• Flaubert: Similar because Gustave Flaubert and the Goncourt brothers were close friends and central architects of 19th-century French realism who rebelled against bourgeois romanticism; differs because Flaubert sought classical serenity, architectural balance, and absolute authorial impersonality through *le mot juste*, whereas the Goncourts cultivated a feverish, impressionistic *écriture artiste* full of nervous idiosyncrasies, neologisms, and personal diary gossip.
  • Zola: Similar because Émile Zola and the Goncourts championed literary Naturalism, conducting rigorous documentary research on working-class environments and physiological degradation; differs because Zola operated with epic, panoramic broad strokes and a deterministic pseudo-scientific ideology of biological heredity, whereas the Goncourts focused on aristocratic refinement, delicate nervous sensibilities, and exquisite sensory miniatures.
  • Maupassant: Similar because Guy de Maupassant and the Goncourts were master chroniclers of Parisian society, human hypocrisy, and physical pathology; differs because Maupassant wrote with direct, unadorned, crystalline clarity and narrative economy, whereas the Goncourts deployed an intentionally complex, dislocated, and ornamental prose style.
  • Huysmans: Similar because Joris-Karl Huysmans began as a disciple of the Goncourts' nervous naturalism and shared their connoisseurship of rare art, aesthetic decadence, and exquisite sensory refinement; differs because Huysmans evolved beyond secular aestheticism into occultism and deep Catholic mysticism, whereas the Goncourts remained secular 19th-century materialists and aesthetic collectors to the end.
  • Sainte-Beuve: Similar because both Charles-Augustin Sainte-Beuve and the Goncourts were formidable chroniclers and critics of 19th-century French literary personalities; differs because Sainte-Beuve produced formal biographical criticism for major newspapers and academic institutions, whereas the Goncourts recorded scandalous, uninhibited, uncensored dinner-table gossip and intimate psychological vulnerabilities in their private diary.
  • Daudet: Similar because Alphonse Daudet and Edmond de Goncourt were intimate friends, fellow diarists, and founding members of the naturalistic circle; differs because Daudet's fiction was characterized by Mediterranean warmth, humor, and sentimental empathy for the vulnerable, whereas the Goncourts maintained a detached, aristocratic, and often biting cynicism toward human nature.
