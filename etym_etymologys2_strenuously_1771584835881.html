PIE *ster- “stiff, rigid, hard, unbending” (Pokorny *ster-¹; LIV *ster- “to become stiff”; reconstructed) → Greek strēnēs (στρηνής) “harsh, rough, intense” (probable model for the Latin word; Beekes notes this may be Pre-Greek, so the link is debated) → Latin strēnuus “active, brisk, vigorous, prompt” (attested in Plautus and Cicero, 2nd–1st c. BCE; etymology traditionally tied to *ster-, but de Vaan registers the chain as uncertain) → Latin adverb strēnuē “vigorously, briskly” (attested alongside the adjective) → English strenuous (adjective, 1590s, borrowed directly from Latin strēnuus) → English STRENUOUSLY (adverb, formed with the native suffix -ly, c. 1650) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The connection of Latin strēnuus to PIE *ster- “stiff” is traditional but uncertain; de Vaan treats the etymology as unresolved, and Beekes suggests the Greek strēnēs may be Pre-Greek. (2) ANTI-MERGE GUARD: Latin sternere “to spread out” is NOT related to strēnuus; it descends from a different root, PIE *sterh₃- “to strew, spread” (English strew, stratum, Latin strāmen). The two *ster- roots must be kept strictly separate. (3) The -nu- element in strēnuus is unexplained, which is why the chain between Greek strēnēs and Latin strēnuus is recorded as probable rather than certain.
