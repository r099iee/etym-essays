lousy is an informal adjective whose central modern meaning is "very poor, bad, or contemptible", but whose historical core sense is the literal one: "infested with lice". The word is a textbook example of semantic pejoration—a slow, relentless darkening in which a plain biological fact (lice crawling on a body) was first transferred to the human character and then generalized into a vague intensifier of disgust and worthlessness. In a literary context such as "a lousy knave", the word carries a terminal, pathetic weight: it does not merely say that the person is bad, but that he has been reduced to the status of a crawling, parasitic irritant, stripped of honor, dignity, and any claim to respectability.

Sense 1: The literal, entomological meaning. The oldest and etymologically primary sense of lousy is "infested with lice", describing a person, an animal, or a garment crawling with the parasitic insects of the order Phthiraptera. In pre-modern Europe, lice were an almost universal affliction of the poor, the crowded, and the unwashed; to be "lousy" was a plain, observable condition of squalor and neglect. This sense is now rare and chiefly historical or medical, surviving in phrases such as "lousy with lice" and in veterinary and entomological writing. The biological reality behind the word explains everything about its later figurative power: lice are persistent, maddeningly irritating, difficult to eradicate, and strongly associated with the lowest social strata.

Sense 2: The transferred moral meaning: "contemptible, base, mean". Because the condition of being infested was associated with poverty, dirt, and social contempt, the adjective was metaphorically transferred from the body to the character: a "lousy" person was one as repellent, irritating, and lowly as a person crawling with parasites. By the 16th and 17th centuries this sense was fully established—Shakespeare uses "lousy" in exactly this contemptuous way—and it is this sense that operates in the phrase "a lousy knave", where the two terms compound: the man is not merely a dishonest servant-boy (knave) but a creature so degraded as to inspire both pity and acute disgust. The semantic bridge is the universal human revulsion toward vermin: what disgusts the body is transferred to what disgusts the moral sense.

Sense 3: The modern colloquial intensifier: "very bad, inferior, worthless". In contemporary informal English, lousy has become a general-purpose adjective of condemnation: lousy weather, a lousy film, a lousy job, a lousy excuse. Here the biological origin is largely dormant; the word simply means "of poor quality" or "causing dissatisfaction". This is the most frequent modern usage, and it shows the word's final stage of bleaching—the loss of both the literal sense and the specifically moral sense in favor of a broad, emphatic negative.

Sense 4: Feelings and health: "miserable, unwell". When applied to a person's state of body or mind, lousy means "wretched, miserable, sick": I feel lousy = "I feel terrible/unwell". This usage keeps a faint echo of the physical discomfort of infestation—the itching, the malaise—but has become a colloquial synonym of "awful" in the sense of bodily or emotional distress.

Sense 5: The American idiom "lousy with": "abounding in, well supplied with". A curious and important secondary idiom, chiefly American, is lousy with, meaning "full of, swarming with, abounding in" (e.g., lousy with money). The construction is a literal extension of "infested with"—if something is crawling with lice it is full of them—so "lousy with X" came to mean "teeming with X". The connotation is often slightly negative or envious: an abundance that is almost excessive. This shows how a word of disgust can flip into a marker of glutted plenty.

Register and usage. Lousy is firmly informal and colloquial; it is out of place in formal academic or business writing but ubiquitous in speech, fiction, and journalism. It is a mild epithet, rarely considered obscene, though "lousy" as a personal insult retains a sting of class contempt inherited from its history. Derivative forms include the adverb lousily and the noun lousiness.

Synonym spectrum and boundaries.

  • Miserable: Similar because both describe a thoroughly unpleasant state; differs because miserable focuses on internal suffering and sorrow, whereas lousy projects external contempt and contamination.
  • Vile: Similar because both express extreme moral condemnation; differs because vile implies active, malicious corruption, whereas lousy suggests passive, pathetic degradation.
  • Awful: Similar because both mean "very bad"; differs because awful once meant "awe-inspiring" and retains greater emotional force, while lousy is blunter and more physical.
  • Terrible: Similar because both are colloquial intensifiers of badness; differs because terrible evokes fear or dread, while lousy evokes disgust and verminous contempt.
  • Sordid: Similar because both are grounded in filth and degradation; differs because sordid implies mercenary or sleazy motive, while lousy is a more generalized pedestrian badness.
  • Wretched: Similar because both denote a deplorable state; differs because wretched emphasizes pity and misery, while lousy emphasizes active revulsion and worthlessness.
  • Contemptible: Similar because both mean deserving of scorn; differs because contemptible is a formal, reasoned judgement, while lousy is visceral, informal, and physical in origin.
  • Rotten: Similar because both are colloquial negatives derived from decay/vermin; differs because rotten suggests decomposition and can be used of luck or situations, while lousy carries the specific residue of parasitic filth.

Conclusion. The trajectory of lousy is a miniature history of how languages convert physical disgust into moral judgement and finally into vague emphasis: from "full of lice" (14th century) to "contemptible" (16th century) to "very bad" (19th–20th centuries), with the literal sense lingering at the bottom like a fossil. In the context of the disgraced general, the word must be read in its fullest historical depth: it condemns him not merely as a bad man but as a man whose degradation has made him an irritant to the social fabric, a creature stripped of every claim to honor.
