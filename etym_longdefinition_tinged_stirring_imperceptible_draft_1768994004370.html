The four words of this card work as a single sensory chain in the passage: tinged mixes the color, stirring starts the motion, imperceptible sets the scale, and draft delivers the physical effect — "they felt the stirring of an almost imperceptible draft upon their faces." Each word is tuned to the very threshold of sensation, and together they describe a darkness and a breeze so faint that the adventurers can barely credit their own senses. The cumulative tone is one of delicate, almost supernatural precision: not a wind but the stirring of a wind, not darkness but air "tinged" with a "slight and doubtful darkness," and not a breath the men can swear to but one imperceptible enough to be doubted.

  • tinged — To be tinged is to be stained with a small, diffused amount of something: a coloring that changes the whole without being dominant. The word implies the dyeing process itself — something has been dipped or soaked — and it carries a faint suggestion of contamination, which is why it can be used of moral or emotional coloring ("tinged with regret," "tinged with irony"). Here the air is "tinged with a slight and doubtful darkness": the darkness is not solid or total but mixed into the atmosphere like pigment in water. Tint, shade, tone, and hue are near neighbours: a tint is a lightened, often pastel amount of a color; a shade darkens toward black; a tone is an adjustment of brightness or warmth; a hue is the bare name of a color. Tinged differs from all of them in preserving the image of an applied stain and in being the word for the smallest, most diffused addition.
  • stirring — As a noun, a stirring is a beginning of motion, the first faint movement out of stillness — a tremor, a ripple, an awakening. The word reaches from physical motion ("the stirring of leaves") into emotional arousal ("a stirring of hope," "a stirring speech"), and in this passage it sits precisely at the threshold: "they felt the stirring of an almost imperceptible draft" — not a wind but the beginning of a wind, the very first tremor of moving air, before anything can yet be called a breeze.
  • imperceptible — Impossible to notice, detect, or grasp with the senses: too slight, slow, or gradual to be perceived. The word is formal and analytical, a favourite of philosophy and science for changes too fine for observation ("imperceptible gradations," "an imperceptible difference"). It differs from invisible in reaching across all the senses — an imperceptible sound, an imperceptible smell, an imperceptible movement — and in implying a judgment of degree, not of sight alone. Here it sets the scale: the draft is almost imperceptible, at the very edge of what the skin can register.
  • draft — A draft (chiefly British draught) is a current of air, especially a narrow, steady, inward one — the cold seam of air that finds its way into a warm room, or the breath of moving air on the skin. The word is also a magnificent polyseme: it names the act of drawing or pulling; a preliminary version of a document ("a first draft"); a portion of liquid drawn from a barrel ("draft beer"); the depth of water a ship needs to float ("the ship draws nine feet of draft"); conscription ("the military draft"); and a note ordering payment ("a banker's draft"). All these senses hang together on the core image of drawing — air drawn in, words drawn up, liquid drawn off, men drawn into service. In this passage it is the air-sense, the "small current of air" that the skin feels, that delivers the physical close of the sensory chain.

In collocation and register: tinged attaches to with + a color or feeling and is literary-delicate; stirring attaches to of + an abstract or physical object and is poetic-to-formal; imperceptible attaches to almost, nearly, or virtually and is formal-analytical; draft (as air) is the concrete, bodily word, the only one of the four that the skin itself can verify. Together they dramatize a single event of perception: a change so faint in its coloring, so early in its motion, so fine in its scale, and yet so physically present that the men feel it on their faces.
