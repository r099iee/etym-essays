The story of recesses is the story of a word that began as a motion — a going back, a withdrawal, a retreat — and ended as a place, and then as a state of mind. Latin recedere “to go back” produced the noun recessus “a going back, a secret place,” and from that noun English built a family whose members now range from the niche in a cave wall to the school playground and the hidden recesses of memory.

The Indo-European root of “going” and “yielding.” The oldest stage is the reconstructed Indo-European root *ked-, “to go, to yield, to give way.” The root is slender in its attestation — its clearest reflexes are in Latin and Greek — but it is widely accepted: Greek khézein “to go, to ease oneself” (a euphemistic descendant) and Latin cēdere “to go, to move, to yield, to give way” both point to a verb of motion and concession. Latin cēdere became one of the great root-words of English vocabulary, supplying cede, concede, recede, proceed, succeed, secede, exceed, and antecede — and in Roman legal and military usage it carried a precise sense: to cedere was to withdraw, to yield ground, to retire from a position. This is the semantic seed of everything that follows.

Latin compounds: the “going back” becomes a place. To cēdere the Romans prefixed re-, “back,” making recēdere, “to go back, to retire, to withdraw,” attested in Cicero and Caesar — a military and political verb of retreating and retiring. The past participle recessus (“gone back, withdrawn”) was then used as a noun, and the semantic shift is the crucial event of the whole history: “a going back” became “a place to which one goes back” — a retreat, a retired and private corner, a secret and hidden spot. Roman authors exploited this from the start: Pliny the Elder uses recessus for the hidden recesses of nature, and Latin prose loves the phrase in intimis recessibus, “in the innermost retreats,” already foreshadowing the English “recesses of the heart.”

Into English: the seventeenth century. English borrowed recess directly from Latin in the early seventeenth century (first attested 1617), and for a century the word carried the full Roman range: “a going back or withdrawal,” “a cessation or suspension,” and “a secret, retired, or hollow place.” The age’s religious and psychological prose seized on the figurative sense: writers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — Milton among them — spoke of “the recesses of the heart,” “the recesses of the soul,” “the deep recesses of the mind,” where secret thoughts and motives lodge, exactly as Latin had spoken of the recessus animi, the retreats of the mind. The metaphor rests on the same transfer as the Roman one: depth in space becomes depth in the self.

The senses divide: architecture, institutions, psychology. From the later seventeenth century the family split into branches. The physical sense specialised in architecture: a recess became the technical word for a niche, alcove, or set-back in a wall — the “columnated recesses” of the cave passage belong here. The institutional senses developed from the “cessation, withdrawal” meaning: a recess became the period when a court or parliament withdraws from sitting (parliamentary recess, judicial recess), and in American usage the school recess — the break in which children withdraw from lessons — was established in the nineteenth century. The figurative psychological sense meanwhile remained permanently current: “the recesses of memory,” “the innermost recesses of the self,” survive as everyday idiom, and twentieth-century psychology found the metaphor natural for the unconscious.

The plural and the Gothic atmosphere. The plural recesses has a special power that the singular lacks. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Gothic and Romantic prose — Radcliffe, Poe, and their imitators — made “the recesses of caves, forests, and ruined abbeys” a standard engine of dread: the plural multiplies hiddenness, each recess a void into which eye and light cannot reach, where anything might be concealed. In the cave passage this Gothic inheritance is exactly what is at work: the “columnated recesses which the flashed rays of the torches could not always fathom” are not architectural decoration but generators of the uncanny, places where the light fails and the imagination fills the gap.

Modern state. Today recesses runs the full inherited range. It remains the architectural term for a wall set-back (hence “recessed lighting”); it names the innermost, least accessible parts of places (“the recesses of the building”); it is the everyday metaphor for the hidden depths of the mind; and its institutional cousin recess names the break in schools, courts, and legislatures. The word thus spans a retreat in Roman warfare, a niche in a cave wall, a school playground, and the deepest reaches of memory — a single chain of meaning from “going back” to “the places where we go back to hide.”

  • Sound changes: The word enters English as a learned borrowing, so its history is morphological and semantic rather than phonetic; Latin recessus → English recess is a direct loan (16th–17th c.) preserving the Latin form.
  • Key attestations: Latin cēdere (Plautus, 2nd c. BCE); recēdere and recessus (Cicero, Caesar, Pliny the Elder, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE); English recess (1617); Milton’s “recesses of the heart” (17th c.); Gothic novelists’ “recesses of caves and abbeys” (18th–19th c.); school recess in American usage (19th c.); “recessed lighting” (20th c.).
  • Debated points: The PIE root *ked- is widely reconstructed but rests on few cognates (chiefly Latin and Greek), and some handbooks (de Vaan) treat Latin cēdere as of uncertain Indo-European derivation; the root should not be asserted with false confidence.
