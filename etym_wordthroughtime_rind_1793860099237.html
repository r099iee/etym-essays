- Old English (c. 750–1100) - Recorded as rind / rinde, meaning the natural bark of a tree or the outer crust of bread; perceived as an essential protective boundary against weather, fire, and decay.
- Middle English (c. 1150–1450) - Retained the primary meaning of tree bark while expanding naturally to the tough skin of cheeses, melons, and salted pork; used frequently in domestic husbandry and agricultural handbooks.
- Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700) - Citrus fruits imported from the Mediterranean led to rind being widely used for lemon and orange peel in apothecary receipts and cooking; metaphorical usage for a person's outer appearance appeared in Elizabethan drama.
- 18th to 19th Century - Tree bark usages became increasingly poetic and literary (as preserved by Romantic poets and later J.R.R. Tolkien), while culinary literature codified cheese rinds and charcuterie rinds.
- 20th to 21st Century - Commercial snack food popularized "pork rinds"; mixology codified citrus rinds as standard cocktail garnishes, while glaciology adopted "ice rind" for brittle marine ice crusts.
