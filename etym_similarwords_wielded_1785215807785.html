• exerted: Similar because both mean “to put power, force, or influence into effect”; differs because exert stresses the application of effort or pressure and lacks the image of manual mastery — one exerts pressure, one wields a sword.
  • exercised: Similar because both mean “to bring a faculty, right, or power into use”; differs because exercise suggests habitual, systematic, or legitimate use (exercise a veto, exercise restraint) without the energetic, martial physicality of wield.
  • brandished: Similar because both involve holding a weapon in the hand; differs because brandish means to wave threateningly or showily and does not imply effective use — a coward can brandish a sword, but only a master wields it.
  • flourished: Similar because both can describe holding a weapon aloft; differs because flourish emphasises showy, ostentatious display rather than functional control.
  • commanded: Similar because both relate to the exercise of authority; differs because command focuses on giving orders to others, while wield focuses on the effective handling of the instrument of power itself (wield the sceptre, command the army).
  • handled: Similar because both mean “to hold and manage a tool or object with the hand”; differs because handle is neutral and everyday, carrying no implication of mastery, force, or figurative power.
  • manipulated: Similar because both involve controlling something or someone with effect; differs because manipulate now usually carries negative connotations of deceit or sly management, whereas wield is open, forceful, and often admirable.
  • controlled: Similar because both denote directing something effectively; differs because control is broad and impersonal (control a process, control a crowd), while wield keeps the vivid image of a weapon or tool held and used by a skilled hand.
  • plied: Similar because both describe working a tool with the hands; differs because ply suggests steady, repetitive industry (ply a needle, ply a trade) rather than the dominant mastery and figurative force of wield.
  • used: Similar because both denote employing something; differs because use is the plainest, most general verb, with no connotation of skill, force, or authority.
  • possessed: Similar because both can describe holding power or a quality; differs because possess means to own or have, with no implication of active, effective handling — one can possess power and never wield it.
  • swayed: Similar because both can describe the influence one person has over another; differs because sway describes the effect felt by the influenced party (her words swayed him), while wield describes the active exercise of influence by the influencing party.
