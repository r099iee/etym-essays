touchstone denotes, literally and figuratively, the standard by which things are tested. Literally it is a hard black stone — fine-grained jasper, basalt, or lydite — against which gold and silver were rubbed to test their purity: the streak left on the dark surface, compared with streaks of known alloys, revealed fineness within seconds, without fire or acid. Figuratively, and now overwhelmingly, it means any reliable criterion, litmus test, or benchmark: “clarity as an all-purpose touchstone” means clarity treated as the universal assay by which all prose, perception, or thought is rubbed and judged. The metaphor preserves the jeweller’s confidence: as the stone does not lie about gold, so the true touchstone does not lie about quality.

Usage is almost always singular with the indefinite or definite article — a touchstone, the touchstone — followed by of or for: “a touchstone of good teaching,” “the touchstone for authenticity.” It belongs to evaluative, often polemical prose: to nominate a touchstone is to claim authority over judgment, which is why the source sentence’s “all-purpose touchstone” carries gentle scepticism — no single stone, the author implies, can assay everything. Adjectival use (“touchstone lines,” “touchstone films”) follows Matthew Arnold, who famously proposed passages of Homer, Dante, and Milton as “touchstones” of great poetry, turning the assayer’s tool into a portable anthology.

Senses and extensions:

  • Assay stone (literal, now rare): the black siliceous stone itself, still sold to goldsmiths. Bridge: the origin; every other sense is metaphor from this workshop practice.
  • Criterion or test (dominant figurative): “time is the touchstone of friendship.” Bridge: functional analogy — as metal is proved on stone, claims are proved on principle.
  • Exemplary model (Arnoldian): a work or passage embodying excellence against which others are measured. Bridge: inversion — the standard is not the stone but the purest gold kept beside it.
  • Responsive revealer (literary): a character or situation that reveals others’ true nature on contact. Bridge: person as stone; e.g., a plain-spoken child as the family’s touchstone.

Connotation is sober and approving: touchstones are trusted, no-nonsense, anti-fashion. To call something a touchstone praises its reliability; to call it a false or all-purpose touchstone accuses it of overreach, as here with “clarity.” Common collocates — true, reliable, ultimate, critical, cultural, moral, aesthetic, remain, serve as, provide, use as, apply — confirm its judicial role. It differs from benchmark (more quantitative), litmus test (more binary and chemical), gold standard (more economic and superlative), and yardstick (more mensurational): a touchstone tests genuineness by contact, not size by comparison. Mastering it lets a writer compress an entire philosophy of judgment — rub, compare streaks, know at once — into a single compact noun.

For the examination candidate, touchstone is also a strategic synonym upgrade: where weaker writers repeat measure or test, the precise candidate deploys touchstone when genuineness rather than quantity is at issue — the touchstone of a translation is not its length but its fidelity, the touchstone of an apology not its eloquence but its change of conduct. Used sparingly and with its prepositional frame intact (touchstone of/for), it lends an air of seasoned judgment; used promiscuously for every minor preference, it collapses into the very all-purpose vagueness the source sentence criticizes.
