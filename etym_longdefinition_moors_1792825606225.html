Moors, the plural of moor, names in its living everyday sense broad tracts of open and almost treeless high ground — rolling windswept country clothed in purple heather, bilberry, coarse bent grass, bracken, and black spongy peat, rained on from one year's end to the next and grazed by little except sheep, red grouse, and curlews.

In the source sentence the word carries exactly this landscape sense: something bigger than a tree glimpsed up away beyond the North ______, the wild empty uplands north of the Shire where strange things may be seen and half believed. The capital letter belongs to the place name, the North Moors as a region, but the common noun beneath it is the ordinary English word for bleak high wasteland, and Tolkien the philologist chose it precisely for its ancient northern chill.

Core meaning in detail: A moor in Britain is not a mountain and not a field but the land in between ambition and use — too high, too wet, too acid, or too exposed for the plough, so it stays unenclosed and uncultivated, crossed by packhorse tracks and peat cuttings rather than roads. Ecologically it divides into heather moor, kept purple by burning for grouse, and blanket bog, where sphagnum moss has laid down peat ten feet deep over thousands of years, preserving bog bodies, pollen, and the odd crashed aircraft with equal indifference. Walkers know the moor by its signs: cotton grass nodding white over black water, the sudden suck of a bog hole, mist that comes down in minutes, and a silence so complete that a curlew's cry sounds cracked and close.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Open upland wasteland (primary modern sense): the Yorkshire moors of Emily Brontë, Dartmoor, Exmoor, the North York Moors — high exposed country of heather and peat. This is the sense every modern reader reaches for first, and the sense of the source sentence.
  • Low marshy waste or fen (earliest Old English sense, now archaic): the first Old English mōr meant boggy low ground as readily as high ground — any tract of waste, wet or dry. The bridge is infertility itself: high dry heath and low wet fen were one thing to early farmers, namely land the plough could not take, and only later centuries split the meanings between moor for the heights and marsh or fen for the lowlands.
  • A Moor, member of the North African Muslim peoples (homograph with a wholly different root): the medieval Moor — Othello is its most famous bearer — comes through Latin Maurus from Greek Mauros, an inhabitant of Mauretania, and has no kinship at all with the heathland word. The bridge here is pure spelling accident: two rivers of history arriving at one set of letters. Capitalisation and context part them — the North Moors are hills, the Moors of history are people — and the learner must keep them apart.
  • To moor a boat, to make it fast (verb, unrelated homograph): to moor — tie up, anchor, secure — descends from a different Germanic root to do with fixing and fastening, cousin to Dutch meren. The bridge is again coincidence of sound, though poets have had fun joining them, picturing ships moored beside moors. Grammar parts them at once: one is nearly always noun and plural in landscape writing, the other a working verb of harbours.
  • Place name element across northern England (fossilised sense): in names like Blackamoor, Hexhamshire Moors, and the North York Moors National Park, the word is frozen into geography, marking country that resisted enclosure when the valleys were tamed. The bridge is administrative memory: what the commons once called waste, the map now calls heritage, and grouse shooting, sheepwalk, and national park signage keep the old noun alive as boundary and brand.

Register and connotation: moors is a romantic and slightly forbidding word — Wuthering Heights weather, hounds the size of ponies, escaped convicts on Dartmoor. It promises vastness, exposure, and a past that has not been tidied away, from Bronze Age stone circles half sunk in peat to the military ranges still fenced off today. Nobody calls a pretty meadow a moor; the word keeps its northern cold even when the sun is out, and that cold is exactly why hobbit gossip sets its tree-sized marvel beyond them, where the familiar Shire ends and the old wild begins.
