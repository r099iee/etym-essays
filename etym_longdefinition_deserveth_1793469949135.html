Deserveth is the archaic third-person singular present tense indicative form of the verb deserve (equivalent to modern deserves), formed with the historic southern English verbal suffix -eth. Signifying to be worthy of, to merit, or to possess a rightful title to recompense, honor, blame, or consequence by virtue of one's actions, qualities, or intrinsic character, the word carries deep etymological roots in the ancient ethics of service and social obligation. Descended from the Latin compound verb dēservīre—formed by combining the intensive prefix dē- (‘completely, thoroughly’) with servīre (‘to serve’)—the verb originally pictured an individual who serves faithfully and zealously. Through centuries of legal, feudal, and moral application in Late Latin and Old French, this physical reality of devoted service transformed into an abstract principle of justice: the idea that faithful service establishes a legitimate moral claim to reward, rendering the servant ‘deserving’ of what they receive.

In the source text—from Samuel Johnson’s incisive essay on literary emulation in The Rambler (No. 154): “And for this cause, many times that which deserveth admiration would hardly be able to find favor, if they which propose it were not content to profess themselves therein scholars and followers of the ancient,” the verb functions as an objective moral verdict. Johnson is arguing that genuine modern genius often produces work that inherently merits the highest praise, yet societal prejudice in favor of antiquity is so stubborn that even that which objectively deserveth admiration cannot secure public favor on its own merits, forcing innovators to masquerade as mere disciples of classical predecessors.

Exhaustive exploration of polysemy and semantic applications:

  • Merits reward, honor, or positive recognition (primary affirmative sense): To have earned a rightful claim to praise, gratitude, promotion, or glory through inherent excellence, labor, or virtuous conduct. The semantic bridge connects the historical reality of completing thorough service with the resulting moral obligation of the master or society to bestow appropriate honor.
  • Merits punishment, blame, or retribution (adversive/punitive sense): To have incurred rightful condemnation or suffering as the just consequence of wrongdoing or negligence (e.g., ‘he that committeth treason deserveth death’). The bridge extends the concept of earned desert from positive wages to retributive justice: evil deeds earn evil consequences under moral law.
  • Warrants attention, examination, or consideration (evaluative/intellectual sense): Describing an idea, argument, book, or phenomenon that possesses sufficient importance, originality, or weight to demand serious scholarly contemplation (e.g., ‘a hypothesis that deserveth closer scrutiny’). The bridge shifts desert from moral agents to intellectual objects that ‘earn’ the intellect’s focus.
  • Passive entitlement or fittingness (philosophical/legal sense): In moral philosophy and jurisprudence, desert signifies that which is due to an individual according to justice, equity, or natural law, independent of whether it is actually granted by human institutions. Johnson’s usage highlights this tragic gap: a work may truly deserve admiration even while corrupt critics withhold it.
  • Ironic or colloquial inevitability (pragmatic sense): In everyday rhetorical usage, describing outcomes that befall someone as the natural, predictable outcome of their own folly (e.g., ‘he got exactly what he deserveth’). The bridge treats natural consequences as a form of cosmic retribution.

Register, rhetorical structure, and stylistic weight: The verbal inflection -eth belongs to the solemn register of Tudor, Jacobean, and Augustan prose. In Samuel Johnson’s balanced, periodic sentences, deserveth provides rhythmic poise and gravitas, transforming an ordinary statement of merit into a timeless maxim of moral philosophy. It sounds authoritative and judicial, appealing not merely to temporary subjective taste, but to an enduring, objective standard of justice that governs human worth across all generations.
