Idomeneus (Greek Ἰδομενεύς) is the Homeric king of Crete, son of Deucalion, grandson of Minos, co-leader with Meriones of the eighty Cretan ships at Troy. Unlike Tydides, which is a transparent patronymic, Idomeneus is an opaque personal name whose prehistory is contested. The English word is a straight borrowing with almost no phonetic change from Greek through Latin, and its history is therefore a history of literary transmission rather than sound shifts.

The name is already fully formed in the Iliad, where Idomeneus is among the oldest but still vigorous kings, famed for steadiness. Linear B tablets from Knossos attest many Cretan names in -eus, suggesting the form belongs to genuine Bronze-Age onomastics, not Homeric invention. Modern linguistics, notably Beekes, treats the whole -eus class as likely Pre-Greek: the Aegean's earlier non-Indo-European language left a residue of names with characteristic suffixes -eus, -nth-, -ss- (compare Odysseus, Corinth, Parnassus). If so, to seek a PIE root for Idomeneus is a category error; the name predates Greek. Older handbooks proposed Ido-men-eus as he who has the strength of Ida (Mount Ida, Crete's sacred mountain) or from id- "to see" + menos "might", but these segmentations violate known Greek word-formation and find no parallel. The responsible statement is therefore: earliest securely attested form is Greek Idomeneus with meaning "Cretan king"; earlier derivation unknown and probably substrate.

  • Homeric stabilisation: In the Iliad the name scans Īdŏmĕneus with variable first quantity for metre. Formulae such as Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans and Idomeneus, spear-famed fix the name in oral memory. Book 13 gives him an aristeia second only to Diomedes in tactical detail, slaying Othryoneus, Asius, Alcathous, and others. This is the twenty-to-one impression Ruskin's boyhood retained.
  • Post-Homeric myth: The Odyssey (3.191) brings him safely home; later mythographers add the storm-vow legend — he promises Poseidon to sacrifice the first living thing he meets if saved at sea, meets his son, and brings plague and exile. Virgil (Aeneid 3, 11) makes him founder of Salentum in Italy; Diodorus, Apollodorus, and Hyginus repeat the Cretan genealogy. Through these Latin intermediaries the spelling Idomeneus with -eus becomes canonical in the West.
  • French and English borrowing: Renaissance French Hellenists (Amyot) write Idomenée; English Chapman's Iliads (1616) uses Idomeneus, Pope (1715) regularises it. Pronunciation in English follows Latin school rules: I-do-MEN-yoos. Eighteenth-century dictionaries list him among heathen kings with no moralising; nineteenth-century school editions present him as model of aged vigour — the old king who still fights in the front rank, exactly the Ruskinian point that kings work hardest.
  • Mozart and after: The name gains a second life through Mozart's opera Idomeneo, re di Creta (1781, libretto after Danchet), based on Fénelon's Télémaque. Thereafter Idomeneo/Idomeneus is as much an operatic as a Homeric signifier. Classical scholars continue to debate Cretan historicity — was there a Late Bronze Age Idomeneus? — but agree the literary figure is stable.
  • Ruskin context: Ruskin pairs Tydides or Idomeneus as a doublet: the young Argive slayer and the old Cretan slayer both out-kill ordinary men twentyfold. The pairing is Homerically apt: both are celebrated in battle-books for steady slaughter, both are kings, both take less spoil and do more work — the moral Ruskin extracts. The footnote 4 in the source refers to his boyhood reading list (Pope and Scott).

Thus Idomeneus travels unchanged from Bronze-Age-sounding Greek through Latin into modern English, its ultimate root hidden by substrate history, its meaning constant: the Cretan king at Troy.
