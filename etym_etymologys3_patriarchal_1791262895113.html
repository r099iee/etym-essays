The adjective patriarchal is built in English on the noun patriarch plus the adjectival suffix -al, but its roots reach deep into Indo-European kinship and Greek political vocabulary. It fuses two PIE elements: *peter- (father) and *arkh- (to begin, rule). The first gives English father, paternal, patrimony; the second gives arch, monarch, hierarchy, anarchy. Their Greek compound patriarkhes meant head of a lineage or tribe — literally the father who rules the beginning.

In Classical Greek the word was secular and administrative: heads of gene or tribes. With the Septuagint and New Testament it acquired sacred weight, denoting Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, the twelve sons, and later bishops of the great sees — Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, Constantinople — titled Patriarchs. Late Latin patriarcha carried both senses into the Western Church. Old French patriarche and Middle English patriarch at first meant almost exclusively the biblical fathers, venerable bearded founders painted in manuscripts.

  • Political appropriation (17th c.): Sir Robert Filmer's posthumous Patriarcha (1680) argued kings inherit Adam's fatherly authority; patriarchal became a theory of absolute monarchy. Locke's First Treatise demolished it, but the adjective survived with a political charge it never lost.
  • Anthropological stage (19th c.): Maine's Ancient Law (1861), Morgan, McLennan, and Engels made patriarchal family a universal evolutionary stage — from promiscuity to matriarchy to patriarchy to monogamy — tied to the rise of private property, exactly the thesis echoed in the source sentence. The suffix -al now marked a system, not a person.
  • Sociological neutralization (early 20th c.): Weber's patriarchalism as a type of traditional authority — the master ruling by personal loyalty and precedent — gave the term dry academic respectability alongside charismatic and legal-rational authority.
  • Feminist reactivation (1960s–present): Kate Millett's Sexual Politics (1970), Gerda Lerner, and Sylvia Walby redefined patriarchy as the overarching system of men's oppression of women across production, reproduction, sexuality, and culture. Patriarchal shifted from descriptive kinship to critical indictment, exploding in frequency and acquiring its modern pejorative force.

Phonologically the path is conservative: Greek patriarkhes loses its Greek ending in Latin, keeps stress on the second syllable in French and English, and adds the Romance adjectival -alis > -al. The spelling ch preserves the Greek kappa transliterated through Latin. Semantically the drift is from revered founding father, to father-king, to father-ruled family system, to male-dominated society as such. Each layer persists: a Christmas sermon still invokes the patriarchs reverently while a sociology seminar denounces the patriarchal pay gap — the same compound, opposite affects, complete historical arc intact.
