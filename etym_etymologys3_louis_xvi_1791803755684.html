A two-part Germanic name. Louis is the French descendant of a Frankish compound, *Hlūdawīg, built from *hlūdaz, “heard of, famous, loud,” and *wīgą, “battle.” The name therefore means “famous in battle” or “renowned warrior”—a fitting name for a warrior aristocracy, and a strange one for a king executed by his own subjects. The first element descends from Indo-European *ḱlew-, “to hear,” which also gives Greek kleos, “fame,” and English loud; the second belongs to the Germanic battle vocabulary and is sometimes linked with Latin vincere, “to conquer,” though the connection is not secure.

Clovis and Louis. The name entered Latin in two different shapes. The Merovingian king Chlodovech, who died in 511 and is known in French as Clovis, was Latinised as Chlodovechus. The Carolingians produced a second Latinisation, Hludovicus or Ludovicus, for Louis the Pious, son of Charlemagne; this is the form that passed into Old French as Looïs and then Louis. Clovis and Louis are thus the same Germanic name in two Latin disguises, a fact that French historical writing has often exploited: to call the king Louis Capet at his trial was to invoke Hugues Capet, the dynasty's founder, while the name Clovis invoked the first Christian king of the Franks.

The dynastic name. No other name in French history carries so much weight. Louis IX, canonised in 1297, gave the name a permanent association with justice and piety; the Bourbons used it repeatedly; and by the eighteenth century Louis was less a personal name than an institution. Cognates spread across Europe: Ludwig in German, Lodewijk in Dutch, Luigi in Italian, Luis in Spanish, Lewis in English. The French form loses the initial Germanic h- and the final -s is silent, which is why English says /ˈluː.i/ rather than “Lewis” for the French kings.

The numeral. XVI is a Roman numeral: ten plus five plus one. Roman numerals are additive and subtractive, and they were the ordinary way of writing numbers in Latin Europe until the spread of Arabic digits; they survive on clock faces, monuments and regnal titles precisely because they look formal and antique. The number sixteen itself is Latin sedecim, a contraction of sex “six” and decem “ten”; the ordinal sextus decimus supplies English “sixteenth.” French seize descends from the same sedecim, and French royal usage reads the numeral as a cardinal—Louis seize, just as Louis XIV is Louis quatorze—while English converts it into an ordinal with the definite article: Louis the Sixteenth.

Why a number at all. Regnal numbers became standard in later medieval and early modern Europe as a bureaucratic convenience. A king's documents needed to distinguish him from his namesakes, and the numeral did the work of a surname for a family that did not use one. Louis XVI therefore encodes a whole dynastic series in two characters: it asserts that there were fifteen previous kings of that name and that this one stands in their line. Revolutionaries understood the force of that assertion, which is why the Convention called him Louis Capet instead.

The compound as a historical name. Louis XVI denotes Louis-Auguste, born in 1754, king from 1774, deposed in 1792 and executed on 21 January 1793. Around the name cluster the great events of the Revolution: the Estates-General, the fall of the Bastille, the flight to Varennes, the storming of the Tuileries, the trial, the guillotine. The name also became a period label: Louis XVI style denotes the neoclassical furniture and interiors of his reign, with straight tapering legs, fluting, mahogany and classical motifs, a style that continued to be made after 1793 and is still catalogued under his name in auction houses. In English the compound is normally pronounced with the ordinal, and “Louis XVI” is one of the very few regnal names that an educated general reader can be expected to place immediately—alongside Elizabeth I and Henry VIII—because the execution made it a date in world history.

  • Name type: personal name + regnal numeral, a title rather than a surname.
  • Component meanings: “famous in battle” + “sixteenth.”
  • French vs English: Louis seize (cardinal) against Louis the Sixteenth (ordinal).
  • Political counter-name: Louis Capet, used at the trial to deny legitimacy.
  • Secondary modern sense: a neoclassical decorative style, morally neutral and commercially current.
