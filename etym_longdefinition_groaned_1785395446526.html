GROANED, simple past tense and past participle of the verb 'groan': to utter a deep, low, prolonged sound expressing physical pain, grief, exhaustion, or despair — or, of things, to make such a sound under weight or strain. As used of Bilbo — 'I am dreadfully hungry,' groaned Bilbo — the verb conveys an involuntary, half-verbal protest: hunger and fatigue well up into a guttural sound before any sentence is formed, a noise of the body rather than a speech of the mind. The nuance is of heavy, forced release of breath through the throat, deeper and harsher than a sigh, weightier than a moan; in narrative it often marks the moment a character's endurance breaks. Tolkien stages the scene for maximum effect: the party has just emerged from the goblin tunnels, disoriented and famished after days without food, and Bilbo's groan is the first raw sound of the body after miles of terror — the word lets us hear the collapse of the fastidious hobbit into the exhausted creature, hunger overriding speech before good manners can intervene. The verb's range is wider than mere pain. It covers every heavy, sustained, low-pitched sound of strain or protest:
  • Bodily distress — a groan of pain, exhaustion, relief, or despair; the involuntary sound of a body under pressure, as of a starving hobbit or a man in labour.
  • Things under load — floors and stairs 'groan under' a weight; a table 'groans with' food; timbers and beams groan as they take strain; the verb transfers the sound of the body to the object bearing weight.
  • Crowds and audiences — a crowd groans with disappointment; an audience groans at a bad joke; the collective voiced sigh of shared feeling.
  • Figurative and internal — to 'groan internally' is to suffer or object silently; newspapers groan at rising prices; the sense of heavy, unwilling endurance without any audible sound.
  • Comic and theatrical register — since the 18th century, the exaggerated groan of the put-upon has been a staple of comedy: groaning at puns, groaning theatrically at inconvenience.
  • Noun use — 'a groan' is the sound itself, and by extension a complaint or lament ('the groans of the defeated').
The word must be separated from its neighbours. 'Moaned' is softer, more continuous, plaintive or self-pitying, and usually sustained on one note; 'sighed' is a quiet breathy exhalation of weariness or relief without a vocalised throaty sound; 'grunted' is short, staccato, and effortful rather than sorrowful; 'lamented' is articulate, formalised mourning. 'Groan' carries the greatest physical weight of the set: its sound is low in pitch, involuntary, and intimately tied to bodily strain — which makes it the natural verb both for a starving hobbit and for wood bending under load. In register it is vivid, onomatopoeic, and immediate, at home in narrative fiction, journalism, and everyday speech alike, and its -ed past is the standard weak form used for both the preterite and the participle (a groaning table; the table groaned).
