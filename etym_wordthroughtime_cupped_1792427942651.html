- [Late 14th–15th c. (Late Middle English)] — emerged in medieval surgery and therapeutics from the noun *cuppe*, denoting the medical procedure of applying heated glass or horn cups (*cupping*) to the flesh to draw blood or morbid humors to the surface — [perception: regarded as an indispensable, painful, yet cleansing surgical therapy]
- [16th–17th c. (Early Modern English)] — transitioned from surgical bloodletting into bodily mechanics and gestural description, denoting the deliberate hollowing of human hands into a concave basin to scoop drinking water, gather offerings, or hold delicate objects — [perception: recognized as an intuitive, primal human gesture of gathering, drinking, and physical sheltering]
- [18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Industrial Era)] — broadened into mechanical engineering, carpentry, botany, and sports, designating concave physical warpage in cured timber boards, cup-shaped botanical corollas, and the sunken socket of a golf hole — [perception: utilized as an exacting technical descriptor for curved, hollowed geometric surfaces and structural defects]
- [Late 19th–20th c. (Modern Literary & Affective Usage)] — flourished in descriptive fiction, poetry, and cinema as an intimate affective verb and adjective, portraying tender gestures of devotion, romantic affection, and protective sheltering (e.g., hands cupped around a match against the wind, or tenderly cupping a loved one's cheeks) — [perception: deeply associated with warmth, tenderness, intimacy, vulnerability, and gentle physical solace]
- [21st c. (Contemporary Multidisciplinary Usage)] — widely maintained across sports safety equipment (athletic protective cups), competitive swim strokes, therapeutic wellness resurgence (traditional suction cupping popularized by Olympic athletes), and devotional descriptions of sacred prayer gestures — [perception: understood as both an exacting morphological descriptor and an evocative symbol of protective containment]
