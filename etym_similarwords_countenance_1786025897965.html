• Tolerate: Similar because both mean allowing something to happen; differs because tolerate implies enduring without objection, whereas countenance implies tacit approval or endorsement — you do not just put up with it, you let your silence support it.
  • Permit: Similar because both grant allowance; differs because permit is a formal, often official or legal authorization, while countenance is a moral or behavioural acceptance shown by attitude rather than by rule.
  • Endorse: Similar because both express approval; differs because endorse is active and explicit (publicly backing something), whereas countenance is usually passive allowance — approving by failing to object.
  • Sanction: Similar because both give approval; differs because sanction can be official, explicit authorization (or even a penalty), while countenance rarely has official, institutional force.
  • Approve: Similar because both involve favourable judgement; differs because approve can be a purely internal judgement, while countenance always implies that the approval has practical effect — the thing is allowed to go forward.
  • Condone: Similar because both mean overlooking wrong; differs because condone implies forgiveness or excusing after the fact, while countenance implies permission or at least non-objection at the time.
  • Face (noun): Similar because countenance literally means the face; differs because face is the plain anatomical term, while countenance adds expression, composure, and (in extension) approval.
  • Visage: Similar because both name the face; differs because visage is archaic and grander and refers to the features alone, whereas countenance ties the face to inner composure and to sanction.
  • Mien: Similar because both describe a person's outward bearing; differs because mien stresses carriage and demeanour generally, while countenance points specifically at the face and its expressions.
  • Composure: Similar because countenance once meant composure (and still does in "keep one's countenance"); differs because composure names the inner calm itself, while countenance names the visible face through which calm shows.
