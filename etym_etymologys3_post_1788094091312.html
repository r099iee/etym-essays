Three strangers in one word. English post is not one word but three: the doorpost (Latin postis), the relay-station word that became the postal system (Latin posita, "placed"), and the Latin preposition post "after" hiding in the prefix post-. Their convergence is accidental — a collision of spellings that English has learned to sort by context. This essay follows the three genealogies and then shows how they came to share one four-letter home.

I. The doorpost: Latin postis. Latin postis "doorpost, post" (attested from the first century BCE) is of debated origin. The traditional analysis takes it as a compound of *por- "before, in front" (the zero-grade of PIE *per-) and *sta- "to stand" (PIE *steh₂-) — literally "that which stands in front" of the doorway — a formation that fits its building function beautifully; but the exact grade and suffix are not beyond doubt, and conservative dictionaries prefer to leave postis unexplained rather than build on the reconstruction. Whatever its root, the Latin word entered English twice: directly in the Old English period, where post appears in glosses and texts for a doorpost or pillar, and again, reinforced, through Old French post in the Middle English period. The pillar-sense has never stopped producing: doorposts, gateposts, bedposts, lampposts, signposts, goalposts, starting posts and winning posts — two thousand years of framing, marking and fencing all hang on the same upright timber.

  • Post and lintel. The ancient building scheme — two uprights carrying a horizontal beam — is the grammar of the word: from Stonehenge to the Greek temple to the wooden doors of Tolkien's dwarves. The context sentence of this card (“post or lintel or threshold”) lines up the three members of a door; the companion card BAR shares the same sentence, a happy accident of the reading text, though posts, bars and bolts were indeed old neighbours at every medieval gate. “From pillar to post” (sixteenth century) fossilised the pillar in idiom — driven from one place to another, from the whipping post to the pillory — and “deaf as a post" (eighteenth century) fossilised the post's stolid silence.

II. The station: posita → the post. The second word is the great survivor of medieval communications. Latin ponere "to place, put" — historically po-sinere, a compound of the preposition po- and sinere "to let, put" (a verb whose PIE ancestry is not securely recoverable) — has the past participle posita, "placed". In medieval Italy, posta was the place where horses and couriers were “placed" — the relay station of the courier service; the chain of stations became the system, and the courier riding from station to station was “the post". Italian posta passed into French as poste and into English in the sixteenth century: “to ride post", "post-haste", "post horse" — speed by relay was the technology of the age, and the word carried the speed with it.

  • From riders to letters (17th century). “The post" shifted from the couriers to what they carried: "to post a letter" (seventeenth century), "the post has arrived" — the same metonymy that gave MAIL its letters (mail is the bag, post is the relay). The royal and private courier systems merged into one postal institution, and the word absorbed the whole machinery: postboy, postilion, postmaster, post road.
  • From stations to jobs (17th century onward). “A post" — a place at which one is stationed — slid from military to civilian life: a military post, a trading post, an outpost, a sentry's post, and then a position of employment: "to hold a post", "to be posted abroad", "to apply for a post". The stationary image of the upright is behind it all: the post is where one stands.
  • The institution (18th–19th centuries). The General Post Office (established 1660), postmen, postage and postage stamps (the penny post and the Penny Black, 1840), "by return of post": the word became the name of a national infrastructure, part of the fabric of letters, commerce and newspapers. The "Last Post" bugle call — the end of the day at a military post, adopted into remembrance ceremony — carried the station-word into mourning and national memory.
  • The digital post (late 20th century). When the internet needed a verb for placing a message before the public, old "posting" was ready: "to post" to Usenet (1990s), then blogs, forums and social media — a post is what one puts up. The ancient relay word now moves at the speed of light, still "placed" for the reader, and the postal verb and the pillar noun share the spelling without a second thought.

III. The prefix: Latin post "after". The third word is the Latin preposition and adverb post "after, behind", descended from the PIE adverb *pos "after, behind". It never existed as an independent English noun or verb: it entered only as a learned prefix in Latin borrowings from the sixteenth century — postscript ("written after", sixteenth century), postdate (seventeenth century), posthumous — and carried on into English coinages: post-war (twentieth century), post-modern, and the journalistic post-truth (2016). Spelling converged; meaning never mixed: the doorpost stands, the letter is posted, and the war is post.

Register today. Context rules everything: a post office, a signpost, a blog post, a post-mortem — four readings of one spelling, each so familiar that the others are not even noticed. Computing even added a fourth homonym in POST (power-on self-test, the computer's start-up check). Three genealogies — a reconstructed PIE compound of uncertain shape, a medieval Italian relay station, and a Latin preposition — one short word, and a whole civilisation of building, posting and remembering crowded into it: the doorpost of the secret door, the postman's delivery, and the last post of the bugle.
