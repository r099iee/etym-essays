PIE *ureǵ- "to press, push, drive, impel" (Pokorny sets the root as *u̯er- / *u̯erəǵ- "drängen", "to press, to urge"; the exact laryngeal shape is debated). → Latin urgēre "to press, push, drive; to urge, to press hard on someone" (verb; attested from early Latin). → Latin urgēns, urgent- (present participle of urgēre) "pressing, urging" → Old French urgent (adjective) → French urgent "pressing, urgent" → English urgent (adjective, c. 1580) "pressing, demanding haste". → Latin urgentia (abstract noun formed on urgēre) "a pressing, pressure, urgency" → Middle French urgence "urgency, insistent necessity" → English urgency (noun, c. 1600) "pressing nature; insistent need". → English plural formation urgencies "pressing necessities, clamorous demands" — the plural of urgency, in literary and historical prose from the 18th century onward — URGENCIES (our card). NOTE on the debate: the deeper equation of *ureǵ- with the *wreǵ- behind Greek eírgein "to shut in, enclose" has been cautiously proposed by some older scholarship and is disputed; the exact laryngeal shape of the root is likewise debated. Neither uncertainty affects the Latin and Romance family, which is securely documented from urgēre onward. A separate, direct borrowing produced the English verb urge (from Latin urgere, c. 1550), giving English the distinctive triple urge / urgent / urgency drawn from different slices of the same Latin verb.
