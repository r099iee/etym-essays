- 1918–1938 — Czechoslovakia is a hopeful, democratic, Western-facing name; it stands for national liberation, land reform and the only stable democracy in interwar Central Europe.
- 1938–1939 — The hyphenated Czecho-Slovakia becomes a badge of humiliation after Munich; the name now signals truncation and betrayal.
- 1939–1945 — The name survives in exile and resistance; to the occupied it means the lost republic, to the Nazis a state to be erased.
- 1948–1968 — “Czechoslovakia” is a Soviet-bloc country, associated with nationalised industry, censorship and the show trials of the early 1950s.
- 1968–1989 — After the Prague Spring and the Warsaw Pact invasion, the name connotes “socialism with a human face” crushed, and a grey period of “normalisation.”
- 1989–1992 — The Velvet Revolution restores the name’s democratic glow, but the “Hyphen War” shows it can no longer hold the two nations together.
- 1993–present — The word becomes historical and nostalgic; “Czechoslovak” survives in sport, film and diaspora memory, while “Velvet Divorce” turns the ending into a byword for civility.
