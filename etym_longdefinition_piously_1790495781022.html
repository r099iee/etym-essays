piously is the adverb of pious: in a pious manner, that is, devoutly, reverently, dutifully, with the outward signs and inward spirit of religion, loyalty and moral respect. In the source sentence — John Ruskin in Praeterita recalling his Tory childhood, his deep yearning for a fuller “Restoration” which Charles II had not altogether effected, though I always wore a gilded oak-apple very piously in my button-hole on the 29th of May — it is Royalist piety made visible. The 29th of May was Oak Apple Day, the anniversary of Charles II’s restoration in 1660, when loyalists wore oak-apples in memory of his hiding in the Boscobel oak after Worcester. The boy’s gilded apple, worn very piously, is at once religious devotion, political loyalty and filial duty: the intensifier very half-childlike, half-ironic in the older narrator, marking earnestness that is sincere yet also quaint, conspicuous and slightly performative.

In ordinary modern use piously covers the whole adverbial field of pious: doing something with reverence toward God, with dutiful respect toward parents, country, memory or principle, or — by extension and often ironically — with the mere show of such reverence. One prays piously, attends service piously, keeps a parent’s anniversary piously, preserves a poet’s house piously, or repeats a party slogan piously. The manner may be inward (humble, recollected, prayerful) or outward (kneeling, bare-headed, processional, medal-wearing), and the moral judgment of the observer decides whether the word praises or blames. The same sentence — he attends Mass piously every morning — is respectful from a believer and faintly mocking from a sceptic. That double edge is essential to the word today.

  • Core religious sense: devoutly, reverently toward God: e.g. she knelt piously in the chapel, the monks filed piously to Compline, he crossed himself piously. This is the dictionary head sense. The semantic bridge from Latin pius (dutiful toward gods, parents and country) is direct: Christian writers narrowed Roman duty to duty toward God, then to the manner of worship — folded hands, lowered eyes, murmured responses. Adverbs of posture and tone cluster here: humbly, meekly, prayerfully, worshipfully.
  • Filial and loyal sense: dutifully, loyally, respectfully: e.g. he tended his father’s grave piously, the oak-apple was piously worn, the regiment piously keeps the battle honour. This preserves the older Roman pietas, duty to kin, ancestors and fatherland, which Christianity never erased. Ruskin’s use belongs here as much as to church: loyalty to the Stuarts, to father’s Toryism, to Scott and Homer, is felt as religion. The bridge is analogy — a king, a father, a memory can be served with religious fidelity.
  • Sanctimonious and hypocritical sense: with ostentatious or affected devotion: e.g. he piously denounced the play he had never seen, they piously averted their eyes while pocketing the profits. Here piously accuses. From the Reformation onward Protestant, then Enlightenment, then journalistic prose used pious to mean outwardly holy but inwardly hollow, and the adverb followed: to do something piously is to do it with a show of virtue. The reasoning is moral suspicion — conspicuous devotion invites the question of motive, and irony becomes the default reading in secular contexts.
  • Pious-hope sense: earnestly but ineffectually, wishfully: e.g. it was piously hoped the roof would last another winter, ministers piously declare that standards will rise. This grows from the fixed phrases pious hope, pious wish, pious belief (from Latin pia vota, dutiful but unfulfilled prayers): a hope called pious because it is virtuous in intention and powerless in effect. The adverb then means well-meaningly, futilely, self-deceivingly. The bridge is pragmatic disappointment — prayers unanswered become a figure for all earnest ineffectuality, beloved of newspapers and committee minutes.
  • Pious-fraud sense and moralistic register: the fossil phrase pious fraud (Latin pia fraus, a deception practised for a supposedly good end) colours legal, historical and journalistic use: editors piously suppressed the passage, biographers piously omitted the mistress. To act piously here is to censor, bowdlerise or prettify from misplaced reverence. Ruskin himself attacks such piety in art restoration and biography, while practising loyal piety toward kings and parents — the tension is characteristic of Victorian moral language.
  • Domain and stylistic notes: in liturgy and theology piously remains neutral and commendatory; in politics and journalism it is usually ironic (piously intoned manifestos, piously carbon-neutral conferences); in family and memorial contexts it is tender (piously kept letters, piously retold stories). Frequency is modest and literary; everyday speech prefers devoutly, faithfully, dutifully, reverently, reserving piously for deliberate colour, period flavour or sting.
