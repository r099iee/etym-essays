BEFOULED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to befoul — to make foul, dirty, filthy, or impure; to defile, pollute, or contaminate, whether physically with dirt, filth, or waste, or morally and spiritually with sin, disgrace, or corruption. The prefix be- is intensive and transitivizing: where foul describes a state, befoul names the action of rendering something foul, thoroughly and often deliberately.

In The Hobbit, Tolkien writes that "all was befouled and blasted with the comings and goings of the monster": the great halls of Erebor are not merely dusty with age — they have been soiled, defiled, and made foul by Smaug's long habitation, his passages through the treasure-chambers leaving muck, destruction, and desecration. The word here carries both the literal sense (physical filth left by the dragon) and a powerful moral charge (the violation and pollution of a once-sacred dwarven home).

Core meaning: To make foul; to dirty, defile, or pollute, in physical or moral terms.

  • Primary sense (physical soiling): To cover or fill with dirt, filth, dung, mud, or waste. Used of water, food, clothing, nests, and — specifically — places made filthy by animals. For example, "the birds befouled the statues with their droppings".
  • Secondary sense (pollution of water or air): To contaminate a natural resource such as a spring, river, or atmosphere with harmful substances — an early and still fully current ecological sense. For example, "the mine befouled the stream for miles downstream".
  • Tertiary sense (moral and spiritual defilement): To corrupt, debase, or pollute something held sacred or pure — a reputation, a name, a promise, a holy place, a soul. This is the dominant sense in older religious and moral writing. For example, "he befouled his good name with lies".
  • Sense of desecration: Closely allied to the moral sense, used of sacred places, altars, and sanctuaries that are violated by unworthy presence or action.
  • Animal and ornithological sense: To foul a roost, nest, or perch with excrement — common in hunting and falconry usage, and still the everyday use applied to birds and caged animals.

Connotations and register: Befoul is markedly more intense and literary than plain dirty or soil. The intensive prefix be- adds thoroughness and deliberateness; befouled suggests a willful or wholesale defilement rather than casual grubbiness. It is at home in moral, religious, and poetic contexts, and is frequently chosen when the writer wants to evoke disgust, sacrilege, or pollution on a grand scale. It remains current, though formal: "the river was befouled by sewage" is natural, if elevated, modern English.

Grammatical notes: A regular weak verb; befouled serves as both past tense and past participle, and also as an adjective ("befouled waters"). It is normally transitive, though passive and reflexive uses are common ("he befouled himself with corruption"). The related noun is befoulment (chiefly ecological), and the agent noun befouler is rare. The word is strongly negative in every register; it has no neutral or positive senses whatsoever.

Related semantic fields: defilement, pollution, contamination, desecration, corruption, taint, stain, smirch, soiling, filth, muck.

In the context of the card: "all was befouled and blasted" — befouled (soiled and defiled) pairs with blasted (scorched and shattered by the dragon's fire) to give a double portrait of ruin: filth plus destruction. Thorin's ancient halls, once "broad and fair", have been turned into a monster's lair, and the language of befoulment makes the violation feel almost sacred — as if the mountain itself had been profaned.
