The history of cloven is the history of a strong verb growing old gracefully: the everyday action of splitting wood hardening, through Scripture and superstition, into one of the most atmospheric participles in English.

Deep root — the carving hand: The trail begins with reconstructed PIE *gleubh-, to cut, slice, or carve, the root also behind Greek glyphein, to carve or engrave, the ancestor of English glyph. It names the deliberate incising hand, the knife and chisel rather than the smashing hammer. By Grimm's law the voiced aspirate shifted in Germanic, giving Proto-Germanic *kleubaną, to split or cleave, with the hard northern k where Greek kept its softer sound.

Old English — the strong verb at work: Old English clēofan was a Class 2 strong verb, conjugating by vowel music rather than dental suffixes, and its participle clofen described riven timber, slaughtered carcasses, and parted substances generally. The ablaut series survives today in the trio cleave, clove, cloven, with cleft as the weak-formed rival that later stole much of the verb's ordinary work.

Middle English — variants swarm: After the Conquest the verb appears as cleven with a cloud of participial spellings, cloven, clove, cleft, yclove, scribes choosing by dialect and metre. Alliterative poets use it for split shields, riven helms, and divided hosts, the battlefield keeping the word bright while husbandry kept it fed.

The Bible — two immortal fixings: No force shaped cloven more than translation. The divided hoof marking clean beasts in Leviticus and Deuteronomy came into English as the cloven hoof, and the flame-tips of Pentecost in Acts came as cloven tongues like as of fire. Tyndale, the Geneva Bible, and the Authorised Version of 1611 between them drove the participle into every parish ear, yoking it simultaneously to dietary law and to the Holy Spirit, to cud-chewing cattle and to heaven's fire.

The Devil's foot — Pan in a black coat: Parallel to Scripture ran folklore. The goat-legged Pan of Arcadia, piper and frightener of flocks, lent his shaggy thighs to medieval images of Satan, and the cloven hoof became the one deformity no disguise could hide. Jest-books, witch pamphlets, and ballads rang the changes: the fine gentleman whose shoe slips to show the hoof, the dancer whose footprints betray him in snow. The word absorbed a sulphurous tinge it has never quite lost, which is why even a rutted lane called cloven feels faintly fateful.

Modern sorting — cleft takes the prose, cloven keeps the poetry: In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the weak form cleft captured most everyday duty, cleft palates, cleft sticks, mountain clefts, leaving cloven to the hoof, the hymn-book, and the storyteller. Dictionaries now mark it literary outside zoology, yet fantasy and folk tale keep reaching for it precisely because of its age-music. Its milestones read:

  • PIE carving root: *gleubh-, to cut and carve, kin to glyph, naming the incising hand behind the later splitting verb.
  • Germanic hardening: *kleubaną, to split, with Grimm's-law k, spreading through Old Norse kljufa and Old High German klioban as well as English.
  • Old English strength: clēofan, a Class 2 strong verb with ablaut participle clofen, doing daily work in woodpile and shambles.
  • Biblical elevation: the cloven hoof of Leviticus and the cloven tongues of Acts, fixing the word in pulpit English for four centuries.
  • Folkloric darkening: Pan's legs re-tailored as Satan's, making the cloven foot the unhideable mark of evil in tale and jest-book.
  • Modern division of labour: cleft for clinics and climbing guides, cloven for herds, hymns, and haunted lanes, each guarding its own register.

So when night travellers plunge into a deeply cloven track, four thousand years of cutting, carving, Scripture, and superstition run in the rut beneath their feet, and the lane divides like a hoof, like a tongue of fire, like everything English has ever called fatefully in two.
