Linear derivation chain for AVIGNON (single root, pre-Roman substrate): 1. Pre-Indo-European / ancient European hydronymic root *ab- / *avan- ("water, river, stream, marsh") — a water-word stratum older than the Indo-European invasion of western Europe, probably from a pre-Indo-European or early Celtic substrate. There is no PIE etymology in the strict sense for this element. 2. Celtic reflex *abonā ("river") — the derived river-word, attested in the daughter languages as Welsh afon ("river"), Cornish avon, Breton avon, Irish/Gaelic abhainn ("river"); the same element recurs across the English landscape in the repeated river-name Avon (literally "the river") and in the Roman Abona of Wiltshire. 3. Gaulish (Celto-Ligurian) Avennio — the name of the settlement of the Cavares on a marshy reach of the Rhône; built on the water-root *av- + the Gaulish place-suffix -iō/-iōnem; glosses as "town at the water/marsh." 4. Latin Avenniō, Avenniōnis — the Latinized third-declension form of the Gaulish name, attested as the Roman civitas Avennio under the Empire; the -iōnem suffix (Latin accusative) is characteristic of Gaulish place-names (cf. Vesontio → Besançon). 5. Provençal / Old Occitan Avinhon — the medieval Romance development of the Latin name in the speech of southern France. 6. Old French / French Avignon (/aviˈɲɔ̃/) — the standard French form, which carried the name into the chronicles of Christendom during the Avignon Papacy (1309–1377). 7. Modern English AVIGNON (/ˈavinjˌɒn/) — used in English chronicles, histories, and biographies from the 14th century onward; the anglicized form with first-syllable stress. Note on debate: the root *ab-/*avan- is an ancient European hydronymic element, not a PIE root; its precise linguistic stratum (pre-Indo-European substrate vs. early Celtic) is debated, but its "water/river" meaning is secure from the cognates (Welsh afon, Irish abhainn, English Avon). The suffix -iōnem is a Gaulish place-forming element; the water meaning is carried by the root, not the suffix.
