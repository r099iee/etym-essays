Proto-Indo-European *dʰrews- "to fall, to break apart, to crumble, to drip" → Proto-Germanic *dreusaną "to fall" (cognates: Gothic driusan, Old Saxon driosan, Old High German triosan) → Old English drēosan "to fall, to sink, to perish" (attested from the 8th c.) → Old English drēor (noun) "gore, spilt blood; that which falls or drips" → Old English drēorig (adjective) "gory, blood-stained; sad, sorrowful, dejected" (attested in Old English poetry) → Middle English drery / dreri "sorrowful, dismal, gloomy" (c. 1300) → Early Modern English dreary "cheerless, dull, dispiriting" (16th–17th c.) → Modern English dreary (of weather, landscape, mood, existence). Note on collateral relatives: the clipped poetic adjective drear (attested 1620s) is a back-formation from dreary, not an independent chain. English drizzle (1540s) and drop (Old English dropa, "a drop") belong to the same Germanic family of falling and dripping, but they are collateral formations, not stages in dreary's own ancestry.
