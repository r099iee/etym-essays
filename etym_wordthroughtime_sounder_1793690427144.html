- **Adjectival Comparative (More Sound)** - From Old English sund (healthy, safe), this form has consistently denoted a higher degree of integrity, health, or reliability. Its connotation has always been positive, indicating improvement or superiority in quality, stability, or logical coherence.
- **Noun (Telegraph Sounder)** - Emerging in the mid-19th century, this specific technical usage referred to a device making audible clicks for telegraph signals. Its perception was purely functional, a crucial tool in early communication, directly linked to the act of making 'sound'.
- **Noun (Depth Sounder)** - Evolving from the verb 'to sound' (measure depth), this term initially referred to a person or lead line. With the advent of sonar in the early 20th century, it shifted to denote advanced electronic devices (echo sounders). Its perception moved from a manual task to a high-tech navigational and scientific instrument, maintaining its core function of measurement.
- **Noun (Collective for Boars)** - This specialized term, derived from Old French, has maintained a consistent, niche usage within venery (hunting language) since Middle English. Its connotation is neutral, purely descriptive of a group of wild boars, without significant shift in societal perception beyond its specific domain.
