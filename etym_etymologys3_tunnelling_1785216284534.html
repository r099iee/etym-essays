TUNNELLING: from a cask to a net to a pipe to a mountain road THE BARREL The story of tunnelling is a story of metaphor. It begins not with excavation but with barrels. In the ninth century Medieval Latin has tunna, "cask, barrel," a word also found in Old English tunne — the "tun," a great cask of wine or ale — and in Old High German tunna, ancestor of the modern German Tonne. The relationship of these forms is uncertain: the word is a North Sea Germanic container-term that floated around the trading coasts of northwestern Europe, possibly of Celtic extraction, since Old Irish toun means "hide, skin," a covering stretched over a frame, which some scholars compare. No Indo-European ancestor can be securely reconstructed; the word is simply a container-word that the trading peoples of the North Sea coast shared, and no Proto-Indo-European root should be forced upon it. THE NET From French tonne, "a tun," a diminutive was formed: tonnel, tonnelle, literally "small cask." But the same word took a surprising second life: French also used tonnelle of a long, funnel-shaped net used to decoy birds, a bird-net narrowed and elongated like a small cask or funnel. English borrowed that word in the early fifteenth century as tonnel — a bird-net, a fowler's tool. For a hundred years a tunnel was, in English, a net for catching birds, not a passage in the earth at all. THE TUBE The leap that made the modern word was purely metaphorical, and it is the heart of the whole story: anything long, hollow, and tubular reminded English speakers of this net. By 1540 tunnel meant "tube, pipe" — a flue, a chimney, the narrowest of passages. The word had become a shape-word: it named anything hollow and elongated, the way a funnel-shaped net is hollow and elongated. This is the moment the modern tunnel is born, though it does not yet know it. UNDER THE EARTH From tube to underground passage was the final step, taken in the 1660s, when tunnel came to mean a passage cut under a river or through a mountain — the older native word for such a thing having been mine. So thorough was the English extension that French, which had supplied the word, re-borrowed it in 1878 as tunnel. The verb to tunnel, "to excavate underground," dates from 1795 (intransitive) and 1856 (transitive), and the present participle tunnelling — with the British doubled -l-, while American English writes tunneling — belongs to this later, engineering era. The great age of Victorian tunnelling — Brunel's Thames Tunnel, the Alpine railway bores — gave the word heroic, industrial, public-works glamour: the tunnel was the road that defeated a mountain. THE MODERN METAPHORS In the twentieth century the word tunnelled into new territories by the same process of metaphor that created it. Quantum tunnelling (late 1920s) named the astonishing phenomenon by which a particle passes through a potential-energy barrier it lacks the energy to climb — the particle, as it were, digs no hole but slips through the wall, and the word was chosen precisely because it made the invisible motion vivid. In networking, tunnelling is the encapsulation of one protocol inside another so that data can travel over an incompatible network — VPN tunnelling, SSH tunnelling — the private passage driven through the public network. Tunnel vision (20th century) names the pathological narrowing of the visual field, and by metaphor the narrowing of attention: seeing only down one corridor. Covert tunnelling — smugglers' tunnels, prison-escape tunnels, tunnels under borders — uses the oldest association of all: that a tunnel is secret, hidden beneath the visible surface. SOUND AND FORM The sound of the word has been stable since its French loan: the English form tonnel preserved the French short o and the -el, which later respelled to -el and then -nel; the modern spelling tunnel, with its single medial n, settled by the seventeenth century. The doubled -l- of the British participle tunnelling reflects the ordinary English rule that a stressed short vowel before a single consonant doubles the consonant before -ing (cf. travelling, modelling), while American usage follows the simplified spelling tunneling. The RP pronunciation /tˈʌnəlɪŋ/ is straightforward: stressed /ʌ/, schwa in the second syllable, and the -l- + -ing cluster that gives the word its doubled letter in British writing. IN THE HOBBIT When Tolkien's goblins are described as "tunnelling down long ago" and striking the black lake, the word carries all of this history at once: a cask that became a net, a net that became a pipe, and a pipe that became a road driven through the heart of a mountain. The goblins are tunnellers by nature, miners enlarging ancient caves, and their tunnelling is at once industrial and legendary — geological, silent, vast, and secret, exactly the associations the word has accumulated since it first left the fowler's field. And the irony of the scene is also in the word: the goblins tunnelled down until their road ended at the water, dead-ending in a lake with "no reason to go that way" — a tunnel that led nowhere, the road beneath the ground that reached the end of its own making. The derivation chain, from the earliest stage: Medieval Latin tunna "cask, barrel" (9th c.; North Sea Germanic, possibly Celtic) → Old French tonne "tun" → tonnelle "small cask; funnel-shaped decoy net" → Middle English tonnel "bird-net" (early 15th c.) → "tube, pipe" (1540) → "underground passage" (1660s) → verb to tunnel "excavate underground" (1795, transitive 1856) → English tunnelling (present participle, British -ll-).
