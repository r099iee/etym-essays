The root: *bʰeh₂- "to speak". The entire family begins with the Indo-European verb "to speak", reconstructed as *bʰeh₂- — the root of Greek phēmí "I say" (the Homeric phēmi), of Latin fārī "to speak" and fāma "report, fame", of fātum "what is spoken (by the gods) — fate", and probably of English "ban" (a proclamation) via Germanic. "Fame" and "fate" are thus cousins of "fable": fame is what is said about you; fate is what has been said about you; a fable is a thing said — a tale.

Latin fabula: from "talk" to "play" to "fable". From fārī Latin built the noun fabula "a tale, a story, a play" — literally "an instrument of speaking" (the root fā- plus an instrumental suffix). The word had a wide range in classical Latin: it meant "gossip, idle talk" (what people say), "a story or tale" (what is told), and in the theatre "a play" — the genres fabula palliata (comedy in Greek dress), fabula togata (comedy in Roman dress), and fabula praetexta (historical drama) are named from it, and Plautus's prologues call their own comedies fabulae. But the sense that mattered most for the future was the humble one: the short moral tale. Phaedrus, a freedman of Augustus, wrote Latin verse versions of Aesop's animal stories in the 1st century CE, explicitly calling them fabulae Aesopiae — "Aesopic fables" — and thereby fixed "fable" as the name of the beast-tale with a moral.

Through French to Middle English. Old French took the word as fable "tale, story, fable" (attested from the 10th–11th centuries), and the great medieval fable-collections made it a household word: Marie de France, writing in Anglo-Norman French in England around 1170–1200, composed her Fables (the Ysopet, "little Aesop") in the very dialect that would feed English vocabulary. Middle English borrowed fable in the 13th century; Chaucer uses it of the beast-tale, most famously in the Nun's Priest's Tale, whose cock-and-fox story he presents as a fable with a moral ("Taketh the fruyt, and lat the chaf be stille"). At the same time the verb to fable arose (late 14th century), meaning "to tell tales, to invent, to feign" — and from that verb comes our word.

Fabled is born: from "invented" to "celebrated". The participle fabled is first recorded in the early 17th century (1610s) as an adjective: "celebrated in fable, legendary". The semantic path is the interesting part. Through the 16th century "fabled" could still simply mean "invented, fictitious" — the participle of "to fable". But because the things celebrated in fables are famous by virtue of being told about, the word crossed over from "made-up" to "made famous by story": a fabled city is a city the stories have made glorious. By the 18th century the adjective was standard in elevated prose for legendary geography and myth — "fabled shores", "fabled Nile", "fabled Atlantis" — and the 19th century, the age of Romantic travel and orientalist wonder, attached it to distant, half-mythical places and their treasures.

Modern life.

  • 20th century: "fabled" finds its true home in fantasy literature — Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) gives us "the fabled treasure of the Mountain lying without guard or owner", where the word does double duty: the treasure is famous in story and, at that moment, unguarded and up for grabs.
  • The fabulous branch: its cousin fabulous took a separate, spectacular path — from Latin fabulōsus "full of fables, legendary" it moved through "incredible, astonishing" to the 20th-century colloquial "fabulous!" = "wonderful", a full journey from "told in tales" to "great, man".
  • 21st century: journalism and tourism marketing keep the word alive ("the fabled markets of Marrakesh"), while plain speech prefers "legendary".

Phonetic and structural notes. Latin fābula → Old French fable involved the loss of the medial vowel and shortening; Middle English took the form as fable, and the adjective fabled is a native formation with -ed. The root-initial f- corresponds to Greek ph- and to the PIE *bʰ — Latin f regularly continues PIE *bʰ (as in frāter beside Greek phrátēr). Key attestations in brief: Homeric Greek phēmí; Latin fabula (Plautus, 2nd c. BCE); Phaedrus, fabulae Aesopiae (1st c. CE); Marie de France, Fables (c. 1170–1200); Middle English fable (13th c.); verb "to fable" (late 14th c.); adjective fabled (1610s); Tolkien 1937.
