- c. 1400 — a bookish verb of echo and resonance, used of bells, vaults and voices, with a strong sense of a space returning sound.
- 16th c. — extends to public noise and reputation: a name or deed “resounds” through a country, and to resound someone’s praises becomes a familiar poetic formula.
- 17th–18th c. — standard in elevated prose, sermons and epic translation; the acoustic and the celebratory senses coexist, and the word settles into its modern written form.
- 19th c. — Victorian rhetorical prose (Carlyle among others) keeps the full, sonorous verb alive; the participle “resounding” hardens into an adjective of emphasis, as in “a resounding victory”.
- 20th–21st c. — literal use narrows to written narrative and journalism; the fame sense becomes archaic, while “resonate” takes over the figurative work of striking a chord with an audience.
