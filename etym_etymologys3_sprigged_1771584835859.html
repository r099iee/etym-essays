The biography of sprigged is small-scale but revealing, for it shows a word travelling from the hedgerow into the dressmaker's shop and then into the potter's kiln. The story begins with an ordinary noun for an ordinary thing — a sprig, a small shoot or twig — and ends with a precise adjective of textile and ceramic decoration whose every use still carries a faint breath of gardens and muslin.

1. The root: springing forth. If the standard reconstruction is right, the ultimate ancestor of the English word is the Indo-European root *spergh-, "to move with haste, to spring, to burst forth." The root's cognates show its force: Greek σπέρχω (spérkhō) "to hasten, to urge on," Sanskrit स्फूर्जति (sphūrjati) "to burst forth, to flash, to rumble," Latin spargere "to scatter, to sprinkle." In the Germanic branch the root produced *springan-, "to leap up, to spring forth," and from it Old English springan — a verb of motion whose botanical sense, "to sprout," is attested early. The whole family is built on the image of sudden, eager emergence: water springs from the earth, a year springs from winter, a twig springs from a branch.

2. The noun sprig and its obscure birth. The noun sprig appears in English around the late fourteenth century meaning "a small shoot, twig, or spray," and here the record becomes honestly uncertain. The Oxford English Dictionary marks the origin as obscure. Two explanations compete. The first sees a Scandinavian loan: Norwegian sprik and related dialectal forms mean "a forked twig," and English, in its centuries of contact with Scandinavian speech, may have taken the word and reshaped it under the pull of the native verb spring. The second sees a native formation: a sprig is "that which springs" from a branch, built on the model of the verb spring just as sprout and spray cluster around the same idea of green emergence. Both accounts point to the same semantic core — a sprig is a little thing that has sprung out — and neither can be settled from the written record. What matters is that the noun, whatever its precise source, was felt to belong to the family of spring: a sprig is a shoot that springs.

3. From noun to verb. The next step was the English habit of turning nouns into verbs. From the noun sprig the language made to sprig, meaning "to decorate with sprigs" and also "to plant or fix a sprig," attested from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The verb is a modest member of a large class — English has long made verbs from nouns with effortless ease — but its fate was to become useful in the decorative trades, where "to sprig a fabric" meant to scatter it with small floral motifs. The decorative sense is a natural extension of the botanical one: the flower-sprays printed or woven on a cloth were conceived of as little sprigs, and a cloth covered with them had, as it were, sprung into flower.

4. The participle and the textile trade. From the verb came the past participle sprigged, and from the seventeenth century the participle entered the working vocabulary of the cloth trade. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were its golden age. Sprigged muslins and cottons were among the most fashionable of dress fabrics — light, fresh, and delicately floral, the summer costume of the respectable young woman — and the word appears throughout the fiction and letters of the period. The particular genius of the term lies in its scale and its tone: a sprigged fabric is decorated with small, scattered sprigs, not with large formal bouquets or heavy brocade. It is a word of lightness, intimacy, and youth, and novelists used it to dress characters who are gentle, fresh, and domestic — exactly the register the card's sentence exploits when it places the sprigs on a worn rose-coloured gown and lets the fading fabric speak of elegance remembered.

5. The potter's kiln. In the eighteenth century the word took a second, craft-specific life. English potteries, above all Staffordshire and the factories of the Wedgwood family, developed the technique of "sprigging": applying small moulded clay motifs — leaves, flowers, classical reliefs — to the body of a vessel before firing. Such wares are "sprigged" or "sprigged pottery," and the technique gives the word a precise technical sense alongside its textile one. The bridge is again the sprig itself: the small applied motifs are the potter's version of the little shoots that name the fabric. A single root of meaning — the small vegetable spray — now feeds three trades: the cloth, the flower pattern, and the ceramic ornament.

6. The modern word. In contemporary English, sprigged is a period-flavoured adjective, most at home in historical fiction, in descriptions of vintage dress, and in the technical vocabularies of textiles and ceramics. It is used of old-fashioned floral cottons and muslins, of wallpapers scattered with small flowers, and of pottery carrying applied reliefs. It is rarely applied to bold or modern designs, because its whole semantic weight lies in delicacy and scatter — the small sprig, not the branch. As a word it is a tiny museum: it preserves, in one adjective, the botanical shoot of the hedgerow, the fashionable muslin of the Georgian summer, and the clay relief of the potter's wheel, all bound together by the ancient idea that things which are small and green and scattered have sprung, as the root would say, into being.
