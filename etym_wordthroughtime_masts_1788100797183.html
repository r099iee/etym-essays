- Old English (c. 900–1100) - mæst: purely the ship's timber spar; the word belongs to the shared nautical stock of the North Sea and Baltic peoples.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) - mast: standard rigging term as ships grow from single-masted cogs to three-masted carracks.
- Age of sail (16th–19th c.) - the mast becomes the symbol of seafaring itself; 'before the mast' names the common sailor's life (Dana, 1840); 'nail one's colours to the mast' means never surrendering.
- 20th c. - the word migrates ashore: radio masts, television masts, aerial masts — still tall vertical supports, now of airwaves instead of sails.
- Today - the nautical sense is partly historical and fully literary; metaphorically the word stands for anything tall and columnar, while half-mast still marks public mourning.
