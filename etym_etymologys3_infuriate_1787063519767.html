The history of infuriate takes us from the oldest layers of the Indo-European lexicon, through the religious vocabulary of ancient Rome, into the rage of Greek and Roman myth (the Furies), and finally into English in the 17th century as a learned Latinism. Along the way the core idea — fury — has remained remarkably stable: an explosive, divinely-tinged anger that takes possession of a person, drives them out of their senses, and overwhelms all restraint.

The Indo-European root. The word descends ultimately from a Proto-Indo-European root whose exact form is debated. The most widely cited reconstruction is *dheu- (or *dʰeuH-), meaning “to blow, to be turbid, to rise in a cloud” — a root that also underlies words for smoke, dust, and mental agitation. The semantic link proposed is that the furious mind is one that “rises up” and is “turbid”, like a storm or stirred water. However, this connection is not certain: some scholars derive Latin furere (“to rage, rave”) from a different, possibly unconnected source, and the ultimate origin of the Latin verb is classified as uncertain in the OED and in de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin. What is certain is the Latin development itself, which must be presented without the insecure PIE link being overstated.

Latin furere and its family. In classical Latin the verb furere meant “to rage, rave, be mad”, and it generated a rich family of words for supernatural and pathological anger. Chief among them was furia, “rage, fury, madness” — and, capitalized in the plural, Furiae, the Furies, the Roman goddesses of vengeance who pursued and tormented wrongdoers. The Furies gave the word its permanent depth: to be seized by fury was, in the Roman imagination, to be possessed by a goddess, to be driven out of one's senses by an external, avenging force. From furia came the verb furiāre, “to drive mad, to madden”, and from furiāre came the prefixed form infuriāre — literally “to put fury into” — “to enrage, madden” (the in- prefix here is the “in, into” prefix, not the negative one). The past participle infuriātus meant “enraged, maddened”. The word thus carries, embedded in its morphology, the ancient image of fury being poured into a person like a liquid into a vessel.

Entry into English. English borrowed the word directly from Latin during the great wave of Latin and Greek loanwords in the early modern period. The verb infuriate (“to make furious, to enrage”) is first attested in English in the 1620s, formed on the model of the Latin past participle infuriātus. Within a few decades, around the 1660s, the adjective infuriate (“enraged, maddened”) also appeared, again directly from the Latin participle. This was a classic learned borrowing: the word arrived fully formed, with its Latin grammar visible, and it entered the literary language of the 17th century, where it was used in sermons, histories and tragedies to describe the terrible, god-driven anger of the age's favourite subject matter — tyrants, mobs, and tragic heroes.

Development in modern English. From the 17th century onward infuriate was a permanent, if not common, member of the vocabulary of rage. In the 19th century it became more frequent in journalism and fiction, and by the 20th century it was fully naturalized as an everyday strong verb: “to infuriate” is now a standard word for making someone extremely angry, and its participles infuriating and infuriated are among the most common forms. The separate adjective infuriate became rare and survives only in formal or literary prose. The noun infuriation (attested from the 18th century) remains formal and uncommon. Meanwhile the ancient religious aura has faded: modern speakers use the word without any conscious sense of the Furies, yet the intensity the word still guarantees is a direct inheritance from the Roman goddesses of vengeance — an unusually long-lived and vivid semantic chain that stretches from a reconstructed Indo-European sound to a modern newspaper headline.
