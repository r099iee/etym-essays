- [Old English (before 1000)] - The noun furh means a long, narrow trench in the earth — specifically the single straight cut made by a plowshare; purely agricultural, an everyday farming word with no special register.
- [16th–17th century] - The verb "to furrow" emerges and the word begins its slide from field to face: furrows are now also the deep parallel creases in the skin of the forehead or brow, marks of age, strain, or thought.
- [18th–19th century] - The figurative sense settles: "to furrow one's brow" becomes the fixed literary expression for concentration, displeasure, or anxiety — quiet, dignified, and more legible than a frown, the agricultural image of a field being plowed by feeling still quietly present.
- [Modern register] - Both senses survive; the literal farming sense grows more technical and remote as mechanized agriculture replaces the plow, while the brow sense is the everyday workhorse in narrative prose — deep, straight, and hard to erase, like a cut made by iron through loam.
