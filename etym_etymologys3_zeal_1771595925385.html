The word zeal is one of the few Greek passions to have entered English with its temperature intact. Its whole biography is a study in ambivalence — a word that means holy ardour and fanatical excess, emulation and jealousy, in the same breath. To follow it is to trace how a Greek noun of rivalry became a Church word, then an English word of the Reformation, then a secular word of causes and crusades. The Greek source: emulation and jealousy at once. The Greek noun zēlos was defined by Liddell and Scott as "a noble passion" — eager rivalry, emulation, the burning ambition to excel. But from the very beginning the same noun could mean "jealousy," the resentful envy of another's possession or success. The Greeks could not decide whether the heat in the heart was admiration or envy, and the word preserved both fires. Etymologists since Watkins have reconstructed an Indo-European base *yā-, "to seek, request, desire," but the deeper history of zēlos is uncertain — Beekes is characteristically cautious, allowing the possibility of a Pre-Greek element — so the PIE stage must be marked as a hypothesis rather than a certainty. What is certain is that zēlos is a genuine Greek formation, not a borrowing. The personification. The ambivalent noun was potent enough to be given a divine face. In Hesiod's Theogony (8th–7th c. BCE), Zēlos — Emulation personified — is a child of the river-goddess Styx, brother of Nikē (Victory), Kratos (Strength), and Biē (Power): the children of the oath, the forces that enforce divine order. The personification shows how thoroughly the Greeks associated zealous striving with the competitive, agonistic energy of their whole culture — the same energy that drove the Olympic games and the rhetorical contests of the courtroom. Zeal, in this earliest view, was the engine of excellence. The biblical revolution. The Hebrew Bible and then the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament took the word and split its ambivalence along theological lines. The Septuagint used zēlos for God's own "jealousy" — the possessive ardour of a covenant deity who will tolerate no rival ("the zeal of the LORD of hosts will perform this," Isaiah 9:7). The New Testament used it for religious ardour: Paul speaks of the Jews' "zeal for God" (Romans 10:2) and of a godly jealousy (2 Corinthians 11:2). Jesus' disciples apply the Psalmist's words to him: "Zeal for your house consumes me" (John 2:17). In the same first century, Josephus records the sect of the Zēlōtai, the Zealots — Judean resisters who turned zeal into armed rebellion against Rome and were crushed at Masada. The word now carried both the holiest devotion and the most dangerous extremism. The Church word. Christianity carried zēlos into Latin as zēlus, and the word is explicitly a Church word: it entered the Western languages through the liturgy and the Vulgate rather than through classical literature. English took it twice over, on two different routes from the same Greek fire. On the direct channel, Old French zel gave Middle English zele in the late fourteenth century, in the Bible translations and devotional prose of the age — Wycliffe's Bible and its successors used it for the religious fervour of the Psalmist and the prophets. On a side channel, the derived Late Latin zēlōsus ("full of zeal") passed through Old French jalos and emerged as Middle English jelous by about 1200 — the possessive, suspicious sense of jealous — so that the same Greek heat split into two English words that now look unrelated: zeal and jealous are etymological twins. The Reformation and the double edge. The sixteenth-century Reformation made zeal a weapon. Both sides of the religious wars claimed zeal for God; the word hardened into partisanship — earnest devotion to the faithful, fanaticism to opponents. The noun zealot, borrowed in the early fourteenth century from the name of the ancient sect, became a standing name for religious extremists. This is the point at which the modern polarity of the word set in: a "zealous" servant was still praised in Scripture and sermon, but "zealotry" was already the charge of the enemy. Ruskin and the secularization. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries zeal had been fully secularized while keeping its religious heat. The philosophers of the Enlightenment distrusted it — Hume and the moralists opposed zeal to reason and cool judgment. It is exactly this opposition that Ruskin stages in his own sentence when he swears that "no zeal for the reputation of any individual, no personal feeling of any kind, has the slightest weight with me": he is disclaiming the hot, partisan commitment that would compromise his cool critical judgment. The phrase is a deliberate piece of self-portraiture — Ruskin casts himself as the dispassionate judge of art who happens to be defending Turner, and zeal is the very emotion that would disqualify him. The word had come a long way from the fire of the Zealots, but its temperature had not dropped. Today. Modern English keeps the polarity. "Zeal for the work" is admiring; "zealotry" is a warning; a "zealot" is a fanatic. The word still carries the ancient rivalry and striving of its Greek source — the urge to excel, the heat that can burn toward either devotion or excess — and it is precisely this built-in ambivalence that makes it so useful to a writer like Ruskin, who wants to say "I care intensely" and "I am not carried away" in the same breath.
