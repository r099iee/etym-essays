The weasel is a small, slender, long-bodied carnivorous mammal of the family Mustelidae, above all the least weasel of the genus Mustela (M. nivalis), the smallest living carnivore of the northern hemisphere, renowned for its rapid, darting movement, its ability to enter the smallest burrows and gaps, its acute senses, and its extraordinary ferocity in proportion to its size. In loose popular usage the word is also applied to allied mustelids — stoats, polecats, martens, minks — which are easily confused with it, and English natural-history writing has had to keep careful company boundaries: the stoat is the larger relative that turns white (as ermine) in snow, the polecat is heavier and more pungent, the marten is a tree-dweller of the pine forests.

Beyond zoology, the word has a rich figurative life. Since at least the early twentieth century a “weasel” is a cunning, sneaking, or evasive person; the verb “to weasel out” of a difficulty means to escape by slyness and equivocation; and the phrase “weasel words” (attested from the early twentieth century) denotes deliberately vague, slippery, misleading language. The animal’s reputation for stealth and slyness is ancient — the weasel is the type of cunning in European folklore — and modern idiom has built directly on it.

Polysemy and secondary senses.

  • Zoological sense (primary) — the small mustelid of the genus Mustela, especially M. nivalis (the least weasel) and, in some North American usage, M. erminea; a lithe, low-slung hunter of rodents.
  • Applied to allied mustelids — stoat, polecat, marten, mink, and (in some dialect use) the ferret; “weasel” is often the generic popular term for the whole tribe of slender, sneaking carnivores.
  • Figurative, of a person — a cunning, sly, sneaking, or untrustworthy person: “that weasel of a lawyer,” “a weasel in the office.”
  • The verb to weasel (out) — to back out of a commitment by evasive means; to slip out of a difficulty as a weasel slips through a crack.
  • Weasel words — deliberately vague or misleading language, so-called from the supposed habit of weasels of sucking eggs while leaving the shell to appear whole; the image was made famous by Theodore Roosevelt’s attack on “weasel words” in 1916.
  • Weaselly (adjective) — thin, shifty, sly in appearance or manner.
  • Etymology-bearing sense — the animal’s pungent anal musk, which gave the whole family of Germanic names their literal meaning “the stinking (or reeking) animal.”

Natural history and behaviour. The least weasel is scarcely larger than a mouse — body length rarely exceeding 20 cm — with a slender, sinuous body that lets it follow prey down the burrows of voles and mice. It has short legs, a long neck, a short tail, and a high metabolic rate that forces it to hunt almost continuously; it kills with a bite to the nape of the neck, and it is famously fearless, attacking quarry far larger than itself. Its senses are extraordinarily keen: eyes and ears tuned to the smallest movement in grass, and a muzzle crowded with sensitive vibrissae (whiskers) that map its world in the dark. When alarmed it discharges a pungent musk from its anal scent glands — the odour that, in the folk-etymological arithmetic of the Germanic languages, gave it its name.

The word in context (The Hobbit). In J. R. R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit, Chapter 2 (‘Roast Mutton’), the narrator says of Bilbo’s prim, quiet approach toward the trolls’ fire that “I don’t suppose even a weasel would have stirred a whisker at it.” The choice of the weasel is precise: no small animal of the English countryside is quicker to detect, and then to react to, the faintest disturbance, and the whisker — the tactile vibrissa at the muzzle — is the most delicate organ of its surveillance. To say that a weasel would not have stirred a whisker is to say that Bilbo produced no perceptible sound, scent, or movement at all; it is hyperbole of the highest order, and it transforms the hobbit’s comic primness into something almost elvish.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. A stoat is larger and turns white in winter; a polecat is heavier, dark-furred, and muskier; a marten is a tree-dwelling mustelid of the pine forest; a ferret is the domesticated hunting form of the polecat; a mink is a semi-aquatic mustelid farmed for fur; a badger is a large, burrowing, flat-bodied mustelid; an otter is the fully aquatic mustelid. Figuratively, weasel sits among sneak, snake, fox, rat, and cur as a term of contempt for the sly and evasive.
