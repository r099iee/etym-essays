- (1500s) - The verb issue first means "to go or come out, to emerge": troops issue from gates, water issues from rocks — a plain, living verb of emergence.
- (1500s–1600s) - Extends to sending forth: kings issue commands and proclamations; books are "issued" (published); the phrase "issue forth" becomes a staple of high prose.
- (1600s–1700s) - Financial and military senses solidify: money is issued (put into circulation), troops are issued supplies; "issue" also means "result from" (from the noun's "outcome" sense).
- (1700s–1800s) - The bureaucratic age: passports, warrants, stamps, banknotes, and rations are issued by institutions; the noun issue gains "point in question" (what is at issue).
- (1900s) - Official issuing dominates: statements, visas, shares, warnings; the literal "issued from" (emerged) retreats to literary and historical prose.
- (1937) - Tolkien uses the archaic-emergence sense in The Hobbit: "He issued from the Gate" — the dragon's coming forth made grand by the old verb.
- (today) - Issued = standard verb of official publication and distribution; the emergence sense survives as a deliberate archaism for epic and formal prose; the noun issue has even become colloquial for "problem".
