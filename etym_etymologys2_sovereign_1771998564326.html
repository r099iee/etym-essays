PIE *uper / *upér 'over, above' (reconstructed; c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE) → Latin super 'above, over, beyond' (attested 3rd c. BCE) → Late/Vulgar Latin *superānus 'pertaining to what is above; chief, principal, supreme' (formed with the adjectival suffix -ānus; not directly attested in classical literature, but securely reconstructed and confirmed by later glosses recording superanus 'one set above, chief') → Old French soverain, sovrain 'supreme; sovereign (ruler)' (11th-12th c.) → Anglo-French soverain (12th-13th c.) → Middle English soverain, sovereign 'supreme; a monarch' (13th c., borrowed through Anglo-French and Old French) → Modern English sovereign 'possessing supreme or ultimate power; a monarch' (14th c. onwards).

Note on the spelling debate: The modern spelling with internal -g- and -eign is not the genetic path from super; it reflects medieval folk-association with English reign (from Latin rēgnum 'kingdom, rule', PIE *h₃reǵ- 'to straighten, rule'). Because sovereign and reign both named royal power, scribes reshaped soverain toward sovereign, adding a silent g that has nothing to do with the word's ancestry; the true descent is from super alone.
