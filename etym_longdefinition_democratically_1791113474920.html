Democratically is the manner adverb of democratic: in a way that embodies democracy — by popular vote, with political equality, or more broadly in an egalitarian, inclusive, unhierarchical manner.

The central procedural sense is political: chosen, decided, governed or organized through democratic institutions such as elections, majorities, representation and accountability. One speaks of democratically elected leaders, a democratically adopted constitution, power transferred democratically. Here the adverb modifies verbs of choosing and ruling, asserting legitimacy through popular consent rather than inheritance, force or appointment. It is the antonym of autocratically, dictatorially, arbitrarily.

  • Procedural-political sense: by democratic process. This is precise and institutional, used in law, journalism and election monitoring: democratically accountable, democratically legitimate. It implies suffrage, competition, transparency and reversibility.
  • Egalitarian-social sense: in an equal, inclusive, non-elitist manner, without voting. Examples: to share food democratically, to run a family democratically, to dress democratically. Here democracy becomes ethos: everyone counts the same, no privilege, plain fellowship. Durant's sentence — an animal accepted almost democratically into human society as comrade — is this sense pushed to affectionate irony: the beast becomes a citizen of labour.
  • Distributive sense: widely spread among the people, accessible to all. Economists write of wealth distributed more democratically, culture made democratically available, meaning diffused rather than monopolized. This overlaps with democratization of technology or luxury.

Polysemy grew by broadening from constitution to character. Once democratic meant praiseworthily equal as well as technically elective — democratic manners, democratic dress, democratic schools — the adverb naturally followed. The bridge is American and French revolutionary ideology: democracy not just as ballot box but as social condition without aristocracy. Tocqueville's democratic social state made this explicit.

Usage is almost always positive in modern liberal discourse, though critics use it ironically: democratically decided to censor can be sardonic. Collocations include democratically elected, chosen, decided, governed, organized, structured, run. Position is flexible: they democratically elected her / they elected her democratically. Register is formal-political in sense one, warm-informal in sense two.

In short, democratically asks how: by the people, as equals, for all — whether in parliament or, playfully, in a camp where a tamed beast joins the circle as comrade.

Further nuance: democratically can modify almost any verb of collective life — to deliberate, to protest, to decentralize democratically — always signalling that legitimacy flows upward from participants rather than downward from command. In education it praises student voice; in workplaces it praises flat hierarchies and co-determination; in families it praises negotiation over patriarchy. Critics sometimes allege inflation: when everything kind becomes democratic, the procedural core blurs. Defenders reply that the ethos and procedure share a root conviction that each person counts as one and none as more than one.
