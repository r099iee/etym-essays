dampness — the state or condition of being slightly wet. In Maspic's remark — "But there's certainly a touch of dampness in the air" — the word carries an almost scientific, diagnostic weight. This is not a casual weather observation but a clue-reader's deduction: the party is descending into the Chaur of Mars, a place the wild vortlups avoid, and dampness is the first tangible evidence that water, and therefore (in Maspic's logic) life, "perhaps of a dangerous kind", exists beneath the arid surface. The tone is cool, sensory, and foreboding: dampness here is neither agreeable nor merely unpleasant but a neutral physical index — the feel of humidity on the skin, the direct opposite of the bone-dry desert above. Unlike moisture or wetness, dampness suggests something pervasive, slightly clammy, and persistent: an atmosphere that has absorbed water into its very substance, as a cave wall weeps and cool air clings.

The core, primary sense is simple: dampness is a moderate, perceptible wetness — less than saturation, more than dryness. It is the quality of a cellar wall after rain, of a towel left folded overnight, of morning air before the sun burns off the dew. Crucially, dampness is felt rather than seen: it registers as a chill on the skin, a heaviness in the breath, a clinging to cloth and stone. This "condition" sense is what makes it a state noun: dampness is not an event (like a splash) but a standing property of a place, an object, or the air itself. It is the abstract noun built on the adjective damp plus the Germanic suffix -ness, which converts a quality into a state.

Polysemy radiates outward from this core in several directions:

  • Physical / atmospheric sense: dampness as moisture suspended in or absorbed by air, soil, walls, and fabric — the standard everyday sense, dominant in weather talk, building science, and domestic life ("the dampness of the English climate", "damp rising up the walls").
  • Mining and industrial sense (historical): from the same root family, the noun damp in mining jargon meant a noxious or explosive underground gas — choke-damp (carbon dioxide, which suffocates) and fire-damp (methane, which explodes). This is a striking semantic bridge: the word that once named a deadly vapour in a pit is the ancestor of the mild adjective "slightly wet", and the fear of "the damps" left a permanent trace in the verb to damp = "to deaden or extinguish" (a flame, sound, or enthusiasm).
  • Verb senses: to damp (to moisten; also to deaden) and to dampen (to make damp; to reduce or dull — "to dampen spirits", "to dampen enthusiasm"). The figurative verb sense — suppressing energy or feeling — is a modern metaphoric shift from the physical damping of sound and fire to the psychological damping of emotion.
  • Technical / scientific usages: in building science, dampness is a measurable defect (rising damp, penetrating damp, condensation); in meteorology it overlaps with humidity; in acoustics and engineering, "damping" (with the same root) is the reduction of oscillation amplitude — a purely technical jargon sense far removed from wetness.
  • Figurative / literary sense: dampness as a mood-word — the "dampness" of a tomb, a cellar, or a gothic ruin connotes stagnation, enclosure, and faint menace; the word can describe a chill of fear or despondency ("a dampness of the spirits"), a metaphoric transfer from the physical clamminess of skin to a felt emotional atmosphere.

Register and collocation complete the picture. Dampness is standard, plain, and slightly formal; it collocates with enclosed and subterranean spaces ("the dampness of the cave/cellar/tomb"), with weather ("the dampness of the air"), and with health (the historical fear that damp air carried fevers and consumption). Among its synonyms the boundaries are precise: moisture is the broad neutral term, often desirable; wetness implies visible saturation; humidity is the quantitative meteorological measure of water vapour in the air; dankness adds cold, gloom, and the smell of mould; clamminess is the bodily, slippery dampness of skin and palms; mugginess is the oppressive warmth of damp air. Dampness alone stops short of soaking while exceeding dryness — the word for water that has become a condition rather than an event.
