Rune is a word whose entire history is a double life: it began as a term of secrecy and whisper, became the name of an alphabet, and has ended as both a modern programmer's unit of text and a token of mystery. No word better illustrates the semantic bridge from oral secrecy to written lore — a bridge walked by a single Germanic word-family over more than two millennia. 1. The deep root: from roar to whisper The chain begins in the reconstructed Indo-European root *reu- / *rū-, a base associated with vocal sound — roaring, bellowing, murmuring, and, crucially, whispering. Handbooks differ on the exact gloss and shape, and the step from "make a noise" to "whisper a secret" is culturally inferred rather than directly attested, but the direction is natural: quiet speech is how secrets and counsels travel. From this base the Germanic peoples built a word of enormous importance: Proto-Germanic *rūnō, meaning "secret, mystery, confidential counsel, secret speech." The word is attested across the whole Germanic world — Gothic rūna, Old Norse rún, Old English rūn — and it also spread beyond Germanic into the Baltic and Finnic languages (Finnish runo, "poem, song," is a loan from this family), evidence of how widely the concept of hidden counsel travelled. 2. The fateful semantic step: from secret to letter The Germanic peoples possessed a distinctive alphabetic script — angular, straight-stroked letters adapted for cutting into wood, bone, and stone. Because the letters were hard to carve and were used by a literate elite, the same word that meant "secret, mystery" attached itself to the characters themselves: a rune was a letter that was also a secret, writing as exclusive lore. This double meaning is visible in the earliest records. Old English rūn means both "counsel, mystery" and "a runic character," and Old Norse rún appears in the sagas and Eddic poetry as alphabet and spell alike — cut to communicate, or cut to charm. The union of secrecy and script is the single most important fact about the word's history, and it is the reason runes acquired their magical aura: writing that ordinary people could not read was, to them, a form of hidden knowledge. 3. Middle English: the two lives continue In Middle English the word survives in two forms and two senses. The secret-sense continues in roun and in the verb rounen "to whisper," preserved in the poetry and in set phrases about secret counsel. The letter-sense, meanwhile, persists wherever the old script is remembered — largely in northern and antiquarian contexts, since the roman alphabet long ago displaced runic writing for everyday use. The word never quite died, but it retreated to the edges of the vocabulary, awaiting revival. 4. The revival and specialization of the modern word From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries onward, antiquarian scholarship rediscovered the runic inscriptions of Scandinavia, Germany, and Britain, and the word rune was revived as the standard name for the letters of the Germanic alphabets. The Romantic movement then re-mystified the script: runes became the archetype of ancient, arcane writing, and fiction — Gothic novels, heroic fantasy, and finally Tolkien — layered the mystique back onto the alphabet without erasing its alphabetic core. The modern word thus holds both senses at once: a real letter of a real ancient alphabet, and a mark charged with hidden meaning. 5. The modern technical sense The twenty-first century gave the word its most surprising continuation. In the Go programming language, a "rune" is a Unicode code point — the integer value representing one character — and the term was chosen precisely because a rune is a single atomic unit of writing. The ancient word for a secret carved letter has become a programmer's name for a character. The arc is complete: from whisper, to secret counsel, to carved letter, to magic, to the smallest unit of digital text. 6. The runes on Thrór's map In the scene from The Hobbit, Gandalf points to "that rune on the West side" and the red runes of the map, reading what others skim past — the mark of a hidden passage. Tolkien, a philologist who knew the runic inheritance of Old English and Old Norse intimately, uses the word in both of its lives at once: the runes are real, readable letters of a writing system (he had designed the dwarf-runes himself), and they are also secrets — writing that keeps a door hidden until it is read. The rune on the map is the double meaning of the word made concrete: a letter that is also a secret.
