1. BLOTTED (past tense and past participle of the verb "to blot")

The core sense is to stain, smear, or mark with a blot: a dark, irregular spot of discoloration, most classically one made by ink, mud, paint, or blood seeping into a surface. In the literary context before us — "The zoorth had blotted out the sun" — the verb carries its fullest phrasal weight: not mere dimming or covering, but total effacement. The sand-storm absorbs the sun's disk the way ink soaks into a blotter, and the whole ancient slope falls into a twilight "colored like dried blood." The image is deliberately tactile and viscous: a blot seeps, spreads, and stains; what is blotted is not hidden but swallowed.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • To blot out (phrasal verb) — to obliterate, erase, or wipe from existence: a line struck from a page, a name deleted from a record, a memory or sin expunged, a landmark or heavenly body extinguished. This is the sense used in the passage and the one that dominates modern usage ("the clouds blotted out the moon").
  • To blot (absorbent sense) — to dry by absorption, as blotting paper takes up wet ink; hence the noun blotter (a pad of absorbent paper, 19th c.).
  • Figurative / moral sense — to stain a reputation or character: "a blot on one's name," "a blot on the escutcheon" (a stain upon family honor, drawn from heraldic imagery of a blotted coat of arms). "To blot one's copybook" means to spoil a previously good record by one bad act (19th c., from school exercises written in copybooks).
  • Noun "blot" — the spot itself, and by extension any disgrace or blemish: "a blot on the landscape" (something ugly or out of place), "a blot on civilization."
  • Games sense — in backgammon, a "blot" is an exposed single piece vulnerable to capture (16th c. usage), a specialized survival of the "unprotected mark" idea.
  • Technical / scientific — "blotting" in molecular biology (Southern, Northern, Western blots, 1970s) names the transfer of DNA, RNA, or protein onto a membrane — an echo of the ink-and-paper blot, punningly preserved in the laboratory's vocabulary.


2. ERE

An archaic preposition, conjunction, and adverb meaning "before, previous to, earlier than (in time)." In the passage — "the sun ere they reached the bottom of the ancient slope" — it is the conjunction "before." The word is a deliberate archaism: once the plain everyday word of Old English for "before," it survives today only in elevated, poetic, scriptural, and mock-archaic registers ("ere the dawn," "ere long," "ere now," "ere ever").

  • Primary sense — before (of time), used as a preposition ("ere nightfall"), a conjunction ("ere we began"), or an adverb ("ere now" = before this time).
  • Fixed phrases — "ere long" (soon, before much time passes), "ere now," "erewhile" (formerly, a while ago), "erenow."
  • Register — the word is fossilized in ordinary speech, where "before" has wholly replaced it; "ere" now signals the language of the King James Bible, epic, fantasy prose, and historical fiction, lending a sentence a slow, solemn gravity.
  • Rare / obsolete derivatives — "erer" (earlier), "ereyesterday" (the day before yesterday), "erewhile"; the related "erstwhile" (former) descends from the superlative of the same root.


3. SINISTER

Primary modern sense: giving the impression of evil, harm, or menace; ominous, threatening, foreboding — "a sinister smile," "a sinister motive," "a sinister shadow." Secondary, technical senses descend from the literal Latin meaning "left":

  • Primary sense — threatening or suggesting harm or evil; ominous and menacing in manner or appearance.
  • Heraldic sense — "sinister" denotes the bearer's left (the viewer's right) side of a shield; a "bend sinister" is the diagonal band from the bearer's upper right to lower left, historically used as the mark of illegitimacy — the base-born child's bar across the arms.
  • Anatomical / technical — "sinistral" (left-handed, or turned to the left), "sinistrorse" (spiraling leftward, of shells and climbing plants), "sinistrodextral" (left-to-right), "sinistre" in old medicine.
  • Augural / astronomical — the left quarter of the sky, where omens appeared; from Roman augury, where the left could be favorable or ill-omened depending on the augur's orientation.
  • Folkloric / cultural bridge — the historical bridge runs from "left" to "evil" through ancient associations of the left hand with awkwardness, impurity, and ill luck (Latin sinister also meant "awkward, perverse, unlucky"); English imported this moralized package in the 15th century and has since lost the literal "left" sense except in the technical registers above.
