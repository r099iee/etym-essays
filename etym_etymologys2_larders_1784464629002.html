LARDERS is the plural of LARDER. The word is a borrowing via Old French from Latin; no secure PIE ancestry is established for the Latin base. One linear chain. 1. Latin lardum (also laridum) “bacon, pig fat, lard” (attested in Classical Latin; a humble food-word of everyday register). Deeper origin: not securely reconstructed — no accepted PIE equation; some handbooks float a link to a PIE root *las- “to be greedy, desire” (cf. Greek λιλαίομαι liláomai “to long for”), but the connection is speculative and not established. 2. Medieval Latin lardārium “the place where salted meat and bacon are kept” (storage-derivative of lardum; the -arium suffix marks a place — cf. cellarium). 3. Old French lardier “meat store, bacon tub, place for lard” (attested from the 12th–13th c.; also lardoir ‘larding needle’ from the verb larder ‘to lard’). 4. Anglo-French / Middle English larder “room for storing meat” (attested c. 1300). 5. Modern English larder “cool food store” (14th c. onward); sense generalised beyond meat by the 17th–18th c. 6. English larders (plural) — the card's exact inflected form — LARDERS.
