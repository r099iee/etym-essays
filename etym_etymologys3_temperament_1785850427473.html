FROM STRETCHING TO MIXING

Temperament's deepest attested history runs back to Latin and a root whose meaning is not "emotion" at all but measurement and mixture. The traditional reconstruction begins with a Proto-Indo-European root *temp-, "to stretch, span" — the idea being that time and due measure are conceived as a stretched span, something drawn out and measured — though modern authorities (de Vaan) treat the ultimate ancestry of Latin tempus as uncertain, so this PIE stage is traditional rather than proven. What is secure is the Roman development. From tempus, "time, season, due measure, right moment", Latin formed temperāre, "to mix in due proportion, to moderate, to restrain" — a verb of blending and keeping within due measure, used of mixing wine with water (the greatest of Roman domestic arts), of regulating the seasons, of restraining anger. The word's whole psychology lives in that verb: the ideal was temperantia, the self-restraint of keeping everything in due proportion, which English knows as temperance.

THE HUMORAL THEORY OF CHARACTER

From temperāre Latin formed the noun temperamentum, "due mixture, proper proportion", and medical science seized on it. Greek medicine — the Hippocratic school, systematized by Galen in the second century CE — taught that the body is governed by four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) corresponding to four qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry), and that health and character depend on the mixture of these humors. A person's temperamentum was literally their humor mixture; the ideal was the well-tempered, balanced blend, and the four dominant types were the sanguine (blood: cheerful, optimistic), the choleric (yellow bile: hot-tempered, ambitious), the melancholic (black bile: gloomy, thoughtful), and the phlegmatic (phlegm: calm, sluggish). This humoral theory governed European medicine, astrology, and characterology for more than fifteen hundred years: the medieval world read temperament off the humors, the Renaissance theatre — most famously Ben Jonson's "comedy of humours" (Every Man in His Humour, 1598) — built characters from them, and Elizabethan and seventeenth-century English writers used temperament as a medical word meaning the bodily constitution that resulted from the humoral blend.

THE WORD ENTERS ENGLISH

English took temperament in the fifteenth century, from Latin temperamentum with reinforcement from Middle French tempérament. In sixteenth- and seventeenth-century English the word is firmly medical: it names the proportion of the four humors in the body and the character that flows from it. But the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries began to loosen the medical tie. Moral-psychological essayists used temperament for the inherent constitution of mind and passion — the given emotional shape of a person, still understood physiologically but increasingly as a matter of character rather than of bile. The word's meaning drifted from "the physical mixture" to "the resulting natural disposition", and this is the bridge to everything modern.

MUSIC, METALS, AND THE MODERN MIND

Meanwhile the word was crossing from medicine into two very different technical worlds, both carrying the old "proportion" idea. In music, from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, "temperament" named the system of tuning in which intervals are slightly adjusted so that an instrument can play in all keys — the great controversy between just temperament and equal temperament, settled in practice by Bach's The Well-Tempered Clavier (1722), which proved the merits of tempered tuning. In materials science, the same word family tempered steel and glass: heat treatment that gives metal its "temper" or "temperament" — its proportionate hardness and elasticity. In the nineteenth century, Romanticism made "artistic temperament" a byword for the volatile, sensitive nature of the creative person, and writers and anthropologists spoke of "national temperament" — the supposed character of a people. In the twentieth century, psychology claimed the word for science: temperament became the biologically based, early-appearing core of personality, the dimensions of activity, reactivity, and emotionality visible in infants, on which experience and learning build the fuller structure of personality. The everyday sense — the calm, pragmatic temperament of the card's leader — is the direct descendant of the ancient "mixture", with the four humors long forgotten but the idea of a given, balanced emotional nature intact.

SOUND AND FAMILY

Phonetically the word has been regular in English: Latin temperamentum (tem-pe-ra-men-tum) passed through Middle French tempérament into English as temperament in the fifteenth century, and modern pronunciation /ˈtemprəmənt/ simply syncopates the medial vowel, so that the second "e" of "temper" is weakened and nearly lost, giving the four-beat /ˈtem.prə.mənt/. The spelling preserves the full Latin sequence. The family is a distinguished one: temper (verb and noun), temperature, temperance, and tempera (the egg-based paint, so called because pigments are "tempered" or mixed with the yolk) all descend from temperāre; tempest is related in the end — it comes from Latin tempestas, "season, weather, storm", from tempus — though it has long been a word of storms rather than of measure. All of them preserve the Roman image of due proportion — the image that turned the stretching of time and the mixing of wine with water into a theory of human character.
