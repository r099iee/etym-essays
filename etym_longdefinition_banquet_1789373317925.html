Banquet is a culturally and historically resonant noun and verb denoting an elaborate, formal feast prepared for a large gathering of guests, typically convened to celebrate a momentous occasion, commemorate a historic milestone, or formally honor a distinguished individual. Far more than a mere act of communal eating, a banquet is a highly ritualized social institution characterized by ceremonial decorum, culinary extravagance, structured courses, and formal speechmaking or toasts. In an extended and figurative sense, the word evokes an overwhelming abundance or sumptuous presentation of any intellectual, aesthetic, or spiritual delights—an "intellectual banquet" of philosophical discourse, or a "visual banquet" of masterly paintings.

In literary and biographical history—as captured in the source passage concerning Marcel Proust's formative years in 1890s Paris—Le Banquet was the title of a prestigious, short-lived literary revue founded in March 1892 by the young Proust alongside his brilliant classmates from the Lycée Condorcet, including Robert Dreyfus, Daniel Halévy, Fernand Gregh, Robert de Flers, and Léon Blum. The adolescent intellectuals deliberately named their journal after Plato's celebrated dialogue The Symposium (traditionally titled Le Banquet in French). In Plato's work, Socrates, Aristophanes, Alcibiades, and other Athenian luminaries gather for a celebratory drinking party where, in lieu of drunken revelry, each guest delivers an eloquent oration on the nature, origin, and philosophy of love (*Erōs*). For Proust and his circle, the title encapsulated their ideal of high-minded aesthetic commensality, where literary sketches, philosophical meditations, and artistic critiques were savored like rare vintage wines. It was within the pages of Le Banquet that Proust published his earliest critical portraits and prose vignettes, later collected in his debut volume, Les Plaisirs et les Jours (1896).

The term displays extensive polysemy, subtle distinctions of register, and specialized technical applications across varied domains:

  • Ceremonial and State Feasts (Primary Diplomatic Sense): A highly orchestrated, protocol-governed formal dinner hosted by heads of state, monarchs, or academic institutions. Renowned examples include sovereign state banquets hosted at Buckingham Palace or the Élysée, and the world-famous Nobel Banquet held annually in the Blue Hall of Stockholm City Hall, where laureates dine with the Swedish royal family and deliver formal laureate addresses.
  • Renaissance Dessert and Confectionery Course (Historical Sense): In Tudor, Elizabethan, and Stuart England (16th to early 17th century), a banquet (or "banket") referred specifically and exclusively to the delicate, luxurious final course of a meal—what modern diners call dessert. Consisting of marchpane (marzipan), candied fruits, spiced wafers, marmalades, jellies, and spiced sweet wines (such as hippocras), this sweet collation was served away from the greasy smells of the great dining hall in a specially constructed, richly decorated garden pavilion or rooftop turret known as a banqueting house (the most magnificent surviving monument of this tradition being Inigo Jones's classical Banqueting House at Whitehall, London).
  • Political Vehicle for Democratic Reform ("The Banquets Campaign"): In nineteenth-century French political history, the Campagne des banquets (1847–1848) was a nationwide series of large, ticketed political dinners organized by moderate republicans and liberal reformers to circumvent King Louis-Philippe's strict royal bans on political rallies and public assemblies. Reformers delivered fiery reformist speeches disguised as dinner toasts; the French government's heavy-handed suppression of the grand Paris banquet scheduled for February 22, 1848, was the immediate catalyst that ignited the Revolution of 1848, toppling the July Monarchy and birthing the Second French Republic.
  • Hospitality Industry and Event Management (Commercial Jargon): In modern catering and hotel management, "banqueting" refers to the high-volume operational division dedicated to servicing private corporate events, wedding receptions, gala fundraisers, and convention luncheons. Professionals speak of "banquet event orders" (BEOs), "banquet captains," and dedicated "banquet halls" designed with movable partitions and warming stations.
  • Aesthetic, Sensory, and Intellectual Abundance (Figurative Sense): A rich, lavish feast offered to the mind or senses. Literary critics speak of a novelist offering an "intellectual banquet" of ideas, while art historians describe a gallery exhibition as a "visual banquet" overflowing with color and vitality.
  • Transitive and Intransitive Verb: To entertain guests with a sumptuous feast, or to participate in luxurious, celebratory dining (e.g., "the ambassadors banqueted the victorious generals until dawn").

The historical semantic bridge that carried banquet from an ordinary wooden bench to a grand royal feast is one of the most delightful trajectories in Romance lexicography. Its root traces to the Proto-Germanic noun *bankiz ("bench, mound"), which entered northern Italy during the sixth-century Lombard invasions. In Old Italian, banco denoted a wooden bench, table, or counter (whence modern financial *banks*, where money-changers sat at wooden benches). By affixing the diminutive suffix -etto, Italian formed banchetto, literally meaning a "small bench." In medieval Italian dining customs, light between-meal collations, snacks, or informal drinks were taken while sitting casually on small benches, rather than at the solemn, formal high table. Consequently, banchetto transferred metonymically from the bench itself to the light repast eaten upon it. When Renaissance French royalty and nobility encountered Italian court dining during the Italian Wars of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, they enthusiastically borrowed the Italian word into Middle French as banquet. Under the lavish patronage of the Valois court and the culinary influence of Catherine de' Medici, French aristocratic culture elevated the modest informal collation into a spectacular display of culinary artistry, sugar sculpture, and royal magnificence. English subsequently imported the word, initially preserving both the sense of an intimate sweet dessert course and expanding into the modern grand ceremonial feast.
