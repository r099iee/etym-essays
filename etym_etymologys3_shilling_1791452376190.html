The history of shilling begins in the reconstructed vocabulary of the Germanic peoples, where a word for a measured portion of value already existed before any coin bore the name. The common Germanic form is usually reconstructed as *skillingaz, and its descendants are spread across the whole family: Old English scilling, Old Saxon and Old High German scilling, Middle Dutch schellinc, Old Norse skillingr, and Gothic skilliggs. That breadth tells us the word is older than the separate coinages of Europe; it belonged to a shared northern European vocabulary of weighed and counted wealth, and it travelled with the languages as they separated.

The "division" hypothesis. The most widely accepted explanation connects the word with a root meaning "to cut, divide", reconstructed as PIE *(s)kel-. On this account a shilling was originally a slice of a larger unit, a partition of a pound, a mark or a herd, and the term belonged to the same conceptual world as score, share and lot. The hypothesis is attractive because it explains the word's earliest function: in every early Germanic society the shilling is a unit of account before it is an object. It is money as arithmetic, not money as metal.

Anglo-Saxon accounting and the wergild. In the laws of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms the shilling is everywhere, and almost never as a coin. It prices injury, theft and homicide. The Mercian and Wessex codes set a ceorl's life at 200 shillings and a thegn's at 1,200, with intermediate ranks and graded tariffs for wounds to teeth, fingers and ribs. These are not sums anyone counted out in silver; they are legal fictions, conversion rates between honour and compensation. The shilling thus entered English not from the marketplace but from the courtroom, and its earliest English connotations are forensic and hierarchical rather than commercial.

  • Regional instability: the value of the accounting shilling varied by kingdom and by century, commonly reckoned at four or five pence in some regions and twelve in others, which is why the later standardisation to twelve pence was an act of state rather than a natural fact.
  • Scots divergence: the Scots pound was worth one twelfth of sterling after the medieval exchange was fixed, so a Scots shilling came to equal an English penny, giving later English speakers the phrase "a Scotch shilling" for something contemptibly small.
  • Continental parallels: the Norse skillingr and the Gothic skilliggs show the same drift from accounting unit to small coin, a shared Germanic trajectory rather than an English peculiarity.

From reckoning unit to silver coin. England minted no shilling until the reign of Henry VII, when a large silver coin worth twelve pence, often called the testoon, was issued around 1504. From that moment the word acquired a body. Tudor debasements, Elizabethan recoinage and the Stuart experiments in milled coinage turned the shilling into one of the most handled objects in English life, and the coin's silver content became a political subject in its own right. By the eighteenth century the shilling was the standard unit of everyday transaction, the price of a dinner, a seat in the pit, a hackney ride across London.

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. This is the period in which the shilling becomes socially eloquent. "Taking the King's shilling" names the recruiting sergeant's token, and the phrase carries the whole coercive machinery of the press gang and the volunteer regiment. "Cutting off with a shilling" names the legal trick by which a disappointed heir is left a token so that the will cannot be challenged. The Victorian shilling then generated its own cultural forms: the shilling shocker, a sensational novella sold cheaply and condemned as poison for the young; the shilling magazine; the shilling seat in the gallery. In Dickens and in the periodical press the shilling is the unit of respectable poverty, the sum that separates comfort from want.

Decimalisation and after. The shilling survived two world wars but not the arithmetic of a decimal age. On 15 February 1971 the United Kingdom replaced pounds, shillings and pence with pounds and new pence, and the shilling became, in effect, five new pence before being phased out of circulation. The coin vanished, but the word did not. It persisted in idiom, in historical fiction, in the phrase "not the full shilling", and above all in the currencies of East Africa, where the East African shilling had been issued from 1921 and where Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Somalia and South Sudan still call their money by that name.

Sound and spelling. The most conspicuous change is the palatalisation of Old English sc- to the modern sh-, the same shift seen in ship, shall and fish. The final -g- weakened and was lost in pronunciation, leaving the two-syllable shilling, and the spelling settled in the Middle English period. The stressed vowel is short, /ɪ/, which distinguishes it from the long vowel of shining and gives the word its compact, clipped feel.

  • Semantic drift: accounting unit, then silver coin, then small change, then historical relic and post-colonial currency name.
  • Connotative drift: legal compensation, then everyday value, then token trifle, then affectionate archaism.
  • Literary attestations: the shilling runs from the wergild clauses of Anglo-Saxon law through Chaucer's pilgrims, Shakespeare's taverns, Dickens's counting-houses and Wodehouse's drawing-rooms, a continuous presence in the record of English social life.
