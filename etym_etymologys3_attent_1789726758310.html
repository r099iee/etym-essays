The story of ATTENT begins not with listening but with pulling: with the Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch." Pokorny lists it among the great extension roots, and de Vaan traces Latin tendere — to stretch a bow, a tent-cloth, a hand — directly to it. Roman soldiers tended tents because tent-cloth is stretched hide; archers tended bows because the string is stretched; minds, by metaphor, could be stretched too. Prefix the directional ad-, "toward," and you get attendere: literally to stretch the mind toward a thing, to lean the senses forward. Its past participle attentus meant both "stretched toward" and, by completed metaphor, "heedful, intent, expectant." Already in Cicero and Livy, to be attentus is to be braced: ears up, eyes forward, soul aimed.

Old French inherits this as atent / attent (12th century), with the same double edge of waiting and wanting: atente is expectation, a lover's or vassal's held breath. The Anglo-Normans carry it across the Channel, and Middle English (late 14th century) records attent in devotional, courtly and scholastic prose — attent ears at sermon, attent scholars at book, attent lovers at command. Chaucerian and Gowerian English also builds the longer attentif from Old French attentif, and that longer form slowly wins in prose because Middle English prefers its -ive adjectives for moral qualities. By the 16th century attentive is the neutral word for polite, sustained regard, while bare attent retreats into verse, where its single hammered syllable fits metre and sounds older, sterner, more northern.

  • PIE to Italic — the stretching metaphor: physical stretching of cloth, cord and limb supplies the image for mental attention; to attend remains, etymologically, to stretch toward.
  • Latin — moralising the stretch: attendere / attentus acquires ethical weight: the good citizen, soldier and auditor is attentus; the inattentive one is slack, lax, morally loose.
  • Old French — waiting as meaning: atente (expectation, Modern French attente) shows how "stretching toward" slides into "waiting for"; intentness becomes imminence.
  • Middle English — devotion and diligence: monastic and courtly texts use attent for prayerful focus and obedient service; the connotation is warm, approved, diligent.
  • Early Modern — bifurcation: attentive takes over everyday courtesy ("attentive host," "attentive reader"); attent survives as poetic compression in Spenserian and later antiquarian diction, valued for hardness and brevity.
  • 1960 — Hughes's cold revival: in "Thrushes" (Lupercal) Hughes wrenches the warm courtesy clean off the word. Paired with sleek and coiled steel, attent becomes pure predatory electronics: no kindness, only trigger. Critics rightly hear the anti-sentimental programme — Baker, Hughes and the new nature writing refusing to sentimentalise song while forgetting killing.

Phonetically the path is unremarkable and therefore secure: PIE *ten- with nasal-infix present *tn-neu- yields Italic *tende-; Latin attendere keeps the double -tt- from assimilation of ad-tendere; Old French simplifies to atent (Modern French attentif, attente with restored -tt- by learned spelling); Middle English borrows both spellings atent / attent. Received Pronunciation today gives /əˈtɛnt/, with reduced first vowel and checked final cluster — a sound-shape that itself feels stopped, checked, held. No serious etymological debate surrounds this chain; OED, Etymonline and de Vaan agree on *ten-, and the only nuance worth stating is stylistic: Hughes's attent is not a different lexeme from attentive but its deliberately beheaded twin, shortened for terror.

In contemporary perception the word is thus double-layered: a dead everyday adjective resurrected as a live poetic shock. To call thrushes attent is to say that nature's attention is not our kindness but its hunger — the same stretched nerve, tuned to a darker station.
