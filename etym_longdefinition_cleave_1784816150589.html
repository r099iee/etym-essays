CLEAVE — verb (transitive and intransitive; past cleaved, cleft, or clave; past participle cleaved, cleft, or cloven). Core meaning: to split, sever, or cut cleanly through something with force or a sharp blade; to divide along the grain or natural line of separation. The word is distinct, in origin and meaning, from the homograph cleave meaning "to adhere, cling" (as in "to cleave to one's principles").

In the dwarves' bitter, archaic song over the sword recovered from the trolls' hoard, the wish that the blade may soon cleave goblins once again uses the verb in its full Old English force: to split, sever, or hew through something — here, an enemy's body — with a single decisive stroke of a sharp blade. The word is chosen for its epic resonance: cleave is the verb of heroic weaponry, the sword's action of parting flesh and bone as the axe parts wood along its grain. Unlike the neutral split, it implies force, intent, and an edge; unlike cut, it suggests a clean, violent division made through something resistant. Within the context the tone is solemn, martial, and slightly archaic — the diction of saga and old song rather than of modern report — and the phrase carries a grim satisfaction, a promise of vengeance held over from the goblin wars. The very sound of the word, with its long vowel and hard final consonant, reinforces the image of a heavy blade biting clean through.

Full polysemy and grammatical range.

  • To split or sever with force (primary sense): to divide something resistant along its length with a blade or axe — to cleave wood, to cleave a skull, to cleave an enemy's shield in two.
  • Nautical and poetic: to cut or pass through: a ship cleaves the waves; an arrow cleaves the air — the object is the medium itself, divided as the thing passes.
  • Intransitive: to part or open by a natural line of division: wood cleaves along the grain; a rock cleaves under frost — no agent required, the split happens along a plane of weakness.
  • The homographic verb "to cleave" (adhere): a completely different word from Old English clifian "to cling, stick," meaning to remain attached or faithful ("to cleave to one's husband," "to cleave unto the Lord"). It is etymologically unconnected to the splitting verb; the two have collided in sound.
  • Derived forms and compounds: cloven (past participle, surviving in "cloven hoof"); cleft (a fissure or split, and the past tense/past participle); cleavage (the act or capacity of cleaving, and, in mineralogy, the tendency of a crystal to split along planes).
  • Technical jargon: cleavage in geology and mineralogy (1805); cleavage in biology, the division of cells; cleavage plane, cleavable rock; in modern figurative usage, "cleavage" for the line of division in society or opinion.
  • Figurative and archaic use: to cleave an issue (to divide it decisively), "cleft in twain," the Bible's "cleave the altar" — the verb belongs to elevated, ceremonial, or epic registers.

Semantic bridges. The splitting sense descends directly from the Old English strong verb cleofan, a verb of heroic weaponry native to the vocabulary of sword and axe. The separate "cling" sense descends from Old English clifian, from a different root meaning "to stick" (the root of clay and glue); the two words began to converge on the same sound in Middle English as the splitting verb weakened, and the confusion has been a standing puzzle of English ever since. The technical senses — cleavage in geology and in biology — are late applications of the image of splitting along natural lines, and the modern figurative uses (cleavage in society, the cleaving of an argument) are recent extensions of that image.

Register and usage notes.

  • Literary/archaic: cleave in its splitting sense is now strongly literary and epic; it signals a deliberately ancient, saga-like voice.
  • The cling sense survives in formal and religious prose: "to cleave to," "cleave unto."
  • Grammar: cleave is a defective verb with a contested past — cleaved (regular), cleft, and clave all occur; cloven is the archaic past participle, fixed in "cloven hoof."
  • Distinction: cleave (split) has nothing to do with cleave (stick); the homography is coincidence of sound over centuries.
