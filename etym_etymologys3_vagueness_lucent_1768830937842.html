I. VAGUENESS: from wandering to woolly

The modern English noun vagueness is the nominal shadow of the adjective vague, and the entire sense-history of the word turns on a single, persistent image: movement that refuses to settle. The chain begins in reconstructed Proto-Indo-European, where the root *weg- carried the sense "to be strong, be lively" — the idea of vigor and restless activity that also lies behind English wake and watch and Latin vegere "to be vigorous." In Latin this root gave the adjective vagus, "wandering, roving, strolling," built on the verb vagari "to wander, roam." The classical picture is physical: a vagus is a wanderer, a straying soldier, a planet — the Latin for "wandering stars" was stellae vagae — anything that will not hold still. That image of not holding still is the seed of everything the word means today.

From Latin the adjective passed into Old French as vague, still with the physical sense "wandering, unfixed, straying" (a vagabond is literally a "wanderer," from the same root). The crucial shift took place in French in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, when the word turned inward: what wanders physically becomes what wanders mentally, and vague came to mean "indefinite, imprecise, not to be pinned down" — a meaning applied first to speech and thought, then to outlines, sounds, and impressions. English borrowed the adjective in the 1540s, at first with the physical sense, then absorbed the French metaphorical sense, and by the seventeenth century vague was the standard word for the indefinite and imprecise. The noun vagueness, formed on vague with the suffix -ness, is first attested in the 1660s, and it has been the abstract name of the quality ever since.

The semantic history is thus a continuous metaphor. Because the root image is of something that wanders, vagueness always implies an element of evasiveness or refusal: the vague thing will not be caught, will not be fixed, will not hold still long enough to be measured. That is why the word carries two opposite valuations. On the one hand it is a censure — vague writing, vague promises, a vague answer are criticisms of imprecision and evasiveness. On the other hand it is an aesthetic — vague longings, vague premonitions, the "curious vagueness" of a land whose edges will not hold still, where the word names a soft, dreamy, atmospheric indistinctness that can be beautiful. Modern English keeps both faces: a scientist condemns a vague hypothesis, a poet welcomes a vague mood, and both are speaking the same language of things that drift away from definition.

  • Sound and form: The noun shows the shortening and centralization of the vowel in the adjective (vague /veIg/ → vagueness /veIgnEs/); the spelling with -ness is regular.
  • Register: Vagueness is standard, register-neutral vocabulary; the adjective is far more frequent, and the noun belongs to intellectual and critical prose.
  • Literary attestations: From seventeenth-century philosophy (Locke on vague ideas) through Romantic poetry (the vague and the sublime) to modern fiction and prose, the word has been the standard name of indistinct perception — of horizons, memories, longings, and fears that refuse sharp outline.
  • In the card's context: the horizon of Embelyon has a "curious vagueness," and it is this that unsettles the speaker, for a horizon is normally the sharpest line in any landscape. By making the horizon vague the prose suggests a world whose spatial laws are slack — a land that refuses to define its own edge, so that sight cannot complete its work.

II. LUCENT: the learned light

Lucent is a much younger and far more straightforward word, and its history is the history of a learned borrowing. It comes directly from Latin, where the present participle lucens, lucent- of the verb lucere "to shine" meant simply "shining, bright, luminous." Lucere is itself a denominative verb built on the noun lux, lucis "light," and lux descends from the same Proto-Indo-European root *leuk- "light, brightness" that gives Greek leukos "white," Sanskrit rocate "it shines," and English light itself. The chain from PIE *leuk- to lux to lucere is one of the most secure in Indo-European reconstruction, and lucent inherits the whole luminous family.

The word entered English in the late sixteenth century as a consciously literary, high-register adjective — the kind of word an educated writer chose when plain light was not quite grand enough. Its earliest English uses describe the shining of air, water, and flame: a lucent glow, lucent streams, lucent flames that glow softly and evenly, transmitting light while dissolving form. In the seventeenth century, poets from Milton onwards used it of heavenly and enchanted light, and the word has kept that ornamental, slightly archaic lustre ever since.

What is striking about lucent's semantic history is how little it has changed and how narrowly it has been preserved. It never generalized into everyday speech, because the language already had bright, shining, luminous, and radiant to do that work. Instead lucent specialized: it names a brilliance that illuminates yet blurs, a light without edges, a glow that dissolves the forms it falls on — which is exactly why it pairs so naturally with vagueness in the card's context. And its nearest kin lucid (from the same Latin family, via lucidus "clear, bright") has carried off the intellectual sense "easily understood," leaving lucent purely and simply luminous. In modern English lucent is rare and ornamental, at home in poetry, fantasy, and nature writing, always carrying the Latin light of lux into English as a polished, learned glow.

  • Sound and form: The word is disyllabic, with stress on the first syllable (lu-cent /l-Ent/); it has never varied in spelling or pronunciation since its borrowing.
  • Register: Rare, formal, literary; used where a luminous quality is to be described with elegance and a hint of antiquity.
  • Literary attestations: Milton's lucent flame, the lucent air of romantic landscape poetry, and modern fantasy prose where a glow is soft, diffuse, and slightly unreal — "lucent and uncertain as water," as the card's context has it.
