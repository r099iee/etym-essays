The story of cereals begins not in a field but in a temple. Latin Ceres was the goddess who presided over grain, harvest, and maternal nourishment, counterpart to Greek Demeter. Her name, on the standard etymology, continues PIE *ker- "to grow, to increase," also seen in Latin creare "to create," crescere "to grow," and English create, crescent, cereal. From the theonym Romans derived the adjective Cerealis, "pertaining to Ceres," used in phrases like ludi Cereales (festival games for Ceres) and, crucially, in Pliny's cerealia dona, "gifts of Ceres" — i.e., grain crops. The goddess's name thus became the generic label for the plants she protected.

For over a millennium the adjective stayed cultic and poetic rather than botanical. Medieval Latin retained Cerealis for rites and allegory; vernaculars used corn, grain, blé. The modern botanical-food sense was re-created in French. In the early 19th century French agronomists and dieticians needed a scientific collective term for farinaceous grasses — wheat, barley, oats, rye, rice, maize — and revived Latin cerealis as French céréale (noun, feminine). From there it passed into English.

  • PIE *ker- to Latin Ceres: Root meaning growth and nourishment yields a divine personification: she who makes grow. Italic sound laws preserve k- as c-; -eres suffix forms a nourisher-noun. Ceres is already attested in the early Roman calendar and the Aventine cult (493 BCE).
  • Ceres to Cerealis: Regular -alis adjectival formation (as in regalis, mortalis). Meaning slides from "of the goddess" to "of grain" by metonymy — the giver stands for the gift.
  • Classical usage: Cerealia as neuter plural for grain-produce, games, and offerings. Never yet the everyday farmer's word; frumentum and granum covered that.
  • French scientific revival (c. 1800–1840): Céréale appears in agricultural treatises and dictionaries as a classificatory noun. The plural céréales denotes the crop group as opposed to légumes (vegetables) — exactly Durant's contrast of cereals and vegetables.
  • English borrowing (1830s): First as adjective cereal (cereal grasses, cereal crops), then as noun cereal (a single grain type) and cereals (the class). American breakfast innovation (1890s–1900s: Kellogg, Post) creates the narrowed sense "breakfast cereal," unknown in French.
  • 20th-century globalisation: Green Revolution discourse makes cereals a statistical-technical category (FAO cereal production = wheat + rice + coarse grains). Nutrition science splits whole vs. refined cereals.

Durant's sentence preserves the older, broad sense: cereals as the cultivated grass-seeds that cooking made digestible — wheat, barley, millet — set against vegetables. The plural here is the crop-class plural, not multiple bowls of cornflakes. The goddess is still faintly present: to call wheat a cereal is to remember, however distantly, that Romans thanked Ceres for bread. The breakfast-box sense is a later American narrowing that must not be read back into Durant.
