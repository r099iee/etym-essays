**SWISH: a word born entirely from a sound** Unlike nearly every other word in the goblin chant, SWISH is a purely modern formation: its entire recorded history spans less than three centuries, and it has no ancestor older than the sound it imitates. The verb appears in English in 1756, first intransitive, meaning "to move with a swish or flourish, with a sound like 'swish'"; it is what lexicographers call a phonetic, imitative, or sound-symbolic word, coined directly from the acoustic experience of something brushing, sweeping, or slicing through the air. The very shape of the word is the sound: the initial sibilant *s*, the breathy glide *w*, the high hissing vowel *i*, and the soft closing *sh* reproduce the friction of a moving body against the air — faster and thinner than the deeper *swoosh* or the heavier *swash*. **The eighteenth century: from verb to flourish** In 1799 the verb became transitive — "to cause to swish," hence "to flourish, brandish" — and the word began its career as a verb of elegant, confident motion. The transitive step is typical of sound-symbolic words: once a sound is named, the action that produces it is named too, and the verb then extends from making the sound to making the motion. By the early nineteenth century *swish* described the flourish of a blade or a whip, and the movement of skirts and scythes, all the while keeping the acoustic core intact. **The nineteenth century: the noun and the lash** In 1820 the noun was derived, naming the sound itself: "a swish," the noise of something moving through the air. It was a short step from rustling silk to the brandished lash: by 1856, in slang, *to swish* meant "to flog, to lash," and a *swish* was the stroke or its sound — exactly the usage Tolkien's goblin drivers enact in "SWISH, smack!" The school and the prison, the stable and the parade-ground all knew the word in this register: the sound of the cane or whip cutting the air before it lands. This is the sense that gives the goblin couplet its terrible economy — two syllables contain the whole arc of a blow. **The twentieth century: fashion, sport, and slang** In the twentieth century the word sprouted a luxuriant set of secondary senses. In basketball, a *swish* is a shot that passes cleanly through the net without touching the rim — the pure, impact-free sound of cloth brushing air, a perfect metaphor for a perfect throw. As an adjective, *swish* came to mean fashionable, smart, stylish, from the sweeping, confident elegance the word had always suggested. And from the 1930s, in homosexual slang, *swishy* (and the noun *swish*) described an effeminate man, drawn from the notion of a mincing, swishing motion — a usage that has since become dated and offensive in mainstream registers. Meanwhile the expressive family grew: *swoosh*, a fuller, broader rush of air (made famous by sportswear branding in the late twentieth century); *whish*, a lighter rustle; *swash*, for water surging against a shore; and, in the twentieth century, *whoosh*, the emphatic variant of motion. **Why the word works in the verse** What makes SWISH so potent in the goblin verse is that it has kept its original imitative force intact. Placed before *smack*, it isolates the sound of speed — thin, fast, and airy — against the heavy iron percussion of the other chant-lines ("Hammer and tongs! Knocker and gongs!"). Its hissing *s* and gliding *w* consonants put the listener in the path of the whip, waiting for the stroke. The word has no history of high culture or learned meaning to dilute it: it is, and remains, the noise of a blow in motion, coined fresh from the sound itself and still sounding like what it means.
