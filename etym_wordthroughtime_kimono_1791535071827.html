- 17th to mid-19th century: Generic Japanese Garment Term - In Japan, the ordinary native compound for any article of clothing; in early European trade logs of the Dutch East India Company, an exotic luxury robe brought home from Nagasaki as a prized diplomatic gift or curio.
- 1860s to 1910s: Aesthetic Icon of Japonisme - Embraced passionately across Victorian and Belle Époque Europe during the artistic Japonisme movement; celebrated by Western painters (e.g., Monet, Whistler) and aesthetic dress reformers as the height of Far Eastern elegance, exotic refinement, and natural draped beauty.
- 1920s to 1960s: Western Loungewear and Haute Couture Adaptations - Adapted by Western fashion houses (such as Paul Poiret) into comfortable, flowing dressing gowns, opera coats, and bathrobes; revolutionized Western pattern-making through the introduction of the seamless 'kimono sleeve', liberating women from rigid corsetry.
- 1970s to 1990s: Formal Cultural Heritage & Corporate Metaphor - Within Japan, increasingly reserved for formal rites of passage (Coming of Age Day, weddings, tea ceremonies) rather than daily attire; in American corporate jargon, emerged as the controversial idiom 'open the kimono' to denote revealing internal company financials.
- 2000s to Present: Global Cultural Heritage & Modern Streetwear - Revered worldwide as masterworks of textile craftsmanship, while lightweight kimono-cardigans become staples in global bohemian streetwear; simultaneously scrutinized in modern discourse surrounding cultural appropriation and preservation.
