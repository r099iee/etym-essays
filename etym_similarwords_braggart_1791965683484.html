• boaster: The plainest synonym, naming the same habit of self-praise; it is neutral in tone and lacks the theatrical, type-cast quality of braggart, which insists on a whole personality built out of boasting.
  • braggadocio: Either a boastful person or empty boasting itself, and the closest literary rival; it derives from Spenser's character Braggadocchio and carries an Italianate, operatic flamboyance, whereas braggart is blunter and more English.
  • blowhard: Colloquial, especially American, for a loud, self-important talker; it emphasises volume and tediousness rather than the specific content of the claims, and it is more dismissive than condemnatory.
  • show-off: Someone who performs for admiration; the motive is the same, but a show-off may display skills or possessions rather than claim deeds, and the word is childish and mild where braggart is adult and contemptuous.
  • swaggerer: A person who walks and talks with arrogant confidence; the emphasis falls on bearing and physical manner, whereas braggart focuses on speech and the content of the boast.
  • windbag: A person who talks too much and says too little; it targets length rather than self-praise, and it lacks any accusation of vanity about achievement.
  • egotist: Someone absorbed in himself and inclined to talk about himself; the fault is self-centredness rather than the specific claim to glory, and an egotist need not exaggerate anything.
  • gascon: An archaic literary term for a boaster, from the stereotype of Gascons as swaggering soldiers; it is a regional type-word like braggart but far rarer and more bookish.
  • fanfaron: An obsolete English borrowing from French for a swaggering boaster, virtually identical in sense; it survives only in historical prose, which makes braggart the practical choice.
  • peacock: A metaphorical label for a vain, display-loving person; it targets appearance and strutting, not verbal claims, and is often used affectionately in a way braggart never is.
