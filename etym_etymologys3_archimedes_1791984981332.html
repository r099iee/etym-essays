Archimedes is a Greek compound name of a familiar heroic type, and its history divides neatly into two questions: what the name meant when it was coined, and what it has come to mean since.

The morphology of the name

Ἀρχιμήδης is built from ἀρχι-, the combining form of ἀρχός “leader, commander” (itself from ἄρχω, “to be first, to rule”), and from the element -μήδης, which belongs to a large family of Greek names: Diomedes, Laomedon, Hippomedon, Menedemos. The second element is conventionally connected with μήδομαι “to plan, devise” and its noun μῆδος “counsel, plan”, a stem inherited from PIE *med- “to measure, take appropriate measures”, which also yields Latin meditari, medicus and modus. On that reading the name means something like “chief in counsel” or “leader of plan”.

Where the uncertainty lies

Two points deserve caution. First, the sense of the second element is not fixed: μῆδος in Homer covers “plan, counsel, craft” and in some later texts the plural μήδεα can mean “genitals”, a semantic byway that has tempted over-ingenious interpretations. Second, it is not certain whether the first element is the prefix ἀρχι- “chief” or the bare stem ἀρχο- of ἀρχός “leader”; the two analyses give slightly different compounds, “chief in counsel” and “counsellor-leader”. Neither is provable, and the standard account should be given with that reservation. What is clear is that the name was a normal, repeatable Greek name, not a unique title, and that it carried no suggestion of mathematical genius.

Dialect and transmission

Syracuse was a Dorian colony, and the local pronunciation of the name would have had a long alpha where Attic has eta. The form that reaches us, however, is the Attic-Ionic spelling Ἀρχιμήδης, because the literary tradition that preserved him — Polybius, Plutarch, the mathematical manuscripts — was written in the Attic-Ionic koine. Latin took the name as Archimedes, with the genitive Archimedis, and the Latin nominative is the source of every western European form: Italian Archimede, French Archimède, German Archimedes, English Archimedes.

The English word

  • Pronunciation: English keeps the Latin stress pattern but moves the accent to the penultimate syllable lengthened in Latin verse, giving /ˌɑːkɪˈmiːdiːz/; the ch is /k/, not /tʃ/, because the Greek chi was a voiceless stop in the Latin borrowing.
  • Orthography: the -es ending looks plural to English speakers but is simply the Latin nominative singular; there is no singular “Archimede” in English, though Italian has one.
  • First English use: sixteenth-century mathematical and antiquarian writing, where the name appears alongside Euclid and Vitruvius as an authority rather than a person.

From person to principle

The semantic extension is the interesting part. A name that began as the label of one Syracusan becomes, in sequence: a body of geometrical treatises; a set of machines; a law of hydrostatics; an adjective for any reasoning that requires a standpoint outside the system; and finally a generic noun for a prodigy, as when a newspaper calls a child an “Archimedes”. The bridge in every case is the same: the anecdotes about levers and baths present him as the man who could move the world by understanding it, and that image transfers easily from the person to the principle.

  • Middle English and Renaissance readers knew him chiefly through De mensura circuli and through Vitruvius' story of the crown.
  • William of Moerbeke translated several treatises from Greek at Viterbo in 1269, giving the Latin west a fuller Archimedes.
  • The Basel edition of 1544 printed the Greek text with a Latin translation and made him a standard author.
  • Galileo's La bilancetta (1586) reworked the crown problem and fixed the bath story in the scientific imagination.
  • Heiberg's edition and his identification of the palimpsest in 1906, and the imaging work of the 2000s, recovered texts — the Method and the Stomachion — that had been unread for seven centuries.

Modern reference

Today the name is everywhere and mostly invisible: in the Archimedes number of chemical engineering, in the bryozoan genus whose spiral axis resembles a screw, in the 1839 steamship that first proved the screw propeller, in the 1987 Acorn computer, and in the phrase “Archimedean point”, which philosophers use without thinking of Syracuse at all. For a reader of the source passage, the effect is a pleasant collision of scales: the sentence describes a Roman official parting the brambles from a neglected grave, while the name on the grave has become one of the load-bearing words of modern science.
