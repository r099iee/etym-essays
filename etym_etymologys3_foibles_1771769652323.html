The biography of FOIBLES is one of the strangest semantic journeys in English vocabulary: a word that begins as a verb of weeping, passes through the meaning "lamentable," then "weak," then names a specific part of a sword blade, and finally settles in the affectionate description of human character. Each stage of the journey is attested, and each is a small chapter in the history of the Romance languages and of English. The journey begins in Proto-Indo-European with the root *bʰleh₁-, "to howl, to cry, to lament" — an expressive root of vocal lamentation that gave Latin its verb flēre, "to weep." Flēre is attested from the earliest Latin literature, in the comedies of Plautus in the second century BCE, where weeping is already a fully developed poetic and emotional theme. From flēre, Latin formed the adjective flēbilis with the productive suffix -bilis, "able to be, deserving of," so that flēbilis meant "worthy of tears, lamentable, mournful": Ovid, in the first century BCE to CE, uses it of things that deserve weeping. At this point the word has no connection to weakness whatsoever — it is entirely an adjective of mourning. The decisive shift happens inside the history of the word in the provinces, in the Vulgar Latin and early Gallo-Romance that would become French. In that environment flēbilis — or rather its weakened pronunciation *fleible — moved from "lamentable, deserving tears" to "weak, faint," a shift attested across the Romance area and standard in the history of the word family. The semantic logic is easy to trace: what is lamentable is pitiable, what is pitiable is infirm, what is infirm is weak; a people whose ancestors had learned to say "woe to the weak" transferred the tears from the occasion to the victim. By the time Old French is attested in the ninth to twelfth centuries, feble and foible simply mean "weak, feeble," and the word has become the ordinary adjective for lack of strength, exactly the word that English later borrows as feeble. The identity of the two English words is complete: feeble and foible are doublets, two branches of the same Old French word, feeble the adjective that stayed an adjective and foible the noun that specialized. The fencing chapter is the pivot of the whole story. In sixteenth-century France, the adjective foible was substantivized to name the weak half of a sword blade — the part from the middle to the point, which bends and yields in the parry, set against the forte, the strong half near the hilt. The fencing manuals of the period fixed the technical pair: the foible attacks and the forte defends, and the fencer who understands where the blade is weak wins his duels. English borrowed the technical noun directly from French, and the word is attested in English fencing literature from 1648. At this point foible is a purely technical word of the sword, known to gentlemen who studied the art of defense and to the writers of fencing manuals. The moral transfer follows within a generation. In the 1670s, English writers begin to use foible metaphorically of the "weak part" of a person's character — the place where the temperament bends and yields, as the blade bends at its foible. The metaphor is precise and physical: just as the sword gives way at its weak half, so the person gives way at their weak point, and both give way under pressure. The transfer is so natural that within a few decades foible has become a standard word of biography and the moral essay, applied to the small, forgivable weaknesses that make a character human. The Restoration and eighteenth-century essayists — the tradition of the Spectator, of Steele and Addison, and of the tolerant moralists who preceded Gibbon — found the word perfect for their purposes: it named a weakness without condemning it, a failing with a smile. The final softening, from "minor failing" to "endearing eccentricity," is the work of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. As the word moved into ordinary literary use, the moral weight drained out of it almost entirely: the foible became not the vice but the quirk, the harmless vanity, the charming oddity that one half expects and half smiles at. The plural became the norm — we speak of a person's foibles, not of a single foible — and the collocation "human foibles" fixed the word in the affectionate, tolerant register that this card's sentence uses: the "foibles of the human race" are what an inexhaustible wonder and a tolerant curiosity find endlessly amusing. The history is thus complete: from the howl of PIE lamentation, through the lamentable weakness of Romance, the weak half of a Renaissance blade, the vulnerable point of a seventeenth-century character, to the warm, smiling plural of modern English — a word whose entire journey has been a progress from tears to laughter.
