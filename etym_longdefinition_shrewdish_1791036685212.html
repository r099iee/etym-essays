Shrewdish is a rare, chiefly literary adjective meaning somewhat shrewd — possessing or requiring a modest degree of practical cleverness, astuteness, cunning, or good judgment, especially in reading people and situations. The suffix -ish softens and hedges: not brilliantly shrewd, but shrewd enough, a little crafty, fairly sharp.

The base shrewd today is positive: a shrewd businessman, shrewd observer, shrewd investment. It means keen-minded, discerning, able to see through appearances to advantage, combining intelligence with worldly experience. Shrewdish inherits this but downtones it. To say Bertram must put in some shrewdish work is to say he needs to scheme a little, think cleverly, plan artfully — with comic understatement typical of Wodehouse, implying Bertie Wooster is not naturally shrewd and even a small amount will be an effort.

Historically shrewd traveled from evil to clever, so shrewdish carries faint echoes of that older cunning. It can shade toward artful, wily, or scheming when context involves outwitting formidable aunts, rivals, or social obstacles, but it rarely means dishonest; it means resourceful within social rules.

  • Mild-clever sense: fairly sharp, quick to grasp advantage. A shrewdish move in negotiation is sensible and slightly crafty.
  • Hedged-character sense: he is shrewdish describes someone intermittently astute rather than consistently brilliant; damning with faint praise or affectionate concession.
  • Comic-understatement sense: some shrewdish work frames plotting as labour, making cunning sound like homework — very Wodehousian bathos.
  • Archaic-evil echo: in older texts shrewed meant wicked; modern shrewdish never means wickedish, but literary readers may catch the ghost of mischief.

Domain usages: in business, shrewdish is too vague for formal analysis and appears mainly in narrative colour. In everyday talk it functions as a hedge like tallish, greenish — I have a shrewdish idea he is lying means I have a fairly sharp suspicion. In literary criticism it can describe narration that is mildly ironic or perceptive without full cynicism.

Morphology matters: -ish in English forms approximatives (reddish, tallish), pejoratives (childish), and relational adjectives (British). Shrewdish uses the approximative function, signalling partial degree. It is not a dictionary headword in many concise dictionaries, being a transparent derivative, which explains its rarity and slightly playful nonce feel. A writer coins it when shrewd feels too strong and a little cunning too wordy.

Register is informal-literary, arch, humorous. collocates with work, idea, fellow, move. It praises practical intelligence while keeping expectations low — perfect for describing well-meaning but dim comic heroes forced to think.

Further nuance: learners should note collocations, frequency, and common confusions. These words live in distinct registers and carry period flavour that modern equivalents lack. Mastering them requires reading widely in comic and rhetorical prose and noticing how understatement, irony, and moral judgment shape tone. Pay attention to surrounding verbs, articles, and prepositions, which often signal the intended sense more reliably than the isolated headword.

  • Collocational clue: surrounding words select the sense; legal, literary, and slang contexts each favour different readings.
  • Register clue: formal versus jocular framing tells you whether the author condemns, pities, or jokes.
  • Period clue: Edwardian narrators use euphemism and litotes where modern writers would state feeling directly.

Pedagogically, anchor the word to one vivid scene from the source text, then branch to secondary senses through explicit bridges. Ask what changed: who acts, who suffers, what bridle is missing, what vehicle carries the metaphor. This habit turns isolated vocabulary into a network of motivated extensions rather than arbitrary list-memorization, and it preserves humour that flat glosses lose.
