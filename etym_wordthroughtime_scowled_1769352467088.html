- [Early English (16th c.)] - Borrowed from Scandinavian with the sense "to contract the brows in anger or displeasure"; from the start the word carries a hostile, threatening register. - [17th–19th c. literary use] - Consolidated as the standard verb for a menacing, angry frown in fiction; a scowl marks villainy, rage, or menacing disapproval, with a dark, aggressive emotional colouring. - [Contemporary sense] - Broadened into everyday usage to describe any angry or bad-tempered contraction of the face, from a child's sulky face to an adult's fierce glare; the menace has softened, but the overt displeasure and hostility remain.
