COMPOUND WORD — TWO ROOT WORDS IN ISOLATION: CHAIN 1 — the element "whirl": PIE *kʷer- "to turn, to whirl, to bend" (reconstructed; no date; a root of rotation, with the allied *kʷel- "to turn" standing behind the "wheel" words) → Proto-Germanic *hwerbaną "to turn, to move about, to wander" (c. 500 BCE–c. 500 CE; Germanic shifted the labiovelar *kʷ- to *hw-) → Old Norse hvirfla "to turn round, to spin" (attested in Old Norse records from the 13th c.; an intensive formation in *-il- built on hverfa "to turn") → Middle English whirlen "to whirl, to spin" (14th c.; borrowed into English from the Scandinavian verb) → English whirl "to spin rapidly round" (16th c.) → compound whirligig "a thing that whirls" (c. 1440; recorded as "whirlegigge") ← whirl + gig → FINAL: English whirligigs (plural; the form used in the Narnia passage). NUANCE (chain 1): English also had the native Old English verb hweorfan "to turn, to go, to return" with its derivative hwyrfel "a circle, a ring" (surviving in dialectal wharve "a spindle-whorl"), so the Middle English whirlen reinforced rather than replaced an existing native word; both descend from the same Germanic base. CHAIN 2 — the element "gig": (Unknown / expressive origin — no PIE or Germanic etymon is established; the OED records the origin as obscure and probably echoic) → Middle English gigge "a whipping-top, a spinning toy" (attested mid-15th c.) → compound whirligig "a thing that whirls" (c. 1440) ← whirl + gig → FINAL: English whirligigs (plural; the form used in the Narnia passage). NUANCE (chain 2): The "gig" element hums with the sound of a spinning toy, and the same buzzing family produced giggle and probably jig (via Old French gigue "a fiddle" and Middle High German gîga). Later English extended gig to a flighty girl, a light two-wheeled carriage (18th c.), a musician's one-off engagement (20th-c. jazz slang), and simply a job — but none of these is securely a cognate, and the element's ultimate origin remains unknown.
