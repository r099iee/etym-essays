- [Late 16th c.–17th c.] - Enters English from Late Latin allusivus, denoting speech or writing that plays upon words, hints through parables, or makes playful symbolic comparisons - [connotation: witty, rhetorical, learned, figurative, emblematic]
- [18th c.–19th c.] - Specializes in classical rhetoric, literary criticism, and belles-lettres to describe discourse rich in erudite echoes of Greco-Roman antiquity, scripture, and canonical poetry - [connotation: scholarly, refined, gentlemanly culture, polite indirectness]
- [Early to Mid-20th c.] - Modernist poetry and prose (Eliot, Joyce, Pound, Proust) adopt radical, complex, and multilingual allusiveness as a primary aesthetic technique to assemble fragments of a shattered Western tradition - [connotation: intellectually demanding, avant-garde, labyrinthine, masterly intertextuality]
- [Late 20th c.–Present] - Broadens across post-structuralist theory, postmodern art, cinematic homage, and colloquial speech to denote any cultural artifact dense with easter eggs, intertextual tributes, or veiled subtexts - [connotation: self-referential, sophisticated, layered, intertextual]
