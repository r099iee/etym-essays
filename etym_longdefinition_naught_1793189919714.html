Naught means nothing at all — not anything, zero in quantity, value, or effect — and survives in modern English chiefly as an archaic or literary word kept alive by a small set of fixed idioms such as come to naught, set at naught, and all for naught.

In the source sentence the Hawk says it saw naught to remark save a band of crows flying south, using the classic pronominal sense: nothing worth remarking, nothing except the crows. The construction with save meaning except is itself archaic, so the sentence is doubly old-fashioned in diction, fitting a fable-like narrator whose birds speak in the elevated style of traditional tales.

Grammar in detail: naught functions chiefly as a pronoun (naught remains, naught avails), as a noun naming nothingness or the figure zero (a naught, three naughts), and fossilised in adjectives and verbs built on it — naughty originally meant worthless or poor before it softened toward mischievous, and naughtiness preserves that older moral arithmetic in which a person of naught is a person of no worth. The spelling twin nought is the same word with a different settled use: nought now dominates for the mathematical zero in British English while naught dominates for the literary nothing.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Nothing, not anything (primary pronominal sense, from Old English): I saw naught, naught was said, all was naught. The bridge is composition itself: Old English ne meaning not plus aught meaning anything yields not-anything, a reinforced negative of the kind early English loved.
  • The figure zero or a cipher (noun sense, from the 15th c.): add two naughts, a row of naughts. The bridge runs from absence of things to absence of quantity: nothing to count becomes the sign for no number, since medieval reckoning needed a written mark for an empty place.
  • Worthlessness or failure in fixed idioms (from the 14th c.): to come to naught is to end in failure, to set at naught is to despise or disregard, all for naught is all in vain. The bridge is metaphorical extension: what amounts to nothing is what fails or is scorned, so the idioms convert arithmetic zero into judgments of value.
  • Ruined or useless condition (predicative adjective shading, literary): his plans were naught, their labour was naught. The bridge narrows the idiom to a bare predicate: the outcome itself is nothing, hence futile.
  • The game of noughts and crosses (British noun, 19th c.): the zero-shaped mark O against the cross X in tic-tac-toe. The bridge is purely visual: the letter O looks like the cipher, so the cipher lends its name to the game piece.
  • Moral or spiritual nothingness (scriptural and philosophical register): man is but naught before eternity, all flesh is naught. The bridge carries the arithmetic sense into theology: human pretension reckoned against the infinite totals to zero, a favourite King James and pulpit turn.

Register and connotation: naught sounds deliberately old — scripture, Shakespeare, fable, and proverb — and modern writers reach for it precisely to tint a sentence with age, solemnity, or whimsy. Outside the fixed idioms it would sound affected in ordinary speech, where nothing does all the work; inside them it is invisible and idiomatic, since millions who would never say I found naught happily say it all came to naught. Do not confuse it with not, its clipped descendant, which does purely grammatical negation with none of the solemnity.
