- c. 8th c.–14th c.: Root word true / truth (from Old English trēowe) evolved in Germanic and Middle English to signify loyalty, faithfulness, honesty, and alignment with reality.
- 1700s–1780s: The noun truism was coined in Early Modern English during the Enlightenment by combining true with the noun suffix -ism, originally meaning an undeniable or self-evident truth in philosophical debate (Swift, Berkeley).
- 1800s–1900s: Gradually acquired its modern pejorative nuance, increasingly applied to boring, platitudinous, or trivial statements that add nothing of value to serious intellectual discussion.
- 1920s–1970s: Formalized in analytic philosophy and epistemology to categorize tautological or analytically empty propositions, alongside structuralist analysis of cultural doxa (Barthes).
- 1980s–Present: Widely used in political critique, cultural theory, and literary analysis to describe the empty rhetoric of media spin and ideological common sense.
