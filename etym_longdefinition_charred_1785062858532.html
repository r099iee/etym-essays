**CHARRED (past-participle adjective) — burned to a carbon crust while the interior survives** At its plainest, **charred** means scorched, blackened, or partly burned by fire or intense heat, so that the surface of a material has been turned to carbon — to a charcoal-like crust — while the interior is left more or less intact. It is the past participle of the verb *to char*, "to burn the surface of something to charcoal," and it carries the double meaning of the verb: deliberate, controlled scorching on the one hand (a charred steak, a charred barrel stave, charred toast) and the accidental residue of fire on the other (a charred beam after a fire). The word occupies a precise place on the continuum of burning damage: singed (the merest pass of flame over hair or cloth) → scorched (superficial singeing or discoloration by dry heat) → charred (the surface turned to carbon) → burned (broad destruction by fire) → carbonized (complete conversion to carbon). Charred sits at the hinge where damage becomes carbon, the moment the material stops being merely discolored and becomes genuinely coal-like. **The brewing sense: porter and roasted barley** In the context — porter as "a dark, heavy style of beer brewed from charred or malted barley" — the adjective is doing precise technical work. Porter, the London-born dark ale of the 18th century, owes its deep brown-black color and its roasty, chocolatey, coffee-like flavors to barley that has been strongly roasted in the kiln — literally charred. The nuance is deliberately physical: the word does not mean merely "dark malt" but grain burned at the surface, its sugars caramelized and carbonized, exactly as a log is charred before it becomes charcoal. This is the same treatment that charred whisky and bourbon barrels receive (toasting and charring the inner staves releases vanilla, caramel, and smoky compounds into the spirit), and the same controlled scorching a chef applies to a grill-pan steak or a piece of toast. In this brewing and distilling sense, charred is a term of craft — a deliberate, measured burning chosen for its flavor and color, not an accident. **Polysemy — all the surfaces that get charred** - **Cooking**: a charred crust on grilled meat, vegetables, or bread is prized for its flavor (the Maillard browning plus true carbonization); "blackened" cooking is a deliberately heavy spicing technique that yields a charred-looking crust. - **Fire damage**: a charred building, tree, or body part after a conflagration — the forensic register of fires, where "charring depth" is measured to determine how long and hot a fire burned. - **Wood and charcoal**: to char wood is to turn it toward charcoal; the historic charcoal-burner's craft of smothered burning, and the modern use of charred, activated carbon for filtration. - **Cooperage and spirits**: charred oak barrel interiors are standard for bourbon (which by law must be aged in new charred oak), Scotch whisky (toasted and charred casks), and wine (toasted staves). The char level — #1 to #4 — is a technical scale of barrel charring. - **Roasted coffee and malt**: the "char" of very dark roasts; in brewing, "chocolate malt" and "black malt" are the charred-level malts that give stout and porter their roast bitterness. - **Chemistry and industry**: carbonization of organic material is a stage in making charcoal, coke, and activated carbon; "char" is also a technical noun for the partly carbonized residue. - **Figurative use**: charred occasionally describes something emotionally "burned up" or destroyed by crisis — a charred marriage, a charred career — though the literal sense dominates overwhelmingly. **Register and distinction from neighbors** Charred is a vivid, physical, concrete word with no exact English synonym: burned is broader and more neutral; scorched is milder (superficial); singed is faintest; blackened overlaps in cooking but is usually a seasoning technique; carbonized is the technical term for complete conversion; toasted is gentler (browned without carbonizing). In the brewer's and distiller's world, charred is a precise marker of process and flavor; in everyday speech it is the word for the pleasant crust of grilled food and the grim residue of fire alike. It is an adjective that has quietly become a keyword of craft food, craft beer, and craft spirits — a word whose core physical meaning, "surface turned to charcoal," has been stable for three centuries while its contexts have multiplied.
