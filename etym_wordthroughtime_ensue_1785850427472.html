- 14th c. ME ensuen: follow, pursue, strive after — active human or agentive tracking of a goal or person
- Later ME / 15th c.: also come after in order or time — dual pursuit and sequence readings
- 16th–17th c.: chronological succession strengthens; resultative uses expand in narrative and chronicle prose
- 18th–19th c.: standard literary verb for "happen afterward," especially of conflicts, debates, and public events
- 20th c.: journalistic cliché zone ("chaos ensued") — efficient aftermath marker; slightly formal vs "happened next"
- Contemporary: still chiefly intransitive resultative-sequential; active "pursue" sense largely obsolete; perception is of cool, compressed consequence rather than vivid onomatopoeic action
