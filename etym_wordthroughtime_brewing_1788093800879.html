- Old English brēowan: literal beer-making only — a domestic and manorial craft word, brewing ale for the hall, the monastery and the shire.
- Middle English brewen: extended to preparing any drink by mixing or steeping — the word begins to cover potions, possets and medical draughts.
- 15th–16th c. figurative turn: “brewing mischief / treason / deceits” — the cauldron and the plot converge; the word gains a sinister flavour.
- 16th–17th c. extension to events: storms and troubles “brew” — the meteorological and political senses are born together in the pamphlet and drama of the age.
- 18th–19th c. industrialisation: brewing becomes big business (porter, pale ale, scientific fermentation after Pasteur), and the noun “a brew” begins its modern career.
- 20th–21st c.: “a brew” = a cup of tea in British colloquial speech, while “brewing” remains the standard ominous journalistic verb for imminent trouble — the homely and the catastrophic senses now live side by side.
