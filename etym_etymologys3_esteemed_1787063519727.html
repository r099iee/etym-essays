Latin origin: esteemed descends from Latin aestimare, "to appraise, value, estimate, set a price on." The Latin verb is first attested in the classical period (1st century BCE) and is of uncertain ultimate origin. The traditional etymology connects it with aes (genitive aeris), "bronze, copper, money," so that aestimare would mean literally "to determine the worth of something in metal or money" — the verb being formed from aes with a suffix. This link is semantically attractive (an aestimatio is literally a valuation) and was long standard, but modern scholarship doubts it: the morphology is awkward, and no securely cognate Italic form confirms it. Some scholars prefer a connection with a root meaning "strong," others leave the word "of unknown origin." The honest summary: aestimare meant "to value, appraise," and its pre-Latin ancestry is debated.

Romance transmission: Vulgar Latin and Old French carried the verb forward: Old French estimer (12th century) meant "to estimate, value, judge, think." English borrowed the verb from the French form in the 15th century (Middle English estemen), while the later, more technical "estimate" (17th century) was taken directly from Latin aestimare by scholars. The two words — esteem and estimate — are thus doublets: one brought through French with the meaning "regard, respect," the other through Latin directly with the meaning "to calculate worth." Spanish estimar and Italian stimare show the same Romance development, with "to think, to judge" and "to value" both alive in them.

Semantic drift in English:

  • 15th century: to esteem = "to appraise, set a price on, value" — the financial sense, close to its Latin ancestor.
  • 16th century: the sense shifts from "to put a price on" to "to put a high price on" — to value highly, to respect, to regard with favour. The participle esteemed becomes an adjective meaning "highly regarded." This is the semantic bridge: appraisal becomes admiration when the appraised value is high.
  • 16th-17th centuries: "esteem" also means "to judge, consider" ("I esteem it a duty"), a sense preserved in formal "I esteem it an honour." The noun esteem ("high regard") appears c. 1580s; self-esteem is coined in the 1650s and becomes a central term of psychological discourse in the 20th century.
  • 18th-19th centuries: "esteemed" settles into formal address — letters, parliamentary speech, honorary titles, newspaper notices ("our esteemed correspondent"). It becomes the standard adjective of public respect.
  • 20th-21st centuries: sincere use remains in obituaries, references, honours, and formal introductions; colloquial use is often knowingly formal or ironic ("the esteemed leadership"), while the word keeps its genuine warmth when applied sincerely.

Cultural profile: "esteem" carries the trace of money in its etymology but has long been a moral and social word: to esteem someone is to acknowledge that their worth has been weighed and found great. The shift from counting coins to counting merit is one of the clearest examples in English of a valuation word becoming a respect word. In the Tolkien context — "now is the time for our esteemed Mr. Baggins" — Thorin uses the adjective ceremonially, investing a small hobbit with formal honour; the very word's history of "weighing worth" underwrites the compliment.
