Hoisted is the past tense and past participle of the verb hoist, meaning to have raised, lifted, hauled, or elevated a heavy load upward, especially through the application of mechanical devices (such as ropes, pulleys, tackles, winches, or cranes) or through concerted, strenuous physical exertion. Originating as an essential maritime command for setting sails and loading vessel cargo, the term carries an enduring connotation of overcoming significant gravitational resistance and securing a burden at a higher elevation.

The diverse polysemous applications of hoisted span nautical, industrial, colloquial, military, and figurative contexts:

  • 1. Maritime and Rigging Operations:
    • Raising Sails, Flags, and Signals: In naval terminology, denotes the formal raising of canvas sails up the masts, hauling signal flags to the yardarms, or hoisting the national ensign (e.g., the crew hoisted the mainsail as the ship cleared the harbor).
    • Cargo and Anchor Loading: Refers to using shipboard cranes, capstans, and block-and-tackle gear to haul heavy anchors, barrels, and cargo from dockside up into the hold.
  • 2. Manual and Physical Elevation:
    • Lifting Living Beings: Describes boosting, lifting, or bodily hauling a person or animal upward into a tree, onto horse saddle, over a wall, or onto a comrade's shoulders in celebration (e.g., they hoisted him up into the branches of the oak tree or the winning captain was hoisted aloft by his teammates).
    • Strenuous Manual Handling: Applied to lifting heavy rucksacks, beer kegs, or stone masonry blocks through raw physical leverage.
  • 3. Construction, Engineering, and Mechanical Systems:
    • Crane and Derrick Operations: In civil engineering and construction, describes using overhead gantry cranes, tower cranes, or hoists to lift steel I-beams, prefabricated concrete slabs, and heavy machinery to upper floors of building frameworks.
    • Mine Shaft and Elevator Transport: Refers to vertical lift mechanisms (mine hoists) hauling ore skips and miners out of deep subterranean shafts.
  • 4. Idiomatic, Colloquial, and Figurative Usages:
    • "Hoist with His Own Petard": A famous Shakespearean idiom (from Hamlet, where a petard was an early explosive siege engine) meaning to be caught in one's own trap, harmed by one's own scheming, or blown into the air by one's own bomb. The semantic bridge equates the upward blast of the explosive device with being violently hoisted by one's own weapon.
    • Colloquial Drinking and Salutations: In informal slang, to hoist a beer or hoist a glass means to raise a drink to one's lips in celebration or toast, bridged by the physical upward motion of lifting a heavy glass mug.
    • Slang for Shoplifting or Larceny: In historical British and underworld slang, to hoist meant to shoplift or pickpocket (derived from lifting goods off a shop counter).

Syntactically and descriptively, hoisted emphasizes both the mechanical mechanics of vertical translation and the tangible weight of the object being elevated, distinguishing it from lightweight, effortless lifting.
