- Middle French maraud (15th c.): "rogue, vagabond" — a low criminal; origin obscure, perhaps from a dialect word for a tomcat. Connotation: contemptible scoundrel.
- French marauder (17th c.): "to go marauding, plunder" — coined in the era of the Thirty Years' War, when unpaid soldiers and deserters lived off the land by force. Connotation: military breakdown, lawless violence.
- English (late 17th c.): marauder and to maraud borrowed from French military jargon; English accounts of continental wars introduce "marauding parties". Connotation: exotic, soldierly, predatory.
- 18th–19th c.: "marauding" widens to every kind of plundering band — brigands, pirates, border tribes; colonial and adventure literature fix "marauding hordes" in the popular imagination. Connotation: frontier terror, thrilling lawlessness.
- 19th–20th c.: extended to animals ("marauding wolves") and then to impersonal destructive forces — fire, storms, armies. Connotation: destructive agency, no moral restraint.
- Modern: journalism (marauding gangs, militants), fantasy (Tolkien's "marauding fire of the Dragon"), and playful domestic use (marauding toddlers at the biscuit tin). Connotation: lawless mobility with a slightly archaic-heroic flavour.
