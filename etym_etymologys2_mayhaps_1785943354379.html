MAYHAPS is built from the modal verb may + the noun hap "chance, fortune", with an adverbial -s. The two chains are given separately before the joining step. Chain — may 1. Proto-Indo-European *magʰ- "to be able, to have power" (the root of Greek mēkhanē "device" and, via Latin machina, English machine). 2. Proto-Germanic *maganą "to be able, to have strength, to avail". 3. Old English magan "to be able, to be allowed, to avail"; present tense mæg (the form "may" descends from mæg by regular sound change), attested from the 8th century. 4. Modern English may, modal auxiliary of possibility and permission. Chain — hap 1. Proto-Indo-European *kob- "to suit, fit, succeed" (Pokorny; the reconstruction is widely repeated but rests on slender Germanic evidence — debated). 2. Proto-Germanic *hampaz / *hamb- "fitting, suitable, convenient" (cf. Old English gehæp "fit, convenient, opportune"). 3. Old Norse happ "chance, good luck, fortune" (the word borrowed into English; its sense drifts from "good fortune" to neutral "chance, event"). 4. Middle English hap "chance, luck, an event, what befalls" (12th–15th c.; the source of hap, hapless, haphazard, happen, and perhaps). 5. Early Modern English: the phrase it may hap "it may happen" is contracted into the adverb mayhap "perhaps" (attested 16th c., Shakespeare-era). 6. Early Modern English: an adverbial -s (on the model of perhaps, unawares, needs) is appended, yielding mayhaps (attested late 16th – 17th c.). MAYHAPS. Debated / nuanced note: the PIE root of hap is not secure. Some authorities derive Old Norse happ from *kob- "to fit, succeed" (Pokorny's IEW, sense "to suit"); others regard the word as having no reliable Indo-European etymology and allow a substrate or independent Germanic formation. The final -s is analogical (an adverbial genitive/plural ending borrowed from the pattern of perhaps), not part of the original compound.
