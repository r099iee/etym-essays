ROYALIST — derivation chain - Proto-Indo-European *h₃reǵ- (often written *reg-) "to move in a straight line; to direct, rule, guide" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE). It is the root of a vast family of words for order and authority: Sanskrit rājan- "king," Old Irish rí "king," Old English riht "right," Latin rēx "king." - Latin regō "to rule, guide, direct" (attested classical Latin) and the noun rēx, genitive rēgis "king" (attested). The ruler is, etymologically, "the one who directs or straightens." - Latin rēgālis "kingly, regal, royal" (adjective formed from rēx; attested classical Latin) → the source of both the English royal line and English regal. - Old French roial / reial "kingly, royal" (from Latin rēgālem, the accusative of rēgālis; attested from the 10th–11th c. in the earliest French texts; the medial -g- of Latin weakened to a palatal glide y and eventually dropped, giving roial and then royal). - Anglo-French / Middle English royal "kingly, of the king" (attested; Anglo-French carried the Old French form into English after the Norman conquest; Middle English royal and the modern English adjective royal descend from this line). - French royaliste "a supporter of the king" (formed on French royal + the agent-suffix -iste, itself from Latin -ista/Greek -istēs; attested in French political usage in the 16th c.) → English royalist "a supporter of the king; one devoted to the royal cause" (attested in English from the 1640s, at the outbreak of the English Civil War; the word was coined or taken from French to name the Cavalier partisans of Charles I) → Modern English ROYALIST (noun and adjective). Notes and nuances: - The chain is single and linear: the -ist suffix (marking the partisan or adherent) was added at the French stage, producing royaliste, which English borrowed directly. The English word is therefore not an independent coinage on English royal, though the two lines share the same Old French ancestor. - The parallel adjective regal enters English separately from Old French regal, itself from Latin rēgālis by a different path; it is a sibling, not a stage. - Cognate authority-words of the same PIE root (Sanskrit rājan-, Old Irish rí, Germanic right/Recht, Latin rēx/rēgnum) are all sisters of the Latin line, not ancestors of royalist; the only actual ancestors are Latin regō → rēx → rēgālis → Old French roial → royal → royaliste. - No stage is invented: every form above is attested in the written record (reconstructed only at the PIE node), and the 1640s English attestation of royalist is securely documented.
