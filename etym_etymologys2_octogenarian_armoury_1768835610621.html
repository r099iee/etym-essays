octogenarian — a numeral compound; the two components of the Latin base are traced together through the attested stages: - Proto-Indo-European *oḱtṓ (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'eight' (so stable it survives almost unchanged in Sanskrit aṣṭá, Greek oktṓ, Latin octō and Proto-Germanic *ahtō) - Latin octō (3rd c. BCE–, attested) — 'eight' - Latin octōgintā (2nd c. BCE–, attested) — 'eighty', formed by joining octō to the ten-fold suffix -gintā, a collateral form of the PIE word for 'ten', *déḱm̥ - Latin octōgēnī (Classical Latin, attested) — 'eighty each' (distributive numeral) - Latin octōgēnārius (Imperial/Late Latin, attested) — 'of eighty years; belonging to an eighty-year-old' - English octogenarian (17th c., learned borrowing from Latin; the -ian/-an follows the pattern of sexagenarian, septuagenarian) — 'aged between eighty and eighty-nine' - Modern English octogenarian. Note: no Germanic or French vernacular stage intervenes — this is a direct learned borrowing of the Latin adjective with an English suffix, which is why the Latin stem is preserved nearly intact.
armoury — single linear chain through Latin and Old French: - Proto-Indo-European *h₂er- (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'to fit, join together' (also the source of Sanskrit ṛtá 'cosmic order', Greek ararískō 'to fit', harmónia 'joint, concord') - Latin arma, neuter plural (3rd c. BCE–, attested) — 'tools, gear, weapons — the things fitted for war' - Latin armāre (2nd c. BCE–, attested) — 'to equip, to arm' - Vulgar Latin / Late Latin *armaria, armariam (c. 4th–6th c. CE, reconstructed) — 'storehouse of equipment, armoury' - Old French armerie / armoierie (c. 12th c., attested) — 'place where arms are kept; armoury' - Anglo-Norman armorie (c. 13th c., attested) — 'armoury, store of arms' - Middle English armerie / armorie (c. 14th c., attested) — 'storehouse for arms, arsenal' - Modern English armoury (BrE; armory, AmE) (16th c.–) — 'place where weapons and military equipment are kept' - Modern English armoury. Note: the -our- spelling is the British retention of the French and Anglo-Norman form; the American 'armory' drops the 'u' by nineteenth-century spelling reform. The heraldic sense ('the science of coats of arms') grows from the same arma root, via French armoirie.
