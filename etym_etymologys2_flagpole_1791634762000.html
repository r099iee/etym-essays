This compound noun combines two distinct historical elements: the fluttering cloth ensign flag + the slender vertical wooden or metal post pole.

[Root 1: flag (fluttering cloth / ensign)]
Proto-Indo-European (uncertain imitative root)
*(s)plak- / *bʰlak-
"to flap, flutter, beat in the wind"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*flak- / *flakkōną
"to flutter, wave, flap loosely"
↓
Middle Low German / Middle Dutch (c. 14th–15th c.)
vlagge / flagge
"fluttering cloth banner, maritime ensign"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
flagge / flag
"cloth ensign flown for identification"

[Root 2: pole (slender wooden staff)]
Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*pāg- / *peh₂g-
"to fasten, fix, make firm"
↓

*pāks-los
"stake fixed into the ground"
↓
Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
pālus
"stake, prop, pale, wooden post"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 1st–3rd c. CE early loan)
*pāluz
"stake, pole, upright wooden beam"
↓

"stake, pole, post, measuring rod"
↓

↓

[Compound Formation]
Early 19th Century American English (c. 1826)
flag-pole
"tall vertical pole for flying a flag"
↓
Modern English (late 19th c.–present)
flagpole
"freestanding or architectural mast
for hoisting flags; corporate idiom"

Note: English traditionally used 'flagstaff' (formed with native Germanic 'staff', Old English 'stæf') as the standard nautical and civic term for several centuries. In the early 19th century, American English developed the compound 'flagpole' (utilizing 'pole', an early West Germanic borrowing from Latin 'pālus'). Over the course of the 19th and 20th centuries, 'flagpole' became the universal everyday term in North American English, while 'flagstaff' is preserved in formal, heraldic, and British nautical registers.
