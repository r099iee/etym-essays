Bulgaria is a country name that preserves the memory of a people who no longer exist as a distinct group. The Bulgars were a Turkic-speaking steppe confederation; the Bulgarians are a Slavic-speaking nation. The word's history is the record of that absorption, and it explains why the name looks Turkic while the language, religion and culture are overwhelmingly Slavic and Orthodox.

The ethnonym and its disputed origin. The Bulgars appear in late antique sources from the fifth century onward, associated with the steppe between the Volga, the Don and the Caucasus. Their name is recorded in Greek as Boúlgaroi (Βούλγαροι), in Latin as Bulgari, and in Old Turkic forms such as Bulɣar. The most widely repeated etymology connects it with a Turkic verb bulga-, "to stir, mix", giving the sense "the mixed people" or "those of mixed stock". A second tradition reads it as "rebels" (from a verb meaning "to stir up, confuse"), and a third appeals to a river name. None is demonstrable, and careful reference works label the origin uncertain. What is certain is that the name was already an ethnonym before it became a place-name.

From Old Great Bulgaria to the Danube. In the seventh century the Bulgar leader Kubrat united several steppe groups in a confederation that Byzantine writers called Old Great Bulgaria. After his death the confederation fragmented; one son, Asparuh, led a group across the Danube into the Roman province of Moesia, defeated the Byzantine army in 681, and established a state with its capital at Pliska. The treaty of 681 is conventionally taken as the foundation of the First Bulgarian Empire, and it is the moment at which "Bulgaria" becomes a Balkan territory rather than a steppe people.

  • Demographic fusion: the incoming Bulgars were a warrior elite ruling a much larger Slavic and Romanised population; within two centuries they had adopted a Slavic language and, in 864, Orthodox Christianity.
  • Script and liturgy: the disciples of Cyril and Methodius, notably Clement of Ohrid, established Slavonic learning; the Cyrillic alphabet was developed in the Preslav school in the late ninth century.
  • Imperial rivalry: under Simeon I (893–927) Bulgaria challenged Byzantium for hegemony in the Balkans and briefly claimed the imperial title.

Byzantine conquest and the Second Empire. Basil II, nicknamed Boulgaroktónos, "the Bulgar-slayer", annexed the state in 1018 after the battle of Kleidion (1014). Byzantine rule lasted until 1185, when the brothers Asen and Peter raised a revolt and founded the Second Bulgarian Empire centred on Tarnovo. That state survived until 1396, when the Ottomans completed their conquest. During nearly five centuries of Ottoman rule, "Bulgaria" persisted as a name for a land and a church: the Bulgarian millet and later the Bulgarian Exarchate (1870) kept the identity alive, and the vernacular literary revival of the 19th century (Paisii of Hilandar, 1762) made the name a national programme.

The modern state and its dates. The April Uprising of 1876 and the "Bulgarian horrors" controversy in the British press — Gladstone's pamphlet and Disraeli's Realpolitik — put the name on every European front page. The Treaty of Berlin (1878) created an autonomous principality of Bulgaria and a separate province of Eastern Rumelia, which united with it in 1885. Full independence was declared in 1908. Bulgaria fought in the Balkan Wars, allied with Germany in both world wars, was occupied by the Red Army in 1944, and became a People's Republic in 1946 under Georgi Dimitrov, later dominated by Todor Zhivkov (1954–89). The regime fell in November 1989; a new constitution followed in 1991; NATO membership came in 2004 and EU membership in 2007.

Cultural associations. The name carries a cluster of familiar associations: rose oil from the Kazanlak valley; yoghurt and Lactobacillus bulgaricus; the Cyrillic alphabet, which Bulgaria regards as its national gift to the Slavic world (celebrated on 24 May); Orthodox chant and the notni chant tradition; the horos and the uneven rhythms of Bulgarian folk music, which fascinated Bartók; and the great monasteries of Rila and Bachkovo.

Word shape. The English form comes from Medieval Latin Bulgaria, with the regular Latin suffix -ia for a country, and corresponds to Greek Boulgaria and Old Church Slavonic Blŭgarīja. The Bulgarian endonym is Bŭlgariya; the transliteration varies (Bulgaria, Bulgariya), and the stressed -a- in English /bʌlˈɡeəriə/ reflects the Latin form rather than the Bulgarian one. The word's 1,500-year journey — steppe ethnonym, Balkan empire, Ottoman province, modern republic — has left it stable in form but extraordinarily layered in reference.
