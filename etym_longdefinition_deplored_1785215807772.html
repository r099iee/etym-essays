Deplored is the past participle of the verb deplore, "to feel or express deep sorrow, grief, or regret for; to lament; to regard as regrettable or bad." In the present context it appears in the solemn dedication of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859): Mill writes to "the beloved and deplored memory" of his late wife, Harriet Taylor Mill, who died in 1858 — "the inspirer, and in part the author, of all that is best in my writings." Here the participle is used in its oldest, most emotive sense: not the modern "to disapprove of," but the classical "to weep for, mourn, lament inconsolably." The nuance is grief with reverence: the memory is not merely regretted but actively mourned, bathed in tears, and honoured as sacred. Deplored in this dedication means "lamented and mourned with the deepest personal love" — the tone of a eulogy and a monument combined, the past participle holding the completed act of mourning before the reader.

The word must be set along the scale that runs from grief to censure, because both senses are alive and the history of the word is precisely the movement between them. The mourning sense: to deplore a death, a loss, a passing is to weep for it — deplore here is "lament," "bewail," "mourn," and it is at home in dedications, elegies, and obituaries. The regret sense: to deplore an action or state of affairs is to regard it with regret and disapproval — one deplores a decision, a state of affairs, an outbreak. The censure sense (dominant in modern use): to deplore violence, corruption, racism, or an atrocity is to condemn it strongly and publicly, the word functioning as a mild but firm form of denunciation in editorials and official statements — "the Council deplores these attacks." The three senses shade into one another through the idea of heartfelt negative judgment: what is deplored is felt as a wrong, and the word registers the feeling — tears in the oldest sense, moral anger in the newest. In Mill's sentence the moral force is still present — he deplores the loss of her moral inspiration — but the register is sorrow, not censure.

Collocations reflect the double life: with objects of grief (deplored the death, the loss, the passing), with objects of disapproval (deplored the violence, the decision, the state of affairs, the treatment), and with intensifying adverbs (deeply, greatly, openly, publicly). The adjective deplorable, "lamentable, regrettable; (modern) shameful, disgraceful," and the adverb deplorably complete the family. The register is formal and literary throughout; deplored is at home in dedications, obituaries, editorials, and diplomatic statements, and markedly out of place in casual speech. The past participle form is used both in the simple past ("she deplored the loss") and in the passive ("the memory, deplored by all"), and in the present context it is the participle modifying "memory" that carries the full weight of a man dedicating his life's work to the woman he called the inspirer and part-author of all that is best in his writings — a single word that compresses grief, reverence, and moral homage into one completed act of mourning.
