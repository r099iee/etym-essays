1. The two building blocks. Modern without is not a single inherited word but a fossilised compound of two ancient elements that each have long, independent histories. The first component descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *wi-, meaning "apart, away, in two". This root appears across the Indo-European family: in Sanskrit ví ("apart, asunder"), in Latin vitare ("to avoid", literally to turn apart), and in Greek forms built on the idea of separation. Through regular Germanic sound changes this root yielded the Proto-Germanic adjective/adverb *wiþra- ("against, opposite"), attested most clearly in Gothic wiþra ("against, opposite"). Old English inherited the shortened form wiþ with the meaning "against, opposite, toward". The second component descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *úd ("up, out, away"), which yielded Proto-Germanic *ūt "out" and then Old English ūt "out, forth" and the adverbial genitive ūtan "from outside". Cognates include Sanskrit úd "up, out" and Gothic ūt "out".

2. Formation of the compound in Old English. At some point in the Old English period, probably by the later first millennium, these two elements were fused: wiþ + ūtan produced the compound adverb-preposition wiþūtan (also written wīþūtan), meaning "outside, on the outside". The semantics at this stage were strictly spatial: wiþūtan described a position exterior to a place, object, or boundary, and it could be used adverbially ("on the outside") or prepositionally ("outside of"). This spatial sense is abundantly attested in Old English prose and poetry, where it often forms the antonym pair binnan and wiþūtan ("within and without") — a pairing that has survived into modern set phrases.

3. The Middle English privative shift. The decisive semantic change occurred in Middle English (roughly the 12th–15th centuries). Alongside and partly in competition with the parallel development of with itself — whose Old English meaning "against" flipped to "in company with" under the influence of the closely related Old Norse við, which carried both senses — the compound withoute(n) began to take on the privative meaning "not having, in the absence of". The semantic bridge is transparent and can be stated precisely: what lies "outside" one's hands, one's house, or one's reach is what one does not possess. The spatial exterior of a person's possession became a metaphor for lack, so that “wiðūtan ēhta” ("outside property") was reanalysed as "not having property". By the end of the Middle English period, attested in Chaucer and in Wycliffite and mystery-play texts, the privative sense had become at least as common as the spatial one, and the form had settled as withoute / without after the loss of the final -n (a general Middle English weakening of final syllables).

4. Phonetic development. The compound underwent several convergent sound changes. The Old English long ū in ūtan shortened in the unstressed second element of the compound, and the final -an weakened and then fell away, leaving without(e). The initial wiþ- element kept its þ (the "th" sound), which in modern Received Pronunciation surfaces either as the voiced fricative /ð/ before a following vowel (as in without us, giving /wɪˈðaʊt/) or as the voiceless /θ/ in careful or phonological contexts (giving /wɪθˈaʊt/). The modern form is thus the end-point of millennia of Germanic consonant shifts, medial vowel weakening, and final-syllable loss.

5. Modern status and fixed phrases. By the early modern period, English felt the need to disambiguate the two senses. The spatial meaning was ceded to outside (and to the renewed coinage withoutside and the phrase without doors), while without specialised almost entirely in privation. This division of labour is why without today reads as a purely negative "not having" word, and why its spatial past survives only in fixed expressions (within and without), in legal and financial jargon (without prejudice; selling a share "without" = excluding the dividend), in mathematics (without loss of generality, abbreviated WLOG), and in deliberately archaic or poetic writing. The King James Bible (1611) preserves the older spatial sense in passages such as "the people which is without" and "without the camp". In contemporary English, without is one of the two or three most frequent negative prepositions in the language, serving as the indispensable negator of accompaniment, instrument, possession, condition, and concession. Its history is a model case of how a spatial preposition can become a purely logical or privative operator through a gradual, metaphorically grounded semantic drift.
