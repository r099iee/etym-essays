The English verb forego (frequently spelled forgo in modern standard orthography) is a nuanced lexical term that encompasses two distinct semantic branches that have intertwined over centuries of Germanic and English linguistic history. In its most prevalent contemporary usage—the precise meaning employed in the source reading—forego signifies to voluntarily abstain from, do without, give up, relinquish, or waive an enjoyment, pleasure, convenience, benefit, or course of action. In its separate, older etymological sense, it means to precede, go before, or lead the way in time, sequence, or spatial placement.

Primary Senses and Functional Applications:

  • Voluntary Renunciation and Abstinence: In moral, practical, and everyday contexts, to forego denotes making a conscious, deliberate decision to pass up a desirable opportunity, indulgence, or satisfaction, often in deference to a higher duty, long-term objective, ethical conviction, or practical constraint. Examples include foregoing rich desserts during a dietary regimen, foregoing leisure to study for professional examinations, or, as in the Trobriand anthropological passage, refusing to forego sexual intimacy despite supernatural precautions against pregnancy.
  • Temporal and Sequential Precedence: In its original morphological formulation (*fore-go*, "to go before"), the verb means to precede chronologically, logically, or physically. While the base verb in this sense is somewhat archaic in contemporary conversation, its participial forms remain deeply entrenched in standard formal English:
    • The foregoing: Used as an adjective or nominalized collective noun designating statements, arguments, chapters, or evidence previously mentioned or appearing earlier in a text (e.g., "Based on the foregoing analysis, the court finds for the plaintiff").
    • A foregone conclusion: An immortal English idiom originally coined by William Shakespeare in Othello (Act III, Scene 3), denoting an inevitable result, predetermined outcome, or decision arrived at prior to the examination of evidence.
  • Legal, Commercial, and Contractual Surrender (Waiver): In legal drafting, finance, and corporate negotiations, to forego is employed as a synonym for formal waiver or relinquishment—such as an executive foregoing an annual performance bonus, an injured party foregoing the right to litigation in exchange for an out-of-court settlement, or a sovereign nation foregoing territorial claims under a bilateral treaty.

The Orthographic Duality: *Forego* versus *Forgo*:

The spelling of this verb represents one of the most famous and persistent orthographic dilemmas in the English language, reflecting the collision of two completely distinct Old English verbal prefixes:

  • The Prefix *for-*: In Old English, for- (cognate with German ver-) was a privative, intensive, and ablative prefix indicating loss, departure, exclusion, rejection, or destruction (as seen in forbid, forlorn, forsake, forget, and forgive). The Old English verb forgān literally signified "to pass by, go away from, leave undone, or abstain from."
  • The Prefix *fore-*: In contrast, Old English fore- (cognate with German vor- and related to before) was a spatial and temporal prefix indicating priority, advance placement, or precedence (as seen in forecast, forefather, foretell, foresee, and foreword). The Old English verb foregān literally signified "to go in front of, precede, or walk ahead."

Semantic Convergence and Prescriptive Guidelines:

During the Middle English period (1150–1500), unstressed vowels in prefixes underwent systemic phonetic reduction, neutralizing the acoustic difference between /fɔːr-/ and /fər-/. Scribes and early printers began using the spellings forgo and forego indiscriminately for both meanings. Throughout the Early Modern period—including the works of Shakespeare, John Milton, the King James Bible, and nineteenth-century literature—forego was overwhelmingly preferred by writers and typesetters for both the "abstain" and "precede" senses.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, modern prescriptive style guides (such as Garner's Modern English Usage, Fowler's Modern English Usage, and the Chicago Manual of Style) have vigorously advocated a functional orthographic separation: reserving forgo strictly for "abstain/relinquish" and forego strictly for "precede/go before." However, comprehensive historical dictionaries (including the Oxford English Dictionary and Merriam-Webster) confirm that forego remains an established, widely accepted alternative variant for "abstain" across prestigious literary and academic English, exactly as demonstrated in Will Durant's prose.
