scowled is the past tense and past participle of the verb scowl, and it is also used as a participial adjective. The word names the act of contracting the face into a severe, angry, or bad-tempered expression: the eyebrows are drawn down and together, the forehead wrinkles, and the corners of the mouth are usually pulled down or the lips compressed. A scowl is a public, visible display of displeasure — unlike mere sadness, it is directed outward and carries an edge of hostility, contempt, frustration, or disgust.

PRIMARY SENSE — the angry facial expression (verb, past tense)

As a finite verb, scowled records a past action of frowning in an angry or ill-tempered way. It is normally intransitive and is most often followed by a directional preposition such as at, over, across, upon, or on:

  • To scowl at someone — to direct a hostile, frowning gaze at a person, signalling anger, contempt, or open disapproval. Example: "The teacher scowled at the whispering pupils."
  • To scowl over / across at someone — the exact construction in the context card ("scowled over at Lennie"), where the directionality of the look across a physical space is emphasised.
  • To scowl upon / on something — an older, more literary construction, often used of personified fate or fortune "scowling upon" an undertaking.
  • Absolute use — to scowl without any object, simply wearing the angry face as a settled expression: "He sat in the corner and scowled."

SECONDARY SENSE — the noun scowl

The same word family produces a noun, scowl, meaning the angry facial expression itself — a deep frown of displeasure or hostility. "A scowl crossed his face," "her face was fixed in a scowl." The noun and the verb share every nuance, and scowled as a past participle functions adjectivally in phrases like "a scowled reply" (a reply given while frowning) or "scowled brows" (brows knitted in anger).

TERTIARY SENSE — transferred and figurative applications

Because the verb describes a face that looks dark, threatening, and closed, it is routinely transferred to non-human subjects that present a forbidding appearance:

  • Of the sky and weather — "a scowling sky," "the scowling clouds": dark, lowering, threatening weather, as if the heavens themselves were frowning. This is the atmospheric sense that links the word to storm and shadow.
  • Of landscape, buildings, and features — "scowling cliffs," "a scowling fortress": scenery or architecture that looks gloomy, inhospitable, and menacing.
  • Of personified abstractions — Shakespeare's English uses scowl of fortune and fate looking darkly upon human affairs, giving the word a tragic, fatalistic colouring.
  • Of eyes and faces — "scowling eyes," "a scowl of contempt": the expression itself may be detached from the whole face and attributed to a single feature.

MODERN REGISTER AND USAGE

In modern English scowled is chiefly a literary, narrative word — the stock verb of fiction for a character's visible anger. It is stronger than frowned, which may indicate mere concentration or worry, and it is closer to glowered and glared, though it emphasises the drawn-down brows and compressed mouth rather than a wide-eyed stare. It appears in journalism ("he scowled at the press"), in stage directions, and in everyday speech when someone describes an openly hostile expression.

HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE

Behind the modern "angry face" sense lies a much older notion of looking out from under a lowered cover. The word enters English from Scandinavian, where the parent verb meant "to cast down the eyes, look askance, squint, or lower the brows." That downward, shadowed gaze — the posture of someone peering from concealment or drawing the brows down like storm-clouds to shade the eyes — hardened in English into the specific, overt signal of angry displeasure. The atmospheric "scowling sky" preserves this ancient bridge: a brow drawn down over the eyes is metaphorically the same lowering shadow that darkens the heavens before a storm.
