EVOLUTION OF TINDER-BOXES: TWO ANCIENT WORDS, ONE VOLATILE COMPOUND Tinder-boxes is a transparent English compound, and its biography is really two biographies joined by a hyphen. The first element, tinder, is one of the oldest fire-words of the Germanic languages. Its ancestor is the Proto-Germanic noun *tundr-, 'tinder, combustible material', built on the Germanic verb-base *tund-, 'to kindle, set fire to' - the same base that appears in Gothic tundnan, 'to be kindled', and Old Norse tenda, 'to kindle'. The ultimate origin of this base is uncertain: no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon has been agreed, and the OED treats the root as of obscure origin. The noun itself is well attested across the family: Old English tynder, Old Norse tundr, and Old High German zuntra all name the dry flammable substance that catches a spark - charred cloth, dried fungus, or shredded bark - and all descend from the common Germanic word. English inherited it directly: Old English tynder became Middle English tinder and modern English tinder, the word for the fuel that makes fire possible. It is a pleasant modern irony that the ancient fire-word now survives most famously as the trade-name of a phone application whose whole business is the 'spark' of a first match - a commercial metaphor built on the oldest meaning of the word. The second element, box, has a completely different and older Mediterranean history. It begins with Greek pyxos, 'box-tree, Buxus sempervirens', attested from the fifth century BCE. The box-tree was named for its extremely hard, fine-grained, yellow wood, and the origin of pyxos itself is uncertain - no secure Indo-European etymology is agreed, and a non-Greek, possibly Mediterranean substrate source is suspected. From the tree came the container: Greek pyxis (genitive pyxidos), 'a box or case, originally one of boxwood', attested from the fifth century BCE as the word for the small boxes that held cosmetics, perfumes, and the pebbles or ballots of Athenian voting. The Romans borrowed the word as pyxis or buxis - the spelling with the Italic b- is the ancestor of the Western forms - and from Latin buxis the word entered Old English directly as box, recorded from the eighth and ninth centuries, long before the Norman Conquest brought French influence. English box is thus one of the earliest Latin loanwords in the language, in use for well over a thousand years. The meeting of the two roots (17th century) The two words met in the seventeenth century to form the compound. The tinder-box, in its hyphenated form, is attested from the late seventeenth century: a small box holding tinder, flint, and a steel striker, the standard means of kindling a fire before the friction match appeared. For the households, travellers, and soldiers of the early modern world, the tinder-box was ordinary daily hardware - the box that held the means of fire itself - and losing one was a genuine small calamity. The word named the whole kit in one compound: fuel, striker, and container, everything needed to turn a spark into a blaze. The hyphenated spelling tinder-box remained standard for the literal object, and the word entered the vocabulary of every guide, traveller, and domestic economy of the period. The match makes the box obsolete (early 19th century) In the early nineteenth century the friction match made the tinder-box obsolete as living technology. The match - a splint of wood tipped with a combustible paste struck against a prepared surface - was faster, cheaper, and more reliable than the old flint-and-tinder kit, and within a generation the tinder-box retreated into the historical and period vocabulary. The literal sense did not die; it simply became the property of museums, historical novels, and period fiction, where it does exactly the work Tolkien gives it: it places a story in a world before matches, where a fire must be struck with flint and steel and where losing one's tinder-box is a real difficulty. The metaphor (19th century to the present) Even as the object became obsolete, the word gained a new life as a metaphor. From the nineteenth century onward, tinderbox - now usually written solid and without the hyphen - names a situation or region ready to burst into flame: a powder-keg of a different substance. The Balkans have been called the tinderbox of Europe, a volatile border region the tinderbox of the Middle East, and any stock of grievances waiting for a spark a tinderbox. The metaphor is a perfect one, because the tinder-box's whole purpose was readiness: the tinder inside would catch a spark at the slightest touch and hold it until it was blown into flame. A political tinderbox is a situation in which the smallest incident - a rumour, an insult, an accident - can set off a conflagration. In the twenty-first century the literal object is entirely historical, while the metaphor is more alive than ever, and in Tolkien's The Hobbit the ancient, homely compound does its period work: Oin and Gloin have lost their tinder-boxes, and the loss of the fire-kit on a mountain is a small, concrete calamity that grounds the epic adventure in the practical detail of a pre-modern world - the world that the word itself came from.
