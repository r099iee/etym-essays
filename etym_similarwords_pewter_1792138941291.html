• Britannia metal: Similar because both are tin-based alloys utilized for casting tableware and decorative vessels; differs because Britannia metal is specifically a modern, high-grade, completely lead-free alloy of tin, antimony, and copper that can be rolled into sheets and spun on lathes, whereas historical pewter was predominantly cast in molds and frequently contained significant amounts of lead.
  • Tinware: Similar because tin is the primary metal in both substances; differs because tinware consists of thin sheets of iron or steel electroplated with a protective coating of tin (tinplate) to prevent rusting, whereas pewter is a solid, homogeneous cast alloy throughout.
  • Sterling silver: Similar because both have served throughout history as prestige domestic tableware and luxury presentation items; differs because sterling silver is an expensive precious metal composed of 92.5% pure silver and 7.5% copper with a bright reflective shine, whereas pewter is a base-metal alloy characterized by a muted, darker, non-precious patina.
  • Latten: Similar because both were standard base-metal alloys used in medieval church fixtures, spoons, and domestic utensils; differs because latten was a brass-like alloy of copper and zinc (similar to bronze or brass) exhibiting a warm golden-yellow color, in sharp contrast to the cool grayish-silver of pewter.
  • Pot metal: Similar because both are low-melting base-metal alloys used in casting; differs because pot metal (or white metal) is a cheap, brittle, mechanically inferior alloy composed primarily of zinc, lead, and scrap metals used for disposable industrial hardware, lacking the food-safe historical refinement and artisan prestige of fine pewter.
  • Peltre: Similar because it is the exact Spanish and historical Romance cognate for pewter; differs because peltre in modern Latin American usage often colloquially refers to enameled steel cookware (graniteware or speckled camp mugs) rather than solid tin alloy.
