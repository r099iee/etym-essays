Starveling is a noun — and, secondarily, an adjective — for a living creature worn to skin and bone by hunger: a famished child, a neglected stray, a stunted plant, anything dwarfed and hollowed by want of nourishment. The word carries pity and contempt in equal measure, and the pity is usually uppermost: to call a creature a starveling is to accuse whoever should have fed it.

In the source sentence the word is flung as a stable-yard insult: ‘What dost thou, starveling hack!’ One horse (or ass) sneers at another — a hack being a worn-out hired horse — doubling the abuse: not only a broken-down hireling but a half-starved one, ribs showing, belly tucked, coat staring. The fable's comedy runs on such mutual scorn between beasts who are none of them well fed, and starveling lands precisely because every listener can picture the bony, rough-coated animal at once.

Core meaning in detail: Morphologically the word is starve + -ling, the old diminutive and depreciative suffix (as in weakling, hireling, underling): literally ‘a little starver’, ‘a poor creature that starves’. As a noun it denotes the sufferer — ‘a starveling at the workhouse door’; as an adjective it denotes the condition — ‘a starveling stray’, ‘starveling crops’. The suffix adds a curl of the lip: a starveling is not merely hungry but characteristically half-starved, stunted, never-to-thrive — hunger as identity rather than accident.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A person or animal emaciated by hunger (primary sense, 16th c. onward): ‘the starvelings of the famine years’. The bridge is direct composition: one who starves, marked by the diminutive as pitiable and small. Dickensian London, Irish famine roads, and every besieged city in literature are crowded with starvelings.
  • A stunted, undersized specimen of any kind (extended sense): ‘a starveling tree’, ‘starveling hopes’. The bridge runs from bodily wasting to any arrested growth: a sapling in poor soil is starved exactly as a child on short rations is, and the word follows the analogy gladly.
  • A mean, scraggy, good-for-nothing fellow (contemptuous, Shakespearean): Shakespeare's Petruchio calls a servant ‘a starved fellow’, and the Holofernes set use starveling for a thin, squeaking tailor — think of Starveling the tailor in A Midsummer Night's Dream, named for his trade's proverbial leanness (‘nine tailors make a man’). The bridge is the old joke that hunger makes men shrivel in spirit as well as flesh.
  • A miser, one who starves himself or others (figurative): ‘a starveling usurer’. The bridge turns deprivation inward: the miser is a self-made starveling, and his household starves with him — the word accuses the withholder, not the weather.
  • Adjectival use — half-starved, meagre, inadequate (from 16th c.): ‘a starveling portion’, ‘starveling wages’. The bridge is conversion: the noun's pity becomes an adjective for anything too thin to sustain life — portions, salaries, excuses, applause.

Register and connotation: starveling is literary and old-fashioned — sermons, novels, and fables rather than food-bank reports, which prefer malnourished. Its archaism is its force: the word summons Oliver Twist, Lowood School, and famine lithographs, and it judges as it pities. As an insult, as in the fable, it is stable-yard blunt — the animal equivalent of calling a man a scarecrow. Modern charities avoid it precisely because of that sneer; novelists keep it precisely for the same reason.
