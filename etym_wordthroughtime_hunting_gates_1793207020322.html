- Old English — the barrier word: geat names the gate, door, and opening; every homestead and town has one.
- Viking age — the merger: Norse gata (road, way) pours into northern English, so gate comes to mean both barrier and street — Coppergate is a road, not a door.
- Enclosure — the five-bar standard: as open fields are hedged and walled, the barred field gate becomes the universal joint of the farmed landscape.
- 1750–1850 — the hunting heyday: organized fox-hunting makes the gate a sporting object — opened for the field, jumped by the bold, cursed by Cobbett as squirearchy's latch.
- Victorian — literature fixes it: Surtees's and Trollope's riders measure every run in gates jumped and gates waited at; the gate is the novelist's fence-post of courage.
- 2004 — the hounds go: the Hunting Act ends hunting with dogs, but the gates stand on — farm furniture, bridleway furniture, and still jumped by riders out exercising.
- Today — five bars of heritage: hunting-gates are jumped, photographed, painted, and disputed at every bridleway inquiry; the red coats are rarer, the oak is not.
