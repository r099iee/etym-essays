The evolution of mummers is unusual among English words: it has no Indo-European pedigree, no classical Latin ancestor, and no chain of literary borrowings from Greek or French. Its origin is onomatopoeic — it begins in the oldest, most physical stratum of human language, the imitation of a sound the mouth makes. The whole biography is therefore a study of how a primitive hum could grow, across a few turbulent medieval centuries, into the name of one of Europe's most tenacious folk traditions, and how that name then receded, with the tradition, into history and folklore.

1. The imitative seed: the sound "mum." Close the lips and try to speak: the result is the resonant hum "m-m-m," a sound of enforced silence, of muttering, of a voice muffled behind cloth. This sound, or something like it, has been coined independently in language after language to mean "mute, silent, mum": English "mum," "mumble," and "mute"; Latin mūtus and murmur; Greek mýein "to close (the lips or eyes)"; Sanskrit mūka "dumb." Imitative vocabulary of this kind cannot be traced by the regular comparative method — it is not a shared inheritance but a shared human reflex — and that is precisely why the dictionaries of "mummer" all begin with the same honest disclaimer: origin uncertain, probably imitative. From this humble base, medieval Europe built a family of words for disguise: to be "mummed" was to be muffled, covered, hidden.

2. The medieval practice: mask, silence, and seasonal licence. In the later Middle Ages, across the North Sea cultures of England, France, the Netherlands, and the Rhineland, this cluster of "mum" words attached itself to a specific and immensely popular practice: the masked, often silent or distorted-voiced performance that took place in the deep winter, at Christmas, Twelfth Night, New Year, and Plough Monday. Middle Dutch mommen "to go about in disguise" and Old French momer "to play masked" name the act; mommerie names the spectacle. The performers — the mommers, the mummers — masked their faces, muffled their voices, and entered houses uninvited to dance, sing, play games, and perform the ritual drama of the year's turn. This was licensed disorder in the strictest sense: the masks annulled identity, so rank, gender, and social restraint could be suspended for a night, and the records of medieval and early modern towns show authorities repeatedly banning "mumming," "disguising," and "liveries" out of fear of the violence and scandal that disguised bands could bring. The word's colour was never purely innocent: a mummer was a reveller on the edge of respectability.

3. The folk drama: the Mummers' Play. Out of this matrix grew the particular English tradition that fixes the word's modern meaning: the mummers' play, a short ritual drama of death and resurrection surviving most famously in its Hero-Combat form. In the classic text, a champion (St. George, or a Turkish Knight, a Prince of Paradise) is killed and is revived by a quack Doctor — a pagan-looking cycle of death and rebirth folded into the Christian midwinter. The players travel in disguise from house to house, are paid in food, drink, or coins, and close their performance with a collection. Collectors from the eighteenth century onward — antiquarians such as John Brand, and later the folklorists of the Folklore Society and the English Folk Dance and Song Society — recorded these plays from Yorkshire to Hampshire, and the antiquarian revival of the Victorian era rekindled the tradition that had been fading through the eighteenth century. The word "mummer," which had been sliding toward the general sense of "masked reveller," was pulled back and re-attached to this specific genre of folk theatre.

4. Sound and form changes. Phonologically the word is unremarkable, and its history is a matter of spelling and suffix more than sound shift. The imitative base "mum" was extended by the agent suffix -er (or its Middle English -ere), giving "mummer" "one who mums," exactly as "runner" means one who runs; the spelling with double m stabilized in early modern English, and the plural "mummers," the form on this card, reflects the fact that the performers always appear as a company. The related noun "mummery" (Middle English mumerie), originally "a masked performance," went on to develop its own separate life as a word of scorn: a "mummery" became any ceremony felt to be empty, pompous, or hypocritical — "mere mummery" — and this satirical sense is now, arguably, more common in educated usage than the festive one.

5. Decline and institutional survival. As professional, scripted, indoor theatre displaced seasonal street drama, the mummers' tradition shrank to the villages, and with it the word. By the nineteenth century "mummer" was already being felt as an archaism — a word of the past, of the countryside, of folklore. Two twentieth-century institutions kept it alive: the revived English village mummers' plays, performed at Christmas and Plough Monday by modern folk groups, and above all the Philadelphia Mummers Parade, a vast New Year's Day spectacle in which thousands of costumed performers ("fancies," "comics," string bands) march through the city — a direct descendant of the eighteenth-century Swedish and English seasonal mumming brought to colonial Pennsylvania. In that parade the word has made a dramatic return to the present tense: today's "mummers" are costumed, masked, noisy, and unmistakably American, and the parade is one of the oldest continuous folk celebrations in the United States.

6. Summary of the semantic path.

  • Imitative "mum" (silence, muffling) → "one who mums," i.e. performs muffled and disguised (medieval) → "a performer of the seasonal masked folk play" (late medieval and modern) → "a member of the modern civic mummers' parade" (contemporary).
  • The word gained and lost the carnivalesque charge of the medieval midwinter: once a slightly dangerous word of licensed disorder, it is now a historical and folkloric word, dignified by antiquarian scholarship and kept vivid by the Philadelphia parade.
  • Its satirical offshoot "mummery" carried the energy of the old festival's critics into modern English, where empty ceremony is still "mummery" today.
  • No sound-shift narrative applies in the usual sense, because the word was never inherited through centuries of regular change; it was coined, spread, and respelled within the documented period of Middle and early modern English.

Literary attestations anchor the story at every stage: the civic records of fifteenth-century England that ban "mumming" and "disguisings"; the Christmas revels of the Tudor court; Shakespeare's England, where "mummers" appear among the entertainments of the great houses; the antiquarian collections of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries that preserved the plays; and the twentieth-century folklore revival that returned the word to active use. The hum of a muffled voice, the mask of a midwinter reveller, the death-and-resurrection play of a village square, and the glittering costumes of a Philadelphia New Year — all of it is contained in the six letters of the word on this card.
