Bacilli is the Latinate plural of bacillus, literally a little staff or rod. Its journey from Roman walking sticks to germ warfare and global pandemics spans two millennia and the entire germ revolution.

Latin baculum meant staff, stick, sceptre, cudgel - the emblem of walkers, shepherds, and magistrates. Diminutive bacillum / bacillus meant little stick, wand, or rod, used for pointers, sceptres, and paltry sticks. The form is transparent: bac- stick plus -illus diminutive. Cognates are sparse; de Vaan notes possible relation to Greek baktron staff, suggesting a Mediterranean wanderwort for stick rather than deep PIE inheritance, hence the cautious *bak- reconstruction.

  • Classical Latin: baculus / baculum as walking stick; bacillus rare, mostly for small sceptres or rods of office. No medical sense.
  • Medieval / Botanical Latin: bacillus revived for rod-like plant parts, coral branches, and liturgical wands. Shape metaphor, not germ.
  • Early microscopy (1670s-1850s): Leeuwenhoek sees animalcules including rods but calls them animalcules, not bacilli. Ehrenberg in 1835 coins Bacterium (from Greek bakterion little staff) for the same shape in Greek dress.

The decisive turn comes with German bacteriology. Ferdinand Cohn and Robert Koch need a genus name for rod-shaped spore-forming bacteria distinct from cocci (spheres) and spirilla (spirals). They Latinize as Bacillus (capitalized genus, 1872-1876), with plural bacilli. Koch's 1882 discovery of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, popularly the tubercle bacillus, and 1883 Vibrio cholerae make bacillus a household terror. English borrows bacillus in the 1880s and plural bacilli soon after; newspapers speak of comma bacilli, typhoid bacilli, plague bacilli.

Phonologically English keeps Latin spelling but anglicizes: /bəˈsɪləs/ singular, /bəˈsɪlaɪ/ plural in traditional English, /bəˈsɪliː/ in restored Latinate or medical speech. Both plurals accepted; -i is correct, bacilluses colloquial. Lowercase bacilli means any rod-shaped bacteria generically; capitalized Bacillus means the specific genus including B. anthracis (anthrax) and B. subtilis.

  • 1880s-1920s: bacilli as invisible enemies in public-health crusades: boil milk, wash hands, ventilate. Literature uses them as apocalyptic agents, as in the prompt where insinuating bacilli will devour man.
  • 1928-1950s: antibiotics (penicillin, streptomycin) turn bacilli from fate to treatable infection; tuberculosis sanatoria empty.
  • Present: strict taxonomy reserves Bacilli as class (Firmicutes including Bacillus, Listeria, Staphylococcus). Popular speech loosely says bacilli for any germs, even cocci and viruses, which microbiologists deplore but novelists love.

Culturally, bacilli embody modern anxiety: invisible, insinuating, democratic killers that ignore rank. To imagine bacilli devouring man and freeing the planet, as the prompt does, is early eco-revenge fantasy, prefiguring zombie plagues and Andromeda strains. The tiny rod, once a shepherd's stick, becomes nature's sceptre of judgment.
