• Earthy: Similar to earthen in relating to earth; differs because earthy has drifted into a figurative register meaning unrefined, bawdy, or "down-to-earth" in personality, while earthen stays purely literal — made of earth or clay.
  • Clay: Similar to earthen in being earth material; differs because clay is the noun naming the raw material itself, whereas earthen describes the finished quality of being made of earth, clay, or soil.
  • Adobe: Similar to earthen in being a building material of earth; differs because adobe is the specific mud-and-straw brick dried in the sun, far more precise than the general earthen.
  • Earthenware: Similar to earthen in the material of baked earth; differs because earthenware is a noun restricted to fired clay pottery, while earthen is an adjective of material that can modify walls, floors, or causeways.
  • Embankment: Similar to causeways in being a raised structure of earth; differs because an embankment is a wall or foundation built to hold back water or support a railway — a barrier first — while a causeway is a thoroughfare, a raised road across wet ground.
  • Bridge: Similar to causeways in enabling a crossing; differs because a bridge is a structure spanning a gap, carried on piers and open beneath, while a causeway is a solid raised bank crossing low or wet ground.
  • Viaduct: Similar to causeways in carrying a road across difficult terrain; differs because a viaduct is a long bridge of arches or piers over a valley or water, while a causeway is an earth-and-stone bank across a floodplain.
  • Dyke / Dike: Similar to causeways in being a raised bank; differs because a dyke is built to keep water OUT of low land, a drainage or flood barrier, while a causeway is built to carry traffic ACROSS wet ground.
  • Levee: Similar to causeways in a raised earthwork near water; differs because a levee is a river or coastal flood embankment protecting land, while a causeway is a raised road for passage.
  • Marsh: Similar to marshland as the same wetland terrain; differs because marsh is the simple noun, while marshland emphasizes the land as a terrain class or territory.
  • Swamp: Similar to marshland in being wet, low land; differs because a swamp is wetter and often wooded, more permanently flooded, while marshland is typically open, reedy, and often tidal.
  • Bog: Similar to marshland in being soft, waterlogged ground; differs because a bog is acidic, spongy peatland that accumulates moss and can swallow objects, while marshland is mineral, reedy wetland.
  • Fen: Similar to marshland in being wet lowland; differs because a fen is alkaline, fed by groundwater and rich in reeds (the English Fens), while marshland is the broader neutral term for tidal and coastal wetland.
  • Moor: Similar to marshland in being open, wet country; differs because a moor is high, open, often heathery ground, while marshland is low-lying wetland at the sea's edge.
  • Wetland: Similar to marshland in being land covered by water; differs because wetland is the modern ecological umbrella term for all waterlogged habitats (marsh, swamp, bog, fen), while marshland is the specific coastal or reedy class.
  • Morass: Similar to marshland in treacherous wet ground; differs because morass is literary and often figurative for any entanglement, while marshland is the neutral geographic term.
  • Quagmire: Similar to marshland in soft, sinking ground; differs because a quagmire emphasizes the danger of sinking and is often figurative, while marshland is a descriptive terrain word.
