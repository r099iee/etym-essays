Shrouds, the plural of shroud, gathers two great families of meaning under one spelling: cloths that wrap and ropes that steady. In its oldest sense a shroud is a garment or wrapping — above all the linen winding-sheet in which the dead are laid out — and by natural extension any thin covering that wraps, veils, or conceals, from grave-clothes to fog. In the source sentence it carries exactly this veiling sense: stray shrouds of mist drift across the face of Buck Hill, and through those ragged veils the round yellow and red windows shine — the mist imagined as torn linen wrappings half hiding the hill, with light glowing through the rents.

Core meaning in detail: The cloth sense begins with Old English scrūd, simply a garment or article of clothing. Death narrowed it: by the later Middle Ages the shroud above all meant the burial sheet, the plain linen in which a body was wrapped before coffining, when coffins were a luxury. From that solemn centre the word spread outward to anything that covers as a garment covers a body: a mantle of snow, a shroud of secrecy, and Tolkien's shrouds of mist. The rope sense begins alongside it from the same root's other Germanic branch, Old Norse skruð, gear or tackle: the standing rigging — the heavy lateral ropes or wires running from masthead to hull — that clothe and steady the mast as garments clothe a body. A sailor points aloft to the shrouds as readily as a mourner lays out the shroud; the spelling converged while the trades stayed distinct.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Burial garment or winding-sheet (primary historical sense, from Old English, narrowed by the 16th c.): the linen cloth wrapping the corpse, as in laid out in her shroud. The semantic bridge is narrowing by solemnity: among all garments, the one worn only once and never changed became the garment, and the general clothing-word shrank to the grave.
  • Any covering, veil, or mantle — mist, snow, fog (poetic and general, the source sense): shrouds of mist, a shroud of silence. The bridge is visual and tactile analogy: drifting mist wraps a hillside the way linen wraps a limb, hiding outlines, softening shapes, letting glints show through, as the Buck Hill windows shine through their mist-wrappings.
  • Standing rigging of a vessel (nautical, from the 15th–16th c.): the sets of ropes or wires (fore-shrouds, backstays, topmast shrouds) bracing the masts athwartships. The bridge runs through the Scandinavian sense of shroud as gear or clothing of the ship: the ropes dress and equip the bare pole exactly as clothes dress a body, and sailors still speak of a mast stripped of its shrouds like a man stripped of his coat.
  • To shroud — to wrap, conceal, or veil (verb, from the 16th c.): the hills were shrouded in mist, the deal was shrouded in secrecy. The bridge is straightforward denominal conversion: to put a shroud upon, whether of linen, weather, or silence, and hence to hide from view.
  • Protective casing or cover (technical modern): the metal or fabric shroud over machinery, a propeller-shroud or fan-shroud directing airflow, the burial-shroud of a cable joint. The bridge is the garment idea engineered: a fitted cover that clothes the mechanism, keeps weather out, and streamlines the form within.
  • Parachute suspension lines (aeronautical): the cords joining canopy to harness are called shroud lines. The bridge is direct nautical transfer: what steadies the mast steadies the canopy, rope for rope, and airmen borrowed the sailors' word complete with its seamanship.

Register and connotation: shrouds carries shadow even at its most innocent: the burial cloth stands behind every mist and metaphor, lending the word a hush that veil and cover lack. Sailors use it matter-of-factly aloft, engineers use it drily in workshops, and poets use it precisely for that penumbra — a covering that half reveals what it hides, as lamplight reveals itself through mist. Tolkien's stray shrouds keep both halves of the effect: ragged, drifting, slightly eerie wrappings, but with warm domestic windows burning through them, death's word softened into weather.
