AVERTED is the past tense and past-participle form of the English verb avert, which is a Renaissance-era borrowing from Latin avertere, literally "to turn away" - the prefix ab- ("away, off") joined to the verb vertere ("to turn"). Because the note records the inflected form, the word functions grammatically as a finite past-tense verb ("she averted her eyes"), as a perfective participle ("the strike has been averted"), and as a passive or participial adjective ("an averted disaster", "the averted gaze"). Across all of those grammatical roles the meaning splits into two great provinces that have coexisted since the word entered English in the late fifteenth century: a concrete, spatial sense of turning the eyes, face, or attention away from something; and a preventive, protective sense of turning aside a threat, danger, or misfortune before it can strike. Both provinces rest on the same root image - rotation plus deflection - so that the modern word never loses touch with the physical idea of "turning something away".

  • Sense 1 - Physical turning away of the eyes, gaze, or face. The oldest and most literal application. To avert one's eyes, gaze, look, face, or glance is to turn them deliberately aside, usually because what lies before one is painful, shocking, indecent, frightening, or unbearable. It is a chosen act, not an involuntary reflex: one averts the gaze out of tact, disgust, reverence, grief, or fear of being seen. This sense survives intact in modern English ("she averted her eyes from the gruesome scene") and descends directly from the Latin participle aversus, "turned away, averted", which already described a face or glance deliberately turned aside.
  • Sense 2 - Preventive turning aside of a threat or disaster. The dominant modern sense. To avert war, catastrophe, a strike, a famine, a collapse, or a failure is to deflect an undesirable event that is foreseeable or already imminent, acting while there is still time. The reasoning is metaphorical: the disaster is imagined as an object hurtling toward the speaker, and one "turns it aside" in the nick of time. The key nuance separating avert from the blander prevent is timing and direction - an averted danger was coming, and it was deflected at the last moment, whereas a prevented danger may simply have been made impossible in advance.
  • Sense 3 - Turning away of the thoughts, mind, or attention. A partial abstraction of Sense 1. One can avert the mind, the thoughts, or the attention from a painful subject, deliberately refusing to dwell on grief, guilt, scandal, or responsibility. The phrase is common in moral, psychological, and historical writing: "he averted his thoughts from the memory", "the public averted its attention from the crisis".
  • Sense 4 - The morally loaded "averted gaze" of denial or complicity. A strongly evaluative modern nuance. In journalism and political prose, "averting one's gaze" from an atrocity, an injustice, or a crime is a figure for willful ignorance - looking away rather than intervening. The collocation carries an implicit accusation of cowardice or complicity, and it is one of the most frequent contemporary uses of the participial form.

Grammar and morphology. As a regular weak verb, avert forms its past and participle with the ordinary -ed suffix: avert - averts - averting - averted. The form is virtually always transitive: one averts something - eyes, thoughts, a glance, a disaster, a suspicion. When used intransitively ("the danger averted" meaning "turned aside"), the construction is rare and archaic. In attributive position the participle is common: "averted crisis", "averted disaster", "averted tragedy", "the averted gaze". In the passive voice it reports a completed act of successful prevention and therefore carries a quiet note of relief - the feared thing did not happen.

Register and collocation. The word belongs to standard written English, slightly formal, and at home in news reporting, official statements, diplomacy, and narrative prose; in casual speech it yields to "looked away", "prevented", or "stopped". Its strongest collocations are gaze, eyes, face, glance, look, suspicion, disaster, catastrophe, war, conflict, crisis, famine, strike, tragedy, threat, and danger. "Averted" frequently appears in retrospective summaries: "war was averted", "a crisis was averted", "the strike was narrowly averted".

Technical and domain usage.

  • Risk management, insurance, and emergency services: "averted loss", "averted catastrophe", "near-averted accident" - standard in safety and crisis communications.
  • Epidemiology and public health: "averted cases", "averted deaths", "averted disability-adjusted life years" - in modelling the disease cases or deaths prevented by vaccines, masks, or lockdowns.
  • Economics and finance: "averted recession", "averted default" - in commentary about policy measures that head off economic collapse.
  • Diplomacy and international relations: the classic collocation "war averted" belongs to statecraft, treaty history, and retrospectives on international crises.

Historical semantic bridge. The word's career moves from the concrete to the abstract. Latin avertere meant to turn something physically around, away, or off - a ship, an army, a person's attention. Early English uses of avert (from the 1480s-1500s) kept the physical sense, chiefly of turning away the eyes or face, and by the seventeenth century the preventive sense - turning aside a blow, a plague, an evil - was firmly established, in part through biblical language about averting wrath and punishment. The two senses have coexisted ever since, with the preventive sense steadily gaining ground through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries until it became the default. On the card, the sentence "If war can be __________, it is my duty to avert it" uses the word in its modern preventive sense, making it a solemn moral claim: the speaker holds it his duty to turn aside war, the greatest of disasters, before it begins.
