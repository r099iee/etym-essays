Embroil is a transitive verb meaning to draw a person, group, or state into a complicated and troublesome situation — typically a quarrel, dispute, scandal, or war — from which escape is difficult. It is used overwhelmingly in the passive or with a reflexive object: one is embroiled in something, or one embroils oneself. In the source context, “a feud that might otherwise embroil the tribe for generations”, the verb is causal and transitive: the feud would drag the whole tribe into its cycle, generation after generation. That is the word's essential gesture — not merely to be involved, but to be caught up and entangled.

Core sense

  • To involve in a conflict or controversy. “The dispute embroiled the entire cabinet”; “he was embroiled in a lawsuit with his former partner”. The emphasis falls on the difficulty and contentiousness of the involvement.
  • To entangle in complications. The situation is not simply unpleasant but complex: obligations, alliances, and consequences multiply, so that every attempt to withdraw creates a new commitment.
  • To draw in against one's better judgment. Embroilment often implies that the person or body did not seek the entanglement, or sought only a small part in it and was pulled deeper.

The underlying metaphor

The word's French source, embrouiller, means “to entangle, confuse, muddle”, and its base brouiller originally described mixing or stirring food together. The picture is of threads or ingredients stirred into a single confused mass: once combined, they cannot easily be separated again. English keeps that image just below the surface. To be embroiled is to have one's interests stirred into someone else's quarrel, so that pulling out means tearing something apart. This is why the word suits politics, litigation, and family conflict better than it suits mere tasks or schedules.

Grammar and constructions

  • Embroil somebody in something: “The general embroiled the country in a war it could not win.”
  • Be/get/become embroiled in something: the commonest pattern by far — “the company became embroiled in a corruption inquiry”.
  • Embroil oneself in something: reflexive and usually critical, suggesting avoidable involvement — “he embroiled himself in a dispute that was none of his business”.
  • Embroiled with somebody: less common, but idiomatic for a personal or institutional antagonist — “she was embroiled with the tax authorities for years”.
  • The verb has no intransitive use: one does not “embroil” in the sense of becoming involved without an object. The derived noun is embroilment, and the past participle embroiled functions as an adjective.

Secondary and archaic senses

  • To throw into confusion or disorder (seventeenth century). Early English use could mean simply to muddle or mix up, without any quarrel: papers embroiled on a desk, affairs embroiled by bad management. This literal sense has largely disappeared, leaving the social and political one.
  • To involve in a broil or tumult. The resemblance to broil (“a noisy quarrel”; also “to cook over fire”) encouraged the sense of heated conflict, although the two words are etymologically unrelated. The modern meaning owes something to this accidental association.
  • Financial and legal entanglement. In modern usage one can be embroiled in debt, in litigation, or in regulatory proceedings — contexts where the emphasis is on complication rather than physical conflict.

Register and connotation

  • Formal to journalistic. It belongs to news reports, diplomatic commentary, and historical writing; in casual speech one is more likely to say “caught up in” or “dragged into”.
  • Strongly implies mess, heat, and unwanted publicity. “Embroiled in controversy” is a stock phrase of political reporting, and its repeated use has made the word slightly clichéd.
  • It attributes agency ambiguously: sometimes the subject is the aggressor who drags others in (“the senator embroiled his party in scandal”), sometimes the victim who is dragged (“the charity found itself embroiled in the dispute”). Context decides whether the tone is accusatory or sympathetic.
  • Common collocations: deeply embroiled, embroiled in controversy, embroiled in scandal, embroiled in a dispute, embroiled in litigation, embroiled in war, become embroiled, get embroiled, embroil somebody in, embroil oneself in.

Boundary with near-synonyms

Involve is neutral and can describe any degree of participation; embroil adds complication, conflict, and difficulty of exit. Implicate usually suggests connection with wrongdoing or a crime, and is common in legal reporting; embroil need not imply guilt at all. Entangle is broader and can be literal (entangled cables) as well as figurative; embroil is almost exclusively figurative and social. Ensnare and enmesh stress capture and helplessness, often with an agent who sets the trap; embroil stresses the messy complexity of the situation rather than a trap. Mire suggests slow, sticky progress and is often used of stalled negotiations; embroil suggests heat and contention. Drag into is plainer and more colloquial, and foregrounds the agency of the person doing the dragging.

A false etymological friend

Despite the shared letters, embroil is not related to broil in the sense “to cook by direct heat” or “to be very hot”. Broil comes from Old French brusler, “to burn”, ultimately from Latin ustulāre, “to scorch”. Embroil comes from French embrouiller, “to entangle”, whose base is the cooking-and-mixing verb brouiller. The similarity of sound and spelling is accidental, though it has probably helped the English word keep its atmosphere of heat and trouble.
