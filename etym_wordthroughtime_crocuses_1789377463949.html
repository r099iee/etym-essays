- Bronze Age to Classical Antiquity (c. 1600 BCE – 4th c. CE) - Appears in Aegean Minoan frescoes (Akrotiri) and Greek literature as *krókos* (borrowed from Semitic *karkōm*), identifying both the saffron-yielding plant *Crocus sativus* and its precious golden spice/dye; transferred into Latin as *crocus*.
- Middle English (c. 1380–1450) - Enters English via medical and spice trade manuscripts as *crocus* (and *crocus-rose*), strictly designating the medicinal, culinary, and dyeing plant saffron.
- 16th to 17th Century (Tudor & Stuart Eras) - The flower transforms into a beloved ornamental garden perennial with the introduction of Dutch and Balkan species; the regularized English plural *crocuses* emerges alongside Latin *croci*; alchemists simultaneously coin *crocus martis* for reddish-yellow iron polishing powders.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Firmly established in canonical English literature (Wordsworth, Tennyson, Wilde) and popular folklore as the universal emblem of early spring awakening pushing through winter snow.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains worldwide preeminence in domestic horticulture, naturalized lawn landscaping, and botanical conservation, symbolizing vernal hope and unyielding vitality.
