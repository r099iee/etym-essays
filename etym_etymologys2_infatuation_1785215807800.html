Latin fatuus "foolish, silly, insipid, tasteless" (attested 1st c. BCE; also used of food or wine that had lost its flavour — the insipid — and by metaphor of persons who had lost their wits) [origin uncertain: no securely established PIE root; a minority reconstruction PIE *bhat- "to strike, blow, to make senseless" is sometimes proposed, and an alternative hypothesis connects fatuus with Latin fārī "to speak" (PIE *bheh₂-), i.e. "one who babbles nonsense"; both remain conjectural] → Latin infatuāre "to make foolish, to besot, to dote upon" (post-classical Latin; prefix in- "into, in the direction of" + fatuus) → Latin infatuātiō, genitive infatuātiōnis "a making foolish, a besotting, a depriving of reason" (Late Latin; also used in medieval theology of delusion) → English infatuation "the act of depriving of reason; folly, delusion" (early 16th c., attested by the 1540s; learned borrowing from Latin) → English infatuate (verb, 17th c.) and infatuated (adjective, 16th c.) [sister formations] → Modern English INFATUATION "a blind, intense, short-lived romantic passion or unreasoning admiration" (the modern sense established in the 18th–19th c.)
