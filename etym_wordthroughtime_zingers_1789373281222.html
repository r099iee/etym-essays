- Early 20th Century (c. 1910s–1920s) - The root *zing* originates in American English as an onomatopoeic interjection and noun imitating the high-pitched whistle of a bullet, speeding automobile, or vibrating wire; quickly adopts the figurative slang sense of lively energy, zest, or pep.
- 1950s - The agentive noun *zinger* emerges in American sports journalism, particularly baseball, designating a blistering fastball or hard-hit line drive that zings through the air with extraordinary speed.
- 1960s to 1970s - Crosses into entertainment, comedy writing, and political journalism to describe a devastating one-liner, sharp comic retort, or shocking plot twist; popularized during televised presidential campaigns and political satire (*Rowan & Martin's Laugh-In*).
- 1980s to 1990s - Solidified as a central concept in American campaign politics and media analysis ("debate zingers"), where candidates deliberately rehearse bite-sized, caustic retorts designed to dominate 24-hour news broadcasts and breakfast table recaps.
- 21st Century - Deeply embedded across global English in journalism, literary criticism, and digital media, describing punchy rhetorical strikes, devastating social media clapbacks, and sudden narrative shockers.
