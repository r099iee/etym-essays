- Pre-Latin Celtic to Late Latin (c. 6th–8th c.) - [Young Servant or Retainer] - Celtic *wasso-* ('young man, servant') Latinized into *vassallus* / *vassus* to describe unfree or free personal attendants and servants of Frankish chieftains.
- High Middle Ages (9th–14th c.) - [Noble Feudal Holder of Fiefs] - Under Carolingian and Anglo-Norman feudalism, elevated from humble servant to an honorable, free nobleman who performed homage (*commendatio*) and held land in exchange for military service.
- Early Modern Era (15th–17th c.) - [Feudal Decline & Juridical Subjecthood] - As feudal military levies gave way to professional standing armies, vassal became a legalistic term for hereditary tenants or royal subjects owing homage to the crown.
- 18th–19th Century (Enlightenment to Industrial Era) - [Metaphor of Political Subjugation] - Evolved into a political metaphor describing small conquered nations, puppet rulers, or sycophantic courtiers subordinate to imperial powers.
- 20th–21st Century (Modern & Contemporary) - [Geopolitical & Economic Subservience] - Used predominantly in geopolitics ('vassal state'), corporate critique ('vassals of capital'), and fantasy literature to depict dependent, subordinated entities.
