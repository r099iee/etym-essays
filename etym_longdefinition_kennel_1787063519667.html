Kennel is the word for a dog's shelter: a small house or hut for a dog to sleep in, and by extension any place where dogs are kept, bred, or boarded. In the source context, a chained dog has been 'forgotten in a kennel for a week' — the picture is of the classic wooden dog-house, small, cramped, and neglected, the symbol of a dog's lonely confinement.

Core sense — a dog's shelter: The primary meaning is the small structure, traditionally of wood, open at the front, in which a dog sleeps and shelters outdoors. This is the sense in everyday use: 'the dog curled up in its kennel'. The word stresses the dog's dependence on its little house — and, in pitiable contexts like the source text, its imprisonment within it.

  • Sense 1 — A dog-house: the small shelter for an individual dog; the standard British word (American English prefers doghouse, but kennel is also used).
  • Sense 2 — An establishment for boarding dogs (the kennels): a commercial or institutional place where dogs are kept while their owners are away — 'we left the dog at the kennels for the fortnight'.
  • Sense 3 — A breeding establishment: where dogs are bred and raised ('she runs a golden-retriever kennel'); a kennel of this kind is identified with the breed it produces.
  • Sense 4 — A pack of hounds (archaic/historical): the hunting sense — 'a kennel of hounds', and the place and the pack together in the language of the hunt.
  • Sense 5 — Figurative contempt (rare, historical): a kennel as a mean, cramped, dirty dwelling for humans — 'living in a kennel' meaning in a hovel; now only figurative or jocular.
  • Sense 6 — The verb to kennel: to put or keep a dog in a kennel, or (of a dog) to take shelter in one — used mainly in formal or sporting prose.

Register and tone: Kennel is plain, everyday English in Britain, equally at home in the garden, the veterinary surgery, and the boarding industry. Its tone ranges from the cosy (a warm kennel, a kennel with a blanket) to the cruel (a dog forgotten in a kennel), and it can carry strong emotional weight in contexts of neglect, since the kennel is the emblem of the dog who is shut out and left alone. American usage has shifted partly toward doghouse for the small structure — famously in 'in the doghouse', meaning in disgrace — but kennel remains the standard word for the boarding or breeding establishment on both sides of the Atlantic.

Grammar and collocation: Kennel is a count noun, freely plural (kennels); the plural alone names the establishment ('the kennels'), and in British English a place of business can be treated as singular ('the kennels is fully booked'). The verb to kennel is regular (kennelled/kenneling in British spelling). Common collocations include 'boarding kennels', 'a breeding kennel', 'to put the dog up at the kennels', and 'a kennel of hounds'.

Polysemy summary: Every sense of kennel is anchored to the dog and its housing: the single shelter, the many shelters of a boarding or breeding business, the pack that a hunting kennel houses, and the contemptuous extension to any mean human dwelling. The word has stayed close to its Latin root canis, 'dog', for eight centuries without ever wandering far from the animal that built its meaning.
