Essay on MINCE (the chopped-small strand). The verb mince descends from one of the oldest Indo-European roots for smallness, *mei- (1), “small, to lessen, to diminish” — the same root family that produced Latin minus (“less”), minor (“smaller”), minimus (“least”), and minister (a servant, literally “a lesser one”). From the adjective minutus, “small, made small, reduced to tiny pieces”, Latin built the denominative verb *minutiare, “to chop into small pieces”, which passed into the Gallo-Romance and Old French of the early Middle Ages as mincier and menuzer — to cut small, to chop fine. Old French in turn handed the verb to Anglo-Norman and to Middle English, where mincen is attested from the late fourteenth century (Chaucer writes of meat “ymynced” small). The semantic core at every stage is identical: reduction to tiny, uniform pieces, most at home in the kitchen.

From that concrete culinary base the word grew a rich set of secondary senses. Because chopping small implies daintiness and fineness, to mince acquired the sense “to walk with short, dainty, affected steps” (“mincing gait”), and from daintiness of motion came daintiness of speech: to mince words is to soften or trim one's utterance, and a minced oath is a swear-word politely reduced (“zounds” from “God's wounds”, “drat” from “a pox”). The idiom not to mince matters (to speak plainly) keeps the image of refusing to cut the truth into little pieces. All these branch from the same root idea of cutting small, and all are alive in modern English. The noun mince also stands alone in British usage for minced beef (as opposed to American ground beef), and the verb has a technical life in food processing: to mince is to pass meat through a blade grid that reduces it to small, uniform pieces, a technique the medieval cook performed with a chopping knife on a board, and the modern factory with a mechanical mincer. The progression is continuous — hand-chopping, knife-mincing, machine-mincing — and every stage keeps the root idea of smallness intact.

In phonology the word also shows a small but instructive historical detail. Middle English mincen and Old French mincier rest on the same base, and the English form reflects the ordinary Anglo-Norman palatalisation of the -ti- cluster, yielding the soft /s/ sound at the heart of mince. The doublet relationship with the Latin adjective minutus is visible in the many English descendants of the same root: minute (“tiny”), diminish, diminutive, minuscule, and even menu (a French adjective, “small, detailed”, pressed into service for a bill of fare). Mince is thus a kitchen word in a large family of smallness words, and its particular claim to fame is that it survived where its siblings stayed abstract: it kept the concrete, physical meaning of making things small, and then — crucially for this card — attached itself to a dish.

The transfer to the festive table is the historical heart of this card. When mince combined with pie, the compound named a pie whose filling was minced meat — and for centuries that was literally true. The early modern English mince-pie was a rich, sweet-savoury pie of chopped beef or mutton, suet, dried fruit, and spices, an emblem of celebration and of the Twelfth Night feast. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the proportion of meat shrank while fruit and sugar grew, and by the nineteenth century the meat had vanished entirely: the filling became the dense spiced dried-fruit and suet mixture still called mincemeat. The compound mince-pie thus preserves, in its name alone, a vanished ingredient and a vanished taste — a kind of culinary archaeology in two syllables, exactly the fossil the lexicographer must explain.

Essay on PIE(S) (the mixed-filling strand). The second strand of the compound is the pastry word pie, whose origin is the most debated part of the word's history. The standard scholarly etymology links English pie to the name of the magpie: Latin pīca, via Old French pie, the pied black-and-white bird celebrated for gathering a jumble of odds and ends into its nest. By this account, a pie — a crust enclosing a miscellany of meats, fish, or fruits — was nicknamed after the bird precisely because it too held a mixed hoard. The word pie appears in Middle English from about 1300 in exactly this pastry sense, and the magpie itself continued to be called a pie in English and survives in piebald (“pied, patchy”) and the Pied Piper.

The etymology is, however, not beyond dispute, and a careful etymologist states the debate plainly. Some authorities prefer a derivation from a word for “pasty, folded” or from Medieval Latin forms for dough, and the absence of any attested intermediate “pie = pastry” in Old French before the English record is a genuine difficulty for the simple bird-bridge. Nonetheless the magpie-nickname remains the usual and most widely cited explanation, strongly supported by the parallelism of other European languages, and no rival theory has displaced it. The deeper origin of the bird-name itself — whether from an imitative base for the bird's chattering, or from an adjective meaning “pied, painted” — is likewise uncertain and not securely attached to any Proto-Indo-European root.

What is not in doubt is the semantic career of the English word. From a single mid-fourteenth-century name for a mixed-content pastry, pie expanded to cover every sweet or savoury dish with a crust — apple pie, pork pie, shepherd's pie (a crustless “pie” with a potato topping), mud pie, and the figurative pie in the sky and easy as pie. The compound mince-pie, joining the chopped-small verb to the mixed-crust noun, crystallised in the late sixteenth century and has stayed a fixed festive word ever since. In the medieval kitchen the pie was also a practical food technology: the tall, sturdy crust (the coffin or coffyn, from Latin cophinus, a basket) served as a sealed cooking and preserving vessel, allowing rich mixtures of meat, fruit, and spice to be baked and kept for days — a form of pre-refrigeration food preservation that explains why so many medieval dishes, from mince-pies to raised game pies, were pie-shaped. The mince-pie is thus the descendant of both a word and a culinary technique.

By the Tudor and Stuart periods the mince-pie had become a fixture of the English Christmas, served with wine and cheese, and its sweet-savoury character — meat, suet, currants, cloves, mace — was precisely the mixed-hoard quality the magpie-nickname had promised four centuries earlier. Puritans and reformers attacked the Christmas pie as popish and luxurious; its defenders answered with cookery books and pamphlets; and by the eighteenth century the meat had largely disappeared while the name remained. In Tolkien's scene the double etymology is quietly at work: a word about cutting small meets a word about mixed plenty, and together they name a little hoard of richness — precisely the excess the dwarves demand and the abundance the hobbit must surrender. The plural mince-pies on Bofur's lips, finally, is the form that makes the word a dish-type rather than a single portion: not one dainty tart but a platter, a household stock, the whole institution of the English festive table packed into two words.

  • *mei- (1) “small, to diminish” → Latin minutus → *minutiare → Old French mincier → Middle English mincen → English mince → mince-pie.
  • Latin pīca “magpie” → Old French pie → Middle English pie (c. 1300, pastry sense) → English pie → mince-pie.
  • Semantic drift: mincemeat once contained meat; the name outlived the ingredient — a fossil sense preserved in the modern festive pastry.
  • Debate: the magpie-nickname origin of pie is standard but not universally accepted; no secure PIE equation exists for pīca.
