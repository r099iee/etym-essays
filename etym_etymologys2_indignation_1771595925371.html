INDIGNATION — single root word; the chain from PIE to the present noun.

1. PIE *deḱ- “to take, to accept, to receive; to deem fitting” (reconstructed root — source of Latin decet, dignus, doceo; Greek dékhomai, dóxa; Sanskrit dāś-; de Vaan ‘*dek-’, ‘to take’).
2. Latin dignus “worthy, deserving — lit. ‘acceptable to be taken up as fit’” (adjective formed from the root with the suffix -no-; attested in classical Latin, 2nd–1st c. BCE; same root also gives Latin decet, ‘it is fitting’, and doceō, ‘to teach’).
3. Latin indīgnus “unworthy, undeserving” (privative in-, ‘not’ + dignus; attested in classical Latin).
4. Latin indīgnārī “to deem unworthy; to take offence; to be indignant” (deponent verb from indīgnus; attested in classical Latin).
5. Latin indīgnātiō “righteous anger, displeasure at unworthiness” (noun from indīgnārī; also the technical rhetorical figure in Cicero and Quintilian; attested 1st c. BCE).
6. Old French indignation “indignation, displeasure, anger at unworthiness” (attested from the 12th century; borrowed from Latin indīgnātiō).
7. Middle English indignacioun / indignation “indignation, righteous anger” (attested from the late 14th century, e.g. in Chaucer and Wycliffe).
8. English INDIGNATION “righteous anger provoked by injustice or unworthiness” (modern form and senses; adjective indignant, verb to be indignant).

Note on debated points: the PIE root *deḱ- ‘to take, accept’ is standard, but it must be kept distinct from the homophonous-looking PIE *deyḱ- ‘to point out, show’ (the source of Latin dīcere, ‘to say’ — dictate, and Germanic *taikjaną — teach, token). The Latin adjective dignus is undisputed in its formation from *deḱ- with a -no- suffix; the semantic bridge is ‘accepted as fitting, taken up as worthy.’ The rhetorical sense of Latin indignātiō (the orator’s appeal to righteous anger) is well attested and is the direct ancestor of the word’s public, polemical register in modern use.
