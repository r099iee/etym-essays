vexing (adjective; present participle of the verb vex) — causing annoyance, frustration, worry, or perplexity; troublesome in a way that rattles or perturbs the mind.

Primary sense: annoying and rattling

Vexing describes what disturbs and irritates: a vexing problem, a vexing delay, a vexing question. The word is stronger than merely "annoying" — it carries the root sense of agitation and jolting, a disturbance that shakes the composure rather than merely testing the patience. In the card's context — "Mr. Bennet, how can you abuse your own children in such a way? You take delight in vexing me. You have no compassion for my poor nerves" — Mrs. Bennet accuses her husband of deliberately tormenting her by his teasing, and the word is exactly calibrated: not the heavy persecution of an enemy, but the sharp, repeated jabs of a sarcastic wit against a "poor nerves" sensibility.

Senses of the verb vex

  • To annoy or irritate (modern, dominant): to bother persistently and irritatingly.
  • To distress or afflict (older, stronger): to trouble, torment, harass; "vexed with care."
  • To agitate or toss (archaic, physical): of the sea or wind vexing a ship.
  • To perplex: a "vexed question" is one long disputed and hard to settle.
  • Legal sense: an action brought "vexatiously" is one made to harass an opponent rather than to win a genuine claim.

Polysemy and secondary meanings

  • Legal jargon: "vexatious litigation" — the bringing of repeated, groundless lawsuits purely to oppress or wear down an opponent; "vexatious conduct" in court.
  • Vexed question / vexed issue: a problem so disputed and complicated that no settled answer exists — a "vexed question" in scholarship or public debate.
  • Vexatious in canon and civil law: of a complaint or appeal made without reasonable grounds and with intent to harass.
  • Conversational framing: "the vexing thing is that..." — an everyday idiom for an irritating situation.

Semantic bridge

The bridge is physical. Latin vexāre was the frequentative of vehere, "to carry": it meant to keep carrying something back and forth — to shake, jolt, toss. The Romans transferred the jolting of a springless wagon or a stormy sea to the mind: to vex someone was to shake their peace. English received the verb in the 14th century and has kept both the physical and the mental senses; "vexing" as an adjective grows directly from the mental one.

In context

Mrs. Bennet's protest — "You take delight in vexing me" — shows the word at the exact boundary between strong and mild: his teasing genuinely agitates her (she "fancied herself nervous"), yet the word, coming from her, also gently mocks her own theatrical sensitivity. It is a verb of the nerves, which is precisely why Austen's most nervous character reaches for it.
