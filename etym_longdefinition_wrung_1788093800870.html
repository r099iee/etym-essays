WRUNG is the past tense and past participle of the strong verb wring — the verb of twisting and squeezing. Its home image is a cloth or a body gripped in both hands and turned against itself so that everything inside is pressed out: water from a towel, sweat from a brow, the neck of a chicken, a confession from a prisoner. The past form wrung carries all of that violence done and finished.

Primary senses.

  • To squeeze out liquid by twisting: "she wrung the cloth over the basin" — the standard domestic sense, also of soaked garments, sponges, hair; "wringing wet" = so soaked that water could be wrung from the fabric.
  • To twist the neck of a bird or small animal so as to kill it: "wrung the chicken's neck" — a quick, practical farm death.
  • To press or twist the hands together in distress: "to wring one's hands" — the fixed gesture of helpless anguish, grief, or anxious waiting, recorded from the fifteenth century.

Figurative senses — the bridge from twist to force.

  • To extract by pressure or force: to wring a confession, an admission, a promise, or money out of someone — the image is of the truth or the payment being squeezed out of a reluctant person as water is wrung from a cloth. "They wrung every last coin from the tenants."
  • To afflict, distress, torment: "wrung with grief", "heart-wringing" — the heart is metaphorically twisted and squeezed by emotion; the sense is literary and emotional, common in melodrama and journalism ("a heart-wringing tale").
  • To extract sound (poetic — the sense in the source passage): "the melody of harps they wrung" — music drawn out of twisted wire strings as if squeezed from them; the image fuses the twisting of the strings and the wrenching of melody out of the instrument.
  • Mechanical senses: a "wringer" or wringing machine squeezes water from laundry (19th-century domestic technology); a wring is also a turn or twist given in manipulation ("a wring of the wrist").

Idioms and near-fossils. The word is fixed in a compact set of living phrases: “wringing wet” (soaked through), “to wring one's hands” (to display helpless distress), “to wring something out of someone” (to extract by pressure), “heart-wringing”, the colloquial threat “I'll wring your neck”, and the domestic “wringer”, each one a snapshot of a stage in the word's drift from twist to torment.

Grammar and register. As a class III strong verb (Old English wringan: wrang, wrungen) the modern forms are wring – wrung – wrung, unchanged since the sixteenth century; "wrung" is also used transitively with particles: "wrung out", "wrung from". In register, the literal senses are everyday; the figurative senses (extortion, affliction) are formal or literary. The word never lost its violence: even the mildest use — wringing out a cloth — contains the image of a thing twisted beyond its shape to give up what it holds. That single image is the semantic bridge for every extension: water, words, money, breath and feeling are all wrung out of something that does not want to give them.
