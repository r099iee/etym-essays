- Shut in is first a literal verb phrase — a door or gate shuts someone in — a purely physical act of closing someone inside a space.
- By the 17th century the phrase hardens into an adjective: places shut-in by hills, walls, or water — enclosures of geography.
- In late 19th-century American English the adjective turns into a noun naming a class of person: the chronic invalid or homebound elder, the shut-in, who must be visited by clergy and charities.
- Through the 20th century the noun keeps its pitying, protective register while the adjective spreads into technical jargon: the shut-in well of the oil industry, the shut-in gorge of river geographers.
- Today the adjective still paints vivid enclosed landscapes; the noun for a person is mildly dated, outflanked by neutral homebound, but the pandemic briefly revived it for a world shut in at home.
