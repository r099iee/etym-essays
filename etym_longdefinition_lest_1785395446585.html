LEST is a conjunction meaning 'for fear that' or 'in order that ... not': it introduces the very event that someone is trying to avoid, and it is almost always followed by a plain subjunctive form of the verb. In The Hobbit, Bilbo lies on the eagle's ledge after his rescue, 'a mixture of surprise at being saved from the fire, and fear lest he fall off that narrow place into the deep shadows on either side.' The word binds his fear to its object in one compact syllable, and the plain subjunctive form 'fall' after it gives the sentence its grave, formal music. Lest is a cautionary word: it always looks forward to a harm and expresses a precaution taken so that the harm will not come to pass. Here the danger is twofold - a physical drop into the deep shadows on either side of the narrow ledge - and the word gathers both directions of that fear into a single arch of prevention. Its tone is bookish and old-fashioned, the tone of solemn prose and of careful people writing careful warnings, and Tolkien uses it deliberately to make Bilbo's muddled, half-starved consciousness sound precise at the very moment when everything else about him is chaotic. GRAMMAR AND SYNTAX
  • Lest is a subordinating conjunction: it introduces a clause of negative purpose or fear. Standard use: 'I stayed quiet lest I wake the baby' - i.e. I stayed quiet for fear that I might wake the baby.
  • The verb after lest is traditionally subjunctive: lest he fall, lest they be caught. In older and formal English the subjunctive is mandatory; in looser modern use the indicative sometimes appears, but the subjunctive remains the educated norm.
  • Lest can also mean 'in case / so that ... not' in constructions of precaution, and historically 'unless' (a Middle English sibling sense, no longer current).
  • Occasionally lest is followed by should: 'lest he should fall' - an emphatic, slightly archaic variant of the same subjunctive construction.
POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Negative purpose: 'in order that ... not' - he worked quietly lest he disturb them.
  • Fear: 'for fear that' - she hesitated lest she say the wrong thing.
  • Precaution: 'against the possibility that' - wear a helmet lest you fall.
  • Older / obsolete 'unless': a sense inherited from Middle English, now restricted to archaic and biblical style.
  • Fixed phrase: 'lest we forget' - the national remembrance phrase in Britain and the Commonwealth, taken from Rudyard Kipling's 1897 poem 'Recessional'.
DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • In case: introduces a mere possibility, often neutral ('take an umbrella in case it rains'), with none of the dread that lest carries.
  • So that ... not: expresses purpose without any note of fear; lest fuses purpose and fear into one word.
  • Or else / otherwise: threaten a consequence to the listener, whereas lest expresses the speaker's own precaution or fear.
  • Unless: expresses a condition ('unless you leave, I will call') rather than a feared event; in modern English the two are quite distinct, though historically close.
USAGE AND REGISTER Lest has vanished from everyday speech, replaced by 'in case' and 'so that ... not'. It survives as a deliberate, bookish, slightly archaic choice in literature, formal prose, legal and religious texts, and the fixed phrase 'lest we forget'. Using lest signals careful, elevated diction - precisely why Tolkien reaches for it at Bilbo's moment of extremity, and why it gives the sentence its formal gravity.
