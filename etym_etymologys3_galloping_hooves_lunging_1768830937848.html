galloping — The biography of this word is the story of a Germanic verb that emigrated to France and came home again wearing French clothes. It begins with the Proto-Germanic root *hlaupaną, "to leap, to run," whose daughters cover the Germanic world: Old English hlēapan gave English leap; Old Norse hlaupa gave the English lope, "to run with long, easy strides"; Old High German hloufan became German laufen, "to run." The ultimate PIE origin of *hlaupaną is debated and best left open. The decisive event happened in the Frankish courts, where the root was compounded with *wala, "well," to form *wala hlaupan, "to run well" — a phrase applied to the running of horses. Old French took this as galoper, with the noun galop, and French gave the word to the rest of Romance (Italian galoppare, Spanish galopar). Middle English borrowed it in the thirteenth century as galopen, for the specific four-beat gait of a horse at full speed — the gait in which all four feet leave the ground twice in each stride — and from the verb came the noun gallop, the agent noun galloper, and the present participle galloping. The word kept its equestrian home so firmly that its figurative uses transfer the horse's runaway speed to anything that accelerates beyond control: galloping consumption was the nineteenth-century name for tuberculosis, and galloping inflation is the modern idiom. In the passage, galloping is used acoustically — "the sound of galloping hooves approached" — and the word's whole history of unrestrained equine speed is packed into that approach: the hammering rhythm, the loss of control, the animal stretched to its limit, the approach of something that will not stop. Key developments: (1) the migration from Germanic to French and back, which is why galloping is a Romance-shaped word for a Germanic gait; (2) the specialization from "run well" to the precise four-beat equine gait; and (3) the figurative transfer from horse to disease and economics, which keeps the word alive far beyond the stable.
hooves — The biography of the hoof begins with a blow: the Indo-European root *koph₂- means "to beat, to strike," and the hoof was named, in all probability, as the striking instrument of the running animal — the thing that beats the ground. The Indo-Iranian daughter is Sanskrit śapha, "hoof, claw"; Greek kóptein, "to strike, cut, chop," preserves the verbal sense; and the Germanic branch developed *hōfaz, "hoof," from which descend Old English hōf (modern hoof), Old Norse hófr, Dutch hoef, and German Huf. The plural hooves, with its voiced v, is a relatively modern English regularization — hoofs is the older plural, and hooves was formed by analogy with the f/v-alternating plurals (wolf/wolves, calf/calves) in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, eventually becoming the standard form. Biologically the hoof is a single enlarged toenail — a casing of keratin, the same substance as horn and nail, growing over the bones of the horse's foot — and etymologically it is the striking foot: what the word describes is less the organ than its action. That action is what the ancient world depended on. The horse's hoof was the primary technology of speed, war, trade, and message-bearing; the sound of hooves was the sound of power arriving, of riders and couriers and armies, and the drumming of hooves remains one of the most physically thrilling sounds in literature. The idiom "on the hoof" (livestock sold and transported while still alive; by extension, on the move without stopping) keeps the word close to its practical, moving origin. In the passage, the sound of galloping hooves announces the black horse before it is seen, and the word's hardness — its horny, unstoppable, ground-beating core — anchors a supernatural scene in the most physical reality the medieval world knew.
lunging — The biography of this word moves from static measurement to kinetic explosion. Its root core is the Proto-Indo-European adjective *dl̥nghós, "long," which produced Latin longus (whence longitude, prolong, elongate), Proto-Germanic *langaz (whence English long, length, lengthen), and Sanskrit dīrghá, "long," which survives in Hindi and Odia as the learned borrowing dīrgh. The decisive development came in Late Latin, where ad- plus longus formed allongāre, "to lengthen," passing into Old French as alongier and allonger, "to lengthen." In French the verb generated the noun allonge, "an extension" — and it was the fencing masters who seized the word: a lunge (French allonge) is the attack in which the swordsman extends the sword arm and drives the entire body forward to its full length, the body itself becoming the "lengthening." English borrowed lunge in the eighteenth century as a fencing term, and from the noun grew the verb to lunge, "to make such a sudden forward thrust" — first of fencers, then of any creature throwing the body forward in one explosive extension: a tiger lunging, a horse lunging, a boxer lunging. The semantic journey is thus a lengthening that became a leap: the etymology of the word is an extension that turned kinetic. The equestrian training term "to longe a horse" (exercising a horse in circles on a long rope) is a distinct but closely related word, from French longe, "a long rope," and the two spellings are often confused. In the passage the horse is "lunging break-neck along the bank of the pool," and the word's fencing ancestry gives the motion an edge of violence and precision — not merely running, but thrusting its whole frame forward along the line of its charge, the rider's wild black hair streaming behind the attack. Key developments: (1) the root's sense of length; (2) the lengthening verbs of Late Latin and Old French; (3) the fencing noun allonge that made "length" an attack; and (4) the broadening of the verb from sword to body, so that any creature throwing itself forward in a full-body extension is now said to lunge.
