**A word from the steppe: the evolution of HORDES** The plural noun *hordes* is one of English's great travelers — a word with no Indo-European ancestry at all, whose path runs from the horse-mounted encampments of Central Asia into the heart of modern English prose. Its origin is the Old Turkic word *ordu*, attested in the Orkhon inscriptions of the 8th century CE, where it meant a royal residence, a camp, an army, or the seat of a khan — the movable capital of a nomadic ruler, a whole court on horseback that struck camp, marched, and re-formed with the seasons. That single word seeded an extraordinary family across Eurasia, and it is the ancestor of English *horde*. **The two directions of the steppe spread** - Eastward, *ordu* entered Mongolian as *ordo* / *ordu* "court, royal encampment, palace." The Mongols' own Altan Ordo, the "Golden Camp" of the khan, furnished the historical name by which Western Europe long knew the Mongol-Tatar realm: the *Golden Horde*. For this khanate the Russian form *orda* (taken from the Turkic term, attested from the 14th century) denoted the camp and host itself. - Westward, through the Kipchak and Tatar dialects of the western steppe, the form *urda* / *orda* reached the Slavic world. Russian adopted *orda*; Polish took it as *horda*, adding the initial h- that the OED notes as characteristically Polish. **The passage into Western Europe and English** - From Polish, the word entered Middle French as *horde* (16th century), and some accounts add a German stage *Horde* along the way; either way, the French form carried it to the Atlantic. - English took it up about 1550, where it first meant, in the travel-accounts of the age, "a tribe of Asiatic nomads living in tents." The early attestations belong to the literature of exploration and empire — accounts of Muscovy, Tartary, and the Silk Road that returned to English readers with the dust of the steppe still on them. - The meaning darkened fast. By 1610 the word had acquired its transferred, pejorative sense of "any uncivilized gang," and by the modern period it meant any vast, disorderly, threatening mass of people — or, as in Tolkien, of goblins. **Semantic drift: from a people to a plague** The drift is a textbook case of generalization plus pejoration: - The original sense named a real, specific, organized social form: the khan's encamped host. It was not insulting in itself — it was the neutral Turkic word for a ruler's camp-and-army. - In European mouths, however, the word was inseparable from fear of the steppe invaders. The Mongol conquests of the 13th century had made "the Golden Horde" a byword for irresistible, alien, barbaric force, and the word carried that freight into English. - The pejoration stepped out of the historical frame: once a horde was "any uncivilized gang," it was a short step to any overwhelming multitude, and the sense of disorder — of a mass that has no discipline, no count, no civilization — became permanent. - The plural *hordes* sharpens the pejoration still further. The singular can name a single (still menacing) group; the plural insists on multiplicity without limit — hordes, wave after wave — and it is the form that English uses whenever the emphasis is on sheer, unstoppable quantity. **Socio-cultural uses across the centuries** - In 16th–17th century travel and history writing, *horde* stayed close to its source, naming Tatar and Mongol peoples — it was used freely, if not always neutrally, of the tribes of the steppe. - In 18th–19th century journalism and political rhetoric, "hordes" became a stock term of fear for immigrant and colonized populations — the "hordes of Asia," "hordes of the East" — where the old steppe menace was recycled as a racialized scare-word. - In the 20th–21st centuries, the word migrated into popular genre: "hordes of zombies," "hordes of tourists," "hordes of locusts." The gaming term "horde mode" (endless waves of enemies) is a direct modern heir of the old military sense, and it preserves, quite exactly, the original idea of the khan's host come to overwhelm a fixed position. **The linguistic side-branch: Urdu** Along a separate branch of the same family, Turkish *ordu* "camp" passed into Persian and Hindustani as *urdū* "camp," and the phrase *zabān-i-urdū* — "the language of the camp" — gave the name *Urdu* to the great lingua franca born in the polyglot encampments of the Mughal conquerors (the language-name is attested from 1796). A word that began as a khan's tented capital thus ends up simultaneously as an English noun for hostile multitudes and as the name of one of the world's major languages. **Literary life and Tolkien** - The word's literary career is nearly all menace: it is the noun of invading armies and swarming mobs from the 17th century to the present. - Tolkien gives it its most famous twentieth-century speech: Gandalf's weary report — "No ponies, and no food, and no knowing quite where we are, and *hordes* of angry goblins just behind!" — where the plural noun collapses the company's whole predicament into one overwhelming image. - In that sentence every stage of the word's history is silently present: the steppe camp, the Golden Horde, the uncivilized gang, the faceless tide — a word born of nomadic encampments and khans' tents, hardened into a byword for overwhelming, wild, threatening multitudes.
