conceiving (verb, present participle and gerund of conceive) — The act of forming a thought, idea, or plan in the mind; imagining, envisaging, or coming to understand something; and, in the biological sense, the act of becoming pregnant. All these senses descend from the same Latin image: concipere, "to take in" — to take something into oneself.

Primary sense — forming in the mind. To conceive an idea, a plan, or a work is to bring it into being mentally, to give it initial shape and coherence before it is expressed or executed. "Conceiving life" in the passage on this card means forming a conception or picture of what life is and should be: the very activity that gives the word conception its philosophical weight. In this mental sense conceiving shades into imagining (forming an image or possibility) and into understanding (taking a thing fully into the mind: "I cannot conceive how it happened").

Secondary sense — the biological sense. A woman (or female animal) conceives when a child is conceived in the womb: when new life is "taken in" at the moment of fertilization. This is the oldest continuous sense in the language, present in English since the 13th century and in Latin from the start; in modern everyday speech it has become the dominant sense of both conceive and conceiving. Medicine and law speak of the moment of conception, and "conceiving" in this sense is the neutral clinical word for becoming pregnant, distinct from the stages that follow.

  • Philosophical and psychological use: "conceiving" names the mental act behind every concept (conceptus, from the same Latin verb): to conceive of justice, of God, of a future society is to hold a coherent notion of it. The construction "conceive of X" is the standard modern way to say "imagine that X exists or is possible" — "I can hardly conceive of it".
  • Rhetorical and literary use: a writer conceives a poem, an engineer conceives a machine, a statesman conceives a policy; the word lends the act of creation a meditative dignity, as in the Italian concetto and the English "conceit".
  • Theological use: "conceived of the Holy Ghost", "the immaculate conception" — the word has carried the central mystery of Christian incarnation talk since the earliest English theology, joining the mental and the physical senses in one doctrine.
  • Everyday formal use: "I cannot conceive of any reason to object", "as one would conceive the situation" — a polite, slightly formal way of saying "understand" or "imagine".

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The unity of the word lies in its Latin structure: con- "together, with" + capere "to take, seize". Whatever is conceived is taken in and held — an idea taken into the mind, a child taken into the womb, a situation taken in whole by the understanding. The mind-sense and the body-sense have coexisted in English from the first; what has changed is their relative weight. For centuries the mental sense led in prestige usage, while the physical sense was plain and everyday. In the twentieth century the physical sense won ordinary speech so completely that "conceive an idea" now sounds formal and literary — a reversal worth remembering when reading older prose such as Pater's, where "conceiving" still wears the full intellectual clothing of its Latin origin.
