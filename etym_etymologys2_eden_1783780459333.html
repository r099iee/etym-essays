Chain for Eden (a non-Indo-European chain, traced from its earliest recoverable Near Eastern source): 1. Sumerian edin (𒂔, "steppe, plain, open flat land, uncultivated grazing country"; attested in cuneiform from c. 2900 BCE; the antonym of the irrigated field) → 2. Akkadian edinnu / edinu ("steppe, plain, desert, common land"; a loan from Sumerian into the Semitic Akkadian of Mesopotamia; attested in the 2nd millennium BCE) → 3. Biblical Hebrew ʿēden (עֵדֶן, "plain" as the toponym in Genesis 2:8 "a garden in Eden, in the east"; generally taken as a loan of the Akkadian word, but already conflated in the Hebrew tradition with the homophonous Hebrew root ʿ-D-N "to be soft, delicate, luxuriate," which yields ʿēden "delight, luxury, pleasure"; the biblical text is dated c. 6th–5th c. BCE) → 4. Ancient Greek Edem / Éden (Ἐδέμ, the transliteration of the name in the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament, c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → 5. Latin Eden (the transliteration in Jerome's Vulgate, c. 405 CE) → 6. English Eden (from the Latin Vulgate and the English Bible tradition — Wycliffe 1382, Tyndale 1530, the King James Bible 1611 — with the name already in Old English biblical paraphrase from c. the 8th–10th c. CE). Debated / nuanced stages: (a) whether Hebrew ʿēden is a genuine loan from Akkadian edinnu (the standard view among scholars) or a native Semitic word related to the root ʿ-D-N "delight" is debated; many scholars see a convergence — a geographical loanword that was reinterpreted through a homophonous native root meaning "delight," producing the famous "Garden of Delight" reading; (b) the connection of the Semitic name to the Sumerian edin, while widely accepted for the Akkadian form, involves transmission across three millennia and several languages, so each link (Sumerian → Akkadian → Hebrew) rests on comparative loanword evidence rather than direct documentation; (c) the exact Septuagint spelling fluctuates between Edem and Éden in the manuscripts, both being simple transliterations rather than translations.
