DISUNION is a Latin-prefixed French borrowing, and its biography is really two biographies joined by the negative prefix dis-. The second half of the word, union, is the older story. It reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European root *oi-no-, "one, single," one of the great numerals of the family: Sanskrit eka "one," Greek oine "the one on a die, the ace," Old Irish oen "one," and the Germanic *ainaz that gives English one, only, and alone all descend from it. Latin inherited the root as unus "one," and from unus the classical language built a small family of words for oneness: the verb unire "to make one, to unite," and the noun unio "oneness, unity," which the elder Pliny also used for a single pearl — because a pearl, a natural object of one substance and one perfection, is the concrete image of unbroken oneness. It is from this pearl that the later abstract sense of "unity" grew: the medieval schools and canon lawyers needed a word for the bond that holds persons, churches, or states in one, and they found it in the pearl of unio.

The first half of the word, the prefix dis-, is one of the most productive negative prefixes of Latin. It descends from an Indo-European deictic element meaning "in two directions, apart," and in Latin it expressed reversal, separation, or the undoing of an action — the same prefix in divide, distract, dissolve, and dismember. Attached to unire, it reverses the whole act of joining: Late Latin disunire means literally "to un-join, to sever what has been made one." The marriage of the two halves happens precisely here, in the Late Latin and early medieval period, when the Church — anxious about the disunion of the communion of believers — needed a precise verb for the undoing of unity. From the verb the Romance languages made a noun: Old French desunion "separation, discord," then Middle French desunion, "rupture of alliance."

English borrowed the noun in the late sixteenth century, and from the start it carried a charge that the modern word has never quite shed. In the seventeenth century it served religious and civil controversy — writers like Milton used it of the divisions that threatened church and commonwealth — and in the eighteenth century, on the far side of the Atlantic, it found its greatest subject: the American republic. "Disunion" became the abstract name of the greatest American fear, the breakup of the federal Union. Antebellum orators declaimed against "the spirit of disunion"; "disunionist" was a term of accusation applied to abolitionists and secessionists alike; and when the Civil War came, the word named what had actually happened. The Webster-Hayne debates, the nullification crisis, and the secession winter all turned on this one noun.

The twentieth century then performed a quiet revolution on the word, turning it inward. Psychology took the image of a broken whole and applied it to the self: the "neurotic disunion" of the mind, the dissociation of its faculties, the loss of inner coherence. It is exactly this sense that William Empson deploys in the passage from which this card comes, when he says his last example of ambiguity was "not concerned with neurotic disunion but with a fully public theological poem." The word thus crosses a century: from the political menace of a shattered republic to the clinical and literary diagnosis of a shattered self, yet always retaining its core geometry — a unity that has fallen out of harmony with itself.

Summary of the eras:
  • PIE to Latin (c. 3000 BCE - 1st c. BCE): *oi-no- "one" to unus "one" to unio "oneness; a single pearl" and unire "to unite."
  • Late Latin to Old French (4th-12th c.): dis- + unire > disunire "to sever"; Old French desunion "discord, rupture."
  • English borrowing (late 16th c.): the noun enters English as desunion, settled as disunion.
  • American political sense (18th-19th c.): "disunion" names the feared breakup of the federal Union; disunionism and the Civil War.
  • Psychological and critical sense (20th c.): "neurotic disunion" — the self's loss of coherence; Empson's use.
  • Modern status: formal, literary, and historical; the fierce political edge has faded but the geometry of broken oneness endures.

Sound changes along the borrowed chain are regular: the Romance voicing and loss of Latin endings (Latin unus to Old French un; disunire to desunir to desunion), the English respelling of des- as dis- by association with the Latin prefix, and the shift of stress onto the second syllable of the English noun /dɪsˈjuːnjən/.
