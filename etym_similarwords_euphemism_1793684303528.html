• Circumlocution: Similar because both avoid direct, blunt phrasing by talking around an issue rather than naming it plainly; differs because circumlocution specifically denotes using an excessive number of roundabout words or verbose speech (which can be accidental, tedious, or evasive), whereas a euphemism is a targeted lexical substitution specifically designed to soften an unpleasant, taboo, or painful concept.
  • Innuendo: Similar because both convey meaning obliquely rather than stating it openly; differs because an innuendo is an indirect, covert hint or subtle insinuation—frequently derogatory, salacious, or malicious—whereas a euphemism is typically designed to reduce offensiveness, preserve decorum, and spare social embarrassment.
  • Doublespeak: Similar because both employ sanitized, evasive language to obscure harsh or controversial realities; differs because doublespeak (derived from Orwell's 1984) carries a distinctly deceptive, propagandistic, and manipulative intent practiced by governments, corporations, and institutions to actively distort truth, whereas euphemism encompasses harmless interpersonal politeness and everyday psychological coping.
  • Minced Oath: Similar because both soften potentially offensive or taboo speech; differs because a minced oath is specifically a semi-phonetic alteration or deliberate mispronunciation of a profane curse, swear word, or blasphemy (e.g., "gosh" for God, "darn" for damn, "heck" for hell, "zounds" for God's wounds) rather than a conceptual replacement for a broader taboo reality.
  • Understatement (Litotes / Meiosis): Similar because both tone down the blunt intensity of a statement to make it less abrasive; differs because understatement is a rhetorical figure that minimizes the magnitude, importance, or severity of an event for dramatic, ironic, or humorous effect (e.g., calling a hurricane "a bit of a breeze"), rather than substituting vocabulary to shield social sensibilities.
  • Periphrasis: Similar because both involve indirect, multi-word phrasing rather than direct nomination; differs because periphrasis is a broader grammatical and stylistic figure of speech involving the use of a descriptive phrase in place of a single noun or verb (e.g., "the author of Paradise Lost" for Milton), which may serve poetic, elevated, or rhythmic purposes without necessarily avoiding taboo.
  • Genteelism: Similar because both replace crude terms with polite alternatives; differs because a genteelism is a self-consciously refined, affected, or hypercorrect substitute used primarily by speakers attempting to signal higher social class or extreme delicacy (e.g., "serviette" for napkin, "perspire" for sweat), often perceived by critics as bourgeois or pretentious.
  • Softener: Similar because both function to blunt the harsh edge of an uncomfortable communication; differs because a softener can include conversational hedges, polite prefacing clauses, or tonal cushions (e.g., "I'm afraid that...", "With all due respect..."), whereas a euphemism is a specific lexical replacement for an objectionable word.
  • Sugarcoating: Similar because both aim to make an unpleasant fact more palatable to the recipient; differs because sugarcoating is an informal, sweeping term denoting the deliberate downplaying, glossing over, or deceptive framing of bad news, whereas euphemism operates at the level of specific diction and vocabulary.
  • Prettification: Similar because both make rough, ugly, or mundane concepts appear more attractive and acceptable; differs because prettification implies superficial aesthetic dressing up or cosmetic embellishment across broader visual, literary, or conceptual contexts, rather than a codified verbal taboo substitution.
  • Dysphemism: Relevant as the exact polar opposite counterpart of euphemism, denoting the deliberate substitution of a harsher, more offensive, derogatory, or vulgar term for a neutral or mild one (e.g., "croak" or "pushing up daisies" for die, "loony bin" for mental hospital), highlighting the boundary of euphemistic speech.
  • Orthophemism: Relevant as the neutral baseline of communication, referring to the straightforward, literal, and non-judgmental designation of a concept (e.g., "die", "feces", "penis") without either the softening veil of a euphemism or the derogatory bite of a dysphemism.
