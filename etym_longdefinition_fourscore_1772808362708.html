In Gibbon's sentence FOURSCORE measures the celebrated span of the "happy period" of the Roman empire: "During a happy period of more than fourscore years, the public administration was conducted by the virtue and abilities of Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, and the two Antonines." The word is an archaic vigesimal numeral meaning eighty — four times twenty — and it is deliberately chosen to give the passage a scriptural and monumental solemnity that the plain word "eighty" would not carry. Gibbon, writing in the eighteenth century, invokes the register of the King James Bible and of classical oratory (the same word that Lincoln would use in 1863 in "four score and seven years ago"), thereby framing the Antonine age not as a mere statistic but as a sealed, almost biblical interval of civic virtue — a finished block of time whose close will mark the beginning of the empire's decline. The core sense of FOURSCORE is thus: the cardinal number eighty, expressed by the vigesimal (base-twenty) system in which score = twenty. Unlike modern English, which counts in tens (eighty = eight tens), older English, like French (quatre-vingts, "four twenties") and Danish (firsindstyve), counted in twenties for the numbers between twenty and a hundred and beyond. "Score" originally meant a notch or tally-cut, and by extension the count marked by such notches — and in the medieval counting of sheep, cattle, and other goods by twenties, score came to mean the number twenty itself. FOURSCORE, therefore, is the transparent compound four + score = 4 × 20 = 80.
  • Cardinal numeral eighty: the core sense — fourscore = four times twenty = eighty; used of years, ages, men, units of any kind.
  • Archaic/biblical register: the word belongs to elevated, archaic, or scriptural prose; it signals age, duration, or quantity with solemn dignity (cf. "fourscore years," "fourscore and ten").
  • Vigesimal system marker: it exemplifies the base-twenty counting system of older Germanic and French numeration, contrasting with the modern decimal eighty.
  • Scriptural/oratorical weight: in the phrase "fourscore and seven years ago" (Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, 1863) the word carries the resonance of the Authorized Version and of the whole American civic founding narrative.
  • Metonym for a full lifetime: "the span of fourscore years" stands for the biblical three-score-and-ten (70) exceeded, i.e., a long, full, blessed life.
  • Fixed collocations: "fourscore years," "fourscore and ten" (90, by the older counting), "fourscore winters," "fourscore men" — mostly in historical, biblical, and formal literary contexts.
The semantic reasoning for the word's power is entirely a matter of register. Both "eighty" and "fourscore" name the same number, but "eighty" is the plain, neutral, decimal form of everyday modern speech, while "fourscore" is the literary and scriptural form. Gibbon's choice of the older numeral quietly transforms a statistical fact (roughly 84 years, AD 96–180, from Nerva's accession to Marcus Aurelius's death) into a monumental, closed interval — a "happy period" that feels providential, measured out by a higher arithmetic than the everyday decimal count. The word also connects Gibbon's prose to the classical and biblical tradition: Roman historians and Christian Scripture alike used score-based counting, so FOURSCORE ties the Antonine age to the whole inherited vocabulary of European high culture. Register and usage: FOURSCORE is now archaic and literary. It survives almost exclusively in fixed phrases, historical pastiche, biblical quotation, and the Lincoln echo. In ordinary modern speech it has been entirely replaced by "eighty." Collocations are limited and frozen: "fourscore years," "fourscore and ten," "fourscore winters," "fourscore men," "a life of fourscore years." The word cannot be freely pluralized as a count noun in modern use (one does not say "two fourscores"), and it takes no article ("the fourscore years" refers to a specific named span, as in Gibbon). Pronunciation is /fˈɔːskɔː/ (FAW-skaw), with stress on the first syllable, preserving the two-element compound four + score.
