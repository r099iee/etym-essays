Stinging is the present-participial, gerundial, and deverbal-adjectival form of the verb to sting - and the adjective is perhaps even more alive than the verb. Anything that stings inflicts a sharp, localised, burning-or-pricking pain, and the word has carried that sensation - with complete consistency - from insect bites to the cold wind to the most wounding sentences of a critic.

1. The physical senses: what stings.

  • Stinging animals. Bees, wasps, hornets, ants, scorpions, and stingrays "sting" by injecting venom through a sharp organ; the sensation is a sudden, hot, needle-sharp pain that burns and throbs locally. Biology reserves the adjective for the aculeate (stinging) Hymenoptera - bees, wasps, ants - and for the cnidarian animals, jellyfish and anemones, whose stinging cells (cnidocytes, which discharge nematocysts) fire tiny harpoons of toxin.
  • Stinging plants. The stinging nettles (genus Urtica and relatives) are the commonest stinging plants: their hollow, brittle hairs (trichomes) act as hypodermic needles, injecting irritants (histamine, acetylcholine, formic acid) that produce exactly the burning, prickling pain the word names. "Stinging nettle" is the plain English name of the plant from the Middle Ages onward.
  • Stinging elements and substances. Cold is traditionally stinging ("a stinging wind", "the stinging cold of the northern passes"), as are smoke, salt spray, chlorine in a swimming pool, antiseptic on a wound, and - the image in this card's sentence - fire: Tolkien's arrows "flickered as it fled as if with stinging fire", i.e. fire that bites and pricks like a swarm of stings as it flies. The sensory core is the same: many tiny points of burning pain.

2. The figurative senses: what stings without touching.

  • Words. Stinging criticism, a stinging rebuke, a stinging retort, stinging satire: language that wounds like the strike of a wasp - brief, precise, and painful out of proportion to its size. The transfer is pure sensory metaphor: the sharpness of a needle-bite becomes the sharpness of a sentence, and the word implies that the blow landed on a particular spot - on a person's pride, vanity, or record.
  • Events. A "stinging defeat" or "stinging loss" is one that hurts the way a sting does: sudden, undeserved-feeling, and lingering; headlines use the word whenever an upset has been inflicted. A "stinging blow" is a setback that is both sharp and unexpectedly vicious.
  • Conscience and memory. The noun sting has long named the pricking of conscience or regret ("the sting of conscience", "the sting of death" in the King James Bible, 1 Corinthians 15:55), and "stinging remorse" keeps the image of an inward wound that refuses to heal.

3. Domain usages.

  • Medicine. Clinicians describe certain pains as stinging (burning, prickling, localised) - the stinging pain of nettle rash, of venom, of neuropathic irritation is a standard descriptor distinct from dull, aching, or throbbing pain.
  • Entomology and botany. "Stinging" is a precise classifier in both sciences: stinging insects (the aculeate Hymenoptera), stinging hairs (trichomes), stinging cells (cnidocytes). The word does the work of a technical term without leaving ordinary speech.
  • Police work (an offshoot of the noun). A "sting" or "sting operation" is an undercover trap - the target is lured, then suddenly caught, i.e. "stung". This sense (early 20th-century slang) keeps the suddenness of the sting while shifting the pain to the person arrested; the adjective stinging is not used there, but the family is felt.

4. Grammar and form.

  • Stinging is the present participle and gerund of sting (a strong verb: sting - stung - stung, continuing Old English stingan - stang - stungon - gestungen). As participle it is used predicatively and attributively (a stinging nettle; the wind was stinging); as gerund it names the act or sensation (the stinging of the cold); as deverbal adjective it is fully established, with the characteristic "causing X" reading and a negative emotional bias when transferred to words.

5. Semantic bridge in one line.

  • A sting is a small wound with a disproportionately sharp, burning pain - so anything that hurts small-but-hard, from a frozen gust to a sarcastic clause, is stinging; the word is the English adjective of sharp, burning, pointed pain, physical or moral.
