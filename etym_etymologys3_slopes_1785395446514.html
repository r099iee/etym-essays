The biography of SLOPES begins in motion, not in land. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *sleubʰ- expressed sliding and slipping — a body losing its footing, a thing gliding off the level. Its most famous Italic reflex is Latin lūbricus, "slippery," which modern English still shows in the loan layer of lubricate and lubricious, and the same root stretches across the family into the slippery-verbs of the north. Proto-Germanic made the root a strong verb, *slūpaną, "to slip, slide, creep," and Old English inherited slūpan, with the prefixed āslūpan, "to slip away, to escape." The decisive grammatical event in the English career of the word is an accident of inflection: the old past participle slopen was re-formed into a new base verb, slope, recorded from the mid-fifteenth century meaning "to flee, to run away" — slipping away unnoticed, exactly as survives in the modern colloquial "to slope off." By about 1500 the participle had also yielded an adjective, sloping, "slanting" — a surface that makes one slip, or descends at an angle — and from that adjective-noun, the noun "a slope" was formed in the early 1600s as an inclined stretch of ground, first documented in the 1610s. The whole trajectory is a single figure: ground that has, as it were, slipped down to its present angle.

The Germanic family. The sister branches of the word are rich across the Germanic world, and they confirm that the slippery-motion sense is ancient. Gothic sliupan, "to slip, to creep," is attested in Wulfila's fourth-century Gothic Bible, showing the root in East Germanic; Old High German sliofan and sliefen, "to slip, to creep," continue into modern German schliefen, "to slip into," and schlüpfen, "to slip in/out, to hatch" — the same idea of a body easing through a narrow opening; Old Saxon slūpan runs parallel in the Continental West Germanic sphere. English's own slip and slippery come from a neighbouring but distinct root, PIE *sleipʰ- → Proto-Germanic *slippaną, and the two families have intermingled in the same semantic field for millennia; the OED is appropriately cautious in judging that the verb slope "probably" derives from the old past participle rather than with certainty. Yet the historical arc is clear: bodily sliding becomes social evasion (to slope off), and social evasion becomes the fixed geometry of terrain (a slope of land) — the motion hardening into a shape.

  • From verb to adjective to noun (15th–17th centuries): the word entered English as a verb of flight; the participle gave the adjective "slanting"; and the noun was formed from the adjective-noun about 1610. This backward-born noun — a noun formed from a participle of a verb — is a striking case of English word-formation, and it explains why the modern noun still feels faintly dynamic: a slope is ground in a state of having slipped.
  • The early modern working word (16th–18th centuries): in early modern English, slope worked as a verb of oblique motion and a military term — "slope arms" fixed a musket slantwise on the shoulder — and as an adjective of building and carpentry. The surveyor's and geographer's noun gradually took over, and by the 18th century "a slope" was the standard term for any graduated decline or rise of ground.
  • The mathematical slope (19th century): with the development of analytic geometry, the word entered mathematics as the measure of a line's gradient — the ratio of vertical to horizontal change. The everyday idea of steepness was quantified into a number, and "slope" became a term of the classroom and the laboratory alongside the landscape word.
  • The idiom "slippery slope" and the ski slope (20th century): the 20th century gave the word two famous fixed forms: the ski slope of winter sport, and the argumentative "slippery slope" — the rhetorical image of a small step leading inevitably to a disastrous slide. Both play on the word's ancient connection with slipping, and both show the noun's range from pure geography to pure argument.

Socio-cultural factors and the modern career. The modern career of slope has been shaped by three forces. First, the growth of surveying, civil engineering, and mountaineering from the 18th century made the noun the workmanlike technical term of inclined ground — neutral, exact, and standard in science and journalism. Second, the rise of winter sport in the 20th century gave "the slopes" a recreational life of its own, so that a plural that once named the descents of a mountain now names a ski resort's runs. Third, the persistence of the old verbal sense kept a ghost of motion inside the noun: "the ground sloped away" remains the literary way to set terrain moving, and "slope off" keeps the 15th-century sense of quiet escape alive. In the preserved sentence from The Hobbit, the plural does its classic literary work: "only ridges and SLOPES falling towards lowlands and plains" — the slopes are the fixed, graded declivities that carry the eye and the road downward, neither a cliff's drop nor a plain's flatness, land slipping steadily down toward the glimpsed plains, and the old ghost of slipping is exactly what gives the sentence its quiet sense of descent.
