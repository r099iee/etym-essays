- 1860s–1870s (scandalous debut with Poems and Ballads, 1866) - notorious name: aesthetic revolt, paganism and sensuous excess, at once publicly reviled and defiantly defended.
- Late Victorian era (1870s–1890s) - reputation tamed into respected technical mastery: 'the greatest living master of sound', admired for incantatory rhythm and alliteration.
- Early 20th century (modernism, Eliot, Leavis) - dismissed as decadent word-music, a cautionary figure of poetry of pure sound over meaning.
- Mid-20th century onward (close reading, Empson) - revived as a touchstone of verbal craft; his ambiguities read as deliberately engineered patterns rather than intoxication.
- Present day - canonical Victorian poet of formal mastery; the name itself signals musicality, excess and late-Victorian Decadence.
