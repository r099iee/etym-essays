In Macaulay's History of England, the phrase "a state of ignominious __________" describes the condition of England under the late Stuart kings before the Glorious Revolution of 1688 — a condition Macaulay, with fierce Whig partisanship, regards as national shame. The "ignominious vassalage" is the country's subordination to Louis XIV of France: Charles II and James II took secret French subsidies, aligned English foreign policy with the French interest, and reduced an independent kingdom to the position of a dependent client. The word vassalage is a feudal metaphor deployed at the level of statecraft: England is imagined as a vassal holding its dignity from a foreign lord, bound by the obligations of homage and service to a power it should have defied. The tone is contemptuous, rhetorical, and portentous; ignominious (shameful, dishonorable) attaches public humiliation to the dependence, so that the phrase condemns not merely subordination but the disgrace of it — the very sin the Glorious Revolution was to wash away.

At its historical core, vassalage is the legal condition of a vassal in the feudal system: a free man who holds land (a fief) from a lord in return for homage, fealty, and service, especially military service. It is thus an institutional and originally honourable relation, founded on reciprocal sworn obligations, not a state of mere slavery. The word names the relation itself, the body of obligations that bound tenant and lord, and the whole medieval social order built upon them.

  • Feudal sense (the primary historical meaning): the state or condition of being a vassal; the tenure of land held from a lord under homage, fealty, and service — e.g. the vassalage of a knight to his liege.
  • Collective sense: the whole body of a lord's vassals; the feudal host or tenantry considered as a group.
  • Valour sense (Old French inheritance): in medieval French, vasselage also meant "valour, prowess, feats of arms," because the ideal vassal was above all a bold fighting man; this heroic sense is now archaic but survives in chivalric literature.
  • Political and metaphorical sense (18th–19th c.): national or individual dependence on a foreign power; subordination reduced to disgrace — "reduced to vassalage," "held in vassalage," Macaulay's "ignominious vassalage," and the figurative "economic vassalage" or "vassalage of the mind."
  • Modern political jargon: in international-relations writing, the condition of a client or puppet state — formally independent but politically and militarily subordinate to a dominant power, often described as "vassalage" to a hegemon.

The word must be carefully set beside its neighbours, for the feudal vocabulary has fine discriminations. Servitude is the general condition of being under another's service or control, without feudal ceremony; bondage and serfdom go further, into unfreedom — the serf is tied to the land, the bondsman to a master, neither bound by the reciprocal oaths of the feudal contract. Subjection is simply being under another's power; subordination is the fact of lower rank; dependence is reliance, which may be innocent. What vassalage uniquely carries is the idea of a sworn, institutional, and originally dignified dependence — a free man's conditional submission — and it is precisely the corruption of that dignity that Macaulay calls ignominious: England the independent kingdom reduced to the ritual role of a tributary.

In register the word is historical, formal, and rhetorical; it is not current in everyday politics, having given way to dependence, clientage, and satellitism. Its characteristic collocations are state/condition of vassalage, reduce/reduced to vassalage, perpetual vassalage, feudal vassalage, and the figurative economic vassalage or vassalage of the mind. The noun vassal ("a feudal tenant") and the adjective vassal ("subordinate, servile") are its close family. Etymologically it is a French-derived word of the high Middle Ages: Old French vasselage, "the service and state of a vassal," from vassal, a word whose deepest ancestor is a Celtic one — Gaulish *wasso-, "a young man, a servant" — making vassalage one of the few English words of indisputable Continental Celtic stock.

Polysemy & Modern Usage. In international relations, vassalage describes political puppet-state subordination or historical feudal land tenure. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: Medieval Latin vassallus (servant, from Celtic *wasto- "young man, servant") moved from medieval land-tenure contracts to international political dependency: the honourable sworn relation of the feudal vassal becomes, in modern rhetoric, the shameful dependence of a whole nation on a foreign lord — exactly the transfer Macaulay performs when he calls England's subordination to France "ignominious vassalage."
