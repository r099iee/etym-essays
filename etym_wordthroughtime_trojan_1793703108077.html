- **Ancient Greece/Rome (Homer, Virgil)** - Primarily a demonym and adjective referring to the inhabitants and city of Troy, often with connotations of valiant defense, but ultimately tragic defeat. The "Trojan Horse" immediately established a secondary connotation of cunning deception and hidden danger.

- **Medieval/Renaissance Europe** - Continued use in historical and literary contexts, maintaining the dual connotations of heroic resistance and treacherous stratagem. The story of Troy remained a foundational narrative, influencing chivalric romances and political allegories.

- **17th-19th Century (English Literature)** - The adjective "Trojan" began to be used more frequently in a figurative sense to describe someone or something exhibiting great courage, endurance, or industriousness, drawing from the Trojans' prolonged defense. The "Trojan horse" idiom solidified its meaning as a hidden threat.

- **20th Century** - The figurative senses became more common in everyday language. The "Trojan horse" metaphor expanded into new domains, notably computing, to describe malicious software. The word also saw commercial appropriation, such as the "Trojan" brand of condoms, leveraging connotations of strength and protection.

- **Contemporary Usage** - "Trojan" remains a versatile term, encompassing its original historical meaning, its figurative senses of resilience and deception (especially in "Trojan horse" malware), and its commercial brand identity. The ancient myth continues to inform modern understanding and usage, demonstrating the enduring power of classical narratives.
