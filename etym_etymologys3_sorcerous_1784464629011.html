Sorcerous is the adjective of sorcerer, and the whole family rests on Latin sors, sortis—the lot, share, or portion cast to divine or assign fate. The Proto-Indo-European background is commonly given as *ser-, ‘to line up, bind, arrange’, the same broad root area that yields notions of ordered sequence (as in series) and of things allotted into places. That derivation is standard in the handbooks but not beyond dispute; an honest account flags it as the usual hypothesis rather than a certainty.

  • The lot in Roman practice. In Rome a sors (plural sortes) was a lot drawn or thrown for oracular and distributive purposes: fate made visible as a token. The Sibylline books were consulted by lot; judges and heirs were assigned by lot; the word carried both ‘share of an inheritance’ and ‘response of the oracle’. This double sense—distribution and divination—is the deep semantic engine of the family.
  • From lot-caster to sorcerer. Vulgar Latin *sortiarius names the person who works by lots—the fortune-teller or spell-dealer who reads allotted fate. Old French sorcier carries that figure into medieval French; Middle English borrows sorcer / sorcerer in the late fourteenth century, the extra -er being English reinforcement of an agent already marked. Sorcery names the art; by the late sixteenth century sorcerous supplies a ready adjective ‘of or like a sorcerer’.
  • Sound and form shifts. The chain is conservative phonetically—Latin sors → sortiarius → Old French sorcier → Middle English sorcer—with the -arius agent suffix reducing to -ier in French and settling as -er in English. The noun-to-adjective step adds the common suffix -ous (Latin -osus, ‘full of, having the quality of’), the same suffix in famous, glorious, marvellous.
  • Semantic drift. The movement runs from technical lot-casting toward general magic: casting lots to learn or assign fate → divination → forbidden or hidden knowledge → the sorcerer’s craft in general → any quality of that craft. In modern literary English a sorcerer is a spell-worker, often with a darker or more folkloric cast than a sanitized stage magician; sorcerous inherits that literary, slightly archaic glamour, and in fantasy-worldbuilding it has become the standard adjective for a sorcerer school or class of magic.
  • Socio-cultural context. The word’s persistent darkness is inherited from medieval and Renaissance attitudes: the lot-caster worked at the margin of religion and law, sometimes consulted, sometimes persecuted, always dealing with what should not be known. English legal and moral discourse of the 16th–17th centuries keeps sorcery and witchcraft close together, and the adjective kept their weight. In modern fantasy that weight is deliberately revived for atmosphere.
  • Literary attestations. Tolkien’s ‘strange and sorcerous’ applies the adjective to Gandalf’s smoke-wreathed look in dim light. The word does not catalogue a spell; it colours appearance with the old fate-and-magic stem—Bilbo’s guest briefly seen as something from the world of lots, power, and hidden craft. The form is the adjective itself: the quality of the sorcerer cast over a hobbit-hole evening, at once a party trick and a glimpse of an older, grimmer order of power.
