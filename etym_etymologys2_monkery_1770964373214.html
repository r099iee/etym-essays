Single lexical root: monkery (English monk + noun suffix -ery). One root word is involved; the suffix -ery is derivational. Derivation chain: 1. Greek mónos "alone, solitary" — attested from Homer, 8th century BCE; of uncertain etymology, with no secure PIE etymon agreed (older proposals linking it to PIE *men- "to think" are rejected by modern handbooks). NOTE: because the deep root is unknown, this chain honestly begins at attested Greek rather than at a reconstructed proto-form. 2. Greek monachós "solitary, alone; (Christian) monk" — mónos + adjectival suffix -achos; the specifically monastic sense crystallises in Christian Greek with the monastic movement of the 4th century CE (the desert fathers of Egypt). 3. Late Latin monachus "monk" — borrowed from Greek monachós, attested in Christian Latin from the 3rd–4th centuries CE (Jerome, Augustine); also monachē "nun". 4. Vulgar Latin monicus (also monacus) "monk" — syncopated colloquial variant, the direct source of the Germanic and Old English loans. 5. Old English munuc "monk" — attested from the early Old English period (8th century onward); the form reflects the syncopated spoken Latin monicus rather than the classicising monachus. 6. Middle English monek / monk "monk" — attested from the 12th century; modern English monk. 7. English monk + suffix -ery (from Old French -erie, ultimately Latin -āria + -ia) → monkery "the state, life, or system of monks; a place of monastic seclusion; monasticism" — first attested in the 16th century, in Reformation-era polemic. Final node: monkery. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The deep origin of Greek mónos is unknown; beginning the chain at attested Greek is the honest, zero-guesswork choice. The pejorative colouring of monkery is a matter of coinage context rather than etymology: the word was formed by entirely normal suffixation, but it entered English precisely when the Dissolution of the Monasteries was giving monasticism a violently negative public image, so its earliest attestations are already contemptuous.
