lumber — Timber sawed into boards, planks, or beams for use in building; the commercial end-product of the sawmill. In American English, the standard word for sawn building wood; in British English, the word still also means "heavy, clumsy, useless objects" — attic clutter, disused household goods. The two senses coexist under one spelling, and beneath both lies an older verb meaning "to move clumsily."

The literary moment. In the sentence "They break up the float and sell the lumber and he walks in the streets and hears tongues he has not heard before," the word is the terminal state of the flatboat. The vessel that carried the protagonist downriver, with all its accumulated life, is reduced without sentiment to its material value: "lumber" is the disenchanted word for the boat's corpse. The tone is deliberately prosaic — spare, reportorial prose — and the ordinariness of the noun is precisely what makes the moment bleak: a journey ends not in triumph but in a sale of boards. The phrase also prepares the man's entry into the strange city, where "he hears tongues he has not heard before"; the wood that no longer floats becomes the emblem of a life unmoored, and the word's quiet materiality grounds an almost metaphysical displacement in the most concrete object imaginable.

Primary sense — sawn timber (American English). Since the seventeenth century, American English has used lumber for "timber sawn into rough boards or planks for use" — the vocabulary of the sawmill, the lumber-yard, the freight ledger. The sense was seized upon in colonial New England from the British noun meaning "accumulated bulk," and it became the standard, flat, utilitarian American word for building material.

Secondary sense — clutter (British English). The older and originally dominant sense is "heavy, clumsy, useless objects; disused household goods" — the odds and ends that gather in attics and storerooms. British English still says "a lumber-room" for a junk-room, and this sense preserves the word's true root, which is not wood at all but clumsiness.

  • American sense (c. 1660s): "timber sawn into rough planks for use" — the sawmill and lumber-yard sense; the standard US word for building wood.
  • British sense (1550s): "heavy, clumsy, useless objects; junk" — attic clutter; a lumber-room is a junk-room; the sense that never fully gave way to the American narrowing.
  • The verb to lumber (c. 1300): "to move heavily and clumsily" — historically the FIRST sense, from which the noun grew; a lumbering bear, a lumbering gait.
  • The verb to lumber (1809, American): "to cut forest trees and prepare timber" — a back-formation from the American noun.
  • The industry vocabulary: lumber-yard (1777), lumber-mill (1830), lumber camp (1839), lumberjack (1830s) — the whole trade built on the American sense.
  • The root surprise: lumber is ultimately kin to lame — a word about brokenness, lameness, and awkwardness, which became, in America, the word for the very substance of building.

Why the word means what it means. The historical bridge is a study in the humbling of a word. The noun lumber was recorded in English only from the mid-1550s, and then meaning "heavy, clumsy, useless objects" — a sense that grew out of the older verb, to lumber, "to move clumsily" (c. 1300), borrowed from a Scandinavian source akin to dialectal Swedish loma "to move slowly, walk heavily" and Old Norse lami "lame," behind which stands a reconstructed PIE root *lem- "to break in pieces." The word's extra -b- is unetymological, added by analogy with humble and nimble. In colonial New England the "bulky clutter" sense was seized upon for the great export of the forests — sawn timber — and the American narrowing was complete. The word that began as "broken, lame, clumsy" became, through the clutter of storerooms, the standard word for building material.

Register. As sawn timber, lumber is flat, utilitarian, commercial — the vocabulary of the yard and the ledger. As clutter, it is homely and slightly contemptuous. As a verb of motion, it is vivid and physical. The card exploits the first sense while quietly releasing the third: the flatboat is broken up into its own boards, so that the old sense of "brokenness" reasserts itself within the modern one.
