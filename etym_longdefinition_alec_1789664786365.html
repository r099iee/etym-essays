ALEC is a masculine given name in English, conventionally understood as a pet form or diminutive of Alexander, which itself derives from Greek Alexandros meaning defender or warder-off of men. In contemporary British usage it functions both as a familiar short form used in place of Alexander and, increasingly since the late nineteenth century, as an independent legal given name bestowed at birth. Its register is familiar, unpretentious and warmly traditional, contrasting with the formal, imperial weight of Alexander.

In the immediate context, ALEC is not a common noun at all but a proper-name identifier: it stands for the middle name of John Alec Baker (1926-1987), the Essex naturalist and author of The Peregrine (1967) and The Hill of Summer (1969). The name therefore carries referential rather than lexical meaning: it picks out a unique historical individual whose privacy, reticence and posthumous reputation are central to the surrounding passage.

  • Primary sense: pet form of Alexander. Historically Alec arose in Scotland and northern England as a hypocoristic, alongside Alex, Sandy, Eck and Saunders, formed by shortening Alexander and adding the familiar -c/k ending. It shares the underlying meaning defender of men but pragmatically signals intimacy, approachability and everyday British masculinity rather than classical grandeur. Semantic bridge: shortening plus sound affection creates familiarity while retaining etymological sense in the background.
  • Secondary sense: independent given name. From the late Victorian period onward, Alec began to be registered as a full Christian name in its own right, no longer treated as merely a nickname. Census records show steady use in England, Scotland, Wales and the Commonwealth through the first three quarters of the twentieth century, peaking around the 1920s-1950s. Parents choosing Alec often intend solidity, modesty and no-nonsense Britishness. Bridge: the common process of diminutive emancipation, where a nickname acquires autonomous status through frequent independent use.
  • Tertiary senses and associations. Culturally, Alec evokes mid-twentieth-century Britain: Alec Guinness, Alec Douglas-Home, Alec Issigonis, and fictional Alecs in literature and soap opera. Connotations include quiet competence, reserve, dry humour and decency rather than flamboyance. As a surname element it appears rarely. In wordplay it is homophonous with smart Aleck/Alec, meaning a know-all, though spelled differently. Bridge: onomastic stereotyping, where famous bearers colour the perception of the name for subsequent generations.
  • Grammatical and pragmatic behaviour. As a proper noun, ALEC takes no plural or article in normal use, is capitalised, and does not inflect except for possessive Alec's. It cannot be defined by synonyms in the way common nouns can; its similar words are variants and cognates. Its meaning is established by baptismal act and social recognition rather than by description.

The distinction between common-noun polysemy and proper-name reference matters for learning: you do not look up Alec to understand what kind of thing it describes, but to recognise whom it identifies and what cultural resonances it carries. In this passage, recognising Alec as an unfashionable, plain, mid-century English middle name reinforces the portrait of Baker as an obscure, private, unshowy figure, in contrast to the mythologising barnacles of speculation that later attached to him. The ordinariness of the name becomes part of the pathos.
