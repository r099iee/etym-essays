• Hamadryads: Similar because both are Greek tree-nymphs bound to trees; differs because a hamadryad's life is absolutely bound to a single tree and ends with its fall, while dryads proper may be freer forest-nymphs — the difference is one of attachment.
  • Naiads: Similar because both are Greek nature-nymphs and appear side by side in the same litany; differs because naiads are water-nymphs of springs and streams (Greek naein, "to flow"), while dryads are tree- and oak-nymphs — the boundary is habitat.
  • Nymphs: Similar because dryads are a species of nymph; differs because nymph is the general term for all young female nature-deities, while dryad names specifically the tree-bound member of the class.
  • Oreads: Similar because both are mountain-and-wood nature spirits of Greek myth; differs because oreads haunt mountains and rocky heights, while dryads are bound to trees — habitat again separates them.
  • Nereids / Oceanids: Similar because both are classes of Greek nature-nymphs; differs because these are sea-nymphs of the Mediterranean, while dryads are land- and tree-nymphs; the element differs entirely.
  • Tree-spirits: Similar because both name the animate spirit of a tree; differs because tree-spirit is the modern, general, flattened term that loses the classical gravity and oak-specificity of dryad.
  • Wood-nymphs: Similar in naming a female spirit of the woods; differs because wood-nymph is looser and can cover any woodland sprite, while dryad specifically connotes the Greek oak-bound nymph of classical myth.
  • Tree-elves / Wood-elves: Similar in naming forest spirits of folklore; differs because elves belong to the northern European (Norse/Germanic) tradition and are not bound to single trees, while dryad is Greek and oak-specific.
  • Fauns: Similar because both appear together in classical woodland mythology; differs because fauns are goat-footed male woodland gods, while dryads are female tree-nymphs — gender and bodily form divide them.
  • Satyrs: Similar as male companions of the woodland nymphs; differs because satyrs are male, goat-like, lustful spirits of the wild, while dryads are female, bound to trees, and not inherently goat-like.
  • Sprites / Sylphs: Similar in being delicate nature-spirits; differs because sprites and sylphs are generic or air-elemental fairies of later folklore, lacking the specifically Greek, oak-bound, tree-soul meaning of dryad.
