Infallibly carries in its five syllables a whole history of Western anxiety about error - who may claim to be beyond it, and what happens when that claim moves from altar to market. Built from Latin in- (not) plus fallere (to deceive) plus -ible (liable to) plus -ly (in manner), it literally means in a not-liable-to-be-deceived manner, and its career runs from scholastic theology through papal politics to comic prediction about cheques.

Fallere: Latin fallere meant to deceive, disappoint, lead astray, escape notice - a broad verb covering trickery and honest mistake. Its PIE source is usually given as *ghwel- to deceive or go astray, seen in Greek phallos-like forms and Germanic cognates for fraud. Classical derivatives include fallax deceitful, fallacia trick, falsus false. The sense always joins intellectual error to moral slip: to be deceived is to fall, and Latin likes the image of truth as firm ground. English keeps the family in fallacy, false, fault and fail, all of which stumbled down from the same root via French.

Fallible to infallible: Late Latin coined fallibilis liable to err for theological anthropology: humans, unlike God, can be deceived. Medieval schoolmen then negated it to infallibilis not liable to err, to describe Scripture, the Church, and in restricted conditions the Pope and general councils when defining faith and morals. The term is thus a high-medieval technicality, not a Classical word, forged to draw a clean line between Creator and creature. Borrowing into Middle French infallible in the 14th century and Middle English infallible in the late 15th kept that solemn charge through Reformation controversy, where Protestants and Catholics hurled infallible at each other as both boast and insult.

  • Doctrinal phase (13th-16th c.): infallible as attribute of God, then of Church teaching; reverent and absolute, hedged by careful conditions about ex cathedra speech.
  • Philosophical generalization (17th c.): Descartes, Locke and others ask what, if anything, is infallible in reason - ideas, senses, signs? The word enters epistemology as test for certainty.
  • Secular hyperbole (18th-19th c.): infallible cures, infallible instincts, infallible judges, infallible machines - advertising and journalism borrow the halo while emptying the theology. Dictionaries record unfailing as everyday synonym.
  • Adverbial routine infallibly (17th-20th c.): Formation with -ly is regular; usage slides from in a manner free from error to invariably, as in Byron and Dickens. Wodehouse inherits the weakened but still pompous adverb and aims it at weekly bills.
  • Modern definition and irony: Vatican I (1870) defined papal infallibility narrowly, keeping the strong sense alive in Catholic doctrine, while secular English uses infallibly mostly to mean reliably or, with a wink, predictably badly.

Phonetically the path is conservative: Latin fallere to French faillir to fail shows regular loss, but the learned borrowing infallible kept double l and clear vowels, and English adds stress on the second syllable with reduced ending. Semantically the drift is from capacity (cannot err) to frequency (does not fail) to fate (is sure to happen), each step preserving certainty while lowering the stakes from salvation to solvency. In the cheque sentence that descent is complete and deliberate: the speaker canonizes his own bankruptcy forecast, making financial embarrassment as sure as dogma. Modern readers hear both layers - the echo of councils and the clink of coins - which is why infallibly still sounds funnier and weightier than just always.
