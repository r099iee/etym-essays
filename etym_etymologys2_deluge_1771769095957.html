Linear derivation chain for the single root word DELUGE: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *lewH- (also reconstructed *leu-, *(s)leuH-) 'to wash'. Reconstructed, not directly attested; the root of washing and pouring is securely reconstructed on the evidence of Greek, Latin, and other branches (see LIV, Pokorny). Stage 2 — Latin (archaic, attested in the 2nd c. BCE onward): luere 'to wash' (the simple verb, surviving chiefly in compounds) and lavāre 'to wash, bathe' (the full-grade denominative form). Latin lavere/lavāre are the direct continuation of the root's 'wash' sense. Stage 3 — Latin (classical): diluere 'to wash away, to dissolve, to weaken by mixing' — formed with the prefix dis-, 'apart, away,' + luere, 'to wash': literally 'to wash apart.' Attested in classical Latin prose (Cicero, Pliny); its participle dīlūtus gives English dilute. Stage 4 — Latin (classical, 1st c. BCE onward): dīlūvium 'a flood, an inundation' — a noun built on the stem of diluere with the suffix -ium, literally 'a washing-away, a washing apart'; a parallel feminine form dīlūvies 'a flood' also existed. Attested in Latin literature from the classical period. Stage 5 — Old French (13th c.): deluge 'the Flood (of Noah); a great flood' — inherited/adapted from Latin dīlūvium, with the regular Old French reshaping of the Latin vowel sequence. Stage 6 — Middle English (14th c.): deluge 'the Flood of Noah; a great flood' — borrowed from Old French; in its first English uses it names the biblical Deluge, and this remains its primary sense for three centuries. Stage 7 — English (modern): deluge 'a severe flood; a great quantity of something arriving at the same time; (verb) to flood, to overwhelm' — the fourteenth-century borrowing continuing in both the literal and the figurative senses; the figurative 'deluge of X' construction is established from the seventeenth century onward. Note on debated stages and nuances: (1) The exact PIE form — whether the root contained a laryngeal (*lewH-) and whether an initial *s- was present (*(s)leuH-, cf. Greek klúzein, assumed s-mobile) — is debated among handbooks; the core 'to wash, to pour' semantics are not in doubt. (2) The Slavic verbs of pouring (e.g., Russian lit' 'to pour') are sometimes compared with the root but are generally assigned to a distinct root and are NOT part of this chain. (3) English dilute, ablution, launder, lavatory, lotion, latrine, and lava are all collateral members of the same Latin family as diluere/dīlūvium, but they are not on the direct ancestor line of deluge itself; only dīlūvium → Old French deluge → English deluge is the direct line.
