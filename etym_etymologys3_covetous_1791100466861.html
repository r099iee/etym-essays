Covetous descends from heat — boiling, seething desire — through Roman lust, medieval sin, and King James moral thunder into modern envy. Its root is Proto-Indo-European *kup- "to boil, be agitated, desire," which vividly fuses physical heat and emotional craving. The same root yields Sanskrit kupyati "he boils with anger," Old Church Slavonic, and Latin cupere.

Latin cupere "to long for, desire eagerly" (cf. cupidus "desirous," cupiditas "passionate desire, greed, ambition," Cupido the god of erotic desire) was morally neutral — one could cupere glory or wisdom. Late Republican moralists, however, increasingly paired cupiditas with avaritia: desire curdled into greed. Cicero contrasts cupiditas pecuniae with virtue; Sallust blames Rome's fall on cupiditas. Christianity sealed the negative turn: cupiditas became Augustine's shorthand for disordered love, opposed to caritas.

  • Vulgar Latin innovation: Classical Latin had no frequentative verb "to covet"; Vulgar Latin created *cupidietare (replacing cupere) meaning "to keep desiring, hanker after," first visible in Romance outcomes. Old French coveitier (12th c., covoitier, cuveitier) means "to desire, lust after," already with illicit overtones.
  • Conquest borrowing: After 1066, Anglo-Norman coveiter enters Middle English as coveiten (c. 1225, Ancrene Wisse): "to desire wrongfully what belongs to another." The noun coveitise (covetousness, avarice) arrives simultaneously, one of the Seven Deadly Sins in penitential literature.
  • Adjective formation: Middle English adds native -ous (from Old French -ous, -eux, Latin -osus "full of") to make covetous (c. 1300, covetouse): "full of coveting." Parallel to gluttonous, lecherous, envious, it labels a habitual sinner, not a momentary wanter.

Phonology shows normal Anglo-French development: Latin cu- /ku/ palatalises before vowels in French (cu > co / cov-), English restores /kʌv/ spelling cov-; loss of -d- in cupidietare > coveitier is regular intervocalic lenition. Modern RP /ˈkʌv.ɪ.təs/ has three light syllables, stress on cov-, exactly like jealous, envious.

The word's cultural zenith is the King James Bible (1611). The Tenth Commandment — "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife..." (Exodus 20:17) — fixes covet and covetous as the English names for illicit desire. Wyclif, Tyndale, and Coverdale had used covet earlier, but the KJV made it proverbial. Shakespeare, Milton, Bunyan, and Victorian preachers wield covetous for the grasping sinner. Dickens's catalogue — "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous" — is a secular Decalogue: six present participles hammering like hammers before the adjective lands the verdict.

  • Legal afterlife: Covetous never became a technical legal term (law prefers fraudulent, acquisitive), but covet appears in theft and property rhetoric. Modern psychology prefers envious, acquisitive; covetous retains biblical gravity.
  • Modern narrowing: Today covetous almost always implies another's possession: you covet thy neighbour's car, award, spouse. Mere ambition is ambitious; wrongful desiring of another's is covetous. The eyes typify it: "covetous eyes, covetous glance."
  • Debate note: Derivation from PIE *kup- via Latin cupere is uncontroversial (de Vaan, Watkins). Some older sources posited a separate root *keu-, but current consensus unites cupere, cupidus, Cupid under *kup-ei- "to desire." The Vulgar Latin *cupidietare is reconstructed, not directly attested, but demanded by all Romance forms (French convoiter, Italian cupidigia).

From boiling pot to boiling heart to grasping hand: covetous names desire that cannot bear another's good.
