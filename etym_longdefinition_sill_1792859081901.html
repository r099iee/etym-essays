Sill is, in its central architectural sense, the horizontal member that forms the bottom of an opening: the strip of wood, stone, or concrete you rest your elbows on beneath a window, the timber you step over at a door, the ledge on which a frame sits. Every building that has framed apertures has sills, because gravity and weather demand them: the bottom of a hole in a wall must be closed, levelled, and drained, and the sill is the component that does all three, carrying the weight of the frame above while shedding rainwater outward so it does not rot the wall beneath.

In the source sentence the word carries exactly this homely, physical sense, charged with sudden action: with a dart he sprang to the sill and thrust a long arm out and downwards. Someone vaults or lunges to a window, lands at its lower ledge, and reaches down through the opening. The sill here is a boundary made solid, the last interior surface before the drop outside, a launchpad for leaning, climbing, escaping, or snatching. The reader needs no dictionary because the body knows the object: knee-high or waist-high, hard under the palms, the place where inside ends.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Window sill (primary modern sense): the shelf-like ledge at the foot of a window, inside or outside, on which objects are set and elbows leaned. The bridge from the general to the particular is frequency: windows are the openings people touch most, so the bare word sill defaults to window in everyday speech.
  • Door sill or threshold (ancient and still current): the strip at the foot of a doorway, called a sill by carpenters and a threshold in general speech. This is the word’s oldest job, reaching back to Old English, where syll meant the foundation beam of a house: every entrance was literally built on a sill.
  • Structural sole plate or foundation beam (carpentry sense): the heavy horizontal timber laid on a foundation wall to carry a timber-framed structure, as in a house sill or barn sill. The bridge is structural hierarchy: the lowest beam that receives all the load above is the sill of the whole building, not just of one opening.
  • Vehicle sill (automotive sense, 20th c.): the panel below a car’s doors, between the wheel arches, also called the rocker panel in American usage. The bridge is positional analogy: it is the lowest longitudinal member of the body shell, the threshold you swing your feet over when entering, exactly as with a door.
  • Geological sill (technical sense, from the 19th c.): a sheet of igneous rock intruded horizontally between older strata, as distinct from a vertical dyke. Miners borrowed the builder’s vocabulary: just as a timber sill lies flat between courses of a building, the rock sill lies flat between beds of country rock, a concordant intrusion.
  • Canal or dock sill (nautical and civil-engineering sense): the submerged ledge at the entrance of a dry dock or lock over which vessels pass. The bridge is functional: it is the bottom of a water-gate opening, the underwater threshold of the harbour’s doorway.
  • Figurative threshold (literary extension): to stand on the sill of discovery, the sill of adulthood. The bridge is the oldest metaphor in the house: a boundary you pause on before crossing, so the concrete ledge lends its shape to any moment of hesitation before a new state.

Semantic bridges and register: every sense preserves three entailments of the carpenter’s original: lowest position, load-bearing duty, and boundary function. A sill is always underneath something, always holding something up, and always marking where one space ends and another begins. That triple logic is why geologists and engineers could adopt the word without strain: the rock sheet and the dock ledge are both underneath, both supportive, both transitional. In ordinary speech the word is domestic and tactile rather than grand; palaces have thresholds and portals, but cottages have sills you can sit on, and children’s literature is full of figures springing to sills, as here, precisely because the object belongs to the scale of the body, the height of a leap, the width of a handhold.
