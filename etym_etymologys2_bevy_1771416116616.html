1. PIE *peh₃- / *po(i)- “to drink” — Pokorny I 839; one of the most securely reconstructed PIE verb roots. 2. Latin bibere “to drink” — the inherited Italic verb, the ordinary word for drinking. 3. Old French beivre “to drink” (9th–12th c.; later boire) — from Latin bibere. 4. Old French bevee / bevée “a draught; a drinking, a drinking-session” (12th–13th c.) — a noun formed from beivre. 5. Anglo-French bridge → Middle English bevye “a company” (14th–15th c.) — the received etymology holds that a bevy was originally “a company of drinkers,” a group gathered for a bevee. 6. English bevy (15th c.) “a company; a bevy of quails, a bevy of roes” — but the earliest English records are terms of venery, flock-words of the medieval hunt, not drinking-words. 7. English bevy “a company of ladies / young women” (16th c.); “a bevy of swans, of beauties” (17th–19th c.); modern journalese “a bevy of officials / executives / proposals” (20th–21st c.) — English bevy, the root word. Note on the debate: the PIE drink-root and the Old French bevee stage are secure, but the semantic leap from “a drinking-session” to “a flock of quails” is debated. Some scholars doubt the drink-origin altogether and treat bevy's earliest English senses (flocks of quails and roes) as primary, leaving the word's ultimate origin unknown; handbooks therefore hedge the connection.
