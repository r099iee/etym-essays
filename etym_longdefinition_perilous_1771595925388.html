perilous — full of danger, fraught with risk, attended by peril; exposing one to the chance of harm, loss, or destruction. In the literary context — Ruskin explaining the original design of the work that became Modern Painters — the adjective applies not to a physical journey but to an intellectual influence: the critiques he attacks have "their perilous tendency as guides of public feeling." The danger Ruskin alleges is not a broken ladder or a stormy sea but the corruption of public taste: false criticism is perilous because it misleads the feelings of a whole reading public, training people to admire what is meretricious and to despise what is true. The word therefore works on two registers at once — it borrows the gravity of physical danger (the "perilous seas," the "perilous pass" of romance) and applies it to a moral and aesthetic threat, making the critics' errors sound less like mistakes than like hazards that can shipwreck a nation's judgment. The tone is earnest, prophetic, and slightly old-fashioned: Ruskin speaks as a guide warning travellers away from a treacherous road. Polysemy in historical order.
  • Sense 1 (the core, 13th c. onward): "full of or involving peril; dangerous, hazardous, fraught with risk" — a perilous journey, a perilous undertaking, perilous times.
  • Sense 2 (the moral and metaphorical extension, 16th c. onward): "fraught with danger to the reputation, welfare, or safety of others or of oneself" — a perilous doctrine, a perilous tendency, a perilous example; the sense Ruskin employs.
  • Sense 3 (the romantic and poetic register): the elevated, chivalric adjective of the "perilous seas" and "perilous pass" — danger imagined with heroic colouring; a "perilous" situation is grave and possibly fatal, not merely risky.
  • Sense 4 (formal and technical): in insurance and legal usage, of exposure to a peril — where peril itself is the technical term for a cause of loss (fire, flood, shipwreck).
  • Related formations and variants: peril (n., 13th c., from Old French), perilously (adv.), perilousness (n.); the obsolete/archaic variant parlous — a Middle English corruption of perilous, used from the 14th to the 16th c. and surviving as a jocular or regional word ("a parlous state of affairs"); the obsolete periculous, directly from Latin.
The semantic bridge. The word descends from Latin periculum, "a trial, a test," which belongs to the great PIE root *per-, "to pass through, lead across, go forward." To "go through" something came to mean to undergo a trial, to put to the test; and because a test is dangerous — it can be failed, it can destroy — the meaning of periculum slid by metonymy from "the procedure" (the trial) to "its outcome" (the risk, the danger). This is a textbook case of semantic transfer: the test and the danger it poses became the same word. English peril, borrowed in the thirteenth century from Old French, kept the noun; the adjective perilous came through Old French perilleus from Latin periculosus, "dangerous, full of peril." Boundaries against near-synonyms.
  • Dangerous is the plain, factual, all-purpose adjective for anything likely to cause harm, and it carries no elevation.
  • Hazardous is the more technical, clinical word — "hazardous materials," "hazardous working conditions" — and it involves chance and probability rather than an imminent threat.
  • Risky is impersonal and outcome-oriented; a risky investment, a risky manoeuvre, is a bet on the future.
  • Unsafe is practical and everyday, applied to structures, practices, and places.
  • Fraught (with danger) is literary and diffuse, suggesting a situation charged with risk.
  • Precarious means dangerously insecure, dependent on chance — a position that may collapse at any moment.
  • Treacherous suggests hidden, deceptive danger — the road or ice that looks safe and is not.
  • Parlous is the archaic/corrupt twin of perilous, now jocular or dialectal.
Grammar and register. Perilous is a qualitative adjective formed from peril + the suffix -ous ("full of, having"), exactly parallel to hazardous, dangerous, and venturesome. It can be attributive ("a perilous journey") or predicative ("the path was perilous"), and it takes the intensifiers of formal prose ("most perilous," "truly perilous"). The register is literary and slightly archaic: perilous is the most elevated member of the danger family, implying not mere chance but an imminent, dire, possibly fatal threat, and it carries a romantic-heroic colouring that dangerous entirely lacks — a "dangerous climb" is a statement of fact, a "perilous climb" is an episode from a romance. Modern everyday speech prefers dangerous and risky; perilous survives in elevated, prophetic, or deliberately old-fashioned contexts. Modern use. In contemporary English perilous appears most often in (1) literary and historical prose, where it supplies the gravity of chivalric romance, (2) journalism and political commentary, where a "perilous situation," "perilous moment," or "perilous path" lends an earnest, warning tone, (3) moral and intellectual discourse, where a doctrine or tendency may be called perilous to the public welfare — exactly Ruskin's usage — and (4) the technical vocabulary of insurance, where the noun peril names a cause of loss. The word is a favourite of the sermon, the editorial, and the adventure story — the three genres that still want a danger that feels like fate rather than mere probability.
