Stage 1 — PIE *dṓm / *dem- “house; to build” (Pokorny *dem-; the root of the Indo-European word for the dwelling and the family that lives in it; attested reflexes include Sanskrit dámaḥ “house” (Rigveda), Avestan dam-, Greek dómos “house”, Latin domus, Armenian tun — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE). Stage 2 — Latin domus “house, household, family” (attested in the earliest Latin; the private home, the hearth-and-household unit). Stage 3 — Latin dominus “master of the house, lord” (domus + -nus; the paterfamilias as lord of the household; attested in classical Latin, 1st c. BCE). Stage 4 — Latin dominium “lordship, sovereignty, right of ownership; property, estate” (dominus + -ium; the technical word of Roman jurisprudence for the master's right of ownership and control). Stage 5 — Old French / Anglo-Norman dominion “rule, sovereignty; a territory under a ruler” (from Latin dominium; 12th–13th c.). Stage 6 — Middle English dominioun “lordship, rule, territory” (13th–14th c.). Stage 7 — Modern English dominion, with the plural DOMINIONS “the sovereign territories of a ruler” (Macaulay's “dominions of Charles V”; also the constitutional “the Dominions”, the self-governing British colonies, from the 1860s). NOTE: the same Latin dominium produced a doublet family in English — Old French demeine/demaine gave demesne “land retained by the lord”, and French domaine gave domain “a territory; a field of authority; an internet address” — so dominion, demesne, and domain are all descendants of the one Roman house-word.
