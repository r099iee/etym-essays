TRIUMPHS is the plural of English "triumph", a word with a Greek intermediate whose ultimate origin is debated. The attested chain is as follows. - Greek "thriambos" "a hymn or processional song in honour of Dionysus; a Bacchic procession; the cry of such a procession" (attested from the 5th c. BCE, e.g. in the context of dithyramb and comedy; origin debated - see note below). - to Latin "triumphus" "a triumph; the triumphal procession of a victorious general" (borrowed from Greek "thriambos", probably via Etruscan mediation; attested from the 3rd-2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus and in the shout "triumpe" in processions). - to Old French "triumphe" / "triomphe" "a triumph, victory, festive procession" (attested from the 12th c.). - to Middle English "triumphe", "tryumph(e)" "a triumph, a victory, a festive procession" (attested from the 14th c., e.g. in Chaucer and Gower). - to English "triumph" "the Roman victory ceremony; any great victory or success" (attested from the 16th c.) to English "triumphs" (plural form, regular -s plural, attested from the 16th c.) to TRIUMPHS (modern English form). DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The Greek "thriambos" has no accepted Indo-European etymology; it is widely believed to be a loanword, possibly from a Pre-Greek substrate or from an Anatolian/Phrygian source, connected with cultic cries and processions. The Latin form "triumphus" shows the regular treatment of Greek aspirates in Latin (Greek "th" became Latin "t", Greek "mb" became Latin "mb"), but whether Latin borrowed directly from Greek or through Etruscan is debated; the Etruscan form (spelled "triumpe" in Etruscan inscriptions) suggests Etruscan mediation was at least possible. The precise route from Bacchic hymn to Roman military procession is also debated by ancient historians.
