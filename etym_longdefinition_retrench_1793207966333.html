Retrench is a verb meaning to cut back, reduce, or curtail — most often spending, costs, or commitments — in the name of economy and necessity. A household retrenches when it gives up servants and suppers; a company retrenches when it closes branches and sheds staff; a government retrenches when it pares budgets after war or waste. The word always implies deliberate cutting in response to pressure, never accidental loss.

In the source passage Cobbett praises a lord who has had the courage to ______ in all sorts of ways, contrasting him with a neighbouring stock-jobber who rattles by in a coach with four horses and out-riders. The praise is pointed: retrenchment here is moral as well as financial — the old landed gentleman living plainly within his straitened means, while the new money of the funds flaunts what the war economy has given it. For Cobbett, to retrench is to confess honestly that the tax-eaters have thinned your purse, and to refuse to ape riches you no longer hold.

Core meaning in detail: The verb divides into transitive and intransitive uses that share one image, the knife. Transitively, one retrenches expenses, establishments, or superfluities — one takes the knife to them. Intransitively, one simply retrenches: one lives more narrowly, spends less, draws in one's horns. Both senses carry approval in Cobbett's world and anxiety in the modern boardroom, where retrenchment means the good years have ended.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To cut back expenditure, to economise (primary modern sense, from 17th c.): families, firms, and states retrench when income falls. The bridge from cutting in general to cutting costs runs through household management: the establishment is imagined as something overgrown, to be pruned back to the quick.
  • To curtail, lessen, or abridge (general sense, 16th c.): to retrench privileges, ceremonies, or appetites. The bridge is the same knife applied beyond money: anything swollen — powers, phrases, pleasures — can be retrenched to decent measure.
  • To cut off or pare away a part (earliest English sense, 16th c.): literally to slice off, as a surgeon retrenches dead flesh. The bridge is the French original, retrencher, to cut off: the earliest English uses keep the physical blade before the metaphor takes over.
  • To fortify with retrenchments (military sense, 16th–18th c.): to retrench a camp is to cut a defensive ditch and bank across it — to cut the ground into a fortress. The bridge runs through the sister noun retrenchment, an interior earthwork: the same cutting that prunes a budget digs a rampart.
  • To live more plainly, to draw in (intransitive, social sense, Cobbett's use): the lord retrenches in all sorts of ways — fewer horses, plainer table, no London season. The bridge joins money to honour: retrenchment honestly avowed becomes a badge of old-fashioned virtue against new-fashioned show.
  • To restrict or confine (of rights and statements, formal): to retrench a claim or a commission within narrower bounds. The bridge narrows cutting to bounding: the knife becomes a fence, and retrenchment means keeping within it.

Register and connotation: retrench is formal and slightly old-fashioned — editorials, budgets, and history books rather than kitchens or pubs, where people say cut back or tighten belts. It dignifies its subject: retrenchment sounds like prudence and self-command, never like panic, which is why ministers announce retrenchment and leave journalists to translate it as cuts. Cobbett's praise shows the word at its most moralised: to retrench is to face facts like a gentleman. Its noun, retrenchment, is commoner in modern use than the verb itself.
