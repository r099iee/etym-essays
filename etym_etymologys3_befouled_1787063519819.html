1. The primeval stink: PIE *pu- — Befoul leads back to one of the most evocative reconstructed roots of Proto-Indo-European, *pu- (also written *pū-), "to rot, stink, be foul, putrefy". It is an imitative, sensory root whose short explosive syllable suggests a bad smell, and it generated a vast family of "foul" words across the family: Latin putere "to stink" (whence putrid, putrefy, pus), Greek pýthein "to rot", and the Germanic adjective at the heart of this word. From its very beginning, the word-family fused physical stench with moral disgust — a fusion that survives in befouled today.

2. The Germanic adjective — Proto-Germanic *fūlaz, "foul, dirty, rotten, putrid", produced Old English fūl — a word whose modern descendant, foul, still carries nearly the full range of the original: filthy, offensive, rotten, morally abominable, breaking rules (a "foul" in sport), and clogged or blocked (a "foul" chimney). The Old English word already had strong moral colouring in the earliest records, where fūl glossed both physical filth and sin, and where the foulness of the devil and of hell is a constant theme.

3. The denominative verb — From the adjective, Old English formed a class I weak verb, fȳlan, literally "to make foul". The formation shows a classic sound change, i-umlaut: under the influence of a following -jan suffix, the long back vowel ū of fūl was fronted to ȳ. This is the same causative pattern seen throughout Old English word families — fūl/fȳlan "foul / to make foul" is a textbook pair, exactly parallel to full/fyllan "full / to fill". This fȳlan is the direct ancestor of modern to foul.

4. The intensive prefix — Old English could intensify such verbs with the prefix be- (cognate with German be-), which added thoroughness, deliberateness, and a focus on the object affected. befȳlan thus meant "to make foul all over, to defile, to pollute" — exactly the modern meaning. The compound is attested in Old English religious and homiletic prose, where befȳlan is used of moral defilement, the pollution of holy things, and the taint of sin. The prefix turned an ordinary verb of soiling into a word of deliberate desecration.

5. The French complication — Into Middle English came a competing word from French: defoulen, from Old French defouler, "to trample down, oppress, violate" (from Latin de- + fullare, "to full, trample cloth"). For centuries the two families overlapped and contaminated one another in spelling and sense: defoulen "to trample/oppress" and befylen/befoulen "to make foul" were used almost interchangeably in Middle English moral writing, and the senses of violation and oppression bled into befoul. By early modern English, defoul had largely died out, and befoul absorbed the borrowed word's force.

6. Modern English — Befoul settles into its modern form by the 16th-17th centuries. Shakespeare and his contemporaries use it of moral and physical pollution alike. In the industrial age the word gained an ecological application — befouled rivers, befouled air — that remains prominent in environmental writing today. Its moral and religious senses survive mainly in formal, poetic, and fantasy registers, which is precisely the register Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit, where the dragon befouls the dwarf-halls with his filthy coming and going.

  • c. 4500-2500 BCE: PIE *pu- "to rot, stink, be foul".
  • c. 500 BCE - 100 CE: Proto-Germanic *fūlaz "foul, rotten, dirty".
  • c. 700 CE: Old English fūl "foul" (adjective) and fȳlan (verb) "to make foul" by umlaut.
  • c. 950-1050 CE: Old English befȳlan "to defile, pollute" in religious prose.
  • 12th-15th c.: Middle English befylen/befoulen; the French defoulen converges in meaning.
  • 16th-17th c.: Modern befoul stabilises; used by Shakespeare and his contemporaries.
  • 19th-21st c.: Ecological sense (befouled rivers, befouled air) joins the moral and physical senses.

7. Nuance and debate — The Old English verb fȳlan is straightforwardly the umlauted denominal from fūl; there is no serious dispute about the core chain. The main historical complexity is the convergence of the native befylen family with the borrowed defoulen family in Middle English, which muddied spelling and senses for a time. Modern befoul is the native survivor that absorbed some of the borrowed word's force — a small, clear case of two unrelated words meeting and merging in meaning.
