- 9th–11th Century (Old English): Personal Faith & Sacrament of Baptism - In Anglo-Saxon England (crīstendōm), originally signified the personal state of being a Christian, religious instruction, or the holy sacrament of baptism itself.
- 12th–15th Century (Middle English): The Geopolitical Realm of Christian Europe - Shifted decisively into a territorial and civilizational noun denoting the entire geographic and political commonwealth of Christian nations (the realm of Christendom), defined in existential contrast to Islamic and pagan lands.
- 16th–17th Century: Shattered Unity & Denominational Fragmentation - Following the Protestant Reformation and the devastating Wars of Religion, the ideal of a politically unified Christendom dissolved into rival Protestant and Catholic sovereign states, though the term survived as a cultural umbrella denoting shared European heritage.
- 18th–19th Century: Secularized Hyperbole & Civilized Baseline - Came to be employed colloquially in sweeping idioms (e.g., 'nowhere in Christendom', 'the finest scholar in Christendom') to mean 'the entire civilized Western world', carrying an elevated, slightly nostalgic rhetorical register.
- 20th–21st Century: Historical Category & Post-Christian Re-examination - In modern usage, functions primarily as an academic historical term referring to the medieval political and religious order, or in sociology to analyze 'the end of Christendom' in an increasingly secular, pluralistic global society.
