- c. 1300–1400 (Middle English entailen) - Dual physical and legal meaning: physically "to notch, chisel, sculpt, or carve stone and wood in relief" (from Old French entailler); simultaneously adopted in Anglo-Norman royal law courts to denote establishing a fee tail (restricting land inheritance strictly to specified direct lineal descendants).
- 1450s–1700s (Early Modern & Common Law Primacy) - The physical carving sense became archaic, and the legal meaning dominated English jurisprudence; to entail an estate meant placing it under legally binding settlement to prevent spendthrift heirs from mortgaging, alienating, or selling ancestral lands outside the family bloodline.
- 1750s–1850s (Victorian Metaphorical Extension) - Popularized in classic English literature (notably Jane Austen’s Pride and Prejudice) as both a dreaded legal reality and a social metaphor for any burdensome duty, inescapable family obligation, or lifelong consequence attached to privilege or rank.
- 1900s–Present (Formal Logic & Generalized Consequence) - Formalized in symbolic logic and semantic philosophy (as logical entailment, $\vDash$, where the truth of premises guarantees the truth of the conclusion); broadened universally across economics, governance, and daily speech to denote any necessary consequence or prerequisite.
