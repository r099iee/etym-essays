• Petitioner: Similar because both denote someone making a formal request; differs because petitioner is a neutral, bureaucratic, or legal term (e.g., signing a petition or filing a court motion) that does not imply the deep emotional submission or kneeling humility of a supplicant.
  • Suppliant: Similar because it is an etymological doublet sharing the identical meaning; differs because suppliant entered English earlier via French and carries a more poetic, archaic tone, whereas supplicant retains a Latinate, formal quality.
  • Pleader: Similar because both make earnest requests; differs because pleader emphasizes the verbal or rhetorical argument made on someone's behalf (such as a legal advocate), rather than the personal posture of submission.
  • Beggar: Similar because both ask for assistance from a position of need; differs because beggar carries strong connotations of extreme poverty, street destitution, and asking for alms/money, whereas supplicant is a dignified, often sacred or formal role before kings or deities.
  • Mendicant: Similar because both involve asking for aid; differs because mendicant specifically refers to members of religious orders (such as Franciscans or Dominicans) who have taken vows of poverty and survive on charitable donations.
  • Postulant: Similar because both petition for acceptance; differs because postulant is a technical ecclesiastical term for a candidate seeking admission into a religious monastic order.
  • Aspirant: Similar because both desire something from a higher authority; differs because an aspirant is actively striving for ambition, high office, or honor, rather than humbly pleading for mercy or favor.
  • Solicitor: Similar because both ask for something; differs because solicitor in British English is a legal professional, and in American English someone actively seeking business, sales, or contributions.
  • Suitor: Similar because both petition someone of status; differs because suitor specifically refers to someone pursuing marriage and romance or pursuing legal proceedings in court.
  • Intercessor: Similar because both present prayers or petitions; differs because an intercessor petitions on behalf of someone else, acting as a mediator between the authority and the guilty party.
  • Client: Similar in the context of ancient Rome (patron-client relationship); differs because a client is bound by social, political, and financial reciprocity rather than temporary humble supplication.
