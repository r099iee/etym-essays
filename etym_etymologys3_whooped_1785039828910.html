WHOOPED is the past tense of a word whose history is the history of a noise. It begins not in a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root but in the echoic gesture of the human voice — the sharp, rising, excited cry — and it has no Indo-European ancestry at all: it is an imitative formation, and its meaning has never drifted far from its sound. The direct English line passes through medieval French. Old French huper or houper, itself imitative, was the huntsman's shout, the loud rising call used to rouse hounds or signal to companions in the field; a close cousin, huer, gave English the phrase hue and cry, the communal shout of pursuit after a felon, recorded from the thirteenth century. The very idea of a cry that carries across distance and unites a group was built into the word from the start.

Middle English took up whopen or houpen in the mid-fourteenth century, meaning "to shout with a loud, excited voice," and the noun houp named the cry itself. The spelling history is instructive: the word was borrowed with an h- and was often written houp, but around 1450 it acquired the initial wh-, an unetymological flourish borrowed from native words such as what and whale, which fixed the modern written form while the pronunciation stayed closer to "hoop." The mismatch between spelling and sound has persisted for six centuries, and it is a small but telling sign of how the word was carried in speech rather than books — people knew the cry before they knew the letters.

From the hunting field the cry widened into war and celebration. The frontier of British North America coined war-whoop (1761) for the battle cry of Native American warriors, a fierce exultant yell of attack that terrified enemies and rallied allies; the word's association with pursuit and the chase, inherited from the hunter's halloo, made it the natural name for a war-cry. Medicine named whooping cough (1739) for the disease's characteristic gasping, crowing "whoop" — the convulsive intake of breath between coughing fits — and the disease remains known by that echoic name to this day. Ornithology followed suit: the great white North American crane was named the whooping crane (1791) for its loud, far-carrying call, and the bird carries the name still. In every case the sound itself did the naming; the word has functioned for centuries as a phonetic label attached to whatever makes a whoop.

The celebratory senses developed alongside the martial ones. Whoop it up (1881) meant to make a disturbance or celebrate boisterously; whoopee (1845) became the very word for uninhibited revelry, and even spawned the whoopee cushion (1931), the joke novelty whose flatulent squawk is, etymologically, a whoop of sorts. The pattern is perfectly consistent: the word has never meant anything but the sound of a loud, excited human cry and the things that resemble it. That is why whoop sits so naturally in games, hunts, celebrations, and war — it is the cry of sudden, overwhelming emotion, joyful or derisive, exultant or alarming.

Socio-culturally, whoop has always belonged to the vigorous, informal register. It is a word of action and noise, not of reflection; it names the moment when the voice takes over from the thought. In literature it carries an energy that plain shouting lacks — a shaped, rising, rhythmic quality that suggests exultation rather than mere volume, and that is why writers from the ballads to the modern novel reach for it when a character bursts out in triumph or derision. Tolkien's goblin-drivers, who whooped and cracked their whips behind the running captives, give the word back to its oldest hunting grounds: a pack-voice of pursuit, exultant and predatory, ringing down the cavern corridors. The echoic shape, unchanged across eight centuries, lets the reader hear the goblin shout as clearly as Tolkien heard it — the oldest kind of word, a noise that became a verb and never forgot what it was.
