DRAT is an exclamation of mild anger, vexation, or annoyance, and, by extension, a verb meaning "to curse mildly." It is a euphemism, a soft oath, that gestures at damnation and decay while committing to neither. As an interjection it stands alone: "Drat!" a sudden, small explosion of irritation. As a verb it takes an object in the old imprecatory idiom: "drat him!", "drat it!", "drat the thing!" the speaker vents real feeling, but the feeling is deliberately kept small. In The Hobbit, Thorin, facing the necessity of going back into the abominable tunnels to search for the missing burglar, dismisses Bilbo with a blustering "drat him, I say" — exasperated resentment at the inconvenience the hobbit has caused, yet far too homely and comic to be a true malediction. The word vents genuine feeling while deflating it: it is the anger of an inconvenienced host rather than of an enemy, and its very quaintness drains the threat out of the threat.

Origin and euphemistic character. Drat is a minced oath, a clipped and softened form of "God rot (him)!" an imprecation calling divine decay upon the offender. In "drat him" the speaker wants to curse, but the curse has been bleached: god is gone, and the rot reduced to a mere tap of consonants, and the result is an oath with no religious or destructive force whatsoever. It belongs to the long English tradition of safe swears, darn for damn, gee for Jesus, gad for God, tarnation for damnation, the swearwords of speakers who wish to sound angry without actually being profane.

  • Sense 1 (interjection): "an exclamation of annoyance, vexation, or mild anger," "Drat! I've left my keys." The default use, quick, rhetorical, and self-contained.
  • Sense 2 (verb, imprecatory): "to curse mildly; to express annoyance at or toward someone or something," "drat him!", "drat it!", "drat those stairs!" The idiom is fixed and archaic, preserving the old grammar of imprecations, curse him, confound it, blast it.
  • Sense 3 (as a noun, rare): a mild curse or oath, "he gave a drat and a sigh"; the substantivised use is marginal but attested, treating the interjection as a countable little curse.
  • Sense 4 (euphemistic register): the word is defined as much by what it is not as by what it is: it is not profane, not strong, not truly angry; it is the stage-oath of the blustering but harmless character, the vexation of the uncle, the father, the housekeeper. Its register is dated, folksy, faintly theatrical.

Register and stylistic bridge. Tonally, drat is dated, colloquial, and comic. It belongs to the vocabulary of Victorian farce, the comic novel, the music hall, and the period drama, the safe oath of the bluff, hearth-side character who means nothing by it. In contemporary English it survives precisely because of its datedness: writers and speakers use it to sound old-fashioned, playful, or self-consciously gentle, and Tolkien's dwarven grumbling shows it at work, a swearword that keeps the shape of a curse while signalling that no harm is intended. The bridge from "God rot" to "drat" is one of erosion and disguise: over two centuries of use the oath lost its divine subject and its destructive verb, keeping only the angry gesture, the phonetic ghost of a curse, energetic on the surface, harmless beneath.
