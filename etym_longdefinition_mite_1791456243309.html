mite is a small word with several distinct but overlapping lives. In the Anki context — “the poor wee mite got a nasty thorn in his little foot” — it means a tiny creature, here a dog, regarded with pity and affection. More generally it can mean a small child or a person one feels sorry for; a microscopic arachnid; a very small amount; or a small coin, especially in the biblical phrase “the widow’s mite”. The senses are not all from the same source, and the word is a good example of how homonymy, metaphor and sound change can braid together.

Primary sense in context: a small creature or child. The phrase poor little mite is chiefly British and informal. It is used of a child, an animal or occasionally an adult who looks small, vulnerable or unfortunate, and it usually carries sympathy rather than contempt. “The poor mite was so ill”; “the little mite had a thorn in his paw”; “she’s only a mite, but she bosses the whole family”. The word can be affectionate, pitying or gently humorous, and it often implies that the creature is braver or more engaging than its size would suggest. In Wodehouse the dog is not merely small; he is a full comic character, and mite signals both his tininess and the narrator’s tender amusement.

The arachnid sense. In zoology a mite is any of a very large group of minute arachnids, traditionally placed in the order Acari (or subclass Acarina) together with the ticks. Mites have eight legs as adults, though the larvae of some species have six; they are not insects. They live in soil, dust, plants, stored food, the fur and feathers of animals, and the human skin. The house dust mite is a major source of allergens; the itch mite causes scabies; the varroa mite devastates honeybee colonies; spider mites are serious agricultural pests; and the tiny red velvet mites are familiar in gardens. The scientific sense is precise and neutral, but the everyday word still carries a faint sense of something almost invisibly small, which is why it lends itself to metaphor.

The quantity sense. A mite can mean a very small amount, and as an adverb it means “slightly” or “a little”. “I’m a mite tired”; “that’s a mite too much”; “he was a mite put out by the question”. This use is chiefly British, informal and slightly old-fashioned or folksy; in American English it survives mainly in dialect or in deliberately homely writing. It can also appear as a noun: “a mite of comfort”, “not a mite of evidence”. The sense develops naturally from the idea of a tiny object: a mite is so small that it becomes a unit of smallness, and then a vague quantifier.

The coin sense and the widow’s mite. Historically a mite was also a small coin. The English word is recorded from the mid-fourteenth century for a Flemish copper coin of very low value, conventionally described as about a third of a farthing. It was used by Wyclif and later translators for the Latin minutum in the Gospel story of the widow who put two small coins into the Temple treasury (Mark 12:41–44; Luke 21:1–4); the Greek word behind the Latin is lepton, the smallest Jewish coin. From this story come the phrases the widow’s mite and to contribute one’s mite, meaning a small but sincere contribution. A mite box is a box for collecting small alms, especially in churches and Sunday schools.

Obsolete weight. A further technical sense is a small unit of weight, one twentieth of a grain, used in historical pharmacy and assaying. This sense is obsolete in ordinary use but appears in older dictionaries and in historical metrology.

Two etymologies? English has two words mite that are usually treated as homonyms. The arachnid word is native: Old English mīte “mite, tiny insect”, from Proto-West Germanic *mītā and Proto-Germanic *mītǭ, perhaps literally “the cutter” or “biting insect”, from a root meaning “to cut”. The coin word is a borrowing from Middle Dutch mijte or Middle Low German mīte, a small coin; it is probably ultimately related to the same Germanic complex, but English received it by a different route. The two words have influenced each other semantically: the coin sense reinforced the “small amount” sense, and the arachnid sense reinforced the “tiny creature” sense. In the Anki context the relevant sense is the creature sense, which belongs with the native arachnid word and with the affectionate use of mite for a small person or animal.

Register and modern use. Poor little mite is marked as British and informal, and it is increasingly associated with older speakers and with period fiction. A mite in the sense “slightly” is similarly informal and regional. The arachnid sense is fully current in science, medicine and pest control, where it is neutral and precise. The coin sense survives mainly in biblical and charitable contexts. The word is a homophone of might, and in dialect writing mite is sometimes used as an eye-dialect spelling of might; that use is unrelated to the noun.

  • Small creature: the sense in “poor wee mite”, sympathetic and affectionate.
  • Arachnid: a microscopic eight-legged arthropod, often parasitic or allergenic.
  • Small amount: “a mite” = slightly; “a mite of” = a little bit of.
  • Coin: a small low-value coin; hence “the widow’s mite” and “one’s mite”.
  • Weight: one twentieth of a grain, an obsolete apothecaries’ measure.
