SLOPES is the plural of SLOPE, which denotes a stretch of ground whose ends lie at different heights — a surface that slants upward or downward, an incline. A slope is defined by the geometry of deviation from the level: one end or side of it is higher than the other, and the word names that graded declination as a piece of terrain, whether gentle or steep. In The Hobbit, Bilbo looks forward from the high path and "could see before him only ridges and SLOPES falling towards lowlands and plains glimpsed occasionally between the trees": the slopes are the declivities that carry the eye — and the road — downward out of the mountains, the graded ground between high ridge country and the flat plains beyond. The tone is gradual, traversable decline: neither a cliff's drop nor a plain's flatness, but land slipping steadily down. Narratively the slopes open the descent toward Mirkwood, and their gentleness is deceptive, for the ground is already moving beneath the travellers.

The word has a ghost of motion inside it. Modern use is chiefly nominal — the noun names the fixed shape of an inclined surface — but the verbal sense survives, and the very next sentence of the passage in The Hobbit uses it, "the ground sloped away," and that ghost of movement is exactly what gives the noun its quiet sense of downward pull. Historically the word began as a verb and an adjective of slanting before the noun was formed in the early seventeenth century, so the plural SLOPES is a noun born from motion: an inclined surface is ground that has, as it were, slipped down to its present angle.

  • Sense 1 — an inclined surface of ground (primary topographical sense): a stretch of ground that rises or falls at an angle; a hillside, a declivity, or an ascent. This is the sense in The Hobbit and in everyday geography: the slopes of a mountain, a north-facing slope, a gentle slope.
  • Sense 2 — any slanting surface (general physical sense): a slope may be of timber, a roof, a floor, a road, or a bank: the slope of a roof, the slope of a railway line, a slope of a table. The bridge is the same geometry of deviation from the level applied to any material.
  • Sense 3 — the measure of inclination in mathematics and surveying (technical sense): in mathematics, the slope of a line or curve is its gradient — the ratio of vertical change to horizontal change, the number that measures steepness. The bridge is the transfer of the everyday idea of steepness into exact quantification; the mathematical sense dates from the 19th century.
  • Sense 4 — a recreational or sporting surface (skiing and winter sports): a ski slope, a piste, a slope designated for sledding or skiing; "the slopes" in a ski resort means the runs. The bridge is the topographical sense applied to the specialised surface of winter sport.
  • Sense 5 — the verb and the idiom "to slope off" (verbal sense): the verb to slope means to lie at an angle or to move obliquely, and the colloquial "to slope off" (or "to slope away") means to slip away quietly, to leave furtively. The bridge is the old verbal sense of the 15th century, "to flee, run away" — slipping away like a body that loses its footing — which survived in the idiom long after the noun took over.

The semantic bridge in one paragraph: every sense of slope grows out of one picture — a surface or a body leaning off the level. The word's oldest recorded English meaning was a verb, "to flee, run away" (mid-15th century), a development of the Old English idea of slipping; the participle then yielded the adjective meaning "slanting," and from the adjective-noun the noun "a slope" was formed in the early 1600s for an inclined stretch of ground. From that noun, the geometry of inclination was quantified into the mathematical slope of a line, applied to the slopes of a mountain and of a ski run, and, through the surviving verb, allowed the word to keep its old sense of quiet escape in "slope off." The plural SLOPES foregrounds the landscape reading: a series of graded declivities carrying the eye down, which is precisely what Tolkien's sentence needs — the slopes are the fixed, descending geometry of the mountain's edge, waiting to be walked.

Register, grammar, and collocations: slope is the neutral, workmanlike term of the geographer, the surveyor, the engineer, and the skier — technical without being clinical, standard in news, sport, and science, and entirely at home in literary prose. It is a countable noun, regularly pluralised. Common collocations include steep slope, gentle slope, north-facing or south-facing slope, ski slope, the slopes of a mountain or volcano, slope angle, slope of a graph (mathematics), and as a verb the ground slopes away, to slope off. In The Hobbit the plural is part of a landscape inventory — ridges, slopes, lowlands, plains — and each term carries the eye one stage further down; the slopes are the middle stage, neither crest nor plain, the graded descent itself.
