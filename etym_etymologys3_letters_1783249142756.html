The word letters, in the sense of literature and learning, is a plural that grew out of the singular letter, and the biography of that singular is famously obscure. The attested Latin word is littera, with the by-form litera, which meant 'a letter of the alphabet, a writing, a document'. Beyond that point certainty stops. One leading theory, recorded since antiquity, derives littera from Greek διφθέρα (diphthera), a hide or skin used as writing material, transmitted to Latin through Etruscan — a plausible route, since the Etruscans were the great intermediaries who gave the Romans their alphabet itself, and a d-to-l shift is not impossible in that transmission. A rival, equally ancient theory connects littera with the Latin verb linere 'to smear', since writing on wax tablets was literally a matter of smearing a pointed stylus across the wax; this theory would ultimately lead back to the PIE root *slei- 'slimy, sticky', the ancestor of English slime. Both remain hypotheses; the honest verdict is that littera has no secure Proto-Indo-European ancestry and must be classed as an uncertain culture-word, probably filtered through Etruscan. This matters: unlike nearly every other word in this vocabulary, letters cannot be traced to a single PIE root, and a correct etymology must say so rather than invent one. The Latin plural's second life. What is certain is the internal history of the Latin plural. Litterae accumulated a second life as a collective term for 'writing, records, and hence literature and scholarship'. The Romans' litterae humaniores, 'the more humane letters', was the ancestor of what we call the humanities and, through Oxford, of Literae Humaniores ('Greats'). Latin litterātus ('learned, literate'), litterātor ('grammarian'), and litterātūra ('writing, grammar, literature') spread this family into the vocabulary of learning; litterātūra is of course the direct ancestor of English literature and French littérature. Meanwhile the word travelled the Romance roads: Old French letre, then lettre, carried both the concrete sense 'alphabetic sign, epistle' and the institutional sense 'learning', and the phrase lettres patentes (open letters) became the English letters patent, the royal open document. Italian lettera (pl. lettere), Spanish letra (pl. letras, as in letras clásicas), and Portuguese letra (pl. letras) all carry the double sense of alphabetic sign and learning. Into English. Anglo-French passed the word into English as letter by the thirteenth century, displacing the native Germanic compound bōcstæf ('book-staff'), the Old English word for an alphabetic sign. The singular took the concrete senses — the alphabetic character, the written epistle, the document — and the plural, on the model of Latin litterae and French lettres, took the full collective meaning of 'scholarly culture'. The 14th and 15th centuries show letters already meaning learning; Chaucer's 'clerk' of Oxenford is a man of learning. But the institutional hardening belongs to the Renaissance and the Republic of Letters (res publica litterarum), the international community of scholars corresponding across Europe in the 16th–18th centuries. It was in this world that 'a man of letters' became a fixed title — the French homme de lettres rendered into English — meaning a scholar-writer devoted to humane learning, and 'arts and letters' a frozen collocation for creative and scholarly culture. Narrowing and survival. The 19th century, which narrowed literature to imaginative writing (a shift the OED dates with care), left letters as the broader, more elevated term: it still covered history, oratory, philosophy, and correspondence, and it carried an honorific, humanistic warmth. Macaulay, writing in 1848, stands exactly at the hinge: his 'Roman arts and letters' uses the older, capacious sense — the whole written culture of Rome — which in his own day was beginning to be voiced as 'literature'. The twentieth century continued the narrowing of everyday usage, so that today letters survives chiefly in set phrases — arts and letters, man of letters, woman of letters, the republic of letters — and in academic degrees (Doctor of Letters, D.Litt.) and institutional names (the Letters building of a university). It has acquired a slightly old-fashioned, even grandiloquent flavour, which is precisely why Macaulay's sentence sounds both classical and faintly elegiac. The word is thus a museum piece of high culture that is still perfectly legible: a plural that began as marks on a page and ended as the whole edifice of the written tradition.
