- Frankish warband — the backer: a Germanic warand is the protector who answers for his man, shield and surety in one.
- Norman conquest — the paper arrives: Old Northern French warant crosses with the Conqueror as both the man (guarantor) and his sealed paper (authorisation).
- Medieval England — guarantee everywhere: merchants warrant goods, lords warrant titles, friends warrant friends — the word is the economy's handshake.
- Tudor law — the state takes the seal: arrest and search warrants make the word an instrument of crown power — and, by the same paper, a limit on it.
- 18th c. — the countryman's confidence: I warrant you settles into bluff assured speech, Fielding's farmers and Austen's neighbours staking their word daily.
- Today — justification owns the verb, paper owns the noun: reports tell what the facts warrant while police knock with warrants in hand — the old backer still answering, in boardroom and doorway alike.
