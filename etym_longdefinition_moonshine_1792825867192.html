Moonshine in the source sentence means arrant nonsense: If that's where you get your news from, you'll never want for ______ — you'll never go short of foolish talk, tall tales, and empty chatter. The speaker dismisses the source as a fountain of the unsubstantial, and chooses a word that is itself a little piece of poetry: talk with the luminosity of moonlight and exactly its solidity.

The noun's three great senses share one image — brightness without body. First and oldest is the light of the moon itself, silvering fields and seas since Old English. Second is foolish talk, illusion, and pretence — the American moonshine of the 19th century that survives in phrases like whole moonshine and mere moonshine. Third is illicitly distilled liquor, the moonshine of Prohibition stills and Appalachian hollows, also called moonshine whiskey. Each sense is the same metaphor pointed a different way: a glow you cannot grasp, whether it is light on water, sense in talk, or honesty in a bottle.

Core meaning in detail: As moonlight, the word is pure poetry — drunk with moonshine, sleeping in the moonshine — the pale cold illumination that romantics loved and navigators used. As nonsense, it belongs to bluff dismissive speech: to call a story moonshine is to say it shines and signifies nothing, a judgement delivered with a countryman's snort. As liquor, it is history in a jar: untaxed corn whiskey made by night (hence the name — distilled by the light of the moon, away from the revenue men), colourless, fiery, and celebrated in song from the hills of Tennessee to the bothies of Scotland, where it answers to poteen and peatreeking.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The light of the moon (oldest sense, Old English onward): fields white with moonshine — the silver illumination itself. This is the literal compound (moon + shine) and the fountain of all the rest, still current in poetry and in the names of songs, roses, and racehorses.
  • Foolish talk, pretentious nonsense, illusion (Tolkien's sense; from the 15th–19th c.): never want for moonshine — never lack for empty chatter. The bridge is insubstantial brightness: as moonlight looks like something you could gather and proves to be nothing in the hand, so the tale shines in the telling and vanishes under examination. Tudor writers already called alchemy and flattery moonshine; American speech of the 1800s fixed it as plain nonsense.
  • Illicit distilled liquor (from the late 18th–19th c., Prohibition's glory word): corn whiskey or rum made secretly and sold untaxed. The bridge is night work: the stills ran by moonlight to hide the smoke from excisemen, so the product took the light's name — glow made drinkable, danger made saleable, celebrated in a hundred bluegrass songs.
  • To make or run illicit liquor (verb, chiefly moonshining): he went moonshining in the hills. The bridge runs from product back to practice: because the drink is moonshine, making it is moonshining — night labour under a complaisant moon.
  • Romantic illusion, daydream, unreality (literary shading): moonshine of love, lover's moonshine — the tender self-deception of the smitten. The bridge narrows the nonsense sense to the heart: love, like moonlight, transfigures the commonplace and dissolves by daylight, and poets from Shakespeare's lovers to Tin Pan Alley have lived profitably in that glow.
  • Smuggled or furtive activity generally (extended verb sense): older Scots and Irish writers have cattle moonshined (driven by night) and goods moonshined ashore. The bridge generalises the liquor's night from the still to any contraband moved under the moon's conniving light.

Register and connotation: moonshine is affectionate even in contempt — nobody hates moonlight, so even the nonsense sense scolds with a wink. The liquor sense is outright romantic: outlaws with copper stills, racing stock cars born from running the revenue (the seed of NASCAR), ballads of mountain dew. Tolkien's hobbit uses the talk sense with exactly this warmth: not lies (which would accuse) but moonshine (which merely laughs) — gossip as harmless glow, shining and signifying nothing, the staple diet of inn parlours.
