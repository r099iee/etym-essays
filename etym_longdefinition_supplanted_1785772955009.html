Supplanted is the past tense and past participle of the verb to supplant, meaning to take the place of (something or someone), to displace and occupy the vacated position or function. The word is used across political, technological, scientific, and biological registers, and it still carries a faint echo of its Latin source: supplantare, "to trip up," from sub- "under" + planta "sole of the foot" — the image of tripping someone by getting under their feet. In older and more literary prose, supplanted can therefore imply craft, treachery, or competitive ousting — the favorite supplanted at court, the heir supplanted by a rival, the incumbent supplanted by an intrigue. In modern technical and scientific prose the word is cooler and more systemic: steam supplanted sail, smartphones supplanted compact cameras, one species or method supplants another through fitness or efficiency rather than personal malice.

  • Core sense — replacement/displacement: B occupies the functional place formerly held by A. The past form is typically passive or past: "X was supplanted by Y," "Y supplanted X." No claim about whether the replacement was fair, legal, or gradual — only that the change is complete.
  • Political and interpersonal sense (with intrigue): A rival, heir, favorite, or officer is supplanted through scheming, favoritism, or force. Bridge: the old heel-tripping image survives here — displacement by craft rather than by merit alone. Often carries mild disapproval.
  • Technological and economic sense: A product, format, or method supplants its predecessor because it is better, cheaper, or more convenient; "smartphones supplanted standalone cameras" (this card's context). Bridge: the verb's competitive structure transfers from persons to things, and the "scheming" connotation drops away.
  • Scientific and evolutionary sense: A species, allele, or strategy supplants another in a niche through fitness, competition, or environmental change. Bridge: the same replacement logic, now impersonal and ecological.
  • Linguistic and cultural sense: A word, custom, or institution supplants an older one: "the new spelling supplanted the old," "the decimal system supplanted older units of measure."
  • Religious and biblical resonance: In the Bible, Jacob "supplanted" his brother Esau (taking his birthright and blessing; the name Jacob is folk-etymologized as "heel-catcher"). This gave the verb a long theological life meaning "to take another's rightful place," often with moral judgment. Bridge: the heel of the foot is literally in the Latin; the biblical heel-trip made the metaphor a scripture.
  • Register and boundaries: Standard formal English; less casual than "took over," less official than "superseded" in bureaucratic use. It always asserts that B now holds A's place: supplanted differs from displaced (which stresses removal, not succession) and from replaced (the blandest general verb of substitution). Antonyms: retained, entrenched, maintained, preserved, upheld.
  • Romance divergence — impersonation: Modern Spanish suplantar has strengthened a "personate/impersonate" sense (suplantar identidad, "identity theft"), while English kept "take the place of" — an instructive example of daughters of the same Latin verb specializing differently.

Collocations: supplanted by technology, supplanted his rival, gradually supplanted older forms, supplanted in favor of, was supplanted as leader. Emotional coloring ranges from neutral historical description to faint disapproval when human scheming is implied; the word is frequently passive precisely because it emphasizes the result — the completed change — rather than the agent's motive.
