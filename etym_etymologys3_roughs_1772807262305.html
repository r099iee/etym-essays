FROM SHAGGY HIDE TO HIRED THUG: THE LONG DESCENT OF ROUGHS The word rough is an old Germanic adjective of texture and surface, and its leap from describing the feel of things to naming violent men is one of the great slides of English along the scale of refinement. The story begins not in classical Latin or Greek but in the Germanic world of unpolished material life: wool, hides, ground, and weather. THE GERMANIC BASE. Old English rūh (with stem rug-) meant 'not smooth to the touch, coarse, hairy, shaggy'; of hides, undressed; of land, uncultivated. It continues West Germanic *rukhwaz and Proto-Germanic *rukhaz, with clear sisters in Dutch ruig 'shaggy' and German rauh/rau 'rough.' A higher Proto-Indo-European attachment is sometimes proposed, linking the family to a root meaning 'to smash, tear up' and citing Sanskrit rūkṣa- 'rough' and Latin ruere 'to rush, collapse,' but these are tentative and not established; the immediate history that matters for English is the Germanic 'shaggy/uneven' adjective and its West Germanic kin. RADIATION FROM TEXTURE. From physical texture the word spread in every direction. By the fourteenth century rough applied to what is harsh to experience or handle: stormy weather and seas, rude and coarse speech, rough treatment, crudely made objects, unpolished literary style. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it took on the sense 'approximate, unrefined, preliminary,' visible in rough estimate (from c. 1600) and rough draft (from the 1690s), where the idea of an unfinished surface is transferred to unfinished work. Noun uses followed: broken or uncultivated ground, the disagreeable side of things ('the rough of it'), and in golf, the rough, the unmown grass bordering the fairway, where the original meaning 'uncultivated ground' survives almost untouched. The fixed phrase diamond in the rough (from the eighteenth century) made the 'unpolished but valuable' idea proverbial, and rough-and-ready, rough-and-tumble, and roughshod all exploit the same image of untamed energy. THE SUBSTANTIVIZATION. The decisive step for the card came when the adjective became a noun naming a type of person. A rough, 'a coarse or violent man, a rowdy,' is attested by 1837, in the midst of the Victorian urban era when great cities swarmed with street gangs, brawlers, and hired muscle. The plural roughs then became the collective label for such men: gangs of roughs, hired roughs, street roughs, called in by a powerful man to do violence for him. Scholars have noted a possible shaping contact with ruffian, a Romance loanword meaning 'brutal fellow,' which is phonetically close; the Century Dictionary even speculated that 'a rough' might be partly an abbreviation of ruffian reshaped to match the native adjective. The semantic slide is continuous: unrefined surface, to unrefined behavior, to unrefined man, to professional violence. Roughness in texture becomes roughness in manners, and roughness in manners becomes a career of brutality. SOCIOCULTURAL CONTEXT. The noun sense grew up in the streets and courts of nineteenth-century Britain and its colonies, in a literature of crime, sensation fiction, and journalism that chronicled urban violence. 'Roughs' were the human equivalents of storm-tossed seas and untilled ground: forces of nature that could be summoned but not refined. The word appears in Victorian and Edwardian writing about public disorder, prize-fighting, and the paid bully-boys of gentlemen and gangsters. By the early twentieth century the plural had settled into British-influenced slang and period prose, and in the modern age it reads as dated or deliberately old-fashioned, displaced by thugs, goons, toughs, and hoodlums. THE MEANING ON THE CARD. In the Feng Yuan episode, Xue Pan's roughs are 'rough' first in manners and morals, then in the specialized noun use: professional jaggedness. The etymological path from shaggy hide to hired thug is the whole argument of the word in one narrative moment. The adjective's plural stands alone as a class of violent men, and in the story they are the tactile metaphor made flesh: privilege's coarse edge, called in to smash whatever stands in the young master's way. The word belongs to the vocabulary of force disguised as description, and its history is a warning that words about surfaces easily become words about people. Key points to remember:
  • Rough is native Germanic, first a texture word meaning 'shaggy, coarse, uneven.'
  • The person-sense 'a rough' is substantivization of the adjective, attested from 1837.
  • The plural roughs means street thugs and hired strongmen, period-flavored in modern prose.
  • The higher PIE link is tentative; the German and Dutch cognates (rau, ruig) are solid.
  • Possible, unproven contact with ruffian shaped the noun sense.
  • Golfing roughs, art-publishing roughs, and rough drafts are separate, later senses.
