FOUR ROOT WORDS — each isolated below. === TINGED === Stage 1 — PIE: *teng- "to soak, wet, dip" (attested via reflexes in Greek and Italic). Stage 2 — Latin: tingere / tinguō "to dip, wet, moisten, dye, stain"; past participle tinctus (attested from the 1st century BCE). Stage 3 — Old French: teindre "to dye"; participle teint "dyed, tinted, stained" (attested from the 9th century). Stage 4 — Middle English: tingen "to tinge, dye lightly" (attested from the 15th century). Stage 5 — English: tinge "to color lightly, to give a slight trace of color" (16th century). Stage 6 — English: tinged (past participle, preserved inflection). === STIRRING === Stage 1 — PIE: *(s)twer- "to rotate, turn, whirl" (the root of many turning-motion words; Pokorny's *twer-). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic: *sturjaną "to stir, disturb, move briskly" (reconstructed on the evidence of the attested Germanic verbs). Stage 3 — Old English: styrian "to move, agitate, rouse" (attested from the 9th century). Stage 4 — Middle English: stiren "to move, excite, agitate" (attested from the 13th century). Stage 5 — English: stir "to begin to move; to agitate" (verb and noun; 13th–14th centuries). Stage 6 — English: stirring (gerund/noun: "the beginning of motion"; also participial adjective). === IMPERCEPTIBLE === Stage 1 — PIE: *keh₂p- "to seize, grasp, take" (attested across Italic, Germanic, and other branches). Stage 2 — Latin: capere "to take, seize, grasp" (attested from the 3rd century BCE); in composition with per- "through" as percipere "to grasp thoroughly, perceive" (attested 1st century BCE). Stage 3 — Latin: perceptibilis "that can be perceived, graspable" (Late Latin; attested by the 4th century CE). Stage 4 — Late Latin: imperceptibilis "not perceivable" (formed with the negative prefix in-; Late Latin/medieval usage). Stage 5 — English: imperceptible "not perceptible, beyond the grasp of the senses" (attested from the 16th century). Stage 6 — English: imperceptible (modern form, unchanged). === DRAFT === Stage 1 — PIE: *dʰreǵʰ- "to draw, drag, pull" (attested via Germanic, Celtic, and other reflexes). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic: *draganą "to draw, drag, pull" (reconstructed; attested in the Germanic verb family). Stage 3 — Old English: dragan "to draw, drag, pull" (attested from the 8th century). Stage 4 — Middle English: draught "the act of drawing; something drawn" (nominalized verbal form, attested from the 13th century; also draght, draught). Stage 5 — English: draught "a drawing or pulling; a current of air drawn in; a drawing of liquid" (14th century onward); spelling variant draft (attested from the 16th century, the gh dropped in pronunciation). Stage 6 — English: draft (the air-current sense; also the drawn-document, drawn-liquor, conscription, and nautical-depth senses, all radiating from the core image of "drawing"). NOTE ON THE DRAUGHT/DRAFT SPLIT: the spelling draught and its pronunciation /drɑːft/ remained the British norm, while the American spelling draft (a phonetic respelling of the 16th century) carried the word into new meanings; the two are the same word split across orthography and are routinely merged as draft in American usage.
