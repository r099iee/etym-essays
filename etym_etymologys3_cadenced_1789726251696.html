The evolution of cadenced is the story of a musical “fall” becoming a general aesthetic of rhythm. It begins not in English but in the Indo-European idea of falling, passes through Latin rhetoric and Italian music, enters French and English as cadence, and only then acquires the participial adjective cadenced to praise anything that moves well in time.

  • PIE and Latin foundations: PIE root *kad- “to fall, yield” underlies Latin cadere “to fall, drop, die, come to an end.” From cadere Latin formed present participle cadens and Late Latin noun cadentia “a falling, cadence.” In Latin rhetoric the fall of the voice at clause-end (cadentia) was already an object of study: Cicero and Quintilian taught orators to let periods fall gracefully.
  • Italian mediation: in 16th-century Italian musical theory cadenza meant literally “a falling” — the falling of voice or fingers toward closure — then technically the concluding flourish of an aria or concerto. Travelling musicians and theorists carried the term north.
  • French and early English cadence: Middle French cadence (16th c.) meant both musical close and rhythmic flow of verse or prose. Borrowed into English in the late 14th–16th c., cadence first meant rhythmic flow in poetry, then military marching rhythm (“cadence call”), then intonation in speech. Shakespeare, Milton and later prosodists use it freely.
  • Birth of cadenced: the adjective/participle cadenced is attested from the 17th–18th c. as writers needed a word for “endowed with cadence.” Early uses are musical and prosodic: cadenced verse, cadenced periods. By the 19th century, with the rise of rhythmic prose (De Quincey, Ruskin, Pater) and Celtic revival interest in musical peasant speech, cadenced becomes a favourite term of literary praise, exactly as in the Aran passage.
  • Sound changes: phonetically the line is regular: Latin /kaˈdeːre/ → Italian /kaˈdɛntsa/ with affrication of -ti- before vowel → French /kadɑ̃s/ with nasalisation and loss of final vowel → English /ˈkeɪdəns/ with Great Vowel Shift diphthongisation of a to /eɪ/ and stress retraction. Addition of -ed /d/ is the normal English participial suffix, devoiced after /s/ in spelling but voiced as /zd/ cluster simplified to /nst/ in careful speech, often /ˈkeɪdənsd/.
  • Semantic drift: from concrete “falling” to musical “closing formula” to general “rhythmic flow” to evaluative “beautifully rhythmic.” Each step preserves the image of controlled descent and return. The modern sense has bleached the idea of literal falling but retains the feeling of arrival and rest.
  • Literary attestations and culture: 18th-c. elocutionists, 19th-c. Celticists (Standish O’Grady, Yeats, Synge), and 20th-c. stylisticians all use cadenced to link peasant or archaic speech to music. To call Aran-nurtured phraseology cadenced is thus ideological: island speech is heard as preserving a lost musical English.

In short, cadenced compresses two millennia: a PIE fall, a Latin orator’s pause, an Italian singer’s flourish, and a modern critic’s delight in well-timed sentences. No stage is conjectural beyond the secure PIE root; the Romance chain is fully attested in OED and Etymonline.
