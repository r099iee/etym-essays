Congo most commonly denotes a vast region of Central Africa centred on the Congo River and Congo Basin, and by extension either of two modern states — the Democratic Republic of the Congo (capital Kinshasa) and the Republic of the Congo (capital Brazzaville) — as well as the river, rainforest, and historically the Kingdom of Kongo.

In the Upper Congo context of the source passage, it refers specifically to the upper reaches of the Congo River in the late nineteenth century, the arena of early colonial exploration, missionary reports, and notorious accounts of violence and cannibalism reported by European travellers. Capitalised always, it functions both as a proper geographical name and as a historical-cultural shorthand for remoteness, colonial exploitation, and tropical wilderness in English literature.

The term is highly polysemous because over five centuries Europeans applied the same name to a kingdom, a river, a forest, a colony, and two successor republics:

  • The Congo River: the second-longest river in Africa and second-most voluminous in the world, formerly called the Zaire (1971–1997). Example: shipping on the Congo. This is the physical-geographical core sense.
  • The Congo Basin / Congo rainforest: the great equatorial forest and drainage basin covering much of Central Africa. Example: deforestation in the Congo. Extended by metonymy from river to watershed.
  • The Kingdom of Kongo (c.1390–1914): a powerful Central African state encountered by the Portuguese in 1483. Historians still write Kongo with K for the precolonial kingdom and Congo with C for the colonial/modern referents.
  • Upper Congo / Lower Congo: colonial-era subdivisions of the river course; Upper Congo means the Lualaba and upstream stretches in present-day DRC. The source passage uses exactly this sense.
  • Colonial Congo: Congo Free State (1885–1908, King Leopold II's personal possession), Belgian Congo (1908–1960), French Congo / Middle Congo. In literature Congo often evokes Heart of Darkness, colonial horror, and rubber atrocities.
  • Modern states: Democratic Republic of the Congo (formerly Zaire) and Republic of the Congo (Congo-Brazzaville). Journalists disambiguate as Kinshasa-Congo vs Brazzaville-Congo, DRC vs ROC.
  • Cultural-metaphorical Congo: in older English usage, a symbol of impenetrable jungle, primitivism, or chaos — e.g. Conrad, Vachel Lindsay's poem The Congo. Now dated and often regarded as pejorative/colonialist; modern style guides advise precise geographical reference.
  • Other uses: Congo as surname or given name in African diaspora; Congo music/dance (Congolese rumba); Congo cloth, Congo red (a dye), Congo peas (pigeon peas in Caribbean English); Congo eel (amphiuma); place names in diaspora e.g. Congo Town.

Grammatically Congo is a singular proper noun, used attributively (Congo rainforest, Congo crisis, Congo question) and in compounds (Congo-Brazzaville, Congo-Kinshasa, Upper-Congo, Middle-Congo). Pronunciation stresses first syllable. Do not confuse Kongo (ethnonym/kingdom, Kikongo language group) with Congo (modern English/French spelling of river and states). In French: le Congo; in Portuguese: Congo; in Kikongo: Kongo, Nzere.

Register is neutral-geographical today, but historical literary uses carry heavy colonial connotations. Modern careful usage specifies which Congo is meant, gives dates for historical entities, and avoids using Congo as generic shorthand for Africa.
