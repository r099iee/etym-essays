A genuinely debated etymology. The origin of throe is one of the acknowledged puzzles of English etymology — the OED and Etymonline both present alternatives openly, without deciding. Candidate A: Old English þrēa (also þrāw, þrēat in related formations) "threat, calamity, punishment, evil", from Proto-Germanic *þrawō, a family whose other members cluster around menace: Old High German drawa "threat" and the verb that became German drohen "to threaten", Middle Dutch droeve "sorrow". On this account Middle English throwe "pain, agony" is the Germanic "threat/calamity" word narrowed to the sufferer's experience — the threatened thing becoming the thing suffered. Candidate B: Old Norse þrá "longing, yearning, suffering", a Scandinavian borrowing into northern Middle English, favoured because the word first appears in areas of Norse settlement and because the semantic core "painful longing" fits "throes" remarkably well — the longing of the labouring mother, the long agony of the dying man. The two candidates are not as far apart as they look: Old Norse þrá is itself a reflex of Proto-Germanic *þrawō, the very stem behind Old English þrēa — so both proposals may simply be two semantic routes by which one Germanic word reached Middle English. In the meantime the honest lexicographic statement is: attested from c. 1200 as throwe; exact source debated between the native "threat" word and the Norse "longing" word.

Middle English: throwe. Whatever its source, the Middle English noun throwe/throue (c. 1200) means "pain, agony, distress, pang" — and from the start it leans plural. The records of the 13th–15th centuries show it in religious writing (the torments of hell and of the dying sinner), in romance (the agonies of wounded knights and starving lovers), and in medical and devotional prose (the pangs of childbirth: "childbed throwe"). Even in Middle English the plural is the natural habitat: throwes come in waves. It is also in this period that the word collides with a homophone heritage: Middle English throw — the noun of "time, while, turn" (from Old English þrāg) and the verb "to twist, hurl" (from Old English þrāwan) — overlaps with throwe in form and in bodily imagery: the writhing of pain looks like throwing, and the two families begin to blur in spelling and in popular speech, a blur that would eventually stamp the word's spelling.

The 16th–17th centuries: respelling and the fixed collocations. In the early modern period the word's spelling stabilises as throe (with -oe-), a spelling widely thought to have been shaped by the current spelling of throw; some scholars suspect deliberate differentiation on the part of printers, others simple confusion — the OED treats the -oe- as "probably after throw". What is certain is that by the 17th century the two great collocations were fixed: death-throes and birth-throes (or labour throes). English now had one word for the two universal moments of violent passage, and the word acquired its solemn, dramatic register: the throes are what the body does when it is entirely in the grip of a force beyond itself. Shakespearean and Jacobean drama, with its dying heroes and tragic queens, made "throes" theatrical property; and the biblical language of travail — the woman in labour, the earth in travail — reinforced the birth-pang image for generation after generation of readers.

The 18th–19th centuries: the figurative leap. The decisive extension came in the 19th century: "throes" left the body and entered history and politics. "The throes of revolution" — applied to the upheavals of 1789 and their heirs — "the throes of war", "the throes of a crisis": the body politic went into labour, spasmed and convulsed like a mother or a dying man. The idiom "in the throes of" crystallised in this period: to be in the throes of a process is to be helplessly, painfully in the middle of it, too engaged to escape until it completes. The singular noun throe faded almost to nothing; the plural throes and its collocations carried the whole semantic load, and the word passed from epic fiction into journalism, where it has remained a favourite of crisis reporting.

The 20th–21st centuries: creative and institutional throes. In the last century the word extended further into everyday figurative use: the throes of creative work ("in the throes of writing"), of corporate crisis ("the throes of a takeover"), of personal transition ("the throes of adolescence"), of technological change ("the throes of a digital revolution"). The register remains dramatic, but the pain has weakened into upheaval: modern "throes" usually means a violent middle-phase, not necessarily a physical agony. Epic fiction never lost the physical core — Tolkien's "His last throes splintered it to sparks and gledes" uses the word for the convulsive, terminal struggle of a dying dragon, exactly the meaning it had in 1200.

Homophone caution. Throughout its modern career throes has been shadowed by its homophone throws: "the last throws of the match" (dice throws) versus "the last throes of the match" (final agonies) are a standing spelling trap; the -oe- spelling is the flag that distinguishes the word of pain from the verb of hurling.

Summary of the drift. Throes begins around 1200 as a debated inheritance or borrowing meaning "pain, pang, agony"; pluralises by habit because agony comes in waves; acquires the -oe- spelling and the fixed collocations death-throes and birth-throes in the early modern period; leaps from the body to the body politic in the 19th century as "the throes of revolution"; and today names any violent, inescapable middle-phase of a struggle — while never quite losing the image of the writhing, heaving form in its final extremity. The semantic journey is one of progressive abstraction: from the pang of the body to the pang of history.
