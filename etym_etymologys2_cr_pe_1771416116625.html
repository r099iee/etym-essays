Derivation chain for CRÊPE: 1. (Deeper origin uncertain.) Latin crispus — “curled, frizzled, wrinkled, crimped” (attested from the 2nd century BCE, e.g., in Plautus). The word’s own etymology is debated: no securely attested PIE root is generally agreed, and some scholars suspect a non-Indo-European (Mediterranean or other) loanword source. State as unknown/debated. 2. Latin crispa — feminine of crispus, used as a noun for “crimped, wrinkled cloth; crinkled fabric” (Late Latin; the textile specialization of the adjective). 3. Old French crespe — adjective “curled, wrinkled, frizzed” and noun “crinkled cloth” (10th–12th century), the direct Romance descendant of Latin crispus. 4. Middle French crepe / crespe — the crimped fabric named for its texture (by the 16th century). 5. French crêpe — “crimped fabric” (17th century onward), and by a parallel semantic extension the same word — “thin crinkled pancake” (attested in French from the 19th century). 6. English crêpe / crepe — “crimped, crinkled silk fabric” (borrowed from French in the 18th century, c. 1795; black crêpe for mourning). Final node: CRÊPE. Note on the debate: the chain is secure from Latin onward. Only the pre-Latin stage is uncertain — Latin crispus has no agreed etymology (some authorities mark it as of unknown, possibly non-Indo-European origin). The French pancake sense is a semantic extension of the same word, not a separate borrowing.
