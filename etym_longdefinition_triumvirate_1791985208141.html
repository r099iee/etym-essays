Triumvirate is a noun for a group of three men holding power together, and especially for either of the two Roman alliances that dominated the late Republic. It comes from Latin triumvirātus, the office or college of the triumviri, literally “three men”. In the source context the reference is to the First Triumvirate of 60 BCE, the informal pact between Pompey, Crassus and Caesar.

The word has a precise Roman sense and a loose modern one. Roman triumviri were boards of three magistrates with defined duties; the “First Triumvirate” was not a board at all but a private political alliance, and the “Second Triumvirate” of 43 BCE was a legally constituted commission of three for the settlement of the state. Modern English uses the word for any three-person leadership — a government, a company, a literary grouping — usually with an implication of shared and slightly irregular power.

Roman boards of three

  • Triumviri capitales: three minor magistrates concerned with prisons, executions and night policing, established around 290 BCE.
  • Triumviri monetales: the three mint officials responsible for coinage, whose names and types appear on Republican denarii.
  • Triumviri coloniae deducendae: commissioners for founding colonies and distributing land.
  • Triumviri rei publicae constituendae: the commission of 43 BCE, created by the lex Titia for five years, to restore the state — the legal form of the Second Triumvirate.
  • Triumviri epulones: the college charged with the public banquets of the gods, later enlarged and renamed the septemviri.

The First Triumvirate

In 60 BCE Pompey returned from the East with a settlement the Senate would not ratify and veterans to settle; Crassus wanted relief for the tax-farmers of Asia; Caesar wanted the consulate. The three pooled their influence, and Caesar was elected consul for 59 BCE. He carried Pompey's settlement, gave Crassus his concession, and took the Gallic command for five years. The alliance was renewed at Luca in 56 BCE, when Pompey and Crassus became consuls again and Caesar's command was extended. It collapsed with Julia's death in 54 BCE and Crassus' death at Carrhae in 53 BCE, and the breach between Pompey and Caesar led to civil war in 49 BCE.

The Second Triumvirate

After Caesar's assassination in 44 BCE, Antony, Octavian and Lepidus met near Bononia and had themselves appointed triumviri rei publicae constituendae by the lex Titia of 27 November 43 BCE, for five years. The commission was legal, unlike the earlier alliance, and it was backed by proscriptions: Cicero was killed on 7 December 43 BCE. The three defeated Brutus and Cassius at Philippi in 42 BCE, divided the empire, renewed their compact in 40 BCE and their powers in 37 BCE. Lepidus was sidelined in 36 BCE, and after Actium in 31 BCE Octavian ruled alone; the title survived only in the imperial memory.

The modern general sense

  • A three-person executive: a government or committee of three, especially one that concentrates power.
  • A corporate trio: the chief executive, the finance director and the chairman, or any three figures who effectively run an institution.
  • A cultural grouping: “the triumvirate of Romantic poets”, a loose critical label for three dominant figures.
  • A set of three things: journalistic use, as in “a triumvirate of crises”, where the three items are treated as a governing combination.

Grammar and derivatives

The noun is countable and normally takes the definite article: the triumvirate, a triumvirate of. The members are triumvirs (singular triumvir), and the adjective is triumviral. The Latin plural is triumviri, used in classical scholarship. The related forms duumvirate (two men) and quadrumvirate (four) exist but are rare; troika is the most common modern synonym for a ruling three.

Register and connotation

The word is formal and slightly bookish. It implies concentrated power, back-room agreement and a certain irregularity — a triumvirate is usually something that has taken over, or that bypasses the official constitution. Applied to a company or a cultural movement it is often half-ironic, lending mock-Roman grandeur to a management team or a group of poets. Typical collocations: “form a triumvirate”, “the ruling triumvirate”, “a triumvirate of”, “the first/second triumvirate”.

Why it matters in this reading

The passage makes the alliance the hinge of the late Republic: Caesar's election as Pontifex Maximus, Pompey's prestige, and Crassus' wealth combined to form “the first triumvirate”, which the author calls more important historically than Cicero's consulship. The word is doing a lot of work here, because it names as a formal arrangement what was in fact an informal pact. That is exactly the kind of retrospective labelling historians use to make sense of a messy political reality.
