- The pagan midwinter festival — the old Germanic Yule: a heathen twelve-day feast at the winter solstice, with sacrifice, ale and the turning of the year.
- Absorbed by Christianity — from the 10th century (Hákon the Good’s Norway) Yule is fused with Christmas, and “Yule-tide” comes to mean simply the Christmas season.
- Suppressed and provincialised — in Reformation England the Puritan attack on Christmas (the bans of 1647–60) pushes “Yule” into dialect, Scots and the countryside.
- The Victorian revival — 19th-century Christmas sentimentalism resurrects the word: Yule log, Yule-tide cheer, “Yuletide” on the new Christmas cards; fantasy literature (Tolkien) gives it archaic warmth.
- Today — “Yuletide” is the standard festive-season word; the hyphenated “Yule-tide” is the older, deliberately antique form; neopagans reclaim “Yule” for the solstice itself.
