- 9th–11th c. (Old English) - *Befeallan* meant to fall into, drop down, or happen by chance/providence; deeply tied to fate (*wyrd*) and divine judgment.
- 12th–14th c. (Middle English) - Evolved into *befallen* / *bifallen*, expanding as a ubiquitous narrative marker in chivalric romances and moral chronicles to introduce momentous happenings and sudden tragedies.
- 15th–17th c. (Early Modern English) - Syntactically standardized both intransitively ('if it so befall') and transitively ('the doom befalling him'), maintaining a grave, philosophical tone in Elizabethan tragedy and King James prose.
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Era) - Shifted predominantly toward negative or calamitous events (misfortunes, disasters, illnesses befalling someone), cementing its literary gravity.
- 20th–21st c. (Modern English) - Preserved primarily in formal, poetic, legal-providential, and high-literary registers to evoke destiny, unavoidable adversity, or solemn reflection.
