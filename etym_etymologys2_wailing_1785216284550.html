PIE *wai- "ah! alas" — an imitative interjection of woe; onomatopoeic in character, so it survives without regular sound-change across the whole family → Proto-Germanic *wai "woe" (cf. Old English wā, Gothic wai) → Old Norse vei "woe" → denominative verb væla "to lament, utter woe" → Middle English waylen "to cry aloud in sorrow, lament" (borrowed from Old Norse, c. 1300) → English wail (verb) "to cry aloud in grief; make a mournful piercing sound" → WAILING (present participle) "crying piercingly; lamenting" Note on debated/nuanced points: The interjection *wai- is shared far beyond Germanic — Latin vae, Welsh gwae, Old Irish fāe — confirming its antiquity as a pan-Indo-European exclamation rather than a derived noun-root; as an interjection it fell outside the regular consonant-shifts (Grimm's Law), which is why it looks so similar everywhere. The verb form is uniquely Norse-built: other branches kept the word as an exclamation or noun (Latin vae; Old English wā) and did not generally form a "to wail" verb from it, so English owes its verb specifically to the Old Norse denominative væla. The spelling with reflects the Middle English smoothing of the borrowed diphthong.
