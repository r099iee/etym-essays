COMPOSURE descends from the Latin compound verb compōnere, "to put together, arrange," which itself joins two elements of independent Indo-European origin: the prefix com-, from PIE *ḱóm, and the verb pōnere, from the PIE root *seh₁-. The two strands are traced in isolation below, then joined at the Latin compound and carried into English. CHAIN 1 — the com- element (the "together" prefix): 1) PIE *ḱóm "beside, along with, together" (preposition and prefix, reconstructed; the source of Latin cum/com- and of the Celtic com-/co- prefix of Old Irish, but with no secure reflex in Indo-Iranian — the common Vedic "with" words come from different roots) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Italic *kom "with, together" (reconstructed; the Italic preposition and prefix). 3) Latin cum "with" (preposition); com-, con- (prefix, with regular assimilation: col- before l, cor- before r, co- before vowels and h) — attested from the earliest Latin records. 4) Latin compōnere "to put together, arrange" (com- "together" + pōnere "to put") — attested from the classical period; the compound that yields composure. 5) Old French composer "to put together, arrange, compose" (from Latin compōnere, with the -pon- stem remade) — c. 9th c. onward. 6) Middle English composer / compose "to put together, arrange, settle" (borrowed from Old French, 1530s). 7) English composure "a composing; arrangement; calm self-possession" — COMPOSURE (formed in English on the verb compose + the suffix -ure, 1590s). CHAIN 2 — the pōnere base (the "put" element): 1) PIE *seh₁- "to throw, cast, let go" (reconstructed; also the root of English sow and seed, the seeds being "cast" into the earth, and of Greek hīēmi "to send, let go") — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Italic *sinō "to let go, permit, leave" (nasal-infix present, from *si-né-h₁-) — reconstructed. 3) Latin sinō, sinere "to allow, permit, leave" (attested; the base of the compound). 4) Latin *posinō "to put down, place" (po- "down, away, off" + sinō "to let") — the traditional analysis of pōnō, standard in textbooks but debated (see note). 5) Latin pōnō, pōnere "to put, place, set down" (attested from the classical period). 6) Latin compōnere "to put together, arrange, set in order" (com- "together" + pōnere); its participle compositus "arranged, settled" — classical Latin. 7) Old French composer "to arrange, compose" — c. 9th c. onward. 8) Middle English compose "to put together, arrange, settle" (1530s). 9) English composure "the act of composing; arrangement; the state of being settled and calm" — COMPOSURE (1590s). NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The derivation of Latin pōnō from *posinō (po- + sinō) is the standard textbook account, but it is not without caution: de Vaan, in his Etymological Dictionary of Latin, treats the analysis with reserve, noting that pōnō could in principle reflect a different reshaping. The higher, historical part of the chain — Latin compōnere, Old French composer, English compose, English composure — is fully secure, and the modern sense of composure ("calm self-possession") is an English development from the verb's sense "to settle, order." (2) The prefix chain from PIE *ḱóm to Latin com- is secure and uncontroversial; the only debated point in that line is whether any Indo-Iranian reflex of *ḱóm survives — modern authorities generally hold that the common Vedic "with" words derive from other roots, so no Indic node is drawn.
