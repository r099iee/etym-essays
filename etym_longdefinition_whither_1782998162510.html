WHITHER is an archaic interrogative adverb meaning 'to what place; where (to)'. In this card it appears in Book First of William Wordsworth's The Prelude (1805), where the poet, newly released from the 'vast city', asks: 'Long months of ease and undisturbed delight / Are mine in prospect; whither shall I turn, / By road or pathway, or through trackless field, / Up hill or down...'. The question is deliberately open-ended and celebratory: any direction is possible, and the whole landscape lies before him, marked or unmarked, rising or falling. Because whither is archaic and dignified, it lifts the line out of ordinary enquiry into the register of destiny and pilgrimage. The word belongs to the old three-way system of English place-adverbs built on the who/what stem: the triplets here, there, where (location); hence, thence, whence (source); and the goal-triad whither, thither, and the near-speaker form rarely used today. Modern English has collapsed almost the whole system: we now say 'where are you going?' where Elizabethan English would have asked 'whither go you?', and 'from where' for whence. Whither survives as a conscious archaism in poetry, formal prose, and the King James Bible ('Whither thou goest, I will go' — Ruth 1:16).
  • Primary sense — destination: whither asks for the goal of motion: 'Whither shall I turn?' = to what place shall I turn? It is the dynamic counterpart of static where.
  • Extended sense — figurative goal: even in Old English the word was used of mental and moral direction — 'whither the argument tends', 'whither his thoughts wandered' — the destination being an outcome, intention, or line of thought rather than a physical place.
  • Rhetorical / exclamatory sense: whither is often used to lament a course of events, as in the editorial cliché 'whither Britain?' or 'whither technology?', where the question implies anxiety about where things are heading.
  • Literary and biblical register: the word carries a solemn, elevated tone from its long use in the Authorised Version and in Shakespeare; Wordsworth exploits exactly this dignity in The Prelude.
  • Whithersoever (archaic compound): 'to whatever place', a common intensified form in older English ('whithersoever I go').
Grammar and usage notes:
  • Whither is always an interrogative or relative adverb of place; it is never a noun or a preposition.
  • It takes motion verbs (go, turn, fly, wander), never state verbs; 'where are you' is static, 'whither goest thou' is dynamic.
  • Its spelling trap is wither (to shrivel or fade), a verb with no connection to place; the two differ only in the silent h after w.
  • In modern prose whither is obsolete except in deliberate archaism; editors who want a natural modern equivalent use 'where (to)' or 'to what place'.
Semantically, whither concentrates the Romantic creed of the wanderer: nature, not the city grid, will direct the steps. Wordsworth's use is hopeful, expansive, and meditative — the very breath of the poem's great opening sigh of release — and every later poetic use (Tennyson's 'Whither, O whither?' and the many Victorian echoes) inherits that weight.
