- Antiquity (c. 6th c. BCE – 1st c. CE) - Began in Greece as functional lapidary inscriptions (*epigrammata*) carved onto tombs, monuments, and votive offerings, before evolving in Hellenistic collections into poetic miniatures and in imperial Rome (Martial) into biting, satirical couplets with a stinging punchline.
- 16th to Early 17th Century - Re-entered English through Renaissance humanism (Thomas More, Ben Jonson), establishing itself as a prestigious classical poetic genre in rhymed couplets designed to lampoon courtly vices and personal rivals.
- 18th Century (The Augustan Age) - Mastered by Alexander Pope and Jonathan Swift as the quintessential neoclassical weapon of political satire and philosophical wit, tightly engineered within the rhymed heroic couplet.
- 19th Century - Transitioned triumphantly from verse to prose dialogue, reaching an immortal peak in the society plays and essays of Oscar Wilde, where it became synonymous with sparkling paradoxical inversions of conventional morality.
- 20th Century to Present - Celebrated as the gold standard of literary wit, satirical elegance, and intellectual one-liners in drama, journalism, and high-style fiction (notably Marcel Proust and Dorothy Parker).
