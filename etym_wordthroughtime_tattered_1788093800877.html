- 14th c. - tater (from Old Norse tǫturr 'rag'): a plain noun for a torn scrap; 'taters' = ragged clothing in the vocabulary of medieval poverty.
- Late 15th–16th c. - the adjective tattered is born from the new verb 'to tatter': beggars, broken soldiers, tatter'd banners; perception: the badge of poverty and ordeal.
- 17th–18th c. - tatterdemalion ('a ragged fellow') appears; the figurative drift begins — fortunes and reputations 'in tatters'; the tattered Continental army of the American Revolution enters national memory.
- 19th c. - Romantic and Gothic prose furnish tattered cloaks, manuscripts and flags; 'tattered soldiers' becomes a staple of war writing; perception: ruin with pathos and endurance.
- 20th–21st c. - figurative use dominates (tattered hopes, economy, reputation), while the literal sense stays vivid for flags, books and clothes; the word now always suggests having survived too much.
