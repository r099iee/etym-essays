• written: Similar because both are past participles of write with the meaning "set down in writing"; differs because written is the ordinary modern form, neutral in tone, whereas writ is deliberately archaic, poetic, or legal, adding elevation and gravity.
  • inscribed: Similar because both mean letters set down on a surface; differs because inscribed stresses cutting or engraving into a hard material such as stone or metal, with ceremony and permanence, whereas writ means simply "written" and draws its weight from archaism rather than from the surface.
  • engraved: Similar because both suggest words durably fixed in a medium; differs because engraved names the physical incising of a hard material by a graver's tool, whereas writ foregrounds the literary and biblical solemnity of being set down, not the technique of carving.
  • etched: Similar because both imply marks permanently fixed on a surface; differs because etched suggests the corrosive or needle-cut line of engraving into metal or glass, whereas writ is the archaic word for any written-down matter and is bound up with the idioms writ large and writ in stone.
  • recorded: Similar because both describe something set down for keeping; differs because recorded shifts the focus to preservation and storage (records, archives, audio), whereas writ foregrounds the act and manner of writing itself and its gravity.
  • transcribed: Similar because both mean copied or written out; differs because transcribed is a technical, mechanical word for copying one text into another form, whereas writ is archaic and expressive, not technical.
  • penned: Similar because both are literary words for the act of writing; differs because penned is a figurative verb ("to pen a letter") about authorship, whereas writ is an archaic participle about the result — the state of having been written.
  • documented: Similar because both imply something set down as a record; differs because documented is modern, bureaucratic, and about evidence and records, whereas writ is poetic, archaic, and about the solemn fact of inscription.
  • chiselled: Similar because both suggest marks made durably; differs because chiselled is about carving with a chisel into stone or wood, whereas writ is about writing in the general sense and survives chiefly in fixed idioms.
