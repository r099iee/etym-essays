• pulled: Similar because both describe moving something toward oneself; differs because pull is general and unemphatic, while haul insists on great effort and weight.
  • dragged: Similar because both describe moving something heavy against resistance; differs because dragging implies resistance and unwilling weight (a person or object on the ground), while hauling can move a load that comes more freely.
  • towed: Similar because both describe pulling something along; differs because towing specifically means pulling a vehicle or vessel behind another, often by rope, not with the body.
  • tugged: Similar because both describe strong pulling; differs because tugging suggests repeated, short, insistent jerks, while hauling is one sustained pull.
  • yanked: Similar because both are forceful pulling; differs because yanking is sharp, sudden, and informal, while hauling is steady, sustained effort.
  • heaved: Similar because both describe great physical effort; differs because heaving stresses lifting from below rather than pulling backward or along.
  • lugged: Similar because both describe carrying or dragging something heavy; differs because lugging suggests carrying a clumsy, awkward load rather than pulling it along a line.
  • wrested: Similar because both describe pulling with great force; differs because wresting implies twisting something away against resistance, while hauling is straight-line pulling.
  • wrenched: Similar because both describe violent pulling; differs because wrenching adds twisting violence and the danger of damage, while hauling is brute steady force.
  • transported: Similar because both mean to move a load; differs because transporting is neutral, efficient logistics, while hauling keeps the muscular effort in view.
  • carried: Similar because both move something from one place to another; differs because carrying means bearing something on one's person, while hauling means dragging or towing it.
  • carted: Similar because both describe moving heavy loads; differs because carting implies using a cart or vehicle and is informal, while hauling can be by hand.
  • schlepped: Similar because both describe laborious carrying or dragging; differs because schlepping is informal, humorous, and Yiddish-flavored, without hauling's workmanlike dignity.
  • drew: Similar because both describe pulling; differs because drawing is a gentle, controlled pull, the opposite of hauling's heavy strain.
  • bridle: Similar because it is the head-gear by which a horse is controlled, including the bit and the straps that connect to the reins; differs because the bridle is the whole controlling apparatus on the horse's head, while the reins are the two straps held in the rider's hands.
  • bit: Similar because the reins act upon it, transmitting pressure to the horse's mouth; differs because the bit is the metal mouthpiece inside the horse's mouth, not the straps in the hand.
  • halter: Similar because it is a head-strap used to lead or restrain a horse; differs because a halter has no bit and is used for leading and tying on the ground, not for riding control.
  • headstall: Similar because it is the part of the bridle around the horse's head; differs because it refers to the frame holding the bit, not the hand-held straps.
  • harness: Similar because it is a set of straps used to control or attach a draught animal; differs because a harness is the full working rig connecting a horse to a cart or plow.
  • leash: Similar because it is a strap used to hold and control an animal; differs because a leash restrains a dog on the ground and carries no idea of riding or steering.
  • lead rope / lead line: Similar because it is a strap attached to a halter for leading a horse; differs because it is for ground work, not mounted control.
  • tether: Similar because it is a rope that confines an animal to a fixed spot; differs because a tether anchors rather than steers, its purpose restriction, not direction.
  • curb: Similar because it is a means of restraint, and as a verb means 'to check or hold back'; differs because a curb is a general check (and in riding a chain below the chin), lacking the paired hand-held steering function.
  • lines (chiefly U.S.): Similar because it is the ordinary American word for the pair of driving reins; differs because 'lines' belongs to driving and carriage use rather than riding.
  • traces: Similar because they are straps of a harness; differs because traces connect the collar to the vehicle and pull the load, rather than guiding the animal.
  • martingale: Similar because it is a controlling strap of riding tackle; differs because it acts on the horse's head, not in the rider's hands.
  • check (noun): Similar because 'the reins' are a check, a means of stopping, and 'to check a horse' is to hold it back on the reins; differs because 'check' is a general verb of stopping with no leather-and-bit machinery behind it.
  • control / command (figurative): Similar because 'the reins' are the standard figure for control; differs because they are abstract nouns of authority, not physical straps.
