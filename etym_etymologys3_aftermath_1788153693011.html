1. The word math and the mowing root. The second element of aftermath is the Old English noun mǣþ / mæþ, "a mowing; the crop of hay or grass", a word formed from the verb māwan, "to mow". Behind the English pair stands the Indo-European root reconstructed as *h₂met-, "to mow, reap, cut down", attested with certainty in Greek (ἄμητος, "harvest"), in the Italic and Germanic verbal families (Latin metere, "to harvest, reap", Old English māwan), and elsewhere; it is one of the securely established agricultural roots of the Indo-European lexicon. Old English mǣþ (with the feminine ē-grade of the same base) meant both the act of mowing and its produce, and it bred a small family: ǣfemǣþ "evening mowing", and later the explicit compound that interests us.

2. The compound appears (16th century). The transparent compound after + math is first recorded in the sixteenth century (OED places the first attestations in the 1520s–1530s) with the plain rural meaning "a second mowing; the grass that grows after the first crop has been cut". The word's structure is so clear that no one invents it; it merely names what any mower knows — the field grows again. In the early records it competes with aftergrass and the dialect eamath, but aftermath wins in bookish and southern usage, partly because its anatomy ("what comes after the mowing") is legible to anyone.

3. The agricultural centuries (17th–18th). Throughout the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word lives quietly in husbandry manuals, estate accounts, and the rural poetry of the period. It denotes the late crop, valuable as autumn pasture, sometimes cut again for hay, sometimes fed off by beasts; the "aftermath" is part of the annual rhythm of the meadow, and pastoral poets like Thomson (in The Seasons) describe the field's second growth without any figurative strain. In this period the word is strictly concrete: a thing, not a result.

4. The figurative turn (19th century). The nineteenth century releases the metaphor. Once "aftermath" means "the second growth", nothing prevents its extension to any later growth or later phase: an aftermath of the season, of a reign, of an age. Victorian essayists and critics, cultivating an agricultural imagery of growth and maturity, employ it for the late, refined flowerings of cultures — and precisely here the word enters the sentence the student now studies, Pater's "an aftermath, a wonderful later growth" for the French phase of the Renaissance. The shift is characteristic of Victorian usage: figurative, elegant, and still consciously tethered to the meadow.

5. Catastrophe gives the word its modern face (20th century). The First World War completes the transfer. From 1914 onward "the aftermath of war" floods the newspapers, and the word's centre of gravity moves from the harvest to the ruins: aftermaths are of wars, famines, crashes, disasters. What had been a second crop becomes, in effect, the wreckage or the long tail of an event. "In the aftermath of" hardens into a standard locution of reportage and history-writing, used almost always of trouble. The etymon is still audible to the careful — things grow after the cutting — but the connotation has darkened from renewal to consequence.

6. Structure and fortune of the parts. The simplex math did not survive in standard English: by the nineteenth century it lived only in dialects and in compounds, and today most speakers know it solely as half of aftermath — a fossil embedded in a modern word, like the ham in hamburger's older analysis. The dialect survivor eamath (from Old English ǣfemǣþ, "after-mowing") preserves the family in rural speech, and mow itself remains the everyday verb.

7. The word now. In present-day English aftermath is a settled abstract noun, mildly formal, journalistic, and almost invariably attached to negative events; the rural sense survives in agriculture and in historical writing about husbandry. No other English word packs quite this double memory — of grass and of catastrophe — and Pater's sentence, standing at the hinge, remains the classic illustration of the word's fruitful ambiguity. The history of aftermath is thus a miniature of semantic change: a farming term that lent its image to culture, and a culture-word that was then darkened by war.
