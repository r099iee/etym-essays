Stowaways is a young word with two very old parents, and its history is really the history of the verb to stow meeting the adverb away in the age of steam. Neither component is ancient in English in the form we now use, but both reach back through Germanic into Proto-Indo-European. The stow-side: from standing to storing The verb stow descends from the Old English verb stōwian, "to place, keep, put away", which was itself formed from the Old English noun stōw, "a place". That noun is attested abundantly in the earliest English records and survives fossilized in placenames such as Chepstow, Felixstowe, and Padstow — every -stow on the map is literally "a place". Behind Old English stōw lies a Proto-Germanic *stōwō "a place, a spot", and behind that the great Proto-Indo-European root *steh₂-, "to stand, to set in place", the same root that gave Latin stāre, Greek histánai, and English stand. The semantic thread runs: "that which stands" → "a standing-place" → "a place" → "to put something in its place" → "to pack and store away". This is a quiet, workaday word, and it stayed workaday: stowen in Middle English meant simply to put things in place, and by early modern English to stow was already the sailor's verb for securing cargo and gear below deck. The away-side: a prepositional phrase hardened into an adverb The second element, away, is one of the oldest and most idiomatic of English adverbs. It began as the Old English prepositional phrase on weg, "on the way, hence", made of the preposition on and the noun weg, "a way, road", which descends from Proto-Germanic *wegaz and ultimately from the Proto-Indo-European root *weǵʰ-, "to carry, convey, go". The phrase on weg contracted to aweg and then to away; the old phrase is still visible in formations such as runaway, castaway, and stowaway itself, where -away keeps its ancient sense of "from this place, hence, off". The joining: from cargo to people The collocation to stow away is attested from the sixteenth century in its literal sense, "to stow goods away, to put cargo out of sight in the hold". Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the phrase remained unremarkable, a plain record of good seamanship. The decisive change came in the middle decades of the nineteenth century. Three forces met: the rapid expansion of steam-powered merchant shipping, which made long voyages faster and cheaper; the mass emigration of the era, which filled liners with poor and desperate passengers; and the tightening of port and customs controls, which made legitimate free passage harder to obtain. The result was a new social type — the man or woman who crept aboard unseen, hid among the stores and cargo, and presented themselves, hungry and half-frozen, only when the ship was beyond recall. English answered this new reality by turning the verb phrase into a noun: stowaway is first recorded in the 1850s, and the plural stowaways became the standard term in maritime law, shipping regulations, and the penny press within a decade. Socio-cultural afterlife By the turn of the twentieth century the stowaway was already a stock figure of adventure fiction — at once a criminal, a victim, and a romantic rebel — and the word travelled with that image into film and pulp literature. In the twentieth century the noun's sense widened beyond ships: the stowaway of the aeroplane (notoriously, in the wheel bays of jets, an almost always fatal gamble) and of the freight train joined the maritime original. Since the 1980s, with the rise of containerization and the tightening of European and American border regimes, the stowaway has become a figure in humanitarian and asylum discourse, the object of carrier-liability fines and of human-rights litigation; the neutral word now does heavy legal work while still flickering with its old adventurer's glamour. Literary and linguistic notes
  • The noun's formation mirrors older and parallel compounds such as castaway and runaway, all built from verb phrase + away, and all originally nautical.
  • The word is now lexicalized as a single noun and takes the ordinary plural in -s.
  • The spelling has been stable from the first records; the hyphenated form stow-away appears in nineteenth-century sources but did not persist.
  • The transferred verb sense "to conceal oneself for free passage" appears in the same decades as the noun and fixed the word's modern double identity as both a legal term and a piece of nautical folklore.
