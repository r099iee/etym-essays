PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*pau-
"few, little, small; child, young animal"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th–4th c. BCE)
παῖς (paîs, stem paid-)
"child, boy, youth; servant"
↓
Hellenistic / Byzantine Greek (c. 4th–8th c. CE)
παιδίον (paidíon)
"little boy, child, servant boy"
Note: An alternative etymological hypothesis derives paggio from Latin pagēnsis ("peasant, rustic") from pāgus ("country district"). However, leading Romance linguists (including Meyer-Lübke and modern OED philologists) favor the derivation from Byzantine Greek paidíon via medieval Italian maritime trade in the eastern Mediterranean.
↓

↓

↓
Middle English (c. 1300–1350)
page

--- ROOT WORD 2: PAGE (Leaf of a Book / Document) ---

PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*peh₂ǵ- / *pag-
"to fasten, fix, attach, drive in"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000–500 BCE)
*pangō
"to fix, fasten, plant"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE)
pangere
"to fix, fasten, drive in, plant; compose, write"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE)
pāgina
"trellis of vines; strip of papyrus fastened together; leaf of a book, column of writing"
Note: Formed from the verbal root of pangere with the noun suffix -ina, originally referring to a framework of grapevines fastened together, metaphorically transferred to strips of papyrus fastened into writing leaves.
↓
Old French (c. 12th–13th c.)
page
"page, leaf of a manuscript or book"
↓
Middle English (c. 1350–1400)
page
"page of a manuscript, side of a leaf of paper"
↓
Modern English (present)
PAGE (Leaf / Document)
