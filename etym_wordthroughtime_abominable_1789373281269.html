- 14th Century - Enters Middle English via Old French *abominable* from Late Latin *abōminābilis*; firmly anchored in biblical scripture, theology, and ecclesiastical law to denote idolatry, heresy, and unnatural sins that invite divine wrath.
- 15th to 16th Century - Spurious Renaissance folk etymology takes root across European humanism; scholars falsely derive the word from Latin *ab homine* ("unnatural to man / inhuman"), resulting in widespread spellings as *abhominable*, an orthographic vanity satirized by William Shakespeare in *Love's Labour's Lost*.
- 17th to 18th Century - Classical Latin etymology (*ab-omen*) is definitively restored; the spelling returns to *abominable*; the word broadens to describe political tyranny, heinous social offenses, and literary barbarisms in Enlightenment essays.
- 19th Century - Semantic bleaching sets in: alongside solemn moral condemnation, the word is increasingly used in Victorian colloquial speech for wretched weather, terrible food, or atrocious typos; entering English domestic idioms.
- 1921 - Enters global pop culture and cryptozoological lore when journalist Henry Newman mistranslates the Tibetan *Metoh-Kangmi* as the "Abominable Snowman" during the British Mount Everest Expedition.
- 1931 to Present - Samuel Beckett famously applies the term to the mangled 1919–1927 NRF printing of Marcel Proust's novel ("the abominable edition"); in the modern era, it comfortably spans high ethical gravity (war crimes) and expressive critical hyperbole.
