Asperity in modern English has two closely linked hard edges: (1) harshness or sharpness of manner, temper, or speech — a cutting, irritable, ungracious tone; and (2) roughness of surface, sound, climate, or conditions — something physically or environmentally severe and uneven. When Wodehouse writes that words "jarred the Wooster pride, inducing asperity," he means the first sense: Bertie answers sharply, with wounded, curt irritation.

The manner sense dominates contemporary use. To speak with asperity is to speak with a bite: short sentences, cold politeness, sarcasm, or open rebuke. A review written with asperity is not merely critical but caustic; a reply full of asperity stings. It implies a loss of smoothness — social smoothness — just as sandpaper loses smoothness. The word carries a literary, slightly formal register; it is stronger and colder than irritation and less explosive than anger.

The physical sense survives in technical and literary contexts: the asperity of a rock face, of desert winters, of a harsh voice timbre. Engineers even use asperities in the plural to mean microscopic peaks on a nominally smooth surface that govern friction — a precise, modern revival of the Latin root sense of roughness.

  • Harshness of temper or tone (primary modern sense): severity, crabbedness, acrimony in speech or behaviour. Bridge: metaphorical transfer from a rough surface that scratches the skin to rough words that scratch the feelings.
  • Roughness of surface, sound, or texture: unevenness, ruggedness, hoarseness. Bridge: direct continuation of Latin asperitas "unevenness of ground, roughness."
  • Severity of conditions or hardship: rigour of climate, poverty, discipline, or fate — "the asperities of war." Bridge: extension from physical roughness to experiential harshness endured by the body or mind.
  • Technical plural — asperities in tribology/geology: minute protuberances on surfaces. Bridge: 20th-century scientific narrowing of the physical sense to a countable, measurable feature.

Stylistically asperity is economical: one noun covers both the cause (a rough thing) and the effect (a rough response). Its very sound — stressed -per-, hissing -s- — enacts the quality it names. Writers choose it when harshness feels too blunt and acrimony too moralised; asperity is tactile, immediate, and faintly aristocratic in its disdain.

Common collocations confirm this: with some asperity, with growing asperity, reply with asperity, note of asperity. It rarely stands alone as praise and almost never describes sustained cruelty; it captures the momentary sharpening of an otherwise composed voice — exactly Bertie's flash of offended dignity before Jeeves smooths matters over. For learners, the test is texture: if harsh describes the blow, asperity describes the sandpaper grain of the voice delivering it.
