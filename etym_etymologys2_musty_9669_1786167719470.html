[ROOT CORE (PIE): *mewk- (slimy, slippery, moldy) — via the mold/decay branch, and Latin *mustum via the new-wine/dampness branch — see note] — branch of MUSTY
├── Latin: mūcēre (to be moldy, musty) ──> mūcidus (moldy, slimy, musty) ──> English: mucus, mucilaginous
│ └── Old French: moisir (to become moldy) ──> past participle moisi (moldy)
│ └── English (15th–16th c.): musty — reshaped from moisty (damp) under the sound-influence of moisi (moldy) ◄ TARGET
├── Parallel dampness line: Latin mustum (new wine) ──> musteus (fresh, young, moist) ──> Old French moiste (damp) ──> English moist (14th c.) ──> moisty (damp) ──> musty
└── Cognates of the *mewk- mold line: Latin mūcus (snot, mucus), mūcor (mustiness, mold); Greek mýxa (slime, mucus).
Note: The standard etymology (OED, Etymonline) is that English musty represents moisty ("damp") altered by the influence of Old French moisi ("moldy"). The folk-etymological link to "mouse" (PIE *mūs-) is rejected by modern scholarship. The *mewk- "moldy/slimy" family contributed indirectly through the French form moisi that reshaped the English word, while the actual English base is the mustum/moist line. Both lines are presented above; the direct linear ancestor of the modern English word is moist → moisty → musty.
