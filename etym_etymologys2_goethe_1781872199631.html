PIE *ǵʰeu̯- (also written *gheu-) 'to pour (a libation); to call upon, invoke' (reconstructed) → PIE verbal adjective *ǵʰu-tó- 'that which is invoked (or libated)' → Proto-Germanic *gudą 'god, deity' (pre-5th c. CE) → Old High German got 'God' (attested from the 8th c.) → Old High German gōti(o) / gota 'godparent, baptismal sponsor (godmother/godfather)' (attested in medieval OHG) → Middle High German gōte / göt(e) 'godparent, baptismal sponsor' (12th–14th c.) → Early Modern German surname Göthe / Goethe (fixed as a hereditary surname from the 16th c.; ö later written 'oe') → Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832), the poet → cultural derivatives Goethean, and the Goethe-Institut → GOETHE. Note on the debate: The god-word family is derived by one school from *ǵʰeu̯- 'to pour' (the libation theory, 'the one to whom libations are poured') and by another from the sense 'to invoke' ('the one invoked'); both analyses are represented in the handbooks. The surname's link to the 'godparent' byname (Göte/gote) is the mainstream onomastic account, but an alternative derives the family name from a given name built on Gott-/Got- (e.g., a short form of Gottfried or Gotthard). The popular association of Goethe with the Goths is rejected by scholarship. Spelling note: Göthe and Goethe are the same name, since 'oe' is an orthographic substitute for 'ö'.
