In the sentence at hand — 'the Mountain's feet black with a hurrying MULTITUDE' — the word denotes a vast crowd of people (the gathered armies) moving as one mass. A multitude is a very great number of persons or things considered collectively; it is distinguished from a mere 'crowd' by its scale, and from 'throng' by a slight emphasis on number rather than density.

1. Core sense — a great number; a crowd

  • A large gathering of people: 'a multitude assembled in the square'; 'the needs of the multitude' (the common people, the masses).
  • Used collectively of things too: 'a multitude of sins', 'a multitude of options' — a great number of items of any kind.
  • In the plural, 'multitudes': vast numbers — 'multitudes came to the shore'.

2. The masses / the common people

  • Since the 16th century, 'the multitude' (singular, with the) has meant the common people, the general public, often with a slightly condescending or dismissive colouring: 'the unthinking multitude', 'to follow the multitude'. This sense overlaps with 'the masses' and 'the hoi polloi'.
  • The semantic bridge: the crowd as an undifferentiated mass — from 'a great number of people' to 'the great number of ordinary people' as a social category. The word thus carries a latent tension: admiring scale on one side, distrust of mob mentality on the other.

3. Biblical and liturgical usage

  • The word is prominent in Bible translation (Tyndale, Authorised Version): 'multitudes' of the sick, the feeding of the five thousand, 'the multitude' as the crowd around Jesus. Hence a permanent scriptural register: solemn, vast, slightly archaic.
  • 'A multitude of sins' (Proverbs 10:12 / James 5:20, 'charity shall cover the multitude of sins') has become a fixed proverbial phrase, still current: 'his generosity covered a multitude of sins'.

4. Quantitative, scientific and philosophical uses

  • In mathematics and statistics, 'multitude' is a plain synonym of 'large number' (not a technical term; the technical words are 'set' and 'cardinality').
  • In biology, 'the multitude' is occasionally used for a population; the more productive adjective is 'multitudinous' (very numerous).
  • Philosophically, 'the many' (Greek oi polloi) translates as 'the multitude'; Spinoza's multitudo is a technical political concept revived by contemporary thinkers (Negri and Hardt's 'the multitude' as a global networked class).

5. Register, connotation and modern use

  • Modern everyday speech prefers 'crowd', 'throng', 'host', 'army', 'swarm', 'mass'; 'multitude' is markedly formal or literary, but alive in journalism and rhetoric ('a multitude of problems', 'a multitude of reasons').
  • Connotative spectrum: neutral-huge (a multitude of stars) → solemn (biblical) → mildly pejorative (the unthinking multitude) → technical-political (the multitude in radical philosophy).

Etymology at a glance: Latin multitudo 'great number, crowd, public' from multus 'much, many' + -tūdō (abstract-noun suffix, as in altitude, solitude); English took the word from Latin or via Old French multitude (12th century); first attested in English in the 14th century (Chaucer).
