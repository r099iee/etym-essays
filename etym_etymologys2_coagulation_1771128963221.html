PIE *haeg- 'to drive, lead, move' (c. 4000-3000 BCE) -> Proto-Italic *ago 'to drive' -> Latin agere 'to drive (cattle), to do, act, manage, perform' (attested from the earliest Latin, 3rd c. BCE) -> Latin cogere 'to drive together, force together, compel, collect' (com- 'together' + agere; classical, 1st c. BCE) -> Latin coagulum 'rennet; anything that curdles or coagulates' (a derivative of cogere formed in -ulum; attested 1st c. BCE/CE, e.g. Varro and Pliny) -> Latin coagulare 'to cause to curdle, to coagulate, to cause to mass together' (verb from coagulum; technical Latin) -> Latin coagulatio 'a curdling, a coagulation, a causing to curdle' (abstract noun, attested in Late Latin and medieval Latin medical texts) -> Middle French coagulation 'curdling, the action of making a liquid solidify' (attested 14th c.) -> English COAGULATION 'the process by which a liquid changes to a solid or semi-solid state' (attested c. 14th-15th c., in medical and alchemical writing). Note on the chain: the derivation is exceptionally well-attested and uncontroversial; the only minor nuance is that Latin coagulum was used by Roman writers both for rennet itself and, by extension, for any curdling agent, and that English 'curdle' (a native Germanic verb, ultimately related to 'crud') and 'coagulate' (the Latinate borrowing) have remained partial synonyms ever since.
