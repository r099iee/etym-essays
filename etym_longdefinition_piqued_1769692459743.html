piqued: (1) aroused, stimulated, or excited, most often said of interest or curiosity; (2) feeling irritated, resentful, or offended, with one's pride lightly wounded. Primary sense — the arousal of interest. In contemporary English the overwhelmingly common use is: "the mystery piqued his curiosity," "the documentary piqued my interest." Here piqued means sparked, stirred, sharpened, or awakened. The mental image is a sharp point touching the mind awake: curiosity is "pricked" into life exactly as a needle pricks the skin. This sense is mildly positive and dynamic — the arousal is pointed, lively, and engaging rather than merely pleasant or comfortable. Secondary sense — irritation and wounded pride. "She was piqued by his criticism," "he felt piqued at the snub." Here piqued describes the state of being irritated, resentful, or mildly offended. The image is again a pricking: a sharp remark pierces the protective surface of one's pride and produces a stinging smart rather than a deep wound. A person who is piqued is nettled and sulky, offended but not enraged; the register is closer to pettishness than to fury. Tertiary reflexive sense — to pride oneself (archaic-literary). To "pique oneself upon" a quality is to pride, plume, or congratulate oneself on it, i.e. to take a keen, proud self-satisfaction in something. This is the sense in Jane Austen's passage where Mary "piqued herself upon the solidity of her reflections": Mary feels a sharp, self-conscious pride in her own powers of reflection. Here the "pricking" is internalized as vanity or self-complacency — the pride stings the self rather than others. Noun sense — a fit of pique. The same root appears as a noun: pique meaning a fit of wounded pride, resentment, or spite, as in "she stormed off in a fit of pique" or "he did it out of pique." The noun is historically earlier in English than the verb in this "resentment" sense and the two reinforce each other. Historical physical sense (17th century). When pique first entered English (mid-17th century via French piquer), it could still mean literally to wound, pierce, or sting with a sharp point, retaining the force of its Latin-era source *piccare "to prick." This physical sense has long faded, but it is the master image from which every modern psychological use descends. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • To stimulate or excite (interest, curiosity, desire) — modern, positive.
  • To irritate, nettle, or resent (wounded pride) — modern, mildly negative.
  • Reflexive "to pique oneself on/upon" — to pride or plume oneself; archaic-literary (Austen, 19th c.).
  • Noun pique — a fit of resentment or wounded pride (17th c. onward).
  • To wound or pierce physically — obsolete (17th c.).
  • To rouse, spur, or goad into action — now rare (e.g., "piqued into a reply").
  • Card-game piquet and fabric piqué are separate borrowings, not senses of this word, but share the same root family.
The semantic bridge. The whole family hangs on one image: a needle. A sharp point touching the body produces a sting (physical to irritation); the same sharp point "touching" the mind wakes it (stimulation of curiosity); and when the point grazes self-esteem it produces a smarting pride (resentment and self-regard). All three living senses are metaphoric extensions of the same physical prick — a textbook case of semantic broadening from the concrete to the psychological. Register, collocation, and confusables.
  • "Piqued my interest/curiosity" — standard, neutral-to-positive, very frequent.
  • "Piqued by / at …" — irritated pride; slightly formal and literary.
  • "Piqued herself upon …" — reflexive, archaic, chiefly 18th–19th-century prose.
  • Homophone/confusion risk: peaked ("spiked, at a peak"), peeked ("glanced").
  • Root relatives: piquant (food that pricks the tongue), picket (a sharp stake; a person stationed as a point), pike (a spear), picador (a bullfighter who pricks with a lance), peck (to strike with a pointed beak).
In the test sentence from Pride and Prejudice, "Mary … who piqued herself upon the solidity of her reflections," the word is used in its reflexive self-regard sense: Mary takes sharp pride in the seriousness and good sense of her thoughts, and the metaphor is emotional, not physical.
