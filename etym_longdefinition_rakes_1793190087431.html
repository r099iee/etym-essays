Rakes, the plural of rake, are in their oldest and most familiar sense long-handled hand tools fitted with a toothed crossbar at the head, drawn across hay, soil, gravel, sand, or leaves to gather, spread, level, smooth, or turn the material beneath.

In the source fragment — meadow, and as many more with ______ turn it — the word wears exactly this agricultural dress: haymakers in a meadow turning cut grass with wooden hay rakes so that it dries evenly into hay, the immemorial rhythm of mowing, turning, and cocking that fed every horse and cow in England for a thousand summers before machinery came.

Core meaning in detail: The classic hay rake is a light wooden tool some six feet long, its head a bar set with a dozen or more short wooden teeth, used with a sweeping pull to invert the swath so the damp underside meets the sun. The garden rake is its sturdier iron cousin, with short stiff teeth for levelling seedbeds, covering sown seed, and dragging stones from tilth. The lawn or spring-tine rake fans thin flexible metal tines to comb leaves and moss from turf without wounding it. All share one gesture: the gathered pull toward the worker, the oldest tillage motion after the hoe.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hay and garden tool (primary sense, from Old English): the toothed implement of meadow and garden described above; the sense every reader reaches for first, and the one alive in the source sentence.
  • Croupier’s rake (casino tool): the long flat hoe-like pusher with which the croupier draws chips across the baize. The bridge is shape and gesture — a miniature rake pulling its harvest of counters toward the bank.
  • Stove, furnace, or cinder rake (industrial): the iron poker-rake for spreading coals and dragging clinker from grates. The bridge is function: teeth that stir and level, turned from hay to fire.
  • To rake with gunfire (verb turned noun in phrases like a rake of fire): enfilading fire swept along a line of men or a hull, as a rake sweeps a bed. The bridge is the sweeping stroke carried from tillage to artillery.
  • A dissolute man of fashion, a libertine (homograph, short for rakehell, 17th c.): the Restoration rake who drinks, games, and seduces. This is a different word wearing the same clothes — clipped from rakehell, a hell-raiser — and it survives in period drama and novels of manners.
  • Slope or inclination, as of a mast or funnel (nautical homograph, 17th c.): the angle at which a ship’s masts incline from the vertical. Likely a separate Scandinavian loan, the bridge if any being the slanting line of the rake’s teeth or handle.
  • Geological and technical rakes — fault rake, antenna rake (modern jargon): the angle of slip on a fault plane, the spread of aerial elements. The bridge is the slanting-toothed image borrowed by engineers for anything stepped or angled.

Register and connotation: rakes the tool is honest, rural, and industrious — hayfields, allotments, autumn leaves. The verb to rake darkens the family: raking fire, raking over old scandals, a raking glance. The libertine rake belongs to costume drama and moral judgment, half admired and half condemned. Few plurals hold a meadow tool, a casino implement, and a Restoration villain in one small shape, yet context parts them instantly.
