Element 1 — buzz: an imitative word for humming and harsh drone

The history of buzz is not a normal sound-law history but an onomatopoeic history. There is no Proto-Indo-European lexical root *buzz- in the ordinary sense; there is only the human attempt to copy the low vibrating drone of bees, flies, and later machines with voiced sibilants and a vowel that can be prolonged. Linguists therefore describe buzz as imitative or echoic, formed afresh in many periods rather than inherited by regular descent. That is why dictionaries mark it as of imitative origin and why its spelling wobbles in early attestations between busse, buszen, buzze, buzz.

In Middle English the verb appears in the late fifteenth century meaning to hum like a bee and, by extension, to whisper or murmur confidentially. The noun follows in the sixteenth century for the sound itself. The form stabilises as buzz with double -zz to signal a prolonged voiced alveolar fricative. Phonologically nothing dramatic happens: the initial /b/ supplies the low onset, the vowel /ʌ/ supplies the resonant chest note, the final /z/ supplies the continuant vibration that can be held indefinitely — buzzzzzz. Orthographically the doubling marks short vowel plus sustained consonant, a common English device for noises.

  • Late medieval whisper: to buzz in someone's ear meant to whisper insistently, already linking sound to secrecy and gossip.
  • Early modern bee-drone: Shakespearean and seventeenth-century uses fix buzz as the canonical bee-sound, then extend to any similar drone such as spinning wheels and distant crowds.
  • Industrial buzz: from the nineteenth century buzz becomes the default adjective for electrically or mechanically powered vibration — buzzers, buzz saws, buzzers on doors — because the new machines happened to make the old bee-sound louder.
  • Modern slang: twentieth-century buzz for gossip, thrill, intoxication, and telephone busy-signal all preserve the core idea of persistent low vibration that gets attention.

In compounds buzz- functions as a noise marker: buzz-saw, buzz-word, buzz-cut, buzz-wire. In buzzsaw it does not describe shape but acoustic signature. The circular saw screams at high revolutions, producing exactly the harsh sustained /z/ that the word imitates. American lumber mills of the 1830s–1840s named the tool for its voice, and workers immediately used it as a simile for anything else that roared continuously, including heroic snoring. That is the Maupassant translator's choice: the French original suggests ronfler comme une scie, but English renders the industrial roar as snoring like a buzzsaw.

Element 2 — saw: from PIE *sek- to cut to the American circular saw

The tool word saw has a deep inherited history, unlike buzz. It begins with Proto-Indo-European *sek- meaning to cut or sever, source also of Latin secare to cut, securis axe, and English segment, sickle, scythe through different suffixes. In Germanic this root with a g-extension yields Proto-Germanic *sagō feminine, specifically a toothed cutting implement. The semantic narrowing from general cutting to toothed blade happens already in Germanic; cognates include Dutch zaag, German Säge, Old Norse sög.

Old English sagu, saga is already the carpenter's saw, attested in glossaries around 1000. Middle English sawe continues it without great change except for the Great Vowel Shift raising the vowel to modern /sɔː/. The same form develops a second, unrelated homonym meaning proverb or wise saying, from a different Old English sagu meaning speech, related to say and saga. The two saws coexist today and are distinguished only by context. The tool noun early generates a weak verb to saw, past sawed / sawn, and compounds such as handsaw, backsaw, jigsaw, chainsaw.

  • Hand-tool era: for centuries saw meant a hand-pushed steel blade with set teeth, worked by muscle in pits and workshops.
  • Circular revolution: late eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century patents in England and America mount a toothed disc on a powered arbor. Shaker Sister Tabitha Babbitt's 1813 circular-saw idea, patented improvements by others, and steam then electric drive create the mill saw that screams.
  • American buzzsaw 1839: the Oxford English Dictionary records buzz-saw as U.S. colloquial for a circular power saw, often a large mill saw. Spelling alternates buzz-saw, buzz saw, buzzsaw, the closed form winning in twentieth-century American usage.
  • Verb and metaphor: to buzzsaw through means to cut ruthlessly and fast; sports and political journalism adopt buzzsaw for a team or campaign that shreds opponents, preserving the acoustic violence.

Socio-culturally the buzzsaw marks industrial America: lumber frontier, noise, danger, severed fingers, sawdust, and later horror-film iconography where the screaming blade becomes pure menace. Maupassant's poachers snoring like a buzzsaw therefore carry, in English, a harsher, more mechanical comedy than a simple loud snore — a human throat turned into a lumber mill, sawing the night air.

Attestation detail for buzzsaw as a compound: American newspapers of the late 1830s advertise buzz-saw mills for sale in Pennsylvania and upstate New York; mechanics magazines debate buzz-saw arbors and belts; accident reports lament hands lost to buzz-saws. The Oxford English Dictionary entry traces the hyphenated form to 1839 and the closed form to the early twentieth century. Dialectally the South and Midwest preserve buzz saw for the frame saw that cuts firewood with a tractor drive, while lumber regions reserve buzzsaw for the great mill disc. Both preserve the acoustic motive: you hear the mill before you see it, and the name tells the deaf reader what to hear.

Literary afterlife: Mark Twain, Faulkner, and later Stephen King use buzzsaw similes for snoring, cicadas, and dread. The Maupassant translator who renders French scie as English buzzsaw therefore Americanises and industrialises the image, swapping a generic hand-saw whine for a powered scream — a defensible domestication that keeps the comedy harsh and masculine for Anglophone readers accustomed to lumberfp imagery.
