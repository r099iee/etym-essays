- Classical Antiquity (c. 8th c. BCE–1st c. CE) - Originally the literal mythological name for the sea-brooding kingfisher (Greek alkyōn / halkyōn); perceived with religious reverence and maritime gratitude as a divine bird capable of subduing Mediterranean winter tempests.
- Late Middle English & Scholasticism (c. 1380–1450) - Adopted via Anglo-Norman French and Latin into natural philosophy treatises as a noun designating the 14-day astronomical and meteorological calm around the winter solstice.
- Elizabethan & Jacobean Drama (c. 1590–1650) - Transformed by Shakespeare and contemporaries from a literal maritime weather phenomenon into an expansive political and royal metaphor for civic harmony, national prosperity, and the cessation of factional war.
- 18th & 19th Century Romanticism (c. 1780–1900) - Underwent profound sentimental elevation, shifting decisively from immediate political peace into a retrospective, nostalgic descriptor for lost youth, innocence, and vanished pastoral simplicity.
- 20th–21st Century - Fully established as an elevated literary adjective for idealized historical eras (e.g., the pre-war Belle Époque); concurrently adopted into commercial branding and pharmaceutical nomenclature (notably the sedative Halcion) as an emblem of soothing calm.
