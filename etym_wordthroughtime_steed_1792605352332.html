- [Old English (c. 8th–11th c.)] - stēda originated as a concrete agricultural term for an intact, uncastrated male horse kept for breeding on an estate (a stud-horse or stallion) - [perception: viewed as a practical livestock classification for a virile breeding animal]
- [Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.)] - stede shifted dramatically under Anglo-Norman chivalric culture into high romance and heroic poetry, shedding its purely reproductive sense to designate the armored warhorse, tournament charger, or noble mount of a knight - [perception: elevated into the supreme aristocratic symbol of chivalric honor, martial valor, and equestrian nobility]
- [Early Modern English (c. 16th–17th c.)] - retained in Elizabethan drama and Renaissance epic poetry (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton) as a dignified, archaizing word for any high-mettled horse, while everyday speech standardized on horse - [perception: recognized as an elevated, poetic vehicle for heroic myth, royal pageantry, and grand narrative verse]
- [18th–19th c. Romantic Medievalism & Industrial Age] - revitalized by Romantic poets and Victorian historical novelists (Walter Scott, Byron, Tennyson) to evoke medieval nostalgia, while simultaneously generating technological metaphors such as the "iron steed" for the steam locomotive - [perception: celebrated as the romantic embodiment of fierce spirit, freedom, and animal majesty, contrasted with the smoking machinery of industrialization]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Era] - largely retired from functional everyday equine speech, surviving almost exclusively in high fantasy literature, historical fiction, fairy tales, and self-conscious mock-heroic banter for bicycles, motorcycles, or old cars ("my trusty steed") - [perception: universally perceived as a delightfully archaic, nostalgic, and mythic literary term]
