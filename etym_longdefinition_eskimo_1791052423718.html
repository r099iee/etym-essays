Eskimo is a collective exonym historically used in English for the Indigenous circumpolar peoples of the Arctic from eastern Siberia and Alaska through Arctic Canada to Greenland, principally the Inuit and Yupik peoples and sometimes extended to the related Aleut/Unangan peoples. In contemporary usage it is widely regarded as outdated and, in Canada and Greenland, offensive, with Inuit, Inupiat, Yupik and specific group names preferred, while in Alaska it remains in partial use as an inclusive term because Yupik peoples are not Inuit.

The core ethnonymic sense denotes people, culture and language. An Eskimo person belongs to communities traditionally sustained by hunting sea mammals, caribou, fishing and gathering, with distinctive technologies such as the kayak, umiak, dogsled, harpoon with toggling head, oil lamp (qulliq), and winter snow house (iglu/igloo). Linguistically, Eskimo designates one branch of the Eskimo-Aleut (Eskaleut) language family, split into Inuit-Inupiaq (a dialect continuum from Alaska to Greenland) and Yupik (Central Alaskan Yupik, Alutiiq/Sugpiaq, Siberian Yupik, Cupik), distinct from Aleut/Unangan. Anthropologists historically divided Eskimo cultures into Maritime Arctic, Thule and Dorset predecessors, and modern Inuit-Yupik societies.

  • Peoples sense: In older texts, the Eskimos means the Arctic peoples as a whole. Example: the Eskimo guides who accompanied Peary to the North Pole. Modern guidance: replace with Inuit guides, Inupiat guides, or Yupik guides according to region, unless quoting historically.
  • Linguistic sense: Eskimo languages or Eskimo-Aleut family. This technical compound remains standard in linguistics even where the standalone ethnonym is avoided, though Inuit-Yupik is increasingly substituted.
  • Attributive / compound senses: Eskimo dog (American Eskimo Dog, Eskimo husky, Canadian Eskimo Dog), Eskimo roll (kayak Eskimo roll), Eskimo kiss (nose-to-nose rub, from Inuit kunik), Eskimo boot / parka / pie. These preserve the word in frozen form and are themselves undergoing renaming (e.g., kunik for Eskimo kiss, Inuit roll for Eskimo roll).
  • Adjectival sense: Eskimo art, Eskimo folklore, Eskimo survival skills, meaning pertaining to those cultures. Again now often rephrased as Inuit art or Yupik folklore.

Secondary and extended senses are largely historical or stereotyped and explain why the term became problematic. In 19th-century popular writing Eskimo could connote primitive hardiness, stoicism, exotic ingenuity, or childlike simplicity, reflecting colonial romanticism rather than self-description. In mid-20th-century American slang Eskimo occurred in offensive compounds such as Eskimo brother (a crude sexual joke about men who have slept with the same woman), Eskimo pie jokes, or Eskimo kiss used trivially, all now recognized as demeaning. In sports mascots and brand names (Edmonton Eskimos football team, renamed Elks in 2021; Eskimo Pie ice cream, renamed Edy's Pie in 2021) the word functioned as a branding exoticism that has been retired after protest.

  • Geographical nuance: In Canada Eskimo has been almost entirely replaced by Inuit (singular Inuk) since the 1970s; using Eskimo for a Canadian Inuk is considered insulting. In Greenland, Kalaallit (Greenlanders) is preferred. In Alaska, Eskimo remains an accepted umbrella that includes both Inupiat (who are Inuit) and Yupik/Cupik peoples (who are not), alongside Alaska Native. In Russia/Siberia, Yupik peoples may still be described as Eskimo in English translation.
  • Legal and institutional survival: Names such as Eskimo-Aleut languages, Eskimo Museum, Eskimo Point (former name of Arviat) preserve the term archivally. Style guides (AP, Canadian Press, Government of Canada) advise: do not use Eskimo for people in Canada; specify nation or use Inuit/Yupik; Eskimo-Aleut is permissible in linguistic context with explanation.
  • Semantic bridge for pejoration: The shift from neutral to offensive did not come from a change in dictionary denotation but from recognition that (a) it was never a self-name, (b) its alleged meaning eater of raw flesh was perceived as contemptuous, (c) it lumped distinct peoples, and (d) it accumulated colonial stereotypes. This is classic pejoration by pragmatic association, parallel to Lapp versus Sami.

In short, Eskimo today means, denotatively, a member of the Inuit-Yupik Arctic peoples or their languages, but connotatively it signals dated, outsider discourse except in specific Alaskan and linguistic contexts where it remains functional. Learners should understand it for reading older exploration, anthropology and literature, including sentences like Peary asked one of his guides, but should default in production to precise modern terms.
