Snivelling, the present participle and verbal noun of snivel, describes at once a sound, a bodily state, and a moral judgement: crying with loud sniffles and a running nose, and by extension whining or complaining in feeble self-pity. In the source sentence Gollum's confession pours out together with much snivelling and snarling: tears, mucus, and wheedling self-excuse in one miserable stream, the captive creature alternating between abject blubbering and sudden viciousness.

Core meaning in detail: the physical picture is unmistakable: a person, usually a child or a wretch, weeping while repeatedly sniffing back nasal mucus, breath hitching, speech broken by sniffs. The sound is thin, wet, and repetitive, instantly evoking helplessness. From this bodily base the word extends to any tearful or nasal complaining, whether or not actual tears flow: the employee snivelling about his workload, the politician snivelling about press coverage. Contempt rides with it always; nobody is praised for snivelling.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Weeping with sniffles and a runny nose (primary literal sense): the blubbering child wiped by an exasperated parent, the cold-sufferer snivelling into a handkerchief. The bridge to everything else is the sound-image of weakness made audible: control has collapsed and the nose testifies.
  • Whining or complaining tearfully (primary figurative sense, the source meaning): to plead, apologise, or protest in a small trembling voice calculated to win pity. The bridge runs from symptom to strategy: Gollum's snivelling excuses himself and accuses others at once, and adult snivellers do the same in offices and courts.
  • Showing maudlin self-pity without tears (extended): editorials denounce snivelling sentimentality, coaches ban snivelling excuses. The bridge generalises the sound into an attitude: any complaint that wallows rather than acts earns the name, even when perfectly dry-eyed.
  • Sniffing from a cold or catarrh (older physical sense): to have a snivelling cold is to drip and sniffle without necessarily weeping. The bridge is the shared mucus: seventeenth-century writers used snivel for any running nose, tearful or merely catarrhal.
  • Running at the nose — snivelling candles and wicks (obsolete technical): a candle was said to snivel when its half-melted wick guttered and dripped. The bridge is visual analogy: the candle weeps wax as the nose weeps mucus, and the same verb served both until the age of gaslight.
  • Hypocritical sanctimony — the snivelling hypocrite (moral type): from seventeenth-century satire onward, the sniveller is the canting pretender whose piety drips like a cold. The bridge fuses the wet nose with wet morality: both disgust the robust observer, and the stereotype survives in every snivelling sneak of school fiction.

Register and connotation: snivelling is frankly contemptuous, a word that convicts as it describes. Nurses may use it clinically of colds, but everywhere else it sneers: the snivelling child should stop and take hold of himself, the snivelling adult should be ashamed. Its power in the source sentence lies in the pairing: snivelling with snarling captures Gollum's whole oscillation between worm and wolf, pity-me one instant and teeth the next. Few participles pack a whole character study so economically.
