An Indo-European root of waiting and trusting. The noun ABODE grows out of the verb 'abide', which descends from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *bheidh-, meaning 'to trust, to wait, to remain'. This root is a rich source: it underlies Greek peíthein ('to persuade'), Latin fīdere ('to trust') and foedus ('treaty'), and the whole English family of 'faith', 'fidelity', 'confide'. In the Germanic branch it produced the reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb *bīdaną, 'to wait, to remain', from which Old English inherited bīdan and its prefixed form ābīdan, 'to wait, to remain, to stay'. The semantic core — patient remaining, steadfast waiting — is what gives the whole family of 'abide', 'abode', and even 'bide' their meaning.

Old English and Middle English 'abide'. In Old English, ābīdan (with the intensive prefix ā-) meant 'to wait, to await, to remain, to stay'. Over the Middle English period it became abiden / abide, 'to wait, to remain, to dwell'. The word carried a dignified quality of patience and persistence: to abide was not merely to be present but to remain steadfastly. Because people 'abide in' a place — that is, remain there as their settled home — the verb-phrase 'to abide in a place' became the natural seed of a noun naming that place.

From past participle to noun. The decisive event in the noun's formation was the grammatical reanalysis of the strong past participle of 'abide'. The Old English strong verb ābīdan had the past participle ābiden, which in Middle English developed the form 'abode', meaning 'remained, dwelt'. Over time this participial form, so often met in phrases describing where someone had remained and dwelt, was reanalysed as a noun: the 'abode' became the place itself rather than the act of having remained. This is a classic instance of deverbal substantivization — a verb form hardening into a noun — and it is why the noun 'abode' and the past tense 'abode' (of abide) are identical in form. The modern verb paradigm has since levelled 'abode' toward 'abided' as the usual past form, leaving the noun free to live its own life.

Formal and legal specialization. Once established as a noun meaning 'dwelling place', 'abode' developed a distinctly formal, administrative register. The phrase 'a place of abode', meaning a settled residence, became a legal and official expression, and its negation 'of no fixed abode' or 'without fixed abode' entered police and administrative language to describe a person with no permanent home. This legal sense has kept the word fully alive and functional in contemporary English, even as its literary use became comparatively rarer.

Literary and poetic reach. Alongside its official life, 'abode' retained an elevated, literary tone suited to sovereign or impassioned contexts. Kings and heroes 'take up their abode' in halls and thrones; poets and fantasy writers use it for the dwellings of gods, of the dead, or of timeless peace. Its dignified quality derives from the patient 'remaining' embedded in its etymology — an abode is not a transient lodging but a place where one stays. This is precisely the sense in which a king 'takes up his abode' beneath a mountain and establishes his realm, as in the context of this card.

Modern summary. Today ABODE is a distinctly formal or literary word for a dwelling, distinguished from the everyday 'home' and 'house' by its dignity and permanence, and kept current by legal phrases and by narrative prose. Its history — from an Indo-European root of trusting and waiting, through a Germanic verb of remaining, to a past participle that hardened into a noun — is a compact and elegant demonstration of how grammar and meaning together shape the vocabulary of a language.
