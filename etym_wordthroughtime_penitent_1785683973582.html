- [Late 14th century (c. 1340)] - Borrowed via Old French from Latin paenitens "repenting"; from its first English uses it is a strictly religious word, naming sorrow for sin as the gate to confession and absolution — solemn, churchly, and tied to God.
- [Mid-15th century: the noun] - 'A penitent' emerges as a person performing penance — the one who kneels, is confessed, and does the discipline his sorrow requires; the word also labels the early Christian orders of penitents, so it is a title, not merely an emotion.
- [16th–18th century] - Adjective and noun coexist in sermons and devotional prose; 'penitent' still means “feeling sorrow for sin” but is increasingly used of those formally enrolled in penance — Lenten, publicly humble, marked out in church.
- [Modern register] - Survives as a literary, reverent word for sincere, action-bound regret; no longer common in everyday speech, it keeps its sacramental colour — the penitent kneels and works at amendment — whereas secular sorrow is now said as 'sorry'.
