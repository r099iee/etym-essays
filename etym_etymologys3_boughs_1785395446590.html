EVOLUTION OF BOUGHS: FROM THE ARM OF A BODY TO THE ARM OF A TREE The story of boughs is the story of one ancient metaphor that has hardened into plain vocabulary. The earliest recoverable ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *bheghu-s or *bhaghu-s, meaning 'arm' or 'shoulder' - the limb of a body. The root is exceptionally well attested across the Indo-European family: Sanskrit bahu, 'arm, foreleg', Avestan bazu, 'arm', Greek pekhys, 'forearm, elbow', Armenian bazuk, 'arm', and Tocharian poke, 'arm'. The exact original meaning is debated - the Greek word points toward the forearm and the elbow joint, while the Sanskrit, Armenian, and Tocharian words point toward the whole arm - but the family is secure, and it shows that the Germanic peoples inherited a word whose core was the limb that reaches out from the body. The transference to the tree (Proto-Germanic) In the Germanic branch the word took the step that defines it forever after. Proto-Germanic *bogaz meant both 'arm, shoulder' - as in Old High German buog, 'shoulder', and Old Norse bogr, the shoulder of an animal - and, by transference, 'a tree-limb, a bough'. The transfer is a metaphor of a perfectly natural kind: a bough is the arm of a tree, reaching out from the trunk as an arm reaches from a shoulder, bending under weight and bearing lesser growth. The metaphor is so old that it is already complete in the reconstructed ancestor of the modern languages, which means the tree-sense of the word is at least two thousand years old and probably much older. English speakers in the eighth century, saying bog for a tree-limb, were using a word whose body-sense was still alive beside it: Old English bog meant 'shoulder' and 'arm' as well as 'bough, branch'. Old English and Middle English (8th-15th centuries) In Old English the word lived a double life. As bog (or boh) it meant both the arm or shoulder of a body and the bough or branch of a tree, and a related form boga meant 'something bent, a bow'. The body-sense was the older, but the tree-sense was the more useful, and the two coexisted for centuries. In Middle English the spelling settled: bogh, bough, and bow were used interchangeably until the digraph spelling bough became fixed from the thirteenth century on. During the Middle English period the body-sense dropped away - English found other words for 'arm' - leaving bough entirely to the tree, where it has stayed ever since. The word was an ordinary, unceremonious word for a tree's main structural branches, used without poetic decoration in prose as well as verse; a medieval writer said bough the way a modern writer says branch. The narrowing and poeticisation (16th-20th centuries) The great change in the modern period was one of register rather than meaning. As branch - the neutral, all-purpose word for any limb of a tree at any size - established itself as the everyday term, bough narrowed to the largest structural arms that spring from the trunk and acquired a consistent poetic flavour. Writers cultivating an older, saga-like diction - from the Romantics to Tolkien - chose bough precisely because it carries weight, age, and shelter, the resonance of the greenwood and of the tree's great arms. The plural boughs became the word of verse and nature-writing: birds nest in the high boughs, boughs are shaken by the wind, the cradle is rocked in a bough. The ancient metaphor of the arm never quite disappears: even in the most matter-of-fact use, a bough still reads as the tree's limb reaching out from its trunk. The modern word In Tolkien's The Hobbit, the word is used in its plainest and most practical sense: the Eagles 'had brought up dry boughs for fuel'. Yet even here the ancient dignity does its quiet work. Dry boughs for fuel is the homely, practical detail of a wilderness camp, and the old word keeps that practicality in the register of saga and legend rather than of ordinary household prose. A word that began as the Indo-European word for the arm of a body, passed through the arm of a tree, and survived a thousand years of language change, here feeds a camp-fire on a mountain while keeping its inherited weight of age and shelter - the oldest and strongest of all the words for the limbs of a tree.
