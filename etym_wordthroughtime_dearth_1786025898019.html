- 12th–13th c. - Formed in Early Middle English (*derthe*) as an abstract noun from *deore* ('dear/costly') + *-th*, meaning 'the state of high prices or expensiveness.'
- 14th c. - Shifted rapidly through economic reality to mean 'a severe food shortage, scarcity of grain, or famine' (prominently in *Piers Plowman* and Great Famine accounts).
- 16th c. - Generalized in Early Modern English (Shakespeare) to mean a scarcity or total lack of anything desirable (talent, wit, honor, beauty).
- 18th–19th c. - Adopted into formal essays and political economy to describe statistical deficits, shortages of labor, and intellectual barrenness.
- 20th–21st c. - Maintained as an elevated literary and analytical noun for an acute, consequential lack of essential resources, evidence, or leadership.
