In this passage from Pater's preface, Sainte-Beuve is not a common noun but a proper name invoked as critical authority: Charles Augustin Sainte-Beuve (1804–1869), the dominant French literary critic of the mid-nineteenth century. Pater quotes a dictum associated with him—that the critic's end is to limit oneself to knowing beautiful things intimately and to feed upon them as exquisite amateurs and accomplished humanists ('De se borner à connaître de près les belles choses, et à s'en nourrir en exquis amateurs, en humanistes accomplis'). The literary tone is admiring and programmatic: Sainte-Beuve stands for sensitive, biographical-impressionistic criticism, the model of the cultivated reader who lives with works rather than system-building over them. For Pater the name functions as a warrant for aesthetic intimacy over abstract rule. Polysemy: the senses the name carries today.
  • Proper noun, the critic: Charles Augustin Sainte-Beuve (1804–1869), French critic, poet, and historian of Port-Royal; author of the weekly Causeries du lundi ('Monday Chats'), the most influential critical journalism of his century.
  • The biographical method: The critical practice—often called 'la méthode de Sainte-Beuve' or 'l'homme et l'œuvre'—of explaining a work through the author's life, temperament, and milieu; the pioneer of biographical criticism in the modern sense.
  • The dictum quoted by Pater: The rule of the critic as humanist gourmet of beautiful things, an aesthetic program of intimate acquaintance and refined feeding on art.
  • 'À la Sainte-Beuve' / the Sainte-Beuvean portrait: Adverbial and adjectival phrases naming his style of literary portraiture—impressionistic, biographical, civil, and masterly.
  • Metonym for impressionistic criticism: In English aesthetic prose (Pater, Arnold) the name stands for cultivated, temperament-led reading against abstract theory.
  • The Proust polemic: Marcel Proust's Contre Sainte-Beuve (written c. 1908–1909, published 1954) attacked the reduction of the work to the life; the name now also denotes a famous wrong turn in critical theory.
  • Historical landmark: The name marks the moment criticism became a profession in France—editor of the Revue des Deux Mondes, professor at the Collège de France, senator.
  • Compound name-structure: 'Sainte' + 'Beuve,' a French saint-name compound (Saint Bova), showing that behind the critic stands an older hagiographic name.
Boundaries with related critical types:
  • vs a theorist: A system-builder of genres or poetics; Sainte-Beuve specializes in literary portraits and the writer's milieu, not system-building.
  • vs a moralist-critic: A stern judge of works by ethical standards; Sainte-Beuve privileges taste, temperament, and the living texture of books.
  • vs the New Critics: They bracket biography and focus on the text; Sainte-Beuve made life and character central—the very method Proust attacked and Pater found congenial.
  • vs a mere reviewer: A reviewer produces ephemeral notices; Sainte-Beuve aimed at permanent literary-historical portraiture.
  • vs Proust: Proust is the great antagonist who denied the biographical method; the two names define opposite poles of twentieth-century theory.
Collocations in English criticism are largely proper-name constructions: 'Sainte-Beuve's method,' 'à la Sainte-Beuve,' 'the Sainte-Beuvean portrait,' 'following Sainte-Beuve.' Register is high literary and academic; the name signals Francophile nineteenth-century critical culture. Historical bridge: the name is a French hagiotoponymic compound—'Saint Bova'—fossilized as a surname, and the pioneer of biographical criticism who bore it became a central figure in the modern polemic over how literature should be read.
