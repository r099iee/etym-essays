- 15th c.: 'gruff' (from Dutch/Low German 'grof') first means physically coarse and thick — of texture and cloth; plain, unrefined.
- 17th c.: the sense migrates from things to voices — a rough, low, harsh voice — and to a bluff, surly manner; no longer physical, now social.
- 18th–19th c.: in novels 'gruff' becomes an affectionate character trait — the stern, rough-spoken man with a hidden warm heart; endearing rather than hostile.
- Contemporary: 'gruffly' describes a low, gravelly, abrupt way of speaking that signals caution or self-protection but rarely true malice; a staple of fiction, still mildly affectionate.
