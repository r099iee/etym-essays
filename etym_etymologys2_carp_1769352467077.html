CARP is a pair of homonyms — the fish noun and the complaining verb — with two completely separate histories. Both chains are given in isolation below.
Chain 1 — the fish noun (matches this card's definition and context):
1. Pre-Indo-European Danubian substrate *karpa- — "carp" (reconstructed; the fish was native to the slow rivers of the Danube and Black Sea basins; no PIE etymology exists).
2. Late Latin carpa — "carp" (attested 5th c. CE, Cassiodorus, Variae; a loan from the substrate term).
3. Old French carpe — "carp" (attested 12th–13th c.).
4. Middle English carpe — "carp" (attested c. 1400, borrowed with the spread of monastic fish-farming culture).
5. Modern English carp — the large freshwater fish Cyprinus carpio (from the 15th c. onward).
Sibling branch (Continental): Proto-Germanic *karpa- (reconstructed, borrowed from the same substrate) → Old High German karpho → Modern German Karpfen.
Chain 2 — the verb "to carp" (complain):
1. Old Norse karpa — "to brag, boast, talk noisily, wrangle" (attested, Viking Age; the source of the Middle English verb).
2. Middle English carpen — "to talk, speak, prate, tell" (attested 14th c.; the sense was at first neutral — "to speak, to tell a tale").
3. Modern English carp — "to find fault, complain petulantly or continually" (attested from the 16th c.; the neutral "to speak" sense gives way to the pejorative one, possibly reinforced by Latin carpere "to pluck, pick at, slander", from PIE *kerp- "to pluck, harvest") → carp.
Notes on debated / nuanced stages: (a) The fish noun has no PIE ancestry; its substrate origin means the chain begins outside Indo-European. (b) The verb's development is debated: scholars differ on how much Latin carpere influenced the shift from Old Norse "brag, talk" to English "complain"; both sources likely blended. (c) The two etymons are historically unrelated; their identity in Modern English "carp" is pure homonymic coincidence.
