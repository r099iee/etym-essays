HOTFOOT is an adverb meaning "at full speed, in urgent haste, without delay" — typically used with a verb of motion ("ran hotfoot to the Master") — and, since the late nineteenth century, a colloquial verb meaning "to hurry somewhere at once", almost always in the fixed phrase hotfoot it ("the reporters hotfooted it to the courthouse"). It carries a vivid, almost comic intensity: the mental picture is of a runner whose feet are literally burning with urgency, so the word implies not merely speed but breathless, single-minded haste, often with an undertone of excitement, alarm, or opportunism.

Primary sense (adverbial)

  • With great speed and immediacy: "he set off hotfoot for the capital". The adverb always modifies motion and normally follows the verb, giving it a crisp, folk-flavoured rhythm.
  • Register: informal-to-neutral, slightly old-fashioned in the 21st century, yet a favourite of journalists and storytellers because it compresses "hurriedly and at once" into one kinetic word.

Secondary senses and uses

  • Verb — hotfoot it (from 1896, American English): "to hasten, to go quickly". The pronoun it is an empty object of the kind seen in foot it ("walk"), hike it, make it — a construction that converts a manner adverb into a verb of motion.
  • Noun — a hotfoot (from 1934): the schoolboy prank of slipping a match or strip of paper between the sole and the upper of a victim's shoe and lighting it so the wearer dances with burning feet. The noun is a playful literalisation of the old haste-image: the victim, in effect, runs hotfoot.

Semantic bridge

  • The core metaphor is heat = urgency. A person in a hurry is imagined as having hot, burning feet, so that speed is caused by discomfort: the hotter the feet, the faster the run. The same heat-to-urgency mapping appears in hot pursuit, hot on the trail, a hot tip and hot under the collar.
  • The shift from adverb to verb ("hotfoot it") is a classic English conversion: the phrase "to go hotfoot" was re-analysed as "to hotfoot it", the adverb absorbing the verb's motion sense.
  • The 1934 noun shows how a dead metaphor can be revived and literalised for comic effect, turning the frozen phrase back into a physical event.

Usage and idiom

  • The adverb resists modification: one does not say “very hotfoot” or “quite hotfoot” — the word is already maximal in intensity, like “posthaste”. Its natural companions are verbs of motion: run, ride, march, hurry, set off, come.
  • Because it fuses adjective and noun, “hotfoot” behaves like other body-part compounds of the same vintage (“barefoot”, “bareheaded”): the first element modifies the second, and the whole works adverbially. Unlike them, it acquired a verbal twin (“hotfoot it”), which barefoot never did.
  • In modern corpora the verb outnumbers the adverb in American usage, while British prose still prefers the adverb — a mild transatlantic split worth noting.

Distinctions and pronunciation

  • Not to be confused with "hot-footed" (having hot feet) or casual misspellings; the adverb is a single unhyphenated word in modern usage, though older texts write "hot-foot".
  • Stress: typically ˌhotˈfoot, with a secondary stress on hot and primary stress on foot.
  • The word is idiomatic rather than technical; there is no formal or scientific register, which is precisely what makes it vivid in narrative prose such as Tolkien's The Hobbit, where the grim-voiced fellow runs hotfoot to the Master of Lake-town as the dragon's death is announced.
