INSIGNIA — single Latin-derived chain. The early root is debated; the two main proposals are given below, but the attested chain is secure from Latin onward. • PIE *sekw- "to follow" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE) → *sekw-no-m "that which one follows, a following sign, a marker to follow" — OR, debated alternative, PIE *sek- "to cut" (a mark cut into a surface). The connection of Latin signum to *sekw- ("follow") is the majority view (de Vaan: signum derives from *sekw-no-m, "sign, standard"); the "cut" etymology is rejected by most modern scholars but still appears in older handbooks. State of evidence: signum is attested securely only in Latin; the PIE stages are reconstructions. → Proto-Italic *sekw-o-m / *sek-no-m (reconstructed) → Latin signum "a sign, token, mark, signal; a military standard or banner; a seal" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE in Plautus and Ennius; signum also names a statue, esp. a sacred image). → Latin insignis "conspicuous, distinguished, marked upon" (adjective formed in- "in, upon, on" + signum, c. 1st c. BCE; attested in Cicero). → Latin insigne (neuter noun) "a badge, mark of distinction, ensign; an ornament of office" (the neuter of insignis used as a noun, Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE); plural insignia "badges, marks of honour." → English insignia "distinguishing marks, badges, or tokens of rank and office" (borrowed in the 17th century, 1640s, directly from the Latin plural; first recorded in English in heraldic and antiquarian writing). → Modern English INSIGNIA (used as plural and, increasingly, as singular/mass noun). Note on the debate: the disputed stage is the pre-Latin one — whether signum derives from *sekw- "follow" (the standard modern view) or from *sek- "cut." The Latin-to-English portion is fully attested and uncontested.
