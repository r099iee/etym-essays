The story of fizzle begins, oddly, not in the kitchen or the fireworks factory but in the scatological corner of sixteenth-century English. The word is pure onomatopoeia — its buzz is a phonetic imitation of a soft, escaping hiss — and the earliest attested sense, recorded around 1530, was 'to break wind quietly', a genteel euphemism for the very sound the word was coined to copy. The same imitative base *fis- underlies a whole cluster of Germanic words for exactly this kind of small, private hissing: Old Norse fisa, German fisten, Dutch fiesen, and Old English fīsian, the last of which fed straight into the Middle English fisten and the dialectal fise from which fizzle itself descends. None of these can be traced to a Proto-Indo-European root; they are all independent echoic formations built out of the hiss itself, which is why the derivation properly begins with the sound rather than with an ancient etymon. This is a word whose family tree is horizontal, not vertical — a network of European dialects each making the same small noise. The decisive semantic turn came in the nineteenth century, in the age of fireworks, guns, and domestic gaslight. By the 1840s fizzle had moved from its scatological corner into the world of chemistry and pyrotechnics, where damp powder and wet fuses 'fizzle' — that is, they sputter and hiss feebly instead of igniting cleanly. From this concrete, technical image came the explosive metaphorical use, and it is significant that fizzle out first flowered in American English around 1848, applied to speeches, campaigns, booms, and careers that began with a bang and ended with the damp hiss of a wet squib. The image was immediate and universal: everything that promises a flash and delivers a fizzle became 'fizzled out'. The phrase spread rapidly into general English, and by the later nineteenth century it was a settled idiom of journalism and politics, used of financial panics that sputtered, reforms that died in committee, and movements that failed to catch. Meanwhile the shorter fizz, attested from the 1660s for the hiss of an effervescing liquid, developed alongside it, producing fizzy in the nineteenth century and carrying the sound into the vocabulary of soda-water, champagne, and sparklers. The two words remain close cousins rather than father and son: fizzle is not the child of fizz but a parallel formation from the same imitative base, differing in the frequentative -le suffix, which makes fizzle mean 'to fizz repeatedly, feebly, and with effort'. That suffix is the word's secret engine. Fizzle, like sparkle, wriggle, crackle, and twinkle, is built on the frequentative -le, and the repeated, effortful, diminishing action it encodes is exactly the difference between the confident single hiss of steam and the pathetic series of sputtering gasps that ends in nothing. Phonetically the word enacts its meaning with unusual completeness. The initial f- is a soft, breathy escape of air; the high, tense vowel i gives the hiss its pitch; the double z prolongs the buzz; and the final -le, clipped by the participial -ing in fizzling, folds the sound back on itself like a flame running out of fuel. Sound and sense have never drifted far apart, which is why the word has resisted all pressure toward euphemism or abstractness: it remains a word about a noise, and its failures are always heard. Socio-culturally, fizzling is a word of the anti-climax, and its history tracks the rise of the modern public spectacle — the public meeting, the stock-market boom, the press campaign — because those are the arenas in which things begin with a bang and end with a damp squib. The phrase 'to fizzle out' is the English language's standard verdict on ambitious beginnings that come to nothing, from literary movements to electoral landslides to sporting seasons. It is inherently deflationary and faintly comic: nothing grand ever fizzles, only the pretentious and the over-promised. In The Hobbit, the goblins' 'fizzling torch' draws its whole joke from this history — the torch that cannot light the night is the camp-fire of every failed fireworks display, every damp squib, every enterprise that sputters and dies, and the word quietly mocks its own speakers. Tolkien, the philologist, chose precisely the word whose biography is failure: born as a genteel hiss, grown technical in the age of gunpowder, and fixed forever as the sound of everything that promised more than it delivered.
