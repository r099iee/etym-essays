The story of hashish begins in the Arabic tongue as something utterly innocent: dry grass. The root ḥ-š-š meant to mow or cut herbage, and ḥašīš was hay, the stuff of mangers and thatch, before it narrowed, as such words do, to the one herb that mattered most in its pharmacopoeia: hemp. Medieval Arabic physicians knew cannabis well, prescribing it for pain, worms, and melancholy while warning that it clouded the mind, and Sufi circles debated whether its ecstasies were holy or merely stupor.

The word might never have reached Europe had it not been for murder, or rather for the story of murder. During the Crusades, Sunni writers denounced the Nizari Ismailis of Alamut as hashishiyya, low rabble, and returning Crusaders and travellers embroidered the insult into a romance: an Old Man of the Mountain who drugged youths into a walled garden of pleasures, showed them paradise, and sent them out to kill princes in hope of return. Marco Polo's account around 1300 fixed the legend in the European imagination, and for five centuries dictionaries repeated that assassin meant eater of hashish, with philologist Silvestre de Sacy giving the theory academic lustre in 1809.

From Cairo to Paris:

  • Egypt, 1798–1801: Napoleon's expeditionary force finds its soldiers smoking hashish; General Menou bans it in 1800 under severe penalties, and returning officers and doctors bring both the habit and the word hachisch to France.
  • Paris, 1840s: Dr Jacques-Joseph Moreau studies the drug's effects on the mind, while writers Théophile Gautier, Charles Baudelaire, Gérard de Nerval, and Alexandre Dumas taste green dawamesk paste at the Club des Hachichins; Baudelaire's Artificial Paradises of 1860 make hashish the emblem of the poet's dangerous curiosity.
  • English adoption, mid-19th c.: travelogues, medical journals, and Lane's Arabian Nights carry hashish into English, spelled variously hasheesh and hachish, always with an Oriental perfume no native word like hemp could supply.
  • Global prohibition, 20th c.: Egypt bans it, the 1925 Geneva Convention schedules cannabis internationally, and post-war laws from America to Morocco drive the trade underground; meanwhile Beat writers and hippie travellers romanticise the Rif mountains and Afghan highlands as hashish Shangri-las.
  • Medicine and partial legalisation, late 20th–21st c.: chemists isolate THC in 1964, medicine reclaims cannabinoids for nausea, spasticity, and pain, and a wave of legalisation treats the resin's chemistry under new names while the old word lingers in statutes and literature.

Phonetically the word travelled almost unchanged, a mark of its exoticism: Arabic ḥašīš with its deep guttural ḥ and sharp š became French hachisch and English hashish, the spelling wobbling for decades before settling. English never fully naturalised its sounds, keeping the hissing sibilants that signal its foreignness, and poets have always preferred it to blunter synonyms for exactly that reason. Semantically the path ran from the general to the particular: hay, then herb, then the intoxicating herb, then the resin of that herb, each narrowing driven by the plant's power over the mind. Modern scholarship adds one honest correction: the thrilling bridge between hashish and assassin is probably folk etymology rather than fact, since no contemporary Nizari source mentions the drug, and hashishiyya reads better as street abuse, your common rabble, than as a pharmacological confession. The word remains, in any case, what it has been since Paris: Europe's oldest name for cannabis intoxication, half medicine, half mirage.
