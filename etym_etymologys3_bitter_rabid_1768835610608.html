bitter — The word bitter is older than the English language itself and yet has changed its meaning so little that its earliest recorded sense is recognisably its modern core. Old English biter (c. 725) meant both “having a sharp, biting taste” and, from the very beginning, “harsh, cruel, painful” — the figurative sense is present in the oldest verse. The word is built on the same root as the verb to bite: Proto-Germanic *bitraz is literally “that which bites,” a vivid gustatory etymology in which the tongue experiences the taste as a bite. Behind that stands the great Indo-European root *bʰeyd-, “to split, cleave, divide,” which in Sanskrit and Latin still means “split” but in Germanic specialised to the cutting action of the teeth — and from “the taste that bites” the whole history of the word follows.
  • Old English: biter covers taste and feeling alike — the bitter drink and the bitter (cruel) fate; the taste-to-feeling bridge is already complete in the poetry.
  • Middle English: the word is stable; “bitter tears,” “bitter sorrow,” “bitter enemies” are all attested, and the word takes on proverbial strength — the bitter with the sweet, the “bitter end” of suffering.
  • Phonology: the modern form descends from the Old English doubling and shortening of the medial consonant (biter → bittor → bitter); the spelling -tt- and the short vowel are fixed in the 15th–16th centuries.
  • 18th–19th centuries: the physical sense gains technical uses — bitter as one of the four basic tastes in physiology, and “bitter” as the name of a style of brown pale ale; the figurative sense continues in “bitter cold,” “bitter disappointment,” and political “bitterness.”
  • Modern idioms: “a bitter pill to swallow,” “to the bitter end” (the phrase’s nautical origin from the bitt, the anchor-post, is a famous folk-etymology — the "end" is the anchor cable, though speakers feel the sense of painful perseverance), “the bitter truth.”
The semantic continuity of bitter is almost a lesson in how gustatory metaphor works: a single sensation — the tongue bitten by acridity — has powered three millennia of meaning, so that today a political campaign, a divorce, a winter wind, and a pint of beer can all be “bitter” without anyone noticing the leap.
  • Gustatory science: modern physiology treats bitter as the body’s toxicity alarm — a warning taste for poisons — which is why bitterness is instinctively unpleasant; the emotional sense of the word simply imports that alarm: a bitter rivalry is felt as dangerous, poisonous, to be spat out yet swallowed anyway.
  • Proverbial depth: “the bitter with the sweet,” “bitter as gall,” “a bitter pill to swallow,” “to the bitter end” — the proverbs all build on the same bridge from the tongue to the heart, and the passage’s “bitter competition” stands at the end of that long tradition.
  • Phonology and form: the Old English biter developed a doubled consonant and short vowel in Middle English (bittor, bitter), fixing the modern -tt- spelling by the 15th–16th centuries, while the derived forms bitterly and bitterness were already established in Old English.
What the word contributes to the sentence is duration and corrosiveness: fierce would flare and rabid would rave, but bitter stays — a resentment that lingers and gnaws like a taste that will not wash away, which is precisely the quality the carvers’ lifelong rivalry requires. rabid — The word rabid is a Renaissance immigrant whose career shows a language reaching for a sharper instrument of insult. English borrowed it in the early 17th century (first attested 1610s) directly from Latin rabidus, “mad, raging, furious,” through Old French rabide. Latin itself had built the adjective on the verb rabere, “to rave, be mad,” with the suffix -idus, and the family included rabiēs, the very word that later became the modern medical term rabies. The Latin root is telling: rabere is the same base as rage, and the ancient Romans used the family of the raving dog — the mad animal that foams and bites — as their stock image of fury beyond reason.
  • Latin background: in classical Latin rabidus describes raging people and raving madmen; rabiēs names fury and also the disease of the mad dog. The animal image is present from the start.
  • 17th-century English: the first English senses are “furious, raging, violently mad,” close to the Latin; the word competes with native mad and raging as a learned, emphatic doublet.
  • Medical fixing: from the 17th–18th centuries rabid attaches specifically to hydrophobia — the rabid dog, rabid fox — so that the word becomes the clinical adjective of the disease rabies.
  • The modern figurative leap: in the late 19th and especially the 20th century, the word is loosed from medicine into everyday extremity: a rabid fan, rabid nationalism, rabid opposition. The image is of the mind bitten and maddened — the fanatic as a raving animal, and the frenzy as something contagious that spreads through a crowd.
  • Register today: rabid is emphatic and pejorative; to call someone rabid is to deny them reason entirely, picturing a foaming, infectious excess.
In the corrected passage “rabid” completes the picture of the carvers’ competition. Bitter gives the rivalry its sting and its corrosive residue; rabid gives it the froth and the madness — two diseases of the soul, the one gnawing, the other raving, fused into a single fierce and almost insane contest over wooden sculpture.
  • Medical exactness in context: because the passage is about men whose days of love have guttered, the medical layer of rabid is not irrelevant — it pictures a burning that has become pathological, a passion that has turned into a wasting, biting disease, so the rivalry is not merely strong but sick.
  • Register and extremity: rabid stands at the top of the intensity scale; below it sit fanatical (devoted), fierce (violent), and furious (enraged) — and the sentence pairs it with bitter precisely because rabid supplies the frenzy that bitter alone lacks.
  • Literary afterlife: from the mad dog of fable to the modern rabid fan, the word has never lost the image of the foaming, infectious animal; even in casual use it hints that the intensity spreads from mind to mind like a disease through a crowd.
Together the two words make the competition a double disease: bitter corrodes from within like an acid taste, rabid consumes from without like a frenzy — and the craftsmen are left toiling at the one thing their burned-out days still allow.
