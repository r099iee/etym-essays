oblivion (noun) — /əblˈɪviən/ — the state of being completely forgotten, unknown, or effaced from memory; the condition of having passed out of recollection or notice; and, by extension, a state of unconsciousness or dreamless forgetfulness of the world, or the total destruction that leaves nothing behind.
  • Primary sense — the state of being forgotten. To “fall into oblivion” is to be erased from the memory and record of others: a forgotten civilisation, a vanished writer, a name no one remembers. This is the word’s core, and it is distinct from mere obscurity: something in obscurity is still known to some, whereas oblivion is total, an absence of memory. In the card’s passage — “Turjan closed the book, forcing the spell back into oblivion” — the word names the nowhere from which the spell came and to which it returns, a void of non-remembrance.
  • Secondary sense — unconsciousness, dreamless sleep. From the forgetting of memory the word extends to the forgetfulness of the self: “sank into the oblivion of sleep,” “drink one’s way into oblivion.” Here the subject is not forgotten but oblivious — unaware, senseless, absent to the world. This sense is a natural bridge from the active verb (to forget) to a passive condition (to be forgotten, to be unaware).
  • Tertiary sense — destruction, annihilation. In modern usage, especially in science fiction, fantasy, and disaster writing, oblivion means utter destruction: “the city was blasted into oblivion,” “the ship was swallowed into oblivion.” The bridge is logical: that which is destroyed is forgotten because it no longer exists; total destruction and total forgetting converge on the same nothing. (In gaming and fantasy the word even became a proper name — a realm, a sword, an Elder Scrolls plane — as a shorthand for a place of erasure.)
  • Legal and political sense — official amnesty. Historically, “an Act of Oblivion” was a statute that wiped the memory of past offences from the public record — most famously the English Indemnity and Oblivion Act of 1660, which pardoned those who had fought against the restored monarchy. Here forgetting becomes institutional, deliberate, and civic: the state chooses not to remember, and by erasure grants peace. This sense, now chiefly historical, shows the word’s capacity for official, quasi-sacramental weight.
  • Semantic bridge — how the senses developed. Latin oblivio meant “forgetfulness,” the condition of the one who forgets; the passive application — “the state of being forgotten” — grew naturally, and by the 16th century English used the word for amnesty (the legal erasure of memory) and for the grand metaphysical dark in which the dead and the ruined pass from all record. The modern “destruction” sense completes the drift from process to state to annihilation: to forget, to be forgotten, to be nothing.
  • Register and boundaries. Oblivion is formal, literary, and grave. It differs from obscurity (still known by some), from amnesia (a medical loss of one’s own memory, not erasure by others), from unconsciousness (a temporary medical state), and from nothingness (metaphysical absence of being rather than loss of memory). The adjective oblivious deserves its own note: it has drifted from “forgetful” to “unaware, heedless” — “oblivious to the noise” — losing the grand sense entirely.
  • Related forms. Oblivious, obliviously, obliviousness, and the archaic verb obliviate (to blot out, to cause to forget — famously revived in the memory-erasing spell of the Harry Potter books). The Latinate dignity of the word makes it the natural choice for writers who want an atmosphere of finality and silence.
The semantic career of the word is a single smooth slide: from the everyday Latin act of forgetting, to the state of being forgotten, to the official erasure of crime, to the metaphysical void of the dead, to the total destruction of modern catastrophe — one continuous journey from verb to noun to void.
