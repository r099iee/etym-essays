shrivelled is the past tense and past participle of the verb to shrivel, and as a participial adjective it means wrinkled, shrunken, and dried up — drawn together and creased by heat, age, drought, or loss of moisture. In the source context, the dragon's hot breath "shrivelled the grass before the door": the blast withered, curled, and dried the grass instantly, scorching the dwarves within the tunnel as well.

  • Primary sense — desiccation and shrinkage: To shrink and wrinkle as moisture leaves a thing: leaves shrivel in autumn sun, grapes shrivel into raisins, skin shrivels with age or illness. The word names both the process and, as shrivelled, the resulting state of dried, contracted matter.
  • Secondary sense — withering by heat or dryness: To cause a plant or surface to dry up and curl — exactly the dragon's action on the grass. Frost, fire, drought, and salt all "shrivel" vegetation.
  • Tertiary sense — figurative shrinkage: Of non-physical things: hopes, ambitions, courage, reputations, budgets, and economies can "shrivel", losing vitality, substance, or scale, as if dried up. A "shrivelled" hope is one reduced to nothing.
  • Physical-bodily sense: Of living tissue in extreme states — the shrivelled limbs of the very old, the shrivelled flesh of a mummy, a shrivelled, sunken face. This sense is strongly associated with decay, starvation, and death.
  • Register and connotation: Almost always negative and often vivid. It suggests loss — of moisture, youth, vitality, hope, or size — and frequently carries a Gothic or grotesque flavour in fiction, where shrivelled flesh, fruit, or plants signal age, blight, or the supernatural.
  • Grammar: The verb is both transitive ("the heat shrivelled the grass") and intransitive ("the leaves shrivelled"); the participle shrivelled is used in perfect forms ("has shrivelled") and as an adjective ("shrivelled skin"). The spelling with a single l (shriveling) is American; the double-l forms (shrivelled, shrivelling) are British, as in the source text.
  • Domain uses: Botany and horticulture (shrivelled seeds, blighted fruit), medicine and gerontology (shrivelled, dehydrated tissue), meteorology and agriculture (shrivelled crops in drought), and food science (shrivelled, wrinkled produce).

The historical bridge: The verb shrivel is first recorded in the mid-sixteenth century with exactly the senses it still has — to contract, wrinkle, and dry up — and it probably formed as a frequentative on the same Germanic root that produced shrink: the idea of drawing tightly together. From the physical drying of plants and flesh, the word extended in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to the drying up of spirits, hopes, and ambitions, so that today the shrivelled object may be a leaf, a body, or a dream. Every sense preserves the core image: something full becoming small, hard, wrinkled, and dry.

Shrivelled vs. related damage: A thing that is shrivelled has been diminished by dehydration and contraction — the material draws in on itself and creases. It differs from burnt (altered and blackened by fire), from wilted (drooping from loss of turgor, usually reversible), and from withered (dried through age or lack of vitality, often with a sense of dying). Shrivelling is the specific, irreversible crumpling-and-creasing that comes when moisture leaves and heat or time presses in — which is precisely why the word is so apt for the dragon's blast in the source passage.
