TARNISHED is the past participle and participial adjective of the verb tarnish, used in the passage above of the once-bright silver tassel on Thorin’s sky-blue hood, which has lost its gleam. In its primary, physical sense, it describes a surface — most typically a polished metal such as silver, copper, brass, or bronze — that has grown dull, darkened, or discoloured as the result of exposure to air, moisture, or certain chemicals. Tarnishing is a natural chemical process in which the metal’s surface reacts with sulphur compounds or oxygen in the atmosphere to form a thin film of corrosion (silver sulphide on silver, copper oxides and sulphides on copper and brass). Unlike rust, which destroys iron and steel by flaking and pitting away the metal, tarnish is a surface phenomenon: it clouds, stains, and dims the shine, but the metal beneath remains largely intact and can usually be restored by polishing.

  • Physical sense (metals): the core meaning — a film of oxidation or sulphidation that removes the lustre from silverware, jewellery, brass fittings, buckles, or coins; e.g. "the tarnished silver fork".
  • Extended physical sense: applied by analogy to any object whose brightness, freshness, or gloss has faded — a tarnished photograph, a tarnished mirror, a tarnished gilding; the semantic bridge is the simple loss of surface shine.
  • Figurative sense (reputation): the dominant modern use — a reputation, name, image, or standing that has been "dimmed" or sullied by scandal, dishonesty, failure, or disgrace; e.g. "a tarnished career", "his tarnished legacy". This is a metaphor that transfers the metal’s loss of polish to a person’s or institution’s loss of public esteem.
  • Moral / spiritual sense: a person or quality regarded as "dimmed" morally — purity, innocence, honour, or integrity that has been besmirched; very close to sullied and tainted.
  • Technical registers: in metallurgy and materials science, "tarnish" is a precise technical term for the surface corrosion of non-ferrous metals; in numismatics, tarnish affects coin grading and value; in photography, a tarnished image is dulled by age or chemical degradation; in electronics, tarnishing of connectors can impair electrical contact.
  • Jewellery and silverware trade: professional care routines — anti-tarnish cloths, lacquers, and dips — exist precisely because tarnish is expected, everyday, and reversible.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word entered English in the late 16th century from French ternir, "to make dull, dim, or gloomy", which itself goes back to a Germanic word meaning "to hide, conceal" — the original idea being that a dull coating hides the brilliance of the metal beneath. The bridge from the literal to the figurative is a very natural and ancient metaphor: what tarnish does to silver — covering a bright surface with a dark film so that it no longer shines — is exactly what scandal or disgrace does to a person’s public reputation. The figurative use is first attested in English in the 17th century and quickly became the more prominent sense. In modern English, when someone says "his reputation was tarnished", the listener does not think of metal at all; the metaphor has become so entrenched that the physical sense is now the secondary, specialist use.

Register and nuances. Tarnished is milder than corroded or rusted: it implies damage that is mostly cosmetic and potentially recoverable. In reputation language it is gentler than ruined or destroyed — a tarnished reputation is damaged but not necessarily beyond repair. The word also carries a slightly old-fashioned, even romantic literary tone: poets and novelists use "tarnished glory", "tarnished gold", "tarnished dreams" to evoke faded grandeur or lost innocence. In the Tolkien passage, the tarnished tassel is a powerful image of fallen dignity: Thorin, the exiled dwarf-king, has been reduced by hardship so that even his silver ornament no longer gleams — the physical dulling of the metal mirrors the dimming of his royal magnificence.

  • Synonyms in physical use: discoloured, dulled, dimmed, clouded, darkened, tainted, stained, oxidised.
  • Synonyms in figurative use: sullied, besmirched, tainted, discredited, defamed, marred, spoilt, blemished, debased, degraded.
  • Antonyms: polished, burnished, bright, gleaming, lustrous, spotless, untarnished, pristine, immaculate.

Word-family relations. Tarnish (verb and noun), tarnished (participial adjective), tarnishing (process noun / participle), untarnished (the common antonym), and the rare noun tarnish meaning "the film itself". Note the spelling trap: the -ish of tarnish is not the pejorative suffix seen in babyish but part of the French stem.
