Single root word: Hermits (plural of hermit). 1. PIE *erē- 'loose, separated, alone' (reconstruction DEBATED — Beekes judges the deeper derivation of Greek érēmos unclear; Pokorny's *erē- is traditional and is flagged here as such; the secure record begins with the Greek stage) → Ancient Greek ἔρημος érēmos 'uninhabited, lonely, waste, desert' (attested 8th c. BCE; used by the Septuagint translators for Hebrew midbār 'wilderness') → Greek ἐρημίτης erēmítēs 'a dweller in the desert; a solitary' (érēmos + agent suffix -ίτης; Hellenistic period, 3rd c. BCE) → Late Latin erēmīta 'hermit' (4th c. CE; frequently heremīta, with h- under the influence of heremus 'desert', itself a borrowing of érēmos) → Old French ermite / hermite 'hermit' (10th–11th c.) → Middle English ermite / hermite 'hermit' (12th–13th c.) → Modern English hermit (16th c.; the h- was deliberately restored under Latinate spelling) → plural Hermits /'hɜːmɪts/ 'people living in solitude as a religious discipline.' NOTE — the only genuinely uncertain stage is the PIE root: the Greek stage onward is fully attested. The alternative doublet 'eremite' was borrowed directly from Latin into English as an archaic learned form of the same word.
