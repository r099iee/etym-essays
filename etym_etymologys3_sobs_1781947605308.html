AN ECHOIC WORD, BORN OF THE SOUND ITSELF Unlike most English words, sobs cannot be traced to a Proto-Indo-European root. It is an echoic — or onomatopoeic — Germanic formation, a word that mimics its own meaning: the broken, choked, convulsive intake of breath of heavy weeping. The short open vowel, the voiced sibilant onset, and the hard final -b together imitate the sudden catch, the swallowed gasp, the explosive sob of the chest and throat. Sound-symbolic words of this kind are among the oldest strata of language, and English has a whole family of them (hiccup, sniffle, snivel, whimper); sob belongs to that native, expressive layer rather than to the great Indo-European derivational families. GERMANIC NEIGHBOURS AND THE OLD ENGLISH SHADOW The word's Germanic pedigree is real even though its root is not reconstructable. The Middle English verb sobben appears around 1300, in the Cursor Mundi, and the noun follows. Scholars note a likely kinship with Middle Low German sabben and sabbeln, "to slaver, to mumble," the family of words for wet, broken, choked sounds, and a probable influence from the Old English verb seofian, "to lament, to groan, to complain" — attested as early as the ninth century — which may have helped shape sob in sound and sense. An Old English *sobb- itself is unattested, so the documentary record begins in Middle English; and an old fancy that the word came from Latin singultus, "a sob," is firmly rejected. The honest etymology is: a native Germanic echoic formation, its exact ancestry unrecoverable. FROM SOUND TO NOUN: A WORD'S SMALL CAREER Semantically, sob has moved from sound to emotion and back. In Middle English the verb names the convulsive gasping of weeping; the noun, formed by conversion, names the single gasp, and its plural sobs the whole racking series. The word thus did what many English verbs do — became a noun without any affix — a characteristically Germanic economy that contrasts with the Latinate lamentation or the formal weeping. For centuries sobs remained the plain word for noisy grief, at home in Middle English devotional writing, in Shakespeare (where characters sob and are "choked with sobs"), and in the ballads. EXPANDING THE EMOTIONAL SPECTRUM From the nineteenth century onward the word widened. The core remained the convulsive breathing of sorrow, but sobs of relief, of joy, and even of laughter entered the language — the respiratory spasm is the constant, and its cause may be grief or overwhelming gladness. Dickens and Hardy exploit the word for both pity and comedy; a "sob story" (attested from the late nineteenth century) turns the word's emotional power into a sly, ironic label for manipulative sentiment. The verb's transfer uses — an engine sobbing, a breath choked into sobs — keep the bodily image of irregular, gasping rhythm alive outside the weeping body. POTTER AND THE SOUND OF DESPAIR In The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902) the word is exactly chosen. Peter "shed big tears; but his sobs were overheard by some friendly sparrows": the tears are the liquid, silent grief, but it is the sobs — the audible, convulsive gasps — that carry the sound across the garden and summon rescue. Potter sets the noise of childish despair at the story's darkest point and turns it into the instrument of help. A word with no root deeper than Middle English, born of the very sound it names, thus arrives at one of the most famous rescue moments in children's literature — the broken breathing of a small rabbit heard by friendly sparrows.
