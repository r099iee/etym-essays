- Classical Antiquity - Latin febris ('fever') gives rise to the Late Latin adjective febrīlis in medical texts.
- Late Middle Ages to 17th Century - Borrowed into Middle French as fébrile and subsequently into English (c. 1650) as an exclusive technical medical adjective describing feverish symptoms.
- 19th Century - Victorian novelists and essayists begin extending febrile into psychological character descriptions, depicting nervous, hyper-stimulated temperaments.
- 20th Century - Widespread adoption by journalists, political commentators, and humorists (such as P.G. Wodehouse) to describe tense socio-political climates, volatile markets, and agitated human behavior.
- 21st Century - Continues as a refined literary and clinical term for overheated atmospheres and feverish physiological states.
