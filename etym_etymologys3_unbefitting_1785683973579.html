The biography of UNBEFITTING is really the biography of two English words joined by a hyphenless prefix, and its two halves have very different ages and very different histories. The ancient prefix. The negative prefix un- is one of the oldest elements of the English language, an inheritance from the deepest layers of Indo-European. Its ancestor is the PIE negative particle *ne-, "not," which survives, in one form or another, in nearly every branch of the family: as Greek a-/an-, Latin in-, and Sanskrit a-/an-, and in Germanic as *un-, built on the zero-grade form *n̥- of the particle. Old English un- was already fully productive: the Anglo-Saxons said ungefrǣg, "unheard of," unlæd, "unfortunate," unwēriġ, "untired," and the prefix has been adding negatives to English words without pause for well over a thousand years. Unhappiness is unhappy, unknown is unknown, and a thing that is not fitting is unbefitting. The prefix is so natural that speakers rarely stop to think of its age; in fact it is far older than the word fit on which it is attached, and older than the word befitting from which the compound is built. The obscure base. The interesting history lies in the base word fit, whose origins are genuinely obscure. The adjective fit, meaning "suited, apt, proper," first appears in northern Middle English around 1400, and etymologists have argued about its source ever since. The leading hypothesis, favoured by the OED, derives it from Old Norse fit, "the webbing of a water-bird's foot," the notion being that a webbed, knitted, plaited thing is "well put together" and hence "well suited." A rival theory proposes a loan from the Low Countries: Middle Dutch vitten, "to suit, befit," picked up through the maritime and commercial contacts of the North Sea world. Neither is proven. What can be said with confidence is that the word has no established PIE ancestry on either account, and that older attempts to connect it with Latin aptus or with the PIE root *ped-, "foot," have been rejected by modern scholarship. Fit stands in English as a word of respectable but undocumented birth, in the company of such everyday orphans as lad and big. From fit to befitting. The career of the adjective is a story of steady grammaticalization. From the adjective fit English formed the verb fit, "to make suitable," around 1500, and from the verb came a family of participial and negated forms: fitting (1580s), unfit (1590s), unfitting, and befitting. The verb befit, "to suit, be fitting for," was coined in the 1610s with the Germanic prefix be-, and its participle befitting quickly became an adjective meaning "suitable, proper, becoming." The word was born into the language of rank and decorum: to say that something was befitting was to say it conformed to the dignity of a person's station, and it clustered with the vocabulary of court, office, and honour — a gentleman's attire, a judge's conduct, a lady's speech. The compound is born. Adding the negative un- to befitting produced unbefitting within the same generation, in the early seventeenth century. From the start the word carried the social-moral charge of its base: it did not mean merely "not practical" but "not proper to a person's rank or an occasion," a breach of seemliness and decorum. In the card's sentence — "It was thought unbefitting for a man of his rank to appear at court in such plain attire" — the word lives exactly where it was born: in the world of the early modern court, where dress was conduct, where a man's plain attire could be read as a slight to his station, and where the King's cold displeasure was the sentence the word described. The word's whole history — the ancient Germanic negative, the Norse- or Dutch-born adjective, the Elizabethan participles, and the hierarchical decorum of the seventeenth-century court — is packed into that single judgement. From the court to the drawing-room to the dictionary. For the next two centuries unbefitting served the literature of manners, sermon, and satire, censuring everything from a clergyman's levity to a lady's familiarity. In the nineteenth century it remained a live, if formal, word, at home in the prose of moral essays and historical fiction. In the twentieth century it retreated from ordinary speech, displaced by the blander inappropriate and unsuitable, and survived chiefly in literary, formal, and mildly archaic registers — the very registers in which the word is most at home today. The journey of unbefitting is thus a microcosm of English social history: from the ancient Germanic negative, through a medieval word of obscure birth, to the decorum-vocabulary of the early modern aristocracy, and finally to its modern life as a deliberately formal, quietly judgemental adjective.
  • PIE (c. 4500–3000 BCE): *ne-, "not," the ancestral negative of the family; also source of Greek a-/an-, Latin in-, Sanskrit a-/an-.
  • Proto-Germanic (c. 2500–500 BCE): *un-, "not," from the zero-grade of *ne-; the Germanic negative prefix.
  • Old English (c. 5th–11th c.): un- fully productive (unlæd, ungefrǣg); the base adjective fit has not yet arrived.
  • c. 1400: Middle English fit (adj.), "suited, apt, proper," appears in the north; origin uncertain (Old Norse fit "webbing" or Middle Dutch vitten "to suit").
  • c. 1500: fit (v.), "to make suitable."
  • 1580s–1610s: fitting (adj.), then befit (v.) and befitting (adj.) — the decorum-word of rank and station.
  • Early 17th century: unbefitting coined; "not proper to a person's rank or occasion," the court- and rank-censure word.
  • Modern register: formal, literary, mildly archaic; displaced in speech by inappropriate and unsuitable but preserved in the registers of historical fiction and formal essay.
