PIE *bheidh- "to trust, persuade, confide, rely upon" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; also the source of Latin fīdere "to trust," fidēs "faith," and Greek peíthein "to persuade," showing the root's dual trust/persuasion face) → Proto-Germanic *bīdaną "to wait, await, endure, trust in time" (the Germanic branch shifted the root's "trust" sense into the temporal domain, c. 1st millennium BCE) → Old English bīdan "to stay, remain, wait, await, endure" (a strong verb, attested from the earliest Old English texts, c. 7th–10th c. CE; cognate with Old Norse bíða, Old High German bītan, Gothic beidan) → Middle English biden "to stay, wait, remain, await" (c. 12th–15th c. CE; also the base of the prefix-verb abiden, whence abide) → Modern English bide "to wait, remain, stay; (archaic) to await, endure" (the bare verb recedes from everyday speech but survives in the idiom bide one's time and in literary use, from the 16th c. CE onward) → Modern English bides (third-person singular present indicative of bide) "waits, remains, stays patiently" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The PIE root *bheidh- is well established for the Latin and Greek branches (fīdere/fidēs/foedus; peíthein/pístis), and the Germanic shift of meaning from "trust" to "wait" is accepted in the standard handbooks (e.g., the semantic bridge is that one waits confidently because one trusts in the outcome). The precise internal shape of the root (with or without the laryngeal / aspiration contrasts) is a matter of technical reconstruction rather than dispute. The English weak past forms (bided) are secondary; the archaic strong forms (bode, biden) are attested in Middle English but died out, and are noted here only to record the word's true verbal history.
