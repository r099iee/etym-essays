ROUSE is a transitive verb meaning, first and foremost, to wake someone from sleep, or to stir a person, animal, or force out of a state of rest, idleness, or inactivity. In its figurative extension it means to provoke or excite a feeling, a passion, or an entire group into active energy, anger, enthusiasm, or alertness. It is a strongly dynamic, often sudden word: whatever is roused transitions sharply from a passive or dormant condition into an active one, frequently as the result of an external stimulus or a deliberate effort ('to rouse the crowd', 'to rouse suspicion', 'to rouse oneself').

Primary senses.

  • Awakening from sleep: to cause a sleeper to wake, or to wake oneself ('I roused him at dawn'). This is probably the most basic and concrete sense, and can apply to people, animals, or even circuits of attention.
  • Stirring into activity: to bring someone or something from a state of rest, apathy, or lethargy into vigorous or purposeful action ('she roused the troops'; 'a speech that roused the nation').
  • Exciting an emotion or feeling: to provoke, awaken, or intensify an internal state such as anger, fear, hope, or suspicion ('the news roused public sympathy').
  • Rousing oneself (reflexive): to make an effort to get up, act, or respond, often against one's reluctance ('he roused himself from his chair').

Secondary and tertiary senses.

  • Hunting/falconry (archaic): in traditional falconry, to 'rouse' a hawk was to shake its feathers or plumage, and the word originally described the bird's action of rustling and raising its feathers when disturbed or about to fly — the physical act behind the later figurative sense of 'shaking oneself into motion'.
  • Drinking (archaic - 'a rouse'): historically, a 'rouse' could also be a large quantity of liquor or a drinking bout, a carousal; this sense derives from the verb 'carouse' (> Italian carouso 'fully, right out'), and survives in phrases like a 'boisterous rouse'. This sense is now rare.

Semantic bridge. The core bridge uniting these senses is the idea of a violent or energetic physical disturbance — a hawk shaking its feathers, a sleeper being shaken awake — transferred from the physical realm to the mental and emotional realm. To rouse an emotion is to 'wake it up' and set it in motion, exactly as one wakes a sleeper. The drink sense branches off the shared root of 'stirring up boisterous energy', while the emotional sense has shaded into the broad modern meaning of kindling excitement or activism.

Register and usage. In ordinary modern speech 'rouse' is slightly more elevated and literary than plain 'wake' or 'wake up'; it carries an implication of energy, effort, or emotional intensity. It appears commonly in idioms such as 'rouse oneself', and adjectivally as 'rousing' (a 'rousing speech', 'rousing cheer'), where it means exhilarating or energizing. It is closely related to, and largely built upon, the verb 'arouse', which adds a prefix and is preferred when the meaning is awakening a feeling rather than a person ('to arouse suspicion' or 'arouse desire'). 'Rouse' more often takes a person, crowd, or animal as its object.
