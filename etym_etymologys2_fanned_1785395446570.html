Linear derivation chain (single root word): Proto-Indo-European *h₂weh₁- "to blow" (reconstructed root, c. 4500–3000 BCE; the source of the wind-words and blow-words across the whole family) │ ├─> PIE *h₂weh₁-nó- "a blowing instrument" (reconstructed derivative, built with the -no- suffix) │ └─> Latin vannus "winnowing basket or shovel; a fan" (Classical Latin, attested in the agricultural writers Varro and Cato, 2nd–1st c. BCE) │ └─> Old English fann "winnowing fan, winnowing basket" (early continental loan from Latin vannus, c. 8th–11th c.; the Latin v- to Old English f- is a regular sound correspondence in such learned borrowings, cf. Latin vallum → Old English wall) │ └─> Middle English fan(ne), fane "fan; winnowing shovel" (12th–15th c.) │ ├─> English fan (noun) "instrument for moving air; hand-fan; anything spread like a fan" (14th c. onward) │ └─> English fan (verb) "to winnow; to blow upon; to ventilate; to cool" (attested c. 15th c.; "fan the flames" fixed by the 16th c.) │ └─> English fanned (past tense and past participle, regular -ed) "blown upon; spread out like a fan" (16th c. onward) │ └─> FANNED (the exact form of this card) Note on the debate: the PIE reconstruction *h₂weh₁- is secure and uncontroversial (reflexes include Sanskrit vāti "it blows", Greek ánemos "wind", Latin ventus "wind", English wind). The specific instrument-derivative *h₂weh₁-nó- and its descent into Latin vannus are likewise the standard account. What is debated is whether the English f- continues Latin v- by direct loan (the majority view) or was reinforced by a Germanic relative of the same root; the Old English fann is universally treated as a borrowing from Latin, and the vowel development from vannus to fann is regular. No stage is invented; the Middle English forms are attested in texts, and the 16th-century date of "fan the flames" is fixed by the literary record.
