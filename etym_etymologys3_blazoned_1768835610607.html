blazoned — The story of blazoned begins not in English but on the battlefields and at the tournaments of medieval France, in the language of shields. The noun blason entered Old French in the 12th century meaning simply “shield, buckler,” and within a century or two it had attached itself to the fast-developing science of heraldry: the coat of arms painted on the shield, and the formal, almost algebraic language in which that coat of arms is described. A herald “blazons” a shield when he sets out its tinctures, ordinaries, and charges in their technical order — a coded description that lets a banner be read at a distance. The verb blasonner passed into Middle English in the 14th century as blasonen, alongside the noun blasoun, and the word has carried the memory of the shield and the tournament ever since.
  • Origin and dispute: the deep etymology is contested. The leading account derives Old French blason from a Germanic “torch” word — Frankish *blas-, from an Indo-European root meaning “to shine, burn, blow” — via medieval Latin blaso “torch,” the shield taking its name from its polished, shining boss. A rival account derives it from a Germanic verb of blowing and trumpeting, making the shield the thing proclaimed. The OED pronounces the origin uncertain; the route through the shining torch is the traditional favourite.
  • Heraldic fixity: in the 14th–16th centuries the word hardened into a technical term of arms — “to blazon” came to mean both to paint and to describe armorial bearings — and from the concrete noun grew the verb’s ceremonial core: display by formal colour.
  • Broadening to public proclamation: by the 16th–17th centuries, blazon broadened from shields to speech and writing: to blazon a name or deed abroad was to publish it, trumpet it, declare it far and wide, as a herald’s voice declared a knight’s arms. The verb thus straddles paint and noise, colour and fame.
  • Modern recession: in ordinary speech the word receded; today blazoned is the literary, archaic-flavoured survivor of this history, used of anything — a banner, a sky, a wall — that proclaims itself through bold, formal colour.
In the corrected passage the word does triple work. It is literal (the carvings are painted), ceremonial (they are raised to the dignity of armorial display), and social (their blazonry is “strange colour” — a private heraldry that proclaims identity while remaining illegible to the world outside). The whole history of shields, tournaments, heraldic description, and public proclamation is compressed into the participle, and it turns the peasant carvings into the banners of a silent people.
  • Modern stylistic niche: in present-day English blazoned survives chiefly in literary and journalistic flourish — “a sky blazoned with stars,” “banners blazoned with gold” — where the heraldic past lends the object instant ceremonial weight that plainer words like painted or decorated cannot supply.
  • The noun’s survival: the heraldic noun blazon remains in technical use (a “blazon” is the coded description of a coat of arms), so that the participle still stands within a living, if specialist, vocabulary of emblems and tinctures.
  • Intensity and reach: on the scale of display words, blazoned sits above adorned and embellished (which beautify) and beside emblazoned (which is its twin); it alone insists that the display proclaims something — a family, a claim, a people — through the formal grammar of colour.
It is this final property that makes the participle indispensable to the sentence: the carvings are not decorated, they are declared — and their strange, private colour-language is a declaration no one else can read, a banner raised by hands that will never enter the walls.
