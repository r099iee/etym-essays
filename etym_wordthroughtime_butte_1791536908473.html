- Medieval to Early Modern French - Denoted an earthen knoll, target-bank, or mound used by archers to stop stray arrows, later yielding the idiom for being the target of hostility.
- 18th Century - Borrowed by French-Canadian voyageurs and fur trappers in the Upper Missouri basin to describe the isolated, flat-topped hills rising abruptly from the prairie.
- Early 19th Century - Adopted into American English through the journals of Lewis and Clark and Western frontier travel accounts by Washington Irving.
- Late 19th Century - Standardized by pioneer American geologists (Powell, Gilbert) as a precise scientific term for a flat-topped erosional remnant narrower than a mesa.
- 20th–21st Century - Cemented in global popular imagination through Western cinema (Monument Valley) and toponymy (Butte, Montana) as an iconic symbol of the American West.
