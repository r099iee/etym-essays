- 15th Century (Late Middle English): Habituated, conditioned, or formed into a customary habit - Entered English through Anglo-Norman acustumer, describing personal training and established social customs.
- 16th – 17th Century (Early Modern English): Standard adjective for personal familiarity and established routine - Extensively employed in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and legal texts to describe longstanding practices.
- 18th – 19th Century (Victorian Era): Established marker of character, social status, and domestic routine - Ubiquitous in Victorian fiction (e.g. Doyle's "the countess was accustomed to keep her jewel") to signify ingrained domestic habits.
- 20th – 21st Century: Widely maintained across formal and literary registers - Commonly appears in fixed grammatical collocations ("accustomed to [doing something]") and as a formal attributive adjective ("his accustomed seat").
