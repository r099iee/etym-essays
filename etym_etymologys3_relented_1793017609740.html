To relent is, etymologically, to un-slow by slowing: a paradox preserved in amber. The word's whole career is a long softening, from a Latin adjective for suppleness to an English verb for mercy, and its past tense relented always marks the moment hardness gives way.

Supple in Latin: Latin lentus meant pliant and tenacious at once: the willow that bends without breaking, the glue that holds by yielding, and by extension whatever is slow, lingering, or tardy. From this flexible root late Latin raised verbs of softening, the workshop words of tanners suppling hides and cooks melting fats. Nothing moral yet attaches: lentus is physics, the behaviour of matter under the hand.

Through French delay: In Old French the family settles around lent, slow, and its derivatives: alentir and later ralentir, to slow down or delay. French keeps the Latin sense of retardation faithfully to this day. English, borrowing across the Channel in the late 1300s, takes the softening half of the meaning and leaves the merely slow half behind: Middle English relenten first means to melt, dissolve, or thaw, the intensive re- strengthening a softening already present in the stem.

From thaw to mercy:

  • The physical root (late 1300s): snow relents into slush, salt relents in brine; the earliest citations are all of matter dissolving, the laboratory sense.
  • The inward turn (1500s): hard hearts relent, proud stomachs relent; Tudor writers discover that severity in men melts exactly like snow, and the verb acquires its moral centre.
  • The biblical seal (1500s–1600s): translators render the Hebrew of divine reconsideration as the Lord repented or relented, fixing the word to the highest possible subject: even judgement can soften.
  • The elements follow (1600s–1700s): storms, winds, fevers, and paces relent; whatever pressed hard and now spares inherits the verb, with a personified will behind the weather.
  • The forest (modern fantasy): when a haunted wood relents, as in the source sentence, the verb does its oldest and newest work at once: oldest, because malice melts like snow; newest, because landscape itself is granted intention.

Sound and form: The word never changes its skin much: relenten sheds its infinitive -en to become relent, and adds the regular -ed for the past. The long second vowel, -lent, does expressive work out of all proportion to its history: to pronounce relented is to stretch the very syllable of delay, severity prolonging itself even as it surrenders.

Why melting became mercy: The metaphor that built relent is one of the oldest in moral language: hardness is cruelty, softness is kindness, and change of heart is change of state. Frozen things break those who strike them; melted things receive the hand. English needed a verb for the exact instant when the blow is not struck after all, and it found it in the thaw: relented is the past tense of a rescue.
