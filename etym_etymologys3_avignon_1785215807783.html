**Avignon: a name older than Indo-European, on the Rhône** The place name AVIGNON has no Indo-European etymology in the strict sense; it belongs to the oldest identifiable stratum of western European place-names, the pre-Roman substrate of Gaul. Its history stretches from a prehistoric river-word to a world-famous papal capital to a philosopher's retreat, and every layer is still visible in the modern name. **The substrate and the river-word** The city stands on the left bank of the Rhône at its confluence with the Durance, on a site occupied since antiquity by the Cavares, a Gallic or Celto-Ligurian people. The name is almost universally analyzed as a formation on the hydronymic root *ab-, *abonā-, "river, water," a word of the pre-Indo-European substrate of western Europe that left its trace across the whole Celtic world: Welsh afon, Cornish avon, Breton aven, Old Irish ab and abann, and Scottish Gaelic abhainn all mean "river," and the same element underlies the many English and Scottish rivers called Avon as well as place-names such as Aberdeen and Aberystwyth, from aber, "river-mouth." Whether this river-word was inherited by Celtic from an Indo-European ancestor or absorbed from an older substrate is itself debated; what is certain is that it is the oldest element in the name. On the standard account, Avennio is a Latinized derivative of this root — roughly "place by the river" or "place at the watery marsh" — formed with a Celtic -iō suffix, a fitting description for a town that grew up where the Rhône and the Durance meet. A minority of scholars prefer to derive the name from a Gaulish personal name, *Avennius, with a possessive suffix, yielding "the estate of Avennius"; both explanations are reconstructions, since the pre-Roman forms are unrecorded, but the river explanation is supported both by the site and by the wider Celtic river-naming pattern. **Avennio in the Roman world** Under the Roman Empire the town appears in the historical record as Avennio. Pliny the Elder, in the Natural History of the first century CE, lists it among the towns of Gallia Narbonensis, and the Antonine Itinerary shows it on the road between Arles and Vaison. It was a civitas of the Cavares, a flourishing town of the lower Rhône corridor, the great highway of Roman trade between the Mediterranean and the north. As Latin gave way to the Romance vernaculars of the south, the name underwent the sound changes of Gallo-Romance and Occitan. Through the Latin accusative Avenniōnem — the oblique form through which most Latin place-names passed into Romance — the name became Old Occitan and Provençal Avinhon, with the palatal cluster -nh- that the Occitan of the region still spells in the Mistralian orthography as Avinhoun. When the palatal was absorbed into French, the -nh- became the -gn- of modern Avignon, so that the written form and the pronunciation of the modern city are the end products of more than a millennium of Romance sound change. **The papal century: 1309–1377** It was in the fourteenth century that the name entered world history. Between 1309 and 1377 the papacy, fleeing the turbulence of Rome, established itself at Avignon: seven popes — Clement V, John XXII, Benedict XII, Clement VI, Innocent VI, Urban V, and Gregory XI — and, during the Great Schism that followed, two rival claimants, ruled the Western Church from the Palais des Papes, the vast fortress-palace built on the rock above the Rhône. The period was long called, after Petrarch, the "Babylonian captivity" of the papacy. Avignon became the capital of Western Christendom, a city of curia, cardinals, bankers, and artists, and its name was fixed forever in the vocabulary of church history. Even after Gregory XI returned the papacy to Rome in 1377, the city and the surrounding Comtat Venaissin remained papal territory — a small sovereign state within the kingdom of France — until the French Revolution, when Avignon and the Comtat were annexed to France in 1791. **Mill's Avignon: the philosopher's sanctuary** In the nineteenth century the name acquired a literary identity that is the very opposite of the papal one. Harriet Taylor Mill, the wife and intellectual partner of John Stuart Mill, died in Avignon in November 1858 and was buried in the cemetery of Saint-Véran. Mill, inconsolable, bought a modest house on the edge of the city, near the cemetery, and spent the last thirteen years of his life there — he died in Avignon in 1873 — writing in the shadow of her grave. It was in this pleasant little house that he completed The Subjection of Women, Utilitarianism, the later editions of his System of Logic and Principles of Political Economy, and dictated his Autobiography, in which the sentence about his boyhood individuality appears. The name Avignon, for readers of Mill, means none of the papal pageantry: it means the city of his grief and his last, disinterested labours, "the pleasant little house where the happiest years of his life had been spent in the companionship of his wife." **The modern city** Today Avignon is a celebrated tourist city of Provence: the Palais des Papes, a UNESCO World Heritage Site within its medieval walls; the Pont d'Avignon — the broken Pont Saint-Bénézet of the children's song "Sur le pont d'Avignon," which stops in mid-rhyme over the river; and the Festival d'Avignon, founded in 1947, one of the great theatre festivals of the world. The name thus carries, in a single word, four distinct histories: a prehistoric river-name; a Roman town of the Cavares; the fourteenth-century capital of Western Christendom; and the nineteenth-century retreat of a grieving philosopher. No other small French city holds quite so many lives in one name.
