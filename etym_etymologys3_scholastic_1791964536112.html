scholastic begins with one of the most instructive semantic shifts in the history of European languages: the Greek word for “leisure” became the word for “school”.

Leisure and learning

Greek skholē meant “rest, spare time, freedom from business”, and it belongs to the same root as ekhein, “to hold” — a holding-back from work. In a slave-owning society, leisure was the precondition of philosophy, and skholē came to mean the activity that fills leisure: discussion, lecture, study. By the fourth century BCE it could denote the place where such discussion happened, and so “school”. Latin borrowed the word twice: once as schola, which gives English school, and once as the adjective scholasticus, which gives scholastic.

  • leisure — spare time, freedom from business.
  • learned conversation — the discussion that fills leisure.
  • a school of thought — the group who converse.
  • a school — the building or institution in which they meet.

Latin borrowed the word twice over: as the noun schola, which gives English school, and as the adjective scholasticus, which gives scholastic. The link between leisure and learning is therefore buried inside two English words that no longer look related. The Greek adjective skholastikos, “devoted to study, of a school”, passed into Latin as scholasticus, used of teachers of rhetoric and of learned men generally.

The medieval career

In the Latin Middle Ages scholasticus became an office as well as an adjective: the scholasticus or scholaster was the canon of a cathedral chapter who supervised its school. From the twelfth century the word attached itself to the new universities and to the masters who taught in them by the disputatious method that came to be called scholastica theologia. By the thirteenth century the scholastics had absorbed Aristotle, and by the fourteenth they had split into the via antiqua of the realists and the via moderna of the nominalists.

  • Twelfth century — Abelard’s Sic et non assembles contradictory authorities and asks the reader to reconcile them: the scholastic method in embryo.
  • Thirteenth century — the great summae of Aquinas and his contemporaries give the method its monumental form.
  • Fourteenth century — Ockham and Buridan push logic and natural philosophy towards the new science.

The humanist backlash

Petrarch had already mocked the schoolmen’s jargon; Valla attacked their Latin; Erasmus’s Ciceronianus (1528) turned the quarrel into a question of style; Rabelais and Montaigne made the pedantic scholastic a stock figure of fun; Bacon and Descartes treated the schools as obstacles to knowledge; and Luther’s Disputation against Scholastic Theology (1517) gave the attack a theological edge. Out of this century of polemic English acquired the pejorative sense: scholastic as a synonym for hairsplitting, verbal, sterile.

Into English

Middle English scolastic came from Latin and French and meant “of the schools, academic”. The modern spelling with -ch- is a sixteenth-century learned restoration after Latin scholasticus, matching the respelling of school and scholar. From the seventeenth century the word settled into two tracks: a hostile philosophical sense, in which scholastic names everything the new philosophy rejects, and a neutral institutional sense, in which it simply means “of schools”.

Modern divergence

The nineteenth century pushed the two tracks further apart. In Catholic Europe the revival of Thomism — given programme status by Leo XIII’s encyclical Aeterni Patris (1879) — made scholastic a respectable label for a living philosophical tradition. In the English-speaking world, meanwhile, the word became bureaucratic: scholastic records, scholastic year, and from 1926 the Scholastic Aptitude Test. The pejorative survived alongside both, available whenever a writer wants to accuse an argument of preferring technicality to truth.

Present state

Today scholastic is a three-sided word. In intellectual history it is neutral and precise; in school administration it is formal and slightly antiquated; in polemic it is an insult. The present passage exploits the first and second of these while quietly invoking the third, since the humanist argument it describes — judging scholastic treatises by Ciceronian standards — was itself the origin of the word’s bad reputation.
