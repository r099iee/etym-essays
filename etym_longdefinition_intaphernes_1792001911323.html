Intaphernes (Old Persian: Vindafarnā, Ancient Greek: Ἰνταφρένης) was an illustrious Persian grandee and general of the late sixth century BCE who played a pivotal role in the founding epoch of the Achaemenid Persian Empire. He is historically renowned as one of the elite circle of seven Persian conspirators who overthrew the usurper Gaumāta (the pseudo-Smerdis) in 522 BCE, thereby raising Darius I ("the Great") to the imperial throne of Kings. Beyond his military and political deeds recorded in stone on the famous Behistun Inscription, Intaphernes occupies a celebrated place in classical literature, ethics, and historical anthropology because of his tragic demise and the extraordinary moral choice made by his wife, as chronicled by Herodotus in Book III of the Histories.

The historical and thematic significance of Intaphernes is defined by several key aspects:

  • The Overthrow of the Magian and Imperial Elevation: Following the death of Cambyses II, a Magian priest named Gaumāta claimed the throne by impersonating Bardiya (Smerdis), the deceased brother of Cambyses. Intaphernes joined Otanes, Gobryas, Megabyzus, Hydarnes, Aspathines, and Darius in a daring plot to storm the royal palace in Media and assassinate the false king. During the bloody melee, Intaphernes suffered a grievous wound, losing an eye to a stray dagger or javelin stroke, cementing his reputation for steadfast valor. In the aftermath, the seven conspirators agreed on royal protocols granting them unprecedented privileges, including unannounced access to the royal presence at any time, provided the King was not in the bedchamber with one of his wives.
  • Hubris and Fatal Transgression: Not long after Darius’s accession, Intaphernes attempted to enter the imperial palace to speak with the King. Two royal attendants—the gatekeeper and the king's herald—barred his entry, explaining that Darius was in bed with his wife. Intaphernes, suspecting an insolent fabrication intended to diminish his aristocratic privilege, flew into a rage, drew his scimitar, and brutally mutilated the two royal officers, slicing off their ears and noses, stringing the severed parts onto his horse's bridle, and hanging it around their necks before releasing them. When the mutilated officers presented themselves before Darius, the King feared an imminent coup orchestrated by the seven conspirators. After privately questioning each of the other six nobles to ensure they were not colluding in the treason, Darius arrested Intaphernes, his sons, and his entire extended household, sentencing them all to execution.
  • The Famous Choice of Intaphernes' Wife: Plunged into catastrophe, Intaphernes' wife stationed herself at the palace gates, weeping and lamenting continually. Touched by her unwavering grief, Darius offered to spare one single member of her condemned family, granting her the choice. To the astonishment of the King and the court, she chose to save neither her husband Intaphernes nor any of her children, but rather her brother. When Darius summoned her and demanded to know why she had abandoned her husband and offspring to save a sibling who was less intimately bound to her household, she delivered the iconic philosophical reply:
    "O King, by God’s grace I may obtain another husband, and other children if I lose these; but since my father and my mother are dead, another brother can never be born to me. It was with this thought in mind that I spoke."
    Darius was so deeply impressed by her reasoning and filial piety that he granted the life of her brother and spared her eldest son as well, while carrying out the execution of Intaphernes and the rest of the household.
  • Anthropological and Intertextual Resonance: This famous episode is celebrated as a foundational text in social anthropology, cited by scholars such as Edward Westermarck, Will Durant, and Claude Lévi-Strauss. It demonstrates the ancient primacy of the consanguineal (blood/kinship) bond over the affinal (marital) contract in pre-modern societies. Moreover, the episode provided the direct literary source for Sophocles' tragedy Antigone (lines 905–912), where Antigone justifies her martyrdom for her brother Polynices using the exact logical syllogism spoken by the wife of Intaphernes.

In classical historiography and modern humanities, the name Intaphernes remains a powerful emblem of aristocratic pride colliding with imperial autocracy, serving as the historical canvas for one of world literature's most enduring explorations of kinship and moral priority.
