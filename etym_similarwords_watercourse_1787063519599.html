• Stream: Similar because both refer to a body of moving water; differs because stream is a general, natural term emphasizing the flowing liquid itself, whereas watercourse is a more comprehensive and technical term that encompasses both the moving water and the physical channel/bed (whether natural or engineered).
  • Channel: Similar because both denote a path along which fluid travels; differs because channel emphasizes the physical geometric conduit, depression, or navigational groove (and can apply to non-water media like data or radio), whereas watercourse is strictly hydrological.
  • Canal: Similar because both can refer to linear waterways; differs because canal strictly denotes a human-engineered, artificial waterway constructed for navigation, transport, or large-scale irrigation, whereas watercourse frequently denotes natural brooks, creeks, and rivers.
  • Aqueduct: Similar because both convey water across terrain; differs because aqueduct specifically describes a massive elevated bridge-like conduit or municipal pipeline engineered to transport potable water from a distant source to cities.
  • Brook: Similar because both describe flowing fresh water; differs because brook is a poetic, pastoral term for a small, shallow, natural tributary, lacking the technical, legal, and civil-engineering breadth of watercourse.
  • Culvert: Similar because both convey drainage water; differs because culvert strictly designates a transverse pipe or enclosed tunnel structure that carries water beneath a roadway, railway, or embankment.
  • Waterway: Similar because both denote paths for water; differs because waterway specifically emphasizes navigability for boats, ships, and commerce (such as rivers, lakes, and oceans), whereas a watercourse is often too shallow or narrow for transport.
  • Rill / Rivulet: Similar because both are running water channels; differs because rill and rivulet designate tiny, delicate trickles of water, whereas watercourse spans everything from mountain rills to mighty river valleys.
