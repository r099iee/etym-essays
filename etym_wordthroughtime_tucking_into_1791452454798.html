- 14th century - Tuck means to pull up or gather fabric into a fold; the word belongs to tailoring and household sewing, with no thought of food.
- 16th to 17th century - The verb widens to thrusting and covering: to tuck a letter into a pocket, to tuck a child into bed; the tone becomes domestic and affectionate.
- 18th to 19th century - The phrasal verb tuck into emerges in informal British speech, meaning to eat heartily; simultaneously tuck becomes school slang for sweets and treats, and the tuck shop becomes a fixture of boarding-school life.
- Early to mid 20th century - The idiom is fully established in comic and middlebrow fiction; Wodehouse can use it of a duck, and the register is warm, unpretentious and faintly humorous.
- Late 20th century - Tuck extends to cosmetic surgery (tummy tuck) and to athletic compactness (the tuck position), while tuck into remains a fixed British idiom for hearty eating.
- Contemporary - A cosy, slightly old-fashioned Britishism, still common in speech, cookery writing and light journalism, and readily understood abroad as a marker of British informality.
