ravenously is the adverb of ravenous, and its primary, everyday sense is "in an extremely hungry or predatory manner," carrying the force of overwhelming, animal urgency. In the passage from The Voyage of the Dawn Treader it modifies "hungry": Edmund "would have made much more fuss" about his hat being pressed into service as a fish-basket had he not "by now been so ravenously hungry." The nuance is that ravenous hunger is not ordinary appetite but a state of near-desperation that overrides vanity, dignity, and the instinct to complain; the tone is comic (a schoolboy's hat carrying slippery fish) yet edged with pathos, because the adverb reveals that Edmund's hunger is no longer a lad's peckishness but a genuine, gnawing need. Etymologically the word carries a buried predatory image: to eat ravenously is to eat as a wild beast that seizes and plunders its food — the adverb derives not from the bird raven (a popular error, for the bird-word comes from Old English hræfn and is unrelated) but from the Latin family of rapere, "to seize, snatch, carry off, plunder."

Secondary and extended senses:

  • Predatory intensity beyond food — applied to any appetite or desire so strong it feels like seizure: "ravenously curious," "ravenously ambitious," "ravenously devouring books."
  • Literal bestial application — most at home describing wolves, vultures, sharks, or famished predators: "the dogs fell on the meat ravenously"; the word keeps its 14th-century colouring of a plundering beast.
  • Rhetorical and literary intensifier — because it is stronger than hungrily or greedily, it functions as a dramatic, faintly archaic intensifier of desire, natural in narrative fiction rather than neutral conversation.
  • Survival and famine contexts — "ravenously hungry" is the standard phrase for hunger at the edge of human endurance, used in accounts of shipwreck, siege, and famine.

Synonym boundaries. hungrily is the neutral, unmarked adverb of appetite. greedily shifts the emphasis from biological need to excessive desire — wanting more than one's share is a moral failing, not an urgent hunger. voraciously (from Latin vorare, "to swallow") stresses the sheer quantity gulped down. rapaciously (from rapere, the very root of ravenous) stresses seizure by force, the plundering snatch of a predator. wolfishly makes the animal image explicit, and insatiably insists that nothing will ever satisfy. What distinguishes ravenously is that it fuses the two ideas of desperate hunger and predatory seizure — the ravenous eater is imagined as a creature that preys upon its meal. It is the strongest, most dramatic word of the set, at home in vivid fiction — exactly the force Lewis draws on for the famished Edmund.

Grammatically, ravenously is a regular manner adverb formed by adding the adverbial suffix -ously (-ous + -ly) to ravenous. It typically modifies verbs of eating and states of desire ("ravenously ate," "ravenously hungry"), and by metaphorical extension verbs of searching and reading ("ravenously turned the pages").
