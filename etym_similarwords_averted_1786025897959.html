• Prevented: Similar because both mean stopping an undesirable event from happening; differs because prevented is the general, neutral term for stopping anything at all, while averted specifically implies turning aside a looming, imminent threat, often at the last moment.
  • Avoided: Similar because both mean keeping away from something bad; differs because avoiding is often a passive keeping-away or steering clear, while averting is an active turning-aside of a danger already bearing down on one.
  • Forestalled: Similar because both mean acting in advance to stop something; differs because forestalling means anticipating and acting before the event begins, while averting emphasizes deflecting a danger that is already imminent and approaching.
  • Warded off: Similar because both mean keeping a threat at bay; differs because ward off is more defensive and protective (blows, evil, illness), while avert has a wider, more formal range and applies to eyes and thoughts as well.
  • Preempted: Similar because both head something off before it can happen; differs because preempt means acting first to make an event unnecessary or impossible, while avert means deflecting a threat already in motion.
  • Deflected: Similar because both involve redirecting something away; differs because deflect usually applies to physical objects (blows, balls, missiles) or to questions, while avert applies to dangers and to one's own gaze.
  • Sidestepped: Similar because both mean dodging a difficulty; differs because sidestep is informal, physical, and suggests maneuvering around a problem, while avert is formal and implies deflecting a serious danger.
  • Turned aside: Similar because it is the plain-language counterpart and shares the same spatial idea of rotation; differs because turn aside is an unmarked concrete phrase with no special formality, while averted carries a literary, preventive register.
  • Thwarted: Similar because both frustrate a plan or danger; differs because thwart implies obstructing an adversary's purpose through active opposition, while avert implies redirecting a threat without necessarily confronting an agent.
  • Looked away: Similar because both describe turning the eyes from something; differs because look away is neutral, everyday language, while averting the gaze is formal and often implies shame, discretion, grief, or denial.
  • Headed off: Similar because both mean intercepting something before it arrives; differs because head off is informal and often used of people being intercepted on a path, while avert is formal and used of disasters and dangers.
