• Herbert Spencer: similar because he was the British philosopher most closely identified with laissez-faire social Darwinism, the “survival of the fittest” formula and non-interference with market selection; differs because Spencer built a vast synthetic system of evolution, biology, psychology and ethics, was a professional philosopher rather than a university sociologist, and was far more optimistic about the long-run direction of evolution.
  • Thomas Robert Malthus: similar because his population theory supplied the “struggle for existence” that Sumner applied to economic life; differs because Malthus was a clergyman-economist writing about fertility, famine and checks on population, not a sociologist of custom, and he did not draw laissez-faire conclusions about charity in the same systematic way.
  • Auguste Comte: similar because both are canonical founders of sociology and both tried to make the study of society a positive science; differs because Comte coined the word sociologie, preached a secular religion of humanity and favoured social reconstruction, whereas Sumner distrusted central planning and described custom rather than prescribing a new order.
  • Lester Frank Ward: similar because he was Sumner's leading American contemporary in sociology and also wrote about the struggle for existence; differs because Ward was a defender of active social reform and state education, the mirror image of Sumner's laissez-faire, and is remembered as the father of American sociology's reformist wing.
  • William Graham Sumner: similar because the full name denotes the same man as the bare surname and is used when the surname alone would be ambiguous; differs because it is the complete personal name, more formal and specific, and it cannot refer to Charles Sumner or to a place.
  • Charles Sumner: similar because he shares the surname and is the other globally famous American bearer; differs because he was a United States senator and abolitionist orator, not a social scientist, and his fame rests on antislavery politics and Reconstruction rather than on sociological theory.
  • Émile Durkheim: similar because both analysed how collective customs and moral norms constrain individuals and both helped establish sociology as a discipline; differs because Durkheim worked through statistical studies of suicide and the concept of social facts, rejected individualist utilitarianism, and had no equivalent of Sumner's market apologetics.
  • Thorstein Veblen: similar because he was another Gilded Age American critic of received economic doctrine and wrote about custom, status and habit; differs because Veblen's tone was satirical and evolutionary-institutional, he attacked conspicuous consumption rather than defending competition, and he did not share Sumner's free-market politics.
  • Sumnerian: similar because it is the direct derivative naming Sumner's doctrines or style; differs because it is an adjective of interpretation, used almost exclusively in academic and polemical prose, and it foregrounds the ideas rather than the person.
  • Sumner, Washington: similar because it is the same word used as a toponym; differs because it denotes a city in Pierce County named for Charles Sumner, and its associations are civic and local rather than intellectual.
  • Summoner: similar because it is the occupational source of the surname and remains the only common noun in the family; differs because it denotes the court official who issued citations, a role and not a person's inherited name, and it carries Chaucer's satirical colouring.
  • Social Darwinist: similar because it is the standard classificatory label for Sumner's position; differs because it is a generic ideological category covering Spencer, Malthus, Kidd and others, is often pejorative, and does not identify any particular individual.
