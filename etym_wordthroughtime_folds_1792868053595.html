- Old English — two words side by side: falod, the fenced sheep-pen on the hill, and fealdan, to bend, wrap, and clasp.
- Medieval — the shepherd's metaphor: preachers make the pen the Church and straying sheep the sinners, so the fold becomes home, orthodoxy, and safety in one.
- 16th–17th c. — the crease takes over: printers, tailors, and geologists multiply folds of paper, cloth, and strata, and the verb's family outgrows the noun's.
- 18th–19th c. — landscape poetry: Thomson, Gray, and the Romantics discover folds of hills and downs, and the sheep-pen word becomes the painter's word.
- 20th c. — mathematics and poker: manifold, twofold, and to fold (yield, withdraw) carry the crease into abstraction and the card-table.
- Today — tenderness survives: villages still hide in folds, strays return to them, and arms still fold — the shepherd, the tailor, and the geologist sharing one bend.
