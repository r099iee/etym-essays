- Antiquity to Late Latin — The Liturgical Blessing and the Tombstone: Greek eulogía (‘good speech, praise’) is adopted by the Early Church for blessings; simultaneously, Latin writers conflate it with ēlogium (‘epitaph, tombstone inscription’), blending verbal praise with memorial architecture.
- Late 16th to 17th c. — Humanist Import into English: Renaissance humanists adopt Neo-Latin eulogium as an elevated rhetorical term for formal orations and scholarly discourses celebrating virtuous patrons and heroes.
- 18th c. — Neoclassical Zenith: Reaches the height of its popularity in Augustan English prose (Samuel Johnson, Edmund Burke); used as a stately tool for philosophical critique, balancing civic admiration against moral vanity.
- 19th c. — American Civic Oratory: Widely used by American statesmen (Thomas Jefferson, Daniel Webster, Edward Everett) in monumental addresses commemorating George Washington, Benjamin Franklin, and revolutionary heroes.
- 20th c. — The Ascendance of 'Eulogy': General usage progressively simplifies toward the vernacular doublet eulogy, relegating eulogium to academic, legal, and historical contexts.
- Contemporary Era — Deliberate Archaising Gravitas: Today employed almost exclusively as a stylistic archaism to evoke 18th-century rhetorical formality or to critique overly elaborate, theatrical praise.
