FORDED — verb, past tense and past participle of ford. Core meaning: crossed a body of water by wading through a shallow, passable place; walked across a river or stream on foot at a natural crossing.

In this line of travel narrative, forded describes the act of crossing a river on foot, by wading, at a shallow and therefore passable spot. The clause is precise about place and sensation: the crossing happens at a wide shallow place full of the noise of stones and foam, and forded gathers all of it — the wide, gravelly reach, the rushing water, the stones rumbling underfoot, the foam, the careful, wet progress of the whole company and its pack-ponies. The nuance of the verb is methodical rather than dramatic: to ford is to take the route that the river itself permits, to choose the natural shallow instead of building a bridge or finding a ferry. In the context of the wild journey it marks one of the ordinary, repeated hazards of wilderness travel — businesslike, physical, and slightly cold — while the sensory detail of stones and foam keeps it vivid. The preterite form gives the action the settled finality of a completed crossing: they got the river behind them, and the telling moves on.

Full polysemy.

  • The verb (primary sense here): to cross water on foot by wading at a shallow place; also, more generally, to cross any obstacle in the manner of a ford. The past tense is forded.
  • The noun ford: the shallow place itself where a river or stream may be crossed on foot or by vehicle; a natural, recognized crossing point. The noun is historically primary; the verb is built from it.
  • The place-name element (geographical and historical): ford appears fossilised in countless English place-names — Oxford ("oxen-ford"), Stratford, Hereford ("army-ford"), Bedford, Chelmsford, Ammanford — recording the shallow crossings on which the old roads depended. In this use it is a living memory of medieval travel.
  • Technical/geographical use: in geography and civil engineering, a ford is a recognised geomorphological feature, a shallow, usually firm-bottomed part of a riverbed where crossing is possible; fords may be engineered or improved but are distinguished from bridges and ferries.
  • Related and cognate words: fjord (the steep-sided Scandinavian sea-inlet, from the Norse cognate of ford); German Furt (in Frankfurt, Erfurt); the abstract idea of a passage across water runs through the whole family.
  • Figurative and extended uses (rare): to "ford" an obstacle or difficulty as a metaphor for surmounting a barrier by finding the easy crossing; a literary extension of the physical sense.
  • Derived forms: fordable ("able to be crossed by wading"), fording (the action or a place where crossing occurs), unfordable.

Semantic bridges. The noun ford is an ancient Germanic word for the shallow place where a stream could be crossed; in a land without bridges, the ford was a daily feature of roads and travel, fixed into place-names and shared vocabulary. The verb "to ford" is a denominal formation — built directly from the noun — and appears early; the Old English verb fordian "to go, pass, cross" attests the pattern, and the modern verb was re-formed in the early seventeenth century (1610s). The shift from noun to verb is the standard English habit of converting a place into the action performed there: one fords a river at a ford. The figurative extension — fording an obstacle as one fords a stream — grows naturally from the image of choosing the passable way.

Register and usage notes.

  • Archaic/literary: forded now has an old-fashioned, adventurous flavour, suited to saga, history, and wilderness narrative.
  • Technical: ford and fordable are standard in geography, civil engineering, and travel writing.
  • Place-names: the -ford element is common and betrays the old road system.
  • Distinction: to ford is to wade at a natural shallow; to ferry is to cross by boat; to bridge is to cross by a constructed span.
