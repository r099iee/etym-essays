• Feudatories: Similar because both denote persons or states holding land under feudal tenure and owing service to a superior; differs because feudatory is a precise legal and constitutional term emphasizing the territorial fief (*feudum*) and treaty conditions, whereas vassal carries rich social, literary, and metaphoric connotations.
  • Lieges: Similar because both refer to parties bound in feudal obligation; differs because liege is reciprocal and can refer either to the feudal lord (*liege lord*) or the loyal subject (*liege man*), emphasizing mutual feudal duty, while vassal strictly designates the subordinate status.
  • Retainers: Similar because both describe sworn followers in a lord's service; differs because a retainer is typically a domestic or household soldier/servant maintained directly on the lord's estate and payroll, whereas a vassal holds his own land (fief) with independent tenants under him.
  • Serfs: Similar because both were lower-ranking members in the medieval hierarchy; differs because serfs were unfree agricultural peasants bound to the soil with no noble status, whereas vassals were free men and often aristocrats/knights who held property and commanded their own troops.
  • Subordinates: Similar because both describe individuals under the command or authority of a superior; differs because subordinate is a neutral, generic modern bureaucratic term without the historical, legal, or military bonds of feudal fealty.
  • Satellites: Similar because both describe entities nominally distinct but completely dominated by a more powerful ruler; differs because satellite is used primarily in modern geopolitics (e.g., 'satellite states') to denote ideological and economic client states during the Cold War and beyond.
  • Client States: Similar because both describe political bodies subordinated to an external sovereign power; differs because client state is formal diplomatic terminology emphasizing strategic patron-client relationships without medieval oaths of fealty.
  • Thralls: Similar because both represent individuals subject to another's will; differs because thrall historically means an absolute slave or bondsman (from Old Norse *þræll*), conveying total loss of freedom and bondage, whereas a vassal was an honorable landholder bound by mutual contract.
