SHIRK is a word whose ancestry dictionaries describe with unusual caution, because no secure Proto-Indo-European root, and not even an undisputed immediate source, can be fixed. The most widely accepted hypothesis traces it to Early Modern German Schurke, "scoundrel, rogue, knave, villain," a noun recorded in the sixteenth century and itself of obscure origin. Some scholars connect Schurke to the Middle High German verb schürgen, "to poke, stir," ancestor of modern German schüren "to stir up, poke," the idea being that a rascal is one who stirs up mischief; but the link is disputed, and no convincing Indo-European ancestor has been produced for the word. An older theory that derived it from a PIE root meaning "to cut" — the root of English score and shear — is now generally rejected. The honest statement is that shirk begins, as far as the record shows, in the disreputable vocabulary of rogues.

The same German stock complicates the story through the English word shark. A swindler was called a shark in English by 1599, plausibly from the variant Schorck of German Schurke, and the name was later transferred to the voracious fish (by the 1560s, though the fish sense may be independent). The verb shark, "to live by one's wits," followed around 1600. English shirk enters the record in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries in exactly this company: the first sense of the verb (c. 1580s) was "to go evasively, to slink or sneak away," and by c. 1633 the noun meant "a needy, disreputable parasite," while the verb meant "to practice fraud, to live by one's wits." Whether shirk was borrowed from German directly or shaped by association with the existing English verb shark is still debated; the two words influenced each other and long remained intertwined in the sense of swindling.

The modern meaning grew out of the evasive, slinking sense. If a rogue sneaks away to avoid his debts and obligations, the natural extension is to say that he "shirks" his duty. By 1785 the verb had settled into colloquial use as "to evade one's work or duty," and the agent noun shirker appeared by 1799. The nineteenth century turned shirk into a word of moral judgment: schoolboys were scolded for shirking their lessons, soldiers for shirking their drills, and, most seriously, young men who avoided national service in wartime were accused of "shirking their duty" — a charge of cowardice as much as of indolence. The word carried that sneer through the twentieth century, when "shirkers" were stigmatized in both world wars, and it still does today.

Phonetically, shirk is a compact, emphatic monosyllable: the hissing sh-, the short -ir-, and the hard final -k give it a snappy, sudden, almost furtive sound, and it rhymes with work, lurk, jerk — a sound-shape that itself suggests something sneaky. The word never lost its informal, colloquial flavor; it lives in speech and newspaper prose rather than in formal academic writing.

In modern use the word has broadened and specialized at the same time. In ordinary speech it means avoiding any duty or responsibility out of laziness or fear ("to shirk work," "a shirker"). In economics and game theory it has become a precise technical term: "shirking" describes a worker who supplies less effort than contracted when his effort cannot easily be observed — the central problem of the principal-agent relationship in the theory of the firm. And in social and political rhetoric the old moral charge persists: "shirking one's duty" remains a phrase of accusation. All of this history explains why, in the goblins' song, Nor dare to shirk is so chilling: the word's whole past of parasitism, fraud, and cowardly flight is compressed into a single forbidden act, and the captives are warned that even the sneaking evasion the word describes will bring swift punishment far underground.
