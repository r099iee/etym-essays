- c. 1300s–1400s - Entered Middle English from Old French (*clarioun*), denoting a specific narrow-tubed, shrill brass trumpet used by heralds to sound tactical orders and cavalry charges in medieval warfare.
- 1500s–1600s - Expanded in Elizabethan and Jacobean poetry (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton) as an evocative symbol of martial valor, divine judgment, and the heraldic break of day (e.g., the cock's clarion).
- 1700s–1800s - Integrated into classical music terminology as an organ reed stop and as the high natural trumpet register (*clarino*); increasingly used adjectivally to mean piercingly clear, ringing, and brilliant.
- 1900s to Present - Evolved into a powerful political and rhetorical idiom (*a clarion call*, *the clarion note*) demanding urgent moral awakening, decisive civic action, or unshakeable resolve.
