CHAIN 1 — PRODDING (origin unknown — stated explicitly): Stage 1 — English prod, noun, "a pointed instrument, a goad, a pricking stick" (attested late 16th c.).
Stage 2 — English prod, verb, "to prick, poke, or urge with a point" (16th–17th c.); present participle prodding.
Stage 3 — Modern English figurative extension: "to stimulate, remind, nudge a person into action" (the modern dominant sense), and the 20th-c. concrete instrument, the electric cattle prod. Final node: prodding.
NOTE: The origin of prod is genuinely uncertain. NO PIE ANCESTRY can be securely reconstructed, and no cognate is attested in any other Indo-European language. The Norwegian dialect word prodda "to stab, poke" is often suggested as a source or relative, but the connection remains unproven, and the English word may be independently echoic of the quick jab it names. The chain is therefore deliberately short: it is drawn honestly, not padded.
CHAIN 2 — GOADS: Stage 1 — PIE root *ǵhei- (also written *ghei-) "to drive, impel, set in motion, hurl" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the probable ultimate ancestor of goad, though the Germanic link is DEBATED (Etymonline follows the *ǵhei- connection; the OED marks the origin of goad "obscure"). Cognates: Vedic Sanskrit hinoti, hinvati "he impels, urges, drives, sends" (the n-infix present); Ancient Greek hīēmi "to send, let go, release, hurl" (from *ǵhi-ye-).
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *gaidō "a goad, a pointed driving stick" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE), likely from *ǵhei- with a dental extension (*ǵhei-d-).
Stage 3 — Old English gād "goad, pointed stick; spear-point" (attested in Ælfric's glosses and in the pastoral law, 10th–11th c.).
Stage 4 — Middle English goode, gōde "goad" (12th–15th c.).
Stage 5 — Modern English goad "an iron-tipped stick for driving cattle" → plural goads (the iron-pointed sticks of the passage).
NOTE: If the *ǵhei- link for *gaidō is rejected, the chain collapses to the Germanic portion only (PGmc *gaidō → OE gād → goad). Latin's driving words (agere, agō) come from the unrelated root *h₂eǵ- "to drive," and no Balto-Slavic cognate of *ǵhei- is securely attested; sister-branch evidence is therefore genuinely absent, not omitted. Sister words of gād in Germanic include Old Norse gedda "pike (the fish)," lit. "the pointed one," named for its spear-like snout.
