1. An echoic birth in the 17th century. Unlike nearly every bird-word in English, the dove’s verb has no traceable ancient ancestry: no Old English or Middle English verb for the dove’s sound is securely recorded. The word appears in the 1660s as coo — the earliest dictionary quotations are in natural-history descriptions of doves and turtle doves — coined directly from the bird’s call: an onomatopoeic (echoic) formation, like mew (of cats), croak, chirp, or later moo. English had long possessed the noun dove but evidently felt the need for a dedicated verb of the dove’s soft “coo-roo”, and the 17th century — a period of brisk, plain, newly-coined vocabulary — supplied it. A useful contrast: the cuckoo’s name and verb were borrowed (Latin cuculus via Old French into English cuckoo, itself echoic), while coo is a native English formation from the living sound of the bird.

2. The phonetics of the imitation. The dove’s call is imitated with the velar stop /k/ and a long close back rounded vowel /uː/ — a low, soft, breathy murmur with no consonants at its heart. The reduplication “coo-coo” and the nursery “coochie-coo” (20th century) extend the same sound-symbolism, and because /uː/ is the “rounded” vowel of softness in English sound-words (compare murmur, soothe, croon, lull), the word was ripe from birth for tender transferred meanings.

  • 3. Extension to humans — the 18th century. By the later 18th century the verb has crossed from birds to people: sentimental verse and the novel use “to coo” of lovers’ and mothers’ soft talk. The transfer is sensory — the dove’s murmur is the acoustic image of gentleness, so tender human speech is “as doves speak”. The register is domestic and fond: a woman coos over her child; sweethearts coo in the alcoves of print fiction; and the participle “cooing” begins its long service as an adjective of voice.
  • 4. “Bill and coo” — the early 19th century. The idiom “to bill and coo” (billing = doves touching bills, cooing = murmuring) locks the word to lovers’ endearments; it is recorded from the early 19th century and became a Victorian and Edwardian cliché of courtship fiction — a slightly coy, stagey phrase that half-smiles at the lovers it describes, and thus the first hint of the irony that would later attach to the word.
  • 5. The Victorian sentimental peak. Through the 19th century “cooing” is the standard tender word for mothers, nurses and sweethearts; “the cooing of doves” is stock Romantic and Victorian nature imagery (the dove’s voice as the soundtrack of peace). The participle hardens into a full adjective — “cooing accents”, “a cooing tone” — soft, soothing, slightly mawkish, and the noun “coo” (“not a coo”) enters ordinary usage.
  • 6. The 20th-century spin: journalism and mild irony. In the 20th century “cooed” develops into a journalist’s speech-verb of adoration — “the crowd cooed”, “critics cooed over the film”. Because print loves exact speech-verbs, “coo” joins “gush” and “sigh” in the lexicon of effusive admiration; a knowing irony attaches, and to coo is now often to over-praise sweetly. In Britain the exclamation “coo!” (a shortening of “coo-ee”, from the Dharug-derived Australian bush call that entered English in the 18th century) keeps the sound-family growing at the edges, and the American baby-talk “coochie-coo” domesticates the word in nurseries on the other side of the Atlantic.
  • 7. Today. “Cooing” still names the rooftop dove at dawn and the parent at the cot; it is also the verb of fans, of social-media gush, and of marketing’s softest whisper. Its whole career — pure bird-sound → human tenderness → journalistic irony, all on one soft vowel — makes it a miniature museum of how English sentimental vocabulary is born, worn smooth, and gently mocked.
