- 1711 — maraud enters English from French, meaning "to roam in search of plunder" — first used in accounts of soldiers and bandits ranging the countryside during the wars of the early 18th century.
- 18th c. — marauding becomes standard military vocabulary for foraging and looting by roving troops; it carries the menace of the raiding party and the burning village.
- 19th c. — marauding acquires colonial overtones: "marauding tribes", "marauding dacoits", "marauding pirates" — the exotic raider of empire fiction and dispatches.
- 19th–20th c. — the word extends beyond humans to beasts: marauding wolves, foxes, even storm-winds; the register shifts from strictly military to broadly predatory.
- 20th c. — marauding enters fantasy and adventure prose — Tolkien's dragons, marauding invaders, marauding monsters — the raider becomes a creature of myth and story.
- 21st c. — marauding still means roving to plunder, but in news prose it is now as likely to describe "marauding mobs" or "marauding criminals" as soldiers; the colouring remains menacing, mobile, and destructive.
