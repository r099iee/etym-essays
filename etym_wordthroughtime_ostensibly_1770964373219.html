- Latin "ostendere" (to show, display) - Entered English in the 17th century via "ostensible", meaning merely "that may be shown, apparent, or plausible", a fairly neutral term for how things appear on the surface.
- 18th-19th century shift - Gains a skeptical edge: "ostensibly" comes to signal a deliberate, constructed pretext, a facade presented for public consumption while the real motive is hidden.
- Contemporary sense (apparently but not really) - Standard in journalistic, legal, and political discourse as the adverb of the unreliable narrator, implying scrutiny and doubt: things are "ostensibly" one way while a deeper truth is suspected beneath the surface.
