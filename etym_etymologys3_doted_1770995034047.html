The biography of doted is the biography of a clouded mind that learned to love. Its oldest attested layer is Germanic and physical: Middle Dutch and Middle Low German doten meant "to be silly, to be out of one's wits, to act foolishly", a verb for the unhinged, the dazed, and the senile. English took the word in around 1200 as doten, and for the first century and a half of its life it meant exactly this: to behave foolishly, to be weak-minded, to drivel with age. The deep ancestry of the Germanic root is debated - the most popular hypothesis connects it to the Proto-Indo-European root *dheu-, "to smoke, to cloud, to give off vapor", on the reasoning that a mind affected by "dote" is clouded, dimmed, or smoked over, in the same way that deaf names a clouding of hearing, dust a cloud of particles, and fume a cloud of gas. That link is plausible but speculative; what is securely attested is the Germanic verb family of foolishness itself. The turning point came in the 14th century, when English began to use doten of love. The logic is wonderfully transparent: to "dote" was to have one's wits clouded; to fall in love was proverbially to have one's wits clouded; therefore the person who loved with headlong, uncritical passion could be said to "dote on" the beloved. Chaucer and his contemporaries are among the earliest witnesses to this shift - the verb moving from senility to infatuation, from the mind that has failed to the heart that has taken over. For centuries the two senses ran side by side: dotage kept the old meaning of senile decay (a sense still alive, and still slightly comic, today), while the verb dote increasingly meant to be foolishly and excessively fond. Through the early modern period the word carried a distinct edge of censure. To dote on someone was to love unwisely - a doting parent was a parent whose judgment had been clouded by affection, one who spoiled and indulged to the point of folly. Moralists and novelists used the word to signal the dangerous excess of parental or romantic love, the way a whole household could be tipped off balance by one adored child. This is the sense alive in the card's context, where parents "doted on her, seeing in her their only hope and the sole source of their future happiness": the love is total, but the word quietly flags that their entire world has shrunk to one fragile human being, their judgment surrendered to devotion. In modern English the censure has softened almost to warmth. A "doting mother" or "doting grandfather" is now an image of adorable, pampering devotion, not of senile folly or dangerous indulgence. The old clouding-of-the-mind etymology survives only as a faint undertone of excess - the sense that doting is love with the judgment switched off, tender precisely because it is unreasonable. Doted today reads warmly: it describes the grandmother who buys every toy, the couple who live for each other, the parents whose only hope is their child. Beneath the warmth, if the etymology is right, there still glimmers the old image of a clouded mind - love strong enough to fog the reason, and sweet enough that we long ago stopped calling it foolishness.
