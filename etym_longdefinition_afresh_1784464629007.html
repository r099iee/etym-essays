Afresh is an adverb meaning anew; once more; starting over with a clean or altered arrangement. In the Bag End scramble, the dwarves set out everything afresh in the parlour: they do not merely put food down again, but re-lay the feast as a deliberate new order — trays and small tables reorganized under their own control. The word marks efficiency and takeover at once: Bilbo’s interrupted evening is wiped and rewritten as a coordinated banquet. The tone is brisk, procedural, slightly impersonal; afresh is the adverb of reset, not of apology.

Polysemy — all senses and uses.

  • Core sense — “anew, again from the beginning”: to begin something again, whether or not anything was wrong with the first attempt (“to start afresh”).
  • Sense of renewed condition — “in a new way, freshly”: the action is done over with a clean or changed state, not merely repeated; this is what distinguishes afresh from bare again.
  • Sense of altered arrangement — “in a different manner”: the re-doing involves a new order, arrangement, or perspective (“to set out everything afresh”).
  • Literary / elevated use: in older and poetic English afresh carried a lightly emphatic, almost ceremonial force — something not just done again but newly done, as if for the first proper time (“tears flowing afresh”).
  • Operational / creative use, modern: in project-management and creative language, “start afresh” implies abandoning the current state and beginning from a blank slate with a new perspective.

Historical and semantic bridges. The adverb is a late Middle English formation, not an inherited PIE word. Its first half is the native prefix a-, historically a reduced preposition meaning “on, in, to” (as in asleep, aside, aloft); English speakers had long used the analytic phrase of fresh (also on fresh) for “anew,” and by about 1440 the phrase fused into the single adverb afresh — “on a fresh footing, over again with newness.” Its second half, fresh, rests on Old English fersc “fresh, not salt (of water)” from Proto-Germanic *friskaz “fresh, lively, unspoiled,” reinforced after the Norman Conquest by Old French freis/fres “fresh, cool, new.” The adjective’s oldest English sense was physical — fresh water, water not yet salted — and from “clean, new, unstale” the adverb built its meaning of a clean re-start. The semantic bridge is transparent: physical freshness (unspoiled condition) became figurative novelty, and repetition plus renewed condition became “afresh.”

Register, collocations, and modern life.

  • Register: literary-to-neutral; slightly more elevated and emphatic than everyday again, but entirely natural in modern prose.
  • Collocations: “start afresh,” “begin afresh,” “set out afresh,” “afresh with renewed energy”; very often with verbs of beginning, laying, arranging, or writing.
  • Modern survival: common in narrative and instructional prose; used in computing (“start afresh” = reset), education, and personal-development language.
  • Jargon status: none technical; it remains a general, slightly formal adverb of renewal.

Synonym boundaries. Again is the general word for mere repetition, without afresh’s insistence on a new or clean state. Anew is close in meaning and register but is more literary and bookish. Once more stresses the recurrence of an event, not the freshness of the new arrangement. Over (“start over”) is informal and process-focused. From scratch implies building with no remaining base, whereas afresh simply means starting anew whatever exists. Afresh uniquely keeps the adjective fresh audible inside the adverb — newness of condition, not only recurrence — so the table is not only “again” but newly arranged, as if for a first proper serving.
