desponding (present participle of despond, used as an adjective) — In Macaulay's History of England (1848–61), the word names the inner surrender of hope that accompanies the false belief that society is in decay: he writes that those who compare their own age with "a golden age which exists only in their imagination may talk of degeneracy and decay," but "no man who is correctly informed as to the past will be disposed to take a morose or desponding view of the present." Desponding here is not a settled mood but an active process: a mind in the very act of giving up on the present. Core sense. To despond is to lose heart, to abandon hope; desponding therefore denotes the state or process of becoming disheartened, discouraged, or hopeless. As a present participle it emphasizes ongoing action or a prevailing disposition — "a desponding view" is a view taken from a position of already-relinquished hope. It is close to, but subtly distinct from, the modern adjective despondent: despondent describes a settled condition; desponding pictures hope in the act of failing. All senses and uses.
  • (1) Active participle. "The desponding soldiers" — soldiers in the process of losing hope. Emphasizes the movement of the mind toward despair.
  • (2) Adjective, attributive. "A desponding letter," "desponding language," "a desponding view of the present" — words, attitudes, or persons coloured by surrendered hope.
  • (3) Historical political and economic use. In 18th- and 19th-century prose, desponding regularly named public defeatism — pessimistic assessment of the state of trade, liberty, or the nation ("desponding speeches," "desponding calculations about the national debt").
  • (4) Noun use (rare, by ellipsis). "The desponding" — those who despair (mostly 19th century).
  • (5) Related word-family senses. Despondency (the noun of the state), despondence, despondent (the settled adjective, now dominant), despond (the verb). The verb is now chiefly literary; the noun despondency and adjective despondent carry the modern load.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word is built on Latin despondēre "to promise away, to give up, to resign," from dē- "away, down" + spondēre "to promise solemnly, to pledge." The concrete, almost contractual sense was to hand something over by promise: a father could despondēre filiam, betroth his daughter, "promise her away" in marriage; a man could despond a claim or a right, formally relinquishing it. The decisive step was the Latin idiom despondēre animum — "to give up one's spirit or heart" — used by Terence and Seneca to mean "to lose heart, to despair." The picture is of the animus (one's vital soul and courage) solemnly promised away and abandoned, like a bride handed over in betrothal. English borrowed the verb despond in the 17th century, and desponding as its participle kept exactly that image: hope not merely absent, but given away — deliberately let go. The bridge from contract to psychology is the ancient Roman habit of treating inner states as obligations one could formally surrender. Register and usage notes.
  • Register: literary and formal; desponding is now distinctly old-fashioned beside despondent, and functions best as an active participle ("desponding spirits") or in historical prose.
  • Collocations: "desponding view," "desponding mood," "desponding spirits," "grow desponding."
  • Near-synonyms by nuance: disheartened, discouraged, dispirited, dejected, despairing, disconsolate, forlorn — see similarWords for the boundaries.
  • Antonyms: hopeful, sanguine, optimistic, confident, spirited, buoyant.
