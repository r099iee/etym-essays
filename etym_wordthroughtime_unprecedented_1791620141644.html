- Late 18th c. - Formed in British constitutional and parliamentary rhetoric to criticize or defend official acts lacking established legal precedent; perceived as formal, serious, and legally momentous.
- Early to Mid-19th c. - Extended across the Industrial Revolution to describe explosive demographic growth, mechanical inventions, and unprecedented railway networks; acquired a connotation of awe, progress, and commercial dynamism.
- Early to Mid-20th c. - Heavily utilized in wartime dispatches and geopolitical diplomacy to characterize total war, mechanized destruction, and international treaties; held an ominous and grave societal register.
- Late 20th c. - Broadened into scientific, ecological, and economic analysis (e.g., climate change, financial bubbles), standardizing into a serious academic and journalistic quantifier.
- 21st c. to Present - Overused in 24-hour digital media, corporate press releases, and pandemic public messaging; suffers from severe semantic bleaching, often perceived ironically or with weary cynicism as a cliché for any unexpected disruption.
