relinquish — single linear chain: - Proto-Indo-European *leikʷ- (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'to leave behind, to remain' (also the source of Sanskrit riṇákti 'he leaves', Greek leípō 'to leave', Latin linquō 'to leave') - Latin linquō (3rd c. BCE–, attested) — 'to leave' - Latin relinquō (2nd c. BCE–, attested; re-, 'back', + linquō) — 'to leave behind, to leave, to abandon, to give up' - Latin reliquō, relinquere — 'to leave behind'; the inchoative stem relinquiss- (Late/Vulgar Latin), 'to begin to leave behind', underlies the Romance continuants - Old French relenquir (c. 12th c., attested; also relinquir), with the inchoative -iss- stem relenquiss- — 'to abandon, to give up' - Middle English relinquisshen (15th c., attested; the -ish- marks the French inchoative stem) — 'to give up, to abandon' - English relinquish (16th c.–) — 'to give up, to let go, to surrender' - Modern English relinquish. Related derivations from the same PIE root: Latin relictus, 'left behind', gave English relic and reliquary; Latin dēlinquō, 'to fail in one's duty', gave delinquent; Proto-Germanic *laihwniz, 'a leaving', gave Old English lǣn, 'gift, loan', hence English loan and lend. Note the direct Romance-to-English path: the -ish- in the English verb is the fossil of the Old French inchoative -iss- (relenquiss-), so that relinquish means etymologically 'to begin to leave behind'.
