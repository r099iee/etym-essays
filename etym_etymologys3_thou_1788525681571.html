1. Proto-Indo-European patrimony and comparative philology. The English pronoun thou is a direct inherited descendant of the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European second-person singular nominative pronoun *túh₂ (or *tū). This pronoun represents one of the most stable and enduring core lexical items across the entire Indo-European language family. Direct cognates demonstrating identical semantic and syntactic function include Latin tū, Ancient Greek sú (σύ, Doric tú τύ), Sanskrit tvam (त्वम्), Avestan tū, Old Church Slavonic ty (ты), Old Prussian tū, Lithuanian tù, Old Irish tú, Welsh ti, and Armenian du (դու). In the emergence of the Proto-Germanic branch (c. 500 BCE), the initial voiceless dental plosive *t shifted regularly to the voiceless dental fricative *þ under Grimm’s Law, producing Proto-Germanic *þū (attested in Gothic þu, Old Norse þú, Old High German dū, Old Saxon thū, and Old Frisian thū).

2. Old English morphology and syntax (c. 700–1100). In Anglo-Saxon England, þū was the universal, invariant second-person singular subject pronoun across all genres of Old English literature, from heroic epic poetry like Beowulf to the legal codes of King Alfred the Great and the homilies of Ælfric. It governed regular verb agreement endings in -st or -s (e.g., þū eart "thou art", þū hæfst "thou hast", þū lufast "thou lovest"). Phonologically, the vowel was a long high back rounded monophthong /uː/. The pronominal system was strictly numerical: þū was used exclusively when addressing exactly one person, dual git was used for addressing two people, and plural gē was used for addressing three or more people. No social distinction or politeness modulation existed in the pronoun system of Anglo-Saxon England.

3. Middle English evolution and the T/V sociolinguistic split. Following the Norman Conquest (1066), the bilingual contact between English and Norman French introduced the Continental European T/V politeness system into England during the thirteenth century. Speakers began imitating the French use of plural vous as an honorific address to individuals of higher social rank. Consequently, in Middle English, ye/you became the standard polite address to kings, nobles, church prelates, masters, and strangers, while thou/thee was relegated to intimate family circles, close friends, or downward address to social inferiors, serfs, and children. In the fourteenth century, Geoffrey Chaucer masterfully utilized this contrast in The Canterbury Tales: the noble Knight is addressed as ye/you, whereas the Miller and lower-class pilgrims address one another as thou. Orthographically, Middle English scribes transitioned from the Old English runic thorn (þ) to the digraph th, producing variants like þou, thow, and thou.

4. Phonetic transformation and the Great Vowel Shift. During the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the English sound system underwent the Great Vowel Shift. The Middle English long high back vowel /uː/ in thou was raised and broken into a closing diphthong, evolving phonetically from /uː/ through /ou/ to modern /aʊ/. Simultaneously, the initial dental fricative, which had originally been voiceless /θ/ in unstressed positions, became voiced /ð/ in high-frequency function words, yielding the modern pronunciation /ðaʊ/.

  • The Elizabethan crisis of address: By the late sixteenth century, the use of thou between adult males had become so fraught with potential insult that its use outside intimate domesticity was considered fighting words. The legal record of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries contains numerous defamation suits sparked by one citizen calling another thou in public.
  • The King James Bible and the illusion of sanctity: In 1611, the Authorized Version deliberately retained thou to translate Hebrew attah and Greek su accurately. Over subsequent centuries, as thou vanished from secular streets, its survival in the King James Bible created the widespread modern misconception that thou was an inherently sacred, formal, and elevated word.
  • Oscar Wilde and Victorian fairy-tale prose: In The Selfish Giant, Wilde plays upon this exact intersection of sacred awe and ancient intimacy when the Giant kneels before the wounded child and asks, "Who art thou?", using the archaic pronoun to signal the epiphany of Christ.

5. Dialectal persistence and historical legacy. While standard English abandoned thou in favour of universal you by 1700, the pronoun survived vigorously in the regional dialects of Yorkshire, Lancashire, Derbyshire, and rural Scotland in forms like tha and ta. In linguistics, the history of thou remains the premier textbook case study of grammaticalization, pragmatic pronoun inflation, and sociolinguistic change in the English language.
