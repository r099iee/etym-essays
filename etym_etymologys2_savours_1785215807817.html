Linear chain for SAVOURS (from the PIE root to the third-person singular present of the English verb; note the "taste → know" doublet in Latin, which is not a stage of this word but a collateral development): Stage 1 — PIE: *sap- ("to taste, to perceive by the senses, to come to know"), an attested verbal root whose core meaning was sensory perception (taste), extended to intellectual perception. Stage 2 — Latin: sapere ("to have taste or flavor"; also "to be wise, to know") — the sensory verb, ancestor of both the taste-words and the wisdom-words; and its noun derivative sapor ("taste, flavor"), the direct source of the English line. Stage 3 — Old French: savour ("taste, flavor"), from Latin sapor via the Romance vernaculars (cf. Old French savor/saveur). Stage 4 — Anglo-French / Middle English: savour (noun) ("taste, flavor, smell"; figurative "reputation, savor"), borrowed into Middle English in the 13th century; and the derived verb savouren ("to taste, to have a flavor"). Stage 5 — Modern English: savour (verb) ("to have a specified taste or smell"; figurative "to smack of, to be tinged with a quality"; transitive "to enjoy, to relish"), third-person singular present SAVOURS; American spelling savor / savors. Note on debate: a connection of English sip ("to drink in small quantities") and sift to the same root *sap- has been proposed but is contested and uncertain — noted as possible rather than proven. The Greek word saphēs ("clear, distinct, intelligible") has also been connected to *sap-, but this too is debated and must be treated with caution. What is secure is the Latin line sapor → Old French savour → English savour.
