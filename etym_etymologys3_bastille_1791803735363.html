A word for building. Behind Bastille lies a verb, not a noun. Medieval Latin bastire and Old Provençal bastir mean “to build,” and from them French formed bastille, a fortified structure—originally perhaps a wooden siege tower, then a detached stone fort, especially one guarding a gate. The same family gives bastion (through Italian bastione) and the Provençal bastide, a planned new town. The derivation of bastire itself is uncertain. The standard account refers it to a Frankish *bastjan, “to bind together, build,” from Germanic *bastaz, “bast, the fibrous inner bark used for ropes and baskets,” and ultimately to a reconstructed PIE root *bhas-, “to bind.” That chain is plausible—binding and building are close crafts—but every link before the medieval Latin verb is reconstructed, and some Romance philologists prefer to see in bastire a native Latin or Mediterranean formation. The word's prehistory should therefore be presented as a hypothesis, not a record.

Architecture. In fourteenth-century French a bastille was a free-standing fortification, often square, often guarding an approach. When Charles V rebuilt the wall of Paris on the Right Bank after 1356, the eastern gate, the Porte Saint-Antoine, was protected by just such a work. Construction ran from 1370 to about 1383 under the provost Hugues Aubriot. The result—eight towers, a moat, a massive gate—was called simply la Bastille, “the fortress,” and later la Bastille Saint-Antoine. Its name was thus a common noun promoted to a proper name by eminence, the way “the Tower” names the Tower of London.

From gatehouse to prison. The military function decayed as Paris outgrew its walls. In the seventeenth century the fortress was converted into a state prison, and under Louis XIII and Louis XIV it became the instrument of lettres de cachet—royal orders that consigned people to confinement without trial. Its prisoners ranged from Voltaire, imprisoned in 1717 for satirical verses, to the Marquis de Sade, transferred out in early July 1789 after shouting to the crowd below. Conditions depended on money and connections; some inmates kept servants and ate well, others were held in cells. The symbolic meaning of the place, however, was fixed: it was the king's power to lock up whomever he pleased.

The fourteenth of July. In 1789 the fortress was garrisoned by a small force of invalides and Swiss, and its guns commanded the Faubourg Saint-Antoine. On 14 July a crowd—first a few hundred, then several thousand—demanded entry, mainly to obtain the gunpowder stored in the moat. Negotiations collapsed into firing, the drawbridge was lowered, and the crowd surged in. The governor, the Marquis de Launay, was seized and killed; his head and that of the provost of the merchants were paraded on pikes. The prison contained seven inmates: four forgers, two men held as insane, and one nobleman. The disproportion between the event and the building's reputation is one of the great themes of its historiography, and it is precisely why the Bastille works as a symbol: it had become an idea long before it was taken.

Demolition and relic. The fortress was pulled down between 1789 and 1790, and the stones were recycled—some carved into models of the building and sold as patriotic souvenirs, some used in the construction of the Pont de la Concorde. The site was cleared; a fountain of regeneration was erected in 1793 and later removed, an elephant monument was projected and a plaster model stood there until 1846, and the July Column was completed in 1840. The name survived the building, attaching itself to the square, the district, a metro station and, in 1989, the Opéra Bastille.

Meaning. Bastille has become a word for the thing it once was: arbitrary imprisonment, and the moment it was broken. English uses “Bastille Day” for the French national holiday, a phrase the French do not use; “storming the Bastille” is available for any symbolic assault on an entrenched institution. Carlyle's chapter and Dickens's novel ensured that the fall would be remembered as a scene—crowds, gunpowder, heads on pikes—rather than as a strategic operation. The building is gone; the name is not.

  • Name type: common noun (“fortress”) promoted to a proper name by eminence.
  • Core chain: build → fortified tower → the gate-fortress of Paris → state prison → revolutionary symbol.
  • Historical irony: seven prisoners at the moment of its fall, and a reputation for holding thousands.
  • Modern reference: the square, the opera, the national holiday, and a metaphor for tyranny.
