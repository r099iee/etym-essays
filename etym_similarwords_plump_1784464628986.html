• Drop: Similar because both verbs mean to let something fall; differs because drop is the neutral, general verb, while plump specifies a heavy, abrupt, blunt fall or deposit — weight and unceremoniousness are built into it.
  • Plop: Similar because both are sound-symbolic verbs of a soft, dull fall; differs because plop describes the light, flat sound of something small falling into water, while plump suggests a heavier, blunter impact and set-down.
  • Plonk: Similar because both mean to set something down heavily and unceremoniously; differs because plonk (chiefly British, colloquial) stresses carelessness and a clunking sound, while plump stresses the weight and the heavy thud of the fall itself.
  • Dump: Similar because both mean to deposit something without ceremony; differs because dump can mean to discard waste or to abandon a person, a broader and harsher range, while plump simply means to set down heavily.
  • Plunge: Similar because both involve a sudden, heavy downward movement; differs because plunge describes entering or falling into something (water, debt) rapidly and often intentionally, while plump describes a blunt, single impact or deposit.
  • Thump: Similar because both carry the sound of a heavy blow or impact; differs because thump is the sound or blow itself, while plump is the fall or set-down that produces a heavy, dull impact.
  • Chubby: Similar because both describe rounded fleshiness; differs because chubby is usually affectionate and applied to children or cheeks, while plump describes full, rounded fruit, cushions, or adult figures and is generally neutral or kind.
  • Stout: Similar because both describe a heavy, full build; differs because stout implies a solid, sturdy, heavy-set frame (and a dark beer), while plump emphasizes soft, rounded fullness.
  • Rotund: Similar because both describe a round, full shape; differs because rotund is a formal or jocular word for a sphere-like, often pot-bellied figure, while plump is the plainer, warmer word for rounded fullness.
  • Full-figured: Similar because both describe a generously rounded body; differs because full-figured is a polite, euphemistic term of modern fashion, while plump is the older, more direct adjective for rounded softness.
