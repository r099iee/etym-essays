The story of PALPITATING begins not in some distant Indo-European homeland but in the ordinary Latin verbs of touch and throb. Its root verb, palpare, meant 'to stroke, pat, touch gently, fondle' — a word of the physical senses, describing the soft repeated contact of a hand with a body. Beyond Latin its ancestry is genuinely obscure. The older handbooks, following Julius Pokorny and Calvert Watkins, proposed a Proto-Indo-European root *pal-, 'to shake or touch gently,' perhaps connecting the word with English feel; but the modern comparativist Michiel de Vaan rejects the connection on phonetic grounds, and the likeliest explanation is that palpare is simply onomatopoeic — the soft, patting 'palp' of a caressing hand made into a verb. Whatever its ultimate source, the word entered the record as a humble verb of touch, and everything palpitating has meant since grew out of that. The decisive step was morphological. Latin formed a frequentative, palpitare, by adding the iterative suffix -itare to the verb stem: where palpare described a single gentle touch, palpitare described a repeated, pulsating, hammering action — the heart that thumps and skips, a muscle that twitches, a wing that beats. The frequentative was the perfect vehicle for the heartbeat, and from the classical period Latin writers used it precisely of the heart and pulse. Medical Latin then generated palpitatus, the past participle 'throbbed,' and from that participle English borrowed its verb. The first recorded English use of palpitate comes in the 1620s, in the vocabulary of physicians, and palpitation followed immediately: the noun entered English in the same decade, naming the pathological racing of the heart that patients could feel but physicians could not yet measure. For the seventeenth-century doctor, palpitation was a real, diagnosable symptom of an anxious or disordered body — and its modern cardiological sense is the unbroken descendant of that first clinical usage. The Romance languages preserved the whole family throughout the medieval period: Italian palpitare, Spanish and Portuguese palpitar, French palpiter, all meaning 'to throb,' and Old French palpitation fed back into English as early as the fourteenth century. What is striking is that the figurative career of the word — its leap from the pulse to the passions — happened largely in English and French literary prose of the eighteenth century. As the novel of sensibility flourished, the palpitation of the heart became the stock physical signature of love, fear, and suspense: heroines felt palpitations, readers were made to feel them by sympathy, and the word acquired the charge of trembling excitement it carries today. Victorian fiction intensified the association: the nervous complaints of an anxious age — the palpitations of the governess, the invalid, the young woman in love — became almost a literary genre of their own, and doctors and novelists used the same word for the same thumping heart. The modern word thus holds three lives in one. In cardiology, palpitations remain the clinical term for a rapid, irregular, or abnormally forceful heartbeat felt by the patient. In ordinary literary usage, palpitating describes trembling excitement, suspense, and nervous anticipation — anything that makes the heart beat fast and unevenly. And in the widest figurative reach, the word extends to any rapid, pulsing, quivering motion that mimics the heartbeat: palpitating wings, palpitating lights, palpitating air. Tolkien's use in The Hobbit draws on all three at once. The narrator's aside — 'things that are uncomfortable, palpitating, and even gruesome, may make a good tale' — takes the physicians' word for a racing heart, charges it with the novelists' trembling excitement, and presses it into service as a category of narrative theory: the palpitating episode is the one that makes the listener's own heart keep the same wild time as the hero's, and that, Tolkien argues, is exactly what turns uncomfortable adventure into a good tale. A word that began as the soft patting of a hand has thus ended as a name for the very structure of suspense — a seven-century journey from touch to throb to the art of storytelling.
