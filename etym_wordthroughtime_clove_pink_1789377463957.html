- Late 16th Century (c. 1570–1600) - Coined in Elizabethan England (Turner, Lyte, Gerard's *Herball*) as an exact descriptive compound combining *clove* (describing its eugenol aroma) and *pink* (the notched Dianthus blossom); widely cultivated as *sops-in-wine* for flavoring ale.
- 17th Century (Stuart Era) - Highly prized in formal Jacobean and Caroline knot gardens; John Parkinson's *Paradisi in Sole* (1629) praises the clove-pink as the king of fragrant herbs; used extensively in apothecary cordials and bridal garlands.
- 18th Century - The noun *pink* transfers from the flower to become the standard name for the color pink in English (c. 1720); the clove-pink remains a staple of cottage floriculture even as horticulturists breed larger, double-flowered carnations.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Celebrated as the quintessential cottage garden flower in Victorian literature (including Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*); deeply embedded in floriography as a symbol of steadfast chivalric devotion and pure love.
- 20th Century to Present - Cherished in heritage seed preservation and organic gardening as an irreplaceable heirloom ancestor of the modern carnation, retaining its intense, uncorrupted, clove-like natural perfume.
