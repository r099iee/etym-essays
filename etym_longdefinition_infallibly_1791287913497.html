Infallibly is a manner and degree adverb meaning in a way that cannot go wrong, without error or failure, and by extension invariably, surely, as a matter of course. In the source sentence Because that is what it will infallibly do unless I get a cheque by next week, the speaker predicts a bad outcome with mock-doctrinal certainty: the Boudoir or the paper or the scheme will certainly collapse or embarrass unless money arrives. The comedy lies in borrowing papal certainty for a tradesman's deadline.

At full strength infallibly claims immunity from mistake: an infallible judge never errs, an infallible remedy never fails, an infallible sign never misleads. Weakened by everyday hyperbole, it simply means always, every time, without exception: he infallibly arrives late means he always does. Between those poles lies its Wodehousean use: worldly-wise prediction presented as law of nature, where human folly is the only infallible force. It modifies verbs of doing, happening and judging, and often pairs with will and would to forecast.

Grammatically it is a regular -ly adverb from infallible, itself in- (not) plus fallible (liable to err). It stands before the verb or at clause edge: it will infallibly happen, infallibly, he forgot. Collocations include infallibly correct, infallibly right, infallibly leads to, infallibly happens, will infallibly do, infallibly arrives on time and infallibly spots the weakness. Register is standard and slightly formal and bookish, with a learned, even theological flavour that makes it ripe for irony when applied to aunts, cheques and weekly papers.

  • Without error or failure (core doctrinal sense): In a manner incapable of being wrong, as in to guide infallibly. Bridge: from Latin fallere to deceive, via fallible liable to be deceived, negated to exempt from deception.
  • Invariably and unfailingly (weakened predictive sense): Always, every time, as in he infallibly complains. Bridge: what never fails in principle is observed to never fail in practice, sliding from capacity to frequency.
  • Certainly and inevitably (pragmatic contextual sense): Sure to happen unless prevented, as in the cheque sentence. Bridge: speaker's absolute confidence borrows infallibility to pressure the listener: pay up or doom follows by law.
  • Dogmatically and authoritatively (tonal nuance): Speaking as if beyond dispute. Bridge: ecclesiastical history where infallibly marked ex cathedra pronouncements; secular echo retains the tone without the theology.

To learn infallibly is to hear both the choir and the counting-house: a word forged to protect faith from error, now used to guarantee that bills will bounce and plans will flop unless cash intervenes. Its seriousness is its joke, and its certainty is usually about human certainty to fail. Mastering it means feeling the weight of doctrine behind a light adverb and recognizing when surely is meant as solemn law and when it is meant as worldly nudge.
