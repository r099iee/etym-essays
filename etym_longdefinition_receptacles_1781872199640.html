In Pater's aesthetic manifesto, receptacles is a carefully chosen metaphor rather than a dry synonym for "containers." Music, poetry, and "artistic and accomplished forms of human life" are called receptacles of so many powers or forces: they hold, and make available to the sensitive critic, so many virtues or qualities, as natural products do. The nuance is active and almost electrical—works of art are vessels charged with effect—not mere boxes that store inert stuff. The tone is elevated, philosophical, late-Victorian: the plural noun elevates criticism into a science of receiving specialized impressions from objects that already contain them. The word's root meaning, "that which receives and holds," is exactly what Pater needs: art does not merely resemble a container; it is fitted to receive, to hold, and to make available the powers or forces it possesses. Core sense and register. In general English, a receptacle is a container or vessel used for holding something—a thing, or a place, that receives and holds other things. The register is Latinate and formal, with technical uses in botany, plumbing, electrical engineering, anatomy, and building codes. It differs from container in carrying the etymological hint of "receiving" (from Latin recipere, "to receive"), and from repository in stressing the act and fitness of receiving rather than static storage. Polysemy and modern senses. Receptacle has branched into several distinct senses:
  • General or household sense: any container or vessel that receives and holds things—a bin, tray, box, or holder (e.g., a waste receptacle).
  • Electrical sense: a socket or outlet that receives a plug—the fixed part of a connection that "receives" the male prong (receptacle as the wall outlet).
  • Botanical sense: the floral receptacle, the part of the flower axis that bears the floral organs (the torus or the base that receives petals, sepals, stamens, and carpels); also the fruit receptacle in some plants.
  • Anatomical and physiological sense: a structure that receives and holds, as the bladder or a cavity is spoken of as a receptacle; also philosophical and psychological images of the mind or senses as receptacles of ideas or impressions.
  • Plumbing and engineering sense: in codes and manuals, a receptacle is a contact device installed at an outlet for the connection of a plug, and the term overlaps with "outlet."
  • Paterian aesthetic and philosophical sense: works of art as receptacles of powers or forces, virtues or qualities—the metaphor at the heart of the sentence on this card.
Historical bridge. All these senses descend from Latin receptaculum, "a place or instrument for receiving," formed from the perfect-participial stem recept- of recipere ("to take back, receive, take in") plus the instrumental/locative suffix -culum. The physical sense of a receiving vessel is original; the botanical, electrical, anatomical, and metaphorical senses are successive specializations of the same image of something designed to receive and hold. The Paterian use draws on the oldest figurative power of the word, already available in Latin, of a thing that is fitted to take in what comes to it. Collocations and usage. Common collocations include waste receptacle, floral receptacle, receptacle of dust, electrical receptacle, outlet and receptacle, receptacles of power, receptacles of force, receptacles of virtue (Paterian). Register splits sharply: technical and household English is mundane and precise; aesthetic and philosophical prose reclaims the Latinate noun for the metaphysical storage of sensation. The antonymic or contrasting field includes transmitter, emitter, source, generator—things that give out rather than receive.
