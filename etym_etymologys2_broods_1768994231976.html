PIE *bʰreuh₁- “to boil, bubble, ferment, swell” (c. 4500–2500 BCE) — a root of roiling heat and rising matter — with a sibling warm-grade form *bʰreh₁- / *bʰrē- “to warm, swell” → Proto-Germanic *brōdō “the warmed brood, the young hatched and kept warm” (from the warm-grade twin; c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Old English brōd “brood, offspring, the young hatched at once” (noun; c. 800–1000 CE) and the verb brēdan “to keep warm, cherish, nurture” → Middle English brod (noun) and broden “to sit on eggs, to incubate” (c. 1200–1500) → English brood (noun), and the verb brood “to incubate (of a hen); to sit brooding over a thought” (figurative from the 16th c.) → broods (third person singular present; modern). Debate/nuance: the handbooks differ on whether the bird-word derives from the warm-grade of the boiling root (bʰreh₁- “to warm”) or directly from *bʰreuh₁- “to swell” (the brooded young being the swelled, risen life in the egg); both routes are defended, and the modern form is secure whichever is chosen. Sister careers of the same root: Latin fervēre “to boil” → fervor, fervent, ferment; Greek phrúgō → Latin frīgere → Old French frire → English fry; Proto-Germanic *brewwaną → brew, *brudą → broth, *braudą → bread.
