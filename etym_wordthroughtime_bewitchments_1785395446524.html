- [Old English–Middle English: the literal spell] - Built from be- and the witch (Old English wiccian), 'bewitch' named the literal act of casting a spell: a witch or wizard binding a victim, so that the word always implied a charmer and a charmed, a doer and a sufferer.
- [16th–17th century: danger and delight] - The literal sense carried genuine fear of harmful magic, while a parallel figurative use arose for irresistible attraction — a woman or a sight could be 'bewitching', captivating the eye and mind as if by sorcery.
- [18th–19th century: the figurative takes over] - The pleasing sense ('bewitching' beauty, charm) flourished in romantic and poetic register, while the literal witch-sense receded into folklore, fairy tale, and stage convention.
- [Contemporary sense] - The literal sense is historical or folkloric; 'bewitchments' survives as a stately, dryly understated literary word for a repertoire of spells, enchantments, or shows of magical charm (as in Gandalf's studied art of fireworks and illusory lights).
