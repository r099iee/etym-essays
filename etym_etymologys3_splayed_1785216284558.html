Splayed is the past tense and past participle of the verb splay, and its lineage is one of the language’s clearest: at the head stands the Proto-Indo-European root *plek- “to plait, fold, braid,” a root of the hand, of interlacing fibres and folded cloth. From it the Italic branch produced Latin plicāre “to fold,” the frequentative-class verb that lies behind plica, complicate, and implicate. With the prefix dis- (“apart,” “in different directions”) Latin built *displicāre “to unfold, spread out” — the idea of things folded away from each other until they lie flat and open. Vulgar Latin carried this verb into Old French as despleier “to unfurl, spread out,” a word of banners, sails, and extended wings. English received the word twice. Through Anglo-French desplaier it kept its prefix and became display: originally “to unfold (a banner or document),” hence “to show, exhibit” — an early doublet created as the same French verb entered by different channels. The second borrowing dropped the whole unstressed initial syllable de- — a process called aphesis — giving Middle English spleien and then splay, “to spread out, extend,” recorded from about 1400. From the outset the two doublets divided the family’s labour. Display kept the figurative, social half of the meaning — to show, exhibit, flaunt — while splay kept the literal, physical half: the actual spreading of wings, limbs, cloth, and masonry. English has used splay ever since of unfolding cloth, of spreading wings, and of limbs thrown wide, and the participle splayed, attested from the fifteenth century, settled into its modern role for anything spread flat and broad — hands, feet, fingers, or collapsing timber. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word specialised professionally. Builders adopted splay for the outward sloping of walls, jambs, and embrasures — a splayed window-opening flares outward to admit more light — making the word a precise technical term of masonry. Anatomists and medical writers applied it to feet and legs turned outward (splayfoot), and heraldry preserved the oldest unfolding sense in the “splayed” or “displayed” eagle with wings spread wide. Key stages of the semantic history:
  • Ancient base: PIE *plek- “to plait, fold, braid”; Latin plicāre “to fold”; *displicāre “to unfold, spread apart.”
  • Old French: despleier “to unfurl, unfold, spread out” — a word of banners, sails, and wings.
  • 14th century — the doublets diverge: display (with prefix, “to show, exhibit”) and, by aphesis, Middle English spleien (c. 1400) “to unfold, spread.”
  • 15th century — the participle: splayed is attested for spread-flat limbs and structures.
  • 16th–17th century — architectural use: splayed walls, jambs, and window-openings; the word enters the mason’s lexicon as a precise term of angled widening.
  • 18th–19th century — anatomy and heraldry: splayfoot, splay-legged, and the heraldic splayed/displayed eagle; wide, outward-spreading, pressed-flat limbs.
  • Contemporary — feral and structural: in modern writing splayed connotes a body spread flat and wide, hands paw-like and open, or timbers giving way; a faintly disturbing, tactile charge.
Socio-cultural forces directed the specialisation. The building boom of early modern Europe gave the word its technical niche in masonry — the splayed embrasure became a standard device for widening arrow-slits into gun-loops and admitting light into thick walls — while the rise of natural history and anatomy made “splayfoot” and “splayed limbs” clinical terms. The heraldic tradition kept the pure unfolding image alive in blazonry, where the displayed eagle opens its wings against the field. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the literary and cinematic register darkened: a splayed body is now the stock posture of the fallen, the slain, or the animalistic crouching creature, and the word’s engineering sense — of timber splaying outward as a structure gives way — reinforces the image of something forced open against its natural shape. Tolkien’s splayed in The Hobbit gathers the whole history into one word: the crouching figure’s “flat hands splayed on the floor” are at once a feral paw-spread, an anatomical posture of turned-out limbs, and an architectural flare against the ground — the same hand-root *plek- that folded cloth in the Indo-European world has, four thousand years later, unfolded a creature against the cold stone of the goblin passage, pressed wide and flat in menace.
