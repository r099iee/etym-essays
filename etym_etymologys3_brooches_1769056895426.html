The biography of brooch begins in a place etymology usually cannot reach: the uncertain borderland of the Roman world. The Latin adjective broccus described a protruding, jutting thing — most famously teeth, "with projecting teeth" being its recorded sense in Pliny the Elder (1st century CE) — and its origin is disputed, with a Gaulish or otherwise Celtic-substrate source the most likely guess. Because no secure Proto-Indo-European root can be claimed for it, the whole family is best treated as a non-PIE, "unknown/isolate" root-core. What matters for the word's future is not the root's ultimate age but its meaning: "the pointed thing." Out of that jutting point grew the entire family of spits, needles, spikes, and pins. The Romance divergence. In Vulgar Latin the feminine *brocca hardened the idea into a noun for a pointed instrument, and the Romance daughters then specialised it in different directions. Italian brocco meant a shoot, sprout, or splinter — and from its diminutive broccolo came the green vegetable English knows as broccoli, literally "little shoots," borrowed in the 17th century. Spanish broche became a clasp or brooch; Catalan broca became a drill-bit; Medieval Latin broca meant a pointed stake. Each language took the pointed thing and pointed it at a different object. But the decisive line for English runs through Gallo-Romance: Old French broche meant a spit for roasting, a skewer, a needle — and, by a natural extension, the ornamental pin that a lady wore to fasten her gown, a pin being, after all, a small pointed thing with an honourable job. One word, two lives. The word crossed the Channel as Middle English broche (attested c. 1300), carrying both senses at once: the cook's spit and the lady's ornament. For centuries one spelling did duty for both. This is beautifully attested in Chaucer, whose Prioress in the Canterbury Tales wears "a brooch of gold ful sheene, / On which ther was first write a crowned A, / And after, Amor vincit omnia" — an ornament whose very name still meant "a pin." The semantic split came late and is written into our spelling. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries English re-spelled the ornamental sense as "brooch," reserving "broach" for the spit, for the piercing of casks, and — figuratively — for the broaching of a topic, so that today the jewellery and the piercing tool, once one word, are separated on the page while still sharing one ancestry. Sound and spelling. The divergence is visible even in pronunciation. Both forms carry the open vowel that rhymes with "coach," which is why modern "brooch" is /brəʊtʃ/; the spelling split keeps the family's history on the page. The plural "brooches" /ˈbrəʊtʃɪz/ preserves the palatalised /tʃ/, and the -es plural is the regular English plural added to the sibilant-final noun after its French borrowing, now assimilated to native morphology. The Victorian flowering. In the nineteenth century the brooch became a mass-produced sentimental keepsake — cameos, lockets, jet for mourning after Prince Albert's death, enamel and paste for every rank — so the word came to name the small personal ornament of every social level, from queen to governess. Meanwhile French brocher "to stitch" produced brochure, a "stitched" little book, so that even a modern travel pamphlet is, etymologically, a distant cousin of the royal brooch; and in modern engineering a "broach" is a toothed cutting tool for shaping holes — the pointed thing, still pointing. Cultural arc in summary.
  • Latin broccus "projecting, pointed (of teeth)" (Pliny, 1st c. CE; origin uncertain, plausibly Gaulish) — the pointed thing.
  • Vulgar Latin *brocca "a pointed thing, spike, spit" → Old French broche "spit, needle; ornamental pin" (12th c.).
  • Middle English broche (c. 1300) "a spit; a pointed pin; an ornamental pin" — Chaucer's Prioress wears one.
  • 16th–17th c. spelling split — "brooch" (jewellery) vs. "broach" (spit; to pierce; to open up).
  • 19th c. — Victorian sentimental keepsake; 20th–21st c. — archaeological standard term and the engineer's "broach"; the plural "brooches" in modern use.
Set the plural "brooches" back in the Narnian treasure chamber and the whole biography lights up: the pointed instrument of the kitchen and the workshop has been tamed into the small, personal jewel of the queen's wardrobe — an object of intimate ornament whose very name still remembers the spike it once was.
