The market Fair begins in Rome’s calendar. Latin feriae were the festival days — sacred, work-free stretches when courts closed, slaves rested, and countryfolk streamed into town. Because holidays gathered crowds, crowds attracted hucksters, and hucksters needed no second invitation, the feast-day became the market-day across the late Empire: Church Latin feria came to mean an ordinary weekday (days counted off from the Easter feast), while in Gaul the festive gathering hardened into the commercial institution of Old French feire. The sacred rest had given birth to shopping.

Medieval England seized the idea and chartered it. From the twelfth century kings granted fair-rights — lucrative monopolies on tolls and courts — to abbeys, bishops, and boroughs: Stourbridge near Cambridge became Europe’s greatest mart, St Bartholomew’s at Smithfield sold cloth and horses to the City, and hundreds of country towns held their yearly fairs on saints’ days. The fair had its own law (piepowder courts settling disputes before the dust left the traveller’s feet), its own calendar, and its own populace of chapmen, minstrels, pickpockets, and preachers. Alongside the trading grew the hiring fair — the mop or statute fair where farmhands struck yearly bargains — and the horse fair, the riskiest theatre of dealing in England. Cobbett’s Chertsey fair for horses, cattle, and pigs is this world in miniature: Surrey graziers meeting on a fixed day to set the prices of meat, milk, and muscle for the year.

The trading fair faded as railways, shops, and auction marts stole its business, but the pleasure fair refused to die: the booths, roundabouts (Savage’s steam-driven rides from the 1860s), menageries, and coconut shies detached themselves from the livestock and became the funfair, while the 1851 Great Exhibition reinvented the word upward as the world’s fair — industry and empire under Paxton’s glass. Meanwhile the adjective fair, travelling a wholly separate road from Germanic *fagraz (fitting, suitable) through Old English fæger (beautiful), spread from looks to justice to weather: fair play from the sports field, fair trial from the courtroom, fair-haired from the Norse complexion, fair copy from the scrivener’s desk.

  • Feast to fair: Roman feriae gather the crowds that merchants follow — holiness monetised by holiday.
  • Charter age: royal grants, piepowder courts, and saints’-day calendars make the fair England’s commercial parliament.
  • Horses, hiring, and Chertsey: the livestock fair as labour exchange and price-setting ritual of the farming year.
  • Steam and spectacle: railways kill the trading fair while roundabouts and the 1851 Exhibition reinvent it as fun and display.
  • The other fair: Germanic fittingness becomes beauty, justice, and clarity — a separate etymology sharing only spelling.

That Cobbett capitalises Fair is a last courtesy of the old printing-house, which honoured substantial nouns with capitals: his Chertsey fair stands honoured as honest commerce in a landscape he mostly condemns. From sacred Roman holidays to squealing pigs at Chertsey to the neon funfair, the market fair has kept one constant across two thousand years — people will always gather, gawp, and deal wherever the calendar gives them leave.
