The word goggling represents an expressive, phonaesthetic formation within the English language, reflecting the vivid folk-linguistic tradition of coining words whose sounds mimic physical movement, facial contortions, and optical gestures. Its trajectory from medieval eye-rolling to early modern staring and modern technical eyewear provides a captivating lexicographical case study.

1. Expressive Phonaesthetic Origins (*gog-):

Historical linguists (including the Oxford English Dictionary and Eric Partridge) trace goggle to an expressive, imitative Germanic/Celtic base *gog- or *gok-, signifying unsteady motion, swaying, rocking, or rapid turning back and forth. This expressive root is the source of several related English words:

  • Agog: From Old French *en gogues* ('in a state of mirth, eager excitement, or eager expectation').
  • Gog: Archaic dialectal English for a quagmire, trembling bog, or shaky ground.
  • Jog / Joggle: Shaking or moving with unsteady, repetitive jerks.

2. Late Middle English Emergence (gogelen & gogel-eyid):

The verb entered written English in the late 14th century as gogelen (formed with the frequentative Germanic suffix -elen / -le, indicating repetitive, continuous action). In its earliest attested uses (such as John Wycliffe's Bible translations and late medieval sermons), the verb meant:

  • To roll the eyes restlessly from side to side.
  • To look askew, squint, or suffer from strabismus. The compound adjective gogel-eyid (goggle-eyed) appeared in the *Wycliffite Bible* (c. 1388) in the Book of Leviticus to translate the Latin *strabo* ('squint-eyed' or having defective vision).

3. Semantic Transition in Early Modern English (16th–17th Century):

During the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, the frequentative verb goggle underwent a crucial semantic shift. While maintaining its sense of eye movement, it increasingly described eyes that were naturally protuberant, bulbous, or thrust wide open in extreme astonishment, terror, or foolishness. Playwrights like Ben Jonson and William Shakespeare used the term to mock pompous courtiers or terrified cowards whose eyes bulged in fright.

4. Derivative Nominalization (Goggles) and Victorian Fiction:

  • In the early 18th century, opticians and travelers coined the plural noun goggles (first recorded around 1715) to describe protective glasses with side-blinders designed to prevent the wearer from rolling or straining their eyes against blinding snow, road dust, or harsh sunlight.
  • In 19th-century Victorian literature, authors like Charles Dickens (*The Pickwick Papers*) and Lewis Carroll regularly employed the participle goggling to paint vivid, satirical caricatures of eccentrics, astonished clerks, and bizarre animals with protruding eyes.
  • In 20th-century fantasy literature, most famously in J.R.R. Tolkien's *The Hobbit* (1937), *goggling* became the classic descriptor for the terrifying, bulbous ocular stare of giant monstrous spiders trapped in the dark canopies of Mirkwood. Today, the word remains an evocative stylistic tool across high fantasy and lively narrative prose.
