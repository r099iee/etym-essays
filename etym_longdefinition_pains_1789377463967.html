Pains (used as a plural or mass noun, predominantly in the idiomatic construction to take pains or to be at pains) denotes the deliberate exertion of great care, diligent effort, conscientious trouble, or meticulous labor in order to perform an action properly, master a skill, or achieve an exacting standard of excellence. While morphologically the plural form of pain (somatic suffering or emotional distress), its primary figurative sense has functioned in English since the fourteenth century as a term for rigorous, careful endeavor, reflecting the ancient cultural recognition that genuine craftsmanship, scholarship, and moral duty require personal discomfort, sacrifice, and exhausting toil.

In the source text—Oscar Wilde’s satirical masterpiece The Devoted Friend—the word is delivered with devastating irony by the wealthy, self-righteous Miller: “‘Oh! they will come to you,’ said the Miller, ‘but you must take more pains. At present you have only the practice of friendship; some day you will have the theory also.’” Wilde exposes the monstrous hypocrisy of the Victorian bourgeois moralist. Little Hans is literally toiling to the point of physical collapse, giving up his food, his flowers, his time, and his labor for the Miller. Yet the Miller patronizingly scolds him for not taking enough “pains,” demoting Hans’s genuine self-sacrifice to mere "practice" and asserting that true virtue consists of high-sounding "theory"—a theory that the Miller uses exclusively to justify taking advantage of his devoted friend without lifting a finger in return.

The term encompasses broad semantic depth, specialized registers, and diverse domain applications:

  • Diligence, Craftsmanship, and Care (Idiomatic Exertion): In literature, academia, and the arts, taking pains signifies uncompromising dedication to detail, precision, and perfectionism. An author takes pains to verify historical citations; an architect is at great pains to balance structural integrity with aesthetic harmony; a watchmaker takes infinite pains with miniature escapements. It expresses a conscious willingness to endure tedium, fatigue, and frustration for the sake of quality.
  • Reward and Ingratitude ("For One's Pains"): In the common idiomatic phrase for one's pains, the noun carries a rueful, bitter, or cynical connotation denoting the inadequate, perverse, or insulting recompense received in exchange for one's selfless trouble and labor (e.g., "He tried to break up the tavern brawl and got a broken nose for his pains"). In Wilde's tale, Hans's ultimate reward for his pains is exhaustion and drowning.
  • Somatic and Physical Suffering (Medical Plural): In clinical medicine and daily life, pains functions literally as the plural of physical ache or distress, describing localized or migrating somatic symptoms—such as sharp shooting pains, dull arthritic pains, abdominal growing pains, or chest pains—that indicate injury, inflammation, or neurological pathology.
  • Obstetric Travail (Childbirth): In obstetrics and traditional midwifery, pains has served historically as a direct metonym for the violent, rhythmic uterine contractions of labor (often termed labor pains or the pains of childbed), reflecting the visceral physical travail of bringing new life into the world.
  • Legal and Penal Phraseology ("Pain and Penalty"): Rooted directly in the Latin legal heritage of poena, the singular and archaic plural survive in constitutional and statutory law, notably in bills of pains and penalties (legislative acts inflicting punishment upon individuals without formal judicial trial) and the solemn warning on pain of death (under penalty of execution).
  • Semantic Bridge: The historical bridge connecting physical torture or penal suffering (Latin poena) to conscientious effort (taking pains) lies in the medieval theology of work. In ancient and medieval thought, labor was viewed as the inherited penalty of fallen humanity (the curse of Adam). Consequently, performing hard, painstaking work was experienced as an act of suffering and penance. Over centuries, this concept softened from punitive suffering into the admirable self-discipline and voluntary trouble one undertakes to master a craft or fulfill an honorable obligation.
