Wisps is the plural of wisp, a noun denoting a thin, faint, filmy, or loosely gathered portion of something — most commonly a small drifting streak of smoke, mist, or cloud; a loose strand or tuft of hair; or a small bundle of straw, hay, or twigs. The unifying idea behind every sense is thinness and lightness: a wisp is something so slight and loosely held that it barely holds together, ready to curl, drift, or dissipate at a breath. In the source sentence, "wisps of smoke" rise from the nostrils of the sleeping dragon Smaug — the faintest possible sign of the fires banked within him.

The word's senses fall into three main families:

  • Small bundles of plant material. Historically the earliest and most concrete sense: a handful or small bundle of straw, hay, grass, twigs, or coarse fibre. A wisp of hay or tow was used to wipe a surface, to kindle or feed a flame, or to make a rough torch or broom — and this humble object underlies the famous will-o'-the-wisp, a swamp-light imagined as a torch or lantern carried by a deceptive sprite.
  • Thin drifting or loose material. A wisp of smoke, steam, cloud, mist, or fog; a thin curl or strand of hair; a tuft of grass, wool, or feathers. This is the dominant modern sense and the one used in the source passage. The stress falls on faintness, thinness, and evanescence: wisps rise, drift, curl, and fade.
  • Figurative and transferred traces. A wisp of a smile, a wisp of a voice, a wisp of memory, a wisp of hope — a faint, barely perceptible trace of something immaterial. Here the physical thinness of the object is transferred to intangibles, so that even an idea can be wisp-thin.

Register and connotation. Wisp is a delicate, atmospheric word. Its plural, wisps, is especially common in descriptive and literary prose for smoke, fog, hair, and cloud, where it suggests gentle motion and insubstantiality. In fantasy and folklore the word also connects to ghostliness: a will-o'-the-wisp leads travelers astray, and in modern fantasy and gaming a "wisp" is a spectral light or apparition — a direct extension of the old folk belief in deceptive marsh-lights.

Grammatical flexibility. Wisp also functions as a verb (to wisp, to curl or move like a wisp) and appears in compounds: will-o'-the-wisp, wisp-thin, wispy (adjective), wispiness. The adjective wispy — thin, light, insubstantial, especially of hair and smoke — is now more common in everyday speech than the bare noun.

Historical bridge. The semantic journey runs from a concrete, utilitarian bundle of straw (used for cleaning, lighting, or binding) through thinning smoke, mist, and hair to entirely abstract traces — a classic widening from the specific and material to the general and insubstantial, and a perfect word for the faint residue of a sleeping dragon's fire.

Origin in brief. Wisp entered English from Old Norse (visk, a bundle of straw or twigs) in the Middle English period and has since widened from that homely bundle to any thin, faint, drifting trace. Its plural wisps is today one of the stock words of atmospheric description, equally at home describing a curl of chimney smoke, a drift of morning fog, and the ghostly marsh-lights of folklore.
