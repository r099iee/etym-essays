- [15th century: the tool] - Paddle enters English from padell, "a small spade"; within the same century the flat, pan-shaped blade is adapted as a short broad-bladed oar.
- [17th century: the verbs split] - "To paddle" (propel a boat) and the child's "paddle" (wade barefoot in shallow water) diverge from the same image of short, repeated, dipping strokes.
- [19th century: the steam roar] - The compounds paddle-wheel, paddle-boat, and paddle-steamer (1843) make the word loud, mechanical, and industrial; "paddle your own canoe" (1840s) moralizes it into self-reliance.
- [19th century, chiefly US slang] - The flat instrument lends its name to punishment — to paddle someone is to beat them with a flat blade — the word's harshest register.
- [Contemporary sense] - The steam age fades; paddling returns to small, quiet craft — canoes, kayaks, and Gollum's little boat — where the verb means practiced, noiseless propulsion, "never a ripple."
