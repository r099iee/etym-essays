- [13th–14th c.] - Middle English borrows *bokel* from Old French *bocle*, referring primarily to the convex metal boss in the center of a shield, as well as functional metal clasps on military belts and equestrian gear - [perception: practical military and chivalric hardware; associated with knights, armorers, and saddlers]

- [15th–16th c.] - The noun expands into everyday civilian clothing, while the verb *to buckle* arises meaning to fasten armor, gird oneself for battle ('buckle down'), and physically warp or bend under impact - [perception: active martial preparation and leather craft; increasingly common in municipal trade guilds]

- [17th–18th c.] - Shoe-buckles emerge as the paramount fashion accessory of the European aristocracy and upper-middle class, manufactured in silver, pinchbeck, and precious stones to signal wealth and privilege - [perception: high elegance, aristocratic vanity, courtly prestige, and social distinction (as in Wilde's fairy tale)]

- [19th c.] - Shoe-buckles rapidly decline following the French Revolution in favor of egalitarian shoelaces, while the Industrial Revolution formalizes *buckling* in civil and mechanical engineering as the mathematical science of structural beam failure - [perception: industrial mechanics, civil engineering, and historical costume]

- [20th–21st c.] - Retains functional presence in vehicle safety belts, aviation harnesses, and outdoor gear, while metaphorically dominating idioms of focused discipline ('buckle down') and structural or emotional collapse under extreme duress - [perception: standard utilitarian fastener; universally understood metaphor for structural and psychological breaking points]
