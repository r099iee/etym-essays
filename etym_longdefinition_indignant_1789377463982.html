Indignant is an adjective describing a state of intense, righteous displeasure, resentment, or anger aroused by that which is perceived as unjust, unfair, insulting, base, unworthy, or profoundly offensive to one's sense of dignity and moral order. Far from representing petulant irritability or blind, destructive rage, indignation is fundamentally an ethically grounded emotion: the indignant individual reacts against a perceived violation of justice, equity, decency, or proper desert, feeling a dignified anger that someone has been wronged, cheated, demeaned, or treated with contempt.

In the source text—Oscar Wilde's satirical fairy tale The Remarkable Rocket (1888)—the word characterizes the vocal outrage of the secondary fireworks: "But the Roman Candle and the Bengal Light were quite indignant, and kept saying, 'Humbug! humbug!' at the top of their voices." Wilde crafts a brilliant social satire of offended dignity. Throughout the evening, the self-proclaimed "Remarkable Rocket" has dominated the conversation with outrageous vanity, condescension, and histrionic pity for non-existent tragedies, completely dismissing the dignity and professional pyrotechnic worth of the other fireworks. The Roman Candle and the Bengal Light, recognizing the Rocket's preposterous arrogance and intellectual posturing for the absolute fraud it is, become thoroughly indignant. Their shouts of "Humbug!" represent a desperate attempt by decent, honest fireworks to reassert truth and deflate an insufferable narcissist.

The adjective encompasses broad polysemic nuances, psychological layers, and social registers:

  • Moral and Ethical Displeasure (Primary Moral Sense): In moral philosophy and psychology, being indignant involves an appraisal of injustice. When citizens witness a corrupt official escaping punishment while a poor laborer is penalized, or when employees watch an honest colleague scapegoated for managerial errors, the resulting outrage is precisely indignation. It is an intellectualized anger sparked by the objective unworthiness (Latin indignitas) of the act, motivating calls for justice, reform, and moral accountability.
  • Offended Personal Pride and Dignity: In social dynamics and polite conversation, indignant describes someone whose personal honor, integrity, reputation, or social standing has been unjustly insulted or impugned. A respected scholar accused of plagiarism or a faithful partner suspected of deceit responds with an indignant denial. In this register, the emotion acts as a psychological defense mechanism designed to repel humiliation and re-establish self-respect.
  • Political, Civic, and Social Outrage: In journalism, political science, and civil rights history, indignant characterizes the collective response of populations subjected to tyranny, exploitation, or disenfranchisement. Mass protests, boycotts, and reform movements are routinely fueled by an indignant public conscience rebelling against discriminatory laws, economic extortion, or systemic cruelty. Political pamphlets and manifestos adopt an indignant rhetorical tone to rally citizens against unacceptable status-quo abuses.
  • Satirical and Theatrical Pretense (Mock Indignation): In dramatic literature and satire—such as the works of Oscar Wilde, Molière, and George Bernard Shaw—characters frequently adopt righteous indignation as a theatrical mask. Hypocrites, social climbers, and pompous windbags feign indignation when their secrets are touched or when their inflated status is challenged, using high-minded moral outrage as a weapon to silence critics and distract from their own blameworthy behavior.
  • Literary Aesthetics and Expressive Demeanor: As an expressive modifier, indignant qualifies voices, gestures, gazes, and letters (e.g., an indignant glance, an indignant letter to the editor, an indignant gasp). It conveys stiffened posture, flushed cheeks, widened eyes, and elevated vocal projection, signaling that the speaker considers the topic completely beyond the pale of civilized acceptability.

The semantic bridge connecting ancient Roman concepts of unworthiness to modern political protest and Oscar Wilde's affronted fireworks lies in the classical Latin root indignari: composed of the negative prefix in- and dignus ("worthy, deserving of honor"), the word literally means "to deem something unworthy or disgraceful." When a person encounters behavior that falls beneath human decency or violates basic fairness, their spirit recoils in natural rebellion—transforming the cognitive perception of unworthiness into the fiery emotional fire of indignation.
