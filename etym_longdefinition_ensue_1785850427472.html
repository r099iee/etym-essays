ENSUE is an intransitive verb meaning to happen afterward, either as the direct result of an earlier event or merely in immediate chronological sequence. In the reading passage the word performs its classic modern office: "When the power grid suddenly failed across the city, total chaos began to ensue" — the failure of the grid is the cause, and the chaos is what ensues, following in time because it follows in logic. Ensue compresses the whole idea of aftermath into a single verb: it names the event that comes after another event, especially the event that could not be helped once the first thing happened. Where "follow" needs a person or thing to do the following, "ensue" is impersonal — events ensue, consequences ensue, results ensue — and the word is almost always used of things, not of people following one another down a street.

SENSES AND USES

  • Core sense: to follow in time, to come after — "in the weeks that ensued", "the ensuing silence". The emphasis is on sequence: what comes next in the train of events.
  • Resultative sense: to happen as a consequence — "if negotiations break down, conflict will ensue". Here the emphasis is on causation: the event ensues because of what went before.
  • "Ensuing" as participle and adjective: "the ensuing days/months/years", "ensuing chaos" — the following period or the consequential mess; a frequent journalistic and narrative form.
  • Legal use: "ensuing damages", "ensuing loss" — consequential or indirect losses that flow from a breach of contract, an accident, or a triggering event; the word marks the causal chain that the law follows from cause to harm.
  • Formulaic narrative: "chaos ensued", "hilarity ensued", "a brawl ensued", "a scramble ensued" — the compressed, often ironic journalistic or novelistic formula that summarizes a rapid sequence of consequences in two words; "hilarity ensued" has become a modern catchphrase for a situation that collapsed into comedy.
  • "Ensue from": the construction naming the cause — "great dangers ensue from carelessness"; the cause is the object of "from", the consequence is the subject of "ensue".
  • Obsolete sense: to pursue, to follow after a person (16th–17th centuries) — "to ensue the enemy"; the word began in English as the verb of pursuit and only gradually narrowed to impersonal sequence; the pursuit sense is now obsolete.
  • Register: ensue is formal and literary; it is common in journalism, legal prose, and narrative, but rare in casual speech, where speakers say "happened next" or "followed". It is also typically used of unwelcome events (chaos, conflict, panic), though it is neutral in principle.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: Latin insequī, "to follow closely, pursue, attack", is made of in-, "on, upon, after", and sequī, "to follow" — the root sequī being the same Latin verb that underlies sequence, sequel, consequence, and execute. The original image is physical: a pursuer following hard on the heels of a fleeing enemy. Latin's compound passed into Old French as ensuivre, "to follow after, to result", and the word crossed into English in the fourteenth century as ensuen, where it meant both "to pursue" and "to come after". Over the following centuries the physical pursuit sense died away and the word narrowed to its modern core: the impersonal, resultative "to happen afterward" — a semantic bridge from chasing a person to following an event, and finally to being the consequence of an event. The card's sentence is a perfect modern example: the power grid fails, and chaos ensues.
