RECHERCHÉ (adjective, pronounced /ɹɪtʃəʃˈeɪ/, still felt as a French loanword and frequently printed in italics) denotes something rare, choice, and exquisite because it has been deliberately sought out and selected; by extension it denotes anything out of the common way — obscure, exotic, or recondite — and, in its most pejorative shading, something refined only for show, affected and pretentious. The word is the past participle of French rechercher, "to seek out again, to search closely," and it carries the traces of that searching inside itself: a recherché thing is not merely scarce; it is scarce because someone has gone looking for it, prized it, and chosen it over common alternatives. This is the single most important fact for understanding the word's whole range of senses — the act of the hunt is never far away.

In the present context the word does precise satirical work. The sentence describes "handbooks for the schoolroom parading lists of Greek terms and RECHERCHÉ metres culled from Swinburne and Bridges." The metres in question are deliberately out-of-the-way verse-forms revived from classical Greek prosody by the Victorian poets Algernon Charles Swinburne and Robert Bridges; a schoolboy would need scholarly erudition to recognize them and a taste for the recondite to admire them. The verb "parade" is the key: the handbooks display these metres as ornaments of learning, exhibiting obscure knowledge for its own sake rather than for any use. The tone is mocking and satirical — the very foreignness of the French word underscores the affectedness of the schoolroom parade, and the exoticism that makes a metre "recherché" is here held up for ridicule. The reader is asked to see pretension in the choice of rare metres and rare words alike.

  • Sense 1 — "sought out, choice, exquisite" (the etymological core): something carefully searched for and selected because of its excellence; a recherché delicacy, a recherché vintage, a recherché argument. This sense is wholly positive: it praises the discrimination of the seeker and the rarity of the object found.
  • Sense 2 — "rare, obscure, out of the common way" (the general extension): anything that stands outside ordinary experience — rare vocabulary, rare metres, rare allusions, rare subjects. The emphasis here shifts from the act of choosing to the quality of rarity itself, and the word becomes a near-synonym of uncommon, unusual, or recondite.
  • Sense 3 — "exotic, foreign, strange" (the colouring): because the commonest recherché objects are foreign or ancient (Greek metres, Parisian fashions, rare imports), the word takes on an exotic flavour — a recherché dish is usually a foreign one. The bridge is that what comes from afar is naturally out of the common way.
  • Sense 4 — "affected, pretentious, over-refined" (the pejorative shading): from the 19th century onward, especially in English, the word can censure rather than praise: a "recherché style" strains after refinement, a "recherché manner" is precious and put-on. The bridge is the suspicion that deliberate rarity may be cultivated for display rather than for genuine merit — the search becomes self-advertisement.
  • Sense 5 — "fastidious, over-fastidious" (of persons, taste, and manners): applied to a person's taste, speech, or manners, the word implies a finicky, over-exquisite standard that rejects the ordinary; a recherché palate, a recherché turn of phrase. This is the personal face of Sense 4.

The semantic bridge in one paragraph: the whole range of senses is a single trajectory from action to evaluation. French chercher "to seek" plus the prefix re- "again, thoroughly" gives rechercher "to seek out thoroughly"; the past participle recherché, literally "sought-out-again," describes the thing that has been hunted for with care. Because careful searching is prompted by worth, the participle becomes a term of praise: "choice, exquisite, rare." Because what is chosen with care so often lies outside the common stock, the word attaches itself to the rare and the exotic. And because over-careful choosing can become a pose, the word finally develops its pejorative use — refinement turned to affectation. English borrowed the word at the moment of French prestige (the 18th century, when French set the standard of European refinement), and the three lives of the word — praising choice, marking rarity, mocking pretension — have coexisted in English ever since.

Register, grammar, and collocations: the word is high-register and largely written rather than spoken; it appears in literary criticism, connoisseurship, journalism, and stylish prose. As the past participle of a French first-conjugation verb it agrees in gender in French (feminine recherchée), but in English the masculine base form is used regardless of the noun it modifies and never changes its ending. It retains the French acute accent on the final -é, keeps its French-style pronunciation, and is often set in italics as a foreign word. Common collocations include recherché vocabulary, recherché allusions, recherché metres, recherché subjects, recherché taste, recherché style, and recherché dishes. It is applied to things far more often than to people, and when used of a person or a personal manner the pejorative "affected" sense is usually in play. The word is not to be confused with the common English adjective researched (from the verb research), though the two are distant relatives; in literary use the French spelling and meaning remain the badge of the connoisseur.
