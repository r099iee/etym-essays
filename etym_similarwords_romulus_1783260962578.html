• Remus: Similar because the twins are inseparable in the foundation myth — both suckled by the she-wolf, both heirs of Mars; differs because Remus is the murdered brother, killed by Romulus in the founding quarrel, and never becomes the eponym of anything, while Romulus names the city and the Roman people.
  • Aeneas: Similar because both are the two great Trojan/Latin founders of the Roman people, ancestors of Rome; differs because Aeneas (hero of Virgil) arrived from Troy centuries earlier as a refugee-founder, while Romulus actually founded the city itself on the Palatine.
  • Numa Pompilius: Similar because both are legendary early kings of Rome and figures of the king-list; differs because Numa (the second king) is the pious lawgiver who instituted religion and the calendar, while Romulus is the warlike founder and city-builder.
  • Hercules: Similar because both are mythical strong founders/heroes whose historicity is doubted and whose deeds are classed together (as Macaulay does), and both were apotheosised; differs because Hercules is a pan-Hellenic demigod of labours, while Romulus is the specifically Roman founder-king.
  • Quirinus: Similar because Quirinus is the divine form of Romulus — the same figure worshipped as a god after his apotheosis; differs because Quirinus is the cult name of the deified founder, distinct from the human legendary king Romulus.
  • Mordred / Arthur: Similar because all are legendary British figures of doubtful historicity, the very company Macaulay places beside Romulus; differs because Arthur and Mordred are medieval British legend, while Romulus is the Roman foundation figure, older and more fully institutionalised in civic myth.
  • Theseus: Similar because both are legendary city-founding heroes credited with founding a state (Athens; Rome); differs because Theseus is a Greek hero of many exploits, while Romulus is a specifically Roman king whose whole story is the foundation of Rome.
  • Rōma / Rome: Similar because the city name and the founder's name are etymologically and symbolically one (Romulus = "he of Rome"); differs because "Rome" names the actual city and empire, while "Romulus" names the mythical person behind it.
