To swoop is to descend rapidly and suddenly from above, through the air, in a sweeping curve, usually in order to seize or attack — the characteristic motion of a hawk or eagle stooping upon its prey. The word fuses speed, curved trajectory, and predatory intent: a swoop is never aimless but a decisive, almost inevitable plunge, at once graceful and merciless.
  • Literal core sense: a swift, sweeping descent through the air from a height, most naturally of a bird of prey striking at quarry below. The motion is a curve, not a straight line: the bird circles or soars above, then folds and drops in a smooth arc that carries it down and, in the attack, onward past or onto its target.
  • In the present literary context: the great eagles, noticing the goblins at their wicked work on the mountain, swooped on them and drove them shrieking back to their caves, stopping whatever wickedness they were doing. The past tense swooped carries the full majesty of the eagles' intervention: sudden, overwhelming, morally authoritative. The sweeping curve of the descent matches the circling, lordly flight just described; the motion is smooth, powerful, free of hesitation, and conveys a kind of just violence — a punitive striking-down with the finality of a hawk's stoop. Where the goblins' malice is furtive and creeping, the eagles' swoop is open, direct, and irresistibly swift; the word's very shape — the long, gliding diphthong swoo- and the percussive final -pt — enacts the plunge it names.
  • Figurative senses: by extension, to swoop is to act suddenly and decisively on someone or something, especially to seize an opportunity or to close in on a target: police swoop on a hideout, businesses swoop on a bargain, a rival company swoops in to buy a failing firm. The predatory curve is retained as an abstract pattern: someone high up, watching, descends at the chosen moment and takes what they came for.
  • Noun senses: as a noun, a swoop is the act or motion of swooping (the eagle's swoop), and in the fixed phrase in one fell swoop, popularized by Shakespeare, it means "all at once, in a single sudden act", preserving the image of a single destructive stroke that removes everything in its path.
  • Jargon and technical uses: in military and law-enforcement reporting, a "swoop" or "swoop operation" is a coordinated surprise raid; in aviation, pilots speak of swooping approaches or a plane swooping low; in computer science, "swoop" has been used (chiefly in branding, e.g., a graphics tool) to suggest fast, sweeping action; in zoology and ornithology the term describes raptor hunting behaviour; and in advertising and trade, "swoop" denotes a sudden capture of market share or attention.
  • Grammatical behaviour: swoop is an intransitive or prepositional verb (swoop down, swoop on/upon, swoop in), occasionally transitive in dialectal or archaic use; its past tense is the regular swooped, and it yields the participial adjective use in the present context. Its register is vivid and energetic — never bureaucratic, always visual.
The boundaries against neighbouring verbs of downward motion sharpen the sense. Dived is a head-first plunge, often into water, implying a straight line rather than a curve; plummeted is an uncontrolled, gravity-driven fall with no grace or intent; dropped is the most neutral — a simple downward movement of any kind; descended is slow, dignified, and general; plunged is a rapid downward movement with emphasis on the loss of height but not on the curved trajectory or the predatory aim; pounced is the sudden spring of a cat or a fox onto prey from a stationary position, on the ground or a branch, without the aerial sweep; stoop(ed) is the falconer's technical word for the same raptorial dive and is the closest synonym, though stoop carries hawk-and-hunting technicality while swoop carries breadth and drama; swooshed describes the sound and swishing motion of air rather than the act of descending. What makes swoop distinctive is the whole package: height, watchfulness, curved speed, and intent — the descent of something that has chosen its moment and its victim, delivered in a single elegant, ruthless movement. In the story of the eagles, that full package is exactly the point: the intervention is not a fall but a judgment, and the word's blend of grace and severity makes the rescue read as both an attack and a deliverance.
