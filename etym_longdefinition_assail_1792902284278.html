Assail means to attack someone or something with force and suddenness: to fall upon with blows, weapons, or hostile action, and by a natural extension to fall upon with words, criticism, doubts, or unsettling sights, sounds, and smells. At its heart it is the verb of the leap, an assault that begins with springing forward.

In the source sentence the pursuers will assail the fugitive: here the word carries its full physical menace, a band of hunters closing in to seize or strike their quarry. The speaker does not know how the attack will come, only that flight invites pursuit and pursuit ends in violent contact, so assail hangs over the sentence like a drawn blade.

Core meaning in detail: To assail is to launch oneself at a target with intent to harm, overwhelm, or master it. The action implies vigour, initiative, and often surprise: armies assail fortresses, robbers assail travellers, critics assail reputations, and doubts assail sleepers at small hours. Unlike the colder attack, assail keeps a literary charge of suddenness, as if the assailant leapt from cover, a memory of the Latin salire, to leap, buried in its middle.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To attack physically with blows or weapons (primary, from c. 1200): to fall upon a person, position, or fortress; the original and still central sense, covering everything from fists to artillery.
  • To assault with words, criticism, or argument (figurative, early): to assail a proposal, a reputation, or a witness; the bridge is the duel metaphor, since hostile questions strike like blades and the victim must parry them.
  • To trouble the mind suddenly — doubts, fears, memories (psychological): grief assails the mourner, temptation assails the saint; the bridge runs through personification, as inner states are imagined as enemies leaping from ambush.
  • To strike the senses — noise, odour, spectacle (sensory): a stench assails the nostrils, a din assails the ears; the bridge is the involuntariness of sensation, which seizes attention as forcibly as any attacker.
  • To attempt a task boldly, to have a go at (colloquial, chiefly British): to assail a steep hill, a huge meal, or an examination; the bridge is sporting, since the climb or the paper is treated as a fortress to be stormed.
  • To solicit or proposition importunately (archaic legal and biblical): to assail a person with temptations or offers; the bridge is persistence imagined as repeated assaults on virtue's walls.

Register and connotation: Assail is graver and more literary than attack: newspapers report attacks, but novels, sermons, and histories assail. Its connotation is sudden violent pressure against resistance, and it flatters neither party, the assailant is fierce, the assailed is beset. In the passive, assailed by doubts, it has become the classic idiom of the inward siege, dignifying anxiety with martial metaphor.
