1. Deeper origin — PIE connection debated. The Germanic verb bregdan "to weave, braid, move quickly, pull suddenly, jerk" (from which the bridle-word is formed) is traditionally compared with Lithuanian brėžti "to draw, scratch, streak" and ultimately with a PIE root *bʰre- / *bʰredʰ- "to weave, plait, twist," but the reconstruction is uncertain, and some treat *bregd- as a Germanic-internal expressive formation (the debate is noted below). 2. Proto-Germanic *bregdanan (verb) "to move quickly, pull suddenly, twist, weave, braid" (reconstructed) — the base verb of the noun for the bridle, the "thing that twists, jerks, pulls." 3. Proto-Germanic *brigdilaz (noun) "bridle" (reconstructed; variant *bregdilaz) — derived from *bregd- with the instrumental suffix *-ilaz-; cognates: Old Saxon brīdel, Middle Dutch breidel, Old High German brittil "rein, strap," Old Norse briðill "scarf, band, string." 4. Old English brīdel "bridle, halter, curb" (attested from c. 8th century, in glosses and homilies; the figurative "bridle of the tongue" appears in Old English prose). 5. Middle English bridel / bridle "bridle, restraint" (attested 13th century) — the vowel shortens from Old English long ī to Middle English /ɪ/, giving the modern sound. 6. Modern English BRIDLE (noun and verb) — present day; the verb's three senses (fit a bridle; restrain; show offence) are all attested by the 17th century. Debated / nuanced note: (a) the deeper Indo-European ancestry of Proto-Germanic *bregd- is uncertain — the traditional link to Lithuanian brėžti and a PIE *bʰre- "to weave" is plausible but not universally accepted; some scholars regard *bregd- as a Germanic-internal expressive formation with no recoverable PIE root. (b) The Old English vowel brīdel (long ī) was shortened to /ɪ/ in the modern reflex, a regular Middle English shortening before -d-. The folk-etymology "braid + -le" is essentially right historically — the bridle is "a thing of braided cords" — since *bregd- means "to braid, twist.
