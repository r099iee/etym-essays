vacillating is the present participle of the verb vacillate, used attributively as an adjective. It describes a person, a policy, a course of action, or (less often) a physical thing that cannot hold steady — something that sways first one way and then the other. The image underneath the abstraction is bodily: Latin vacillāre meant "to totter, to sway on one's feet", and English has never quite lost that picture. In the sentence at hand — "called a vacillating spineless weakling" — the word is a verdict on character rather than on balance: it accuses Cicero of habitual indecision, of holding a position only until the wind changes.

Core sense. The dominant modern meaning is "irresolute, given to changing course, unable to commit to a decision or a principle". It is stronger and more damning than undecided: an undecided person has not yet chosen, whereas a vacillating one has chosen repeatedly and abandoned each choice. The word also implies that the wavering is public and consequential — vacillating leadership leaves followers without direction, and a vacillating friend cannot be relied on in a crisis.

Senses and constructions

  • Personal irresolution: "a vacillating politician", "a vacillating temperament", "too vacillating to govern". The fault is located in character, not in the difficulty of one particular decision.
  • Policy and collective action: "vacillating policy", "a vacillating foreign policy", "the ministry's vacillating response". Here the subject is an institution and the fault is a course that lurches from one expedient to the next.
  • Literal swaying: still available for physical unsteadiness — "a vacillating flame", "his vacillating gait" — though modern prose usually prefers flickering, staggering, teetering or oscillating for objects.
  • The between construction: the participle naturally governs between, naming the two poles — "vacillating between hope and fear", "vacillating between defiance and apology". This is one of the word's most characteristic modern patterns.
  • Related forms: vacillation (the act or habit of wavering), vacillatory (rare), vacillatingly (rare). The older adjective vacillant, taken straight from the Latin participle, survives only in archaic and poetic prose.

How the metaphor works. The bridge from body to character is very old. A man who cannot stand upright is, in most languages, a man who cannot be trusted to stand for anything: the same transfer produced unstable, shaky, wobbly and spineless as moral adjectives. Latin made the transfer early, using vacillāre of wavering resolve as well as of wavering legs, and English inherited both senses at once when it borrowed the verb in the 1590s. What the modern adjective adds is duration: a single wobble is an incident, but a vacillating man is one whose whole record consists of wobbles.

Register and connotation. The word is formal, literary and frankly contemptuous. It belongs to the vocabulary of moral and political judgment rather than of neutral description: a historian may report that a cabinet "changed its mind", but a critic writes that it was "vacillating". Typical collocations are vacillating leadership, vacillating policy, vacillating course, vacillating attitude, vacillating between. It is rarely applied to children or to trivial choices; its natural targets are statesmen, parties, generals and institutions, and its natural companions are words of weakness — weak, spineless, irresolute, indecisive.

In this reading. The clause belongs to a catalogue of hostile characterisations of Cicero: he is called a vacillating spineless weakling, a vainglorious braggart, a pusillanimous deserter from good causes, and a pseudo-intellectual. The pairing with spineless is deliberately redundant, a rhetorical doubling that turns a political charge into a physical insult. The charge itself has a real basis in Cicero's career — his exile and recall, his shifts between Pompey and Caesar, his gamble on Octavian, his final stand against Antony in the Philippics — and it has been a staple of his detractors from the Roman historians to modern biographers. Whether the shifts were weakness or the only possible conduct for a civilian in an age of armies is precisely the argument the passage is staging.

Modern note. Vacillating has no technical senses: it is not used in medicine, law or engineering, where oscillating, fluctuating and intermittent do the work. Its currency is chiefly in political journalism, biography and formal commentary, and it retains a slightly old-fashioned, podium-thumping flavour — the sort of word a leader uses about another leader.
