- Late 17th century: born with the verb "bewilder" (1689), "to lead into the wilds, to cause to stray" — the early sense is spatial, about paths vanishing; the noun itself arrives in the early 1800s as the name of this mental straying.
- Romantic era (c. 1800–1830s): poetry and philosophy embrace it — the sublime vertigo of the mind among mountains, mists and vast landscapes; a dignified, awe-tinged confusion.
- Victorian era: becomes the great novelistic word for psychological muddle — Dickensian fog, bureaucratic and emotional tangle; still dignified, bordering on melodrama.
- 20th century: psychologised and secularised — bewilderment names cognitive overload, alienation (Kafkaesque bewilderment), and the shock of the modern city; the tone blunts and gains an ironic edge.
- Today: everyday but bookish — "total bewilderment" is a familiar phrase for bafflement at technology, politics and instructions; the word keeps a distinctly literary, slightly archaic dignity, which is why Tolkien can use it of Thorin's dragon-sickness.
