Diomedes (also Diomede, Greek Diomedes) is in Homer and later myth the son of Tydeus, king of Argos, and one of the foremost Achaean heroes at Troy - second only to Achilles in martial prowess and to Odysseus and Nestor in counsel. In the money-history passage, the armour of Diomedes was worth nine head of cattle because Homer (Iliad 6.234-236) prices his bronze armour at nine oxen when he exchanges it with the Lycian Glaucus, whose gold armour was worth a hundred oxen. The anecdote illustrates primitive valuation in cattle units and also Diomedes as proverbial brave but overshadowed warrior who makes a famously unequal exchange through guest-friendship (xenia).

In the Iliad, Diomedes dominates Book 5 (the Diomedeia or Aristeia of Diomedes): strengthened by Athena, he wounds Aphrodite and Ares, routs Trojans, and duels Hector and Aeneas. He is young, pious, obedient to gods, and a master charioteer with his companion Sthenelus. In Book 9 he rebukes Agamemnon and keeps the army fighting; in Book 10 he and Odysseus conduct the night raid (Doloneia), killing Dolon and the Thracian king Rhesus; in Book 23 he wins the chariot race at the funeral games of Patroclus. Unlike Achilles he is never sulking, unlike Ajax never ponderous, unlike Odysseus never tricky - his epithet is agathos good at war and megathymos great-hearted.

Post-Homeric tradition expands him: co-conspirator with Odysseus in stealing the Palladium, inventor of the Trojan Horse stratagem in some versions, founder-hero in Italy (Arpi, Brundisium, Diomedean islands) after storm-driven wanderings, honoured as hero-god in Adriatic cults. Tragedy and Virgil treat him as a wise survivor who refuses to rejoin war in Latium, counselling peace. Philosophical and rhetorical schools cite him as model of youthful courage tempered by listening to elders (Nestor) and gods (Athena). A second mythic Diomedes - Diomedes of Thrace, owner of man-eating mares slain by Heracles - is distinct and must not be confused, though ancient mythographers sometimes conflate names.

  • Homeric sense: Tydeides, king of Argos, bearer of bronze armour worth nine oxen; exemplar of cattle valuation.
  • Martial sense: aristeia hero, wounder of gods, chariot victor; bravery plus discipline.
  • Counsellor sense: forthright speaker in assembly, partner to Odysseus in espionage; courage with prudence.
  • Founder-hero sense: nostos to Italy, city founder, eponym of Diomedes islands; Italic and Adriatic cult recipient.
  • Proverbial sense: Diomedean exchange means a bad bargain (gold for bronze); Diomedean valour means daring even gods.
  • Distinct homonym: Diomedes of Thrace, giant king with flesh-eating horses, eighth labour of Heracles; cruel tyrant opposite to Argive hero.
  • Modern reception: Shakespeare Troilus and Cressida Diomedes as blunt Greek lover of Cressida; modern novels and games as balanced warrior archetype.

Why nine cattle matters: Homeric society, like the passage argues, lacked coin and priced prestige bronze in oxen - mobile, known-value livestock. That Diomedes armour, though heroic, is bronze versus Glaucus gold explains 9 versus 100. The exchange, prompted by discovery of ancestral guest-friendship, shows honour outweighing market logic, a point economists from Aristotle to Adam Smith cite for gift versus market. Remembering Diomedes thus means remembering both a character - the dependable young king - and an economic fossil - cattle as unit of account.
