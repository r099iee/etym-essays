ASUNDER is among the oldest words in English — not borrowed, but inherited directly from the prehistoric Germanic parent of the language, and behind that from Proto-Indo-European. Its history is the story of how a simple phrase meaning "in a separate state" hardened into a single adverb, specialised toward violent rupture, and then was elevated by the Bible and the Prayer Book into the most solemn vocabulary of marriage and separation. Its career shows the whole range of processes that shape a native English word: sound erosion, fusion, semantic specialisation, and the fixing power of liturgy and literature.

The Indo-European root: the semantics of apartness. The ultimate ancestor of asunder is the Proto-Indo-European root *sen(i)- / *sane-, which meant "apart, separate, without, by oneself." The root carried the idea of standing aside or alone, and its progeny spread across the family. In Italic it produced Latin sine, "without," the word preserved in the tag sine qua non, "without which not." In Indo-Iranian it yielded the Sanskrit adverb sanutár, "away, far off, apart." In Celtic it probably underlies Old Irish sain, "one's own, separate, distinct." Greek, strikingly, shows no secure cognate — its preposition áneu, "without," belongs to a different formation — a reminder that even a well-attested root need not surface in every branch. The core meaning that binds the family is the idea of separation from the group, of being apart and by oneself — the semantic ground on which the whole later development rests.

The Germanic development: sundrō, the adverb of apartness. The line that produces asunder runs through the Germanic branch. The parent Germanic tongue built an adverb, *sundrō, "apart, separately, singly," formed from the root plus an adverbial suffix -trō/-drō — a formation meaning "in a state of being apart." Every old Germanic language kept it. Gothic has sundrō, "apart." Old Norse has sundr, "asunder, in pieces," which travelled with Norse settlers into northern England and reinforced the English word. Old High German has suntar, "apart," the ancestor of modern German sondern, "to separate," and of the prefix sonder- in sonderbar, "strange, special" (literally "set apart, singular"). Old English inherited the adverb as sundor, "apart, separately," and spun off a small family around it: the adjective syndrig, "separate, distinct," which became modern English sundry, "various" (things counted separately); and the verb sundrian, "to divide, separate," which became modern English sunder. The family was already complete in the Old English period: sundor the adverb, syndrig the adjective, sundrian the verb — and the phrase on sundran that would fuse into asunder.

The fusion: from on sundran to asunder. Asunder itself is a fossilised prepositional phrase. In Old English one said on sundran, literally "in a separate state," the preposition on ("in, into") governing the dative of sundor. This is the same construction that produced other English words beginning in a-: abed ("in bed"), afoot ("on foot"), ashore ("on shore"), around ("on round"). As the language evolved, the unstressed preposition on eroded to the mere vowel a-, and the phrase fused into a single word. The fused adverb asunder is attested from the thirteenth century onward. The sound change here is worth noting precisely: the weakening of the initial vowel on → a- is the reduction of an unstressed preposition to a proclitic prefix, the standard fate of on- in such compounds; the word then behaved as one unit, and by the Middle English period it had acquired its modern sense of separation into parts.

Semantic specialisation: from "separate" to "in pieces." The meaning narrowed as the form fused. The Old English phrase on sundran meant simply "in a separate state, apart" — neutral, spatial, without violence. From the very beginning of the fused word's career, however, asunder specialised toward physical rupture: things are "torn asunder," "rent asunder," "burst asunder," "hewn asunder," "dashed asunder" — the word of objects and bodies violently divided into fragments. The neutral "apart" remained available (things could be "asunder" merely by being separated), but the word's characteristic use was the violent one, and the fixed collocations with verbs of tearing, bursting, and rending built up around it. This pairing with violent verbs is the key semantic fact: asunder does not merely state separation, it states the result of a destructive act.

The Bible and the Prayer Book: fixing the solemn register. The word's modern fate was sealed by liturgy. The Authorised Version of the Bible (1611) and the Book of Common Prayer fixed asunder in the marriage formula drawn from Matthew 19:6 — "What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder" — and the same solemn usage runs through the Gospel of Mark and the English marriage service. The Bible also uses the word of the most violent physical rupture: in Acts 1:18 the fate of Judas is described as falling "headlong he burst asunder in the midst." This double life — the violent "burst asunder" and the solemn "put asunder" — is the whole range of the word: it names both the rending of a body and the dissolution of a divine union. Because the marriage formula is one of the most quoted sentences in English, asunder entered the consciousness of every speaker of the language as the word of indissoluble union and its forbidden dissolution.

Literary and modern use: the archaic survival. Through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries asunder was in ordinary vigorous use — Shakespeare and the dramatists use it freely of torn cloth, divided armies, and broken hearts — but in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it retreated from everyday speech into elevated diction. It survived in the literary language of the Romantics and Victorians, and in the fixed collocations "torn asunder," "rent asunder," "split asunder," "burst asunder." In the essay from which the card's context is drawn, the word is used in its abstract, analytical sense: right reason has "put asunder" things that confusion would blend — a perfectly intellectualised use of the ancient physical image, where the separation is of concepts rather than bodies. Today asunder is archaic and literary, confined to the formulaic pairs and to deliberate elevation; the everyday word for separation is apart, while in pieces carries the concrete sense of fragmentation. The word's survival is a monument to the power of the marriage service: one liturgical sentence has kept an Old English phrase alive in the mouths of modern speakers, half a millennium after the everyday language left it behind.

Summary of the drift. PIE *sen(i)-, "apart" → Germanic *sundrō, "apart, singly" → Old English sundor and on sundran, "in a separate state" → fused Middle English asunder, "into pieces" (13th century) → fixed by the Bible and Prayer Book in the solemn marriage formula → archaic literary survival today. The single thread is separation: from a neutral spatial apartness, through violent physical rupture, to the ceremonial prohibition against dissolving a divine bond.
