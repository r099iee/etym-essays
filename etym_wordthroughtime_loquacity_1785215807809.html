- 15th century learned adoption - enters English from Latin as a formal synonym for talkativeness, the habitual fluency of speech that pours out without pause; the register is scholarly and slightly critical.
- 16th-18th century conversation and satire - used of the talkative in moral essays and comedy, where the fluent talker is both admired for ease and wearied for excess; the word carries a mild social censure.
- 18th-19th century literary nuance - the word can be neutral or even admiring of energetic fluency, as Carlyle's "desperate attempts at loquacity" shows talk used defensively as a shield against a dreaded silence.
- Contemporary use - rare, formal, and literary; in ordinary speech "talkativeness" has displaced it, though it retains the precise sense of abundant, fluent, ceaseless speech.
