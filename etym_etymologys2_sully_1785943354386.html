SULLY is a French loanword whose chain runs from the Indo-European word for "pig" through the mud of the pig's wallow to the English verb of defilement. The derivation is given in full below, followed by the honest statement of a genuine scholarly debate about a rival Germanic source. 1. Proto-Indo-European *sū- "pig, swine" (reconstructed; projected c. 4500–3000 BCE; an onomatopoeic/expressive root of grunting, cognate with Latin sūs, Old English sū "sow," Old Norse sýr, German Sau, Sanskrit sūkará- "wild boar," Avestan hū-) — the starting point of the whole family. 2. Latin sūs, suis (m./f.) "swine, pig, hog" (attested from the earliest Latin records) — the direct Italic continuation of the PIE root, with the regular development of PIE *ū as long ū in Latin. 3. Latin suīllus "pertaining to swine, of or like a pig" (adjective from sūs + the suffix -īllus; attested, e.g. suīlla caro "pork") — the adjectival base that underlies the French verb. 4. (Intermediate, debated) Latin/Vulgar Latin *suculāre or a formation on souil "wallow" — the precise Latin intermediate is not directly attested; the standard account proceeds through the noun souil "a pig's wallowing place, mud, mire," traditionally derived from suīllus (the pig-pool). This stage must be flagged as reconstructed, not documented. 5. Old French souil (m.) "a pig's wallow, a muddy pool, mire" (attested) and Old French souiller / suillier "to soil, stain, make dirty; to wallow in mire like a pig" (attested from the 10th–11th centuries) — the French verb retains the animal imagery of its origin: the original "wallowing" sense is that of a pig churning its muddy pool. 6. Middle English sulien / sullen "to sully, defile, soil" (attested from the 14th century; borrowed from Old French souiller and remade with English spelling) — the English form appears in religious and moral prose with the sense "to defile, pollute." 7. English SULLY "to soil, stain, tarnish; to defile morally" (modern form, settled from the 16th century onward) ◄ TARGET — used for physical soiling and, overwhelmingly, for the tarnishing of honour and reputation. NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: (1) The French derivation is the traditional and most widely accepted account: Old French souiller clearly belongs with souil "pig's wallow" and the Latin family of sūs "swine"; the intermediate Latin form is, however, inferred — we can show that the French noun is built on the swine-word, but no attested Latin verb "to wallow like a pig" directly links suīllus to the French verb, and the u of souil is phonologically tricky for a simple derivative of suīllus. (2) A rival theory derives the verb from a native Germanic root: Old English sylian "to sully, defile" (attested, chiefly in religious texts; cognate with Old Saxon sulian and German sühlen "to wash, to soak") continues Proto-Germanic *suljaną "to make muddy," itself from the same PIE *sū- family by way of the sense "wetness, mud." Under this theory, English "sully" may be a native Germanic word, not a French loan, or a blending of both streams; OED and Etymonline note the uncertainty. Either path, however, reaches the same deep root *sū- "swine," so the core etymology — the pig and its mud — is secure, and only the route from root to English is debated. (3) The moral sense "to tarnish honour" is a figurative extension already present in Middle English religious use, not a modern development.
