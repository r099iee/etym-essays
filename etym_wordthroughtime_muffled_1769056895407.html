- Late 14th c. - the word enters English (via French) as a pure clothing verb: to wrap or cover the face and body in thick cloth against cold or for concealment — a muffled man is simply a swathed man
- 16th–17th c. - the sound-sense develops by a natural step: because wrapping a thing in cloth deadens its noise, bells, drums, voices, and cries become muffled, and "muffled drums" hardens into the stock phrase of mourning and solemn ceremony
- 16th–19th c. - the two senses spread into new objects: a "muffler" first means a scarf wound round the neck (the wrapping sense), then the device that deadens an engine's exhaust (the sound sense); the word becomes at once a clothing word and a mechanics word
- Contemporary - register-neutral and common, muffled falls into two collocational families — cloth ("muffled up against the cold") and sound ("a muffled cry," "muffled voices behind the door") — and in literary use it quietly yokes them together into one image of soft, concealed motion
