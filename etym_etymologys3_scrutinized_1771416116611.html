The biography of scrutinized begins with a blade. Its root is PIE *sker-, "to cut, separate, divide," one of the great cutting roots of the Indo-European family, which produced Sanskrit kṛt- "to cut" (whence Hindi kāṭnā, "to cut"), Greek keírō "to shear, shave" and — through the notion of "sifting apart" — krīnein, "to separate, judge" (whence English critic, crisis, criterion), and the Germanic line of shear, share ("a portion cut off"), shard ("a broken piece"), and shred. The same root also yielded Sanskrit carman, "hide, skin," literally "the cut-off thing," which survives in Hindi chām and Odia chamaṛā, "leather." The underlying metaphor of the whole family is separation: to know something is to cut it apart, to divide it into its pieces so that its structure stands revealed. In Latin, that cutting impulse took a strikingly humble turn. From the root came scrūta, "rubbish, broken bits, cast-off rags" — things already cut down and thrown away — and from that noun the verb scrūtārī (attested by the first century BCE) meant to rummage through such refuse, to ransack the junk-dealer's pile in search of a usable coin or a scrap of cloth. The word then began its long climb from the junk-stall to the courtroom, the parliament, and the church. Late Latin built scrūtīnium, "a search, an examination," which early Christianity took up as a technical term: the scrutinium was the formal investigation of catechumens — candidates for baptism — an inquiry into their faith and conduct before admission to the community. From that ecclesiastical and legal sense English borrowed scrutiny around 1400, and the noun's early uses are notably procedural: the scrutiny was the official examination of candidates, accounts, or, crucially, votes. The verb arrived later. Scrutinize appears in English about 1671, formed on Latin scrūtārī (with the possible mediation of French scruter, itself from the same root) and the productive suffix -ize. Its past participle, scrutinized, settled quickly into prose with the meaning "examined with exhaustive attention," and it is this form that carries the appraising look of Xi-feng in Dream of the Red Chamber — a use that perfectly preserves the word's double heritage of sifting and judging. The semantic arc of the whole family is a rise from sordid to sublime: from sifting garbage for a coin or a scrap of cloth, to the systematic investigation that precedes a judgment — ecclesiastical examination, legal inquiry, political accountability. Yet the sifting never disappears from the word. To scrutinize still implies that the truth is not on the surface, that it must be dug out of the rubble of appearance; the French scruter, the Italian scrutare ("to scan, examine"), and the English scrutineer (the official who checks ballots, especially familiar in Australian and New Zealand elections) all keep the searching verb alive. The modern career of the word has been shaped by politics and media. From the counting of votes — scrutiny in its parliamentary sense — the noun extended in the twentieth century to the general condition of being examined by institutions and the public: "under scrutiny," "media scrutiny," "public scrutiny." The connotation shifted accordingly from the neutral, respectful thoroughness of a professional examination to something more invasive and faintly adversarial; being scrutinized is now often an exposure, a judgment the subject cannot escape. That modern charge, however, is already latent in the root: a scrutiny was always the search for what might be hidden, and to be scrutinized has always meant to be read past one's surface. The sound changes and borrowings that brought the word into English are clean and well attested — Latin scrūt- through Medieval Latin and French into late Middle English — so the chain needs no guesswork. From a Proto-Indo-European blade that cut things apart, to Roman junk-sifting, to the church's examination of souls, to the ballot-box ritual of democracy, and to the modern glare of surveillance: scrutinized has always meant knowing by cutting — and seeing, by looking, into the hidden truth of things.
