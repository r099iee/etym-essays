Core meaning. Flurried is the past tense and past participle of the verb to flurry, and also serves as an adjective meaning “agitated, confused, flustered, or disturbed”, usually by a sudden burst of activity, excitement, haste, or alarm. The word lives in two overlapping worlds: the world of weather — where a flurry is a sudden gust or brief swirl (of wind, snow, or spray) — and the world of feeling — where to be flurried is to be thrown into a nervous commotion. In both cases the underlying image is the same: a sudden, brief, irregular disturbance.

Primary senses.

  • Adjective — nervous agitation: flurried people are hurried, ruffled, and mentally disordered by haste or alarm: “a flurried messenger”, “she was flurried by the sudden question”. The emphasis is on loss of calm composure, often with a comic or sympathetic edge rather than deep terror.
  • Verb intransitive — swirling motion: to flurry is to move in a gusty, swirling, or agitated way, like a whirl of wind or a swirl of leaves, snow, or — as in the source sentence — bats bursting from a cave.
  • Verb transitive — to agitate: to flurry someone is to confuse or discompose them with sudden noise, haste, or surprise.

Secondary and extended senses.

  • Meteorological: “flurried” appears in weather writing for brief, light, swirling falls of snow or gusts of wind; a “snow flurry” is the noun the adjective is built from.
  • Commercial / financial: in markets and business, a flurry is a sudden, short burst of activity or trading; “flurried trading” describes a brief spell of agitated buying and selling.
  • Literary texture: the verb and participle are frequent in Victorian and Edwardian narrative prose for the sudden breaking of stillness — a whirl of birds, a gust stirring a curtain, a room thrown into confusion by news.

Related forms. The noun flurry (plural flurries) is the hub of the family: snow flurry, flurry of activity, flurry of deals, flurry of punches. The verb inflects flurry, flurries, flurried, flurrying, and the participle doubles as an adjective. A flurried person is often described as flurried “about” — bustling ineffectively from place to place.

Weather-reporting usage. In meteorology, “flurries” denotes light, brief, swirling falls of snow without significant accumulation; “flurried” is the adjectival form (“flurried snow”). The word also appears in sailing and marine contexts for sudden gusts that flurry the water.

Distinction from panic. Flurried agitation is short-lived and practical — missed keys, interrupted routines, unexpected visitors — rather than deep emotional disturbance. It carries a faintly comic, sympathetic colouring: the flurried hostess, the flurried messenger. This is why the word suits the source sentence: the bats are startled and swirl in a brief flurried commotion, not terrified and scattering permanently.

Register and tone. Flurried is milder than panicked or terrified: a flurried person is discomposed but not in despair — busy, self-interrupting, slightly comic. In the source sentence, “a whirl of bats frightened from slumber by their smoking torches flurried over them”, the verb carries the weather sense: the bats move as a sudden, disordered, swirling gust, startling the travellers. The adjective and the weather sense are connected by the shared image of a brief, irregular commotion, whether of air, snow, animals, or people.
