SCUTTLED is the simple past and past participle of the verb scuttle — to run with quick, short, hasty steps; to scamper, scurry, or bustle along, typically close to the ground, with a busy, slightly undignified energy rather than a long athletic stride. The word conjures small creatures and hasty retreats: a crab scuttling sideways across wet sand, a mouse scuttling behind a skirting board, a person scuttling out of an office to dodge a conversation. The essential semantic features are three: quickness (the pace is hurried), shortness (the steps are small and frequent, the body kept low or compact), and slight comic disorder (the motion is busy, flustered, and faintly funny rather than majestic). In the card's context — "With that the hobbit turned and scuttled inside his round green door, and shut it as quickly as he dared, not to seem rude" — the word captures Bilbo's swift, half-panicked, half-polite retreat from Gandalf's unsettling invitation into the safety of Bag End. The register is comic-domestic and faintly animal: a rabbit-hole bolt with the dignity slightly compromised by haste, exactly right for a hobbit with quick legs and a round door.

Polysemy — the full family of scuttle words: English has at least three distinct words spelled scuttle, and an exhaustive definition must separate them because they are etymologically unrelated homonyms that happen to share a spelling. (1) The running verb scuttle, whose past is scuttled: "to run with quick, short, hasty steps; to scamper" — this is the sense in the card. It first appears in Middle English in the 15th century and is plausibly a frequentative of scud, the family of Germanic words for swift, small-scale movement; it is the only sense in which the plural scuttled as used here. (2) The nautical verb scuttle: "to sink a ship deliberately by cutting or opening holes in its hull" (and, by extension, "to ruin or deliberately abandon a plan, project, or negotiation" — "the deal was scuttled", "they scuttled the proposal"). This verb is entirely separate in origin: it derives from the noun scuttle "a small opening or hatch in a ship's deck or hull", which English borrowed from Middle French escoutille (a hatchway), itself of Germanic-origin-in-Romance stock. (3) The noun coal scuttle: a metal or wooden bucket with a projecting lip, used to carry and scoop coal — a third, unrelated word, from Latin scutella "a dish or bowl" via Old French. These three homonyms regularly collide in dictionaries and in the learner's mind, and distinguishing them is an important part of mastering the word.

The noun sense of the running verb: Besides the verb, the running family also yields the noun scuttle, "a hurried, short-stepped run or flurry of movement" ("he disappeared in a scuttle"), and the derived noun scuttlebutt — a 19th-century naval term. Strictly, scuttlebutt belongs with the nautical hatch scuttle (the "butt" being a cask of drinking water near a scuttle-hatch around which sailors exchanged gossip), so it has been adopted into ordinary English to mean "rumour, gossip" — a semantic extension well worth noting.

Grammatical notes: Scuttled is a regular weak verb form (scuttle, scuttled, scuttled), so the -ed form carries both the simple past and the past participle; in the narrative it is the simple past, recording a completed, quick retreat. It takes the infinitive or gerund construction after it ("scuttled inside", "scuttled to fetch"), and the action is characteristically intransitive, though it often takes a directional complement. Do not confuse the spelling with skittled (to knock over, as in skittles) or skedaddled (to run away, colloquial American).

Register and tone: Modern use is conversational and slightly affectionate; the word belongs to lively narrative prose, children's stories, and animal description rather than to formal or technical writing. Its undertone of undignified haste makes it the natural opposite of strode — and indeed Tolkien sets the hobbit's scuttle in explicit contrast to the wizard's stride.

  • Sense 1 — movement: to run with quick, short, hasty steps; to scamper, scurry, or bustle (the card's sense).
  • Sense 2 — nautical (different word): to sink a ship by cutting holes in it.
  • Sense 3 — figurative (from Sense 2): to ruin or deliberately abandon a plan or project ("the talks were scuttled").
  • Sense 4 — noun (movement): a hurried, short-stepped run ("off in a scuttle").
  • Sense 5 — noun (nautical): a small hatch or opening in a ship's deck or hull.
  • Sense 6 — noun (household): a coal scuttle, the bucket for carrying coal.
  • Sense 7 — derived: scuttlebutt "gossip, rumour" (naval in origin).
