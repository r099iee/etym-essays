stupefy (verb, transitive; stupefies, stupefied, stupefying) means to stun into numbness or senselessness, whether by a blow, a drug, shock, amazement, or crushing boredom. In the source sentence it means to benumb fish with plant narcotics so they float and can be caught. More broadly it covers any making-stupid: to drug, daze, dumbfound, or bore into dullness.

Comprehensive senses in modern use:

  • To benumb with a drug or intoxicant (pharmacological core): To dull the nervous system with narcotics, anaesthetic, alcohol, or fish poison. Surgeons once stupefied patients with opium; criminals stupefy victims with spiked drinks; Tahitians stupefied fish with huteo pulp. This preserves Latin stupor as physical numbness.
  • To stun with a blow or shock (physical sense): To knock senseless. A fall stupefied him; the blast stupefied the crew. Near-synonym to stun, but stupefy implies lingering daze rather than instant knockout.
  • To overwhelm with amazement (psychological sense, now most frequent): To astound, dumbfound, leave agape. The news stupefied the nation; her virtuosity stupefies critics. Here no chemistry is involved, only cognitive overload.
  • To dull with monotony (weakened colloquial sense): To bore into torpor. Stupefying paperwork, a stupefying commute, stupefied by television. The drug metaphor survives but the dose is boredom.
  • Reflexive / passive moral sense (literary): To become hebetated by habit. Stupefied by grief, by poverty, by power — a state where sensibility is lost through suffering or excess.

Domain notes refine the choice. In medicine and law, stupefy signals drugging: stupefied and robbed, found stupefied, to stupefy livestock before slaughter (more humanely, to stun). In sport and journalism, stupefy means to shock: to stupefy the favourites, a stupefying upset. In education and criticism, stupefying means mind-numbing: stupefying jargon, stupefying detail. In theology and older literature, to be stupefied is to be made foolish by sin or wonder, echoing Latin stultify.

Polysemy logic is unified by the image of a blow to sensibility. Latin stupēre already meant both body-stunned and mind-amazed; English simply distributed those across contexts (blow, drug, news, boredom). The fish passage shows the bridge: a chemical that numbs gills is described as persuading fish into cooperation, blending pharmacology with psychology. Secondary metaphoric shifts therefore include:

  • From body to mind: numbed limb → numbed intellect (stupefied with drink → stupefied with sorrow).
  • From acute to chronic: knocked out once → dulled by routine (stupefying job).
  • From transitive to participial adjective: stupefied (dazed state) and stupefying (causing daze) now function as adjectives: a stupefied expression, a stupefying revelation.

Grammar: always transitive (to stupefy someone/something); intransitive use is obsolete. Formality is standard to slightly literary; stun and daze are plainer, amaze lighter. Adverbial collocations include utterly stupefied, half stupefied, stupefied with horror, stupefying ignorance. Do not confuse with stultify (to make futile or prove absurd, a legal-logical term) though they overlap in making stupid.
