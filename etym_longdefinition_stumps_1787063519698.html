Stumps (plural of stump) are the short projecting bases that remain when the upper part of something — most often a tree — has been cut down, broken off, or destroyed. A tree stump is the truncated trunk left standing after felling: a short, blunt, rooted column of wood, usually flat-topped where the saw has cut, often with the beginnings of the root system spreading below ground. The word's range, however, extends far beyond the forest: a stump is any short blunt projecting remnant — the stump of a severed limb, of a tooth, of a pencil, of a candle, of a tail — and the plural stumps commonly designates a scattered collection of such remains, as in the field of blackened stumps that Tolkien describes in The Hobbit, broken and blackened stumps left where a vanished forest once stood.

The physical idea is consistency of shape and fate: a stump is what is left when the long thing has been cut short, and it implies both the former presence of the whole and the violence or decay that removed it. The word is therefore inherently backward-looking, a monument to absence, and this gives it a powerful figurative life in descriptions of ruin, disaster, and war — the stumps of a fallen forest, the stumps of ruined buildings, the stumps of a generation's hopes.

Primary senses

  • Tree stump: the base of a felled, broken, or burned tree left standing in the ground; the principal and oldest sense, recorded in English since the early fourteenth century.
  • Any short projecting remnant: the stump of a limb (an amputated arm or leg), the stump of a tooth, the stump of a tail, the stump of a candle or pencil. Here the word denotes a truncated remnant of anything long.
  • Plural stumps, a field of remains: a group or scatter of such truncated bases, especially of trees, as an image of a cleared, felled, or devastated land.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Cricket (the wickets): in cricket, each wicket consists of three upright wooden stumps supporting two bails; a batsman is bowled when the ball knocks a bail from the stumps, and stumped when the wicketkeeper breaks the stumps while the batsman is out of his ground. The stumps is thus the technical name of the wicket posts, and stumps in the phrase that's stumps (drawn at the close of play) marks the end of a day's cricket.
  • To stump (verb, to perplex): I'm stumped = I am baffled, unable to answer. This sense, recorded from the early nineteenth century, probably derives from the idea of being brought to a standstill, as if caught on a stump or up a stump (stuck, in difficulty). It is a common informal verb.
  • To stump (verb, US political campaigning): stumping for a candidate, on the stump, a stump speech — from the practice, recorded from the 1820s in frontier America, of climbing onto a tree stump to address an outdoor crowd. The stump became the improvised platform of democracy, and the verb means to campaign by making speeches.
  • To stump (verb, to travel heavily): dialectal and older sense to walk heavily or clumsily, to stump along, akin to plodding; related to the heavy base-like shape.
  • To stir one's stumps: a dialectal idiom meaning to hurry up, bestir oneself, get moving — the stumps here are one's legs.

Grammar and register

  • Stump is a count noun: a stump, the stumps, a stump-field. The plural is the natural form for a landscape of remains, as in the Tolkien context.
  • Register ranges from neutral and literal (forestry, cricket) to informal (I'm stumped) to dated dialect (to stir one's stumps).
  • As a verb it has three live senses: to perplex (informal), to campaign (US), and (dialectal) to trudge heavily.

Collocations and phrases

  • tree stump — the felled base of a tree.
  • blackened/broken stumps — stumps left by fire or ruin (the Tolkien image).
  • stump speech — a campaign address (US).
  • on the stump — actively campaigning (US).
  • stumped — baffled, unable to answer.
  • up a stump — in a fix, at a loss (dialectal).
  • stir one's stumps — get moving (dialectal).
  • the stumps (cricket) — the wicket posts; stumps drawn closes play.

Semantic relations

  • Near-synonyms for the general sense short blunt remnant: stub, butt, remnant, remainder; for tree remains: trunk (the full standing body, by contrast), bole, roots.
  • Antonyms and contrasts: a stump is the opposite of a full tree, a complete limb, a whole post; it is defined by truncation. The word always implies the former whole and the cut.
