WHEELBARROW is a transparent English compound of two native Germanic roots, each with its own chain. CHAIN 1 — WHEEL: 1. PIE *kʷel- "to turn, revolve, move around" (3rd millennium BCE); the source of a wide family of turning-words across the Indo-European languages. 2. PIE *kʷé-kʷl-o- "wheel" (reduplicated noun, literally "that which turns"); attested by its descendants, e.g. Sanskrit cakrá- "wheel", Greek kúklos "circle, ring, wheel" (source of English cycle), and the Germanic line. 3. Proto-Germanic *hwehwlą / *hwēlą "wheel" (regular Germanic reflex: *kʷ- became *hw-, and the second reduplicated syllable was lost). 4. Old English hwēol "wheel" (attested from the 8th century). 5. Middle English whele, wheel (13th–15th centuries; the cluster hw- simplified to w- during this period). 6. Modern English wheel "wheel" — first element of the compound WHEELBARROW. CHAIN 2 — BARROW: 1. PIE *bher- "to carry, bear" (the root of English bear "to carry"). 2. Proto-Germanic *beraną "to carry, bear". 3. Old English beran "to carry, bear" (attested from the 8th century). 4. Old English bearwe "barrow, basket, carrying-frame" (a formed noun built on the same carry-root; attested in Old English records). 5. Middle English barwe, barow, barowe "barrow, carrying frame" (13th–15th centuries). 6. Modern English barrow "carrying frame" — second element of the compound WHEELBARROW. COMPOUND PATH: - wheel + barrow — English wheelbarrow, first recorded c. 1350 as a one-wheeled handcart with two rear handles and a front wheel. - Note: the related noun barrow "burial mound" is a different Old English word (beorg "hill, mound", from PIE *bhergh- "high"); it has no connection to the "carrying" barrow of wheelbarrow.
