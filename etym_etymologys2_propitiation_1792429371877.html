Note on historical debate and semantic bridge: Classical philologists (including Michiel de Vaan and Ernout-Meillet) trace Latin propitius ("favorable") to the PIE root *peth₂- ("to fly, rush"), formed with the spatial prefix pro- ("forward"). The original archaic sense belonged to Roman augural divination, characterizing a bird of omen that "flies forward toward the augur" or "swoops favorably near," indicating the gods' willing inclination toward the petitioner. Over centuries, this specialized augural observation shifted into a general moral adjective ("gracious, benevolent"), which then generated the transitive causative verb propitiāre ("to make someone well-disposed / turn away their anger"). In early Christian Latin, Saint Jerome selected propitiātiō to render the Hebrew kappōreth ("mercy-seat") and Greek hilasmos, permanently fusing Roman sacrificial appease-ment with biblical redemption.
