The ancestry of impromptu reaches unusually deep for so light and festive a word, beginning with one of the oldest reconstructible verbs in Indo-European, the root *h₁em-, meaning to take or distribute. From this root Latin built the everyday commercial verb emere, to take or buy, the same verb that survives in exempt, redeem, and sample, all of them originally transactions of taking. Prefixed with pro-, forth or forward, emere became prōmere, to bring forth or fetch out, the verb of the storekeeper carrying goods from the back room and of the speaker producing words from memory. Its past participle promptus, literally fetched out, slid by the most natural metaphor in the world into ready, at hand, prepared, because what has been fetched out is what stands ready for use.

From promptus the Romans formed the adverbial phrase in promptu, in a state of readiness, and its extended form in promptu esse, to be on hand or available. This was sober administrative Latin, the language of accounts kept ready for inspection and troops held ready for marching, with no association whatever with poetry or wit. The phrase lived on through medieval Latin into the learned French of the seventeenth century, where it was compressed into the single fashionable word impromptu, first recorded in the 1640s for a short verse composed on the spot at a salon's demand. French polite society had made a game of instantaneous composition, and the old Roman readiness found glamorous new employment as the name of the game.

English borrowed the French word within a generation, first in the 1660s for occasional verse of the same salon kind, and the borrowing reveals how prestige borrowing works: English gentlemen wanted the French trick and took the French name with it. Through the eighteenth century impromptu remained chiefly literary, denoting poems, toasts, and compliments dashed off in company, and dictionaries defined it against the plainer native resources of offhand and unprepared. The word's spelling never naturalized, keeping its French final u and its foreign air, which helped preserve its complimentary tone: calling a speech impromptu rather than unprepared wrapped the same fact in continental admiration.

The nineteenth century gave the word its two great extensions. First, musicians adopted it as a genre title: Jan Václav Voříšek, Schubert, and Chopin published short lyrical piano pieces as Impromptus, works of careful art presented as graceful improvisations, freezing the fiction of spontaneity into printed notation. Second, general usage widened from verse to every kind of performance, so that speeches, sermons, concerts, and ceremonies could all be impromptu. The phonetic shape steadied into the modern English pronunciation with stress on the second syllable, while the meaning completed its long slide from kept in readiness beforehand to produced with no readiness at all, a reversal accomplished so gradually that no generation noticed the contradiction.

In the twentieth century the word democratized completely, moving from salons and concert halls into offices, streets, and sitting rooms. The impromptu meeting, the impromptu party, and the impromptu press conference made spontaneity an organizational virtue, and the approving overtone proved essential: institutions that could act impromptu advertised themselves as agile rather than sloppy. Tolkien's impromptu orchestra of young hobbits, bursting into a dance-tune with no rehearsal, catches the word at its most characteristic modern pitch, where readiness means high spirits and whatever instruments are at hand. From a reconstructed verb of taking, through Roman storehouses, French salons, and Viennese piano stools, the word arrives in the present as English's permanent compliment to the unprepared triumph.

  • PIE taking root: *h₁em-, to take or distribute, the deep ancestor behind Latin buying, redeeming, and sampling.
  • Latin readiness: prōmere and promptus turn fetching-forth into preparedness, and in promptu names the state of being on hand.
  • French salon game: 1640s compression into impromptu, witty verse composed on demand as fashionable social proof.
  • English borrowing: 1660s adoption for occasional verse, keeping French spelling and complimentary tone intact.
  • Musical and general widening: 19th-century piano Impromptus plus extension to speeches, ceremonies, meetings, and parties, completing the slide from readiness to spontaneity.
