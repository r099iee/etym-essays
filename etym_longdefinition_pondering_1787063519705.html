pondering is the present participle and gerund of the verb ponder, and in the source text — Tolkien's The Hobbit — it pictures a mind at work over a mysterious map: the would-be king "would often borrow Thorin's map and gaze at it, pondering over the runes and the message of the moon-letters Elrond had read." At its core, to ponder is to think about something slowly, carefully, and at length; the participle pondering captures that act in progress — a mind actively hefting and balancing ideas, exactly as scales weigh objects.

PRIMARY SENSE — CAREFUL, DEEP, SUSTAINED THOUGHT

  • To consider something with sustained attention and deliberation: "she pondered the question for a long time", "he pondered his next move", "we pondered the strange message all night".
  • The object of pondering is almost always something weighty: a problem, a decision, a mystery, an offer, a course of action — never a triviality; you do not ponder what to have for breakfast.
  • The time frame is extended: one ponders over hours, days, or across a whole journey; pondering is slow, deliberate thought, the opposite of a snap judgment or a reflex.
  • It implies an active, deliberative mind — pondering is not daydreaming or reverie; it is working on a problem, turning it over, weighing possibilities against one another, testing conclusions.
  • In the source text the object is precisely a puzzle: the runes and the hidden moon-letters. To ponder over them is to stare, weigh, and interpret — to try to extract hidden sense from ancient marks, a perfect emblem of patient intellectual labour.

SECONDARY SENSE — TO WEIGH, TO DELIBERATE

  • The verb retains a shadow of its original, physical meaning (see the etymologies): to weigh, to balance alternatives in the mind — "he pondered the advantages and disadvantages of each plan", "they pondered whether to go or to stay".
  • This is the "scales of judgment" sense: the mental action is modelled directly on the physical action of weighing goods on a balance, an image already fixed in Classical Latin.
  • Constructions: ponder + direct object (ponder the future), ponder + over/on/about + object (ponder over a problem), or ponder + an interrogative clause (pondered how to proceed, pondered what it might mean).
  • The noun pondering (gerund) names the activity itself: "after much pondering, she agreed" — the collective act of thinking a matter through.

REGISTER, TONE, AND RELATED FORMS

  • Register: pondering is neutral but carries a literary, reflective tone; it is more dignified and slow than thinking, more focused than musing, less agonised than brooding, more leisurely than calculating.
  • ponderous is a related but distinct word from the same Latin family, meaning "heavy, weighty, laborious" — do not confuse the two; ponderous weighs down, pondering weighs up.
  • Common in philosophical, ethical, and narrative prose: a character ponders a moral dilemma; a scholar ponders an ancient text; a traveller ponders the road ahead; a detective ponders a clue.
  • Because it implies weight of subject, pondering flatters its object: whatever is being pondered is presumed to deserve deep, serious thought — which is exactly why Tolkien gives the act to a would-be king studying his inherited map.

SEMANTIC BRIDGE — FROM WEIGHING GOLD TO WEIGHING IDEAS

  • The Latin ancestor ponderare meant literally "to weigh" with scales, and English has never quite lost the image of the balance; a modern speaker who "weighs the options" is using the same metaphor.
  • Pondering in the source text is doubly apt: the runes are themselves a kind of weight to be measured, and the map is a treasure to be assessed — the physical and the mental weighing coincide in one scene.
  • Modern usage has fully transferred the metaphor to the mind: nobody imagines actual scales, yet the sense of balancing considerations survives in every use of the word.

GRAMMAR AND FORMS

  • Base verb: ponder; third-person singular: ponders; past tense and past participle: pondered; present participle and gerund: pondering.
  • In the source text the -ing form is a free (adverbial) participle describing the subject's accompanying action: he gazed at the map, pondering over the runes.
  • The verb is both transitive (ponder the question) and intransitive with a preposition (ponder over/on/about something); the source text uses the intransitive construction with over.

SUMMARY

  • pondering = the act of thinking about something carefully, deeply, and at length, with the connotation of weighing considerations against one another.
  • It belongs to the vocabulary of reflection, deliberation, and contemplation, and in the source text it captures a quiet, absorbed moment of mental labour over a mysterious map.
