• Beleaguered: Similar because both mean surrounded and pressured from all sides; differs because beleaguered is now chiefly figurative (a beleaguered government) and carries weariness and harassment, while besieged is concrete, military and formal.
  • Blockaded: Similar because both describe cutting off access to a place; differs because a blockade (especially naval or economic) isolates a place without necessarily attacking it, whereas a siege combines encirclement with pressure and often assault.
  • Invested: Similar because in military language to invest a town is exactly to besiege it; differs in being a technical, formal term now largely confined to historical writing.
  • Surrounded: Similar because both mean enclosed on all sides; differs because surrounded is neutral and physical and implies no hostility or military machinery.
  • Encircled: Similar because both mean ringed around; differs because encircled stresses the geometric ring and can be friendly or scenic (a town encircled by hills).
  • Hemmed in: Similar because both mean confined so that movement is impossible; differs in register — hemmed in is informal and lacks the formal structure of siegecraft.
  • Beset: Similar for figurative pressure (beset by doubt); differs because beset is a passive verbal form used almost entirely of troubles and anxieties, with no military image.
  • Embattled: Similar because both describe being under attack; differs because embattled implies active fighting and a defensive stance, not necessarily complete encirclement.
  • Laid siege to: Similar because the phrase means exactly to besiege; differs in being periphrastic and emphasising the active operation rather than the resulting state of being besieged.
  • Under siege: Similar because it names the same condition; differs in being a prepositional description rather than a participle adjective.
  • Cut off: Similar because both describe isolation; differs because cutting off may be partial (only routes severed), while a siege encloses completely.
  • Trapped: Similar because both mean unable to escape; differs because trapped is general and often accidental, and requires no besieger.
  • Inundated / deluged (figurative): Similar for the figurative sense of overwhelming attention (besieged by requests); differs in being a pure metaphor of quantity — a flood of things — rather than of hostile pressure from all sides.
