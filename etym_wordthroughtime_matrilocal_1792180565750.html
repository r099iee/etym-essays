- Late 19th c. (1870s–1890s) — Coined in Victorian anthropology (by pioneers such as Edward Burnett Tylor and Lewis Henry Morgan) alongside patrilocal to construct evolutionary models of human kinship, carrying strong academic connotations of "primitive" social stages.
- Early 20th c. (1900s–1930s) — Adopted into systematic ethnography by Franz Boas, Bronislaw Malinowski, and Edward Westermarck; stripped of crude unilineal evolutionary bias and redefined as a neutral, functional classification of post-marital domestic residence.
- Mid-20th c. (1940s–1960s) — Standardized in comparative structural anthropology (notably George Peter Murdock’s Social Structure, 1949), where rigorous distinctions between matrilocal and uxorilocal were fiercely debated and codified.
- Late 20th c. (1970s–1990s) — Re-examined by feminist anthropologists (such as Eleanor Leacock and Marilyn Strathern) to challenge patriarchal assumptions about universal female subjugation; extended into sociobiology and primatology to describe female philopatry in animal populations.
- 21st c. — Retains established ethnographic precision while being increasingly deployed metaphorically in modern urban sociology and economic demography to describe contemporary multigenerational family clustering around maternal grandparents.
