STOLE is the past tense of the strong verb steal ("steal, stole, stolen"), whose core sense is to take another person's property without permission or right. In the sentence supplied - Bilbo "stole from the shadow of the doorway" - the word carries the secondary but ancient intransitive sense of moving quietly, stealthily, and unobserved, rather than committing theft. The word is thus Janus-faced: on the one hand a common criminal verb, on the other a delicate verb of furtive motion used constantly in literary prose. It is also, by pure chance, a homograph of an unrelated noun stole (a long scarf or an ecclesiastical vestment), a fact that any thorough treatment must flag.

Primary senses

  • Transitive - to take dishonestly: to appropriate someone else's money, goods, property, or ideas secretly, without consent and usually with the intention of keeping them. "They stole the gold cup." This is the default modern meaning and the legal sense.
  • Intransitive - to move furtively: to go or come quietly, slowly, and secretly, so as not to be noticed. "He stole out of the house at dawn." Attested in English from the Old English period and preserved especially in literary and narrative prose.
  • To obtain by indirect or wrongful means: "to steal a kiss", "to steal a glance", "to steal a march on someone" - taking something intangible, brief, or temporal. The register softens from crime toward audacity or romance.
  • Sport - a stolen base: in baseball, a runner advances to the next base while the pitcher is delivering the ball; the "stolen base" is a recognised statistic. "He stole second base in the fifth inning."
  • To win over or win unexpectedly: "to steal the show" (to outshine everyone), "to steal someone's heart" (to win their love), "to steal a win" (to snatch victory against expectations). Wholly figurative, carrying a flavour of audacity and charm.
  • Software and design jargon: informal "steal" of an idea, design, or code - a neutral-to-joking register meaning to copy or reuse something effectively, often with the blessing of the community ("steal this design system").

The noun homograph

  • An ecclesiastical vestment: a long, narrow band of silk or other fabric worn over the shoulders by clergy, often decorated with crosses; it hangs down at the front and is worn at the celebration of the liturgy.
  • A woman's long scarf or shawl: a fashionable garment of fur or silk worn loosely over the shoulders, popular in the 20th century - the "evening stole".

These noun senses descend from Greek and Latin (Greek stolē, Latin stola), entirely separate from the Germanic verb, and their identity of spelling with the past tense "stole" is accidental.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridges

The bridge between "take dishonestly" and "move quietly" is a natural one: thieves must move silently, so the verb came to describe the manner of movement itself - "he stole away". From that developed the modern emphasis on silence and secrecy in idioms like "steal a moment". The figurative uses (show, heart, base) all presuppose an action achieved surreptitiously, unexpectedly, or effortlessly, which is why the word feels light, clever, and winning rather than purely criminal.

Register and nuance

  • Literary and high-register for motion; colloquial and everyday for theft.
  • Neutral in legal contexts, but strongly negative in moral contexts (thieving, stealing from the poor).
  • Playful and admiring in figurative uses ("she stole the evening").

The supplied context exploits exactly this duality: Bilbo has, after all, just taken the cup - he has literally stolen it - and yet "stole from the shadow of the doorway" describes his creeping motion. Tolkien layers both senses in a single sentence.
