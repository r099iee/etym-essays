FAGGOTS (a plural noun, from the singular faggot) are bundles of twigs, small branches, or brushwood bound tightly together and used chiefly as fuel — the classic kindling-and-firewood unit of the pre-industrial hearth, oven, and forge. In the welcome-song of the Elves of Rivendell in The Hobbit, chapter III, the line "The faggots are reeking, / The bannocks are baking!" paints a warmly sensual picture of the Last Homely House: resinous faggots are smouldering (the archaic verb to reek meant "to emit smoke or steam"), their smoke curling upward while the bread rises on the hearth. The word's rough, rustic heaviness plays against the Elvish song's fluting lightness, grounding the scene in snug domestic reality — wood gathered, bound, lit, and burning for the travellers' comfort. The tone is cosy, hospitable, and faintly antique: a haven that smells of pine, woodsmoke, and baking bread after the chill of the mountains.

Set apart from its near-neighbours, FAGGOTS differ from kindling (small dry splinters used only to start a fire), from logs and firewood (larger, seasoned pieces that burn long), and from brushwood (loose, unbound scrub). A military fascine is a related bundle used in fortification and road-making. The word is also a homonym of the British butcher's dish faggots (balls of offal and breadcrumbs, 1851) and of a much later pejorative slang term (1914) — neither of which belongs to Tolkien's text, where the meaning is unambiguously the firewood bundle.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings

  • Culinary sense (1851): faggots are traditional British meatballs of minced offal — liver, heart, and pork — bound with breadcrumbs and onion, wrapped in caul fat (or bacon) and baked in gravy; a West Midlands and Welsh delicacy. The semantic bridge is the "bundle": the wrapped parcel of offal mimics the bound bundle of sticks.
  • Ironmongery: in the old metal trades a faggot was a bundle of iron bars or scrap steel welded together for forging — any bound collection of rods.
  • Historical punishment: from the 1550s "fire and faggot" meant death at the stake for heresy; the faggot was literally the bundle of firewood heaped around the condemned person — a grim extension of the fuel sense.
  • Military engineering: a fascine, the Latin-derived cousin, is a bundle of brushwood used to fill ditches, build ramparts, and consolidate marshy ground; the same Latin family appears in fasces, the rods-and-axe insignia of Roman magistrates.
  • Confectionery (1733): faggots once meant candied orange peel — strips of peel rolled into little bundles.
  • Needlework: fagoting (or faggoting) is a decorative openwork embroidery stitch in which threads are bundled and bound into a lacelike seam.
  • Music: Italian fagotto, from the same "bundle" root, named the bassoon because its wooden sections bundle together; English later borrowed bassoon separately from French.
  • Verbal use: to faggot is to bind into bundles — still current among woodmen and thatchers.
  • Later slur (US, 1914): a pejorative slang noun for a homosexual man, unrelated in origin to the firewood word — a sound-alike homonym that must never be read back into older texts such as Tolkien's.
  • Earlier abuse of women (16th c.): "an old faggot" was a term of contempt for a woman, from the idea of a worthless bundle of refuse.

The semantic engine is always "bundle": anything made of gathered, bound material — sticks, iron rods, peel, wool threads, or contemptuously a person — could claim the name. In Tolkien's verse only the fuel sense is active, and it is the oldest, most homely layer of the word.
