WANTON: An adjective with four connected strands of meaning: (1) deliberate, unprovoked, and cruel or malicious, especially in law and crime; (2) sexually immodest, lascivious, or promiscuous; (3) playful, extravagant, capricious, or luxuriantly unrestrained; and (4), in historical use, undisciplined, ill-bred, rebellious, or unruly. PRIMARY SENSE IN MODERN USE: In contemporary English, wanton most often describes harm that is pointless and cruel — destruction carried out with no military, economic, or rational justification, simply because it can be done. "Wanton destruction of historic monuments" and "wanton disregard for safety" name acts committed with conscious indifference to the suffering or loss they cause. This is the legal sense: under criminal law, wanton conduct is reckless or malicious behaviour showing extreme indifference to human life or property, a standard ingredient of charges such as wanton endangerment, wanton cruelty, and wanton neglect. SENSES AND CONTEXTS:
  • Law and crime: "wanton destruction," "wanton endangerment," "wanton negligence" — malicious, reckless action executed with conscious indifference to consequences; the statutory sense since the 19th century.
  • Sexual and moral: a wanton woman, wanton desires — lascivious, immodest, promiscuous, deliberately unchaste; the sense dominant from the 16th to the 19th centuries and still a literary or moralistic word for unbridled lust.
  • Playful and aesthetic: a wanton breeze, wanton curls, wanton children, wanton growth of vines — free, playful, unconstrained, luxuriant movement, the "wanton" of lyric poetry from the Renaissance onward.
  • Historical: rebellious, undisciplined, ill-bred — the original sense of "untrained, unruly," applied to servants, children, and the wild.
  • Figurative and adverbial: "wanton waste," "wanton folly," "wanton cruelty" — extravagance and carelessness applied to resources, money, and pain.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE: Middle English wantowen (later wantoun) is a compound of the prefix wan- "lacking, un-" and the past participle towen (also togen) "brought up, trained, educated," from Old English tēon "to pull, draw, bring up, rear." The literal meaning is "untrained, untaught, ill-bred": a wanton creature is one that has not been pulled into line, not disciplined, not reared to know its place. From this deficit of discipline the word grew in three directions — the unruly and rebellious (undisciplined), the playful and extravagant (unconstrained by rule), and the unchaste (unrestrained by morality) — and, in modern legal usage, the cruelly careless (unrestrained by conscience). Every modern sense is a development of the single idea of being ungoverned. REGISTER, COLLOCATIONS, AND LITERARY ATTESTATIONS: Wanton is a word of moral and legal judgement, at home in statutes, sermons, journalism, and literary prose, and almost never neutral: to call an act wanton is to condemn it as needless and cruel, and to call a person wanton is to accuse them of ungoverned desire or behaviour. It collocates with destruction, cruelty, waste, neglect, endangerment, disregard, and violence in the legal and journalistic senses, and with breeze, curls, growth, play, and fancy in the poetic senses; the adverb wantonly ("wantonly destroyed") and the noun wantonness ("the wantonness of youth") complete the family. The word's literary career is long and rich: Shakespeare uses it repeatedly, both for luxuriant playfulness (wanton winds, wanton growth) and for unchaste desire, while Milton and the seventeenth-century moralists made it a staple of warning against lust; in the nineteenth century the legal sense enters the statutes of England and America, and in the twentieth the phrase "wanton destruction" becomes a standard charge in war reporting and international law. In the very sentence that produced this card — "it must not be wanton" — the adjective draws on all its centuries at once: whatever is at stake must not be left ungoverned, unprovoked, and cruel.
