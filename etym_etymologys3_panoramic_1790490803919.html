No English speaker ever said panoramic before about 1813, because its parent noun panorama did not exist before 1787 — one of the rare words whose birthday can be fixed to a patent application. The Irish miniature-painter Robert Barker, struck by the view from Calton Hill in Edinburgh, patented a method of painting vast cityscapes on the inside of a cylinder so that a spectator on a central platform saw a seamless 360-degree prospect. He needed a name that sounded learned and total, and stitched together two Greek elements already familiar from scientific coinages: pan- “all” and horama “sight, view, that which is seen.” Panorama was thus a deliberate neoclassical brand name, launched with Barker’s London rotunda in Leicester Square in 1791–1792, and panoramic followed within a generation as its routine adjective.

  • Greek building blocks: pas, pantos and horan, horama. Pan- comes from Greek pas (stem pant-) “all, every,” seen in pantheon, pantomime, pandemic. -orama comes from horama “sight, spectacle, thing seen,” noun of result from the verb horan (Attic horao) “to see, look, perceive,” whose deeper Indo-European ancestry is disputed (often referred to PIE *wer- “to perceive, be on guard,” source also of warden, aware, panorama’s cousin diorama). Neither element ever combined in antiquity: no ancient Greek said panorama. The compound is pure Enlightenment Greek-dressing, like photograph or telephone a century later.
  • 1787–1800: Barker’s patent and the rotunda craze. Barker’s specification (1787) and his Leicester Square Panorama (opened 1793) made panorama an instant fashionable noun; rivals copied the building and the word, adding cyclorama, diorama (1822), georama, cosmorama. Reviews in The Times and guidebooks taught Londoners to say “we went to the Panorama” the way later generations said “we went to the pictures.” The adjective appears as soon as critics needed to describe the experience: panoramic view, panoramic effect, panoramic drawing (OED’s first panoramic citations cluster 1813–1820).
  • 1810s–1850s: from building to landscape. Once the rotunda had taught the eye what total vision felt like, travellers carried the word outdoors. Byron, Scott, and Ruskin’s parents’ generation call Alpine prospects, sea views, and city rooftops panoramic without thinking of Leicester Square. The post-chaise passage belongs exactly here: a child around 1823–1824 treats four carriage windows as a private travelling panorama. Map-makers and guidebook writers follow — “panoramic map,” “panoramic sketch from the summit” — and the military adopts panoramic reconnaissance sketches of fortresses and coasts.
  • 1840s–1900s: photography mechanises the sweep. Fox Talbot’s and Daguerre’s successors immediately chase the panorama market: cylindrical daguerreotype plates, swing-lens cameras (Sutton, 1859), multi-plate albumen joins pasted into folding strips. Technical vocabulary multiplies — panoramic camera, panoramic negative, panoramic print — and the illustrated press sells “panoramic views of London / the Alps / Niagara” as engravings and then photographs. Dentistry later borrows the optics for the panoramic (panoral) X-ray that circles the jaw. Each invention re-anchors the adjective in hardware while keeping Barker’s promise: everything, at once, without turning away.
  • 1890s–1960s: cinema and the clipped verb pan. Film directors rediscover Barker’s problem — how to show everything — with the moving image: panning shots, panoramic screens, Cinerama’s three-projector 146-degree arc (1952), CinemaScope, IMAX. The verb to pan (first recorded c. 1916, clipped from panorama camera movement) detaches and becomes everyday grammar of film-making; hardly anyone panning a phone camera today hears Barker’s rotunda in it. Adjectival use rides along: panoramic shot, panoramic epic, panoramic western.
  • 20th–21st c.: the figurative takeover and digital ubiquity. Historians, novelists, and reviewers extend panoramic from sight to knowledge: panoramic histories, panoramic novels, panoramic surveys of an industry. Meanwhile consumer electronics return the word to literal vision with phone “pano mode,” 360-degree real-estate tours, panoramic sunroofs, and VR panoramas — Barker’s central platform rebuilt in pixels. Frequency soars in marketing precisely because the connotation (mastery, openness, luxury) sells. From patent-office coinage to phone button, the trajectory is one of continuous widening: a showman’s trademark became the default English word for seeing everything at once.

Note on debated ancestry: the Greek verb horao/horan is treated by Beekes as possibly Pre-Greek (substrate) rather than cleanly inherited from PIE *wer-, so the chain above the Greek stage is provisional; what is certain is the modern chain horama → panorama (1787) → panoramic (c. 1813), documented in patent rolls, playbills, and dictionaries.
