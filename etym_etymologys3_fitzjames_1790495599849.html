FitzJames joins two histories that meet only in Norman England: a Latin word for son turned into a prefix for bastards, and a Hebrew patriarch's name turned into a British king's name. Each must be traced separately before Scott fuses them.

Fitz begins in PIE *dʰeh₁(y)-, to suckle, nurse, produce — seen in Sanskrit dhayati (sucks), Greek thelys (female, nursing), Latin femina (woman) and fecundus (fruitful). Latin filius (son) and filia (daughter) are usually referred here as the suckling, via a formation *dʰeh₁-ileh₂- (de Vaan), though the details are debated and some prefer *bʰeh₂u- (to be, grow). What is certain is Classical filius (son, with f from *dh by Italic devoicing, ili intact). Spoken Latin keeps it; Gallo-Romance palatalises final -ius to -z: Old French filz, fiz (pronounced /fits/), written in charters as filius X in Latin and fiz X in French — Robert fiz Willame (Robert son of William). Norman scribes carry fitz to England in 1066, where it freezes as a hereditary surname prefix: Fitzgerald (son of Gerald), Fitzalan, Fitzhugh, and for kings' natural sons a discreet screen — FitzRoy (son of the king), FitzClarence (William IV's brood), FitzJames (sons of James II, and by courtesy of James V in fiction). Pronunciation keeps /fɪts/ with audible t; spelling varies Fitz-, fitz-, Fitz before standardisation.

  • James from heel to throne: Hebrew Ya'aqov (he grasps the heel, supplanter — Jacob holding Esau's heel in Genesis) passes via Greek Iakobos and Latin Iacobus to Late Latin Jacomus (with -m- for -b- by dissimilation) to Old French James, Jammes (/dʒaməs/) to Middle English James /dʒeɪmz/. The same Hebrew name gives Jacob, Jack, Jim, Diego, Jaime, Jacques. Scottish Stewarts favour it obsessively — James I to VII — so son of James in Scotland almost means son of the king. The real James FitzJames (1670-1734), son of James II and Arabella Churchill, raised in France, Marshal, Duke of Liria, embodies the type: acknowledged, Catholic, exiled, victorious at Almanza, dead at Philippsburg.
  • Scott's fusion (1810): seeking a disguise-name for James V wandering the Trossachs, Scott reaches for the bastardy-prefix and makes it honourable mystery: James Fitz-James, Knight of Snowdoun — James son-of-James, a king pretending to be his own natural son. The hyphen advertises artifice; the Snowdoun (old name for Stirling Castle rock) winks to knowing readers. The poem sells 20,000 copies in a year; FitzJames becomes stage, opera (Rossini's Donna del lago, 1819), diorama, and tartan-ware. Ruskin, born 1819, grows up when every drawing-room knows the reveal in the guard-room.
  • Sound and spelling: /fɪtsˈdʒeɪmz/ with secondary stress on Fitz and primary on James; the medial capital J is preserved against normal compounding to show the patronymic joint. Dropping the hyphen (FitzJames) is modern editorial and genealogical practice; Scott's first edition hyphenates (Fitz-James).

In sum, FitzJames is Norman son + Hebrew supplanter via French, made royal by Stewarts, made chivalric by Scott, and made moral by Ruskin's child-mind — a surname that became a king, and a king who taught a boy what kings should do.
