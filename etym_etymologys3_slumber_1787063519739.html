Roots in a Germanic sleep-word. Slumber descends from an old West Germanic verb for sleeping or dozing. Old English had the noun slūma (slumber, sleep) and a related verb slūmian (to doze, be drowsy), built on the Proto-Germanic base *slūm-. Beyond Germanic, the standard dictionaries connect the family to a reconstructed Indo-European root meaning "slack, limp, sluggish" — the same root family that gives English slow, slack, sloth, and sluggish. On this theory, to sleep is to go slack, to let go: the word names the physical release that sleep requires. The Indo-European stage is a reconstruction, however, and some etymologists treat it as uncertain; the Germanic evidence itself is solid and early.

Middle English remodeling. In the Middle English period the plain Old English verb was reshaped into a frequentative: slomeren (13th–14th centuries), the -er- suffix giving the sense of repeated or ongoing light sleep — dozing, nodding off, sleeping in fits. From this verb Middle English formed the noun slomber or sloumber, and by the fourteenth century the modern form slumber was in place, meaning light or gentle sleep. The word thus entered the literary language already carrying a nuance of softness, repetition, and gentleness that plain "sleep" lacked.

Chaucer to the Romantics. From its earliest literary use, slumber was a poet's word. Chaucer uses it; Spenser drapes it in dreamy allegory; Shakespeare, whose "sleep" knits up the ravelled sleeve of care, also reaches for slumber when sleep is gentle, enchanted, or threatened. Milton, Wordsworth, Keats, and the Romantics made slumber a fixture of meditative and nature poetry — slumbering landscapes, slumbering seas, the slumber of the spirit. The eighteenth century also fixed the religious and funereal euphemism "the sleep of death": eternal slumber, the last long slumber, where sleep becomes the gentle face of mortality.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Victorian literature keeps the word's poetry while adding sentiment and comfort — slumber as the soft, innocent sleep of children and the aged. In the twentieth century, American English domesticated the word in "slumber party" (a children's sleepover, attested from the 1930s–1940s), a friendly diminution of a thousand years of solemn poetry. At the same time, journalism and science adopted slumber for dormant states: slumbering volcanoes, slumbering giants of industry, the slumbering economy — metaphors that turn geological and social forces into sleeping creatures that might wake.

Modern use and the dragon. Today slumber remains overwhelmingly literary and descriptive. It is the natural word for enchanted or mighty sleep in fantasy — Tolkien's sleeping dragon, whose "fires were low in slumber," is a perfect modern instance: the slumber of a creature whose fires are banked but not dead. The word's thousand-year path — from a Germanic "going slack," through frequentative dozing, through the allegories of the Renaissance and the meditations of the Romantics, to the fantasy and sentiment of the present — has left it permanently poised between peacefulness and latent power.

Summary. Old English slūma → Middle English slomeren (to doze) → slomber/slumber (light, gentle sleep) → the poets' word for peaceful sleep, dormant nature, and eternal rest → the modern literary standard for anything asleep that might yet wake.
