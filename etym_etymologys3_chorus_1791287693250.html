Chorus begins not as sound but as place and movement. Ancient Greek khoros meant first a dancing ground, typically a marked circle in a village or sanctuary, and then the band of dancers and singers who performed there. Etymologists find no secure Indo-European pedigree: Robert Beekes and others list khoros as probably Pre-Greek, a substrate word from the Aegean peoples who preceded Greek speakers, possibly akin to khortos "enclosed yard, pen." That origin would make sense: a chorus is an enclosed ring of bodies. Latin borrowed the word whole as chorus for troupes of singers and, crucially, for the chorus of Roman tragedy and comedy adapted from Greek models.

In Athenian tragedy (5th century BCE) the choros was a group of twelve to fifteen masked men who sang and danced the choral odes between episodes, commenting on the action, voicing communal wisdom, warning the hero, and embodying the city. Pindar's victory odes, Sophocles and Euripides, and Aristophanes' comic choruses gave the word its enduring double sense: a collective voice and the song that voice sings. Roman writers (Horace, Seneca) theorized the chorus as moral commentator, a view the Renaissance inherited wholesale.

Through Church Latin chorus acquired a musical-liturgical life: the choir that chants, the chancel where they stand (English choir / quire is the same word by a different French route). Medieval mystery plays and humanist school dramas revived the classical chorus as narrator-commentator. When English finally borrows chorus directly in the 1500s (earlier choir already existed via Old French quer), it means both the ancient tragic device and any band singing together. Shakespeare uses Chorus as a prologue figure in Henry V and Pericles: a single actor speaking for all.

  • Phonetic development: Greek kh [kʰ] > Latin ch [kʰ] > Late Latin /k/ > English /k/; initial ch spelling retained as a learned hellenism though pronounced /k/; vowel o > ɔː in RP /ˈkɔːrəs/ with non-rhotic lengthening; plural choruses / choroi preserves both native and learned options.
  • Semantic branches: dancing ground > dancing-singing band > tragic commentator > church choir > refrain sung together > any simultaneous utterance (a chorus of approval) > theatrical dancing ensemble (chorus line, chorus girls/boys).
  • Socio-cultural context: each revival (Renaissance tragedy, 18th-century oratorio, 19th-century musical comedy, 20th-century pop song structure of verse-chorus) re-anchors chorus to collective participation; democracy loves a chorus.
  • Literary attestations: Aristotle's Poetics on the chorus; Horace's Ars Poetica prescribing its moral role; Shakespeare's Choruses; Milton's Samson Agonistes with Hebrew-Greek chorus; T. S. Eliot's Murder in the Cathedral reviving ritual chorus; modern musical theatre distinguishing principals from chorus.
  • Doublet note: choir and chorus are etymological doublets: both from Greek khoros / Latin chorus, choir via Old French quer/quer with palatalization, chorus as direct learned borrowing preserving /k/.

In popular music chorus now most often means the refrain: the repeated hook everyone sings together after verses. In theatre it means the ensemble behind the stars, often dancing in unison (the chorus line). In ordinary speech it means any simultaneous outburst: a chorus of complaints, groans, or praise. All preserve the ancient core: many voices acting as one body in a ring.

The source insult "one of the chorus of male guests" draws exactly on the theatrical sense: not a principal with lines and agency, but one of the interchangeable background gentlemen in evening dress who fill Act 2 country-house scenes in touring musical comedy, smiling, singing together, adding tone but no individuality. To be called chorus is to be called scenery that sings.
