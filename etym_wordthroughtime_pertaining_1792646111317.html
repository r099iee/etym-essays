- [14th c. (c. 1330s–1380s)] — Entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman *pertenir* and Old French *partenir* from Latin *pertinēre*, initially applied in legal codes, royal charters, and ecclesiastical land grants to describe lands, rents, and rights belonging to a lord, abbey, or the Crown - [perception: regarded as a solemn, binding feudal and legal term of possession and rightful attachment]
- [15th–16th c. Renaissance & Humanism] — Broadened from material appurtenances to intellectual relevance, scholastic disputation, and rhetoric, describing arguments, evidence, and biblical passages that relate directly to a theological proposition or legal claim - [perception: valued as a precise, formal instrument of scholarly analysis, logic, and forensic disputation]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Statutory Law] — Cemented in statutory drafting, equity jurisprudence, and philosophical treatises (e.g., Locke, Blackstone), standardizing fixed legalistic formulas such as "pertaining thereto" and "matters pertaining to the crown" - [perception: seen as an indispensable, formal drafting term marking jurisdictional and proprietary boundaries]
- [19th–21st c. Contemporary & Lexicographical Standard] — Ubiquitous in academic writing, administrative regulation, and dictionary lexicography, where "pertaining to" functions as the definitive grammatical formula for defining relational adjectives and thematic scopes - [perception: perceived as an objective, dignified, and formally neutral connector of relevance and belonging]
