HEATHER is a single-root word, but its derivation from “heath” is debated and nuanced, so the chain is given with that caveat made explicit.

CHAIN — HEATHER:
Stage 1: PIE *kait-, meaning “wasteland, overgrown land, thicket, forest” (root proposed by Pokorny; c. 4000–3000 BCE).
Stage 2: Proto-Germanic *haiþiz / *haiþī, meaning “heath, wasteland, untilled open land” (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE).
Stage 3: Old English hǦþ, meaning “heath, untilled land, waste, heather-covered country” (attested c. 8th–11th c. CE).
Stage 4: Middle English heth(e), meaning “heath, wasteland; the plant growing there” (c. 1100–1500 CE).
Stage 5: Northern / Scots form hather, hether, hedder, meaning “the heath plant, Calluna vulgaris” — a formation on the same stem with the -er suffix (recorded from c. 1300 onward, e.g. in early Scots and northern Middle English).
Stage 6: Modern English heather, “the low evergreen flowering shrub Calluna vulgaris of moors and heaths” (from the 14th c.; standard by the 16th c.).

Debated / nuanced points: The precise route from “heath” to “heather” is not fully agreed. The most widely accepted view treats heather as “heath + -er,” a noun formed on the plant’s habitat, first in northern and Scots dialects (whence the recorded forms hather, hether, hedder). Some scholars, however, prefer a distinct development from a root meaning “shrub,” and a minority derive it from a metathesised variant of the same *kait- stock. The early spelling hather (with a) shows that the vowel was not stable and that the word developed independently in the north; no one stage is attested in an unbroken written chain, so the gap between Middle English heth(e) and the recorded hather/hedder forms is flagged here as the word’s one genuinely uncertain link.
