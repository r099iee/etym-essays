1. Greece: the Strangler is named. The Greek noun Σφίγξ (Sphinx) is a regular derivative of the verb σφίγγω (sphíngō), “to squeeze tight, bind, strangle” — the name declares the monster’s function. Hesiod’s Theogony (c. 700 BCE, line 326) already knows her as Φίξ (Phix), the Boeotian spelling, “ruin to the Cadmeans”; the classical form Σφίγξ is standard from the 5th century BCE. The verb’s own pre-history is obscure: Beekes treats σφίγγω as of uncertain, possibly Pre-Greek origin, and no Indo-European root can be reconstructed with confidence — one of the rare cases where the most famous monster of Greek myth carries a possibly pre-Greek name at her core. A romantic theory that the Greek word adapts an Egyptian term is generally rejected: Egyptian sphinxes were called šsp-ꜥnḫ, “living image”, an unrelated phrase.

2. The monster’s poetry and the riddle’s text. The Sphinx entered Attic tragedy with full force: Aeschylus’ Seven Against Thebes alludes to her; Sophocles’ Oedipus Tyrannus (c. 428 BCE) turns on the solved riddle; Euripides’ Phoenissae (c. 409 BCE) stages Oedipus’ triumph. The riddle’s canonical wording — “what is that which has one voice, and is four-footed, two-footed and three-footed?” — is preserved in Apollodorus’ Library (3.5.8), and the verse form of the riddle in Athenaeus (Deipnosophistae 10.455). On vases and reliefs from the 7th century BCE — and on the François Vase (c. 570 BCE) — the winged, woman-headed lion devours youths; on grave monuments she is a daimon of transition, the watcher at the door of death. In word-history the Greek name means: strangling daemon → enigma on legs → guardian of thresholds, with her meaning darkening and lightening in turn as Athens rationalises myth into theatre.

  • 3. Egypt: the “living image” becomes a Sphinx. Egyptian šsp-ꜥnḫ (“living image”) named the recumbent lion-bodied statues with royal heads that guarded temples, tombs and processional ways from the Old Kingdom onward. The Great Sphinx of Giza (c. 2500 BCE, 73 metres long, carved from the bedrock of the pyramid plateau, generally associated with Khafre) was called in New Kingdom texts “Horus-of-the-Horizon” (Heru-em-akhet, Harmachis); the Dream Stela of Thutmose IV (c. 1400 BCE) records the god speaking to the sleeping prince and promising kingship if the sand is cleared from the colossus; and the avenue of sphinxes linking Karnak and Luxor shows how the form multiplied along royal roads. When Greek and Roman visitors reached Egypt, the Greek word “sphinx” crossed over to these monuments: Diodorus and Strabo describe the statues, and Pliny (Natural History 36.77) counts the Giza colossi — the word now spanned two beings, the Greek riddler and the Egyptian guardian.
  • 4. Rome and the medieval world. Latin inherits Sphinx as the riddle-monster; Ovid and Statius keep the Theban version alive in poetry, and late-antique handbooks file a “sphinx” among the wonder-beasts. Medieval Latin encyclopaedism (Isidore of Seville’s Etymologies) preserves the creature as a text-marvel, while the Oedipus story circulates in romances and in the Latin tradition of the riddle, so that the question survives even when the sculpture is forgotten. The Middle English record (early 15th century, in the Troy-Book tradition) knows only the Theban monster of the riddle — Egypt’s stone faces are still centuries away from the European ear.
  • 5. English: from Lydgate to Egyptomania. English texts record “sphinx” from the early 15th century, always as the riddle-monster of Thebes. The Egyptian monument returns to the European consciousness with the Napoleonic expedition (1798–99): Denon’s engravings and the new Egyptology put the Great Sphinx before the reading public; the 19th century adds the fractured nose, the drifting sand, the “riddle of the Sphinx” as a phrase for anything ancient and unexplained, and the tourist photograph. The word’s centre of gravity shifts decisively from Greece to Giza: for modern speakers “the Sphinx” is above all the stone face in the desert — “as old as the world itself” in Wilde’s phrase — with the Greek strangler as a literary footnote.
  • 6. Figurative and commercial life. The 19th-century press turns “a sphinx” into the unreadable person — statesmen, bankers, railway directors sit sphinx-like through interviews; the metaphor enters ordinary prose. Freud’s Interpretation of Dreams (1900) makes the sphinx’s riddle the emblem of the Oedipus complex, fixing her in psychology. In the 20th century the word multiplies commercially and zoologically: the great sphinx on film posters, the “sphinx moth” (whose larva bows like a sphinx), the hairless “Sphynx” cat bred in Toronto in 1966 (the spelling deliberately Egyptianised), puzzle brands and hotel names — the stranger is now a friendly mask.
  • 7. Today. SPHINX still names three things at once: the monster of Thebes, the colossus of Giza whose face KNOWS everything and tells nothing, and any silent, puzzling person. The semantic thread through all three is the same — something that holds the answer and will not give it — which is precisely why the word, after 2,700 years, remains the language’s favourite figure for a secret.
