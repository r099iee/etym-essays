- Mid-15th c. — Late Middle English Latinate loan: borrowed from Late Latin obloquium, appearing in John Lydgate and devotional treatises to designate slander, evil-speaking, and verbal detraction.
- 16th c. — Renaissance polemics: adopted by Protestant and Catholic controversialists during the Reformation, each accusing the other of heaping false obloquy upon holy truths.
- 17th c. — Legal and political development: utilized in state trials and parliamentary debates to describe public defamation, treasonous slander against the crown, or the general dishonor of disgraced officials.
- 18th c. — Augustan rhetoric and Johnsonian prose: Samuel Johnson, Jonathan Swift, and Edmund Burke employ obloquy to analyze public opinion, political factionalism, and the immense social courage required to defy prevailing orthodoxies.
- 19th c. — Victorian historiography: becomes a hallmark of grand narrative historians like Thomas Babington Macaulay and Thomas Carlyle to chronicle the rise and fall of historical statesmen subjected to the shifting tides of popular sentiment.
- 20th c. – Present — Elevated literary and journalistic usage: maintained in high-level political commentary, legal history, and literature to describe the catastrophic storm of public scandal and collective moral indignation.
