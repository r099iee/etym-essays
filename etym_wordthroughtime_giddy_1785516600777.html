- [Old English divine-possession phase] - giddy (Old English gydig) meant 'possessed by a god, divinely inspired, ecstatic, or mad', carrying sacred-and-terrifying weight as the state of one overtaken by a supernatural power.
- [Middle English madness phase] - By the 13th-14th centuries the divine halo faded and the word meant plain 'insane, crazed, foolish', used of the deranged or the witless as a blunt term of contempt.
- [Early Modern character phase] - Giddy settled onto flighty, frivolous, careless personalities (a 'giddy young thing'), shifting from clinical madness to light moral disapproval of wantonness and instability.
- [Contemporary physical sense] - The dominant modern sense is physical dizziness and lightheadedness — the vertigo of heights — with the older ecstatic and moral colouring surviving only in phrases such as 'giddy with joy'.
