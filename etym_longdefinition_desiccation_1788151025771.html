Core meaning.Desiccation is the process of drying — the removal of moisture until little or none remains. It is stronger than simple drying: the Latin base siccus, "dry", with the prefix de- meaning "completely, thoroughly", yields the sense of drying out, to the point of brittleness, cracking, and lifelessness. In the passage above, the desiccation of regions that once held empires (Nineveh, Babylon) is the slow triumph of dryness over water — the landscape losing its dampness until agriculture, trade, and population dry up with it.

Senses and domains. The word is at home wherever dryness matters technically:

  • Chemistry and laboratory science: desiccation is the removal of water from a substance, usually in a desiccator over a desiccant such as silica gel or calcium chloride, to obtain a stable, weighable sample. This is the word's oldest modern home (17th-18th c.).
  • Food and industry: desiccated coconut, milk, and powders — moisture removed for preservation and shelf-life.
  • Earth science and climate: desiccation of lakes, soils, and regions — the geological and meteorological drying our passage describes; the desiccation theories of Victorian geography, the vanished lakes of the Great Basin, and modern studies of land degradation.
  • Medicine and biology: desiccation of tissues, cells, or wounds; pathological dryness of the skin; the desiccation phase in taphonomy (how bodies and fossils dry and preserve).
  • Cosmetic and dermatological: "desiccating" formulas strip the skin's moisture barrier; marketing distinguishes dehydrated from desiccated skin.
  • Figurative: a desiccated style, mind, or institution — dried of life, humour, or feeling; the word's contemptuous peak in political invective about dry, bloodless calculation.

Distinctions and semantic bridge.Desiccation differs from dehydration: dehydration is the loss of water, especially from a living body — a clinical, often reversible deficit — while desiccation implies removal to the point of complete dryness rather than mere shortage. A hillside dries; a desiccated leaf crumbles. The bridge to the figurative senses runs through the body: what desiccates loses suppleness, colour, and life, so a desiccated institution is one drained of the living moisture of passion and improvisation. The word is formal and exact, at home in scientific prose, yet it keeps a faint Gothic undertone of mummies, deserts, and rivers that once flowed — precisely the tone of Durant's sentence, in which the drying of Nineveh and Babylon is the death of civilizations.

Contrasts that sharpen the word. The family around desiccation rewards careful sorting. Desiccant is the agent — silica gel, calcium chloride, a drying salt — and desiccator the apparatus; both are purely technical. Desiccated, the participle, is the most flexible member: it describes the state (desiccated fruit, desiccated soil) and carries nearly all the figurative weight. The verb desiccate is transitive in science (the sample is desiccated) and intransitive in nature (the lake desiccated over decades). Arid and aridity describe the climate or condition of dryness rather than the act of becoming dry; drought is the weather event; desiccation alone names the process, and naming the process is what science, geography, and moralists all need. Note too the echo in salsa and sauce: Latin salsus, "salted", is not from siccus, but English's dry-sounding desiccate is easily — and wrongly — associated with the salty and the desiccated foods that preserve by both means, a folk-etymological confusion worth guarding against.
