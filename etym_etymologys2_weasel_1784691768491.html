PIE *weis- “to flow, melt, ooze; (of a foul fluid) to reek” (reconstructed; the same root area yields Latin virus “slime, poison” → English virus, and Sanskrit viṣa “poison” → Hindi vish) → Proto-Germanic *wisulōn / *wisō “weasel, literally the reeking (foul-smelling) animal, from the mustelid’s pungent anal musk” (reconstructed on Old English wesle, Old High German wisula, Old Saxon wisula, Old Norse visla; 1st millennium CE) → Old English wesle / wesole “weasel” (attested, recorded from the 9th c.) → Middle English wesel / wesele / weasel “weasel” (12th–15th c.) → Early Modern English weasel (16th c.) → Modern English weasel “the small mustelid Mustela; figuratively a sly, evasive person” (figurative sense fixed by the 20th c.; also verb to weasel out, adjective weaselly) => weasel (modern English noun) DEBATE / NUANCE: The reconstruction *wisulōn is secure in its Germanic reflexes, but its precise root connection is debated. Most handbooks connect the Germanic animal-name to PIE *weis- “to flow, melt” via the sense “foul-smelling, reeking liquid,” giving “the stinking animal”; the same root certainly underlies Latin virus “slime, poison” and Sanskrit viṣa “poison.” Some scholars have instead sought a non-Indo-European or sound-symbolic origin for the animal name itself, but the *weis- derivation is the standard and best-supported account, and the Germanic word for the odour and the animal-name are treated as a single word-family in the handbooks.
