Spokes are the rods, bars, or wires that radiate from the hub of a wheel outward to its rim, transmitting the wheel's load and holding its circular shape; by extension the word names anything that radiates from a centre in a regular pattern. In The Hobbit, the pine's branches 'sticking out at intervals like the spokes of a wheel' turn the tree into a giant radial structure: the simile imports mechanical regularity into the organic wood, so that each branch reads as an engineered element of the dwarves' makeshift refuge. The tone is wryly architectural — dwarves, the master craftsmen of wheels and mines, would naturally measure a tree by a wheelwright's standards — and the plural spokes emphasizes the symmetrical, countable array of branches at each level of the trunk, a reassuring geometry in a scene of scramble and panic. Where the earlier image of the larch was a child's Christmas-tree joke, this is a craftsman's simile, and the shift in tone — from festive to practical — marks the company's narrowing attention as the wolves close in and every bough must be appraised as structure.

Senses and idioms (polysemy). The word has a small but real range:

  • Wheel member (core) — the radiating rod or wire connecting hub and rim of a wheel, in wooden wagon wheels and modern wire bicycle wheels; the sense in the Tolkien context, and the meaning the word has carried for over a thousand years.
  • Radial pattern (metaphoric) — anything arranged like wheel-spokes: spokes of light, spokes of a spider's web, spokes of a fan, spokes radiating from a junction; a visual geometry of regularity and order.
  • Organizational metaphor — hub-and-spoke models in aviation, transport, and retail, in which a central hub routes passengers or goods along radiating spokes to many destinations; a 20th-century extension of the wheel image to networks.
  • Obstructing idiom — to put a spoke in someone's wheel: to thwart or obstruct someone's plans, from the old practice of jamming a bar into a wheel to immobilize a cart; recorded from the 16th-17th centuries.
  • Dialectal extension — in dialect English, a spoke could also name a ladder-rung or a horizontal bar between two supports, briefly widening the word beyond wheels.
  • False friend — spokesman is NOT related: it derives from spoke, the past tense of speak, a classic false etymology showing how mechanically loaded the wheel-word has become.

Register and imagery. The word is utterly stable in meaning — unchanged in over a thousand years — and yet its modern fortunes were remade by the bicycle age, which replaced heavy wooden spokes with slender tensioned wire and gave the word its most famous modern object. In engineering and cycling it is neutral and technical; in metaphor it supplies the image of orderly radiation, of things held together by a visible pattern of levers radiating from a single centre, which is precisely the geometry Tolkien imports into the pine tree. Distinguished from its neighbours: rods and bars are general solid cylinders without radial implication — a rod may lie flat, a bar may be horizontal; struts are structural supports in any orientation, usually compressive, belonging to engineering generally rather than to the wheel's hub-and-rim geometry; shafts are long rotating axes or arrow-like projections (a shaft of light, a mine shaft), not wheel members; rays and technical radii name radial lines geometrically but without solidity — light radiates, but a ray cannot bear weight. Only spoke names the specific wheel member, hub to rim, and its plural is the natural word for the counted, radiating array that Tolkien's simile needs.
