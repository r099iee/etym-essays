pedants (n., plural; singular pedant) — persons who are excessively, and usually ostentatiously, concerned with minor details, formal rules, and the outward trappings of academic learning. The word today is almost always pejorative: a pedant is someone who fusses over tiny, ant-sized details and pet rules, who parades book-learning as a badge of status while missing substance, proportion, or genuine insight.

Primary sense (pejorative, current). A pedant is a person who makes an irritating display of learning and insists on trivial points of correctness — grammar, spelling, pronunciation, procedure, dates, references — often to the detriment of meaning or of human feeling. The plural pedants is the form used here, naming a class of scholars who are learned but spiritually empty. In Macaulay's sentence, the Byzantine copyists and commentators "themselves destitute of taste, sense, and spirit" can still read and interpret the masterpieces of Sophocles, Demosthenes, and Plato — the tragedy of erudition divorced from judgement.

Historical neutral sense (16th–17th centuries). When the word entered English in 1588 it meant simply "schoolmaster, teacher," without insult. From Italian pedante (tutor, schoolmaster) and French pédant, the word initially named the grammar-school master, the professional instructor of the Renaissance classroom. It was the satirists of the age — Montaigne, Ben Jonson, Molière — who welded the pedagogue to the type of the vain, rule-bound, socially clumsy scholar, and the semantic slide from "teacher" to "narrow-minded stickler for petty rules" was complete by the 18th century.

Polysemy & secondary modern meanings:

  • Grammar pedants / prescriptivists — in modern language politics, "pedant" labels the prescriptivist who enforces traditional rules (split infinitives, stranded prepositions, whom for who) against descriptive usage; the semantic bridge is the old sense of the schoolmaster policing correctness, now applied to anyone policing the speech of others.
  • Pedantry as an intellectual vice — "pedantry" names the vice of prizing method, citation, and detail over discovery and understanding; in philosophy and criticism (e.g. the essays of Schopenhauer and Coleridge on learning) it is the failure of the mind that cannot distinguish the letter from the spirit.
  • Academic jargon / disciplinary self-mockery — scholars call one another "pedants" playfully when they insist on precise definitions, and "pedantic" describes prose choked by footnotes, hedging, or fine distinctions.
  • Pedantic style in writing and speech — an adjective now more frequent than the noun: "a pedantic style" means over-formal, hyper-precise, needlessly fussy prose or speech, the tone of one who corrects rather than communicates.
  • Pedant in literary and cultural theory — the figure recurs as a stock character (the doctus turned ridiculous), from Jonson's The Alchemist and Molière's Le Bourgeois gentilhomme to the modern figure of the "lexicographer pedant" in debates about dictionaries.
  • Rare neutral and self-descriptive uses — "a pedant of scholarship" can, archaically or ironically, mean simply a devoted, exacting scholar; some writers reclaim "pedantry" as a virtue of careful attention (e.g. the Victorian ideal of the rigorous antiquarian).

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. Greek paidagōgos (παιδαγωγός) meant "child-leader": the household slave who escorted boys to school and supervised their conduct. From this homely figure Latin paedagōgus and Italian pedante produced the professional schoolmaster, and the schoolmaster in turn became the comic emblem of rote learning and petty correctness. The whole evolution is a pejoration: respect for the teacher curdled, first into mockery of the pedantic tutor, then into contempt for anyone who parades learning while lacking taste, sense, and spirit — precisely the colouring of the word in this card's context.
