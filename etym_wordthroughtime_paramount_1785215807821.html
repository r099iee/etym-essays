- 15th-16th centuries - paramount enters English as a feudal law term (from Anglo-French 'par amont', above), naming the chief lord above all other lords - the lord paramount - purely hierarchical and legal.
- 17th-18th centuries - the term moves from land law into general use, applied to rank and authority (paramount power, paramount importance), shedding its technical edge while keeping its top-of-hierarchy sense.
- 19th century - in Mill's era it is a settled formal adjective for 'first in importance', used in measured administrative prose and quietly complimentary retrospective writing.
- Modern usage - fixed collocations (paramount importance, paramount duty, paramount concern) in legal, institutional, and political English; now chiefly evaluative priority rather than actual power.
