Ancient Greek (8th c. BCE, Homer)
φάλαγξ (phálanx)
‘battle line; log, roller’
↓
Latin (borrowed) (1st c. BCE)
phalanx
‘compact body of troops’
↓
Early Modern English (1550s)
phalanx
‘dense infantry formation’
↓
Modern English (current)
phalanx
‘massed formation; finger bone’

Note: Greek phálanx has no certain deeper etymology. Older handbooks linked it to PIE *bhel- (‘beam, plank’), but Beekes and modern scholarship treat it as likely Pre-Greek (substrate) vocabulary — a word the Greeks inherited from the Aegean peoples before them — alongside its doublet senses ‘spider’ and ‘venomous swelling’, which resist Indo-European explanation.
