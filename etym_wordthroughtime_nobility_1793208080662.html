- Ancient Rome — renown made rank: nobilitas names the descendants of consuls, fame hardened into a governing class.
- Medieval — the sword becomes the title: dukes, earls, and barons hold land by service, and nobility means the warrior estate above commons and clergy.
- 14th–16th c. — the moral twin grows: poets and preachers praise nobility of soul alongside nobility of blood, and the gap between them becomes satire stock.
- 17th–18th c. — the estate at its height: the nobility monopolize office, army, and church, while philosophers begin to ask what labour justifies the privilege.
- 1789–1832 — the reckoning: French revolutionaries abolish the order outright, English radicals denounce it as in the source sentence, and reform trims its monopoly.
- Today — title as heritage, virtue as compliment: hereditary power mostly ceremonial or abolished, while nobility of character remains English highest moral praise.
