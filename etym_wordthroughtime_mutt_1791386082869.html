- Early 1800s (pre-clipping): *muttonhead* 'stupid person, dullard' - Coined from *mutton* + *head*, evoking the proverbial docility and dimness of sheep; popular in British and American slang.
- Late 1890s–1901: 'clipping to mutt' - Emerged in American urban vernacular (sporting pages, racetracks, vaudeville) to mean a fool, clumsy boob, or hopeless contender in boxing or racing.
- 1900s–1910s: 'mongrel dog' - Rapidly transferred from foolish humans to crossbred, scruffy, or low-pedigree canines; dog fanciers used it pejoratively, while common folk adopted it with warm humor.
- 1907–1930s: *Mutt and Jeff* comic phenomenon - Bud Fisher's syndicated comic strip made Augustus Mutt a household name, popularizing the term worldwide as a harmless, hapless schemer.
- Mid-20th c.–Present: 'beloved rescue dog & affectionate fool' - The canine sense took over as the primary meaning, shifting from contempt to celebration (the mixed-breed dog as healthy, resilient, and charming); human use mellowed into friendly teasing ('you silly mutt').
