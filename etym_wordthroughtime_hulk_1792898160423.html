- Ancient world — the cargo carrier: Greek holkas names the broad ship that must be towed, bulk built for burden rather than speed.
- Medieval — the roomy transport: Latin hulcus and Old English hulc name light fast ships and then broad merchantmen, valued for capacity.
- 1300s–1600s — the stripped hull: English hulk narrows to the dismasted body, the vessel reduced to storage, prison, or breakwater, bulk without voyage.
- 1700s–1800s — prison and harbour furniture: the hulks of the Thames and Portsmouth become floating gaols, and the word darkens with chains and damp.
- 1900s — bulk generalises: hulks of men, cars, and buildings fill novels and newspapers, any mass too big to move gracefully.
- Today — mass with memory: from Tolkien’s sheltering tree to green-skinned giants, the hulk looms, hollow or muscled, always more body than agility.
