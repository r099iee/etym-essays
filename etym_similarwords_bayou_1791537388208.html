• Slough: Similar because both denote sluggish, swampy, or marshy channels with little discernible current; differs because a slough (often pronounced /sluː/ in North America) can describe a muddy backwater, a temporary wetland depression, or an abandoned glacial meltwater channel anywhere in North America, whereas bayou specifically identifies the sub-tropical distributary drainage systems of the Gulf Coast.
  • Swamp: Similar because both describe waterlogged, low-lying southern wetland landscapes supporting wetland trees like bald cypress and water tupelo; differs because a swamp is an expansive area of permanently or seasonally flooded forested land, whereas a bayou is a discrete, winding hydrological watercourse or river channel that flows through or alongside swamps.
  • Marsh: Similar because both are flat wetland ecosystems subject to periodic or permanent standing water; differs because a marsh is an open wetland dominated by non-woody vegetation (grasses, reeds, and cattails), while a bayou is a specific flowing or tidal channel, often flanked by dense riparian forests.
  • Creek: Similar because both refer to minor, secondary natural watercourses smaller than a primary river; differs because a creek typically has a distinct, continuous gravitational flow over a gravel or rocky bed, whereas a bayou is extremely flat, exceptionally sluggish, subject to tidal reversal, and characterized by a silt or mud bottom.
  • Backwater: Similar because both denote quiet, stagnant bodies of water isolated from the primary turbulent river current; differs because backwater is a broad, often figurative term for any stagnant body of water created by an obstruction or overflow, while bayou is a specific geographical feature integral to deltaic drainage.
  • Estuary: Similar because both can involve the mixing of freshwater and ocean tides in coastal zones; differs because an estuary is a wide, open river mouth where marine tides freely intermingle with fresh river discharge, whereas a bayou is a narrow, meandering channel network that may be located miles inland or throughout an alluvial delta.
  • Inlet: Similar because both represent narrow water channels providing ingress into a larger landmass; differs because an inlet is typically a coastal arm of the sea or lake penetrating between islands or barrier beaches, governed strictly by marine tides rather than deltaic fluvial mechanics.
