Apprehend is a formal verb with three distinct but historically united meanings: to seize or arrest a person physically, to grasp or understand an idea mentally, and to anticipate a future evil with anxiety or dread. All three are acts of laying hold — with the hand, the mind, or the foreboding heart — and all survive in active modern use, distinguished only by context and object.

The first and most official sense is to take into custody: police apprehend suspects, criminals, fugitives, trespassers. This is not casual catching but lawful seizure, implying authority, procedure, and successful completion. A constable who apprehends a thief has not merely chased him but laid hands on him and brought him under control. In adventure and science-fiction contexts, the same sense extends to any purposeful capture: to apprehend the girl means to intercept her, seize her, take hold of her — whether to rescue, detain, or confront her.

  • To arrest, seize, or take into custody: the police apprehended the burglar; the criminal remains at large and has not yet been apprehended. The object is always a person (or, by extension, a ship, smuggled goods). The bridge from Latin apprehendere, to lay hands on, is direct and unbroken since the 14th century. It differs from catch by formality and legality, and from capture by implying authority rather than warfare.
  • To grasp mentally, to understand, to perceive: to apprehend a truth, a distinction, a meaning, the nature of a problem. Philosophers distinguish apprehension (direct seeing) from comprehension (full encompassing): you apprehend that someone is in pain before you comprehend why. The bridge is Stoic metaphor — the mind as a hand that grips a clear impression — formalized in Latin rhetoric and inherited by Locke and modern psychology. Example: She is quick to apprehend complex mathematical concepts.
  • To anticipate with dread, to fear that something bad will happen: to apprehend danger, to apprehend ruin, to apprehend that the plan will fail. Here the subject is seized in advance by the future. The bridge is Old French anxiety-sense: danger apprehends you, so you apprehend danger. It survives in literary and slightly formal style: I apprehend no objection means I fear none.
  • Archaic / legal extended senses: to apprehend a writ (to lay hold of it for execution), to be apprehended of (to be taken with), and in older theology to apprehend Christ (to lay hold of salvation by faith). These show the verb’s once-broad combinability with abstract objects.
  • Noun family confirms polysemy: apprehension means simultaneously arrest (the apprehension of the suspect), understanding (quick of apprehension), and anxiety (to look forward with apprehension). No other English verb so compactly unites hand, head, and heart.

Register is consistently formal and written. No one shouts apprehend him! in a playground; they shout get him! Police reports, court judgments, academic prose, and literary narration prefer apprehend precisely because it dignifies and distances. To apprehend the girl sounds procedural, detached, almost clinical — a concern, merely — rather than passionate or violent, which is why adventure writers use it for functionaries, officers, and pursuers who think of people as objectives.

Collocations are diagnostic: you apprehend a suspect, a criminal, a fugitive, a trespasser; you apprehend a truth, an idea, a meaning, a distinction, a principle; you apprehend danger, evil, mischief, consequences, trouble. The object tells you which hand is grasping. Confusion is rare because the domains — police station, classroom, and anxious bedroom — seldom overlap.
