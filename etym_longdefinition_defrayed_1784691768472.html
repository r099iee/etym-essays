Defrayed is the past tense and past participle of the verb to defray, meaning "to provide the money for, to pay or settle a cost, charge, or expense" — most often the expenses of another person or of an undertaking. A benefactor defrays the cost of a journey; an employer defrays an employee's travelling expenses; an estate defrays the cost of a funeral. The word is formal, legal, and commercial in register, drawn from the language of account books, contracts, and administrations, and it usually implies discharging a stated sum in full rather than making a casual payment. Defrayed therefore means "paid, settled, discharged" — an expense fully provided for, and an obligation honestly and completely met. The sense-spectrum of the verb, and of the participle:
  • To pay or bear the cost of (an expense). The core sense: to supply the money that meets a charge, whether one's own or another's — "the grant defrayed all research expenses".
  • To settle, discharge (a debt, bill, or expense). The sense of clearing an account in full, of removing the outstanding sum: "funeral expenses to be defrayed by us" — fully paid out, leaving nothing owing.
  • To pay for on behalf of another or of an undertaking. The institutional sense, common in contracts, wills, and government language: a sponsor, employer, or treasury defrays the costs that an individual or enterprise would otherwise bear.
  • Formal register — the accounting sense. Defray belongs to written, itemised, formally incurred obligation rather than casual spending; one pays a bill but defrays an expense, in documents.
  • Derived nouns — defrayal, defrayment. The nominalisations meaning "the act of defraying; payment, settlement of costs", current especially in legal and parliamentary usage.
In the literary context of The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the word appears in one of the most quietly hilarious passages of the book: Thorin's written contract to "Burglar Bilbo", itemising terms — cash on delivery, one fourteenth of total profits "if any", all travelling expenses guaranteed — and then, with perfect bureaucratic calm, "funeral expenses to be defrayed by us or our representatives, if occasion arises and the matter is not otherwise arranged for." The solemnity of the word, transplanted from the counting-house into a dwarf-king's discussion of his own burglar's corpse, is the joke: death, the most final of eventualities, is reduced to a contingently payable item in an expense schedule. The word's legal chill makes both the humour and the danger unmistakable — Thorin's generosity has a clause, and the clause has a sub-clause. Near-synonyms differ in shade. To pay is the neutral general verb; to disburse is to pay out funds, typically from an official budget; to reimburse is to repay someone who has already spent; to cover is informal and implies sufficiency; to fund and to finance suggest supplying money for a project or purchase; to foot (as in "to foot the bill") is colloquial and implies reluctantly accepting the cost; to settle and to discharge belong to the language of debts and accounts; and to bear, in phrases like "to bear the expenses", is close to defray but suggests endurance as much as payment. Defray is the most formally contractual of them all — the verb of the ledger, the clause, and the settled account.
