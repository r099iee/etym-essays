Part of speech and core sense. Culminating is the present participle of the verb culminate, used here as a participial adjective that governs the phrase in 1795. Its core sense is “reaching the highest point of a development; arriving at a climax or decisive conclusion.” In the source sentence the participle describes the shape of a historical narrative: the book traces the Revolution from 1789 through the Terror of 1793–94 and then culminates—comes to its peak and its close—in 1795. The word therefore does two jobs at once: it marks a summit and it marks an ending.

The verb is intransitive. One does not “culminate something”; a process culminates in a result or with an event. That grammatical constraint matters, because it preserves the underlying image of a line rising to a summit rather than an agent deliberately finishing a task. The participle inherits the constraint: culminating in is the normal frame, and the phrase almost always points backwards to a preceding build-up.

Primary modern senses

  • To reach a climax or highest point. Said of a process, career, sequence, season, campaign or narrative: “the dispute culminated in a resignation.” The emphasis falls on accumulation and escalation, not merely on the last item in a list.
  • To be the climax or crowning element. The subject may be the climactic event itself rather than the process: “the trial was the culminating episode of the affair.” Here the participle names the item that supplies the peak.
  • Attributive adjective. Placed before a noun, culminating means “climactic, crowning, forming the highest point”: the culminating moment, a culminating achievement, the culminating scene of the film. This adjectival use is fully lexicalised in modern English and is the form printed on the card.
  • Completion as well as height. Because a summit is the last point of an ascent, the word often implies that the process is now finished and cannot go further: the culminating event is both the greatest and the final one.

Technical and historical senses

  • Astronomy. A celestial body culminates when it crosses the observer’s meridian and attains its greatest altitude: “the sun culminates at noon.” The upper culmination is the highest transit, the lower culmination the lowest; circumpolar stars have both, one above and one below the pole. This is the oldest English sense of the verb and the source of every other.
  • Astrology and early modern astronomy. From the 1640s, “to culminate” meant to be on the meridian—a technical operation in fixing a star’s position or casting a chart. The word entered English as a piece of learned Latin, not as ordinary speech.
  • Figurative extension. Because the meridian is the highest point a star reaches before descending, the verb was transferred by metaphor to anything that rises to a maximum: fortunes, tensions, careers, passions, prices, epidemics.
  • Narrative and historical writing. Historians speak of a period or story “culminating in” an event, meaning that earlier developments converge upon it. The usage implies causality and teleology, and is sometimes criticised when it smuggles inevitability into a sequence that was in fact contingent.
  • Rhetoric and criticism. A speech, symphony or plot may be said to culminate in a final movement or image; the term is neutral about aesthetic value and asserts only structural prominence.

Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge. The Latin noun culmen meant the top of anything: a roof-ridge, a mountain peak, a crown, the head. Culminare meant to furnish with a top, to crown, to bring to a head. Early modern astronomers adopted the verb for the moment a star “tops out” on the meridian; from that precise technical image the general sense “reach the highest point” followed in the later seventeenth century. The further step from “highest point” to “conclusion” is equally natural: what has reached its summit can go no higher, so the climax is also the end. English did not need a new word for “end”; it borrowed a word for “summit” and let the geometry of ascent supply the rest.

Register, connotation and collocation. Culminate belongs to formal narrative prose—historiography, journalism, reports, academic writing. It is evaluative but not emotive: it presents a sequence as having a shape, with a peak. Typical collocations are culminating in, culminating with, culminating point, culminating event, culminating moment, and a process that culminated in. The tone can be positive (culminating in victory), neutral (culminating in a treaty) or grim (culminating in catastrophe); the word itself merely asserts that the endpoint was the highest or decisive one.

Boundaries. Culminating is stronger and more shaped than ending: an evening can end with coffee, but it culminates in a toast. It implies accumulation, escalation and a peak, whereas concluding and finishing are neutral about height or intensity. It differs from climaxing mainly in register—culminate is the cooler, more analytic term—and from peaking in that a peak may be followed by decline, while culmination usually carries the sense of completion as well.

In this reading. Carlyle’s The French Revolution is described as a book that charts events from 1789 to the height of the Terror and culminates in 1795. The participle signals both the narrative’s climax and its terminus, and it quietly asserts that the whole sequence had a shape—exactly the kind of providential plotting that Carlyle’s style invites and that later historiographical critics would question.
