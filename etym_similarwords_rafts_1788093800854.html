• Boats: Similar because both are watercraft for travel and transport; differs because a boat has a hull and is built and propelled as a vessel, while a raft is a flat platform, usually unpowered or paddled, with no hull.
  • Barges: Similar because both carry cargo on water; differs because a barge is a flat-bottomed boat, built, steered and usually towed or powered, while a raft is a simple buoyant platform, often improvised.
  • Pontoons: Similar because both are flat floating structures; differs because a pontoon is a hollow float (often one of several supporting a bridge or dock), while a raft is a free-floating platform itself.
  • Floats: Similar because both are buoyant objects used on water; differs because “float” is generic (any floating object or platform, also a parade vehicle), while a raft is specifically a craft for carrying people or goods.
  • Scows: Similar because both are flat-bottomed craft for shallow water; differs because a scow is a built boat with a flat bottom and square ends, while a raft is an unbuilt platform of lashed or inflated material.
  • Lighters: Similar because both carry cargo on water, often unpowered; differs because a lighter is a heavy-duty flat-bottomed boat used to unload ships, a professional craft, while a raft is simpler and often makeshift.
  • Ferries: Similar because both carry people and goods across water; differs because a ferry is a scheduled, crewed vessel on a fixed route, while a raft is an ad hoc or recreational craft.
  • Dinghies: Similar because both are small watercraft; differs because a dinghy is a small boat with a hull and often a sail or motor, while a raft has no hull and floats on its own structure.
  • Platforms: Similar because both are flat raised surfaces; differs because “platform” is generic (stage, railway, computing), while a raft is specifically a floating platform used as a craft.
  • Slew / host / heap (for 'a raft of'): Similar because all denote a large quantity; differs because “a slew of” and “a host of” are informal or rhetorical count-phrases, “a heap of” is homely, while “a raft of” keeps the distinctive image of a piled-up mass of logs and is chiefly American.
