Hulk names anything big, bulky, and faintly unwieldy: a mass that looms before it moves, whether the hull of a ship, the frame of a man, or, as here, the vast trunk of a tree, and Tolkien reaches for it precisely because the tree is no longer quite a tree but a thing, huge, broken, and hollow, looming beside the road like a beached ship.

In the source sentence the travellers come on the huge hulk of a tree, still alive with leaves on small branches round the broken stumps of long-fallen limbs, yet hollow and enterable by a great crack: the word holds both halves of that marvel together, the bulk that impresses and the hollowness that shelters, since a hulk is classically a great body emptied of its working innards, a ship without rigging, a tree without heartwood, still standing and still partly alive.

Core meaning in detail: the physical prototype is the ship’s hull stripped of masts and tackle, too clumsy to sail yet too solid to scrap, moored as a store-ship, prison-ship, or breakwater, and from that picture the word generalises to any large heavy body viewed as mere mass: the hulk of a wrecked car, a brooding hulk of a man, and here the hulk of a tree, each carrying the same judgement that size has outlived agility and interior purpose has thinned to a shell.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hollow trunk or stump of a tree (Tolkien’s sense, literary): the huge hulk of a tree, alive yet hollow, and the bridge is the ship analogy, since a gutted trunk stands like a hull, keeled in earth, sheltering travellers as a beached hull shelters stores.
  • Dismasted or unseaworthy ship, stripped hull (oldest English sense, from the 1300s): harbour hulks, prison hulks, sheer hulks for masting, and the bridge is direct identity, since the word first meant a broad cargo vessel and then any vessel reduced to bare hull.
  • A large bulky person or animal (colloquial, often slightly menacing): a great hulk of a fellow looming in the doorway, and the bridge is metaphorical size-shaming, since the human frame is judged as a vessel, all displacement and no grace.
  • A large unwieldy object or mass (generalised): the rusting hulk of a car, the concrete hulks of cooling towers, and the bridge extends the ship’s clumsiness to anything oversized, abandoned, or hard to shift.
  • Half-sunk wreck, visible remainder (journalistic): the hulk of the freighter lay off the reef, and the bridge runs through abandonment, since a hulk is what remains after function departs, the carcass rather than the creature.
  • Comic-book strongman (proper-name extension): the green-skinned Hulk of modern fantasy, and the bridge is deliberate connotation-mining, since the writers wanted bulk, inarticulate power, and a body that is itself the plot.

Register and connotation: hulk is blunt Anglo-Germanic monosyllable, faintly pejorative outside affectionate fantasy, suggesting clumsiness, menace, or decay, and even at its most neutral it looms: journalists, novelists, and referees reach for it when something outsized blocks the way, while Tolkien softens it with huge and with green leaves, so the hulk shelters the hobbits rather than threatening them, bulk redeemed by hospitality.
