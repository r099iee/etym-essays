- Old English — bisig, occupied and diligent: a neutral word for being employed, with no meddling attached, beside bodig, the human body or person.
- Middle English — bisi about others: preachers warn against being busy in neighbours' matters, and the moral sense of misdirected industry takes root.
- 16th c. — the compound is born: busybody for the meddler in church discipline and village quarrels, printed in sermons and proverb collections as a stock reproach.
- 17th–18th c. — comedy adopts it: stage busybodies, Tatler and Spectator sketches, and Austen's village policers of propriety make it affectionately satirical.
- 19th–20th c. — curtains and committees: the neighbourhood busybody becomes a fixture of novels and sitcoms, twitching nets and chairing meetings.
- Today — digital migration: online busybodies police strangers at planetary scale, while the spoken word keeps its teasing warmth among friends and relatives.
