Phoenician as adjective means pertaining to Phoenicia, the ancient Semitic seafaring civilization occupying the coastal Levant, roughly modern Lebanon and parts of Syria and northern Israel, flourishing from about 1500 to 300 BCE, and as noun means a native or inhabitant of that land, its language, or its famous purple dye and alphabet. In the source sentence, the Phoenician alphabet means the consonantal writing system of 22 signs developed by Phoenician merchants and adapted by Greeks with vowels around the 8th century BCE, ancestor of virtually all Western alphabets. More broadly Phoenician evokes maritime trade, Tyre and Sidon, Carthage as colony, cedar, glass, and Tyrian purple.

Capitalized, Phoenician is an ethnonym and culture term, not merely linguistic. Historians use Phoenician city-states for independent polities like Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, and Ugarit neighbours, Phoenician expansion for Mediterranean colonization including Cyprus, Sardinia, and Carthage, and Phoenician art for ivory, metalwork, and purple textiles blending Egyptian and Mesopotamian motifs. In linguistics, Phoenician denotes a Canaanite Semitic language closely related to Hebrew, written right to left in consonantal script, attested in inscriptions such as the Ahiram sarcophagus and Kilamuwa stele.

  • Ethnic and geographic sense: of Phoenicia and its people, e.g. Phoenician traders reached Cornwall. Polysemy bridge: directly from Greek Phoinikes for Levantine coastal people, naming by purple-red colour of dye.
  • Linguistic sense: the Semitic language and its epigraphic dialects, e.g. Phoenician inscription, Punic as late Phoenician. Polysemy bridge: via language-people metonymy where land name stands for speech, then for script carrying speech.
  • Alphabetic and cultural invention sense: Phoenician alphabet as prototype consonantary. Polysemy bridge: via invention-attribution where creators lend name to tool, then tool travels independently to Greek, Etruscan, Latin.
  • Figurative mercantile sense: shrewd trader or middleman, e.g. called the Phoenicians of the modern market. Polysemy bridge: via stereotype from famous trading prowess to generic merchant archetype, sometimes admiring, sometimes pejorative.

Connotations shift by discipline: classicists admire Phoenicians as transmitters of literacy, economists as early capitalists and navigators, biblical scholars as Canaanite neighbours and sometimes rivals of Israel, and modern Lebanese nationalists as prestigious ancestors. Unlike Greek, which suggests philosophy and democracy, Phoenician suggests commerce, craft, and practical literacy, less celebrated in ancient sources because Greeks and Romans wrote the histories. Adjective usage includes Phoenician purple for Tyrian dye, Phoenician glass, Phoenician joints in carpentry famed for precision.

Grammatically Phoenician inflects as regular proper adjective and countable noun with plural Phoenicians, and combines in compounds like Greco-Phoenician, Phoenicio-Punic, Neo-Phoenician. Pronunciation in RP is /fɪˈnɪʃən/ with stress on second syllable and sh-sound, reflecting Latin-Greek transmission. Its prestige today lies less in empire, for Phoenicia never unified politically, than in invisible infrastructure: money economy, navigation, and above all an alphabet simple enough for merchants, later domesticated by Greeks who added vowels to treasure, as the source notes.
