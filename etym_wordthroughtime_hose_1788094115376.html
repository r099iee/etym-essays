- Leg covering / stocking (from Old English hosa) — the everyday word for legwear worn by men and women; entirely neutral and common.
- Medieval clothing and armour (12th–16th c.) — hose as tailored legwear and as mail hose for knights; the word covers both fashion and war.
- Competition from “stocking” (16th–17th c.) — knitted legwear is increasingly called stockings; “hose” begins to sound old-fashioned for garments.
- The water tube (from 1807) — extended to the flexible conduit for liquids; within a century this becomes the dominant sense and the garment sense fades to historical.
- The verb “to hose (down)” (19th c.) — washing with water; practical, domestic register.
- US slang “to hose” = to cheat or defeat harshly; “hosed” = ruined (20th c.) — the connotation plunges from neutral to insulting.
- Computing slang “hosed” = corrupted, unrecoverable (late 20th c.) — technical jargon built on the slang sense; the old garment sense survives mainly in historical fiction and the trade term “hosiery”.
