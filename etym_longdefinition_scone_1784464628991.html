SCONE (noun). A small, round, lightly sweetened quick bread, made from wheat (or historically barley or oat) flour, leavened with baking powder or soda, and baked either on a griddle or in an oven; a standard item of British and Commonwealth tea-table and breakfast culture, typically split, buttered, and served with jam and clotted cream. As a proper noun, Scone (capitalized) is the historic village and former royal site in Perthshire, Scotland, home of the Stone of Scone, the coronation stone of Scottish (and subsequently British) kings. Polysemy — all senses:
  • Culinary sense — the small baked quick bread; varies from dense, plainer, griddle-cooked barley or oat cakes (the older Scottish form, kin to bannocks) to the light, oven-baked, enriched wheat scones of modern British ritual. Variants include fruit scones (raisins, sultanas), cheese scones (savoury), drop scones (poured batter), potato scones (tattie scones, griddled), and soda scones.
  • Tea-table and cream-tea sense — the scone as the centrepiece of afternoon tea and of the Devonshire or Cornish cream tea, served split with strawberry jam and clotted cream. This ritual context gives the word its social warmth.
  • The pronunciation split — the word is pronounced /skɒn/ (rhyming with "gone", Scottish and northern English) or /skəʊn/ (rhyming with "cone", southern English), a famous sociolinguistic marker of region and class; dictionaries record both.
  • The proper noun Scone — the Scottish royal site (Scone Palace, Perthshire); the Stone of Scone (the "Stone of Destiny"), taken to England by Edward I in 1296 and returned to Scotland in 1996. The word also names a town in New South Wales, Australia, and places in other anglophone countries.
  • Extended and figurative use — the word is used generically for any small round quick bread of the scone type; "scone-handed" (colloquial Scottish) means broad-handed; and "scone" is Australian slang for the head.
For the baker: a classic scone dough is a soft, short mixture of flour, butter, milk or buttermilk, and a raising agent (baking powder, or bicarbonate of soda with sour milk or buttermilk), lightly kneaded, patted or rolled to about two centimetres thick, and cut into rounds before baking in a hot oven; drop scones and tattie (potato) scones are cooked on a griddle instead. The word's collocations are nearly all domestic and convivial: "a batch of scones", "warm scones with jam and cream", "scone-and-tea", "cream tea". In the scone's home countries the great debate of cream-versus-jam first (the Devonshire and Cornish orders) has been a gentle national comedy for over a century. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges: the culinary and geographical senses are historically unconnected — the food word appears to enter Scots from the Low Countries (Middle Dutch schoonbroot "fine bread"), while the place-name is a Celtic/Pictish toponym — and they converge only by sound and spelling. The food sense, originally a dense griddle-cake, rises with British tea culture in the 18th–19th centuries to its modern light, oven-baked form and its iconic status as the emblem of buttered hospitality.
