Core Definition (This Card's Sense)

skirts — plural noun meaning the outer parts, edges, or borders of an area; the outskirts or margin of a place such as a forest, town, or hill. A thing's "skirts" are its fringe — the border region where it begins to give way to what surrounds it. The source sentence, "They were camping on the skirts of the forest," uses exactly this spatial sense: the camp lay at the forest's outer edge.

Primary Sense: Outer Edges of an Area

  • Skirts of a forest / wood: the outermost margin of woodland, where trees thin out toward open ground.
  • Skirts of a town or city: the outskirts, the peripheral districts away from the center (today usually called outskirts).
  • Skirts of a hill or mountain: the lower slopes or foot, the border of rising ground.
  • Skirts of the sea or shore: the water's edge, the margin of the land meeting water.

Secondary Sense: The Garment

  • Garment sense (the original): a skirt is an outer garment that hangs from the waist, worn by women and girls, or the part of a garment (a coat, gown, or tunic) that hangs below the waist.
  • Extended garment use: the "skirts" of a gown or robe are its hanging lower parts; historically, "skirt" also named the hanging border of a saddle or a piece of furniture.
  • The garment sense dominates modern everyday English, while the spatial sense is the one used in this card and in literary description.

Tertiary Sense: The Verb to Skirt

  • To skirt (a place): to go around or along the edge of something rather than through its middle — "the road skirts the lake."
  • To skirt (a subject): to avoid dealing with the main point or heart of a matter, to evade or bypass it — "he skirted the issue."
  • Skirting the truth: to keep just within the bounds of honesty without fully engaging with the facts.

Specialized and Technical Senses

  • Skirt steak: a long, thin, lean cut of beef from the diaphragm area of the animal, named for its skirt-like shape.
  • Skirting board (chiefly British): the wooden border or baseboard running along the lower edge of an interior wall.
  • Outskirts (standard modern word): the plural compound that has largely replaced "skirts of the city" in ordinary speech.

Register and Usage Notes

  • The spatial "skirts of X" is literary, poetic, or deliberately archaic today; outskirts is the normal everyday term.
  • The verb "to skirt" is fully current, especially in the phrase "skirt the issue."
  • Related forms: skirt (noun/verb), skirting (noun/adjective), skirted (adjective), outskirts (noun).
  • The plural skirts in the spatial sense may take singular or plural verb agreement depending on whether the border is viewed as one ring or many edges.

Polysemy Summary

skirts unites a garment sense (a hanging lower covering), a spatial sense (the outer edge or border of a place), a verbal sense (to move along or avoid the edge), and technical senses (skirt steak, skirting boards). The historical bridge linking all of them is the idea of a hanging edge: the hem of a garment, transferred metaphorically to the hanging fringe of land, water, or a town, and then to the motion of keeping to the edge of something — whether a road, a forest, or a delicate subject.

Historical Semantic Bridge

Old Norse skyrta "shirt, tunic" (from a Germanic root meaning "cut short") was borrowed into English as skirt in the 13th–14th centuries, where it settled on the lower, hanging part of a garment. By the late 1500s the word was already being used metaphorically of geography: the "skirts of a forest" were its hanging outer edges, exactly as a hem hangs around the body. From that spatial metaphor came the verb "to skirt" (travel along the edge, 17th–18th c.) and the modern figurative "skirt the issue" (avoid the center, 19th c.).
