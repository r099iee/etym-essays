Formalist denotes a person committed to formalism: the doctrine or habit of prioritizing form, structure, technique, or prescribed rules over content, substance, feeling, or function.

In general usage it is often mildly pejorative: a formalist is a stickler, a pedant, a lover of ceremony and correctness who insists on proper procedure while missing spirit or sense. We call a teacher who marks only grammar a formalist, a priest who cares only for ritual a formalist, a bureaucrat who demands forms in triplicate a formalist. Here formality shades into empty formalism, and the suffix -ist marks the person as practitioner.

In aesthetics and criticism the term is technical and neutral or even proud. Russian Formalists of the 1910s-1920s — Shklovsky, Jakobson, Eichenbaum — founded modern literary theory by studying literariness, defamiliarization, plot versus story, and poetic devices rather than biography or morals. Art formalists from Clive Bell and Roger Fry to Clement Greenberg argued that significant form, flatness, colour, and line, not subject matter, make art art. In music, formalists analyse sonata structure, fugue, serial rows; in film, formalist directors like Eisenstein and later Kubrick foreground montage and style. In this domain a formalist is a close analyst of how works are put together.

In law, theology, philosophy, and mathematics, formalism again means rule-centred reasoning. Legal formalists believe law is a closed system of rules applied deductively; theological formalists stress liturgy and dogma; mathematical formalists following Hilbert treat mathematics as manipulation of symbols according to rules. The common thread is autonomy of system over external reference.

  • General-pejorative formalist: pedant for etiquette or procedure; e.g., a formalist headmaster obsessed with uniforms and Latin. Implies rigidity and lack of imagination.
  • Literary-artistic formalist: theorist or critic of technique; e.g., Russian Formalist analysis of narrative devices, Greenbergian formalist art criticism. Technical, often prestigious.
  • Legal-religious formalist: adherent to letter of law or rite; e.g., formalist judge, formalist theologian. Suggests deductive rigour but possible moral blindness.
  • Philosophical-mathematical formalist: defender of formalism as doctrine; e.g., Hilbertian formalist in foundations of mathematics. Highly specialized.
  • Combination sense in the prompt: formalist critical principle paired with rhetorical analysis, i.e., close analysis of verbal texture and structure rather than impressionistic value-judgments.

Polysemy is bridged by the ancient tension between forma shape and content filling it. Whether the form is a sonnet’s metre, a court’s protocol, or a syllogism’s figure, the formalist trusts the visible, repeatable pattern more than the ineffable message. Praise or blame depends on whether the age values order or authenticity: classicist ages honour formalists as guardians; romantic ages mock them as hollow. The quoted passage’s “formalist critical principle” belongs to the mid-20th-century New Critical moment when close reading proudly reclaimed formalism against boozy generalities.

Grammatically countable: one formalist, many formalists; also adjectival: formalist criticism, formalist poetics. Collocates with strict, rigid, narrow, Russian, legal, aesthetic, high. Antonyms include contextualist, historicist, realist, romantic, functionalist, each rejecting form-first in favour of world-first.
