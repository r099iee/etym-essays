- Late Medieval Latin (c. 14th–15th c.) — Coined in scholastic and clerical Latin on analogy with literatim and gradatim: strictly employed by legal notaries and scriptorium copyists to certify that official transcripts matched original charters word for word.
- Late 16th c. (c. 1580s–1600s) — Renaissance vernacular adoption: entered Early Modern English as both adverb and adjective in scholarly controversies, judicial proceedings, and Elizabethan drama, signaling high fidelity to spoken or written text.
- 18th–19th c. — Stenographic and parliamentary standardization: became the definitive technical benchmark for parliamentary reporting (Hansard), courtroom stenography, and print journalism, distinguishing official transcripts from subjective summaries.
- 20th–21st c. — Digital, computational, and theatrical expansion: extended into computer science (verbatim string literals in programming languages), contemporary documentary drama (verbatim theatre), and academic plagiarism detection, retaining its status as the supreme marker of textual fidelity.
