**CROAKING: five centuries of darkness in one harsh syllable** The biography of croaking is the biography of a sound. The word belongs to one of the oldest and most widespread of sound-imitating families, the Indo-European harsh-cry group that built words directly from the raw acoustics of a grating, hoarse throat — the same imitative template that named the crane across Europe and the curlew in India. In the Germanic dialects this type produced Old English cracian, "to make a sharp noise," the ancestor of modern English crack, and Old Norse kraka, "a crow," whence the Middle English crake, a raven or the corncrake's cry. **The word enters English** In the early fourteenth century the Middle English verb crouken appears, meaning "to make a low, hoarse sound," used first of birds — the crow, the raven, and the crane. By about 1400 the word had been extended to frogs, and this frog-and-raven double association fixed croak forever in the low, guttural register: the croak is the sound of the swamp at night and the sound of the raven on the gallows-tree, and both are creatures of darkness and foreboding. The noun croak, a back-formation, is recorded from 1560 meaning "a low, hoarse, guttural sound." **The raven's ill omen and the croaker** Around the mid-fifteenth century the meaning began to drift toward the figurative. Because the raven was a bird of ill omen — the bird that croaks from the roof-tree to announce death — "to croak" came to mean "to forebode evil, to complain, to grumble." A "croaker" (1630s) is a prophet of doom, the man who croaks of coming disaster and refuses to be cheered; the word kept this pejorative, complaining colour into modern English, where "to croak about" something still means to grumble about it without end. This figurative step is the hinge of the word's whole career: an animal sound became a moral verdict, and the bird of ill omen lent its voice to every human pessimist. **The death-rattle (1812)** In 1812 English slang pressed the word into its darkest service: "to croak" means "to die," from the sound of the death-rattle — the last rough, scraping breath that leaves the throat of the dying. The image is brutally physical: death is not an event but a noise, the body's final croak, and the slang verb preserves the imitative core of the whole family even at the moment of extinction. "He croaked" is at once slangy, grim, and blackly comic — the death-rattle rendered as the sound of a frog. **Parallel formations across Indo-European** The same ancient imitative family reappears far beyond Germanic. Latin crocīre meant "to croak," used of the raven; Sanskrit krauñcaḥ named the curlew, literally "the harsh caller," which survives in Hindi as the tatsama kraunch. These are parallel formations, independently coined from the same acoustic experience of a hoarse, grating bird-call — and their existence shows how powerfully the sound of a harsh cry shapes human words across unrelated branches of the family. They are witnesses, not ancestors, of the English word. **The participle in *The Hobbit*** The present participle croaking used in *The Hobbit* carries centuries of accumulated darkness — the raven of ill omen, the frog's harsh pulse, the foreboding croaker, and the death-rattle — compressed into the goblins' panicked, inhuman throat-noises. When the lights go out and the goblin mob descends into "yells and yammering, croaking, jibbering and jabbering," the croaking is the register that most completely dehumanises them: the sound of a mob that has fallen below language into the amphibian dark, the very sound the swamp would make if the swamp could panic. **Register today** Croaking today still describes the low, hoarse, guttural noise of birds, frogs, and failing human voices; it still carries the same animal, ill-omened undertone it has had for five centuries; and it has quietly extended to any strained, rasping mechanical sound. Its -ing form keeps the action continuous, which is exactly what the goblin catalogue needs: not a single croak but a sustained chorus of them, the whole cavern alive with harsh, frightened throats.
