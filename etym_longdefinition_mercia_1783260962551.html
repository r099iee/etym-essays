Mercia (proper noun) — one of the great kingdoms of the Anglo-Saxon Heptarchy, occupying the English Midlands from roughly the 6th to the 10th centuries, and the name of a kingdom that in the 8th century under Offa dominated England south of the Humber. THE HISTORICAL KINGDOM Mercia was, as its name says, the kingdom of the border-people. The Mercians (Old English Mierce, plural of *mierce, 'a border-dweller') were the Angles settled along the western frontier of the Anglo-Saxon settlement, facing the still-British (Welsh) lands beyond the boundary. From that borderland base the kingdom grew through the 7th and 8th centuries into the most powerful of the English kingdoms: Penda in the 7th century, then Æthelbald, and above all Offa (reigned 757–796), who styled himself overlord of Britain, built the great earthwork Offa's Dyke along the Welsh frontier, and issued the first substantial English royal coinage. Mercia's hegemony declined after the Danish (Viking) invasions of the 9th century, when the kingdom was partitioned and absorbed into the expanding Wessex of Alfred and his successors, until in the 10th century the name ceased to denote an independent kingdom and survived as an ecclesiastical and historical term. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Historical-kingdom sense — Mercia as one of the seven kingdoms of the Heptarchy, the Midlands power whose 8th-century supremacy under Offa is a fixed landmark of Anglo-Saxon history; this is the sense in Macaulay's sentence, where Wessex and Mercia stand together for still-pagan, still-Germanic England.
  • Ethnonymic sense — Mierce, 'the border-people', the tribal name from which the kingdom-name derives; the people are named before the land, and the adjective Mercian denotes the Mercian people and their dialect.
  • Ecclesiastical sense — Mercia survived as the name of a see and later of a diocese (the Bishopric of Mercia, later of Lichfield); the ecclesiastical name carried the old kingdom into the church's administrative geography long after the kingdom itself was gone.
  • Modern institutional sense — the name survives in modern regional institutions: West Mercia Police, the Mercian Regiment of the British Army, the (largely honorary) Mercian Brigade, and various local heritage bodies; 'Mercian' is also the scholarly label for the dialect of Old English attested in texts such as the Vespasian Psalter gloss.
  • Etymological-family sense — Mercia is the historical cousin of march 'border region' (the Welsh Marches, the marcher lords), of marquis/marquess 'ruler of a march', of mark (the boundary and its marker), and of margin — all from the same Indo-European root *merǵ- 'boundary, border' that gave Latin margō and Old Norse mǫrk (as in Denmark).
  • Literary-historical sense — in Macaulay and later historians, Mercia is used as shorthand for the interior, inward-looking, still-pagan heart of Anglo-Saxon England, set against the Christianized continental courts of Paris, Toledo, Arles, and Ravenna.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGES The semantic bridge is one of geography hardening into history. The PIE root *merǵ- 'boundary, border' gave Proto-Germanic *markō 'border, boundaryland', Old English mearc 'boundary, mark', and the tribal name Mierce 'the border-people', the Angles who settled on the edge of the English world facing the Welsh. What was originally a descriptive label for a frontier people became, as their territory grew, the name of a kingdom — and the border-people of the 6th century became the heartland of 8th-century England, so that a boundary-born kingdom paradoxically turned into the centre. Each later sense — the diocese, the modern police force, the scholarly dialect label, the poetic emblem of pagan England — is a survival of that original meaning of edge and border, the word having travelled from a line in the earth to the name of a nation.
