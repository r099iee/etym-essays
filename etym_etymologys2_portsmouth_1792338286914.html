Note on historical etymology: Philologists and toponymists (such as Eilert Ekwall and A.D. Mills) note a long-standing debate concerning the first element of Portsmouth. Under the entry for the year 501 CE, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records that a Saxon leader named Port arrived with two ships at a place called Portesmuþa and slew a young British noble. While early chroniclers treated Port as a historical personage, modern historical linguists demonstrate that this is a classic eponymous foundation myth (folk etymology): the chronicler invented the warrior Port from the existing Roman-British geographical name of the harbor. The first element is almost certainly Latin portus ("harbor"), referencing the nearby Roman naval fortress of Portus Adurni (modern Portchester Castle), combined with the Old English genitive -es and mūþa ("estuary mouth"), signifying literally "the mouth of the harbor".
