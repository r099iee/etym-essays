- 1st c. BCE — The name denotes the dictator and, with his agnomen, Sulla Felix; contemporaries debate whether his resignation is statesmanship or exhaustion.
- Imperial era — Becomes a byword for proscription and civil bloodshed; tempora Sullana means an age of terror, and historians treat him as the first warlord of the Republic's collapse.
- Renaissance–18th c. — A standing example in political theory of the strongman who seizes the state; Machiavelli and the civic humanists discuss him alongside Caesar.
- 19th c. — Biographies and school histories fix the image of the cold, lucky, ruthless aristocrat; “Sullan proscriptions” enters general English.
- 20th–21st c. — Used figuratively for dictatorial purges (“a new Sulla”), while scholarship debates his reforms, his sincerity and the mechanics of the proscription lists.
