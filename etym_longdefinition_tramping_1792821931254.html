Tramping is the present participle of tramp, and in its core sense it means walking with heavy, firm, resounding steps — going on foot steadily and purposefully over ground, whether the postman tramping his round in the rain, soldiers tramping the road to the station, or Frodo tramping over the Shire with his friends while the sensible folk shake their heads.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses exactly this rambling sense: Frodo went tramping over the Shire with them — not marching, not strolling, but covering the miles on foot in the companionable, slightly wearying way of young hobbits with more curiosity than sense. The contrast that follows sharpens the picture: more often he wandered alone under the starlight, so tramping here is the social, daylight, dusty-boots half of his walking life, set against solitary night wandering.

Core meaning in detail: Tramp as a verb always keeps the sound of the footfall inside it: the Germanic stem imitates the dull thud of boots on earth, and the participle stretches that thud into duration — step after step after step. Where walking is neutral and hiking is recreational, tramping suggests weight, distance, and a touch of roughness: you tramp through mud, tramp the streets looking for work, tramp the hills for pleasure that still costs effort. New Zealand English has even promoted the word to the national pastime — tramping is what the rest of the world calls hiking or backpacking, with its own clubs, huts, and tracks.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Walking heavily and steadily (primary verbal sense, from the 14th c.): boots tramping the stairs, police tramping the beat. The bridge is onomatopoeia made durable: the thud of the tread becomes the name of the tread, extended by the -ing ending into an ongoing rhythm.
  • Hiking or rambling long distances for pleasure (recreational, dominant in Australasia): weekend tramping in the Tararuas, a tramping club hut. The bridge runs from weary necessity to chosen sport: what the swagman did for survival the office worker now does for joy, keeping the heavy boots and the long miles while shedding the desperation.
  • Wandering in search of work or alms (historical, of vagrants and wayfarers): tramping the roads in the hungry thirties. The bridge is social history: the heavy step belongs to those who have no carriage, so the participle absorbed the whole life of the road — tramping men, tramping artisans, tramps and their circuits.
  • Stamping down or traversing thoroughly (as in tramp down, tramp over): tramping grapes, paths tramped flat by a thousand feet. The bridge narrows the walking to its effect on the ground: each passage presses the earth a little firmer, until the walkers have written their journey into the soil.
  • Sounding with marching feet (acoustic, of bodies of men or animals): the tramping of boots in the corridor, elephants tramping through the night. The bridge carries the verb from the legs to the ears: the participle names the noise of many feet kept in rough time, beloved of novelists summoning armies, policemen, and herds.
  • Sailing without a fixed route (the tramp steamer, nominal cousin): tramping the world's ports for whatever cargo offers. The bridge is metaphorical wandering transferred to ships: where great liners keep timetables, tramps tramp — calling anywhere, carrying anything — and the participle of the road becomes the economy of the sea.
  • Crushing underfoot (overlap with trample): flowers tramped into the mud at the festival's end. The bridge is collateral damage: heavy purposeful feet destroy what they pass over, so tramping over slides toward trampling down, the journey leaving bruised petals behind it.

Register and connotation: tramping is sturdy, outdoor, faintly romantic English — dust, leather, weather, companionship. It never belongs indoors or in cars; it smells of rain and cut grass. In Britain it keeps a ghost of the vagrant about it (to tramp the roads is still to be between employments), while in New Zealand it is wholly wholesome — schoolchildren tramp, prime ministers tramp, visitors are urged to tramp. Tolkien, lover of long walks himself, gives it the happiest of its shades: youth, friendship, and the Shire unfolding boot by boot beneath willing feet.
