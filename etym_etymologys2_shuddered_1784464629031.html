PIE *(s)keud- / *skeud- "to drive, shoot, rush, push violently" (reconstructed Indo-European root of violent, sudden motion; proposed as the deeper base of the Germanic shake-family — the connection is loose and debated, see note) → Proto-Germanic *skudd- / *skud- "to shake, shove, drive" (reconstructed; the shake/drive complex behind the West Germanic forms) → Middle Low German schoderen / schudderen (v.) "to shudder, tremble" and Dutch schudden (v.) "to shake" (continental West Germanic, attested from the 14th century; the Low German/Dutch pair anchors the family across the North Sea) → Middle English shodderen / shuderen / shuddren (v.) "to tremble, shake, shudder, especially from cold or fear" (attested from c. 1300; a frequentative or iterative formation — a "keep-on-shaking" intensive — of native or Low German-influenced stock; the immediate source is debated, see note) → Modern English shudder (v.) "to tremble violently and convulsively" (the standard form, with the noun shudder "a convulsive tremor" following from the verb) → English SHUDDERED (weak past tense and past participle of shudder, formed with the regular -ed suffix) — the card's target form. NOTE on the debate: the immediate origin of Middle English shodderen is not certain. The OED and most handbooks treat it as a frequentative formation, and note the very close parallel to Middle Low German schoderen/schudderen and Dutch schudden "to shake"; whether the English verb is a native formation of the same Germanic stock or a borrowing from the Low German area cannot be settled from the record. The deeper PIE link *(s)keud- "to drive, shoot, rush" is proposed by comparing the Germanic shake-verbs with Indo-European words of violent motion (including the root of English shoot), but it is a loose connection and many dictionaries stop the derivation at Proto-Germanic. What is secure: the continuous Germanic family of shaking, and the English path shodderen → shudder → shuddered.
