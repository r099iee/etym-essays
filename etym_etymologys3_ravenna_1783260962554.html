Few great names in European history sit on so obscure a root as Ravenna. The city's etymological biography is, in the strict sense, a confession of ignorance followed by a well-documented late history: the original meaning of the toponym is lost, while everything the name has meant for two thousand years was added by history rather than by the root.

The uncertain root. Ravenna is a pre-Roman place-name of central-northern Italy, of the kind the Romans took over wholesale from the Etruscans and the Italic peoples they absorbed. Its formation — a double consonant and an -enna-type ending — resembles other Etruscan and pre-Latin toponyms of the region, and most modern handbooks classify it as a substrate word whose original sense is unrecoverable. It is not Indo-European in any demonstrable way, and the popular association with the raven is a folk etymology: the bird-name (Latin corvus) has no linguistic link to the city-name, which was Ravenna long before any English speaker thought of a bird. Scholarly guesses about "slope" or "ravine" in some Italic dialect have been aired, but none commands acceptance, and honesty requires saying so. From this genuinely unknown root, however, the attested history begins.

Roman Ravenna. The town appears in Roman records from the 1st century BC: Caesar passed through it in 52 BC on his way to cross the Rubicon, Cicero knew it as a municipium, and Pliny the Elder described it as a city of the eighth region of Italy, famous for its pinewoods and its harbour at Classe, which Augustus developed as a base for the imperial fleet of the Adriatic. For centuries it was a prosperous but unremarkable provincial city, known for its marshes, its fishermen, and its grain trade.

The imperial and Gothic centuries. In 402 the Emperor Honorius moved the capital of the Western Empire from Milan to Ravenna, choosing the marshy, easily defended city as a refuge from the Gothic threat. From that moment the name acquired its destiny. It was the capital of the last western emperors, of Odoacer (who deposed Romulus Augustulus in 476 and was himself murdered at Ravenna in 493), and then of Theodoric the Great, the Ostrogothic king who ruled Italy from Ravenna between 493 and 526 and built churches for both his Arian court and the Catholic population. After Justinian's general Belisarius took the city in 540, Ravenna became the seat of the Byzantine exarchs — the imperial governors of Italy — from 584 until the Lombards took it in 751. Gibbon's sentence belongs exactly here: Ravenna is where the "German prince" Theodoric, himself an Arian, presided over bishops and councils in the manner of a Roman emperor, the living emblem of Germanic power entering Roman and Nicene civilization.

Decline, revival, and the word in English. After 751 the city's political weight collapsed, and for a thousand years Ravenna was a quiet provincial town of the Papal States, remembered chiefly for Dante's tomb and for its churches. The 19th and 20th centuries rediscovered the mosaics — the court of Justinian and Theodora in San Vitale, the processions of saints and the great apse of Sant'Apollinare in Classe — and made "Ravenna" again a byword for late antique splendour. English writers from the early modern period onward carried the name into English as a learned proper noun, and in modern English it is a UNESCO-listed city whose name, though built on a root we can never translate, speaks of emperors, exarchs, and the last great light of the Roman world.
