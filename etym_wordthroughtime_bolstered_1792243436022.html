- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) — Used solely as a noun, bolster, designating a long stuffed cushion or bed-prop used to support the head or elevate the mattress.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) — Maintained domestic textile meaning; broadened in late medieval architecture and carpentry to describe structural support blocks and bearing pads.
- Early Modern English (16th c.) — Converted into a verb (to bolster), initially meaning to pad with cushions or dress someone up with stuffed padding; quickly acquired figurative pejorative senses of propping up a bad cause, vanity, or fraudulent claim.
- 17th–18th c. (Enlightenment) — Neutralized and elevated in philosophical and rhetorical prose to mean providing solid theoretical, moral, or evidential support to an argument, faith, or policy.
- 19th c. (Industrial Revolution) — Solidified in heavy engineering (railway truck bolsters, stamping press bolster plates, knife bolsters).
- 20th–21st c. — Pervasive in geopolitical, economic, and journalistic discourse (e.g. bolstering liquidity, bolstering alliances, bolstered by confidence), as well as standard participial adjective and past tense usage.
