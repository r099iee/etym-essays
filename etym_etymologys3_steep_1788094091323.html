Three lives of steep: eminence, geometry, complaint. The adjective steep has led three successive lives in English — a life of grandeur, a life of measurement, and a life of grumbling — yet every modern sense runs down one unbroken chain of attested forms from the Germanic adjective *staupaz. Its evolution is a textbook case of a word emigrating from landscape description into ethics, statistics, and slang without ever losing its native image of the near-vertical line.

I. The Germanic inheritance: height as eminence. Proto-Germanic *staupaz is the reconstructed adjective behind Old English stēap, Old Frisian stap, and Middle Low German stōp “projecting, protruding.” The core was verticality — what juts up, stands tall, protrudes. This is the same semantic field that produced the steeple and the promontory: a steep shoulder of land is, in the oldest terms, a shoulder that stands up out of the horizontal world.

II. Old English stēap: towers, cliffs, and high places (7th–11th c.). In Old English, stēap appears in prose and glosses meaning “high, lofty, towering” and also “prominent, projecting,” with the sense “precipitous, steep” already present for cliffs and banks. Old English culture prized height: high hills were places of assembly and theophany, hill-forts and burhs used steep approaches for defence, and churches were beginning to raise towers. The connotation of stēap in this period is strongly positive — height as eminence, strength, sanctity. Phonetically the word was the diphthongal stēap, with a long front diphthong that later levelled.

III. Middle English (12th–15th c.): from lofty to sloping. As the Old English diphthong monophthongized, stēap became stēpe, and the meaning narrowed: “lofty” receded and “abruptly sloping” took command. The narrowing is the signature of a travelling, building society: late medieval England moved by cart and packhorse, and people needed a word for hills, passes, stairs, and roof pitches that could not be comfortably climbed. Steep joined the working vocabulary of pilgrimage roads, town walls, mill leats, and manor stairs.

IV. The Great Vowel Shift (15th–17th c.): /steːp/ to /stiːp/. Middle English stēpe, with its long close-mid vowel, underwent the Great Vowel Shift: long /eː/ raised to /iː/, giving modern steep /stiːp/. The spelling with -ea- stayed frozen as a historical monument. The shift did nothing to meaning, but it did separate steep from the homographic verb steep (Old English stēpan), whose different vowel history took it to /stiːp/ as well — the two words drifted apart semantically while remaining letter-identical, a curiosity that still trips readers deciding whether tea is steeped or a hill is steep — context alone keeps the two apart.

V. Renaissance figurative use: the steep and thorny way. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries moralized the slope. Shakespeare’s Hamlet (c. 1601) sets “the steep and thorny way to heaven” against the primrose path of dalliance: religion becomes topography, virtue an ascent. The King James Bible (1611) at Matthew 8:32 has the Gadarene swine running “violently down a steep place into the sea,” making steep the authorized word for a catastrophic cliff-plunge. In both, the word leaves measurement and enters ethics — and the ethical colour it acquires is strenuous, penitential, earnest.

VI. The quantitative and commercial expansion (19th–20th c.). Industrial society brought graphs. Railway engineering borrowed gradient language; statisticians and journalists turned “steep rise” and “steep fall” into stock descriptions of prices, populations, and epidemics. Because data can fall as violently as it rises, steep became direction-neutral: a steep discount is as steep as a steep premium. Meanwhile the informal registers of Victorian and twentieth-century English developed the complaint senses: “a steep price” (excessive), “a steep demand” (unreasonable), “a steep story” (unbelievable). The semantic bridge for each is the same — what rises sharply costs you dearly, whether in money, effort, or credulity. The twentieth century added the “steep learning curve” and kept the eighteenth-century “steeplechase” alive on racecourses.

VII. Socio-cultural niches today. Modern steep divides cleanly into registers: neutral geography (steep hill, steep roof), quantitative report (steep rise in cases), informal complaint (a bit steep), and fossilized relics (steeple, steeplejack, steeplechase). Nautical steep-to and geological steeply dipping strata keep the word in technical manuals. The old grandeur survives only in the steeple — an architectural term from the same root — while everyday steep has settled into a modest, practical, mildly complaining word.

Summary of the drift. Grandeur (height) → geometry (inclination) → ethics (difficulty) → economics (excess): one image of a rising line, re-read by each epoch. The steep shoulder of land in the source text is thus not only a truthful landscape description but the living descendant of a word that once meant simply “high, towering” — the landscape still speaks its oldest sense.
