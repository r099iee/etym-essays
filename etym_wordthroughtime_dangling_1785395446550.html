- [Late 16th c. borrowing] - Entered English from Scandinavian (cf. Danish dangle), an imitative word for hanging loosely and swaying; neutral and physical, describing anything attached at one end yet free to swing.
- [17th-18th c. concrete sense] - The core sense settles: a rope, limb, or ornament dangling from a fixed end, swaying with gravity — homely and concrete, with a faint wryness about loose, ungainly, or comic suspension.
- [18th-19th c. amorous & idle sense] - 'To dangle after' someone becomes a suitor's pursuit and a dangler an idle hanger-on; the word takes on overtones of aimlessness and being left without commitment.
- [19th-20th c. manipulative sense] - 'To dangle something before someone' means to hold out a temptation or hope without granting it — deliberate bait, implying suspense and control over the offered object.
- [20th c. grammatical sense] - 'A dangling participle' names a modifier left without its proper subject, the grammarian's image of a phrase left hanging unsupported.
- [Modern register] - Informal, homely, and slightly comic, prized in narrative and comic verse for its wobble and motion; formal prose prefers suspended or pendent.
