THUMPING is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb thump, a sound-symbolic English formation with no secure PIE ancestry. The honest non-PIE chain is as follows. (Origin: expressive / onomatopoeic English formation imitating a dull heavy blow; no reconstructible PIE etymon) → Early Modern English thump (verb and noun, 'to strike with a dull heavy sound; such a blow itself', attested c. 1530s) → thumping (present participle and participial adjective, 'striking heavily; producing thumps', attested from the mid-16th century) → extended colloquial intensifying sense 'remarkably large, emphatic, whopping' (19th century; 'a thumping lie', 'a thumping win') → THUMPING (card form, the -ing participle/adjective, retained in inflected form). Note on the record: no single uncontested ancient etymon exists for thump. The -ump frame is a phonestheme shared across English impact-words — bump, jump, dump, hump, plump, stump — where a short vowel plus a nasal plus a bilabial or velar closure encodes bluntness, sudden contact, or swelling mass. A Scandinavian tinge is plausible (Old Norse dumpa 'to thump, knock' is the probable source of English dump), but there is no uncontested Old Norse etymon for thump itself, and honesty keeps the label expressive/imitative rather than inherited. The genuine Indo-European root for 'strike' — *steu-, as in Latin tundō 'to beat, pound' (cf. contusion) and Sanskrit tudáti 'strikes' — is a different shape entirely and is not a parent of thump.
