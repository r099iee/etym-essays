PATRONISING — derivation chain - Proto-Indo-European *peh₂ter- (also written *pəter-) "father" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE). One of the most stable words in the family: Greek patḗr, Latin pater, Sanskrit pitṛ́, Germanic fæder (English father). - Latin pater "father" (attested classical Latin). - Latin patrōnus "protector, defender, patron" (formed on pater + the suffix -ōnus; attested classical Latin). In the Roman system of clientela the patrōnus was the legal protector who defended the interests of his clients — a relationship of protection on one side, dependence on the other. - Late Latin / Old French patron "patron saint; advocate; protector; benefactor" (developed from patrōnus; attested in Old French from the medieval period). - Anglo-French / Middle English patron "a protector, supporter; a patron saint" (attested in English from the 13th century; the word entered with the Norman settlement and was first used chiefly of patron saints and of benefactors). - English patronize (British patronise) "to act as a patron to; to support, favour, encourage" (formed on English patron + the verbal suffix -ize/-ise; attested from the late 16th century, c. 1590s; the neutral, benefactor sense). The sense "to frequent (a business) as a customer" developed in the 18th century and remains current. - English patronising (British) / patronizing (American) "treating someone in a way that implies inferiority, with apparent kindness" (participial adjective; the pejorative sense of the verb — "to treat with condescending superiority" — is attested from the late 18th century, and the participial adjective follows it) → Modern English PATRONISING. Notes and nuances: - The chain is single and linear through the Latin stem; the -ize/-ise verb suffix and the -ing participle are ordinary English derivations, not new roots. - The spelling split is purely orthographic: patronising (British, with -ise) and patronizing (American, with -ize) are the same word; the -ize spelling is the older and, in -ize-using dictionaries, standard even in British English, though everyday British usage commonly prefers -ise. - The semantic turn is the essential recorded fact: the verb was neutral and positive for two centuries (supporting artists, frequenting a shop), and only in the late 18th century acquired the pejorative sense "to treat with assumed superiority," when the imbalance built into the Roman patron–client relationship was applied to social equals. The modern adjective inherits that late shift. - No stage is invented: pater and patrōnus are attested classical Latin forms, patron is attested in Old French and Middle English, patronize is attested in the late 16th century, and the pejorative sense is dated to the late 18th century.
