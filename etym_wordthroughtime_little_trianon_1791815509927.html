- 1670 — a royal pleasure-house of porcelain tiles rises in the Versailles park and gives the name “Trianon” to the spot; it means escape from court etiquette.
- 1687 — the Grand Trianon of marble replaces it; “Trianon” now denotes the king’s private palace, distinct from the official state palace.
- 1762–1768 — Gabriel’s small neoclassical palace rises on the estate; the diminutive name marks a refined private retreat for Louis XV and his circle.
- 1774 — Louis XVI gives it to Marie-Antoinette; the name becomes attached to the queen’s private world, her garden, theatre and model village, and to growing public resentment of royal seclusion.
- 1789–1793 — Revolutionary polemic turns “Trianon” into a byword for waste and concealment; the buildings are emptied, though they survive.
- 1837 — Louis-Philippe converts Versailles into a museum; English visitors and writers, Carlyle included, encounter the place as “Little Trianon”, a relic of royal sickness and retreat.
- 1920 — the Treaty of Trianon is signed in the Grand Trianon, giving the name a new, geopolitical meaning in Central Europe.
- 20th–21st c. — the small palace becomes a heritage attraction and a fixture of Marie-Antoinette mythology; the English name “Little Trianon” fades into period prose.
