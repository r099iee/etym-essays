• blackthorn fruit: Similar because it is the literal botanical paraphrase of a sloe; differs because blackthorn fruit is explanatory prose for field guides, while sloe is the one-word country name everyone actually picks and says.
  • damson: Similar because both are small dark hedgerow plums made for jam and gin; differs because a damson is larger, sweeter, closer to the orchard, while a sloe is wilder, smaller, and far more astringent raw.
  • bullace: Similar because both are wild plums of thickets and old hedges; differs because bullace is rounder, yellower or greener, and more localised, while sloe is the universal blue-black hedgerow plum.
  • haws: Similar because both are autumn hedgerow fruits gathered on country walks; differs because haws are the red mealy berries of hawthorn for jelly, while sloes are the blue-black plums of blackthorn for gin.
  • hips: Similar because both are wild autumn fruits of thorny shrubs, rich in country lore; differs because hips are the scarlet seed-pods of rose for syrup and tea, while sloes are true stone-fruits for frost and spirits.
  • elderberries: Similar because both are dark wild berries turned into wine and winter remedies; differs because elderberries grow in flat creamy-headed clusters on elder, cooked for immunity, while sloes hang singly on vicious thorn for gin.
  • prune: Similar because both are dried or preserved plums with deep flavour; differs because a prune is a cultivated dessert plum dried for eating, while a sloe is a wild fruit infused for drinking.
  • plum: Similar because the sloe is botanically a miniature plum, same genus and stone; differs because plum means the large sweet orchard fruit eaten fresh, while sloe means its tiny sour wild cousin that no one eats raw twice.
