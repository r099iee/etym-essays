Growling is the emission of a low, continuous, guttural sound produced deep in the throat, the classic vocal signature of an animal asserting threat, dominance, or territorial displeasure. The form used in The Hobbit is the present participle of the verb growl, and the -ing ending does vital work: it foregrounds duration and continuity, turning the growl from a single event into an ongoing condition, a sustained, vibrating menace that fills the night air like weather. In the passage, Gandalf listens to the Wargs' growling and yelping together, and the two sounds build a full spectrum of hostility — low menace on one side, sharp distress on the other — and it is exactly this soundscape, more than any single visible act, that convinces the wizard, powerful though he is, that they are in a very bad place and have not yet escaped at all. The growl is felt in the chest before it is heard: it is the rumble of a threat that needs no words, the pack's collective, throaty readiness to kill expressed in pure vibration rather than in posture or speech, and that is why it unsettles even the wisest of the Company. PRIMARY SENSE — THE ANIMAL THREAT SOUND In zoology and ordinary speech, growling is the low rumbling vocalisation of a dog, wolf, bear, or other carnivore, usually but not always accompanied by bared teeth, raised hackles, and a stiffened posture. It is a distance-increasing signal: the animal is effectively saying 'keep back' without committing to attack, and ethologists class it with other threat displays that forestall actual fighting. A dog growls at a stranger, over food, or when its territory, mate, or pups are approached; a wolf growls to order a pack-mate; a big cat growls in warning before it charges. Acoustically the growl sits at the bottom of the canine vocal range, produced by the vocal folds vibrating at low frequency while the jaws are held nearly shut, and it is this combination of depth and restraint that makes it far more menacing than a bark: the animal is holding the explosion back, and the listener knows it. SECONDARY SENSE — THE HUMAN GROWL Transferred to people, to growl means to speak or say something in a low, angry, surly, throaty voice, the words rumbling out as if half-uttered: he growled an answer; she growled a warning through clenched teeth. The semantic bridge is direct and ancient — the speaker is imagined as a beast, the voice deliberately lowered to an animal register to express irritation, resentment, or menace without open violence. The same acoustic image underlies the growling stomach: the belly's low rumbling (borborygmus in medical terminology) has been named by this word since the earliest recorded English uses, and in the 15th century growl was in fact first recorded of thunder and of the belly before it was ever recorded of a dog. TERTIARY SENSE — THE INANIMATE RUMBLE By straightforward onomatopoeic extension, growl applies to any low, continuous, rumbling inanimate sound: the growl of thunder, the growl of an engine, the growl of heavy machinery, the growl of distant artillery, the growl of low-frequency music leaking from a car stereo. In every case the shared feature is a deep, sustained, barely contained vibration, and the word lends the object a hint of suppressed power or threat. MODERN JARGON AND TECHNICAL USAGES
  • Zoology and ethology: 'growl' names the low-frequency threat vocalisation of canids, felids, and ursids, studied as part of agonistic (threat-and-conflict) behaviour; it is contrastive with the bark (alarm), the whine (submission or distress), and the howl (long-distance communication).
  • Acoustics and sound engineering: a 'growl' is a low, guttural, distorted tone; the word describes a gritty, aggressive timbre, and 'growling' names the deep sustained throat-voice used in rock and metal singing.
  • Music: 'growling' is the standard term for the deep, guttural vocal technique of death metal and adjacent genres, a modern coinage built on the same acoustic image.
  • Drinks and slang: a 'growler' is a jug or sealed glass jar for takeaway beer, supposedly named for the rumbling sound of carbonation when opened; 'growler' is also an old four-wheeled cab and, in maritime dialect, a small iceberg that rumbles as it turns.
  • Biology: several animals carry the name because of the sound — the 'growling owl' (the barking owl), and colloquial 'growler' for bears heard low in the dark.
REGISTER, CONNOTATION AND SEMANTIC HISTORY The word is earthy, informal, and intensely sensory. Applied to a person it always implies surly, unpolished, almost animal anger rather than eloquent rage. Its connotation has been strikingly stable since the 15th century — the verb has always meant 'to rumble low and menacingly' — but its application has broadened outward from thunder and belly, to dogs and wolves, to engines, loudspeakers, and distorted guitars, each new sense reusing the same deep, continuous, threatening vibration. The participle growling extends adjectivally to anything that sustains that menace: a growling dog, a growling stomach, a growling engine, growling storm-clouds. In The Hobbit the word does not merely describe noise; it carries the whole drama of unspoken threat, and Gandalf's dread is the reader's, because the growl promises violence that has not yet been released.
