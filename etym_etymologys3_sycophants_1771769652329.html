THE EVOLUTION OF "SYCOPHANTS" 1. The puzzle of the fig. Sycophant is one of English's most colourful Greek loans, and its career is inseparable from Athenian social and legal life. It is a transparent compound of Greek sŷkon "fig" and the agent element of phainein "to show," yielding sykophántēs, literally "fig-shower." Ancient and modern scholars have debated what the fig metaphor originally meant. The traditional schoolroom explanation held that early sykophants denounced people who illegally exported figs from Attica; another line links "showing the fig" to an insulting or obscene gesture used in denunciation; still others treat the whole phrase as opaque slang whose exact motivation was already obscure in antiquity. What is not in doubt is the classical function: in democratic Athens a sykophántēs was a professional or habitual informer — one who brought accusations in the courts, often for personal gain, and who became proverbial for malicious, litigious abuse of the legal system. In a democracy that encouraged private prosecution, the sykophant was the pathological growth on the system — the man who weaponized the courts for blackmail and vendetta. 2. The second element: phainein and the PIE "shine" root. The showing element of the compound belongs to a large and well-attested Greek family built on PIE *bʰeh₂-, "to shine, appear." Greek phainein means "to show, bring to light, make visible," and its derivatives include phantasia "appearance, imagination" (English fantasy), phainomenon "that which appears" (English phenomenon), epiphaneia "manifestation" (English epiphany), and the agent forms in -phantes, "one who shows." The sykophant, then, was literally one who "brought figs to light" — or, as the word was actually used, one who brought secrets, offences, and accusations to light. 3. Latin and French: from informer to parasite. Latin borrowed the term as sycophanta, retaining the sense of informer but extending it toward trickster, cheat, and social parasite. Roman comedy and satire used the word for the flattering, scheming dependent who ingratiates himself at another's table. French transmitted sycophante to English, where the word is first attested in the sixteenth century. Early English usage still often meant a calumniator or false accuser, close to the Athenian legal sense. 4. The great semantic reversal: informer becomes flatterer. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, English specialized the meaning in the opposite direction from the classical one. The courtroom snitch faded, and the courtier-flatterer took centre stage. By the time of Gibbon's century, sycophants were people who fawned on the powerful — patrons, princes, and public men — in order to secure advantage. The semantic bridge is psychological: both the ancient informer and the modern toady traffic in words as weapons of self-interest, the one through accusation and the other through praise. English thus completed a reversal that Latin had already begun: the word moved from "one who shows figs (denounces)" to "one who shows false admiration (flatters)." 5. Literary and socio-cultural career. Sycophant carried the moral freight of the patronage economy that dominated European letters from the Renaissance to the nineteenth century. In an age of dedications, pensions, and court favour, the sycophant was the anti-type of the independent author — which is exactly the contrast Gibbon draws in the passage: surrounded by an age "filled with sycophants," he dedicated his work to no one. The word is thus inseparable from the sociology of print and patronage, and from the ideal of authorial integrity it helped define. 6. The modern word. Today sycophant means simply a servile flatterer who seeks advantage — a "bootlicker" or "yes-man" in elevated dress. The old informer sense is entirely lost to ordinary usage, surviving only in historical and etymological discussion. The adjective sycophantic and the noun sycophancy keep the word's formal, pejorative register alive in journalism, criticism, and biography.
