The verb ensue begins its long walk at the Proto-Indo-European root *sekʷ-, whose oldest sense was physical and pedestrian: to walk in another’s tracks, to trail close behind, to accompany. It is a root of following, and its reflexes are scattered across the whole family with remarkable fidelity. Sanskrit preserved it almost photographically in sácate, “he follows, accompanies,” and in sakhā, “companion” — the person who walks with you — whence Hindi sakhā, “friend,” and the related sahacar, “companion,” literally “moving together.” Greek kept it in hépomai, “I follow,” and Old Irish in sechid, “he pursues.” But the root’s greatest flowering was in Latin, where the deponent verb sequī, “to follow,” generated one of the most productive families in the language: secundus, “the following one,” gave second; secta, “a following,” gave sect and sectarian; sequentia, “a following in order,” gave sequence; consequī, “to follow together with, to attain,” gave consequence and consequent; subsequī gave subsequent; persequī, “to follow through, to prosecute,” gave pursue, pursuit, and persecution; exsequī, “to follow out,” gave execute and executive; and socius, the “sharing companion,” gave society, social, and associate — the idea of following having widened, through companionship, into the whole idea of social life. The compound ancestor The direct ancestor of our word is the Latin compound insequī, “to follow upon, to pursue,” where the prefix in- carries the sense “upon, after” and gives the verb its force of close, hard-following pursuit. From it Old French built ensuir and ensuivre, “to follow after, to result,” and Anglo-French handed ensuen to Middle English in the fourteenth century. The earliest English uses are legal and narrative: a successor ensues a predecessor, a claim ensues a right. At first the English verb meant both “to pursue” — the older, physical sense — and “to follow as a consequence,” the newer, logical one. Both senses are recorded in the 14th–15th centuries, and the ambiguity is revealing: the word stood at the hinge where literal following (a man pursuing another) and logical following (an effect following a cause) were still felt as one movement. The triumph of consequence Over the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the causal sense triumphed. To ensue came to mean almost exclusively “to result, to come after as a necessary effect,” and the pursuit sense died out. The shift is a classic example of semantic abstraction: a word of physical movement (to walk behind) became a word of logical relation (to follow from), the concrete image of the tracker bleaching into the colourless connective of cause and effect. The prefix’s “upon” force survived as immediacy — what ensues follows hard upon what precedes it — so that ensuing carries not mere order but close, consequential succession. The same abstraction, on a larger scale, is what separates following (positional) from consequent (causal); ensuing stands between them, holding both the immediacy of one and the causality of the other. The modern participle-adjective The present participle ensuing then crystallised as a prenominal adjective meaning “that which follows immediately and as the direct consequence of what came before”: the ensuing chaos, the ensuing weeks, the ensuing debate. In this use it has become a stock instrument of narrative and journalistic prose — writers reach for it precisely because it compresses “what followed as a direct result” into a single word, and because it lends a formal, slightly dramatic colouring to any sequence of events. In the passage the word performs exactly this function: the “ensuing judgement” is the verdict that the exaltation of classical verse made inevitable, the consequence of the pedestal before it. The older, colourless following or subsequent would have said only that the judgement came later; ensuing says that it grew out of what came before — the hinge that turns temporal sequence into logical consequence. Sound changes and transmission summarised
  • PIE *sekʷ- → Latin sequī: the labiovelar *kʷ kept its -qu- spelling in Latin (sequ-), with the deponent ending -ī; the *kʷ remained labialised in Latin but simplified in most other branches.
  • Latin insequī → Old French ensuivre: the prefix in- weakened to en-, and the Latin infinitive -ere became -ir/-ivre in the Gallo-Romance conjugation; the softening of the labiovelar produced the -u- spellings (ensuir, ensuivre).
  • Old French / Anglo-French → Middle English ensuen: the standard Anglo-French transmission of legal and literary vocabulary in the 14th century; the infinitive suffix -en is native Middle English.
  • Middle English ensuen → Modern ensue: regular loss of the unstressed -en ending in the modern period, leaving the modern two-syllable form.
The whole biography — from PIE “to walk behind,” through Latin “to pursue,” through French “to result,” to the modern adjective “immediately consequent” — is a single long drift from the body’s motion to the mind’s logic, and the passage shows the word at its most modern, doing work only an abstract causal word can do.
