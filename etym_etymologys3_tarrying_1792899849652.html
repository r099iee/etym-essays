The past of tarrying is unusually honest about its own limits: Middle English tarien appears in the early 1300s meaning to delay or hold back, and within a generation it has learned its lasting sense of lingering and staying. Everything before that first attestation is mist. Lexicographers from Skeat to the OED have peered into it and reported obscurity, and that obscurity is itself part of the word’s story — a common, homely verb with no pedigree papers, doing its work in stables and on highways while grander Latin words kept the chronicles.

The likeliest romance lead has been examined and left unproven. Old French possessed tarder and targier, to delay, descended from Latin tardare (to slow, from tardus, slow, the ancestor of English tardy). The resemblance to tarry is obvious and tempting, and generations of students have assumed kinship. But the phonology resists: French -arder does not naturally yield English -arry, and no intermediate forms bridge the gap. The OED therefore keeps the two families apart — tardy the legitimate Latin heir, tarry the foundling of uncertain birth — and responsible etymology follows that caution.

Stages of the sojourn:

  • Early 14th c. — the hinderer: tarien enters written English as a transitive verb of obstruction: to tarry a man is to keep him from his business. It belongs to the world of delayed journeys and detained messengers.
  • Late 14th c. — the lingerer: Chaucer’s contemporaries use it intransitively: now the subject delays himself, remaining where he is. This reflexive turn is the birth of the modern sense.
  • 16th c. — the Bible’s word: Tyndale and then the King James translators choose tarry more than a hundred times — tarry ye here, tarry till I come — fixing it as the English of sacred waiting. No other book did more for the verb.
  • 17th–18th c. — peak currency: Bunyan’s pilgrims tarry, hymn-writers tarry, lovers in ballads tarry. It is still ordinary speech, the standard word for a willing pause.
  • 19th c. — the parting of the ways: everyday language promotes wait, stay, and linger, while tarry retreats upward into hymns, poetry, and sermons, growing more beautiful as it grows rarer.
  • 20th c. — Tolkien’s seal: The Lord of the Rings and the Silmarillion tradition use tarrying for the Elves’ provisional dwelling — the Exiles tarrying ere they sail West — restoring the word’s oldest gravity and giving modern fantasy its favourite term for sorrowful sojourn.

Morphologically the form is simple: stem tarry plus the participial -ing, with the y retained before the suffix. Phonetically it has sat still for centuries, the short a and trilled-then-approximant r surviving every vowel shift around them. And semantically it has performed the rarest of feats: a word that narrowed upward, shedding its common uses until only the noble ones remained, so that to call a pause a tarrying is now itself an act of honour — a small linguistic memorial to everyone who ever waited well.
