CHAIN 1 — the "army" element (the first root), traced in isolation:
PIE *koryo- (also written *koro-, *kórios) "war-band, host, army" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE; reflected in Greek koíranos "commander, lord")
→ Proto-Germanic *harjaz "army, host, war-band" (reconstructed, 1st millennium BCE; cf. Gothic harjis, Old English here, Old Norse herr, German Heer)
→ Frankish *hari "army, host" (attested in Frankish personal names and in Latinized charters, 3rd–9th c. CE)

CHAIN 2 — the "rule" element (the second root), traced in isolation:
PIE *wal- "to be strong, to rule" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE; reflected in Latin valēre "to be strong")
→ Proto-Germanic *waldaną "to rule, govern, wield" (reconstructed, 1st millennium BCE; cf. Old English wealdan, German walten, and English wield)
→ Frankish *wald "rule, power, authority" (attested in the same Frankish compounds, 3rd–9th c. CE)

COMPOUND AND FUSED CHAIN — the two roots join in one word, which then descends to the target:
Frankish *harjawald / *hariwald "army-ruler, one who rules a host" — a West Germanic military command-compound, also the source of the personal name Harold (Old English Hereweald, Old Norse Haraldr); attested only inside names and Latinized charters from the 6th–8th c. CE
→ Old French heraut, hiraut, heralt "herald; a messenger and master of ceremonies of a prince" (attested 12th c.)
→ Anglo-French heraud, herald "an officer who announced war, conducted tournaments, and came to record arms" (attested 12th–13th c.)
→ Middle English heraud, herald "herald, messenger, announcer" (attested from c. 1300)
→ English herald + suffix -ry (modelled on Old French -erie): heraldry "the science, art, and legal record of armorial bearings" (attested c. 1560s–1570s)
→ English heraldry ◄ TARGET (present day)

DEBATE / NUANCE: The exact Frankish compound form (harjawald vs hariwald) is reconstructed, not recorded verbatim, and its precise vocalism is debated. The PIE root is conventionally written *koryo- with the variant *koro-, and its connection to Greek koíranos "commander" is generally accepted though the exact grade is argued. The "army" element has no connection to the unrelated PIE *ḱer- "to warm, heart" — the resemblance in spelling is coincidental. Everything from Old French heraut onward is securely attested.
