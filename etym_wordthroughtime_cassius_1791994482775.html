- Late Roman Republic & Early Empire (1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE) — Celebrated by senatorial loyalists as "the Last of the Romans" and a brilliant defender of Syria against Parthia, but demonized by the Julio-Claudian dynasty as an envious, brooding regicide whose rash conspiracy destroyed the Pax Romana.
- Medieval Scholasticism (12th c. – 14th c.) — Regarded through feudal and imperial theology as a treacherous rebel against universal divinely ordained authority; placed alongside Brutus and Judas Iscariot in Dante's Inferno (c. 1320), where he hangs silently from Lucifer's teeth, depicted as muscular, dark, and stubbornly defiant.
- Renaissance & Shakespearean Era (16th c. – 17th c.) — Reinterpreted as a masterclass in political psychology; William Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar (1599) immortalized him with the eternal archetype of the "lean and hungry" thinker whose acute perception, rhetorical persuasion, and passionate hatred of autocracy drive the tragedy.
- Enlightenment & Neoclassical Period (18th c.) — Re-evaluated favorably by Whig historians and anti-absolutist philosophers who praised his military genius at Carrhae and defended his hatred of Caesarism as legitimate republican patriotism.
- 19th to 21st Century — Employed in political discourse and journalism as an evocative synonym for a brooding, calculating political conspirator operating behind the throne; also preserved as a distinguished given name, historically borne by abolitionist Cassius Clay and boxing legend Muhammad Ali.
