propinquital - the biography of a word that makes nearness sound like a claim. The story begins in the deictic vocabulary of Indo-European. The PIE root *pro- meant "before, forth, in front" - a word of orientation, pointing forward from the speaker's body into the space ahead. Its reflexes cover the whole family: Sanskrit pra "before, forth" (as in prajā, "offspring", literally "brought forth"), Greek pró "before, in front" (the source of the prefix pro- in prologue, prognosis), and Latin pro, "before, on behalf of, in front of" - the preposition of the advocate who stands before his client, the magistrate before the forum, the thing that lies in front of one. For centuries *pro- was purely a spatial-deictic root: it located a thing in front of the speaker, nothing more. The decisive event was a fusion of *pro- with the enclitic particle *-kwe, "and". Latin prope, "near, close at hand", is generally analysed as *pro-kwe - literally "and in front", that is, "what lies before one" - and the leap is one of the most elegant recorded shortcuts of semantic drift: mere orientation in front of the speaker becomes proximity. What is before me is at hand; what is at hand is near. The "before" becomes the "near", and the root begins its long slide from pure deixis toward social space. From prope, Latin built a family that systematically extended the spatial sense into the social. The adjective propinquus, "neighbouring, adjacent" (prope + the suffix -inquus), was applied first to things lying close, then to neighbours, then to relatives: the semantic equation "those near one = those who belong with one" was already complete in Roman usage. The abstract noun propinquitas (first century BCE) carried both values in a single word - physical nearness, vicinity, and at the same time kinship, blood relationship. This is the crucial historical bridge for the whole family: in Latin, and in the European learned tradition that inherited Latin, nearness of place and nearness of blood were the same word. Contiguity shaded into consanguinity; the person beside you and the person who shares your descent were, etymologically, the same kind of near. The word then entered the Romance and learned European vocabulary. Old French took propinquité as a learned borrowing, and English adopted propinquity in the fifteenth century - the age of legal and theological vocabulary expanding through Latin and French channels. For its first centuries in English the word lived a double life: physically, the relation of things and places lying close; legally and genealogically, the relation of persons connected by blood or marriage (a nobleman's propinquity to the crown was a question of inheritance, kinship, and title). The double life persists today: dictionaries still list "nearness" and "kinship" side by side. Meanwhile the superlative of prope - proximus, "nearest" - produced its own dynasty: proximitas gave English proximity (17th c.); proximus lies behind approximate ("brought near"), proximal (anatomical position), and, through Late Latin appropiare ("to draw near") and Old French aprochier, the verb approach - the act of coming near, the root's most travelled descendant. The prope family, in other words, seeded an entire vocabulary of approach, nearness, and the process of drawing close. The adjective propinquital itself is a late, rare formation - the final stage of the story. English took the noun propinquity and derived an -al adjective, a formation that appears in dictionaries and scholarly prose rather than in common speech, giving the abstraction the cold, systematic flavour of a classification: "of or arising from nearness", filed under a heading. In Peake's hands the word performs its etymology in miniature. Rottcodd "had become attached in a propinquital way" to his carvings: the clinical, Latinate, filing-cabinet register deliberately ironizes real feeling - affection reduced to a category of contiguity, love by adjacency rather than love by judgment. The whole three-thousand-year slide from "in front" to "near" to "kin" to "a causal psychological claim" is compressed into one adjective, and Peake uses the compression as his irony: nearness, the word insists, is not an accident but an argument. The word's contemporary life is exactly this: a stylistic instrument of the literary and the learned, a dictionary word revived when a writer needs to name proximity-born attachment - of place, of shared space, of kin - with wry, pseudo-scientific detachment. Its perception has settled as that of a deliberately elevated coinage that makes nearness itself sound like a causal claim.
