- The nightmare breath — The word first appears in English meaning to gasp and struggle for breath, bound up with the phantasm: the suffocating, breathless gasp of one oppressed by a vision in sleep, the sense of the body suffocating under a nightmare.
- Settling into effort — By the 16th and 17th centuries the nightmarish colour fades and the word narrows to the ordinary breathing of exertion — the short, rapid, audible breaths of running, heat, and hard work — and sprouts the thirsting figurative 'to pant for/after' (to yearn or long for), with 'panting' as the ongoing state of such breath.
- Contemporary sense — Modern English is entirely physiological and plain: the panting dog, the runner at the finish, the child mid-shock in an enchanted wood. The participle is a workhorse of adventure fiction, marking arrival, effort, and the honest, unheroic cost of the body's doings.
