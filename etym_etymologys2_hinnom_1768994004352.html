Single root word: Hinnom (English proper noun; a Semitic toponym). No PIE ancestry — the word is Hebrew, and the underlying personal name is of obscure, possibly Canaanite, origin. Stage 1 — Hebrew gēʾ "valley, gorge, ravine": the common Hebrew noun for a valley (ge, gai), attested throughout the Bible (2nd–1st millennium BCE). Stage 2 — Hebrew Hinnōm "Hinnom" (a personal or family name): the proper name attached to the valley; its meaning is lost and its origin obscure (possibly Canaanite, possibly a clan name). The form appears in the fuller formulas gēʾ-Hinnōm, "the Valley of Hinnom," and gēʾ ben-Hinnōm, "the valley of the son of Hinnom" (Joshua 15:8; 18:16), attested in the biblical record. Stage 3 — Hebrew Gēʾ-Hinnōm "the Valley of Hinnom" as a fixed place-name: attested in the Hebrew Bible (Joshua, Nehemiah, Jeremiah, 2 Kings), where the valley is the site of the Tophet, the sacrificial fire-pit to Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31–32; 19:6). Stage 4 — English Hinnom "the Valley of Hinnom": the English rendering of the Hebrew name, fixed by the King James Version (1611) in Joshua 15:8, 2 Kings 23:10, Nehemiah 11:30, and elsewhere; the toponym Hinnom is the target word, preserved in English as the biblical name of the valley. Note on the parallel branch (for completeness, since Hinnom is the root word): the valley's name also passed through Aramaic Gehannā (גהנא) into Greek Géenna (Γέεννα), the New Testament's word for hell, and into Latin Gehenna and Syriac/Arabic Jahannam — but these descend from the same Hebrew place-name and are not separate roots. The only genuinely unknown node in the whole chain is the origin and meaning of the personal name Hinnōm itself: no convincing Semitic etymology has been established, and most handbooks simply record it as obscure, possibly Canaanite. Everything from the fixed place-name onward is secure.
