- 1400s: enters English (via Latin cataracta, Greek kataráktēs) as a waterspout or heavy pour of water, and as the floodgate or portcullis that slams down — a word of walls, weather and sieges.
- 1500s–1600s: the Deluge enters English Bibles and poetry — the "cataracts of heaven" are opened — while travellers lay the name on the great falls of the Nile.
- 1700s: the medical sense is fixed: an opacity of the eye, explained as a humour "pouring down" over the pupil; couching is the age's remedy.
- 1800s: the Romantics make the true waterfall sense sublime — Wordsworth's "sounding cataract" haunts the memory as the very type of wild grandeur.
- 1900s–today: everyday speech hears "cataract" chiefly in the clinic; phacoemulsification makes it a routine, curable word, while rhetoric still uses it for any roaring, unstoppable outpouring.
