- - Pre-16th century: Old English godmodor (godmother) is an everyday word for the sponsor at a child’s baptism — a person of religious and social standing.
- - 16th century: colloquial contraction produces "gammer" (and "gaffer") — first still meaning godparent, but already sounding homely and old.
- - 16th–17th centuries: the meaning shifts from "godparent" to "old person" — gaffer = old man, gammer = old woman — because godparents were typically elderly and respected; the words become general village terms.
- - 17th–19th centuries: "gammer" is a stock figure of rural life, folklore, and dialect literature — the talkative old woman of the village, keeper of tales, remedies, and gossip.
- - 19th–20th centuries: with the decline of rural dialects, "gammer" becomes archaic and literary; it survives in folk-tale collections, historical fiction, and consciously old-fashioned prose.
- - Today: rare and deliberately quaint; used by writers like Tolkien to evoke folk memory and the older generation — "greybeards and gammers" are the elders whose testimony the young dismiss as legend.
