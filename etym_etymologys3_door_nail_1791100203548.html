Door-nail joins two of the oldest Germanic nouns, door and nail, into a medieval compound for the big-headed studs hammered into plank doors, made famous by the simile dead as a door-nail. Each half has a PIE pedigree and the compound shows medieval craft vocabulary becoming idiom.

Door continues PIE *dhwer-, door or doorway, attested in Greek thyra, Latin fores, Sanskrit dvar, and Old Church Slavonic dveri. The root likely named the doorway opening rather than the leaf, which explains the persistent plural doors for a single entrance in many languages. Proto-Germanic *dura- and *durz give Gothic daur, Old Norse dyrr, Old High German turi, and Old English duru for the opening and dor for the leaf. Middle English dore generalises to both. The form door with double o reflects lengthening before r and standardisation after the Great Vowel Shift.

Nail continues PIE *h3nogh-, claw, hoof, or pointed growth, yielding Greek onyx for claw and nail, Latin unguis for fingernail, and Germanic *naglaz for both fingernail and metal spike. The unity of body and hardware in one word reflects a time when claws, pegs, and spikes shared pointed form and fastening function. Old English naegl covers both senses, Middle English nail splits them by context, and modern English keeps both with stress and compounds disambiguating.

  • Compound formation 14th c.: dor-nayl for the broad-headed ornamental and structural nail clenched through a door plank. Medieval doors were ledged planks studded to resist splitting and to display wealth. OED first records dornail around 1350.
  • Idiom dead as a door-nail 14th-16th c.: Langland, Shakespeare, and Dickens all use it. The joke is that a nail hammered dead, clinched flat so it cannot be drawn, is emphatically lifeless. Repetition in proverbs fixes the hyphenated form.
  • Dickens opening: A Christmas Carol begins with Old Marley was as dead as a door-nail, then the narrator pauses exactly as in the source sentence to wonder what is particularly dead about a door-nail. The source is Marley's line, showing the idiom already a cliché needing a joke.
  • Orthography: doornail, door-nail, door nail all occur. Hyphenated door-nail preserves the compound's two stresses and matches the source rootWord exactly.
  • Socio-cultural context: medieval joinery with hand-forged nails, doors as security and status, proverb culture that turns craft terms into intensifiers like dead, deader, deadest.
  • Phonetics: door /dɔː/ plus nail /neɪl/ with compound stress on door, reduced but clear second element. RP keeps long open o and diphthongal nail.

Why a door-nail is dead has three folk theories philologists repeat: the nail is dead because hammered dead and clinched, because doors are knocked repeatedly and the nail endures blows without life, or because doornail puns on dorn, thorn, though the last lacks evidence. The honest answer is intensification: dead already meant absolute, and door-nail supplied alliterative, concrete emphasis like dead as mutton or stone-dead. Dickens knew the pedantry was the joke.

Modern use is almost entirely idiomatic. Hardware catalogues still list door nails and clench nails for ledged doors, but most speakers meet door-nail only in the simile and in Dickens at Christmas. The hyphenated form survives in classrooms because teachers quote the Carol verbatim, keeping a medieval craft word alive through literature.
