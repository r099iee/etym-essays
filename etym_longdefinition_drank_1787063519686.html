Drank is the simple past tense of the verb drink: it states that someone swallowed a liquid at a time in the past. "They drank his health" (as in Tolkien's The Hobbit) means that the revellers raised their cups and swallowed in honour of Bilbo — in the older idiom, to drink to someone's health is to toast them. Unlike the participle drunk, drank never takes an auxiliary: we say "I drank" but "I have drunk". The pair is one of the best-known irregular ("strong") verb alternations in English, sitting on the same ablaut pattern as sing–sang–sung, ring–rang–rung and begin–began–begun.

Primary meaning. To drink is to take a liquid into the mouth and swallow it — water, milk, tea, wine. The past tense drank simply anchors that act in the past: "She drank the water", "We drank tea together". The word is fully neutral and everyday; it carries no inherent suggestion of quantity, speed, or intoxication. It is used of people and of animals, and it can be used transitively ("drank a glass of milk") or intransitively ("we sat and drank").

  • The toast construction. "They drank his health" — a fixed idiomatic use meaning they raised their cups and drank in honour of him, as a gesture of goodwill. Related forms: "to drink to someone", "a drink to absent friends". This is the exact sense in Tolkien's sentence, where the company celebrates Bilbo's return.
  • Alcohol and its euphemisms. Because drink is the world's most common intoxicant, drink and drank constantly shade toward alcohol: "he drank too much", "a drinking problem", "drunk". In this area drank is often euphemistic — "he drank" can politely mean he was (or became) an alcoholic.
  • Animals and natural acts. "The cattle drank from the stream", "the birds drank from the pool" — the verb applies to all creatures that swallow liquid, which keeps the word ancient and universal.
  • Figurative extensions of the verb. The verb drink has a rich figurative branch — "to drink in" (to absorb eagerly: "she drank in every word"), "to drink to" (to toast), "drinking in the atmosphere" — but the inflected past drank appears in these chiefly as "drank in" (absorbed). The simple form itself, however, resists metaphor: drank on its own is almost always literal.

Grammar. Drank is the preterite of a class III strong verb: its stem vowel changes by ablaut (drink–drank–drunk) rather than taking the weak past suffix -ed. It is used with all persons ("I drank, you drank, he drank...") and never with have/has/had, which require drunk. Negative and question forms use the auxiliary did: "Did you drink?", "He did not drink". A common learner error is "I have drank" — in standard English this is incorrect; the participle is drunk. The distinction is reinforced by pronunciation: drank /dræŋk/ versus drunk /drʌŋk/.

Secondary meanings and semantic bridges. Drank has no independent secondary senses; it is purely the past tense of drink, and its "meanings" are those of the verb viewed through time. The important modern nuance is register, not lexicon: in physical contexts (drank water, drank milk) the word is completely neutral; in social contexts (they drank, he drank all evening) it defaults toward alcohol, and in the third person habitual sense ("he drank") it can quietly mean alcoholism. This split is the direct result of the cultural weight of alcohol: the same word that names thirst-quenching names intoxication, and context alone decides which face it shows. In Tolkien's line the sense is festive and toasting — a cup raised in goodwill — though, amusingly, the following sentence notes that Bilbo was not feeling cheerful despite all the attention.

In the reading context. "They drank his health, and they patted him on the back, and they made a great fuss of him." The sentence piles up three marks of goodwill; drank his health is the most formal and old-fashioned of them, lending the celebration a slightly antique, chivalric air — appropriate for the legend-adjacent world of the Lonely Mountain.
