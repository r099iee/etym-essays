- Old English–Middle English — both halves are ancient ("dust" and "heap"); the idea of the refuse mound is old (dunghill, midden), but "dust-heap" as a compound belongs to the modern era.
- 18th c. — the London dust-trade grows: household ashes collected by dustmen and piled in dust-yards; the "dust-heap" becomes the urban mound where the city's fires end.
- 19th c. — the dust-heap is an institution: sifted by dust-girls for cinders and salvage, dreamed over as buried wealth — Dickens's Our Mutual Friend (1865) builds its plot on the dust-mounds of Mr. Boffin's Bower; the word enters ordinary vocabulary as the heap where anything worthless ends.
- 1888 — Wilde throws the dead Swallow and the leaden heart onto a dust-heap: the word becomes literature's image of pity reduced to refuse, with "ashes to ashes" echoing underneath.
- 20th c. — "the dust-heap of history" fixes the compound as the image of oblivion (close kin of the "dustbin of history" phrase); literal use fades with the end of the dust-yards.
- 21st c. — chiefly figurative in journalism and literary prose ("consigned to the dust-heap of history"), while the literal mound survives in dialect, archaeology, and the story that keeps both alive.
