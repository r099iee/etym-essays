pavenders (singular pavender) is a fictional species name: it names a "beautiful rainbow-colored fish" of the Eastern Sea of Narnia, invented by C. S. Lewis for The Voyage of the Dawn Treader (1952). In the passage, the company makes "an excellent catch of pavenders," a fish "which they all remembered eating in Cair Paravel in the old days," and the Dwarf cleans them for dinner. The word's primary and only lexical sense, therefore, is a fish in a fictional world — and the interesting lexicography is that the word has no referent outside Narnia: no biologist's catalogue, no dictionary of fish names, no recorded history. Its "meaning" is entirely a product of the invented context, which is precisely why it works so well: the reader never doubts that pavenders are simply a kind of fish that anyone in Narnia would know.

Why the word feels real (its secondary, quasi-senses and connotations):

  • Phonotactic plausibility — pavender is engineered to sound like a real English species name: two syllables, stress on the first, an -er ending, sitting comfortably beside gudgeon, pilchard, prawn, bream, and plaice.
  • Golden-age freight — within the book, the fish is bound to the lost splendour of Cair Paravel ("which they all remembered eating in the old days"), so the word carries nostalgia from its first appearance; catching and eating a pavender is a small revival of Narnia's golden age.
  • Echo of pavonine — the rainbow colouring invites a speculative echo of pavonine, "peacock-coloured," from Latin pāvo, "peacock"; this is plausible inspiration for the coinage, not a historical derivation.
  • Edibility and appetite — the word is a food-word; like trout or salmon it names something to be caught, cooked, and enjoyed, and the plural pavenders rolls with the ordinary confidence of a meal remembered.

Because pavender has no real-world senses, it cannot have true polysemy in the way historical words do; its "secondary meanings" are the connotations the invented context supplies — abundance, beauty, nostalgia, edibility, and the charm of a name that sounds old, ordinary, and delicious. As a literary neologism it belongs with Lewis's other naturalistic coinages (the Dufflepuds, the Marsh-wiggles), and any modern use of the word is a knowing wink at the Dawn Treader's feast.

Grammar and form. Pavender is a count noun, regular in both numbers: singular pavender ("a pavender," "a single pavender"), plural pavenders — the form on this card, made by the ordinary English plural -s. It behaves exactly like a real species name: it can be caught, counted, cooked, and eaten ("an excellent catch of pavenders," "grilled pavenders"), it takes the definite and indefinite articles freely, and its plural can denote the whole class of fish in the way "trout" or "salmon" do collectively. Morphologically it is opaque — it has no recognisable English suffix beyond the -er that happens to echo the -er of real fish names, and it generates no derived forms in the book (there is no "pavendering," no "pavenderish"). Its grammar is that of a perfectly ordinary noun, and that ordinariness is precisely part of the author's trick: a word that obeys all the rules of English morphology cannot be anything but an English word, the reader reasons — so it must name a real Narnian fish.

Register and use. The word has no register outside the book because it has no life outside the book. Within The Voyage of the Dawn Treader it is used with perfect naturalness by the narrator — the same narrator who reports the "excellent catch" and the remembered dinners of Cair Paravel — and it carries no comic or ironic marking; it is simply the right name for the fish. In fandom and literary discussion the plural pavenders is the form everyone uses, since the passage itself presents the fish in the plural. For the lexicographer the word is a useful cautionary example: the realism of a name depends far less on its origins than on its sound-shape, its grammar, and the confidence with which it is used — and pavender has all three in abundance.
