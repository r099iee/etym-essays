• Chaise: Similar because both are light open horse-drawn vehicles for quick travel; differs because a chaise (especially post-chaise) is typically four-wheeled and hired with post-horses for longer journeys, while a gig is the owner's own two-wheeled runabout.
  • Buggy: Similar because both are light one-horse two- or four-wheeled traps for everyday use; differs because buggy is the plainer American and colonial cousin, while gig is the smarter, taller-wheeled English Regency article.
  • Trap: Similar because both name light two-wheeled country vehicles; differs because trap is the loose generic (any light spring cart), while gig names the specific tall-wheeled fashionable form.
  • Curricle: Similar because both are fast, dashing two-wheeled gentleman's carriages of the same era; differs because a curricle is drawn by two horses abreast with a pole, while a gig takes only one horse in shafts.
  • Sulky: Similar because both are featherweight two-wheeled one-horse vehicles built for speed; differs because a sulky seats one and belongs to harness racing, while a gig is a road vehicle for doctors, travellers, and town runabouts.
  • Cabriolet: Similar because both are light elegant one-horse vehicles with a hood option; differs because the cabriolet (ancestor of the hansom cab) is French in origin and often hooded, while the gig is open and English.
  • Dogcart: Similar because both are light two-wheeled country traps for one horse; differs because a dogcart has cross-seats back to back originally for sportsmen and dogs, while a gig is a straightforward single-seat runabout.
  • Whisky: Similar because both are light, fast, single-horse two-wheelers of the period; differs because whisky names the lightest, swiftest phaeton-like form (named for whisking along), while gig is the commoner all-purpose tall-wheeled trap.
  • Shay (chaise): Similar because both are one-horse light carriages of rural England; differs because shay is the countrified, often older and humbler form of chaise, while gig carries a smarter, more dashing town polish.
