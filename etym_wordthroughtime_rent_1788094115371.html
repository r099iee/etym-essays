- Old English: the ancestor rendan 'to tear, cut, lacerate' yields the plain past tense rente — an everyday verb for tearing cloth, flesh, or hair
- 13th century: a second, unrelated rente 'income, annual revenue' enters from Anglo-French, and the two identical forms begin a long coexistence; at first the payment word is a legal/administrative term of manorial accounting
- 14th–15th centuries: the tear-word broadens into the noun 'a rent' (a tear in cloth) and the phrase 'rent asunder' enters devotional and legal usage; the payment word gains the verb 'to rent' (c. 1500) and spreads into everyday commerce
- 16th–17th centuries: the Authorised Version (1611) fixes rent 'torn' in the nation's memory ('the veil of the temple was rent in twain', 'rend your heart'), giving the tear-sense a permanent sacred-literary halo; meanwhile rent the payment becomes the default word for tenancy
- 19th–20th centuries: the payment sense entirely dominates — rent control, rent strikes, 'for rent' signs, the Rent Act; the tear-sense retreats to poetry, journalism ('a nation rent by war') and fixed phrases ('rent the air') - 21st century: further metaphorical extensions — 'rent-free' (of someone occupying your thoughts without payment), 'rent-seeking' in economics, and the verb's expansion into hiring everything from cars to holiday homes — while 'rent' as 'torn' survives almost only as a fossilised literary phrase
