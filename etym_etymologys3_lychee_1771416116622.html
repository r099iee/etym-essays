Lychee is a direct loan from the Sinitic world, and its biography is the biography of a luxury. The Chinese name is lìzhī (荔枝), a compound of 荔 lì and 枝 zhī “branch,” but the oldest scholarly tradition glosses the word as 離枝 lízhī, “detached from the branch.” The etymology is a piece of horticultural wisdom: the lychee’s flesh begins to deteriorate within three days of leaving the tree, so the fruit, in effect, must be plucked to be eaten—the moment it leaves the branch, its brief window of perfection opens. Early references to the fruit cluster in the Han dynasty, when the imperial pleasure-park literature of Sima Xiangru celebrated the exotic produce of the south, and the fruit’s fame was sealed by a tribute economy in which relay horsemen, thundering north with fresh lychees, carried the subtropical harvest to the court. Du Mu’s celebrated Tang verse—the “red dust of the horseman” that makes the consort laugh—fixed the lychee forever as the fruit of imperial appetite and extravagance. Sound and form at each stage:
  • Old Chinese compound *re-te, glossed 離枝 lízhī “detached from the branch”: the fruit must be cut from the tree because it spoils within days of leaving it.
  • Classical Chinese (Han dynasty, 2nd c. BC): the fruit appears in imperial literature such as the “Shanglin fu” of Sima Xiangru; the standard modern graph is 荔枝 lìzhī.
  • Mandarin lìzhī 荔枝: the northern reading, tied to the tribute culture of relay horsemen from Lingnan celebrated in Du Mu’s Tang verse.
  • Cantonese lai6-zi1 荔枝 and Hokkien nāi-chi: the southern forms first heard by European traders at Guangzhou.
  • Portuguese (16th c.) lechia, líchia: among the first European records of the fruit.
  • English (18th c.) litchi: the older British botanical spelling; botanical Latin Litchi chinensis.
  • English (19th–20th c.) lychee: the standard modern spelling and the card’s rootWord.
Because the word is a Chinese compound, it has no Indo-European ancestry: there is no PIE root beneath it, and its genealogy is entirely Sinitic, travelling through the regional languages of the southern coast. When European merchants, missionaries, and planters reached Guangdong, they recorded the Cantonese form lai6-zi1 and the Hokkien nāi-chi, and from these southern transcriptions the fruit entered the languages of trade. Portuguese traders of the sixteenth century wrote lechia; British accounts of the eighteenth century gave litchi, still the standard spelling in much botanical writing; and the modern lychee—with its familiar ⟨y⟩—settled into culinary English in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, alongside the spellings lichee and lichi. Each borrowing preserved the sound of a southern Chinese word whose very shape, with its tone-marked vowels, records a specific regional voice rather than a bookish Mandarin reading. Socio-cultural and literary trajectory:
  • Han dynasty: the lychee is the tribute fruit of the south, celebrated in imperial park literature and sent by relay to the court.
  • Tang dynasty: Du Mu’s verse fixes its fame as the fruit of imperial appetite; the “consort’s smile” becomes the emblem of the cost and delicacy of the southern tribute.
  • 16th century: Portuguese traders at Guangzhou record the southern forms; the fruit enters European trade and botanical accounts.
  • 18th century: British naturalists adopt litchi; the tree is described and eventually cultivated beyond China in India, Mauritius, and Hawaii.
  • 19th–21st centuries: lychee settles into standard culinary English; the fruit becomes a familiar supermarket item and global flavour, yet retains a delicate, exotic, southern aura.
In the novel, that exotic pedigree is the point. The girl’s cheeks are “as white and firm as a fresh lychee,” and the simile imports an entire southern world of tribute, perishability, and sensual luxury. The lychee’s whiteness is luminous and its firmness taut; above all, it is fresh—a word the story repeats in “fresh lychee”—and freshness, in the novel’s aesthetic, is the signature of the newly arrived, the untarnished, the briefly perfect. The fruit cannot keep; neither, implicitly, can beauty. One exotic noun performs, in a single image, what pages of description might otherwise labour to say: that this girl glows with a health and value that are exquisite, southern, and destined to fade—precisely the quality of all the young beauties of the Jia household.
