COPIOUSLY is the adverb of copious: it means in large amounts, in abundant or plentiful quantity, in generous profusion. Where the adjective copious describes the thing that is abundant (copious notes, copious tears, a copious supply), the adverb copiously describes the action that produces or delivers that abundance — taking notes copiously, sweating copiously, pouring wine copiously. In the reading passage the word does its classic modern work: 'To drink heartily, copiously, and with relish in large draughts' — the adverb is stacked with two companions ('heartily' and 'with relish') to build a picture of drinking that is not just eager but abundant: many large draughts, in great quantity, without stinting. Copiously names the sheer volume of the action.

CORE SENSES

  • In large quantity: abundantly, plentifully, in great amounts — 'to sweat copiously', 'to bleed copiously', 'it rained copiously'. The emphasis is on volume and plenty.
  • Of production and output: to produce something in great quantity — copiously illustrated books, copiously annotated editions, copiously documented research.
  • Of supply and generosity: to provide or give without stinting — 'the host poured wine copiously', 'the spring supplied water copiously'.
  • Of expression: to write or speak at great length and with rich detail — a copiously written history, copiously detailed memoirs.

POLYSEMY, JARGON, AND METAPHORIC SHIFTS

  • Academic and scholarly: 'copiously annotated' and 'copiously illustrated' are the standard phrases of scholarly editing and publishing, describing editions and works packed with notes, plates, and references; 'copious notes' is the classic student collocation.
  • Medical and physiological: 'copious sweating', 'copious bleeding', 'copious discharge' — the clinical adverb for alarming or abundant bodily output; in medical notes it signals volume that is noteworthy, sometimes dangerous.
  • Literary-rhetorical (historical): the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century rhetorical ideal of copia — abundance of words and matter — lives on in 'copious style', 'copious expression', the writer's or speaker's wealth of language; Erasmus's Renaissance textbook on rhetoric was called De Copia (On Abundance).
  • Meteorological and environmental: copious rainfall, copious snowfall — abundant precipitation.
  • Figurative extension: anything that overflows its measure — copious energy, copious information, copious evidence — is the word's modern spread beyond literal substance to anything plentiful.

WHY THE SECONDARY MEANINGS EXIST (HISTORICAL BRIDGE)

The whole family flows from the Latin noun copia, 'abundance, plenty, wealth, resources', formed from co- ('together') and ops ('wealth, power, resources'): copia is literally 'resources gathered together', plenty in one place. The Romans already used copia and its adjective copiosus for material wealth, for ample military supplies, and — in rhetoric — for abundance of words, so the double life of the word (physical plenty and verbal plenty) is already Latin. The goddess Copia, personifying plenty, is the figure behind the cornucopia, the 'horn of plenty'. English took copious in the fourteenth century and the adverb copiously in the fifteenth, and the modern uses are simply the old Latin richness spread across new domains: from harvest wealth to scholarly notes to clinical sweating to overflowing metaphor, the word has never stopped meaning 'in great supply'.

USAGE AND REGISTER

'Copiously' is still a shade more formal and literary than 'a lot' or 'abundantly' in casual speech; it is the natural choice when the writer wants to stress the sheer volume and generosity of an output or a flow. It pairs naturally with verbs of production and discharge — note, write, illustrate, sweat, bleed, rain, pour — and in careful usage it implies not merely a large quantity but a plentiful, unstinted, almost overflowing supply. The adverb is transparently built from the adjective plus the suffix -ly, and its pronunciation /ˈkəʊ.pi.əs.li/ keeps the Latin-sounding trisyllabic core that gives the word its learned colour.
