trudge — (1) To walk slowly and with heavy steps, wearily and laboriously, as if every footfall is a small effort; to plod. (2) As a noun, such a walk — "a long trudge." The word is the verb of the weary forced march, of soldiers, laborers, and pilgrims for whom travel is measured in effort rather than miles.

The literary moment. In the sentence "At night the steamboats hoot and trudge past through the black waters all alight like cities adrift," trudge does something deliberate and strange: it gives the massive vessels the gait of a weary worker. A steamboat does not literally trudge — nothing in its machinery walks — but the word lends it a heavy, dogged, laborious progress through the black water, and the collision with hoot (the shrill mechanical call of the whistle) sets sound against movement: the boats announce themselves like beasts and move like exhausted men. The transference is the whole point of the image, an industrial sublime in which the machine is humanized by its own weight. The tone is darkly reverent, slightly mournful — great floating engines laboring past in the night, their passage read as plodding rather than gliding.

Primary sense — the weary walk. To trudge is to walk slowly, heavily, and with effort, usually against difficulty — mud, snow, distance, exhaustion. The word is expressive and imitative in origin: the blunt tr- onset is the phonestheme of effortful contact (compare tread, tramp, trip), and the heavy -udge rime (nudge, drudge, grudge) seals each syllable with a dull impact. It belongs from the first to the walking poor.

  • The verb trudge (c. 1540s, Scotland and the north): "to walk laboriously, to plod" — an expressive/imitative coinage; the OED marks its origin unknown, probably imitative.
  • The noun trudge: "a weary, heavy walk" — "it's a long trudge up the hill," "the trudge home."
  • The fixed phrase "trudge on": walking that must continue; endurance rather than energy — the adverb carries the real meaning.
  • The agent noun trudger: one who trudges — rare but attested.
  • Sound symbolism: the tr- + -udge pattern is phonesthemic — the sound performs the meaning of feet planted in mud.


alight — (1) Brightly lit, ablaze, kindled, glowing — a thing set burning or shining with light. (2) As a verb (a separate word): to dismount, descend, or step down from a vehicle or horse; to settle or land (of a bird). This card's sense is the adjective of light: "all alight like cities."

The literary moment. The steamboats pass "through the black waters all alight like cities adrift." The adjective is deliberately grand: the boats are not merely lit but kindled, their windows and lamps glowing like the windows of floating towns. The word belongs to the greatest light-root of the family — the same root that gave Latin lux, Greek leukós, and English light itself — and it carries a faintly archaic, ceremonial radiance that plain "lit" would never achieve. The river is black; the cities of light drift upon it.

Primary sense — the adjective of ignition. Alight (from a- "on" + the verb to light, "to kindle") means literally "lit up, set burning, shining." It is used predictively — a building set alight, a face alight with joy — and it has stayed a literary, rather than a kitchen-table, word.

  • The shining adjective (a- + light "to kindle"): "ablaze, illuminated, glowing" — the word of this card; kept alive in poetry while the plain speech says "lit."
  • The verb to light (a fire): to kindle, to set alight — from the same *leuk- root.
  • Figurative alight: "eyes alight with joy," "a face alight" — the maintained glow of emotion transferred from fire to feeling.
  • The homonym verb to alight (dismount): Old English ālīhtan "to descend, to relieve of a burden," from the OTHER light-root *legwh- "not heavy" — unrelated to the shining alight, a celebrated false-friend pair.
  • Collocations: "set alight," "caught alight," "all alight," "alight with," "to light up."


adrift — Floating without being steered or anchored; drifting at the mercy of current and wind; by extension, without purpose, direction, or moorings — a person or life unmoored. Nautical in origin, psychological in reach.

The literary moment. The steamboats are "all alight like cities adrift" — radiant but unmoored, floating towns with no hold on the land. The word completes the sentence's three-word journey: first the boats trudge (heavy, effortful, still driven), then they are alight (kindled, radiant, still burning), and finally they are adrift — surrendered to the black water, cities that have lost their anchor, driven rather than driving.

Primary sense — nautical. Adrift (from a- "in, on" + drift) means floating at random, cut from moorings or anchor, at the mercy of current and wind. It is a failure of seamanship — a vessel that has lost its hold on land — and the logbook and forecast language of the sea.

  • The nautical adjective (16th–17th c.): "floating at random, without anchor or steering" — a- "in" + drift, coined in the language of ships and the sea.
  • Sea and weather register: "set adrift," "cast adrift," "a raft adrift," "cut adrift" — vessels that have lost their hold on the land.
  • The psychological/social extension (19th c.–present): a person adrift in the city, adrift from family and friends — the nautical helplessness becomes the master-metaphor for a life unmoored, without purpose or direction.
  • The family: adrift is built on drift (n.), itself from the Germanic drive-family — Old Norse drífa "to drive," drif "a snowdrift" — so that adrift means literally "in the state of being driven."
  • Modern uses: "a life adrift," "cast adrift by the economy," "adrift in a sea of information" — the metaphor of directionless movement applied to persons, careers, and ideas.
