Proto-Indo-European *per- (with variant *por-) — "to lead, to pass over/across, to carry through" (reconstructed root; the o-grade *por- form yields the "passage" nouns; reflexes include Latin porta "gate" and portare "to carry", Greek poros "ford, passage", English fare and ford via Germanic); c. 4500–2500 BCE ↓ Latin porta — "gate, door, entrance" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g., Plautus; from *por-tā, a "passage"; cf. the gates of Rome, Porta Capena, etc.) ↓ Latin porticus — "colonnade, covered walkway, gallery, portico, porch" (attested from the 1st c. BCE, e.g., Cicero, Vitruvius; formed from porta with the denominal suffix -icus; some scholars instead connect it to portus "harbor" — see debate note) ↓ Old French porche — "porch, portico, covered entrance" (attested from the 11th c., e.g., the Chanson de Roland, c. 1100; regular development of Latin porticus, with loss of the interior -i- and reanalysis of -cus) ↓ Middle English porche — "covered entrance to a building" (borrowed c. 13th century; attested c. 1300 and in later medieval texts including church-porch records; the spelling stabilizes as porch) ↓ English porches — plural of porch (the plural used of multiple entrances or of porches as a class, as in Macaulay's History of England, 1848) DEBATED / NUANCED: The internal Latin derivation is disputed: most scholars derive porticus from porta "gate" (the colonnade leading to the gate), but a minority connect it to portus "harbor" (the covered quay); both words descend from the same PIE *per-/ *por- "pass through," so the ultimate ancestry is unaffected. English may have taken the word through Anglo-Norman rather than Central Old French; either route gives the same Middle English porche. The PIE root itself (*per-) has a very large family, and some scholars analyze the "passage" nouns as from an o-grade *por-; this does not change any stage of the attested chain.
