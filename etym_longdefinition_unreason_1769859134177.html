unreason: lack of reason; irrationality; the absence, breakdown, or suppression of rational thought — used both as a plain noun and, especially in literary, religious, and philosophical writing, as a capitalized figure for everything that opposes reason, light, and order. Primary sense — lack of reason. At its simplest, unreason is the absence of reason: conduct, argument, or belief not governed by logic or good sense. It can name a single irrational act ("the decision was born of total unreason and panic"), a general state of mindlessness or folly, or a systematic rejection of rational thinking. As a mass noun it has no plural; it denotes a condition rather than countable instances, and it typically contrasts with "reason" as its absence, opposite, or negation. Historical sense — a wrong or an injustice. In Middle English and Tudor usage, "unreson" meant an unreasonable act, a wrong, an injustice, or anything contrary to right and order. It was the direct antonym of "reason" in its legal-moral sense — reason as the principle of justice and right dealing, the "law of reason" that ought to govern human affairs. To do "unreason" to someone was to wrong them; to act "without reason" was to violate the order of the world. This older, moral-legal sense survives faintly in the modern word's suggestion that irrationality is not mere error but a violation of right order. Personified and philosophical sense. In literary, religious, and speculative writing, Unreason (capitalized) is personified as an active, insidious power — a cosmic or psychological antagonist that darkens the mind, fills gaps with confusion, and lets superstition, paranoia, and myth flourish. Writers capitalize the word to turn a mere absence into a presence: Unreason is not the empty space where logic should be but a force that actively grows "uncertainty and disproportion like weeds". This is the sense in the test sentence, where Unreason "darkens that gap of time bridged by our lightspeed ships, and in the darkness uncertainty and disproportion grow like weeds" — a figure of the decay of reason, memory, and truth when communication is severed across interstellar distances. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • Lack or absence of reason; irrationality — general modern sense.
  • A wrong, an injustice, something contrary to right order — Middle English–Tudor sense, now archaic.
  • Folly or foolish conduct — historical-literal use.
  • Personified Unreason — the force or spirit of irrationality, capitalized, in literary, religious, and philosophical writing.
  • In French-derived intellectual vocabulary, the cognate "déraison" and the philosophical discourse on madness as "the other of reason" (Foucault's Histoire de la folie) — the Enlightenment's term for the unreasoning side of the mind.
Near-synonym differentiation:
  • Unreason vs irrationality: irrationality is a dry, analytical term for a specific logical failure or a process that violates rules of inference; unreason is broader and more atmospheric — a systemic collapse of rational order rather than a single fallacy.
  • Unreason vs folly: folly suggests foolishness, indiscretion, or lighthearted misjudgment; unreason carries existential weight and the sense of something darker and more structural.
  • Unreason vs madness: madness implies clinical insanity, derangement, or violent loss of mind; unreason names the vacuum in which rationality decays, not necessarily a medical condition.
  • Unreason vs absurdity: absurdity emphasizes the ridiculous or meaningless nature of existence; unreason points to the absence and active suppression of rational order.
  • Unreason vs nonsense: nonsense is everyday and playful — absurd speech or ideas; unreason is grave, formal, and philosophical.
  • Unreason vs unreasonableness: unreasonableness is a quality of behavior or argument (being hard to deal with); unreason is the noun for the state of irrationality itself.
Register and usage. Unreason is a formal, chiefly literary and philosophical word, rarely heard in casual speech. It clusters with words like darkness, chaos, night, and ignorance, and appears in titles and discourse about the limits of rationality — discussions of madness, mysticism, faith, and the failures of Enlightenment reason. Its rhetorical power comes from its morphology: the prefix un- negates reason, but the resulting noun reads as an entity in its own right — a named force — rather than merely "not reason". In the science-fiction sentence, capitalized Unreason is given agency: it darkens, it lets things grow; it is an antagonist with a career of its own, and the darkness it spreads is where error and disproportion take root.
