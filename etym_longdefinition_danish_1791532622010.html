Danish is the adjective and demonym of Denmark and the Danes, and the name of the North Germanic language spoken there. It is a word of tribal origin: the Dani appear in Latin sources in the sixth century, the Old English Dene in the ninth, and the derivative Denisc — literally "Dane-ish" — is the direct ancestor of the modern form. In contemporary English the word has three centres of gravity: the nation and its people, the language, and, since the early twentieth century, the sweet pastry.

Primary sense: the people and the country

As an adjective Danish modifies anything belonging to Denmark, the kingdom that occupies Jutland and the islands of Zealand, Funen and their neighbours. It is the ordinary attributive form, while Dane is the noun for a person and Denmark the name of the state. The adjective is used across the whole range of public life, from the constitution and monarchy to food, design and sport.

  • The Danish people — the Danes, a North Germanic nation; the plural noun is Danes, not "Dænes" or "Danish" (except collectively in older writing).
  • The Danish state — the Kingdom of Denmark, a constitutional monarchy and member of the European Union (with opt-outs).
  • Danish law and politics — the Folketing, the Danish constitution of 1849, the Danish welfare model.
  • Danish design and Danish modern — the mid-century furniture and craft movement associated with Arne Jacobsen, Hans Wegner and Finn Juhl.
  • Danish food — Danish bacon, Danish blue cheese, Danish pastry, smørrebrød.

The language

Danish is a North Germanic language, descended from Old Norse and closely related to Norwegian and Swedish. It is the official language of Denmark and one of the official languages of the Faroe Islands, and it was historically the language of administration in Norway and Iceland. Its standard form is Rigsdansk, and its most distinctive phonological feature is the glottal stop known as stød.

  • Standard Danish — the Copenhagen-based norm used in broadcasting and education.
  • Jutlandic — the western dialects, often called jysk, with distinctive vowel systems.
  • Insular Danish — the dialects of Zealand, Funen and the islands.
  • Written Danish — uses the Latin alphabet with æ, ø and å.
  • Norwegian Bokmål — heavily shaped by Danish during the union with Denmark (1380-1814).

The pastry sense

In American English "a Danish" is a sweet, flaky pastry, usually filled with fruit, cheese or custard. The usage is a clipped form of "Danish pastry", which itself translates the Danish wienerbrød "Viennese bread" — the pastry was brought to Denmark by Austrian bakers in the nineteenth century and then exported to America under a national label. The semantic route is therefore doubly indirect: an Austrian technique, a Danish name, and an American ellipsis.

  • Danish pastry — the full form, still used in Britain and in formal American writing.
  • A Danish — the everyday American count noun: "two Danishes and a coffee".
  • Cheese Danish, apricot Danish, cinnamon Danish — the common filled varieties.
  • In Denmark — the pastry is wienerbrød, not "dansk", which shows how national labels shift with export.

Other established compounds

  • Danish oil — a penetrating wood finish made from tung or linseed oil and varnish, popularised by Scandinavian furniture makers.
  • Danish axe — the long-handled two-handed axe of the Viking age and the Anglo-Danish housecarls.
  • Danish tongue — an old name for the Norse language (dǫnsk tunga), used in medieval Iceland and Norway.
  • Danish blue — a strong blue-veined cheese, also called danablu.
  • Danish Crown — the Danish royal house and the country's largest pork exporter, a reminder that the adjective now sells products.

Distinction from near-neighbours

Danish must be kept apart from several words that look or sound similar. Dane is the person; Denmark is the country; Norse is the medieval language of Scandinavia; Nordic covers the wider region including Finland and Iceland; Scandinavian strictly means the peninsula and the Danish, Norwegian and Swedish cultural sphere. The false friends Dutch (Netherlands) and Deutsch (German) are historically related to Dutch but not to Danish, and they are a persistent source of confusion for English speakers.

Semantic bridge

The route runs from tribe to territory to language to product. A tribal name, Dani, became the name of a kingdom and its people; the adjective Denisc named their speech; and in the modern export economy the same adjective was attached to a pastry, a cheese, a style of furniture and a brand of bacon. Danish thus shows how an ethnic label can become a cultural and commercial adjective with a life far beyond the tribe that gave it birth.
