PIE (c. 4500–3500 BCE)
*streig-
“to stroke, rub, press”
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE – c. 100 CE)
*strīkaną
“to stroke, rub; move swiftly,
pass over, glide”
(class I strong verb)
↓
Old English (c. 9th–10th c.)
strīcan
“to stroke, smooth, rub;”
“to go, move on, glide”
↓
Middle English (13th c.)
striken
“to hit, smite, deal a blow,”
“to stamp, impress”
(blow-sense possibly reinforced
by Old Norse strjúka “to strike”)
↓
Middle English (14th c.)
stricken
“hit, smitten (past participle)”
↓
Early Modern English (16th c. – present)
stricken
“afflicted, overwhelmed (by grief,
illness, calamity, shock)”
(lexicalised adjective; the ordinary
participle is now struck)

Note: The “hit” sense of strike is a Middle English development; Old English strīcan meant only “to stroke, rub, glide”. Old Norse strjúka “to stroke, rub; to strike” probably reinforced the new sense. English is anomalous in keeping two participles — struck and stricken — with their senses divided.
