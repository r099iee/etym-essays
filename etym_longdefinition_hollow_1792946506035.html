Hollow names, in its most physical sense, a sunken empty place where solid ground or substance gives way to air: a dip in a hillside, a cavity in a tree, a scooped valley between ridges, the inside of a vessel or reed that should be solid but is not. In the source sentence it carries exactly this landscape sense: the walkers follow the Hedge to a point where it bends inwards along the lip of a hollow, the ground falling away beside the thorny wall into a sheltered dip or combe, its rim or lip holding the walkers above the drop as a cup holds shade and cool below its brim.

Core meaning in detail: A hollow is defined by concavity plus emptiness. A hole pierces through, a pit drops steeply down, a valley stretches long and open, but a hollow is a gentle inward curve, a place where the surface sags, cups, or caves in, whether carved by water, worn by feet, rotted in wood, or left by design. The word moves freely between noun, adjective, and verb: the noun names the place itself, the adjective describes anything having that inward empty shape or its acoustic and spiritual consequences, and the verb names the act of scooping it out. Tolkien the countryman uses it for lived terrain, ground you walk along, look down into, and shelter within, not abstract geometry.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Sunken place in land, a dip or small valley (primary noun sense, from Old English, the source sense): the hollow at the field edge, a wooded hollow, the lip of a hollow. The bridge is the original noun holh, a hole or den, widened from burrow and cave to any cupped landform that holds shadow, mist, or water a little longer than the ridge around it.
  • Cavity within a solid, an empty interior (noun and adjective): the hollow of a tree, a hollow tooth, hollow reeds and bones. The bridge runs from landscape to object by shape analogy: whatever encloses air where solidity is expected is hollow, whether hillside or trunk, and the emptiness matters more than the scale.
  • Empty or echoing because empty (adjective, from the 14th c.): a hollow vessel, hollow walls, a hollow sound. The bridge carries the cavity into its consequences: thin walls boom when struck, empty rooms echo footsteps, and the ear learns to hear hollowness, so the word comes to name the drum-like resonance as well as the void that makes it.
  • Insincere, worthless, without substance (figurative, from the 16th–17th c.): hollow promises, hollow praise, hollow victory, a hollow man. The bridge is moral metaphor: as a hollow tree looks sound but breaks under weight, so a promise that looks solid but holds no intent breaks under need, and politics and poetry have used the image ever since.
  • Hungry or yearning emptiness felt in the body (figurative bodily): a hollow stomach, hollow cheeks, hollow eyes. The bridge runs through the belly: hunger hollows the gut and thins the face, so hollow becomes the visible and felt sign of want, grief, or exhaustion, the body itself turned into a cupped place.
  • To scoop or press into a hollow shape (verb, from Middle English): to hollow out a canoe, hollowed cheeks, hands hollowed to carry water. The bridge is denominal conversion: to make a hollow, whether with adze, spoon, or cupped palms, hollowing as the labour that the noun merely names as result.
  • A resonant low sound quality in phonetics and music (technical extension): hollow vowels, hollow tone on a guitar or organ. The bridge returns to acoustics: tones rich in low partials and lacking edge remind hearers of sound rolling round inside a large empty vessel, and engineers and singers borrow the landscape word for the waveform.

Register and connotation: hollow is plain, rural, and tactile, the vocabulary of walkers, farmers, and carpenters rather than surveyors. It feels sheltered and secret when it names land, a place to hide or rest out of wind, and faintly sad or accusing when it names people and promises, the kindly landscape word turned to quiet moral judgement. Poets love its double music: the soft enclosed vowel itself sounds cupped and echoing, and the lip of a hollow, as Tolkien writes it, gives readers both the rim underfoot and the shadowed cool below in a single phrase.
