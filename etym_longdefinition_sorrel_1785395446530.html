Sorrel is a herbaceous plant of the dock family (Polygonaceae), in its commonest English use the species Rumex acetosa, common sorrel, which bears arrow-shaped leaves whose sharp, sour, lemony taste — owed to oxalic acid and its salts — has made it a traditional wild green, salad leaf, pot-herb, and flavouring for centuries. The word is also applied loosely to a whole group of sour-tasting plants: sheep's sorrel (Rumex acetosella), a small-leaved weed of poor acidic ground; French sorrel (Rumex scutatus), a milder, rounder-leaved species of European gardens; and, confusingly but commonly, wood sorrel (Oxalis acetosella), a creeping woodland plant of an entirely different family (Oxalidaceae) that shares the tartness but not the ancestry. In the passage, famished after the goblin ordeal, Bilbo "nibbled a bit of sorrel" and drank from a mountain-stream: the word evokes foraging and meagre wild fare, the sour leaf as the snack of a hungry traveller — welcome, yet thin and unsatisfying, and the sentence quietly measures how near starvation the party has come. The understatement is the point: after "nibbled", "a bit", and the blunt confession that follows — "but it was not much good" — sorrel stands for all that wild country offers a starving traveller: sharp, clean, sour, and utterly insufficient. In English country life sorrel has been eaten for centuries, its acidity lending bite to soups and sauces, and its leaves chewed raw by children; literary use commonly sets it among the edible greens of the wild — sorrel, cress, dandelion — where its tartness marks the boundary between wild food and cultivated sweetness. The nuance is rustic, astringent, and faintly bitter: nourishment with an edge. Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Rumex acetosa (common sorrel): The primary sense — a perennial European herb with arrow- or halberd-shaped leaves and slender spikes of greenish or red flowers, eaten raw in salads, cooked as a pot-herb, or puréed into sorrel soup and sauces.
  • Sheep's sorrel (Rumex acetosella): A smaller, sharper-tasting weed of poor, acid, sandy or heathy ground, often reddish, used sparingly as a wild herb and in folk medicine; the diminutive name marks it as a lesser kin of the common plant.
  • Wood sorrel (Oxalis acetosella): A quite unrelated creeping plant of the Oxalidaceae with clover-like trifoliate leaves and delicate white or pink flowers; it tastes even more sharply of oxalic acid and is the source of the older English "cuckoo's bread" and the shamrock-like emblem of some regions. Modern botanists keep it strictly separate, but the folk name has long conflated it with true sorrel.
  • French sorrel / buckler sorrel (Rumex scutatus): A cultivated, rounder-leaved species grown in kitchen gardens for its milder, more lemony flavour; a common ingredient in French soupe d'oseille and fish sauces.
  • The taste as a semantic anchor: Because the name itself means "the sour one", every transferred use of the word plays on acidity — in cookery the "sorrel flavour" is a standard descriptive term for sharp, lemony, oxalic tartness.
  • Botanical/chemical jargon: In chemistry and medicine, "sorrel" appears in compound names — "salt of sorrel" or "sorrel salt" is a traditional name for potassium hydrogen oxalate (salts of lemon), used for bleaching and stain removal, and "sorrel oxalate" poisoning is a recognised veterinary and paediatric hazard of grazing or foraging.
  • The horse-colour homograph (sorrel, adjective/noun): A distinct word, from Old French sorel, "reddish-brown", describing a reddish-brown horse coat; though identical in modern spelling it is not the plant-word and descends from a separate colour term — a cautionary case of homonymy.
  • Caribbean / tropical sense: In the Caribbean, "sorrel" (or "sorel") denotes the deep-red calyces of the roselle (Hibiscus sabdariffa), brewed as a festive Christmas drink; the transfer happened because the roselle's infusion is tart like the European plant.
  • Field and folk senses: Country usage applies "sorrel" to any sour dock growing in meadows and by waysides, and the word is used figuratively for anything acid or cutting in taste or temper ("sorrel wit").
Historical bridge: the semantic centre of the word has never moved — it is the sourness. The name was coined as a diminutive of "sour" (Old French surele, "little sour one") and has anchored every extension: to the plant group, to its chemical salt, to the unrelated but equally acid wood sorrel, to the tart roselle of the tropics, and even, by folk confusion, to the reddish-brown horse whose colour was felt to echo the plant's autumn hue. All senses radiate from one perceived quality, sharpness on the tongue.
