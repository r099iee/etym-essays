Sombre, American somber, is an adjective meaning dark, shadowy, gloomy or gravely serious, applied equally to colour, light, mood, manner and prospects. In the source sentence her sombre response is the tonal opposite of Bertie's buoyantly: where he bobs up, she shuts down - a short, grave, unsmiling refusal to be jollied, Tails up be dashed. The word thus does double duty as description of voice and judgement on his breeziness.

The core idea is shadow. A sombre colour is muted, dusky, absorbing light rather than reflecting it: sombre greys, browns, blacks and navy suits. A sombre place is dim and overcast. Transferred inward, a sombre mood is overcast too: depressed, pensive, without sparkle. Transferred socially, a sombre manner is solemn, restrained, refusing levity: a sombre announcement, a sombre warning, a sombre assessment. The same shadow covers palettes, faces and futures, which is why journalists write of sombre economic forecasts and mourners choose sombre clothes without changing word.

Grammatically it is gradable (more sombre, most sombre, sombrely, sombreness) and used attributively and predicatively: a sombre dress, the mood turned sombre. Collocations include sombre mood, sombre tone, sombre suit, sombre lighting, sombre news, sombre reflection, sombre memorial. Register is standard and slightly formal-literary in British English; the -re spelling itself signals Britishness. It is less severe than grim, less tearful than lugubrious, more dignified than gloomy, and more visual than grave.

  • Gravely serious in manner and tone (primary, contextual): Her sombre response means stern, unsmiling, weighty, cutting off banter. Bridge: from shadowed place to shadowed voice that admits no light-heartedness.
  • Gloomy and melancholy in mood (psychological sense): Feeling or looking depressed, downcast, joyless. Bridge: inner weather imagined as overcast sky, a standard metaphor from humours to modern mood.
  • Dark and muted in colour and light (visual sense): Dull, dusky, not bright or showy. Bridge: literal etymological sense of shaded, preserved in dress codes and interiors where sombre means respectful restraint.
  • Serious and ominous about prospects (extended sense): Sombre outlook, sombre statistics, sombre warning about debt or war. Bridge: future imagined as landscape under cloud; gravity plus gloom yields foreboding without panic.
  • Solemn and dignified on formal occasions (ceremonial nuance): Sombre ceremony, sombre remembrance, sombre suit at funerals. Bridge: cultural code where darkness equals respect, so shadow becomes decorum rather than depression.

To master sombre is to see one shadow covering three domains - eye, heart and judgement - and to hear in Aunt Dahlia's two-word shutdown the whole British contrast between buoyancy forced from below and gravity imposed from above. The word never shouts; it dims, and in dimming it tells Bertie his jauntiness is, for the moment, out of place.
