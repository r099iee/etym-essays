slung (verb, past tense and past participle of sling) — to have thrown, suspended, or arranged something with a loose, swinging motion, so that it hangs or travels from a point of support. The word covers two great families of action that share one physical idea: the swing. To sling a stone is to hurl it with a whirling rotation of the arm (or with the strap-weapon called a sling); to sling a belt over the shoulder is to toss it loosely so that it comes to rest hanging at the side. In the treasure-chamber passage, Lucy takes down her diamond bottle from its hook and “slung the belt over her shoulder and once more felt the bottle at her side where it used to hang in the old days.” The word captures a quick, practiced, almost habitual gesture — the way an archer, messenger, or warrior carries gear without thinking. She does not hang the belt carefully nor throw it carelessly; she slings it, in one fluid swing of the arm, so that the bottle settles exactly where it used to hang in the old days. The nuance is easeful muscle-memory: a swinging action that re-establishes, in a single gesture, her old physical intimacy with the Narnian past. Looseness is the meaning: slung things are supported at one point and free to swing below it, carried by the body rather than worn stiffly.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Primary verb sense (throw/hurl): to throw with a sweeping, swinging motion — “to sling a stone,” “slung a rock at the window”; the throwing weapon, the sling, is the name’s oldest reference, and the verb still carries that projectile force.
  • Casual placing sense: to put or arrange something quickly and carelessly — “he slung his coat over the chair,” “she slung her bag on the desk”; the throw is softened to a loose, indifferent placing.
  • Suspension and carrying sense: to cause to hang loosely from a support — a belt slung over the shoulder, a hammock slung between two trees, a rifle slung on the back, a bag slung across the chest; this is the sense in the Narnia passage.
  • Medical device sense: “an arm sling” — the bandage or support that suspends an injured limb from the neck or shoulder; the verb “slung” is used of putting the limb into it (“his arm was slung in a scarf”).
  • Baby-carrying sense: “a sling” — the cloth carrier in which a baby is carried against the body, hung from the parent’s shoulder; modern parenting jargon built directly on the suspension sense.
  • Idioms: “to sling mud” (to make abusive attacks), “to sling hash” (American slang for working as a short-order cook), “slings and arrows” (misfortunes, from Shakespeare’s Hamlet, “the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune”).
  • Slang and street sense: “slung low” — worn or carried hanging low (trousers slung low on the hips), or a loose, rolling way of moving; hip-hop and street fashion have made this sense current.
  • Figurative sense (hurled words): “slung insults,” “slung accusations” — to hurl words as one hurls a stone; the projectile idea transferred from matter to speech.
The family’s unity is the swing: in the throw, the arm and the projectile rotate; in the suspension, the object dangles and can rotate freely below its support. Both trace back through a Germanic root of winding and twisting (see etymologyS2), which is why the word’s oldest senses are about turning motion. The verb also shows the archaic strong-verb pattern of English (sling, slang, slung — later levelled to sling, slung, slung), the same ablaut rhythm as sing/sang/sung; “slung” functioning as both past tense and past participle is the modern, levelled form, and the sound of the word — short, low, nasal /slʌŋ/ — matches its swinging, dropping motion.

Slung draws its boundaries against its near-synonyms. Hung names the resulting state of suspension and is static; slung names the energetic act of getting there. Flung implies projection through the air with force or abandon; what is slung ends in an ordered, usable position. Tossed is lighter and more careless, with a hint of dismissal. Draped suggests fabric arranged in folds over a surface, too decorative for a heavy belt. Suspended is clinical and technological. Pitched and hurled are forceful and deliberate, lacking the looseness. Dangled describes a thing already hanging and swaying, with no act of placement. Only slung combines motion, looseness, and deliberate placement: the object travels loosely but lands exactly where the body needs it, and can be lifted away again in a moment.
