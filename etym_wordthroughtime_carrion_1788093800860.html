- (c. 1300) carion - “dead and decaying flesh,” a plain, grim word of battlefields, gallows, and scavengers, borrowed from Anglo-French.
- (14th–15th c.) Grows attributive: “carrion crow” names a real bird, and “carrion” becomes an adjective meaning “feeding on or like rot” — the age of the scavenger phrase.
- (16th–17th c.) Figurative poison: “carrion” as an insult for a loathsome person or thing — moral corruption figured as bodily rot in the abuse repertoire of the age.
- (18th–19th c.) Medical and legal prose: carrion is what corpses become; forensic observation strips the word of superstition and makes it a fact of natural history.
- (19th–20th c.) Ecology adopts it: carrion is a resource — the food of vultures, hyenas, and beetles — and “carrion birds” becomes a standard ornithological phrase.
- (Modern) Biology names carrion beetles, carrion flies, and the carrion flowers that mimic putrefaction; the word remains visceral and literary — seven centuries on, “carrion” still smells of death on the page.
