- 14th–15th c. — the stem gets its name: Middle English stalke names the stiff plant stem, a plain workaday noun of gardens and fields.
- 16th c. — the adjective is born: stalky appears for plants full of stalks or running to stem, the gardener's shorthand for growth gone coarse.
- 17th–18th c. — kitchen and still-room: cooks and herbalists use it for vegetables where rib outweighs leaf, a quiet complaint measured in trimming waste.
- 19th c. — botany and the allotment: seed catalogues and gardening manuals fix stalky as the standard fault-word for drawn seedlings and over-mature greens.
- 20th c. — Tolkien's weeds: the word does literary service in a catalogue of rank plants, proving that even humble adjectives can carry a scene.
- Today — sheds and kitchens: stalky stays exactly what it was, the unpoetic honest verdict on stems that outgrow their welcome.
