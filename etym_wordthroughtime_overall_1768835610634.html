- Middle English overal (adverb & adjective) - "over the whole extent, everywhere, altogether"; in its current compound form it first names spatial and measured totality.
- 14th–16th c. - it settles into the language of summary: "overall length", "overall impression"; the adjective becomes the bureaucratic tool for holding a whole in view.
- 1759 - the noun overalls appears in American usage for heavy bib-and-brace trousers; the word descends from abstraction to the workingman's cloth.
- 20th c. (British) - the singular overall names the one-piece working suit; as garment it becomes the humble, protective uniform of the labourer and the servant, unremarkable enough to merge with the dust it keeps away.
- 20th–21st c. - adjective and noun live in tension: in offices and statistics "overall" rules as the word of total assessment, while the garment keeps its custodial humility - the same word now spans the paper of management and the cloth of the caretaker.
