ECLECTIC (adjective): selecting or borrowing ideas, styles, or elements freely from a wide and varied range of sources rather than committing to any single system or school. In this passage the author uses the word of his own editorial method: because his examples merely illustrate general points about English poetic rhythm rather than establish a critical edition, he feels free to modernise spelling and punctuation wherever convenient and to defer questions of provenance and textual detail to the end of the book. The tone is quietly self-aware and gently apologetic — the word disarms the reader in advance, conceding that the book is not a rigorous textual edition while insisting that the flexibility is deliberate and principled rather than careless. The nuance is not randomness but principled selection: taking, from many editions, whatever best serves the argument.

The word descends from the Greek eklektikós, "selective", and it has a precise philosophical pedigree: in the second century AD a group of thinkers, refusing to bind themselves to a single master, picked out doctrines from Stoicism, Platonism, and Aristotelianism alike and came to be known as the Eclectics. Every modern use of the word is a distant echo of that school's method.

  • Core sense: "choosing from a variety of sources, styles, or schools" ("an eclectic taste in music", "an eclectic approach to editing").
  • Philosophy: "belonging to the Eclectics", the ancient school that combined doctrines from several systems; also applied to the nineteenth-century French eclectic school of Victor Cousin.
  • Art and architecture: "borrowing freely from different periods and styles" — an eclectic building may mix Gothic, Classical, and Oriental elements in a single facade.
  • Of taste and collections: "diverse and wide-ranging in a deliberate way" ("an eclectic playlist", "an eclectic library").
  • Of people: "a person who selects from many schools of thought" — the noun eclectic.
  • Derivatives: eclecticism (noun, "the practice of eclectic selection") and eclectically (adverb).

The semantic distinction that matters most is between eclecticism and syncretism: a syncretic method tries to fuse competing systems into a new coherent whole, while an eclectic one feels no need to reconcile its borrowings — it simply lifts them and sets them side by side. In register the word is approving when self-applied ("I am eclectic"), neutral-to-approving as description, and faintly reproachful in critics who see in it a lack of commitment or a refusal of depth. Its collocations cluster around taste, approach, method, collection, style, mixture, group, and reading.

In practical use eclectic is almost always approving: to call a person, a collection, or a method eclectic is to praise the breadth and independence of the taste that assembled it. The word works in both positive and mildly critical frames — a critic may complain that an "eclectic style" has no unifying principle, while the same style's defender will call it "eclectically rich". It names the middle position between narrow orthodoxy and random promiscuity: the eclectic chooser is guided by judgment, not by fashion, and takes from every source whatever serves the purpose. In this passage the word performs a double service: it excuses the author's freedom with spelling, punctuation, and attribution — he is not editing any one edition but selecting from all — and it quietly announces the principle of the selection, which is usefulness to the argument about rhythm rather than fidelity to a single textual authority.
