- Old English (pre-1100): slūma meant sleep itself, with an already slightly dreamy, poetic register.
- Middle English (13th–14th centuries): "slomeren" came to mean light, drowsy, dozing sleep — gentler than deep sleep, associated with ease and comfort.
- 16th–18th centuries: strongly poetic and literary; personified (the god Sleep/Slumber), used of sleeping nature, and adopted as a euphemism for death ("eternal slumber").
- 19th century: a staple of Romantic and Victorian poetry — slumber as soft, innocent, picturesque sleep.
- 20th century to today: still literary and poetic; "slumber party" (a children's sleepover) in American English; the "slumbering volcano" idiom for anything dormant that could wake — the word keeps both its warmth and its hint of latent power.
