- 14th to 15th Century - Enters Middle English via Old French *révérer* from Latin *reverērī*; primarily utilized in theological and monarchical contexts to denote the fearful obedience, pious awe, and solemn deference owed to Almighty God, holy mysteries, and the Crown.
- 16th to 17th Century - Expands during the English Renaissance and Reformation; writers such as William Shakespeare and John Milton apply *revere* beyond holy rites to classical philosophers, ancient laws, and venerable civic institutions, softening the connotation from dread-filled terror into dignified intellectual respect.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Solidified in moral philosophy and neoclassical letters; thinkers like David Hume and Samuel Johnson employ *revered* to honor statesmen, scholars, and moral exemplars whose lifelong virtue demands universal deference.
- 19th Century - Flourishes across Victorian biography, historical prose, and literary eulogies; the participial adjective *revered* becomes a standard epithet for founding fathers, literary titans (Shakespeare, Dante), and sacred national traditions.
- 20th to 21st Century - Widely utilized in cultural criticism and modern journalism to describe canonical artistic masterpieces, civil rights leaders (e.g., Nelson Mandela), and democratic norms; simultaneously, critics (like Terence Kilmartin on Scott Moncrieff's Proust) interrogate the danger of leaving revered translations and canons immune from necessary critical revision.
