Chain for JUTTED
1. Proto-Indo-European *h₁yeh₁- (older notation *yē-), 'to throw, to cast' — reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE (hypothetical, no direct attestation)
2. Greek ἵημι (híēmi), 'to send forth, to throw' — c. 8th c. BCE (Homeric; a cognate branch of the same root, not the direct ancestor of jut; the source of anesis via ἀνίημι, 'to let go')
3. Latin iacere, 'to throw, to hurl' — 3rd c. BCE (Plautus; from the same PIE root; the source of javelin via iaculum, 'a thrown thing')
4. Latin iactāre, 'to toss, to throw about' — 1st c. BCE (Cicero; intensive frequentative of iacere; the direct ancestor of the Romance verb)
5. Old French jeter, 'to throw, to cast' — 9th–12th c. CE (from Latin iactāre by regular sound change; also the source of English jet, jetty, jettison)
6. Middle English jetten, 'to stick out, to protrude' — 14th c. CE (borrowed from Old French jeter in the extended sense of projecting oneself / being thrown outward)
7. Early Modern English jut, 'to protrude, to thrust out' — c. 1560 (spelling variant of jet in the protruding sense)
8. Modern English jutted, past tense and past participle of jut — present day

Debated / nuanced points: (1) The semantic shift 'to throw' → 'to protrude' happened on French soil, in the pronominal se jeter ('to throw oneself') and the participle jeté — a thing thrown forward is a thing jutting out. (2) Whether English jut is purely a spelling-variant of jet or partly conflated with an obscure native verb (dialectal jut, jot, 'to push, to jolt,' of unknown origin) is debated; the OED notes both possibilities.
