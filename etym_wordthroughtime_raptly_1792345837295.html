- [Late 16th–Early 17th c. Elizabethan & Jacobean Era] — derived from the adjective *rapt* (borrowed from Latin *raptus* / Middle French *rapt*), originally used in theological, mystical, and dramatic verse to describe a soul literally torn out of the body in divine ecstasy or philosophical transport — [perception: regarded as a rare, exalted, and almost terrifying state of holy distraction]
- [Late 17th–18th c. Restoration & Enlightenment] — shifted into the refined spheres of opera, dramatic theater, and salon culture, characterizing cultivated listeners enchanted by virtuosic musical performances or exquisite poetry — [perception: associated with aristocratic taste, refined aesthetic sensibility, and polite appreciation]
- [19th c. Romantic & Victorian Era] — flourished across 19th-century fiction and nature poetry (Wordsworth, the Brontës, George Eliot), denoting the tender, spellbound absorption of lovers, children listening to folklore, or solitaries contemplating natural wonders — [perception: universally celebrated as an emblem of authentic interiority, emotional innocence, and poetic sensitivity]
- [20th c. Modern Era] — stabilized as a premier literary adverb across modern fiction, film criticism, and narrative journalism, predominantly qualifying verbs of watching and listening during transformative cultural or dramatic spectacles — [perception: regarded as a polished, sophisticated literary marker of unforced, spellbound fascination]
- [21st c. Contemporary Digital Era] — increasingly contrasted with the fragmented, scattered attention of the smartphone age; applied to rare moments of deep, sustained cognitive immersion, mindful flow, or captivating visual storytelling — [perception: valued as a precious, increasingly scarce mode of deep human attention and presence]
