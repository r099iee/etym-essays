The etymological lineage of clinging originates in the deep strata of Proto-Indo-European vocabulary relating to adhesiveness, moisture, and cohesion, demonstrating a captivating semantic transformation across Germanic and English linguistic history.

The ultimate root is reconstructed as the Proto-Indo-European base *gley- (with nasal extension *gley-n-), denoting "to stick, glue, smear, or agglomerate into a lump." This prolific root generated an extensive family of English words centered around tackiness, adhesion, and viscosity: it yielded English clay (from Old English clǣg, the sticky earth), glue (via Old French from Latin gluten), cleave ("to adhere," from Old English clifian), and climb (from Old English climban, literally to ascend by sticking and grasping). Across other Indo-European branches, it yielded Ancient Greek glía ("glue"), Latin gluten ("glue"), and Slavic *glina ("clay").

In Proto-Germanic, the nasalized root produced the Class III strong verb *klinganą ("to stick together, contract, draw tight, shrink"), where the initial Indo-European voiced velar stop *g shifted to voiceless *k in accordance with Grimm's Law. This strong verbal paradigm exhibited classic Germanic vowel gradation (ablaut): present *klinganą, past singular *klang, past plural *klungum, past participle *klunganaz. This historical ablaut series survives directly in Modern English irregular principal parts: cling, clang (archaic/dialectal), clung.

In Old English, the verb was attested as clingan. Remarkably, its primary Anglo-Saxon senses focused on physical shrinkage, curdling, withering, and coagulation caused by severe cold or dehydration. In Old English medical and homiletic texts, a paralyzed limb, frostbitten skin, or withered corpse was said to clingan (to dry up and draw tight). However, the underlying sense of "drawing together into tight cohesion" held the latent seed for external attachment: when two surfaces drew together with equal force, they adhered firmly.

During the Middle English period (c. 1150–1500), the verb clingen underwent a decisive semantic shift away from passive withering toward active, sustained adhesion and grasping onto external supports. Concurrently, the present participle underwent major morphological realignment: the Old English participial suffix -ende (e.g., clingende) evolved through Middle English dialectal variants (clingand in the north, clinginde in the midlands) before merging fully with the verbal noun suffix -ung/-ing, yielding the modern participial form clinging.

By the Early Modern English period (the era of William Shakespeare and the King James Bible), clinging was thoroughly established in both tactile and figurative domains. Shakespeare frequently employed the image of clinging vines, shipwrecked mariners, and lovers embraced in desperate devotion. Throughout the 19th and 20th centuries, as psychological vocabulary expanded, clinging was adopted to describe interpersonal dependence, fear of abandonment, and dogmatic conservatism, completing its evolution from physical coagulation to complex psychological diagnosis.
