- Old English — every feathered thing: fugol covers wren and eagle, sparrow and supper indifferently.
- Middle Ages — pulpit and pot: preachers praise the fowls of heaven while cooks roast fowl for feasts; the sacred and the edible share one word.
- 1611 — the Bible fixes it: the fowls of the air in the King James translation lifts the word into poetry for ever.
- 1700s–1800s — the great narrowing: bird conquers the wild kingdom, and fowl retreats to the coop, the menu, and the marsh.
- Victorian — menu and sport: boiled fowl on the table, wildfowl on the estuary, each a specialised survival of the old generality.
- Today — the farmyard remnant: keepers keep fowls, poets borrow them, and shoppers buy poultry — the same creatures under three flags.
