Piping is the present participle and verbal noun of the verb to pipe, and its meanings fan out from two ancient images: the musical pipe and the hollow tube. In the source context (“whistling, crying and piping”) the word denotes the production of a high, thin, reedy sound — the kind of sound a small pipe makes and, by extension, the shrill, penetrating calls of birds or the thready voices of small creatures. This is the oldest strand of meaning, still alive in birding and literary prose (“a piping call”, “piping notes”).

From the same stem, however, the word has grown a remarkably wide set of secondary senses, all of them current in modern English and all of them traceable to the shape or the sound of a pipe:

  • Musical sense: piping is the act of playing a pipe or the music so produced; historically the word also meant “the shrill sound of a pipe” and, as an adjective, “high-pitched, thin, reedy” (a piping voice). The semantic bridge is direct: the sound of the instrument named the sound itself.
  • Conduit sense: from the 19th century piping has been the standard noun for a system of pipes — water piping, gas piping, central-heating piping — i.e. the network of tubes and their installation. The bridge: a pipe is a hollow tube; a collection of tubes is piping.
  • Sewing and upholstery sense: piping is a narrow tube or cord of fabric, often with cord inside, used to trim the edges of cushions, garments and upholstery. The bridge: the trimming is literally a little tube of cloth, shaped like a pipe.
  • Culinary sense: piping is the technique of forcing icing, cream, purée or pastry through a piping bag fitted with a nozzle, to produce decorative shapes or borders. The bridge: the bag is a tube; the action is forcing material through a tube, exactly as water is piped.
  • “Piping hot”: food or liquid that is piping hot is hissing, sizzling or steaming so fiercely that it seems to whistle like a pipe. The idiom, recorded from the 1560s, bridges the sound sense to an intensifier of heat.
  • Nautical, tobacco and computing senses: the verb to pipe has long meant “to convey by means of pipes” and “to signal on a whistle or pipe” (to pipe all hands aboard; to pipe down); the tobacco pipe (16th century) is the same tube-word; and in 20th-century computing, to pipe is to direct the output of one program into the input of another, so that piping is now a piece of everyday programmer jargon.

Grammatically, piping behaves both as a verb form (the -ing form of pipe, used with auxiliaries and in progressive constructions: “the birds were piping”) and as a noun (countable: “a piping of icing”; uncountable: “the piping under the sink”), and as an adjective (“piping notes”, “a piping voice”). Shakespeare exploited the pastoral register of the word in Richard III (c. 1592), where the hunchback king scorns “this weak piping time of peace” — a peace so quiet that only the shepherd’s pipe is heard; the phrase shows how thoroughly the sound-sense had absorbed connotations of fragility, thinness and pastoral innocence.

In register, the sound senses are now literary, poetic or technical (ornithology), while the conduit, culinary and computing senses are entirely everyday. The word therefore offers a compact demonstration of how a single stem has radiated through music, plumbing, fashion, gastronomy and software without losing the trace of its original image: a pipe — an instrument that is also a tube.
