The Aztecs (singular Aztec; Spanish azteca(s); Nahuatl aztecatl, plural azteca’) were the Nahuatl-speaking peoples of the Basin of Mexico who, through the Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan (1428–1521), built the last great Mesoamerican empire — some 5–6 million subjects, tribute provinces from coast to coast, a capital of perhaps 200,000–300,000 people on Lake Texcoco, and a civilization famed for chinampa agriculture, monumental temples, featherwork, poetry, law, and human sacrifice. In the source sentence “the Aztecs knew no other tool of tillage than the hoe” the word denotes these imperial Nahuas as observed by Spaniards, whose hoe-and-digging-stick horticulture contrasts with Old World plough agriculture.

Strictly, historians distinguish Aztec (the 19th-century English umbrella, from Humboldt and Prescott) from emic terms: Mexica (the dominant Tenochtitlan people), Tenochca, Tlatelolca, Acolhua, Tepaneca, and the wider Nahua (all Nahuatl speakers). Popular English collapses all into “Aztecs,” while scholars often prefer “Mexica” or “Nahua” for precision and to honour descendants. The empire fell to Cortés, his Tlaxcalan allies, and smallpox in 1519–1521; survivors became colonial Nahuas, whose language (1.5+ million speakers today), cuisine (maize, beans, chili, chocolate, tomato), calendar, and place-names (Mexico, Chapultepec, Popocatépetl) endure.

Extended senses abound. “Aztec” is an adjective (“Aztec gold,” “Aztec calendar stone”), a language-family shorthand (sometimes confused with Uto-Aztecan), a sports mascot (San Diego State Aztecs), and a design style (Aztec prints, Art Deco). In Mexican nationalism — flag eagle-on-cactus from the Aztlan migration legend, mexicanidad, Chicano “Aztlán” — Aztec heritage is foundational myth. In gaming and fantasy “Aztec” evokes stepped pyramids and sacrifice. Technologically the hoe-only image in the source needs nuance: Aztec chinampas, terracing, irrigation, and digging stick (coa, huictli) formed one of the world’s most productive pre-industrial agricultures, feeding a metropolis larger than contemporary London or Seville without draft animals or iron.

  • The imperial people (noun, plural, primary): inhabitants of the Triple Alliance domain, especially Tenochtitlan. “The Aztecs built Tenochtitlan.” The source sense.
  • The Mexica proper (noun, narrower): the Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco ethnic group. “Cortés captured the Mexica ruler Cuauhtemoc.” Bridge: whole-for-part where Aztec stands for Mexica.
  • Nahua culture generally (noun, broad): all Nahuatl-speaking towns, allied or subject. “Aztec poetry.” Shares language and cosmology; differs in political membership.
  • The empire / civilization (noun, abstract): “the fall of the Aztecs.” Place-people-polity metonymy: people → state → era (1428–1521).
  • Descendant Nahuas (noun, contemporary): “modern Aztecs.” Used journalistically for Nahua communities; scholars prefer Nahua to avoid erasing continuity and change.
  • Adjectival / stylistic (modifier): “Aztec diamond (baseball play), Aztec print, Aztec architecture.” Evokes stepped, geometric, “exotic” prestige detached from history.
  • Mythic homeland reference (proper noun, Aztlan): “people from Aztlan.” Original Nahuatl aztecatl = “one from Aztlan”; English Aztec preserves that migration memory.

Grammar: countable (“Aztecs”), capitalized, pronounced /ˈæztɛks/. Collocations include “Aztec empire,” “Aztec capital,” “Aztec sacrifice,” and “Aztec agriculture.” Register is neutral-popular in English, though scholars debate whether the umbrella erases diversity; in Mexico mexica and nahua carry decolonial weight. To say the Aztecs “knew no hoe but the hoe” is true only of ploughlessness: their coa digging stick, coatequitl labour teams, and chinampa mud-beds achieved yields that astonished Spaniards, a sophistication the older evolutionist framing of the source passage understates.
