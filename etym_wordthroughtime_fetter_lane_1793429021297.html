- Late 13th c. — Track of the idle beggars: First recorded in municipal rolls as Faitereslane (from Old French faitour, ‘charlatan, idle beggar’), describing a gravel road where vagabonds lounged on the outskirts of the City leading toward the Holborn gardens.
- 14th–15th c. — Folk-etymological corruption: The name gradually corrupts through Fewter Lane into Fetter Lane, assisted by popular folk-etymological associations with iron fetters and proximity to prisoners or hound-keepers (fewterers).
- 16th c. — Antiquarian debate: John Stow publishes his Survey of London (1598), documenting the street’s evolution from an unpaved lane of beggars into a respectable street bordered by Clifford’s Inn and substantial timber-framed residences.
- 17th–18th c. — Hub of printing, law, and Dissent: The lane becomes a bustling center of scriveners, printers, and nonconformist societies; Jonathan Swift uses it in Gulliver’s Travels as Gulliver’s modest transitional home; John Wesley gathers at the Fetter Lane Society.
- 19th c. — Industrial publishing and engineering: Neville’s Court and surrounding alleys fill with heavy steam-driven printing presses, lithographers, and legal publishers serving the nearby Royal Courts of Justice.
- Modern day — Corporate law and chambers: Completely rebuilt following heavy damage in the Blitz of 1941, it now serves as a high-density corridor of prestigious commercial law firms, barristers’ chambers, and modern office complexes.
