The verb rouse is one of the most interesting "orphan" words in English: it is first attested in the early fifteenth century in a very narrow, technical setting, and its origins have never been established to everyone's satisfaction. The earliest English record of rousen is from falconry, where a hawk "rouses" — that is, shakes or ruffles its feathers, usually after feeding or bathing. From this small physical image of sudden shaking and fluffing, the word spread in the sixteenth century into the language of hunting and war, and then into general use, becoming one of English's standard verbs for waking and exciting.

Two main origin theories compete. The traditional dictionary account derives rouse from Anglo-Norman and Old French reuser (also rehuser), "to drive back, repulse, drive away", which itself comes from Latin recūsāre, "to refuse, decline, raise an objection". On this theory the French sense "to drive (game) back" passed into English hunting and from there to falconry, where "to rouse" a hawk meant to make it shake itself — and then the sense widened again to all stirring and waking. The competing theory treats rouse as of unknown or imitative origin, perhaps Scandinavian, with the falconry sense as the original and nothing reliably behind it. Because the earliest English evidence is the falconry verb, and because the leap from "drive back" to "shake feathers" is awkward, many modern philologists hesitate over the Latin derivation; the OED therefore labels the word's etymology as uncertain or disputed.

  • Late 14th–15th c. (falconry): rousen means "to shake the feathers (of a hawk)" — the physical, technical starting point.
  • 16th century (hunting and the chase): "to rouse" game = to start or flush it from cover; the word enters the language of sport, the hunt, and the field.
  • 16th–17th century (general waking): the sense widens to waking sleepers, stirring idlers, and shaking people from rest — "rouse" becomes the standard verb of abrupt awakening.
  • 17th–19th century (figurative emotion): the participle roused is applied to passions — anger, courage, jealousy, patriotism — and to crowds and armies; roused becomes a fixture of heroic, romantic, and political prose.
  • 20th century to present: roused is retained as a literary-formal word, joined by the adjective rousing; the purely sexual sense has largely migrated to the prefixed aroused, while roused keeps the wakeful, energetic, emotional core.

Semantic and formal detail: The spread of the word tracks a widening of context — from the mews of the falconer to the forest, the battlefield, the bedroom, and the public square. The prefixed form arouse (with the intensive prefix a-) appeared in the eighteenth century and gradually claimed the senses of "awaken" and "excite (especially sexually)", while plain rouse stayed closer to "wake, stir up, startle". Both descend from the same contested stock, and the two words have shared the family's fortunes ever since. In the participle Roused, as it appears in Tolkien's The Hobbit, we see the classic mid-history usage: words strike the dwarves like a shock, rousing them from despairing stillness into hasty, reckless movement — precisely the kind of sudden stirring the verb has meant since the sixteenth century.
