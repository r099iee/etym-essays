PIE *steh₂- 'to stand, set, be firm' (reconstructed; one of the best-attested roots of the family, with cognates such as Sanskrit tiṣṭhati 'stands,' Greek histēmi 'I set, stand,' and Latin stāre 'to stand') → Latin stāre 'to stand, stand firm' (attested from early Latin, 3rd–2nd century BCE) → Latin obstāre 'to stand before, in the way of; to oppose, withstand' (ob- 'against, in front' + stāre; classical, attested 1st century BCE) — the source formation that gives obstināre its sense of standing against → Latin obstināre 'to persist, be resolute, set oneself firmly' (analyzed as ob- 'against' + stināre, a 'stand-firm' verbal formation related to stāre and to dēstināre 'to fix, determine'; the nasal -in- is the point of debate — see note below; attested in classical Latin, 1st century BCE/CE) → Latin obstinātus 'resolved, firm, stubborn' (past participle, classical) → English obstinate 'stubbornly adhering to an opinion or course' (borrowed in the 14th century, c. 1385, via Old French obstiné and directly from Latin obstinātus) → English obstinately 'in an obstinate manner' (obstinate + adverbial suffix -ly; attested from the late 15th / 16th century CE) → OBSTINATELY — the card's target form: the manner adverb of obstinate. NOTE on the debate: two analyses of obstināre coexist. The standard account (cf. OED) sees ob- + a verb *stināre (a nasalized relative of stāre, the same base as dēstināre and cōnstāre), giving 'to set oneself against'; a minority view derives obstināre directly from obstāre with re-formation. Either way the image is the same — standing firm against opposition — and the 'stand' root *steh₂- is beyond dispute. Related English words on sister branches of the same root include destiny/destination (dēstināre), status, station, stance, and institute (statuere), none of which is a parent of obstinate.
