Derivation chain for stork (plural storks): 1. PIE *ster- 'stiff, rigid, hard' (well attested: Greek stereos 'stiff, solid, hard', Lithuanian staryti 'to stiffen'; the root also underlies English stern, starch, and stare through various routes). 2. Proto-Germanic *sturkaz 'stork' (reconstructed; attested by Old English storc, Old Norse storkr, Middle High German storc, Old High German storah; the formation is a -ka- derivative of an adjective stem meaning 'stiff, rigid', i.e. 'the stiff-legged one', from the bird's stiff, deliberate walking gait). 3. Old English storc (8th-11th c.) 'stork' - attested in glossaries and the Old English corpus; a plain, homely bird-name in use for over a thousand years, with no folklore attached yet. 4. Middle English stork (12th-15th c.) 'stork' - unchanged in form and sense; a familiar rural word for the large wading bird that nested on roofs and chimneys. 5. Modern English stork, plural storks (16th c. onward) - the plural formed by the regular English noun plural suffix -s; the word is one of the very few English bird-names inherited unchanged from Old English into the modern language. Note on the debate: the PIE root *ster- is widely accepted, but some scholars instead connect the bird-name to a root meaning 'to be visible, appear' or see a purely Germanic onomatopoeia; the 'stiff-legged one' etymology (cognate with Greek stereos 'stiff, solid') remains the mainstream view. The -k- suffix formation is paralleled in other Germanic bird-names but not directly attested in the parent language.
