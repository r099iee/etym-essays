The adjective is a French formation on chronologie, which French took from Neo-Latin chronologia in the sixteenth century. The Latin noun is a Renaissance reconstruction from Greek χρονολογία (khronología), a compound of χρόνος (khrónos) "time" and -λογία (-logía), "account, discourse, study", from λόγος (lógos). The Greek noun is not classical: it belongs to the technical prose of the Imperial period, where it names the reckoning and listing of dates.

The problem of khrónos

Greek khrónos is attested from Homer onward, meaning "time", "a span of time" and also "delay". Its origin, however, is genuinely uncertain. Beekes (2010) gives no Indo-European etymology and inclines to treat it as a Pre-Greek word; older handbooks, following Pokorny, proposed a connection with the root *ǵerh₂- "to grow old" (source of Greek γέρας, géras, "honour, prize" and γέρων, gérōn, "old man"), but the consonants do not correspond regularly and the semantic link is loose. The prudent statement is that khrónos has no accepted Indo-European derivation. What is certain is the Greek word's cultural career: Time was personified as Khrónos in later antiquity, and Renaissance writers identified him with the Titan Kronos (Roman Saturn), a conflation that is etymologically false but iconographically permanent — hence the scythe-bearing Father Time.

Khronología and the science of dates

  • The Greek compound names the activity of setting events in a time scheme, not the scheme itself; it is the ancestor of chronicle-writing as much as of modern chronology.
  • Early Christian scholars made the discipline essential: Eusebius of Caesarea's Chronicle (fourth century) tabulated the kingdoms of the world in parallel columns so that sacred and profane history could be synchronised.
  • The Renaissance inherited that project. Joseph Scaliger's De emendatione temporum (1583) and Thesaurus temporum (1606) founded modern chronology, and the Neo-Latin vocabulary of the field — chronologia, chronographus, synchronismus — passed into the vernaculars.
  • The word's prestige in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was enormous: to establish a chronology was to establish the frame of universal history.

French developments

  • French chronologie is recorded from the sixteenth century; the adjective chronologique follows in the seventeenth, formed with the productive suffix -ique (Latin -icus, Greek -ικός).
  • Because adjectives in -ique do not inflect for gender, chronologique has one form throughout the singular — a small point of grammar that English readers of a French title often misread as a masculine-only form.
  • In the eighteenth century the adjective moved from the discipline to the arrangement of books: a table chronologique, a abrégé chronologique promised the reader that dates, not arguments, governed the pages.

English and modern usage

  • English borrowed chronology in the late sixteenth century and formed chronological in the seventeenth, alongside the rarer chronologic.
  • The adjective's modern life is dominated by two phrases: chronological order, the default arrangement of narrative and archive, and chronological age, the calendar measure contrasted with biological or developmental age.
  • Chronological snobbery, popularised by C. S. Lewis and credited by him to Owen Barfield, turns the word into a term of cultural criticism: the assumption that an idea is superseded because it is old.
  • In digital culture the French fil chronologique and the English "chronological feed" have given the adjective a new everyday prominence, naming a sort order rather than a scholarship.

Why the title needs the word

Hénault's Abrégé chronologique is not merely a short history; it is a history whose spine is the calendar. The adjective tells the reader that the book can be consulted by year, that the anecdote of 1744 will be found under 1744, and that the whole is a continuous record rather than a set of essays. In a century that prized portable knowledge, "chronological" was a promise of usability — and the passage on this card, with its precise dating of the king's illness, is a small proof that the promise was kept.
