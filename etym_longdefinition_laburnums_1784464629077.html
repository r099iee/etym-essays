LABURNUMS is the plural of laburnum, the standard English name for a small genus of ornamental trees in the pea-and-bean family Fabaceae (subfamily Faboideae). Botanically, the genus Laburnum contains just two principal wild species — Laburnum anagyroides, the common laburnum or golden chain tree, native to central and southern Europe, and Laburnum alpinum, the Scotch or Scottish laburnum, native to mountain regions of the Alps and Pyrenees — together with garden hybrids, most notably the long-flowered cultivar Laburnum x watereri 'Vossii' and the famous graft-chimera x Laburnocytisus adamii (Adam's laburnum), which carries both yellow laburnum flowers and purple broom flowers on the same plant. The defining feature, and the reason for the folk-names golden chain and golden rain, is the display of pendulous racemes: in late May and June each branch bears long, drooping chains of densely packed, bright yellow, pea-shaped ("papilionaceous") flowers, so numerous that the whole tree appears to pour down gold. The leaves are trifoliate (three leaflets), the bark is smooth and greenish-brown, and the fruit is a slender pod containing several black or dark-brown seeds. Every part of the plant, but especially the seeds and pods, is highly poisonous because of the quinolizidine alkaloid cytisine, which makes the tree a notable hazard, particularly to children, even while it remains a beloved spring ornament.

Primary sense — the botanical tree-name and its plural: The singular laburnum is the tree-name proper ("a laburnum", "the laburnum in bloom"); the plural laburnums is the ordinary collective plural used when several trees are spoken of together ("the street was lined with laburnums", "all parts of the laburnums are toxic"), and it is in this plural, collective form that the word appears in Bilbo's firework reminiscence in Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937). Register: the word is botanical and slightly formal at its root — it is a Latin scientific name pressed into everyday service — but by long use in English gardens it has become a familiar, warm, domestic word of spring.

Secondary sense — the wood and the craftsman's material: Laburnum yields a hard, dense, heavy wood, dark chocolate-brown in the heartwood with a pale sapwood ring, that has long been prized for turning, small cabinet work, veneer, mallet heads, knife handles, billiard-cue butts, and decorative inlay; in the 18th and 19th centuries it was sometimes used as a substitute for ebony. In this material sense "laburnum" names the timber as well as the tree.

Secondary sense — toxicity and the herbal-poison register: Because of its cytisine content, laburnum is a stock example of a common poisonous garden plant; the toxic sense colours the word in gardening manuals, medical and poison-control texts, and folk lore, where the sweet taste of the pods makes the danger sharper. This toxic register coexists oddly but stably with the tree's festive, golden-chain beauty.

Secondary sense — horticulture and the garden industry: In nursery and gardening usage the word extends to cultivars and planting practices: laburnums trained against walls, grown as standards, or arched into pergola tunnels (the famous "laburnum walk"), and hybrid forms chosen for longer racemes. It is in this sense that laburnums are a standard, even clichéd, fixture of the English spring garden and of the Victorian shrubbery.

Secondary sense — toponymy and street names: So fashionable did the tree become in the 19th century that its name passed into English place-naming — Laburnum Grove, Laburnum Road, Laburnum Villas, and houses called "Laburnum Cottage" — so that "laburnum" is today also a domestic place-name element redolent of suburban and village life. This sense is a genuine modern extension of the word, socially mediated by garden fashion.

Metaphoric and literary sense — "laburnums of fire": The tree's drooping yellow chains are so visually like hanging streams or falling sparks that the image transfers readily to fire. In The Hobbit (1937) Tolkien writes of Old Took's Midsummer's Eve fireworks that they "used to go up like great lilies and snapdragons and laburnums of fire and hang in the twilight all evening". The semantic bridge is the golden rain: the folk-name golden rain already likens the flowers to falling precipitation, and the hanging, glowing chains are equally read as hanging firework trails — so "laburnums of fire" names not real trees but firework shapes that copy the tree's cascading yellow mass. This is the word's most famous literary extension and the sense active in the card's context.

  • Sense 2 — plural collective: several such trees; the ordinary plural form.
  • Sense 3 — the wood: hard dark timber used in turnery and cabinet work.
  • Sense 4 — the poison: cytisine-laden seeds and pods; the toxicological sense.
  • Sense 5 — horticulture: cultivars, standards, pergola and arbour plantings.
  • Sense 6 — place-names: Laburnum Grove, Laburnum Road, "Laburnum Cottage".
