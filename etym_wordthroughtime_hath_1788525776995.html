- [pre-9th–11th c.] - Old English hæfþ / hafaþ serves as the universal southern third-person singular present of habban - [connotation: standard West Saxon grammatical form in royal laws and religious texts]
- [12th–14th c.] - Middle English contracts the form into hath in London and Midland dialects, while Northern Middle English develops has - [connotation: standard literary and courtly form in southern England, seen in Chaucer]
- [16th–17th c.] - Early Modern English enshrines hath as the supreme literary, legal, and liturgical standard in the King James Bible (1611) and Shakespeare - [connotation: solemn authority, formal elegance, and sacred majesty]
- [late 17th–18th c.] - Spoken English and secular writing rapidly adopt Northern has; hath retreats into the pulpit, poetry, and high liturgy - [connotation: marked as archaic, ecclesiastical, and elevated]
- [19th–21st c.] - Retained in established proverbs ("music hath charms"), legal archaisms, and deliberate aesthetic/fairy-tale prose (Oscar Wilde) - [connotation: sacred biblical gravity, poetic timelessness, and archaic grandeur]
