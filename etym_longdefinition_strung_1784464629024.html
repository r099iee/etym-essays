Strung is the past tense and past participle of the verb string — to thread objects on a cord or wire, to fit something with a string (a bow, a racket, a musical instrument), or to stretch a cord tight. As a verb form, strung reports a completed action of threading, linking, or tensioning: pearls that have been put on a silk thread are strung; a bow whose sinew has been drawn tight is strung; stars imagined as gems are strung on silver necklaces. It is a strong (irregular) past form that has entirely displaced the older regular form stringed in ordinary transitive use, and it carries a subtle image of tension, sequence, and line that its synonyms rarely share. In the dwarves' song — “On silver necklaces they strung / The flowering stars” — the verb performs a precise piece of mythic craftsmanship. The ancient makers are not piling treasure; they are threading celestial light itself, gem by gem, onto silver chains. Each star is a bead; each necklace is a line of gathered light; the past tense marks the work as already accomplished in the legendary past. The image depends entirely on the linear, sequential sense of string: one thing after another along a cord, the whole running through the whole. Tone: delicate-epic, luminous, artisanal; the word is the vocabulary of necklace-making raised to the scale of a cosmogony. Primary senses of strung / string (verb)
  • To thread (beads, pearls, gems, lights) on a string, wire, or thread — “to string pearls”; “stars strung on silver”; “lanterns strung across the street.”
  • To furnish or fit with a string or strings — “a strung bow”; “a strung racket”; “a strung harp or violin.”
  • To stretch or draw (a cord, a wire, a line) taut between points — “wires strung between poles”; “a clothesline strung across the yard.”
  • By extension, to arrange in a sequence or series — “words strung together in a sentence”; “a string of successes.”
Polysemy and secondary modern meanings
  • Highly strung (idiom): tense, nervous, easily agitated — literally “drawn too tight like an over-tuned string”; the transfer runs from instrument strings under high tension to human nerves.
  • Strung out (slang): exhausted, frazzled, or intoxicated by drugs; originally of addicts whose nerves were frayed as if by an over-stretched cord; now broadened to burnout, fatigue, or desperation.
  • Strung together: loosely linked or improvised — “a few chords strung together” — the sequence sense stripped of craft.
  • String theory (physics): a framework in which fundamental particles are one-dimensional vibrating strings; the name borrows the tension and vibration image, though the string is mathematical, not cordage.
  • String instruments / stringed instruments: the noun and adjective worlds of the musical sense; note that the musical adjective keeps the older form stringed, while the verb's past has become strung.
  • Gaming and computing: “strings” of characters; “stringing along” someone (deceiving or delaying); both are modern extensions of the sequence image.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge of the senses The whole polysemy hangs from the Old English noun streng, “a cord, a rope,” and the verb built from it. The oldest image is a cord: something thin, flexible, and under tension. Threading beads (one thing after another along the cord), stringing a bow (stretching the cord taut), and stringing an instrument (fitting cords to sound) are three uses of the same line-and-tension image. From “tension” came the human psychology of highly strung and strung out; from “sequence along a line” came strings of words and string theory. Every modern sense is a different use of the cord: threading it, tensioning it, or following it point to point. How strung differs from its near-synonyms
  • Threaded — the general act of passing something through an eye or along a line; stresses the passage of the thread, where strung stresses the line that is being loaded and its tension.
  • Hung — suspension from above by gravity; a thing hangs from one point, but stringing lays items sequentially along a cord between two points.
  • Suspended — hanging free, usually from above; no sequential threading, no cord-loading image.
  • Set — fixing stones in metal mounts (jewellery) or placing things in position; no line, no cord, no sequence.
  • Slung — looped or thrown loosely over a shoulder or support; rougher and looser than the neat threading of strung.
  • Beaded — decorated with beads; the result, not the act of threading them on a line.
  • Laced — threaded through eyelets or holes, usually to fasten (shoes, bodices); tighter and more systematic than stringing.
In the card's context The line keeps the exact strong form STRUNG, not the base string and not the regular stringed: the past participle is chosen for its completed, legendary quality and for the metre of the stanza. The necklace image is the oldest recorded use of the verb — threading gems on a cord — and the song exploits that primacy by making stars themselves the beads. Keep string (the base verb, the cord, the sequence) in mind, and every modern sense from tennis rackets to nerve tension follows from the same ancient cord.
