Bleary describes eyes, looks, and mornings dimmed with sleep, tiredness, drink, or tears: unfocused, watery, and slow to see the world sharp.

In the source sentence Sam jumps up looking rather bleary, and the picture is instantly familiar: a hobbit hauled out of sleep, eyes gummed and half-lidded, gaze swimming before it settles, the whole face stamped with the pillow's crease and the mind still wading up from dreams. The understatement of rather is pure English comedy, softening a wreck of a morning into a gentleman's adjective.

Core meaning in detail: The bleary eye does not focus: its lids are heavy, its surface films with sleep or tears, its owner blinks against light that seems too loud. From the eye the word spreads to the look, the face, and then to the whole scene viewed through such eyes, so that mornings, bars at closing time, hospital corridors, and motorway services at dawn can all be bleary. It is tiredness made visible, the opposite of bright-eyed, and it asks for comedy or sympathy rather than alarm.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Bleary-eyed from sleep (primary sense): the morning face, puffy-lidded and blinking, mind still surfacing. The bridge is the adjective's home ground: bleary first and always means just-woken, the eye filmed with sleep.
  • Bleary with tiredness and long wakefulness: the night-shift nurse, the long-haul driver, the student at 4 a.m. The bridge extends sleep to its lack: exhaustion films the eye exactly as sleep does, and the word follows the film.
  • Bleary with drink: the closing-time stare, bright with sentiment and short on focus. The bridge runs through alcohol's watering, reddening effect on eyes, so that the drunk and the sleepless share one adjective and often one bench.
  • Bleary with tears or emotion: eyes brimming after grief, laughter, or yawning fits. The bridge is water: any filming of the eye, whatever its cause, reads as bleary, which is why farewells and funny stories both leave us bleary.
  • Bleary images and weather (transferred sense): a bleary photograph, a bleary dawn through rain. The bridge reverses the lens: the scene itself is described as the bleary eye sees it, unfocused and washed, so the adjective migrates from viewer to view.
  • Bleary with illness or allergy (medical): eyes filmed by colds, hay fever, or conjunctivitis, blinking against daylight. The bridge is symptom overlap: infection waters and reddens the eye exactly as sleeplessness does, so nurses and novelists reach for the same kind word for the sufferer.

Register and connotation: bleary is informal, affectionate, and faintly comic — nobody is grandly bleary, and nobody bleary is quite dignified, which is why the word disarms. To call Sam rather bleary is to love him a little: the loyal gardener caught napping on watch. Headlines use it for stranded passengers and ministers after all-night sittings, novelists for witnesses hauled from bed, and friends for each other over the first coffee. It never blames; it notices, sympathises, and hands over the coffee.
