Hastings looks like a battle and behaves like a place-name, which is exactly what it is. The word is a fossil of Anglo-Saxon social organisation: a group of people named after an ancestor, settled on the Sussex coast, fortified in the ninth century, and then made famous by an event that happened several miles away. Its history is a useful corrective to the idea that place-names are stable labels; Hastings has meant a folk-group, a burh, a port, a battlefield, a resort and a surname, and it has done so in that order.

The -ingas type

Old English place-names in -ingas are among the oldest layers of English toponymy. The suffix descends from Proto-Germanic *-ingōz and marks a group of people: Hæstingas is “the Hæsta-people,” just as Readingas is “the Reada-people” and Berecingas “the Bereca-people.” Such names usually preserve the name of a real or legendary founder and imply a folk-group that had already formed before the name was written down. They are, in effect, the earliest surviving census of Anglo-Saxon England.

The personal name Hæsta is the crux. It is not attested on its own, and etymologists have proposed several explanations. The most attractive connects it with Old English hæst “strife, violence” (cognate with German Hast “haste, passion” and ultimately with Proto-Germanic *haifstiz), which would make it a warrior byname: “the violent one.” Other proposals treat it as a shortened compound or as a pre-English survival. None can be proved, and the honest position is that the first element is obscure.

From folk-group to burh

By the ninth century the Hæstingas had a fortified centre, recorded in the Burghal Hidage as Hæstingaceaster — the ceaster being an old Roman or Roman-style fort. The name shows the normal development from a people to a place: the group name is transferred to the settlement, and the settlement acquires a defensive function in the Alfredian system of burhs. Hastings was thus a royal stronghold before it was a town.

The Cinque Port

After the Norman Conquest, Hastings became one of the Cinque Ports, the five head ports of the south-east coast that supplied ships and men to the crown in return for extensive privileges. The confederation was at its height in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when Hastings, Rye and Winchelsea were busy harbours. The name in this period means wine, wool, fishing and royal service — not battle.

The harbour, however, was doomed. The silting of the Brede and the eastward drift of shingle cut the town off from the sea, and the old port decayed. French raids in 1339 and 1377 destroyed much of the town, and by the sixteenth century Hastings was a small fishing settlement living beside the ruins of its own importance. The net shops — tall, narrow wooden sheds built to dry nets on the steep shingle — are the survivors of that economy.

1066 and the invention of the battlefield

The battle was fought at Senlac, not at Hastings, but medieval convention named battles after the nearest notable place, and “Hastings” won. The site was marked by Battle Abbey, founded by William in fulfilment of a vow and dedicated in 1094; its high altar was placed, according to tradition, on the spot where Harold fell. The abbey made the battlefield a pilgrimage destination and, after the Dissolution, its ruins became a romantic ruin and then a heritage site.

The name “Battle of Hastings” is thus a double metonymy: the battle is named after a town, and the town is named after a people. The phrase has become so fixed that most speakers never notice that the fighting took place elsewhere.

Victorian reinvention

The railway reached Hastings in 1851, and the town reinvented itself as a seaside resort. The pier (1872), the White Rock baths, the funicular lifts and the Regency terraces belong to this period. The Victorian town was respectable, family-oriented and self-consciously historical: it sold 1066 and sea air together. The same period saw the popularisation of the arrow-in-the-eye image and the growth of the “1066 Country” tourist narrative.

Toponymic diffusion

Emigrants and administrators carried the name across the world. Hastings in New Zealand, founded in the 1870s, is the largest namesake; Hastings in Minnesota and Nebraska are railroad towns; Hastings-on-Hudson in New York is a riverside village; Hastings in Barbados is a parish settlement. The surname Hastings is older than most of these and produced Warren Hastings, whose impeachment trial of 1788–95 made the name a byword for imperial corruption and courage in equal measure. Lansford Hastings gave his name to the Hastings Cutoff, the shortcut that helped destroy the Donner Party in 1846.

Literary and documentary attestations

  • Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, s.a. 1066 — the earliest narrative of the battle, naming Hastings as the place where William came ashore.
  • The Burghal Hidage, early tenth century — lists Hæstingaceaster among the fortified burhs of Sussex.
  • Domesday Book, 1086 — records Hastings as a borough with a mint and a market.
  • Camden’s Britannia and later antiquarian surveys — discuss the Roman fort and the battle site.
  • Victorian guidebooks and Bulwer-Lytton’s Harold (1848) — fix the town’s dual identity as resort and battlefield.

What the name carries

To a historian, Hastings is a date; to a Sussex resident, it is a pier and a fishing beach; to a New Zealander, it is a provincial city; to a genealogist, it is a surname. All these meanings descend from a single Old English folk-name whose founder is lost. The word is a reminder that toponymy is not a record of places but of the people who named them, and that the most famous thing about a place may happen somewhere else entirely.
