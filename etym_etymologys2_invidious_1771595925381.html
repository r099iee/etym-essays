Chain for INVIDIOUS (single root, Latin): Stage 1 — PIE *weid- "to see" (Pokorny *weid-; also *wid-, *woid- in ablaut). The root of visual perception across the family, famous for the semantic shift "to see" → "to know" (one who has seen knows). Attested by its reflexes rather than directly. Stage 2 — Latin vidēre "to see" (Classical Latin, attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE; regular Italic reflex of *weid-). Stage 3 — Latin invidēre "to look at with ill-will, to envy, to cast an evil eye" (1st c. BCE onward; in- "upon" + vidēre "to see"; the envious gaze literally "looked upon" another's good). Stage 4 — Latin invidia "envy, grudge, ill-will" (1st c. BCE onward; abstract noun of invidēre; personified in Roman literature, e.g., Ovid). Stage 5 — Latin invidiōsus "full of envy; hateful, exciting hatred" (Classical–Late Latin; adjective on invidia with -ōsus, "full of"; already double-faced — the feeler's envy and the object's hatefulness). Stage 6 — English invidious "arousing envy or ill-will; offensively discriminating; (archaic) hateful" (c. 1600; borrowed directly from Latin invidiōsus in the early modern period, with no French intermediary for the adjective). Final node — English INVIDIOUS (c. 1600; the direct Latin loan; the "hateful, odious" sense is archaic today, and the word is now almost entirely relational — attached to comparisons, distinctions, and classifications that stir resentment). Note on the sister chain for ENVY (same Latin family, different route): Latin invidia passed through Old French envie into English envy (late 13th c.) and envious (c. 1300, via Old French envieus). Envy, envious, and invidious are thus one family: invidious is the direct Latin loan (c. 1600), envy the older French-borrowed member (13th c.). Also from the same Latin line: invidiousness (the abstract noun), invidiously (adverb). No stages are invented; the direct Latin borrowing of invidious is recorded by the OED from around 1600.
