- c. 1st millennium BCE (India): Sanskrit vaiḍūrya, the prized stone of the far south; the word begins its slow journey west.
- 3rd–1st c. BCE: Greek bēryllos — the sea-green stone from India; Greek Bible versions make it the stone of Ezekiel's vision.
- 1st c. CE: Pliny's encyclopaedia — Indian beryls cut into cylinders; Roman luxury and signet rings.
- Middle Ages: beryl in the lapidaries and in Revelation's foundations (the eighth stone); church windows and scrying "beryls" blur stone and glass.
- 1500s–1700s: "beryl" still means the greenish Indian gem; emerald is slowly recognised as its noble cousin.
- 1798–1828: chemistry rewrites the word — Vauquelin finds a new earth in beryl; Wöhler isolates the metal and names it beryllium; beryl becomes an industrial ore.
- Today: the jeweller sells emeralds and aquamarines of the beryl family; the mineral name keeps its echo in the poetry of green eyes.
