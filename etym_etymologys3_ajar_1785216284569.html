Ajar is a small word with a long, strange history — a history that begins not with an opening at all but with a turning. The verb that lies behind it is Old English cierran "to turn, change, convert", attested from the ninth century, itself from a reconstructed Proto-Germanic *karzijaną "to turn" whose ultimate Indo-European ancestry is uncertain: some scholars point to a PIE root *gers- / *ghers- "to turn, bend", but the details of that deeper stage are debated, and the honest statement is that the Germanic turn-word has no securely reconstructable PIE etymon. From cierran, Old English made the deverbal noun cierr "a turn, a turning, an occasion", and Middle English inherited it as char(e) — the word of "a turn of work" (whence the modern "chore") and "an occasion". It is this noun, in the fixed prepositional phrase, that produced our word. The phrase "on char(e)". In the early fourteenth century (around 1325) English attests the phrase "on char(e)", meaning "in the act of turning" — literally "on a turn". A door "on char" was a door caught in the moment of swinging, arrested in the middle of its travel. The sense is mechanical and precise: it names a hinge arrested mid-motion, a moment rather than a state, with no hint yet of secrets or suspense. Over the next three centuries the phrase contracted and specialized. The meaning narrowed from the event (turning) to the result (turned a little way), so that "on char" came to mean simply "slightly open", the small physical consequence of the turn. And the form itself was reanalyzed: the Middle English ch of char, heard in the quick, unaccented phrase, was re-spelled as a j by the seventeenth century, giving "on jar" (1670s) and then the fused "ajar" — first attested in 1718. The modern word is thus the fossil of an entire Old English sentence: "on char(e)", three words meaning "in the act of turning", has become one word meaning "slightly open". Sound and reanalysis. The shift from "on char" to "ajar" is a textbook case of rebracketing — the speaker stops hearing the boundary between "on" and "char" and re-parses the whole as a single word beginning with a vowel, then adds the indefinite article and re-parses again ("on a jar"). The "ch" to "j" change, which no regular English sound law explains, is best understood as a consequence of the phrase's reduced, hurried pronunciation and of the new word's assimilation to the existing "jar" (a vessel) and "jar" (to shake). The connection to the household vessel is a folk etymology — the image of a loose jar lid is memorable but historically wrong — and any influence of the verb "jar" on the form is possible but unproven. The OED and modern handbooks prefer the "char" etymology, and the internal evidence is strong: "on char" is attested two centuries before "on jar", and the sense of "turning" explains the meaning of "ajar" far better than any vessel could. The storyteller's crack of light. For most of its life "ajar" was a plain, domestic word — the door, the window, the gate — with an everyday register. But the nineteenth century gave it a second life. Gothic and Victorian fiction discovered that a door ajar is the most economical image of suspense: it is a recently opened door that may close at any moment, a sliver of light or danger, the threshold of interruption. Dickens, the Brontës, and the great ghost-story writers used it to signal the presence that has just passed through, or is about to return; a door ajar in a haunted house is worth a page of description. The small adjective acquired a frisson, and the modern reader brings that frisson to every "ajar" — which is exactly what it lends the hobbit's narrowing escape route in the dark passage of The Hobbit, where the door "was still ajar" but a goblin "had pushed it nearly to". The word's whole career — from the mechanical moment of a turning hinge, to the plain domestic state of a half-open door, to the Gothic emblem of suspense and invitation — is contained in its five letters. Today "ajar" is still used almost only of doors, windows, and gates, and still carries that suspended, precarious note, caught between two states, exactly as a door on the turn was caught between two positions.
