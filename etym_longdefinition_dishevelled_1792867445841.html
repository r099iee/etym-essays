Dishevelled, spelled with a double l in British English, describes anything whose neat order has come undone: hair uncombed and streaming, clothing rumpled, or, as in the source sentence, a whole house sunk into gloom and neglect.

In the source sentence Bag End seemed sad and gloomy and dishevelled: Frodo's beloved hole, once trim and tended, now stands dusty, curtainless, and comfortless on the eve of his departure, its rooms mirroring the grief of leaving. Applied to a house rather than a head, the word borrows the pathos of a mourner with unbound hair and lends it to walls and windows, so that the building itself seems to grieve.

Core meaning in detail: The word is built from the French image of hair let down: Old French descheveler meant to unbind or disorder the hair, from chevel, a head of hair, and the past participle dishevelled first pictured a person bare-headed with locks streaming. From the head the sense spread to dress in general, then to rooms, gardens, and lives: anything that ought to be combed, folded, or tended but has been left to straggle earns the adjective.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • With hair disordered and streaming (earliest sense, 14th c.): the dishevelled mourner, the wind-dishevelled child. The bridge is the word's birth: hair unbound from its coif or ribbon is disorder made visible.
  • Untidy in dress and person (generalised personal sense): a dishevelled traveller stumbling off the night bus, clothes awry and eyes bleary. The bridge extends the streaming hair to the whole figure: rumpled linen reads like uncombed locks.
  • Disordered and neglected in rooms or buildings (transferred sense, the source meaning): a dishevelled cottage, dishevelled offices after a move. The bridge personifies the place: the stripped, dusty house wears its emptiness the way a mourner wears loose hair.
  • Wild and unkempt in landscape (picturesque extension): dishevelled hedges, a dishevelled garden run to seed. The bridge carries human grooming into horticulture: unclipped branches straggle exactly like uncombed curls.
  • Confused and ragged in affairs or thought (figurative extension): a dishevelled administration, dishevelled prose. The bridge is metaphorical: arguments left uncombed snarl and tangle like neglected hair.
  • Bare-headed as a sign of mourning or frenzy (archaic ritual shade): medieval widows and prophetesses went dishevelled, hair unbound, to show grief or ecstasy. The bridge is cultural: ordered hair meant ordered society, so loosened hair announced that order had broken, in sorrow or in spirit-possession.

Register and connotation: Dishevelled is literary and sympathetic rather than scolding: it pities where scruffy sneers and slovenly condemns. A dishevelled heroine keeps our affection, a dishevelled house keeps our tenderness, because the word always implies a better order lost, whether to grief, haste, wind, or love. Its French tailoring gives it an elegance that survives even the mess it names, which is why novelists reach for it at moments of dignified ruin.
