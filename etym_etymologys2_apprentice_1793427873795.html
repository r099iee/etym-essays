PIE (c. 3500 BCE)
*gʰed- / *gʰend-
‘to seize, grasp, take hold’
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*prai-hendō
‘to seize in front, grasp beforehand’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
prehendere (or prendere)
‘to lay hold of, seize, grasp’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. CE)
apprehendere
‘to seize upon, grasp physically or mentally’
↓
Vulgar Latin (c. 5th–7th c.)
apprendere
‘to learn, lay hold of in mind’
↓
Old French (c. 11th–12th c.)
aprendre
‘to learn, understand’
↓
Anglo-Norman (c. 13th c.)
aprentis
‘learner, one bound to a master of a craft’
↓
Middle English (c. 1300)
aprentys / apprentice
‘bound craft learner, novice’
↓
Modern English (current)
apprentice
‘indentured learner of a craft or profession’

Note: Formed from Latin ad- (‘to, toward’) + prehendere (‘to grasp’), which yielded French apprendre (‘to learn’, literally ‘to take hold of mentally’). The agentive suffix -is reflects Latin -icius (forming nouns of personal condition or servitude). In Anglo-Norman legal practice, aprentis was formalized for guild contracts, entering Middle English alongside Chaucer’s Clerk and the craft guilds of London.
