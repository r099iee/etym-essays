- (Old English, c. 900) - The noun dræht/dreaht means "pulling, drawing" — the action of the strong verb dragan "to draw," nothing more.
- (1200s–1300s) - Middle English draught shifts from the action to the thing drawn: a draught of wine (a drink), a draught of fish (a net-haul), a ship's draught (the water it draws).
- (1500s) - New drawings multiply: a draught of air (drawn through a flue), a draught or sketch (drawn with a pen), the game draughts (pieces drawn across the board), a draught of medicine (a dose drawn from a bottle).
- (1600s–1700s) - "On draught" appears for ale drawn from the barrel; draught horses and oxen — animals that draw — enter the vocabulary of farming and haulage.
- (1800s) - The great spelling split: American English, under Noah Webster's reforms, writes draft for everything; British English keeps draught for air, drink, ships, and animals, and draft only for documents and bank orders.
- (1900s) - Draught becomes a marker of Britishness: draughty rooms, draught beer, draughtsmen; Americans keep "on draught" for beer and little else.
- (1937) - Tolkien gives the old word a mythic air in The Hobbit: "Could there be a draught from that little hole?" — the humble air-current sense carrying the first hint of the dragon's breath.
- (today) - In Britain an ordinary word (a cold draught, a pint on draught); in America a British-flavoured archaism; the document sense lives everywhere as draft.
