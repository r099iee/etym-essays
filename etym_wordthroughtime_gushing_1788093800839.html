- [late 14th century: the physical torrent] - guschen appears in Middle English as a violent verb of liquids: blood gushes from wounds, water from springs; the word is onomatopoeic — it sounds like the sudden pour it names.
- [16th–17th centuries: widening floods] - "Gush" extends to smoke, wind and crowds (the "gushing whirl" of winds and waters); in the 1660s it makes the decisive figurative leap — words, tears and sentiments gush out of people.
- [18th–19th centuries: the sentimental age] - The gushing person — overflowing with feeling, praise or tears — becomes a stock figure of the age of sensibility; the participle gains its mildly mocking edge; oilmen coin "gusher" (1886) for a well that pours without pumping.
- [20th century: the celebrity register] - "Gushing reviews", "gushing fans", "gushing over a star": the figurative sense is now the commoner one, usually affectionate-but-excessive, sometimes insincere.
- [21st century: double register] - Literal gushing (burst pipes, wounds, waterfalls) and emotional gushing (effusive praise, fan culture) live side by side; the word still carries the sound of a sudden, unstoppable outpour — the very sound that ends Tolkien's dragon in "a hiss, a gushing whirl, and then silence".
