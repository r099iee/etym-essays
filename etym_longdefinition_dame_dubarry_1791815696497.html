Dame Dubarry is a proper name, not a common noun: an English honorific attached to an anglicised French surname, used by Thomas Carlyle in The French Revolution (1837) for Jeanne Bécu, comtesse du Barry, the last maîtresse-en-titre of Louis XV. “Dame” is the old English title for a lady, the exact counterpart of French madame; “Dubarry” is the surname she took from her husband, rendered without the French particle as a single word. Carlyle’s form is deliberately archaic and faintly mocking — she is named as one might name an allegorical figure, like Dame Fortune or Dame Nature.

The woman behind the name was born in Vaucouleurs in Lorraine on 19 August 1743, the illegitimate daughter of a seamstress, Anne Bécu. Educated in a Paris convent and later employed in a milliner’s shop, she came to notice in fashionable circles and in 1768 was married to Guillaume du Barry, a soldier of a family whose claims to nobility were contested, largely so that she could be presented at court. She was presented in April 1769 and became the king’s official mistress, installed at Versailles while Madame de Pompadour’s former apartments were refurbished for her. After Louis XV died in May 1774 she was sent first to a convent and then to her house at Louveciennes; she was arrested in 1792 and guillotined on 8 December 1793.

What the name denotes in the source

  • The person: Jeanne Bécu, comtesse du Barry, mistress of Louis XV from 1769 to 1774 — the last woman to hold that recognised position at the French court.
  • A type: the royal favourite, the woman whose influence rests on intimacy rather than office, and whose name becomes a byword for a court ruled by appetite.
  • A rhetorical target: in Carlyle’s sentence she “might pray, if she had a talent for it”, which is a joke at the expense of a notoriously irreligious court. The conditional is the whole point: prayer is one accomplishment she lacks.

Dame — the honorific component

  • French origin: from Latin domina, “mistress, lady”, the feminine of dominus; it entered English through Old French in the Middle Ages.
  • English title: historically a prefix to the name of a woman of rank or authority, and since 1917 the female equivalent of Sir in the British honours system (Dame Judi Dench, Dame Judi).
  • Archaic and literary: a woman of standing, a mistress of a household; also a mother or a wife in older usage.
  • Personification: Dame Nature, Dame Fortune, Dame Rumour — a medieval and Renaissance habit of giving allegorical figures a courteous title, which is exactly the colour Carlyle borrows.
  • Institutional relic: the dame school, a small elementary school kept by a woman in her own home, common in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
  • Stage sense: the dame of British pantomime, a comic female role traditionally played by a man — a usage that has given the word a camp, theatrical flavour quite foreign to Carlyle.
  • American slang: from the late nineteenth century, a woman, often with a disparaging or hard-boiled tone (“a classy dame”); now dated and best avoided.

Dubarry — the surname component

  • Particle: du is the contracted form of French de + le, “of the”, a mark of territorial surname (du Barry, du Guesclin, du Bellay).
  • Toponym: Barry is a French place name; the surname means “of Barry”, that is, of the estate or seigneurie so called. The exact place is not certainly identified, and the family’s genealogy was disputed even in its own day.
  • Not her birth name: she was born Bécu; du Barry came from her marriage and was the name under which she was known at court and in history.

Register, tone and collocation

The compound is not neutral. French usage would give madame du Barry or la comtesse du Barry; Carlyle’s clipped Dame Dubarry drops both the particle and the polite madame, and the dropped particle is audible. The effect is that of an old chronicle or a morality play: a title, a surname, and no Christian name. It sits in the same paragraph as “Duke d’Aiguillon”, “Maupeou” and “the Parlement Maupeou”, all named with the same contemptuous economy, and it prepares for the closing proverb that “each dog has but his day”.
