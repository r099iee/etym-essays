- [1560s] 'coil' (noun) — a heap of gathered rope folded into rings aboard ship; from Old French 'coillir', to gather up
- [1580s] 'coil' (verb) — to wind a rope into a circular heap; a working sailors' word of the deck
- [c. 1602] Shakespeare's 'mortal coil' (Hamlet) — a separate, now-dead sense of 'coil' meaning bustle, turmoil, or noisy disturbance (of obscure origin); the phrase freezes the old sense in amber
- [17th–18th c.] Extended from rope to serpents, hair, springs, and smoke; 'coiling' becomes a favourite vivid verb for snakes in natural-history and travel writing
- [19th c.] Electrical age: Faraday's induction coil (1830s) and coil springs give the word a precise technical meaning; 'coiling' enters physics and engineering
- [20th c.] Figurative pressure: 'coiling' tension, dread, and coiled energy become common in thriller and psychological prose
- [21st c.] Standard in fantasy and action prose for dragons and serpents, in science for DNA helices and solenoids, and in everyday speech for hoses, cables, and smoke
