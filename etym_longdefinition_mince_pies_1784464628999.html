Mince-pies (also written mince pies) are small British pastries, eaten above all at Christmas, filled with mincemeat — in the modern form a dense, sweet, heavily spiced mixture of chopped dried fruit (raisins, currants, sultanas), candied peel, sugar, spirits such as brandy or rum, and suet, usually baked inside a shortcrust or sweet pastry case with a pastry lid or a decorative star. The plural form mince-pies is the ordinary way of referring to them as a batch or a kind: one asks for mince-pies, not a single mince-pie, exactly as Bofur does when he demands “And mince-pies and cheese.” In Tolkien's scene the word is doing precise cultural work: it plants a very specific, hearth-bound, festively English food next to savoury cheese, a rustic pairing that signals abundance, tradition, and unapologetic appetite. The plural matters because Bofur is ordering a dish-type as household stock, as though Bilbo's larders were a public feast-hall; the word itself carries the accumulated weight of an old British food institution.

The compound splits cleanly into its two living halves. Mince means to chop into very small pieces, so mincemeat is by name “finely chopped meat” — and historically that is precisely what it was. The medieval and early-modern mince-pie contained real minced meat (beef, mutton, or veal, sometimes tongue or chicken) combined with suet, dried fruit, and spices. Over the course of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the meat gradually withdrew from the recipe; by the Victorian period the filling was pure fruit, sugar, and suet, and the name mincemeat stayed attached to a mixture that no longer contained any meat at all. A mince-pie today is therefore a small sweet pastry whose name is a fossil of its savoury ancestry, and mince-pie and mincemeat are a perfect illustration of semantic drift in action: the word has outlived the ingredient that named it.

Polysemy must be surveyed exhaustively, because mince-pies is not one word but a small family of senses that share a single form. First, the culinary sense just described: the small sweet spiced fruit pastry of the British Christmas, eaten from Advent through Twelfth Night, traditionally served warm with cheese (the Bofur pairing), brandy butter, or cream. Second, by transparent extension, any pie whose filling is mincemeat, including larger festive tarts and the historical meat versions — in early cookery books minc’d pies or minched pies are simply pies filled with minced meat and fruit. Third, the Cockney rhyming-slang sense, recorded from the nineteenth century: mince pies rhymes with eyes, so “take a butcher’s at your mince pies” means “look at your eyes.” Fourth, transferred and figurative uses of the elements: to mince words (to speak with affected delicacy or to soften the truth), not to mince matters, and minced oath (a softened swear-word), all built on the verb's root idea of reducing something to small, careful pieces. Fifth, the whole phrase mince-pie appears in Victorian and Edwardian Christmas literature as shorthand for domestic festivity, household plenty, and the cosy inversion of a well-ordered home — precisely the value that Tolkien's dwarves are busy dismantling.

The word sits in a tight lexical field of British pastries, and precision matters. A pork-pie is a savoury cold meat pie in a firm hot-water crust, eaten in the hand, with no sweetness at all; mince-pie is sweet and festive. A tart is shallower, open or latticed, usually without an enclosing pastry lid, and in British English tends toward fruit or custard fillings; a mince-pie is closed with a lid. A pasty is a crimped, semicircular hand-pie, typically savoury (the Cornish pasty); a pie alone is the generic pastry container. Mincemeat is not a pie at all but the filling, and it may be bought in jars and used in other dishes. The unique contribution of mince-pies to the set is the fusion of three things: the historical meat-to-fruit drift, the compact single-serving form, and the intense seasonal association with the British Christmas.

Register, collocation, and usage. The word is cosy, old-fashioned, and festive; it belongs to larders, pantries, shop windows in December, and nursery-memory. It collocates with eat, serve, warm, homemade, shop-bought, box of, platter of, and the fixed phrase a mince-pie and a glass of sherry is a whole genre of seasonal hospitality. The singular mince-pie is the portion; the plural mince-pies is the batch, the kind, the institution.

  • Primary sense — festive pastry: small spiced sweet pie filled with mincemeat (dried fruit, suet, spirits), eaten at Christmas.
  • Historical sense — meat pie: the pre-1700 form genuinely containing minced meat, suet, fruit, and spice; the semantic bridge by which the modern name survives.
  • Rhyming slang: Cockney mince pies = eyes (19th c.), a purely phonetic secondary sense with no culinary content.
  • Figurative extensions of mince: mince words, mince matters, minced oath — delicate reduction, softening, or trimming of speech.
  • Emblem of festivity: the Victorian-Edwardian Christmas emblem of hearth and household plenty, available to novelists for cosiness or its inversion.
