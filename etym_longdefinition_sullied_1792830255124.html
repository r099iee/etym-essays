Sullied, the past participle of sully, means stained, dirtied, or dishonoured — made impure either physically, as linen sullied with mud, or morally, as a name sullied by scandal. The word always implies a fall from a prior whiteness: something was clean, bright, or fair, and now carries a mark that draws the eye and invites judgment.

In the source sentence the Three Rings of the Elves are praised precisely because they escaped this fall: his hand never touched them or sullied them. Sauron's touch is imagined as a stain — contact itself would have dirtied what the Elf-lords kept hidden and pure. Tolkien's moral universe runs on exactly this physics of purity: evil soils, good preserves unstained, and sullied carries the full weight of defilement, not mere untidiness. The Rings endure as the unsullied exception in a sullied world.

Core meaning in detail: Physically, sullied describes surfaces that have lost their clean brightness — sullied snow, sullied cuffs, a page sullied with ink. The stain need not be large; a single mark suffices, because the force of the word lies in contrast with what was. Morally, it describes honour, reputation, memory, or innocence marked by shame — a sullied reputation, a sullied victory, sullied hands. Here the stain metaphor governs whole idioms of guilt: we speak of dirty secrets, mud-slinging, whitewashing, and washing one's hands, and sullied is the literary crown of that family. Grammatically it is usually attributive or passive: reputations are sullied by scandal, linen is sullied with mire, and the agent of staining is named with the same gravity as the stain.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Soiled physically, stained with dirt or impurity (earliest sense, mid-14th century): boots sullied with marsh mud; the white cloth sullied by smoke. The bridge is direct inheritance from Old French souillier, to make dirty — the pigsty word brought indoors to describe any brightness dimmed by filth.
  • Dishonoured morally, robbed of good reputation (figurative, fully established by the 1500s): a career sullied by bribery; a triumph sullied by cheating. The bridge is the universal purity-as-goodness metaphor: honour is whiteness, shame is dirt, so scandal stains exactly as mud does. Shakespeare and the chronicles made this the dominant literary sense.
  • Defiled spiritually or ceremonially (religious and epic register): hands sullied with innocent blood; an altar sullied by sacrifice to idols. The bridge carries physical staining into the sacred: what touches the holy must be clean, so unworthy contact pollutes. Tolkien's Rings belong here — untouched and therefore unsullied, holiness preserved by hiding.
  • Tarnished in splendour, dimmed of lustre (poetic, of beauty and nature): her sullied veil; the sullied moon behind factory smoke. The bridge softens dirt into dimming: nothing need be filthy, only less radiant than it was. Poets use it for twilight beauties, faded garments, and compromised ideals alike.
  • Corrupted in wording or record, falsified (rare, of testimony and texts): a confession sullied by coercion; a chronicle sullied with flattery. The bridge extends staining from cloth to truth: just as dye spreads through linen, bias spreads through evidence, and the document can never be washed white again.
  • Violated in chastity or innocence (archaic and literary): a sullied maiden; sullied innocence. The bridge is the oldest moral extension, once common in romance and tragedy, now largely displaced by plainer terms — but it explains the word's enduring gravity: to sully was once to ruin utterly.

Register and connotation: Sullied is formal, literary, and grave — the word of editorials, epitaphs, and epic, never of playgrounds. It judges even as it describes: to call a reputation sullied is to agree the stain matters and the prior whiteness was real. Its favourite subjects are the great white things — snow, linen, honour, memory, victory — because only whiteness shows a stain. The unsullied exception proves the rule: English praises the unsullied name, record, and sheet precisely because sullying is assumed to be the world's default motion.
