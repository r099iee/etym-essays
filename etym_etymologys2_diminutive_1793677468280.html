Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*mey-
"small, little; to lessen"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*menw-
"small, fine, thin"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE)
minuere
"to make smaller, lessen, diminish"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
dēminuere
"to break into pieces, diminish, lessen"
↓
Late Classical / Grammatical Latin (c. 1st c. CE)
dēminūtīvus
"denoting smallness or affection"
↓
Old / Middle French (c. 14th c.)
diminutif
"diminutive, lessening; small word"
↓
Middle English (c. late 14th c.)
diminutif
"grammatical diminutive; small"
↓
Modern English (present)
diminutive
"extremely small, tiny; grammatical suffix"

Note on etymological nuance: In Classical Latin, the verb was properly spelled dēminuere (from the prefix dē- "completely, down" + minuere "to lessen"). During the post-classical era and in Vulgar/Medieval Latin, it underwent vowel assimilation or analogical influence from the prefix dis-, shifting the initial syllable to di- (diminuere, diminūtīvus), which directly yielded Old French diminutif and Modern English diminutive.
