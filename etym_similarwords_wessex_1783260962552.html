• West Saxons: Similar because both name the same people and kingdom; differs because "West Saxons" is the descriptive plural (Old English Westseaxe) from which the contracted proper name Wessex grew, whereas Wessex is the fixed, modernized proper noun.
  • Sussex: Similar because it follows the same naming pattern — Suthseaxe "South Saxons" contracted to Sussex; differs because it designates a separate, smaller Anglo-Saxon kingdom in the south-east, eventually absorbed into Wessex.
  • Essex: Similar because it is the East Saxon counterpart (Ēastseaxe "East Saxons"), formed the same way; differs because it lay in the south-east, never attained Wessex's imperial reach, and now names an English county.
  • Mercia: Similar because Mercia was the other great Anglo-Saxon kingdom and Wessex's chief rival, often paired with it in heptarchy lists and in Gibbon's sentence; differs because its name means "boundary/march people" (Old English Mierce) and its centre lay in the Midlands; it was conquered by Wessex in the 9th–10th centuries.
  • Northumbria: Similar because it was a major heptarchy kingdom like Wessex, and its scholarly culture flourished under Bede; differs because it lay north of the Humber and was Anglian, and it never became the engine of English unification as Wessex did.
  • Anglo-Saxon England: Similar because Wessex was part of this world and its name is inseparable from it; differs because the phrase names the whole culture, period, and set of kingdoms, not the single kingdom.
  • England: Similar because Wessex grew into the core of England and its kings became the kings of the English; differs because England is the whole country and nation, the product of Wessex's success rather than a coordinate kingdom.
  • Hardy's Wessex: Similar because it uses the same name for the same south-western region of England; differs because it is a literary invention of Thomas Hardy, a fictional landscape only loosely mapped onto the historical kingdom and freighted with later, romantic associations.
