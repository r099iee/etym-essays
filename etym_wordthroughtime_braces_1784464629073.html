“Brace” entered English meaning something that clasps or supports — from a word for two arms, the double grip that holds things together.
“Brace” through the centuries spun off a family of supports: braces for the teeth, braces for weak beams, braces for a sail, and the paired shoulder-straps that brace up a man’s trousers.
“Braces” in British English from the 19th century named those trouser straps worn over the shoulders — the everyday alternative to the belt, the very hardware of respectable dress.
“Braces” today in British English still means the trouser-suspenders — a word of settled domesticity, the paired straps that hold up a comfortable householder’s trousers as he puffs at his pipe.
