Shriek is a high-pitched, piercing cry — sharper, thinner, and more sudden than a shout — typically forced out by terror, pain, or extreme excitement. As a noun it names the sound itself; as a verb it names the act of uttering it. The word's defining features are pitch and involuntariness: a shriek is not a composed or communicative call but a reflex of the voice, the panic at the very top of the range. When Bilbo hears that he "may never return," he feels "a shriek coming up inside, and very soon it burst out like the whistle of an engine coming out of a tunnel." Tolkien presents the cry first as an internal pressure, a rising tension, and then as an explosion; the steam-whistle simile makes the sound comic-industrial as well as panicky, so the reader hears both genuine fear and hobbit absurdity at once. The card's core is the noun: the cry as a thing that rises, accumulates, and bursts. The same word works as a verb ("to shriek") and as a participle ("shrieking"), and it freely describes both human voices and animal or mechanical sounds. The acoustic fingerprint of a shriek matters as much as its meaning. A shriek is not merely loud; it is high and thin, produced at the very top of the vocal range, and it typically begins abruptly, with no gradual crescendo. Where a shout opens the throat and throws the voice outward as communication, a shriek constricts the throat and escapes as a reflex — which is why it so often accompanies sudden terror, sudden pain, or sudden overwhelming delight. English speakers can feel the difference immediately in a sentence: people "cry out" or "shout for help" deliberately, but they "shriek with fear" whether they intend to or not. This involuntary, piercing quality is precisely what Tolkien dramatizes when he makes the shriek "come up inside" Bilbo like a pressure building in a pipe before it bursts — the sound is something that happens to the speaker, not something the speaker does. Primary senses and nuances.
  • A high-pitched cry of terror or pain: the central sense — the involuntary piercing outcry of someone in extreme fear or suffering.
  • A cry of intense excitement or delight: the lighter sense — "a shriek of laughter," "she shrieked with joy" — the same piercing sound released in wild pleasure.
  • A harsh, high-pitched sound from animals or machinery: the transferred sense — the "shriek" of a bird, of brakes, of a whistle, of metal on metal — any thin, piercing acoustic event that resembles the human cry.
  • The act of shrieking (verb): to utter such a cry, always with the qualities of suddenness and high pitch.
Semantic bridges. The sense of mechanical and animal noise grows directly from the human cry by metaphor: the ear recognizes the same acoustic profile — high, thin, piercing, sudden — and transfers the word for "cry" to any sound that shares it. This is why a whistle, a siren, or a braking train can "shriek." The sense of delighted shrieking develops alongside the panicked sense because the physiology is the same: extreme arousal, whether of fear or of joy, compresses the throat and raises the pitch. The modern word therefore covers both extremes at once — the scream of genuine danger and the excited squeal of delight. Register and usage. Shriek is ordinary narrative English, vivid rather than technical. It differs from scream in being sharper and more specific in pitch; from shout and yell in being involuntary rather than communicative; and from howl and wail in being high and sudden rather than low and prolonged. On this card the word must remain the noun of that rising internal cry — the thing "coming up inside" Bilbo — not the verb alone, and not a mere shout.
