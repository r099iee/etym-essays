ESSAY 1 OF 2 — DISCONTENTED

The biography of discontented is a biography of the vessel and its overflow. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch," a root of the body and the workshop: it stretched hides across pegs, drew bowstrings taut, and warped the threads of a loom. In Sanskrit it gave tanoti, "he stretches," and tantra, the warp of a loom — and eventually "a framework, a doctrine." In Germanic it underwent Grimm's Law to yield the ancestor of English thin — something stretched until it is narrow. The root's career across the Indo-European world was everywhere the same: stretching was tension, and tension was control.

The crucial event for our word happened in Latin. There the root produced tenēre, "to hold" — the one who holds is the one who keeps something stretched tight and under control. Roman writers then compounded tenēre with con-, "together," to form continēre, "to hold together, to contain, to restrain." From its past participle came contentus. The semantic shift was a masterpiece of psychological metaphor: a person whose desires, appetites, and ambitions were "contained" — held peacefully within inner boundaries like water in a sealed vessel — was contentus, content. The image is thoroughly physical: contentment is enclosure; the vessel neither leaks nor overflows.

The word entered medieval Europe already carrying this imagery. Old French descontent — the negative formed with des- — described the person whose inner vessel had failed: desires spilling over into restlessness, complaint, and agitation. By the fifteenth century Middle English had taken discontent as an adjective, noun, and verb, and in the late sixteenth century English speakers added the participial ending -ed to form discontented — literally "rendered uncontained." Shakespeare's Richard III (c. 1592–94) fixed the noun in cultural memory: "Now is the winter of our discontent / Made glorious summer by this sun of York." The line turned discontent into the name of a public and political mood — a kingdom's dissatisfaction with its ruler — and the word has carried that civic resonance ever since.

From the 16th century onward the word's history is a story of two registers:

  • The personal register: a chronic, restless unhappiness with one's lot — the sense used by the moralists and novelists who describe temperament rather than grievance.
  • The political register: "the discontented" as a class or faction grumbling against authority; "social discontent," "popular discontent" as the fuel of reform and revolt. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century political writing made "discontent" a staple of statecraft: rulers are advised to diagnose and relieve the "discontents" of the people before they harden into rebellion.

Jane Austen, writing at the turn of the 19th century, uses the personal register with complete mastery — and with irony. In Pride and Prejudice, Mrs. Bennet, "a woman of mean understanding, little information, and uncertain temper," "when she was discontented, fancied herself nervous." The joke is precisely the disproportion between vessel and overflow: a mind without inner boundaries converts the smallest dissatisfaction into theatrical nervous complaint. In modern English the word remains squarely on the personal side: "discontented" describes a settled, often self-pitying dissatisfaction with one's circumstances, distinct from the sharper, more transient "dissatisfaction" with a specific thing. The political sense survives in "discontent" and "malcontent," but "discontented" itself has narrowed to the individual mood. The full arc — from the stretched hide of a Neolithic workshop, through the Latin vessel of contained desire, to Shakespeare's winter of public misery and Austen's nervous Mrs. Bennet — is a model of how a physical image of tension and containment became one of the most precise words English possesses for the feeling of being chronically ill at ease with one's own life.

ESSAY 2 OF 2 — CAPRICE

Caprice is the only word in this family whose biography begins with a shiver. Its history starts in two Proto-Indo-European roots that met on the Italian peninsula in the early modern period. The first is *kaput-, "head," the root that produced Latin caput (source of capital, captain, chief, and, in Germanic, English head itself) and, through the Romance languages, Italian capo, "head." The second is *ghers-, "to bristle, stand on end," the root of Latin ēricius, "hedgehog" — the small animal named for its bristling spines — which gave Italian riccio, "hedgehog; curly, bristling."

In early Italian the two combined into caporiccio, "head-hedgehog," a striking physical image: a head whose hair stands up like a hedgehog's quills. The image belongs to the physiology of fear and shock — the goose-bump shiver, the hair rising on the back of the neck, the sudden start of terror. In the sixteenth century the word, shortened to capriccio, began to migrate from the body to the mind: a capriccio was first a sudden fright or a shudder, then a sudden impulse, a start of the fancy, a whim. The bridge is the abruptness: the same unexpected jolt that bristles the hair also seizes the mind with a thought that has no reason behind it.

The word then moved into high culture through the arts. In late sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Italy, capriccio named a musical or literary work that followed the composer's fancy rather than a fixed form — an early ancestor of the free, virtuosic instrumental piece still called a capriccio or caprice (Bach's Capriccio on the Departure of a Beloved Brother, Paganini's 24 Caprices). In the visual arts a capriccio was a landscape or architectural fantasy assembling real buildings in impossible combinations — the playful, unaccountable arrangement that defines the genre from Canaletto to Piranesi. In every case the word means the same thing: the work obeys whim rather than rule.

French borrowed capriccio as caprice in the mid-sixteenth century, and English took it directly from French in the mid-seventeenth century (the earliest OED examples are from the 1650s), at first in the sense of a sudden, motiveless change of mind. By the eighteenth century the word was established in the moral vocabulary of the novelists: Jane Austen uses it of Mr. Bennet — "so odd a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humour, reserve, and caprice" — to name the unpredictable, teasing impulses that make him opaque to his wife after twenty-three years of marriage.

  • Into impersonal life: "the caprices of the weather," "the caprices of fate" — the unpredictable, unaccountable behaviour of nature or fortune.
  • Into art and aesthetics: the capriccio survives as a genre of musical and pictorial fantasy that deliberately follows fancy.
  • Into everyday psychology: caprice is the noun of the capricious person — sudden, unreasoned changes of mood that make behaviour unreliable.

A lively scholarly debate surrounds the word's literal origin. The traditional derivation — capo "head" + riccio "hedgehog," "head with bristling hair" — is the one given by the dictionaries, but an alternative connects the word to capra, "she-goat": a capriccio would then be a "goat-like leap," the sudden, skittish jump of a goat, extended to a sudden leap of the mind. OED presents both, favouring the head-and-hedgehog derivation while noting the goat hypothesis as a serious rival. Either way, the core image is a sudden physical start — the animal jerk of fear or surprise — transferred to the sudden, unaccountable movements of the human will. From a shivering scalp to a whim, from a whim to a genre of art, caprice has spent five centuries naming the moment the mind leaps without asking leave of reason.
