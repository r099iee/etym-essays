smote is the old, strong past tense of smite, and the whole family descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *smēi-, "to smear, rub, daub" — a root of hands spreading grease and pigment over surfaces. In Proto-Germanic the root gave *smītaną, "to smear, daub", and the Germanic daughters keep that physical sense: Old Norse smíta means "to smear" (and also "to throw"), Old High German smīzan "to besmear, besmirch", and English preserved the "stain, defile" sense long enough for it to survive sideways in smut. The crucial semantic development happened within Germanic and came to completion in English: the sense "to throw, fling" — preserved in Middle Dutch smiten and modern German schmeißen, "to throw" — shaded into "to strike", a blow being something hurled at a body, and by about 1200 Middle English smiten meant primarily "to hit, strike, beat", the smear sense receding.

Phonetically, the past tense preserves an ancient ablaut pattern. Old English smītan was a strong verb of the first class, whose past singular was smāt — with the characteristic long ā that marks the first-class past — and this passed through Middle English smot (with the regional rounding of ā to ō) into the modern smote, pronounced with the long o of "boat" in RP /sməʊt/. The paradigm ran smite–smote–smitten by the sixteenth century, and it is one of the few old English strong verbs whose archaic forms survive consciously in literary and biblical English rather than being replaced by a weak past.

The word's use was given an enormous lease of life by the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611), where smote is the standard verb for every blow of men, angels, and the Almighty — "the angel of the LORD smote him", "thou smotest the rock", "the LORD smote the land with a plague". From the seventeenth century the participle smitten took on its own figurative career: a person smitten with blindness (the divine-affliction sense), then, by the later 1600s, smitten with love or admiration — the modern romantic idiom, in which the blow of love is sudden, irresistible, and external. The strong past smote itself fell out of everyday use after the eighteenth century and now belongs to poetry, pastiche, and elevated prose — exactly what Tolkien wants in the passage above.

In society and literature, smote carries the accumulated weight of the Bible, the sermons, and the epic poets. Its subjects are impersonal and its objects passive: conscience smites the guilty, fear smites the coward, awe smites the pilgrim, God smites the wicked. The word therefore structures a whole worldview in which powerful forces act upon weak humans, who can only receive the blow. Tolkien, as a philologist and a devout Christian, draws on this with complete awareness: in the passage above, the pang of fear and loss smites Bilbo like a judgment, and the momentary suspension of self-preservation — forgetting even to draw his sword — is exactly the paralysis of one smitten. Where struck would merely report the event, smote invests it with the impersonal, almost scriptural authority of a blow delivered from outside, dignifying a sudden empathy into an affliction visited upon the heart. It is a deliberate archaism doing the most modern work: making a moral awakening feel like a wound.
