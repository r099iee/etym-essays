The story of ascertaining is the story of the "sifted truth" — of how an agricultural metaphor of winnowing grain travelled thousands of years and several languages to become the modern verb of empirical verification.

Stage one — the PIE root

The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *krei-, meaning "to sieve, separate, distinguish, decide". In the agrarian societies of antiquity, sifting was the primal test of purity: the sieve separated valuable grain from worthless chaff, and what remained was clean, usable substance. This concrete action became the ancient world's favourite metaphor for discernment — the mind "sifts" evidence, "separates" truth from falsehood, and "decides" between alternatives.

  • Sanskrit krṇāti "to separate, kill, injure" preserves the "cut off / separate" branch of the root.
  • The "sieve" branch entered Italic and produced the Latin verb cernere "to sift, separate, perceive, distinguish", attested from the third century BCE in the comedies of Plautus.

Stage two — Latin cernere to certus

From cernere Latin derived the past participle certus — literally "sifted, sorted out", hence "fixed, settled, sure, resolved, determined". The crucial mental step is here: what has been thoroughly sifted is, by definition, settled; the "certain" is the thing no longer in question. Certus became one of Latin's most productive adjectives, feeding English certain, certify, certificate, criterion (via Greek krītērion), and secret (from sēcernere, "to set apart").

Stage three — Old French and the Norman bridge

In Gallo-Romance, certus yielded Old French certain "sure, fixed". Speakers then coined a verbal form by prefixing a- (from Latin ad- "to, toward") and adding the verbal suffix -ener: acertener / ascertener "to make sure, certify, give assurance". Manuscript spellings fluctuate between acertener, asçertener, and ascertener, the as- variant reflecting the normal Old French alternation of a-/as- before certain consonant clusters. Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman carried ascertener into the legal and administrative register of English government.

Stage four — Middle English

Middle English borrowed the verb as ascertainen in the fourteenth century. Its first attested senses are procedural: to inform someone officially, to establish a fact, claim, or title in a manner beyond challenge, to settle a debt or a limit. This was a verb of courts and chancery — making certain was something done to a fact by authority.

Stage five — the Enlightenment reversal

The great semantic turn came in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. As empirical science, travel literature, and Enlightenment rationalism reshaped European intellectual life, ascertain shifted from "to make certain by declaration" to "to discover with certainty by investigation". Bacon, Locke, and their successors promoted a model of knowledge in which facts were not proclaimed but ascertained — checked against observation. The verb's orientation flipped from outward (certifying to others) to inward (assuring oneself).

  • In the eighteenth century ascertain became a prestige word of rigorous fact-finding, used by essayists and scientists alike.
  • Jane Austen (Pride and Prejudice, 1813) applies it deliciously to the Bennet ladies observing Mr. Bingley from an upper window: genteel curiosity re-cast as miniature empiricism, with a gentle satirical charge.

Stage six — the modern register

Today ascertain and ascertaining remain formal and slightly bookish, preferred in legal, academic, and administrative prose. Everyday speech reaches instead for "find out", "determine", or "establish". The modern -ing form used in this card is the ordinary English participial/gerund inflection, continuing the verb's identity as an action of deliberate verification. The thousand-year journey from sieve to science is complete: the farmer's chaff has become the logician's doubt.
