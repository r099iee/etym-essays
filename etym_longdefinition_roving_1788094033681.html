Roving is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb rove, meaning "moving, wandering, or ranging about without a fixed course or destination; roaming restlessly from place to place, or sweeping freely over an area." In "Whenever Smaug's roving eye, seeking for him in the shadows, flashed across him," the word describes a gaze that is never still: the dragon's eye travels restlessly over every corner of the hall, searching, sweeping, and pouncing on any movement. The participle carries both motion and purposefulness — roving is wandering with an aim, however loose that aim may be.

Primary senses.

  • Of persons and animals — wandering, roaming: "a roving life," "roving bands of hunters," "roving shepherds." The subject moves freely, without a fixed itinerary, often across wild or open country; the word is warmer than wandering and more energetic than drifting.
  • Of the eyes or gaze — sweeping, searching: "a roving eye" scans a space restlessly, alighting here and there. This is the sense Tolkien uses of Smaug: an eye that roams the shadows looking for its prey. The bridge from person to eye is the same motion-word applied to the thing that moves most in a waiting body.
  • Of thoughts and fancies — straying: "a roving mind," "roving thoughts" wander from subject to subject; the mental wandering mirrors physical movement, and the transfer is so natural that it is attested from the 16th century onward.

Secondary and idiomatic senses.

  • A roving commission: since the 17th century, a military or naval commission giving an officer freedom to move and act at his own discretion rather than being tied to one post; by extension, any appointment that allows free movement and independent judgement.
  • A roving reporter / roving correspondent: a journalist who travels from place to place gathering stories instead of sitting on a desk beat — the travelling specialist, first prominent in the newspapers of the 19th century.
  • A roving ambassador: a diplomat with no fixed posting, sent wherever needed; the modern diplomatic sense of the old roving commission.
  • To have a roving eye (idiom, 20th century): to be habitually inclined to glance flirtatiously at members of the opposite sex; to be unfaithful in inclination. The idiom grew directly out of the older literal "roving eye" — a gaze that wanders — gaining a moralised, comic colouring in the 20th century.
  • Roving (of things): a "roving camera," a "roving spotlight," even "roving robots" — any device or instrument that moves about freely in search of a target; the pattern is endlessly productive.

Register and nuance.

  • Roving is literary-leaning but fully alive: wandering is the plainer word, roaming the closest synonym, and roving carries a slightly romantic or predatory energy — the freedom of the rover, the search of the hunter. Applied to a gaze it suggests alert, continuous scanning.
  • As a gerund ("his roving brought him to the coast") it names the activity itself; as an adjective it usually precedes the noun (a roving eye) but follows it in literary inversion ("an eye roving"); the derived adverb rovingly ("she glanced rovingly about") is rare but attested.
  • It takes no comparative or superlative in normal use — like most participial adjectives it is used absolutely — and it can be intensified only by adverbs (a restlessly roving eye).

Why the word means all this. The semantic history explains the bundle. The earliest English "rover" (late 15th century) was a pirate — a sea-robber who roamed the oceans looking for prey — borrowed from Middle Dutch rover "robber." Because robbers roam, the noun's habit of movement became its defining feature, and the back-formed verb rove (1530s) meant simply "to wander." From "to wander" the participle took its modern senses: a wandering person, a wandering eye, a wandering mind — and, because the first rovers were hunters of ships, a lingering predatory edge when applied to a gaze like Smaug's. The idiom "a roving eye" (20th century) is the latest layer: the wandering gaze became a moralised joke about flirtation. Every sense thus hangs on one historical equation: the robber roams, therefore roving is roaming — free, far-ranging, and faintly hungry.
