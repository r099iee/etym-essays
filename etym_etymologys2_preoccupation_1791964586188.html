Latin (1st c. BCE)
praeoccupāre

↓
Latin (1st c. CE)
praeoccupātiō
"a seizing beforehand;
rhetorical anticipation"
↓
French (17th c.)
préoccupation
"anxious concern;
mental absorption"
↓
English (16th–19th c.)
preoccupation
"the state of being engrossed;
a ruling concern"

Note: English had the word by the 1550s in the literal sense “prior occupation”; the affective and psychological senses were reinforced by French préoccupation. The Latin rhetorical noun is attested in the technical sense “anticipation of an opponent’s point”.
