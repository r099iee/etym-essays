Chain for the single root word “decrepit”: - (Non-PIE origin; debated) Latin crepāre “to rattle, creak, crack, clatter, burst with noise” (attested classical; widely treated as imitative / onomatopoeic—echoing the noise itself—with no secure PIE etymon reconstructed; state this explicitly rather than inventing a root). - Latin crepitus “a rattle, creak, crackle” (attested; → English crepitus, medical term for a grating sound in joints or lungs; also crepitare → English crepitate, crepitation). - Latin dēcrepitus “very old, infirm, worn out with age” (adjective; formed with de- “down, thoroughly, to the bottom” + crepāre; attested classical and post-classical). - Late Middle English / early modern English decrepit “worn out with age, enfeebled” (borrowed c. 14th–16th century from Latin). - Modern English decrepit “elderly and infirm; (of things) dilapidated” (the form in this note). - Derivative: English decrepitude “the state of being decrepit” (17th century, from Late Latin dēcrepitūdō). Sisters and parallels: Romance reflexes of crepāre—Italian crepare “to crack; to die (colloquial),” French crever “to burst; to die (colloquial)”—show the crack/break image sliding toward total failure. English crack and creak are Germanic sound-imitative parallels, not proven genetic cognates of crepāre; do not merge the trees. Note the debate: the imitative origin of crepāre is the standard view, but a few scholars have attempted to connect it with Indo-European “noise” verbs; there is no consensus, so the chain begins at Latin with the uncertainty stated.
