Rafters are the sloping timber members of a roof that run from the ridge (the highest point of the roof) down to the eaves (the lower overhanging edge), and together they form the structural skeleton, or framework, of a pitched roof. They are not a single beam but a series of parallel inclined pieces, spaced at regular intervals, which carry the roof covering — the tiles, slates, shingles, or thatch — and which transfer the weight of the roof down to the walls. The word here appears in the plural, and the specific literary context is David Hawkes's translation of the opening chapters of The Story of the Stone (Cao Xueqin's Dream of the Red Chamber), where "an imposing five-frame building resplendent with carved and painted beams and rafters" stands facing the guests across the courtyard. In traditional Chinese timber-framed architecture the internal frame was habitually left exposed rather than plastered over, so that beams (liang 梁) and rafters (chuan 椽, jue 桷) became natural surfaces for carving and polychrome painting. The phrase "carved and painted beams and rafters" is a stock formula of palatial description, and its presence here declares that even the hidden "bones" of the mansion — the timbers no one ever touches — have been shaped by wealth, taste, and imperial-style craftsmanship. The tone is one of architectural opulence: the house is not merely decorated but literally built out of luxury, so that every load-bearing member doubles as a work of art.

In technical and professional usage (carpentry, joinery, architecture, and building surveying) rafter is a precise term with several specialized subtypes, and a complete definition must register them:

  • Common rafters: the regularly spaced, parallel members that run from ridge to eaves and directly support the roof covering.
  • Principal rafters: the larger, heavier members (often in pairs) that carry the main load of the roof and into which the common rafters and purlins are fixed, especially in traditional timber-framed roofs.
  • Hip rafters: the diagonal rafters that run along the external angle where two sloping roof planes meet to form a hip.
  • Valley rafters: the diagonal members set along the internal angle where two sloping planes meet to form a valley.
  • Jack rafters: the shortened rafters that run from a hip or valley to the eaves or ridge, shorter than their common neighbours.
  • Purlin (related but distinct): a horizontal member running at right angles to the rafters and supporting them at mid-span; the rafter is the inclined member, the purlin the horizontal stiffener.

The rafter is thus one member of a larger assembly that also includes the ridge board, the eaves, the collar beams (which tie pairs of rafters together against spreading), and, in grand medieval roofs, hammer-beams, wind braces, and queen posts.

Beyond the carpentry term, the plural noun has generated a set of derived and idiomatic senses that any modern dictionary must list:

  • "Packed to the rafters": crowded or filled to capacity, as though an audience or a hall were so full that people were stacked up to the very beams of the roof; first recorded in the early twentieth century and now the most common everyday use of the word.
  • "The rafters rang" / "shake the rafters": a loud noise — applause, cheering, singing — so vigorous that the roof timbers vibrate; a stock image of celebration and collective joy.
  • Raftered (adjective): furnished with rafters, as in "a raftered hall" or "a low-raftered room".
  • To rafter (verb, chiefly technical): to fit or construct with rafters, to provide a roof with its framework of sloping timbers.
  • Homographic hazard — "rafter" (a raftsman): a person who rafts — who transports goods or timber on a raft, or who rides one — is also called a "rafter"; this is a different word built on raft (the float of lashed logs), which is historically a sibling of the rafter-as-beam family through the idea of a frame of lashed timbers, though in modern English the two must not be confused.

The historical bridge from "a hewn pole of a roof" to "a float of lashed logs" is worth spelling out: in Old Norse the same word, raptr, meant a long beam or roof-log, and a raft was literally a frame or bundle of such beams lashed together to carry cargo downstream — the boat is, in origin, a movable rafter-work. In ordinary modern speech "rafter" is a plain, functional word of carpentry and architecture, neutral in tone, though the idioms give it a lively secondary life. It must be distinguished from its near neighbours: a beam is any large horizontal load-bearing timber, of which a rafter is one specialized inclined kind; a joist is a horizontal member supporting a floor or ceiling; a truss is an engineered, triangulated assembly within which rafters may act as the upper chord; a girder is a very large horizontal main beam, typically of steel; a stud is a vertical wall member; and a spar is a pole in a ship's rigging. In a pitched roof the rafter is the sloping element; the whole set — ridge, rafters, purlins, and ties — is what an architect or engineer calls the roof frame.
