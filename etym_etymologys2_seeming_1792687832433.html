Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*sem-
"one; as one, together with"
(source of Latin similis, Sanskrit sama)
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*sōmiz / *sōmija-
"fitting, suitable, agreeable"
(literally 'making as one, agreeing')
↓
Old Norse (c. 8th–11th c.)
sœma
"to befit, honor, conform to"
(adjective sœmr 'becoming, fitting')
↓
Middle English (c. 1200–1300)
semen
"to be fitting; appear, look like"
(borrowed from Scandinavian during Danelaw)
↓
Middle English (c. late 14th c.)
seminge / seemynge
"outward appearance, impression"
(participial formation with suffix -inge)
↓
Modern English (16th c.–present)
seeming
"apparent, ostensible; outward appearance"

Note on historical debate: Philologists historically debated whether Middle English semen was a native survival of an unrecorded Old English *sēman (derived from sōm 'agreement, reconciliation') or a direct Scandinavian loan from Old Norse sœma. The overwhelming consensus among modern Anglicists (including the OED and Calvert Watkins) favors Old Norse origin, pointing to the word's earliest appearance in northern and East Midland texts where Anglo-Scandinavian contact was densest, and where the semantic bridge from 'fitting/becoming' to 'appearing to the eye' naturally developed.
