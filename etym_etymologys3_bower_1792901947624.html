The evolution of bower begins with being itself. The reconstructed PIE root *bheuH-, meaning to be, to exist, and by extension to dwell and to grow, is among the most fertile roots in the Indo-European family, mother to English be, booth, build, neighbour, and a hundred kin. Its Germanic branch produced Proto-Germanic *būaną, to dwell, and the noun *būr-, a dwelling-place, which entered Old English as būr, a room, hut, or chamber, cousin to German Bauer in its dwelling senses and to boor and neighbour through neighbouring paths.

In Old English būr was a humble general word: any chamber or cottage could be a būr, and the compound būrtūn, dwelling-enclosure, gives us the surname and place-name Burton scattered across England. The Norman Conquest, which replaced so much domestic vocabulary with French, paradoxically elevated this native word by confining it. As French chambre took over the grand houses, bour retreated into romance and ballad as the lady's own room, the intimate inner apartment where she kept her maidens, her needlework, and her secrets. By Chaucer's century the bower-maiden was a stock figure, and the word wore an aura of femininity and seclusion it has never lost.

From stone walls to green leaves:

  • Medieval extension — the arbour sense buds: because the lady's chamber was defined by enclosure and intimacy, speakers extended bour to any enclosed intimate place, including thickets and trained vines; the garden bower and the bedroom bower are one sheltering idea in two materials.
  • Renaissance flowering — Spenser to Milton: the great allegorists made the bower a moral landscape, from Acrasia's Bower of Bliss to Eden's shady lodge, fixing the leafy sense as the dominant one while keeping the erotic and spiritual overtones.
  • Sound shifts — gentle and regular: Old English būr lengthened and diphthongized through Middle English bour to modern bower, rhyming with power and tower, a change shared with the whole -our class; spelling wobbled between bour, boure, and bower until print fixed the last.
  • Parallel lives — anchor and trump: sailors named the bow-carried anchor the bower by position, and American card-players anglicized German Bauer, farmer-jack, into the euchre bowers; both are convergent homonyms, not descendants, yet they testify to the word's comfortable familiarity.
  • Tolkien's synthesis — Fangorn: as a philologist Tolkien knew every layer, and his living bower under the trees, complete with a soft bed, deliberately reunites the woodland shelter and the safe chamber after six centuries apart.
  • Modern survival — gardens and weddings: the bridal bower, the garden-centre arbour sold as a bower, and the fantasy hideout keep the word photosynthesizing; the chamber sense now breathes only in ballads, hymns, and historical novels.

Culturally the bower encodes the human hunger for enclosure within nature: not the house against the wild, but the wild trained into a house. Its etymology, reaching back to the verb for existence itself, suggests why the image moves us so deeply: a bower is being-at-home raised to poetry, dwelling with the roof turned to leaves.
