Croydon is a large town in South London — historically a market town in Surrey, since 1965 the centre of the London Borough of Croydon — lying some ten miles south of Charing Cross in the upper Wandle valley below the North Downs. In the source sentence — Ruskin tracing his mother’s line no further than my maternal grandmother was the landlady of the Old King’s Head in Market Street, Croydon — it is homely, unroyal ground for a self-confessed Tory: not Windsor or Versailles but a coaching inn in a Surrey market town, kept by a widowed landlady whose painted sign (Simone Memmi’s King’s Head, which Ruskin wishes he could paint for her) is the only crown in the family. The capital C and the inn-frame mark a proper noun; no common-noun meaning is intended, though the place-name’s old sense, saffron valley, hovers faintly behind market gardens and fairs.

As a place today Croydon is among Britain’s largest district centres: a medieval market and archiepiscopal manor grown through coaching, railways and commerce into a modern city of offices, shops, trams and towers. The Old King’s Head in Market Street belonged to coaching Croydon — the Brighton road halt where quails, travellers and carriers changed horses. Victorian Croydon added the railway (among the world’s first public lines, 1803 Surrey Iron Railway, then London & Croydon Railway 1839), waterworks, schools (Whitgift, Old Palace), andRuskin’s own suburban villas; twentieth-century Croydon added Airport (London’s first, 1920-1959), office Boom (No. 1 Croydon, the 50p building), shopping (Whitgift Centre), and post-war diversity that makes it one of London’s most cosmopolitan boroughs.

  • Head sense: the South London town and borough: e.g. to live in Croydon, Market Street Croydon, the Borough of Croydon. Always capitalised, usually without article except in the Croydon area. The semantic content is purely referential — pointing to streets, tram stops, courts and postcodes (CR0) — with connotations shifting by era: coaching halt, railway suburb, office city, diverse metropolis.
  • Historical-administrative senses: parish, manor, archbishop’s palace, airport, diocese: Croydon Palace (summer residence of Archbishops of Canterbury for 500 years), Croydon Minster (Perpendicular parish church of St John), Croydon Airport (Great War aerodrome turned 1920s air port), Croydon North / South / Central (parliamentary seats). Each names an institution housed in the town; together they map its growth from episcopal manor to transport hub.
  • Market and inn sense (Ruskin’s sense): Old King’s Head, Market Street evokes coaching Croydon: fairs, corn-market, butchers’ shambles, carriers’ yards. A landlady of such a house was a substantial bourgeoise — brewing, stabling, chambering — not gentry yet respectable. Ruskin’s wish to paint her a Simone Martini (he writes Memmi) King’s Head for a sign fuses Sienese altarpiece with Surrey inn-sign, affection condescending only in period manner.
  • Name-origin sense: saffron valley (or crooked valley): antiquaries gloss Croydon as Old English croh (saffron, crocus) + denu (valley): the valley where saffron was grown. Market gardeners still grew watercress and lavender in the Wandle meadows when Ruskin’s grandmother kept her inn. The alternative crooked valley is now less favoured but still cited; both agree the second element is valley and the spelling -don is Downs-like analogy, not hill.
  • Modern extended senses: Croydonisation, Croydon facelift: planners speak of Croydonisation (1960s office-tower redevelopment) and surgeons of a Croydon facelift (rhyming slang: a severe pulled-back facelift, from Croydon + face-lift? Actually from tight ponytail associated with Croydon). Both are jocular-derogatory and show the name’s metropolitan currency beyond geography.
