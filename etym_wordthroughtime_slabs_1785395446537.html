- 14th–15th c., earliest English sense: a large, flat mass of stone hewn or split with broad parallel faces — the material of paving, altars, and tomb covers. Connotation: heavy, solid, monumental, mortuary.
- 16th–17th c.: the word widens from stone to any thick flat piece of solid material — wood, metal, slate, cakes of soap or chocolate. Connotation: still weighty and blocklike, now also the plain everyday stuff of counters and cutting surfaces.
- 19th–20th c.: figurative and colloquial extensions — a 'slab' of meat or bread on the plate, a cricket 'slab' (bat), a surfboard. Connotation: shifts toward the hefty-but-familiar, shedding solemnity while gaining bodily, workaday weight.
- Modern register: concrete, inert, and deliberately flat; 'slab' evokes an unworked plate-like mass rather than a shaped or precious object — the heavy raw material rather than the finished thing.
