wisp: The history of wisp is humble and faint — fittingly, for a word that names the faintest of things. It appears in English in the late thirteenth century as wispe, “a handful or bundle of hay, grass, or twigs,” used for burning, for cleaning, for kindling, or to sit upon: a twist of dried vegetation, cheap, disposable, and alive only when lit. Its origin is uncertain: it is probably native English, from an unrecorded Old English word cognate with Norwegian and Swedish visp, “a wisp,” and the whole Scandinavian–English cluster is itself of unknown origin — no deeper Indo-European root for wisp itself can be securely reconstructed. Etymology has nonetheless fished for relatives. A connection to whisk (from Old Norse visk, “a wisp of hay to sweep with”) is often proposed and as often strenuously denied; whisk itself descends from Proto-Germanic *wisk-, “to sweep, to wipe,” which some scholars trace to PIE *weis-, “to turn, to twist” — a root that would make the wisp a twisted thing, a twist of straw (Sanskrit veṣka- means “noose,” a twisted cord, and Czech vechet is a “wisp of straw”). But every step of that chain beyond the Germanic sweep-verb is speculative, and wisp’s own link to it is the most doubtful link of all; the honest verdict is that wisp is a Germanic word of unknown ancestry.

Its semantic career is a slow levitation. In Middle English the wisp remained material — a torch, a pad, a brush — and even the will-o’-the-wisp (1590s) is named for the bundle of burning straw the marsh-fire was imagined to be: “Will with the wisp,” a traveller with his torch, later a demon light leading men into bogs, and finally, by 1660, a metaphor for anything that deludes and misleads. Only in 1836 does wisp detach from hay entirely and float free: the dictionaries record “thin, filmy portion” — a wisp of smoke, of hair, of cloud. From then on the word lived in romantic and gothic prose as the quasi-material filament that seems to dissolve even as it appears, barely a presence at all. Vance’s sentence exploits precisely that last, weightless stage: the wisp of white is a thread of nothing that will wax into a pillar of black smoke — the ghost-material of the modern sense, still faintly tinged with the folk-treachery of the wisp-light that lures travellers to their doom. The word has never lost the memory of its first meaning — a handful of straw, dry, cheap, and quick to burn — which is why it still feels fibrous, twisted, and a little dangerous.

waxed: The verb wax is a native Germanic strong verb whose roots reach back to the PIE root *h₂ewg-, “to increase, grow, strengthen” — the same root that produced Sanskrit ójas, “vital strength,” Greek aúxō, “to increase,” and Latin augēre, “to increase,” the ancestor of augment, auction, auxiliary, and august. The Germanic branch added an s-extension, giving Proto-Germanic *wahsijaną, “to grow,” and from it Old English weaxan, attested from the 8th century: a strong verb (weaxan, wēox, weaxen) used of growing crops, swelling fortunes, and the increasing light of the moon. The pairing with its antonym wane (Old English wanian, from PIE *h₁wenh₁-, “to lessen”) is ancient; the two were already a fixed pair in Old English, and the idiom wax and wane — the great lunar rhythm of increase and decrease — has survived, largely alone, into modern English, where the bare verb wax in its growth-sense is otherwise archaic.

The verb has three recorded careers. First, the literal growth-sense: in early English wax is used of plants, of the sea, of the body, of illness and of anger (his wrath waxed hot). Second, the lunar-astronomical sense: to wax is to grow in apparent size toward fullness, the moon’s filling, always set against waning — a sense that kept the word alive through the scientific revolution and into modern astronomy. Third, the copular sense of “becoming,” preserved in phrases like waxed eloquent, waxed lyrical, and waxed indignant (recorded from the 18th century), where the verb means “grew or became (in some manner)” and lends the sentence a stately, archaic cadence. The King James Bible (1611) had fixed the prophetic register — “waxed great,” “waxed strong and mighty” — giving the verb an elevation plain grew lacks. Modern English has regularized the past to waxed (the old wōx and waxen having died out except in archaic use), and the word survives mainly in the lunar idiom, in poetic prose, and in the frozen phrase waxed eloquent. In Vance’s sentence the verb carries its full archaic charge: the wisp of white waxes to a pillar of boiling black smoke with the smooth, fated, organic inevitability of the moon filling toward fullness — growth that cannot be stopped, the ancient verb of increase at its most majestic.
