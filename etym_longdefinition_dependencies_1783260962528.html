dependencies (noun, plural) — In Macaulay's History of England the plural noun appears in the imperial frame "in two important dependencies of the crown", where the two are the North American colonies and Ireland. The nuance is political-geographical and administrative: a dependency is a territory subordinate to a governing state — bound to the crown, subject to its authority, and reliant upon it for protection and administration, yet not fully part of the home realm. The phrase "of the crown" sharpens the legal relationship: these lands belong to the monarchical state as possessions, their allegiance running to the crown rather than to Parliament's united kingdom. The tone is detached, grave and managerial — Macaulay surveys imperial hierarchy from above, coolly noting which members of the empire were sound and which "withered and distorted". The word carries an implicit asymmetry of power and a sense of structural subordination, and in this sentence it sets up the tragedy: the two dependencies, mishandled, either break away (America) or fester (Ireland).

The core dictionary sense is a country or territory controlled by, or reliant upon, another state. But dependency is a strikingly polyvalent word in modern English, and its senses radiate from a single image — the picture of something hanging down from a support (Latin dependēre, "to hang down from") — into very different domains:

  • Political / imperial dependency. A territory or colony subordinate to a governing state. This is the colonial sense used by Macaulay and the administrators of empire, and it survives today in the constitutional label "Crown Dependencies" for the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands — self-governing but not part of the United Kingdom.
  • Economic dependency. Reliance of one economy, region, or household upon another for trade, capital, or income: "economic dependency", "dependency on foreign oil". In development economics, dependency theory of the 1960s argued that the periphery stays poor precisely through its structural dependence on the industrial centre.
  • Addiction / chemical dependency. The clinical sense: drug dependency, alcohol dependency, dependency syndrome — physical and psychological reliance on a substance. This is now the most common everyday use of the singular, firmly established in twentieth-century medicine.
  • Dependency in computing. A software package, library, or service that another program requires in order to run: "install the dependencies", "dependency injection", "dependency graph". Build systems and package managers resolve these relationships automatically.
  • Grammatical dependency. In philology and in dependency grammar, a word or clause that depends on another — a subordinate construction. The term has a long technical history in linguistics.
  • Demographic dependency ratio. In population statistics, the ratio of the economically inactive (children and the aged) to those of working age — a standard measure used in planning pensions and social services.
  • Mathematical / logical dependence. A relation of one-way or mutual determination between variables, propositions, or nodes — one thing being functionally or logically conditioned by another.
  • Abstract / psychological dependency. Emotional or financial reliance on another person: "co-dependency", "a sense of dependency". This is common in psychology and self-help literature.

The historical bridge that connects all these senses is the hanging image. Latin dependēre meant "to hang down from": a branch hangs from a tree, a child hangs on its mother. To depend on something is to hang from it — to be suspended by it and supported through it. That physical picture carried over, in the medieval and early modern periods, into logic (a conclusion "depends on" its premises), into grammar (a subordinate clause "depends on" the main clause), and then into politics (a dependency "hangs from" the crown). The political sense is among the oldest English uses of the noun: dependency is attested in the sixteenth century, and the territorial plural dependencies settles into administrative English in the eighteenth century, exactly the vocabulary of empire that Macaulay commands.

Register and collocation: dependencies (plural, territorial) is formal, administrative, and now chiefly historical or legal. Collocations include crown dependencies, colonial dependencies, overseas dependencies, a dependency of the crown, dependent territories. In Macaulay the plural does quiet dramatic work: it names the two territories that, mishandled, either broke away or festered — the two "withered and distorted" members of the imperial body politic.
