Single root word: SLOPES (plural of the noun slope). The chain runs from an Indo-European root of slipping, through a Proto-Germanic verb, a reanalysis of an Old English past participle, and the formation of the noun in the early modern period. Proto-Indo-European *sleubʰ- "to slide, slip" (reconstructed base; ~4500–3000 BCE; the root of slippery-motion words; Latin lūbricus "slippery," whence English lubricate and lubricious, is a cognate formation) → Proto-Germanic *slūpaną "to slip, slide, creep" (reconstructed strong verb; ~1st millennium BCE) → Old English slūpan "to slip, glide" (attested; with the prefix ā- in āslūpan "to slip away, escape") → Old English past participle slopen "slipped, having slipped" — reanalysed as a new base verb (the OED judges this participle-reanalysis "probable" rather than certain) → Middle English slope (verb) "to flee, run away, slink off" (recorded mid-15th c.) → English slope (adjective) "slanting, oblique" (recorded c. 1500, from the participle) and slope (noun) "inclined ground, declivity" (recorded 1610s) → Modern English SLOPES (the regular plural of the noun, recorded throughout the modern period). Note on debate: the derivation of the verb slope from the old past participle slopen is the standard account but is not absolutely certain — the OED describes it as probable rather than proved. The root must not be confused with the parallel Indo-European base *sleipʰ-, which produced Proto-Germanic *slippaną "to slip" and hence English slip and slippery; the two are separate formations in the same semantic field, and "slope" derives from *sleubʰ- alone. The Germanic family is well attested: Gothic sliupan "to slip, creep" appears in Wulfila's fourth-century Gothic Bible; Old High German sliofan / sliefen "to slip, creep" continues into German schliefen "to slip into" and schlüpfen "to slip in/out, hatch"; Old Saxon slūpan "to slip" runs parallel in Continental West Germanic.
