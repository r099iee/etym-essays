Improvidence is the noun for lack of foresight, thrift or provision for the future: the habit or act of failing to save, store, plan or prepare, and hence wastefulness, carelessness and short-sightedness. Primitive improvidence in the chapter heading means the supposed condition of early humans who did not store food or plan ahead — the starting point from which beginnings of provision, hunting, herding, domestication and agriculture develop.

Morally the word is censorious. To call someone improvident is to blame them: they spend what they should save, eat seed-corn, neglect insurance, beget children they cannot support. Victorian political economy made improvidence a vice of the poor; Malthus, Smiles and charity reports contrasted provident artisans (savings banks, allotments) with improvident labourers (drink, debt). Anthropology inverted the judgement: what looks like improvidence — sharing meat immediately, refusing to hoard — may be rational generosity or mobility, not failure. The heading Primitive improvidence thus already encodes an evolutionist ladder from improvident hunting to provident farming.

Grammatically improvidence is uncountable (much improvidence) but pluralizes for acts (improvidences of youth). It takes of or in: improvidence in expenditure, improvidence of peasants. Its adjective is improvident, adverb improvidently. It is the exact negation of providence (foresight, divine care) and prudence, though providence also means God, giving improvidence a faint theological shadow: to be improvident is to act as if no Providence provides.

  • Sense 1 — Economic: failure to save or store: Spending wages at once, failing to keep winter stores, neglecting savings. Example: His improvidence left the family destitute in old age. Bridge: Latin pro- ahead + videre to see → providence foresight → im- not + providence. Direct privative formation.
  • Sense 2 — Managerial: lack of planning: Improvidence in government, military improvidence. Means no reserves, no contingencies, no maintenance. The chapter's beginnings of provision answer this: hunting caches, herds, granaries as cures.
  • Sense 3 — Moral-character: thriftlessness as vice: Improvidence as improvident character, akin to prodigality but weaker: not splendid waste but heedless neglect. Victorian sense links drink, gambling, early marriage.
  • Sense 4 — Anthropological-technical: non-accumulation: Primitive improvidence as alleged stage before storage economy. Modern ethnography reframes it: immediate-return systems (Hadza sharing, Inuit meat division) are not improvident but adapted to perishability and mobility. The term survives in historiography of the idea.
  • Near-contrasts: Improvidence differs from imprudence (rash judgement generally), from extravagance (lavish spending), from negligence (careless omission broadly) and from myopia (short-sightedness metaphorically). It specifically concerns future provision.

Today improvidence is rare-formal (Tier 4-5), kept alive in history of economic thought, insurance law (contributory improvidence) and moralizing headlines, while everyday speech uses short-sightedness, living hand-to-mouth, failure to plan. Its power in the heading is architectural: it names the problem that civilization — cooking, herding, agriculture, food storage — will solve.
