- Antiquity (Classical Latin) - The neuter singular *pirum* (plural *pira*) was cultivated across Roman orchards and documented by Pliny; reanalyzed in Late/Vulgar Latin as a feminine singular noun *pira*.
- 4th to 5th Century (Proto-West Germanic) - Borrowed early from Vulgar Latin by Germanic tribes along the Rhine as *pera / *piru, adopting Roman pomological techniques.
- 8th to 11th Century (Old English) - Established in Anglo-Saxon England as *pere* or *peru* (weak plural *peran*); documented in medical leeches (the *Lacnunga*) and monastic orchard boundaries.
- 12th to 15th Century (Middle English) - Transitions to strong plural inflection *peres* or *peeres*; Chaucer features pear trees famously in *The Merchant's Tale*; dried pears become a staple winter survival ration.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - Spelled *peares* and *pears*; Shakespeare references varieties like the "poperin pear" in *Romeo and Juliet*; pearwood becomes prized for woodcuts and lutherie.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Celebrated in Victorian literature (including Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*); European pomologists breed dozens of prized dessert cultivars ('Bartlett', 'Comice', 'Bosc').
- Mid-20th Century to Present - Develops the widespread British colloquial idiom *to go pear-shaped* (to go disastrously wrong), while retaining global agricultural, gemological, and culinary prominence.
