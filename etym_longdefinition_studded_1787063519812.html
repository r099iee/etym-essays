Studded is the past participle and adjective of the verb stud: to set or cover a surface with studs. A stud, in the relevant sense, is a projecting knob, nail, boss, or ornamental head — anything with a visible raised head fixed into a surface. In the source passage the brim of a leather helm is "studded about ... with white gems": the gems are fixed into the leather so that they stand out from it, decorating and at the same time stiffening the rim. From that single image flows the whole range of the word's senses, literal and figurative.

Core sense — decorated with raised points. Studded things are covered or dotted with projecting elements: a shield studded with brass bosses, a belt or leather collar studded with metal roundheads, a door studded with iron nails (the studs give strength to the wood and later became decorative), boots studded with hobnails. In leatherwork and fashion, "studded" describes jackets, collars, and jewellery set with metal pyramids, gems, or crystals — a style that runs from punk rock's studded leather to couture beading.

  • Figurative scattering. Because studs are scattered across a surface, studded has come to mean "dotted or sprinkled with prominent things": a sky studded with stars, a coastline studded with islands, a speech studded with quotations, an evening studded with celebrities. Here the decorating objects need not be literal nails — the word is a pure metaphor of distribution.
  • Technical studs (same word family). In building, a stud is the vertical timber or metal post in a wall frame ("stud walls"), from the Old English sense of a post or prop; in vehicles, wheel studs are the threaded bolts holding a wheel on; studded tyres carry metal or rubber pins for ice. All descend from the "post/peg" idea of a projecting piece.
  • Defensive and military flavour. A studded surface is often associated with protection or menace: studded armour, studded gauntlets, spiked and studded weapons in fantasy. The projecting points make a surface resistant as well as decorated.
  • Register. "Studded" is used in plain factual description (a studded tyre), in ornamented literary prose (studded with stars), and in fashion commentary; it is emphatically visual, describing things you can picture glinting or protruding from a surface.

Historical bridge and homophones. The decorative stud is historically the same word as the builder's stud: Old English studu meant a post or prop, and the projecting head of a nail, being post-like, carried the name across. A separate word — Old English stōd, a herd of breeding horses — gives the modern stud (a breeding stallion, and colloquially an attractive man) and is not related to studded; the two are homophones with completely different histories. Keeping the three studs apart is essential for reading older texts, where stud may be a wall post, a nail head, or a horse herd.
