Undulates, the third-person singular of undulate, describes in its core physical sense anything that moves or lies in smooth, continuous waves: a flag rippling in wind, a snake flowing across sand, a road rising and falling over dunes, or, as in the source sentence, a mathematical surface that undulates in space like waves on a pond. The subject need not move at all; a landscape can undulate simply by having the shape of waves frozen in place.

In the source sentence the word belongs to the geometry of perception: a surface called ripple appears flat yet undulates in space like waves on a pond, rising and dipping in gentle curves that the eye misreads. Here undulates is precise rather than poetic: it names the exact waveform geometry, smooth periodic rise and fall, that makes the illusion work, and it borrows the pond only to let the reader feel the mathematics.

Core meaning in detail: To undulate is to follow a sine-like path, up and down or side to side, without sharp corners: sound waves undulate through air, eels undulate through water by bending the whole body, crowds undulate when a Mexican wave runs round a stadium. Grammarians note the form: undulates with final -s marks third-person singular present, he, she, or it undulates, the snake undulates, the surface undulates. The noun undulation names the motion or the shape, and the adjective undulating describes anything wavy, from hills to hairstyles.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To move in waves (primary dynamic sense, 17th c.): flags, snakes, swimmers, and sound all undulate when they propagate by successive curves. The bridge is direct from Latin undula, a little wave: to undulate is to behave like water.
  • To have a wavy form while at rest (static sense): rolling prairies undulate, and so do corrugated roofs and the source sentence's rippling surface. The bridge freezes the motion into shape: the land keeps the memory of waves without the water.
  • To rise and fall in sound or feeling (transferred sense): voices undulate in chant, music undulates between loud and soft, and emotions undulate between hope and fear. The bridge carries the waveform from space into pitch and mood: any smooth oscillation borrows the water's verb.
  • To fluctuate in quantity or intensity (figurative, modern): prices, temperatures, and approval ratings undulate through the year. The bridge runs through graphs: a plotted line that waves like water gives the market the motion of the sea.
  • Wave motion in physics (technical sense): light, sound, and quantum fields are described as undulating, and 17th-century natural philosophers set undulation against emission in theories of light. The bridge is the founding metaphor of wave physics itself.
  • Wavy ornament in art and anatomy (specialised): drapery undulates in Baroque painting, and the colon's walls undulate in peristalsis. The bridge is descriptive economy: one verb serves the painter's eye and the physician's gut alike.

Register and connotation: undulates is elegant and Latinate, the cultivated cousin of blunt waves or wobbles. Scientists use it for precision, poets for music: the syllables themselves rise and fall, un-du-lates, like the motion they name. It never hurries and never jerks; things that undulate are graceful even when they menace, like the python crossing the path in a single flowing curve.
