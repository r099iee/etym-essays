**SKRIKING: the hard northern cousin of shriek** The biography of skriking begins in pure sound. The word descends from one of the most purely onomatopoeic bases in Indo-European, the piercing-cry root *skrei- / *sker-, built from an initial sibilant and a harsh velar to recreate the acoustics of a sharp, grating shriek. It is a family, not a single line: the same acoustic template produced shriek, screech, screak, and the name of the shrike bird, and every member of the family carries the same hard, piercing sound at its core. **From Proto-Germanic to the Danelaw** Proto-Germanic shaped the root into the verb *skrīkōnan, "to screech, cry out shrilly," and the Scandinavian dialects produced Old Norse skrækja and skríkja, "to screech, twitter, cry out," together with skrikja, a noun for "a shrieker" — the shrike. Old English possessed its own reflex, scric, the name of the shrike or thrush, literally "the bird with a shrill call," which survived to give Modern English shrike (1540s), the butcher-bird that impales its prey on thorns. Under heavy Scandinavian influence in the Danelaw — the region of northern and eastern England settled by Norse speakers — Middle English formed scriken or skriken (c. 1200) for any piercing outcry. The word was born in the north, and it has never entirely left the north. **The great dialectal split of the 16th century** Thereafter the word divided. The southern, mainstream form was reshaped in the 16th century into shriek — the initial cluster softened from /skr-/ to /ʃr-/, a smoothing that produced the familiar, softer, more French-flavoured word that standard English settled on. But the harsher northern form skrike lived on in the dialects and Scots of Northern England, meaning "to shriek, to weep loudly, to complain," its hard /k/ much closer to the Old Norse original. The two words are the same word split by dialect: the standard shriek is the smoothed, southern development, while skrike is the northern relic that kept the Scandinavian grit. Where shriek went up to London and into the polite printed page, skrike stayed in the farmyard, the mill, and the northern ballad, carrying its ancient hard sound through five centuries of dialect speech. **Parallel continental formations** West Germanic produced its own parallel imitative words from the same template — Old Frisian skrichta, "to screech," and Middle Dutch schrien, "to shriek" — which entered English as screech (1570s, an alteration of earlier scritch) and screak (c. 1500). These are independent coinages of the same acoustic type, not descendants of the Scandinavian verb, and their existence shows how powerfully the sound of a piercing cry shapes human words. Screech, screak, and skrike are all built on the same harsh sibilant-plus-velar skeleton, and all name the same grating, high-pitched noise. **Retreat to the dialect shelf** By the twentieth century skrike was all but extinct in standard English, surviving only as a provincial relic — a word heard in northern dialect writing and remembered in ballads but odd, even comic, in polite London usage. Its archaic, Norse-edged texture was exactly what made it valuable to the philologists and literary revivalists of the early twentieth century, who mined the older strata of the language for epic effect. **Tolkien's philological choice** This is why Tolkien, the philologist, chose the present participle skriking for the goblin cacophony of *The Hobbit*: the archaic word itself sounds like something dragged up from the old northern darkness, giving the mob's terror the deep patina of Norse and Old English heroic verse. In the catalogue "yells and yammering, croaking, jibbering and jabbering; howls, growls and curses; shrieking and skriking," the final pair doubles the high-pitched register — the standard shriek and its hard northern cousin sounding together — so that the passage ends on the harshest, oldest, most piercing note in the whole mob's noise. The word is not merely a synonym for shrieking; it is shrieking as the Scandinavian north would have cried it, the very sound of ancient terror. **The participle today** Skriking now reads as deliberately archaic, dialectal, and literary — a word revived for texture rather than used in living speech. Its -ing form keeps the action continuous and ongoing, exactly what the goblin catalogue needs: not one shriek but a sustained, piercing, grating chorus, the whole cavern crying out at the top of the northern darkness. It remains the hard, northern, truly old cousin of shriek.
