Strayed is the past tense and past participle of stray: to wander off the right road — feet leaving the path, livestock leaving the pasture, hands drifting where the mind has not sent them, thoughts sliding from the subject, souls sliding from virtue. At its heart is always the same image, inherited from its Latin ancestor: wandering outside (extra) the bounds where one belongs.

In the source sentence — ‘His hand strayed to the hilt of his small sword’ — the verb does quiet, chilling work. The hand moves as if uncommanded, drifting of its own accord toward the weapon while the mouth makes promises about the Precious — and the reader understands before the speaker does that the body has already chosen violence. Tolkien picks strayed rather than moved or went precisely because straying is unintentional-seeming: the drift that betrays the desire its owner will not admit.

Core meaning in detail: To stray is to deviate without (necessarily) intending rebellion: the sheep strays through a gap, the traveller strays at an unmarked fork, the eye strays to the clock, the hand strays to the hilt. Intention can be absent, divided, or concealed — which is why the verb serves novelists so well for self-betrayal. The grammar marks the territory: one strays from the path, the flock, the subject, or the straight and narrow, and strays into danger, error, or forbidden places.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To wander from the path or pasture (primary, oldest sense): the sheep had strayed through the broken fence. The bridge is the root itself: Latin extra vagari, to wander outside — livestock outside its bounds is the founding picture of every later straying.
  • To drift idly — hands, eyes, fingers (Tolkien's sense): her fingers strayed over the keys, his eyes strayed to the window. The bridge runs from whole-body wandering to part-body wandering: a hand can go astray exactly as a sheep can, and the verb lends the small drift the same unowned, accidental quality.
  • To wander in thought or speech — minds and conversations: my attention strayed, the lecture strayed off topic. The bridge is metaphor's oldest road: thinking is travelling, so a mind can leave its path as surely as feet — straying thoughts, stray remarks, conversations gone astray.
  • To err morally — the theological sense: he had strayed from the path of righteousness, the strayed sheep of the parable, the straying husband. The bridge runs through Scripture: the lost sheep, the prodigal's wandering, the hymn's prone to wander — so that adultery itself is euphemised as straying, sin figured as leaving the flock.
  • Stray projectiles and currents (technical senses): a stray bullet, stray voltage, a stray dog. The bridge is outcome: whatever has left its intended course and now endangers or puzzles — the bullet without a target, the current without a wire, the dog without a master — is a stray, the adjective frozen from the verb's completed act.
  • The noun — a stray (secondary): the shelter took in three strays. The bridge is zero-derivation twice removed: the act (to stray) gives the adjective (stray animal), which hardens into the noun — the wanderer itself, usually feline, canine, or human and usually pitied.

Register and connotation: strayed is gentle as verbs of error go — it wanders where trespassed invades and sinned condemns. The straying sheep is lost rather than wicked, the straying eye merely human, the straying hand disavowed by its owner. Only the moral sense carries real charge, and even there the word softens: to say a spouse strayed is to choose the kindest available verb for the cruellest fact. Poets love it for exactly this mercy — hands, eyes, and hearts are always straying somewhere in verse — while engineers use it coldly for whatever current or shot has left its appointed channel.
