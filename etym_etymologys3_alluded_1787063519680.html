Games at the root. The verb allude is a Roman import. In classical Latin, lūdere meant “to play” — games, sport, music, stage-playing — and its derivative allūdere (with the prepositional prefix ad-, “to, toward”) meant “to play toward or at”, hence “to jest at, to make mock of, to touch upon playfully”. The deeper Indo-European ancestry of lūdere is uncertain: a root *leid- (“to play, jest”) is often reconstructed, but some scholars connect the Latin verb instead with Greek loidoréō (“to revile, abuse”), which would give allūdere an original colour of mocking attack. What is certain is that the Roman word for play, lūdus, came to embrace everything from gladiatorial shows to schoolroom exercises, and that allūdere kept the light, teasing quality of the verb it was built from.

The English entrance: a mocking verb. English borrowed allude in the 1530s, and the earliest uses are hostile or playful: to “allude” was to mock, to make sport of, to jest at a person. The past form alluded accordingly first recorded “made mock of, derided”. This is the shadow side of the word that modern speakers have entirely forgotten — the allusion as a game played at someone’s expense.

The great shift: from jest to hint. Within a generation, under the influence of French alluder and of the metaphor of “playing upon” a subject without grasping it, the verb pivoted. By the late 16th century allude meant “to refer to indirectly, to touch upon in passing”, and alluded became the workhorse past form of oblique reference. The old mocking sense died out, but it left a trace: the allusion is still a game in the sense of being a subtle, chosen, self-aware gesture rather than a plain statement.

Fixation in the age of rhetoric. The 17th and 18th centuries, obsessed with correctness, fixed the collocation allude to and sharpened the boundary between allude (indirect) and mention or refer (direct). Rhetoricians treated allusion as a figure of speech: the speaker points, the audience completes. This is precisely the modern mechanics of the word — an allusion works only if the target is recognisable, which is why to be “alluded to” in a text implies cultural weight.

The literary age and the novel. In the 19th century, with classical education at its height and the novel dominating letters, alluded flourished in criticism and fiction alike. Authors alluded to the Bible, to Shakespeare, to Homer; critics tracked those echoes. The word became part of the vocabulary of intertextuality before that term existed.

Modern usage: diplomacy, journalism, and the echoing page. Today alluded is regular, formal, and slightly courtly. It is used when direct statement is inconvenient or indelicate:

  • Journalism and politics — “the minister alluded to the pending inquiry”, i.e., pointed without confirming.
  • Literary criticism — “the poem alluded to the Fall”, i.e., echoed an earlier text.
  • Everyday speech — “he kept alluding to my new job without asking about it”, i.e., circling a subject.

Form and sound. Alluded is fully regular (allude – alluded), pronounced /əˈluːdɪd/, and takes to before its object. It belongs to a small family built on Latin ludere: illusion (originally “a mocking or deceiving play”), delude, elude, collude, and the modern ludic. The Tolkien context — “no songs had alluded to him even in the obscurest way” — uses the verb with its full literary charge: the absence of even the faintest allusion leaves the hobbit uncelebrated, unnamed, barely present in the world’s song.
