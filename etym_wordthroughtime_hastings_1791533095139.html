- Old English — Hæstingas is the name of a small folk-group on the Sussex coast, “the people of Hæsta.” It is a local, unremarkable name, recorded in charters and in the Burghal Hidage as Hæstingaceaster, the fortified place of the Hæstingas.
- 1066 — The name is attached to a battle fought several miles inland. From this point on, “Hastings” means the Norman victory as much as the town, and English schoolroom history begins to treat the two as inseparable.
- 12th–13th c. — Hastings flourishes as a Cinque Port; the name suggests shipping, privileges and the cross-Channel wine trade. The abbey at Battle promotes the battlefield as a pilgrimage site.
- 14th–15th c. — French raids and the silting of the harbour reduce the town; the name begins to suggest a decayed port, a place of lost importance.
- 16th–18th c. — Hastings is a small fishing and smuggling town. In antiquarian writing the name still primarily means the battle, and the town is a footnote to 1066.
- 19th c. — The railway and the fashion for sea-bathing turn Hastings into a respectable resort; the name acquires a Victorian, slightly genteel seaside colour, while the battle remains a schoolbook staple.
- 20th c. — “1066 Country” tourism, the annual re-enactment and the abbey ruins make “Hastings” a heritage brand; at the same time the name is exported to New Zealand, Nebraska and Barbados.
- 21st c. — The town markets itself on both its fishing fleet and its battlefield; “Hastings” in British public life most often still means 1066, but locally it means the pier, the net shops and the East Hill.
