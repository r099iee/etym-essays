exuded — the past tense of exude: to discharge or emit gradually through pores, small openings, or the very substance of a thing; to ooze out slowly like sweat, sap, gum, or resin; and, by a famous extended metaphor, to radiate or give off a quality, atmosphere, or aura in such a way that the quality seems to seep from the thing's very being rather than to be projected by an act of will.

The Latin ancestor exsūdāre means precisely 'to sweat out' (ex-, 'out,' + sūdāre, 'to sweat'), and the word's power lies in that physiological image. What is exuded is not emitted deliberately; it is perspired — passed passively through the surface as a by-product of the body's inner state, like moisture forced through skin. In the passage this etymology is brilliantly exploited: the bright carvings, standing immobile all day, with their fantastic shadows shifting and elongating on the wall behind them hour by hour, 'exuded a kind of darkness for all their colour.' The verb performs a paradox — things saturated with colour give off darkness — and it does so by choosing a word of slow, involuntary, vaporous release rather than of deliberate projection. The darkness is not cast like a shadow (the shadows are separately described, moving on the wall); it sweats out of the very substance of the objects, a faint, constant, faintly uncanny emanation that clings to the air around them.

Primary senses

  • Physical — of fluids and matter: sap, resin, gum, latex, moisture, or pus exudes from a tree, a wound, a gland, or a rock; the process is slow, steady, and passive, a seepage through a surface.
  • Of the body: sweat exudes through the pores; the verb keeps its ancient link to perspiration (Latin sūdor, 'sweat').
  • Technical (botany, physiology, medicine): plant exudates (resins, gums, latex) and medical exudates (fluid that seeps through vessel walls into tissue or a wound) keep the physical sense alive in scientific prose; exudation is the process, an exudate the thing exuded.

Secondary senses (figurative)

  • Of qualities and attitudes: to exude confidence, calm, charm, authority, menace, or (here) darkness — to give off an inner state as the body gives off moisture, involuntarily and without effort. 'She exuded confidence' is the modern flagship use; the metaphor retains the old physiology: what is exuded is not said but perspired.
  • Grammatical behaviour: transitive ('the diplomat exuded confidence') and intransitive ('sap exudes from the cut stem'); the object of the transitive figure is always a quality, mood, or atmosphere.

Semantic boundaries with near-synonyms

  • Emit is the neutral, general verb — anything may be sent forth, actively or passively, with no bodily image; exude is always the body's slow confession.
  • Radiate implies light or energy streaming outward with force, brighter and more deliberate; exude is slow, passive, involuntary.
  • Emanate is a neutral streaming-forth with no body; exude keeps the pore-level intimacy of seepage.
  • Ooze is the colloquial, earthy cousin — thick, visible liquid; exude is formal and can be wholly figurative, governing charm and darkness as well as sap.
  • Discharge is abrupt, violent, or final; exude is gradual and steady.

In the passage

  • The word gives the carvings a spectral, almost organic agency: they breathe darkness as a body breathes moisture. One can feel the slow pressure of the secret darkness working its way through the pores of the painted stone, hour by hour, with the sun's rotation — an emanation co-existing with, and quietly cancelling, all their brightness.
