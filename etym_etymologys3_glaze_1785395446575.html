The biography of glaze opens five thousand years ago in a small, shining Proto-Indo-European root, *ǵʰel-, meaning simply 'to shine, shimmer, glow'. From that single syllable the daughter languages spun a web of words for every kind of light — the golden hari of the Rigveda, the greenish-yellow chlōros of Greek vegetation, the pale honey-yellow helvus of archaic Latin — but it was in the Germanic north that the root hardened into a material. Proto-Germanic built *glasą, the shiny, translucent substance, and from it every Germanic tongue took its word for glass: Old English glæs, German Glas, Dutch glas, Old Norse gler. Around that one word grew the whole 'gl-' cluster of shine-words — glow, glare, gleam, glimmer, glitter, glisten, glint — each a different shade of the same prehistoric light.

For a thousand years the English word stayed purely a noun. Old English glæs named the material and the objects made from it, and it was not until late Middle English that the language built a verb on it. The denominal verb glasen, 'to furnish with glass or make glassy', is recorded from about 1374, and in pronunciation the medial cluster softened and simplified: the -s- was gradually lost or assimilated, so that by the fifteenth century glazen had become glaze, its own word. In the sixteenth century the verb began to leave the window-frame. As Renaissance trade, glass-making, and the decorative arts flourished — and as European potteries discovered the refinements of tin-glaze, which Italian maiolica and Dutch Delftware made famous — to glaze expanded from 'to set with glass' to 'to give a smooth, vitreous, light-reflecting coating to any surface'. The potter's art gave the word its technical centre, and the cook soon borrowed it: the shiny finish on pastry and meat is first so called in the same period, so that by the seventeenth century glaze meant the glossy coat of the kiln and of the kitchen alike.

The decisive development for modern English came in the nineteenth century, when the verb crossed from materials to the human face. The image of the 'glassy eye' — bright, hard, and unseeing — is older, but the verb form, 'the eyes glaze (over)', becomes common in the nineteenth-century novel, where it describes the fixed stare of death, of the dying, of the stunned, and of the utterly bored. The semantic bridge is exact: as a potter's clay takes a hard, shiny, lifeless skin in the kiln, so a living eye takes a bright, reflective film as life or attention withdraws from it. The shine that had once been admired as the sign of finish and craft was now repointed at vacancy; the word of the workshop became the word of the waking nightmare, and Tolkien's use of it in the goblins' roasting-song — 'till beards blaze, and eyes glaze' — exploits precisely this double register, the festive craft-word performing the song's most chilling cruelty.

Since then the word has only spread further, carried by its single thread of reflected light:

  • Sound change: the medial -s- of glasen weakened to the voiced fricative /z/ in glaze, matching the older noun glass (with voiceless /s/); the spelling with -z- fixed the new pronunciation in the fifteenth century.
  • Semantic drift: noun and verb grew together and apart — the noun names the coating and the coated surface (pottery glaze, meat glaze, glaze ice), the verb names the action of applying or of becoming glazed; by the nineteenth century the intransitive 'the eyes glazed over' made the body itself the pot.
  • Socio-cultural contexts: the potter's wheel, the maiolica kiln, the pastry kitchen, the photographic darkroom (glazed prints), the print shop (glazed paper), and the highway engineer (glaze ice from freezing rain) each adopted the word as their craft required.
  • Literary attestations: the glazier's trade appears in medieval guild records; the transferred eye-sense is exploited throughout nineteenth-century fiction; Tolkien's 1937 The Hobbit fixes the macabre use for generations of readers.
  • Modern idiom: 'to glaze over' now means both to become glassy-eyed with tedium or shock and, by a second metaphor, to gloss over or skate over a difficulty — the cover-up reading of the same covering word.

What makes glaze a single word rather than a family of accidents is the constancy of its physics: a hard, smooth, shiny surface laid over something else, reflecting rather than absorbing light. From the fourteenth-century window to the twenty-first-century road, from the finished bowl to the dying eye, the word has never changed its image — only the horror that the image can carry has deepened.
