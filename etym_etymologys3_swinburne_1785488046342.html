Evolution essay for SWINBURNE, Part 1 of 2 — the element swīn "swine, pig" An ancient root, imitative of the snout. The first half of the surname Swinburne is the oldest of English words for the pig. Its Proto-Indo-European ancestor, *sū-, is one of the most widespread items in the family's vocabulary for domestic animals, and its origin is plausibly onomatopoeic: it imitates the snorting, snuffling sound of a pig's snout. Whatever the truth of that ancient guess, the root is attested across the whole Indo-European world. Sanskrit has sūkara-, "boar, pig," the ancestor of modern Hindi sūar and Odia śūkara; Latin has sūs, "pig, hog," the word of Varro's and Columella's farm manuals; Greek has hŷs; Avestan has hū, "boar"; and Old Irish has socc, "pig," which also came to mean "ploughshare" for its shape. The pig, it seems, was named early and named everywhere by one of the most stable words in the lexicon. The Germanic development. In the Germanic branch the root was reshaped. Proto-Germanic formed *swiną, a neuter noun, most plausibly as a substantivised adjective *swī-na-, "porcine, of the pig" — that is, the pig as "the porcine animal." (The derivational details are reconstructed and debated, but the shape of the word is secure.) From this developed Old English swīn, the ordinary word for the animal throughout the Old English period, alongside a cluster of related words: sugu "sow," eofor "boar," picga "pig" (a late and obscure formation), and the loan fearh "young pig." Modern English swine preserves the Old English form almost unchanged in sound and spelling. Semantics: from animal to insult. The word's meaning has drifted downward in register. In Old and Middle English, swīn was the plain, standard word for the animal — the word of the swineherd, of the farm, and of place-names. It survives in that neutral use in compounds: swineherd (the herdsman who kept the pigs, a figure of the earliest English literature), swine flu, and the scientific and commercial vocabulary of pig-keeping. But in modern English the simplex swine has retreated to two uses: the collective noun for the animal (and for bacon and pork from the animal) and — far more common in ordinary speech — the insult. Calling a contemptible person "a swine" is a semantic step as old as the word's figurative use in the Bible and in moralising literature, and it gives the word today a coarse, abusive register entirely absent from its placid Old English career. This double life matters for the surname: the poet Swinburne's name begins, to modern ears, with the coarse word — an irony his admirers have not failed to relish. Swine in the landscape. The Old English word was a tireless maker of place-names. Settlements and farms where pigs were kept were named for them: Swindon, "swine hill," is a famous English town; Swinford is the "swine ford"; Swineshead is "swine's headland." The element appears in the Domesday Book and throughout the medieval landscape, testimony to the pig's central place in Anglo-Saxon and medieval husbandry — the animal kept by the poor, pastured in woods and pannage, feeding on acorns and beechmast. The surname element swīn- was likewise used to form personal and place-derived surnames, marking a person as coming from such a swine-place. It is this same element, in this same landscape, that opens the surname Swinburne: the poet's family name begins with the pig. From landscape to surname to poet. The compound swīn + burne therefore names, first, a real place — a stream by which swine were kept — and, second, the family that came from it. The surname is habitational and toponymic, of the ordinary medieval type: it identifies its bearer by origin, exactly as Swindon, Blackburn, and Bournemouth identify places. The poet Algernon Charles Swinburne (1837–1909) inherited the name from his Northumbrian gentry family, who traced their descent from such swine-streams in the north of England. The poet's contemporaries, aware of the etymology, enjoyed the contrast between the muddy, down-to-earth pig-stream and the supremely refined, scandalous virtuoso of the musical line who bore it. The word thus completes a full arc in English: ancient root of domestic economy, the pig; medieval place-name, the swine-stream; hereditary surname of a gentry family; and finally a byword for metrical virtuosity in the handbooks of English prosody, where "Swinburne" names the recherché metres — anapaests and choriambs — culled by the schoolroom grammars. The word today. In modern English the surname's two halves are alive in different ways. Swine survives as the insult and as the collective farm noun. Burn survives as the living Northern English and Scots word for "stream" — the very word Robert Burns took as his own surname's spelling — and in scores of place-names across the map of England and Scotland. And Swinburne itself, as a proper noun, has become a permanent item of English literary history: the name of the age's most daring metrist, whose every resonance — sensuality, rebellion, virtuosity, scandal — is summoned whenever a handbook "culls its metres from Swinburne and Bridges." --- Evolution essay for SWINBURNE, Part 2 of 2 — the element burne "stream, brook" The root: water that boils and wells. The second half of the surname Swinburne is a word of running water. Its Proto-Indo-European ancestor, *bʰreu-, meant "to boil, bubble, well up" — the verb for water that seethes and springs. The root is shared with Latin fervēre, "to be hot, to boil," which gives English fervent and ferment, and with the Germanic word for "to brew," since the same bubbling motion was seen in the fermenting of drink. The semantic image at the root of the surname's second half is therefore not a calm current but a welling, bubbling source: a spring that boils up out of the earth. In Proto-Germanic the root produced *brunnōn, "spring, well, fountain" — a noun formed with the expressive double-n characteristic of Germanic — and from this descended the Germanic family of well-words: German Brunnen, "well, fountain," Old Norse brunnr, and Old English burna / burne, "spring, stream, brook." Old English burna and the two dialects of the island. In Old English, burna (also spelled burne) was the ordinary word for a running stream or brook, especially in the Anglian and Northumbrian dialects of the north. The same word in the southern dialects, with the vowel shift that distinguishes West Saxon, gave the form behind the great -bourne place-names of the south of England — Bournemouth, Eastbourne, Winterbourne — where a "bourne" is a stream, and the surname element -bourne preserves it. The northern form -burn / -burne spread into countless place-names: Blackburn, Whitburn, Hebburn, and the many -burn villages of Northumberland and the Borders. It also entered the common language: in Northern English and Scots, burn is still the living, everyday word for "a stream" — the word a child in the North uses for the brook at the bottom of the garden, and the word that supplies the surname of Scotland's national poet, Robert Burns, whose family name is the same stream-word in its Scots form. The dialectal geography of the island is written into the spelling of its place-names: the same Old English word, burna, became -burn in the north, -bourn/-bourne in the south, and -burne in the surname tradition of the north-eastern gentry, as in Swinburne. Sound changes. The phonological history is regular. PIE *bʰreu- gives Germanic *brēw- with the labial fricative; the *bʰ > *b- shift (Grimm's Law) is regular; the root then took the -nn- suffix giving *brunnōn; and in Old English the initial cluster and medial double-n survived intact into burna. The shift from "welling spring" to "running stream, brook" is a natural narrowing-widening: a brook is what a spring becomes once it runs, and Old English used burna for both the source and the course. The word was thus both concrete and common — the everyday name of ordinary water — which is exactly why it became one of the most frequent elements in the landscape-names of northern England. Landscape and settlement. The -burne element testifies to settlement patterns. Places were named for their streams because the stream was the settlement's defining feature: its water, its mill-site, its boundary, its name. A place called Swinburne was a place where swine were kept by such a burn — a settlement beside a brook in the pannage-woods of the north, named for the two most practical facts about it, the water and the pigs. The surname that records it belongs to the great class of English habitational surnames formed before the fourteenth century, when the growing population of England began to require hereditary family names and men took their names from the places they came from. Swinburne, like Swindon and Blackburn, preserves in its two syllables a snapshot of medieval northern England: the stream, the swine, the settlement, the family. The name and the poet. The surname was borne by the Northumbrian gentry family of the poet Algernon Charles Swinburne (1837–1909). Its geography is entirely apt: the family's lands lay in Northumberland, in the very country where burn still means "stream" in everyday speech, so that the poet's own name — to northern ears — remained transparently "the pig-stream." The poet's first name, by contrast, tells a different story: Algernon is Norman French, from aux gernons, "with moustaches," a nickname-turned-aristocratic forename introduced into England by the Norman aristocracy and borne by the Percys of Alnwick. The poet's full name is thus a perfect emblem of the two strands of English history: a Norman French Christian name sitting before a native English stream-name — the pig's brook in the north of England, the moustachioed Norman baron in the family tree. The legacy. In English literary history the surname has completed its journey from landscape to legend. The stream-word burn lives on in the dialect of the north and in the place-names of the whole island; the pig-word swine lives on as the animal-noun and the insult; and Swinburne itself has become a name of prosodic legend — the source of the recherché metres, the anapaests and choriambs, culled by the schoolroom handbooks of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The poet whom the schoolbooks plunder for exotic Greek metres bore a name that means, in the plainest northern English, "the stream where the pigs drink": the supreme refinement of Victorian prosody permanently yoked, in two syllables, to the muddy banks of a Northumberland brook.
