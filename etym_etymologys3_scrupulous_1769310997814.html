The biography of scrupulous begins in the stone-age workshop of the Proto-Indo-Europeans, with a root whose whole business was cutting and scraping. *(s)ker- / *(s)kerp- meant "to cut, scrape, carve," and it is one of the most productive roots in the entire family: it lies behind Greek keirō "to shear," English sharp and scrap, Latin cortex "bark (that which is scraped off)," and the cutting family of shear, scarf, and carnificare. What matters for our word is that a sharp stone is precisely a "cutting thing," and the Romans called such a stone a scrupus. Plautus already uses the noun for a jagged rock, and the word's sharpness is the seed of everything that follows.

The decisive step is the diminutive. Latin speakers formed scrupulus, "a little sharp stone, a pebble," and Roman daily life gave that pebble a starring role. Sandals were simple leather soles bound with straps, and a tiny stone lodged between sole and foot made every step painful; a traveller had to stop, sit, empty the shoe, and inspect the ground before going on. Classical moralists immediately saw the metaphor: a nagging doubt is exactly a pebble in the conscience — it stops you, makes you examine your step, and will not let you walk on carelessly. Plautus, Cicero, and Seneca all use scrupulus for a small, prickling hesitation, the unease that prevents a hasty, unconsidered decision. The same tiny size gave the word a second, parallel life in the marketplace: the scrupulus became the smallest official unit of weight, 1/24 of an ounce, the unit used to weigh gold and medicines, because minute exactness was measured with tiny stones on the balance. So by the early empire the one word already meant three things at once: a pebble, a doubt, and a grain of weight.

From the noun the Romans built the adjective scrupulōsus, literally "full of little stones," and then, by the same extension, "anxious, uneasy, careful, exact." It is a beautiful semantic chain: a road full of pebbles makes you walk carefully; a conscience full of pebbles makes you act carefully. This adjective travelled into Gallo-Romance as Old French scrupuleux, and entered English in the mid-fifteenth century as scrupulous, at first with the heavy religious colouring of medieval moral theology — a scrupulous man was one tormented by doubt over sin and scruple, the pathology of the over-anxious confessional. The Reformation debates about conscience and casuistry kept the word busy through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, where "scrupulous" described the man who could not rest easy in his conscience.

From the eighteenth century onward the moral anxiety slowly receded and the intellectual precision grew. The word broadened from the conscience to the workbench: a scrupulous scholar, a scrupulous accountant, scrupulous attention to detail. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it became a standard term of professional approval — the doctor, the lawyer, the journalist is praised for being scrupulous in handling evidence, records, and hygiene. Modern English therefore carries both poles at once, and that is exactly the point: scrupulous today unites the meticulous and the moral because both descend from the same sharp pebble, the same refusal to step carelessly over the small things. In the source passage, "over-scrupulous" shows the word's polite edge: too much pebble-watching becomes a faint social joke about a lady fussing over propriety. The full journey — from stone in a sandal, to weight of a grain, to doubt of a conscience, to badge of professional care — is one of the cleanest demonstrations in English of how a concrete noun can become an abstract virtue.
