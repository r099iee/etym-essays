• Muffled: Similar because both describe sound that has been dampened and made dull; differs because muffled is chiefly acoustic and implies wrapping or covering, whereas deadened implies the energy itself has been killed off and applies equally to feeling and force.
  • Muted: Similar because both describe reduced intensity — of sound, colour, or emotion; differs because muted suggests a soft, deliberate lowering of volume or brightness, whereas deadened suggests near-total suppression.
  • Dampened: Similar because both mean to reduce or lessen force or intensity; differs because dampened (when figurative) means discouraged or reduced, while deadened means to extinguish sensation or energy almost entirely.
  • Dulled: Similar because both describe the removal of sharpness, brightness, or sensitivity; differs because dulled is milder and can be reversible, whereas deadened implies a killing-off.
  • Numbed: Similar because both describe loss of physical or emotional feeling; differs because numbed is specifically about sensation, whereas deadened extends to sound, force, and colour.
  • Benumbed: Similar because both describe being deprived of feeling; differs because benumbed is more archaic and literary and is nearly always about the body, whereas deadened is broader.
  • Blunted: Similar because both mean reducing sharpness or impact; differs because blunted keeps the image of a weapon's edge, while deadened keeps the image of death and extinction.
  • Smothered: Similar because both mean suppressing sound or activity; differs because smothered implies covering and suffocation, whereas deadened implies absorption or killing of energy.
  • Stifled: Similar because both mean suppressing; differs because stifled is used of sounds, yawns, or urges held back, whereas deadened is used of ambience, feeling, and force.
  • Silenced: Similar because both describe the removal of sound; differs because silenced means made completely quiet, while deadened means made dull and flat rather than necessarily silent.
  • Subdued: Similar because both describe reduced intensity; differs because subdued implies restraint or quietness, whereas deadened implies the near-extinction of energy.
  • Desensitized: Similar because both describe loss of emotional or physical responsiveness; differs because desensitized is clinical and gradual, whereas deadened is direct and often sudden.
  • Suppressed: Similar because both mean holding energy back; differs because suppressed implies an ongoing force being held down, while deadened implies the force has been killed.
  • Quenched: Similar because both mean extinguishing; differs because quenched is used of fire and thirst, whereas deadened is used of sound, feeling, and vigour.
  • Reddish-brown: Similar because it is the plain descriptive equivalent of the russet colour; differs because it is a colourless, neutral phrase, whereas russet carries the historical weight of undyed homespun cloth and earth-honesty.
  • Auburn: Similar because both are reddish-brown colours; differs because auburn is used almost exclusively of hair, whereas russet applies to cloth, leaves, apples, and landscape.
  • Chestnut: Similar because both are warm reddish-brown colours; differs because chestnut is glossier and browner (the polished nut), while russet is duller, redder, and rougher in texture.
  • Rust / Rust-coloured: Similar because both derive from the same redness of oxidized iron and autumn; differs because rust is the metal-tarnish itself and its colour, whereas russet is a softer, more fabric-like reddish-brown.
  • Tawny: Similar because both are warm brownish-orange colours; differs because tawny is lighter, yellower, and honey-toned, whereas russet is redder, darker, and earthier.
  • Copper: Similar because both are reddish-brown metallic tones; differs because copper implies a bright, reflective metal sheen, whereas russet is dull and matte.
  • Mahogany: Similar because both are rich reddish-browns; differs because mahogany is a dark, glossy wood tone, whereas russet is the colour of plain wool and soil.
  • Umber: Similar because both are earthy brown pigments; differs because umber is darker and browner (burnt umber nearly black-brown), while russet keeps a distinct redness.
  • Sienna: Similar because both are earthy reddish-brown pigments; differs because sienna is a painter's pigment with a yellow-red cast, while russet is a general descriptive colour.
  • Ferruginous: Similar because both mean rust-coloured; differs because ferruginous is a technical, geological, or ornithological term for iron-rust tones, whereas russet is the common literary word.
  • Fusty / Stale (figurative near-opposites of the cloth sense): Similar in being old-fashioned cloth-and-storage words; differs because fusty means stale-smelling, whereas russet is only about the colour and cloth, not smell.
