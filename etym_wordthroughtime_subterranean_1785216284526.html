- [early 17th century: learned adoption] - English borrows subterranean directly from Latin subterraneus in scholarly writing on geography and natural history; it names the world beneath the earth's surface - caverns, buried waters, the secrets of the deep - with a strongly learned, Latinate, almost geological tone.
- [17th-18th century: scientific and architectural sense] - The word serves as the precise, slightly technical synonym of underground for subterranean structures, watercourses, and geological formations, while the parallel French descendant souterrain is re-borrowed as a specialist archaeological term for an underground chamber.
- [19th century: imaginative and mythic expansion] - Subterranean acquires a literary resonance - vast, sunless, secret spaces beneath the world - used of imagined underworlds; its register drifts from the neutral and factual toward the mysterious, even the menacing.
- [20th century: popularization in fantasy and cinema] - The word becomes the standard evocative epithet for fictional underworlds (subterranean lakes, realms, and cities), pairing scholarly weight with the thrill of the unknown; it stays learned yet grows instantly vivid to general readers.
- [Contemporary sense] - The word still denotes anything under the earth, but now in two coexisting registers: the neutral geological (subterranean rivers) and the evocative, ominous register of story, where it "enchants and alarms" - the least common and most suggestive of the underground words.
