- Classical Antiquity to Middle Ages — Classical Greek asylon and Roman asylum: places of sacred immunity (temples, altars, Romulus's sacred grove) where fugitives, slaves, and debtors were shielded from seizure; in Middle Ages, church sanctuary carried this concept forward under canon law.
- 15th–17th c. — Early Modern English legal reintroduction: adopted from classical Latin as a scholarly noun for sovereign political and religious sanctuary, protecting heretics, political exiles, and fallen nobles from royal retribution.
- 18th c. — Philanthropic benevolence: emerging Enlightenment humanitarian movements established "asylums" for vulnerable demographics—orphans, destitute blind, fallen women—connoting gentle, protective institutional shelter away from urban predation.
- 19th c. — The Psychiatric "Lunatic Asylum" Era: rapid expansion of state-funded psychiatric hospitals (e.g. York Retreat, Broadmoor) conceived as idyllic pastoral sanctuaries for mental healing; initially viewed as a triumph of moral therapy and humanitarian medicine.
- Late 19th–Mid 20th c. — Institutional degeneration & dark stigmatization: severe overcrowding, underfunding, and abusive conditions converted "lunatic asylums" into dreaded, carceral symbols of lifelong neglect, horror, and social banishment, permanently tarnishing the word in medical contexts.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Deinstitutionalization & Geopolitical Specialization: abandonment of the term in psychiatry (replaced by "psychiatric hospitals" or "mental health facilities"); the word's primary contemporary usage re-centered upon international human rights, "political asylum", and abstract metaphor for peaceful shelter.
