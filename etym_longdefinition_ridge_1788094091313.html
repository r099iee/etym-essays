Ridge (noun) denotes a long, narrow, elevated line or strip that rises above its surroundings — most familiarly the crest or summit-line of a hill or mountain range, but also any raised band, rib, or edge running along a surface. The word's core geometry is constant: something elongated, raised, and spine-like, standing out from the flat or hollow ground around it. In the reading context the word describes the southern crest of a mountain toward which a lost track climbs — precisely the topographic sense that dominates modern use.

Primary senses.

  • Geography and geology: a long narrow elevation of land, or the line along the top of a range where its two slopes meet; also a long narrow hilltop, an escarpment-edge, or the watershed divide between two valleys. In modern earth science a mid-ocean ridge is the vast submarine mountain chain where tectonic plates spread apart, as in the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — the longest mountain system on Earth.
  • Architecture and building: the horizontal line or beam along the top of a roof where two sloping surfaces meet — the ridge, ridgepole, ridge beam or ridge tile. This sense is as old as the topographic one and follows directly from the 'back' imagery: a roof's ridge is its spine.
  • Agriculture: a raised strip of ploughed earth between two furrows — the classic medieval ridge-and-furrow field system, whose corrugations still scar English pasture. The farmer's ridge is literally a 'back' of soil thrown up by the plough.
  • Anatomy and zoology: any elongated bony or muscular prominence — the alveolar ridge of the jaw behind the teeth, the supraorbital (brow) ridge above the eyes, the dorsal ridge of a whale's back. The oldest sense of the word, in fact, was the human or animal back itself.
  • Meteorology: a ridge of high pressure — an elongated area of high atmospheric pressure, contrasted with a 'trough' of low pressure; the term transfers landscape geometry to weather charts.
  • Everyday material senses: any raised line or rib on a surface — the ridges of corrugated roof sheeting or cardboard, the ridged edge of a coin, tyre treads, corduroy or ribbed fabric, the ridges of a fingerprint, or the ridges left by a comb or rake in soil or hair.
  • Phonetics: the alveolar ridge is a standard point of articulation, where the tongue tip touches to make /t/, /d/, /s/, /z/, /n/ and /l/ — a technical sense borrowed from anatomy.

Semantic bridges. Every sense above is connected by the same mental picture: a raised, elongated form like a spine or backbone. Old English hrycg meant 'back, spine' — the curved ridge of a living body — and by simple shape-metaphor English speakers applied the same word to the crest of a hill, the ridge of a roof, the ridge of a ploughed field, and later to ridges of metal, cloth, skin and pressure charts. The 'back' sense itself has now retreated: modern speakers rarely call a person's back a 'ridge', though the phrase ridge of the nose survives.

Figurative and derivative uses. Ridge is seldom used abstractly, but it appears in the idiom to ride the ridge (to keep to a safe middle course, as a rider keeps to the ridge of a dyke or causeway) and in place-naming (Ridge Road, the Blue Ridge Mountains, Oak Ridge). The verb to ridge means to form into ridges or to mark with ridges (a ridged roof, ridged fingerprints); ridged is the common participial adjective. In knitting and crochet 'ridge' names a raised row of stitches; in ceramics and metalwork it names an ornamental raised band; in hydrology a ridge may be a gravelly bank in a riverbed; in statistics ridge regression (1970) adds a 'ridge' to a matrix diagonal; in data visualisation a ridge plot stacks density curves in overlapping horizontal bands — the newest metaphorical extension of the same spine-like geometry.
