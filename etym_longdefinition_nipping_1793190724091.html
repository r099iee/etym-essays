Nipping, the present participle and gerund of the verb nip, centers on the image of a small sharp closure — two hard edges meeting quickly on something soft, whether teeth on skin, claws on a toe, frost on exposed cheeks, or fingers snatching a shoot from a stem.

In the source sentence the word evokes a crayfish discovered in intimate contact — he woke to find a crayfish nipping — almost certainly the small freshwater crustacean fastening its pincers on the sleeper's skin or clothing down by the water, a sharp tiny pinch that wakes rather than wounds, comic and startling at once.

Core physical sense in detail: To nip is to compress briefly and sharply between two surfaces, taking only a little flesh or material at a time. A crayfish nips with its chelae, a puppy nips at heels, a crab nips a toe, and a gardener's fingers nip a thread or bud. The diminutive scale matters: nipping is biting in miniature, the small sharp version of a chomp, repeated and lively rather than deep and crushing.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Pinching or biting lightly and repeatedly (primary, from late 14th c.): dogs nipping at ankles, frost nipping fingers, crayfish nipping toes. The bridge is the pincer gesture itself, the ancestor of every other use, naming the meeting of two edges on tender flesh.
  • Stinging cold that bites the skin (from 16th c.): a nipping wind, nipping frost on an autumn morning. The bridge is bodily sensation: cold that makes the cheeks tingle feels exactly like tiny teeth, so the language transfers the bite of the animal to the bite of the air.
  • Pinching off growth in gardening (from 17th c.): nipping out side-shoots, nipping back buds to make a plant bush out. The bridge runs through the fingers, which become living pincers that remove tender growth cleanly and shape what remains.
  • Darting quickly in or out (chiefly British, from 19th c.): nipping to the shops, nipping out for a moment, nipping through a gap in the crowd. The bridge is the speed of the pinch: as the bite is quick, so the movement is quick, a small sharp displacement of the body rather than of the teeth.
  • Snatching or stealing small things (colloquial): nipping an apple from a stall, nipping someone's seat while they stand. The bridge extends the quick dart to the fingers taking something small, with a shade of mischief rather than grand theft.
  • Sipping spirits in small sharp mouthfuls (slang, from nip as a small drink): nipping at a flask, nipping whisky through a cold evening. The bridge is the small sharp mouthful: the drinker takes the liquor in bites, and the sting of the spirit answers the sting of the teeth.
  • Checking or curbing growth figuratively (modern extension): nipping a rumor in the bud, nipping trouble before it spreads. The bridge carries the gardener's pinch into abstract management: the sharp early removal that keeps the larger organism healthy.

Register and connotation: nipping is lively and small-scale, more playful than wounding when applied to animals, more bracing than brutal when applied to weather, and briskly informal when applied to movement. A nipping dog is a nuisance rather than a danger, a nipping wind is an invitation to hurry home, and nipping out is the very idiom of the quick friendly errand. Grammar keeps the word active and ongoing: the -ing form stresses repetition and continuation, pinch upon pinch and step upon step, never one finished blow.
