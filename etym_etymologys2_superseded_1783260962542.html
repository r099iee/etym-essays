SUPERSEDED descends from a single Proto-Indo-European root. Linear chain: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *sed- "to sit" (3rd millennium BCE; the root also of English sit, set, saddle, and of Latin sedēre). Stage 2 — Proto-Italic *sedē- "to sit" (reconstructed; the regular Italic development of *sed-). Stage 3 — Latin sedēre "to sit" (attested from the 3rd century BCE; sedēō, sedēre, sēdī, sessum). Stage 4 — Latin supersedēre "to sit above; to refrain from, forbear, desist, stay; to be superior to" — super- "over, above" + sedēre (classical Latin, attested from the 1st century BCE onward: Cicero, Livy). Stage 5 — Old French superseder / superceder "to defer, postpone, hold over" (12th–13th centuries; French spelling -seer/-cer, and some forms reshaped under the influence of Latin verbs in -cedere). Stage 6 — Middle English superseden "to postpone, defer; to set aside, omit" (borrowed c. 1485; early spellings superseden, superceden). Stage 7 — Modern English supersede, the re-Latinized spelling restoring -sede to mirror Latin supersedēre (16th century onward; the only common English verb in -sede). Inflected form: SUPERSEDED (past tense and past participle, also adjective, 17th century onward), meaning "replaced, displaced, set aside as obsolete." FINAL NODE. DEBATE / NUANCE NOTES: - The spelling history is genuine and instructive: English borrowed the word through French, whose forms (superceder, superseder) were easily confused with the large Latin-derived family in -ceed/-cede (exceed, concede, proceed). The modern spelling supersede is a deliberate 16th-century re-Latinization. - The semantics moved in two stages: earliest English "to forbear, postpone, set aside (a law or action)" → 17th–18th century "to occupy the superior or later position and thereby replace." Both derive from the image of the higher seat in supersedēre; the "sit above" physical sense was never a modern English sense.
