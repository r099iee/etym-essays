- Late 16th-c. claim and assertion - borrowed into English, "vindicated" first carries the senses "to lay claim to, to assert" and "to avenge, punish"; the word is forceful and proprietary at its root.
- 17th-c. legal clearing - the dominant sense becomes legal: to clear of blame or suspicion, to defend against accusation; vindication is the verdict that answers a charge.
- 17th–18th-c. intellectual apology - the word extends to ideas: to vindicate a thesis, a cause, a reputation — establishing its truth or worth against attack; it enters the vocabulary of polemic and moral debate.
- 19th-c. reputation and honour - "vindicated" attaches to public character — a vindicated man, vindicated honour — the satisfaction of being cleared in the eyes of others.
- 20th–21st-c. proof by events - the sense drifts from the courtroom to everyday confirmation: a prediction, decision, or instinct is "vindicated" by later events, meaning proven right after doubt; the register relaxes while the note of triumph over skepticism remains.
