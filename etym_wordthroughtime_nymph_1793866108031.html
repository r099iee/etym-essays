- [Archaic & Classical Greek (c. 8th–4th c. BCE)] - Revered with sacred awe as a divine nature maiden and bridal spirit; worshipped with votive offerings at mountain springs, rural caves, and sacred groves.
- [Roman Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE–4th c. CE)] - Aestheticized into pastoral poetry and grand civic architecture; associated with ornamental public fountain monuments (nymphaea) and dramatic metamorphic myths (Echo, Daphne, Syrinx).
- [High & Late Middle Ages (c. 12th–14th c.)] - Absorbed into medieval European romance; conflated with woodland fairies, nixies, pagan water spirits, and dangerous wild temptresses of chivalric lore.
- [Renaissance & Elizabethan Era (c. 16th–17th c.)] - Celebrated in courtly literature and pastoral verse as the supreme aesthetic ideal of feminine grace, youthful allure, and uncorrupted natural innocence.
- [Enlightenment & 19th Century] - Formally codified in empirical natural science; adopted by entomologists for the active immature instar of insects and by botanists for aquatic water-lilies.
- [Late 19th & 20th Century] - Diverged into specialized sporting terminology (subsurface trout fly-fishing lures) and contentious psychological/literary archetypes (hypersexuality in *nymphomania*, the precocious *nymphet* in modern fiction).
- [Contemporary Usage] - Maintained primarily in entomological biology, angling parlance, classical fantasy literature, and figurative descriptions of spirited, youthful feminine beauty.
