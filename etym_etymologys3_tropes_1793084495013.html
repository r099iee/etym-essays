The word tropes has spent three thousand years turning — which is fitting, because turning is all it has ever meant. Its root is PIE *trep-, to turn, a verb of wheeling and changing direction that also yields Greek trepein (to turn, direct, alter) in Homer's epics — ships turning, hearts turning, the turning scales of Zeus.

  • Greek formation: from trepein (to turn) came tropos, a turning — of the sun at the solstices (the tropics are the turning-lines of the sun), of character (a man's tropos is his bent or disposition), of argument, and, decisively for rhetoric, of a word from its proper meaning to another. The sophists and Aristotle's successors catalogued tropoi as the deliberate turnings of eloquence.
  • Sound change: the Greek o-vowel and p survived borrowing intact; Latin tropus kept both, and English trope (via Old French trope) regularised the spelling while keeping the single syllable — three millennia with barely a phoneme disturbed, because learned words travel in books, not mouths.
  • Semantic drift: from physical turning (sun, ships) to mental turning (character, disposition) to verbal turning (figures of speech) — the classic climb from body to mind to language that metaphor theory would later predict.

Rome and the schools: Latin rhetoric adopted tropus wholesale — Cicero and Quintilian devote long sections to the tropes, fixing the canonical four (metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, irony) that schoolboys would memorise for two thousand years. Quintilian already complains that orators overuse them — the first recorded grumble about tired tropes.

  • Sound change: none of consequence — the schoolroom preserved tropus letter-perfect through the Empire and into the Church fathers.
  • Socio-cultural context: rhetoric was the Roman path to power, and tropes were its jewels; to command them was to command courts, senates, and emperors.
  • Literary attestations: Quintilian's Institutio Oratoria (c. 95 CE) remains the great surviving catalogue, defining each trope with examples still quoted in classrooms.

The medieval double life: while schoolmen kept the rhetorical sense, church musicians gave tropus a second career — the trope as liturgical interpolation, new words and melodies woven into the chant from the ninth century. Winchester, St Gall, and Limoges competed in troping the Mass, until the Council of Trent pruned the exuberance. Meanwhile allegorists troped Scripture itself, turning literal histories toward spiritual senses — the word turning in two choirs at once.

English adoption and modern triumph: trope entered English in the 1530s with Renaissance rhetoric manuals, named Shakespeare's stock-in-trade, survived neoclassical scorn of ornament, and was reborn twice: first philosophically, when Emerson and Nietzsche saw all language as faded tropes (fossil poetry), and then popularly, when twentieth-century critics and twenty-first-century fandoms made tropes the everyday word for storytelling's stock devices. From Homer's turning ships to the wiki's trope-lists, the turning has never stopped.
