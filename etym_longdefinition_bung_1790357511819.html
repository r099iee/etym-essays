Bung (verb & noun):

The word Bung is a highly versatile term in the English language, functioning in standard technical domains as a large stopper or plug for casks, and across informal British, Australian, and New Zealand registers as a dynamic colloquial verb meaning to toss, shove, or cram casually, to plug or obstruct, or to pay an illicit bribe.

1. Colloquial Propelling, Cramming, & Placing (Contextual Sense)

  • To throw, toss, or shove casually: In British, Australian, and New Zealand informal English, to bung means to throw, toss, pitch, or put something into a place casually, carelessly, or roughly without delicate handling (e.g., "Bung him in"—meaning shove or throw him inside the vehicle, room, or cell; "Just bung the dirty clothes into the washing machine").
  • To thrust or deliver forcefully: It can also convey shoving or cramming an object forcefully into a tight opening or container.

2. Sealing, Stopping, & Physical Obstruction

  • The barrel stopper (Noun): A cylindrical or conical stopper, plug, or spigot, traditionally carved of wood or formed from cork, rubber, or plastic, used to seal the large aperture (the bung-hole) in the side or head of a cask, barrel, vat, or container used in brewing, winemaking, and distilling.
  • To plug or close up (Verb): To stop, seal, or close an opening with or as if with a bung (e.g., "bung up the barrel").
  • Physically clogged or swollen: Widely used in the participial construction bunged up to describe nasal congestion from a cold (e.g., "My nose is completely bunged up") or an eye swollen shut from an impact or injury (e.g., "a bunged-up eye").

3. Slang Bribes, Finances, & Australasian Idioms

  • Illicit bribe or kickback (Noun & Verb): In British sports journalism, political reporting, and underworld slang, a bung is a secret illicit payment, financial inducement, or bribe given to an official, manager, or player to secure an unfair transaction or look the other way (e.g., "The manager was accused of taking a £50,000 bung"; "Bung the doorman a tenner to get us in").
  • Australian / NZ 'Go bung': An Australasian idiomatic phrase meaning to break down, fail, collapse, go bankrupt, or die (e.g., "The alternator went bung on the highway"; "Their business went bung during the recession").
  • Australian 'Bung on': To put on an act, feign an emotion, or stage an elaborate display (e.g., "Don't bung on an act with me").

4. Semantic Bridges & Historical Convergence

  • From plug to cramming: The historical transition from the cooperage noun (a tight wooden plug hammered into a cask opening) to the verb "bung up" (to stop tightly) led naturally to the colloquial image of jamming, stuffing, or cramming an item or person into an enclosure, which subsequently relaxed into the broad informal sense of tossing or putting something somewhere casually.
  • From pocket to bribe: In 16th-century thieves' cant, a purse or pocket was known as a bung (because it was tightly stopped to protect money). Giving money secretly into someone's pocket evolved into the modern British slang term for a financial kickback.
  • The Australian homophone: The Australasian idiom go bung is an independent loanword from the Yagara/Jandai Aboriginal word bong ("dead"), phonetically assimilated into the existing English word form.
