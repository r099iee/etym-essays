buttresses
- The masonry push-back (14th c.) - Borrowed from Old French bouterez, the building-trade name for the supports that 'push' against a wall; a precise masonry term for a permanent structural member.
- The Gothic age (13th-16th c.) - The buttress, and above all the flying buttress, becomes a central invention of the great cathedrals, carrying the weight of the vault to the ground; the word rises with the church-building age.
- Broadening and metaphor (17th-19th c.) - The term widens from fortress and church to any outward support, and transfers figuratively to anything that props up an argument, an institution, or a state.
- Modern use - In architecture the precise structural term; figuratively, to buttress is to support, strengthen, or hold up, the push-back against any wall's desire to fall.
lofty
- High in the air (14th c.) - Formed from loft + -y; first meaning 'high, towering, in the air', of things that belong to the upper storey of the sky.
- The moral height (15th c.) - Transfers from height to nobility: lofty ideals, a lofty style, a lofty bearing; the word becomes the adjective of the exalted and the sublime.
- The sublime and the remote (16th-19th c.) - The two senses run together; lofty implies sky and air, dignity, and a hint of untouchable distance, even hauteur.
- Modern double register - Still combines physical height with spiritual remoteness: the lofty turret is both high and untouchable, the word for things of the upper air that look down on the ruin about them.
turrets
- The little tower (14th c.) - Middle English touret from Old French tourette, a diminutive of tour, 'tower'; the small, slender towers of castles, often at the angles and projecting from the wall.
- The defensive accent (14th-16th c.) - Part of the vocabulary of fortification: the look-out point, the flanking angle, the little watchful tower that surveys the world.
- From castle to roof to ship (17th-19th c.) - Extends to any small tower-like structure on a building or roof; in the 19th century, the revolving armoured gun-platform of a warship takes the name.
- Modern double life - The word now spans the medieval and the mechanical: the slender turret of a stronghold and the gun-turret of a battleship, with the diminutive still preserving a smallness within the tower.
