- 16th c. - Introduced into English primarily in legal, ecclesiastical, and matrimonial contexts meaning 'to divorce, cast off, or formally put away a spouse or betrothed.'
- 17th c. - Broadened beyond marital divorce to denote the formal disowning or casting off of children, heirs, covenants, or religious allegiances with strong moral disapproval.
- 18th–19th c. - Developed into a core political, financial, and diplomatic term meaning 'to refuse to honor, acknowledge, or pay sovereign debts or treaties' (e.g., repudiation of state bonds).
- Late 19th–20th c. - Expanded into philosophy, science, and everyday discourse to mean 'to reject an accusation, doctrine, or hypothesis with indignation or intellectual finality.'
- 21st c. - Heavily utilized in contemporary political rhetoric and public relations to denote an explicit, urgent public disavowal of extremist ideologies, rhetoric, or rogue institutional actions.
