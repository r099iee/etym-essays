PIE root *ters-, "to dry, become dry; to parch" (reconstructed; pre-3000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *tersā, "dry land" (reconstructed; 2nd millennium BCE) → Latin terra, "dry land, earth, ground" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; from *ters-ā, literally "the dry [land]", the land as distinguished from water and marsh) → Old French terre, "earth, land" (9th c., from Latin terra) + adjective-forming suffix -ier → terrier, "of the earth, earthen" (Old French, 12th–13th c.) → Old French chien terrier, "earth dog, burrowing dog" (a dog that goes to earth after foxes, badgers, and other burrowing quarry; 13th c.) → Middle English terrier (shortened from chien terrier), "a dog that digs out quarry from its earth" (attested 15th c.) → terrier, "a small dog of a breed originally used for turning out foxes and other burrowing animals from their earths" (modern sense) Note on the parallel land-register sense: the same Latin terra produced a second, independent formation — Medieval Latin terrārius, "a roll or register of landed property" (a catalogue of the earth), borrowed into Old French and then English as terrier, "a land register," attested from the 15th c. The earth-dog and the earth-register are therefore homographs that share the root terra but reached English through two separate medieval channels; both are documented, and both descend from the same "earth" source. Note on the root: the modern standard etymology derives Latin terra from PIE *ters- "to dry" (the same root as Latin torrere "to parch", English thirst, and terracotta). The older, now-abandoned association of terra with PIE *dhghem- "earth" (the root of Latin humus and English human) is no longer accepted by comparative linguists; terra's literal sense is "the dry land".
