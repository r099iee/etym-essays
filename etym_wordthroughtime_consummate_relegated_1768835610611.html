consummate:
- A verb of completion (15th c.) - borrowed from Latin consummatus, 'brought to a total'; the earliest sense is final and often ceremonial - completing a marriage, finishing a work - with the Vulgate's consummatum est, 'it is finished,' giving it a sacramental finality
- The adjective of the highest degree (16th-17th c.) - consummate crowns persons and works: a consummate craftsman, consummate skill, perfection not merely flawless but totalled, every part gathered to its ceiling
- Narrowing and refining (18th-19th c.) - the adjective dominates artistic and social judgement, while the verb retreats to formal rituals and to 'consummating' a marriage or an agreement
- Contemporary - supremely skilled, finished, incapable of improvement; a word of final adjudication rather than casual praise, still faintly legalistic and ritual

relegated:
- An official dispatch (c. 1500) - borrowed straight from Latin relegatus; to relegate is a formal, almost bureaucratic act of sending away - banishment with paperwork, a demotion with dignity
- From persons to things (18th-19th c.) - the word spreads to objects and matters: relegated to the attic, to the footnotes, to the margins of history, always a removal from the centre to the periphery
- The sporting sense (20th c.) - relegated to a lower division fixes the word in public speech as the standard term for official demotion
- Contemporary - 'relegated to' still implies a dispatched, secondary standing, and the shade of loss - elevation into exile - flickers beneath every honourable surface
