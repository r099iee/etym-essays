Manu (proper noun, masculine) — The archetypal primal man, universal cosmic progenitor of the human race, and paramount divine lawgiver in Hindu religious literature, philosophy, and jurisprudence. Derived directly from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *men- ("to think, reflect, remember"), Manu literally signifies "the thinking being," "the mindful one," or "the conscious entity," establishing the foundational concept that humanity is fundamentally defined by intellect and self-awareness. In epic and mythological tradition—specifically in the opening chapters of the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa—Manu is revered as the divine king (*Mānavendra*, "Lord of Men") who personally surveyed, designed, and constructed the magnificent royal city of Ayodhyā as the capital of the Solar Dynasty (*Sūryavaṃśa*), laying the physical and ethical foundations of civilized human society.

1. Mythological and Cosmological Role: The Primal Progenitor and the Deluge Hero. In the earliest strata of Indian literature, Manu occupies a station analogous to both Adam and Noah in Abrahamic traditions, acting as both the first human and the sole righteous survivor of a universal cataclysm:

  • The Deluge Legend in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (1.8.1): The earliest detailed account of the cosmic flood (*Pralaya*) in Indian tradition centers on Manu (specifically Vaivasvata Manu, son of the solar deity Vivasvān). While washing his hands in a river, a tiny fish swam into his palms and begged for protection from larger predators, promising to save Manu in return. Manu nurtured the fish in a jar, then a pond, and finally released it into the ocean. The fish, an incarnation of Lord Viṣṇu (the *Matsya Avatāra*), warned Manu of an impending cataclysmic deluge that would submerge the entire cosmos.
  • The Ark of Salvation: Acting upon divine instructions, Manu constructed a massive wooden vessel, placing within it the seeds of all botanical species, herbs, pairs of living animals, and the sacred seven sages (*Saptarṣis*). When the ocean rose and swallowed the earth, Manu tied the cable of his ship to the great horn of the colossal golden fish using the cosmic serpent Vāsuki. Matsya guided the vessel across tempestuous primeval waters to the northern mountains (the Himalayas / *Naubandhana* peak). When the flood receded, Manu descended to the plains, performed sacrificial rites, and produced a daughter named Iḍā (or Ilā), through whom he repopulated the human species. Because all human beings descend from Manu, they are designated in Sanskrit as Mānavas (or Manuṣyas, literally "belonging to Manu").

2. Founder and Royal Architect of Ayodhyā in the Rāmāyaṇa. Beyond his role as cosmic father, Manu is celebrated in epic poetry as the original founder of urban political civilization:

  • The Foundation of Ayodhyā: In the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (Bālakāṇḍa, Sarga 5, verses 5–6), the sage introduces the great kingdom of Kosala and its glorious capital: Ayodhyā nāma nagarī tatrāsīl lokaviśrutā / Manunā mānavendreṇa yā purī nirmitā svayam ("There was situated the world-famous city named Ayodhyā, a city built by Manu himself, the lord of men").
  • Urban and Architectural Splendor: According to the epic, Manu did not merely erect a fortified village; he established a sublime metropolis twelve *yojanas* long and three *yojanas* wide, flanked by broad imperial avenues (*rājamārga*), impassable moats, formidable ramparts, luxurious gardens, and opulent multi-storied palaces adorned with precious gems. Manu organized the city into structured quarters for warriors, merchants, craftsmen, and priests, creating the archetypal urban paradise where dharma flourished unhindered.
  • Father of King Ikṣvāku: Manu installed his eldest son, Ikṣvāku, as the first reigning monarch of Ayodhyā, inaugurating the glorious Solar Dynasty (*Ikṣvāku-vaṃśa*) that produced King Hariścandra, King Sagara, King Bhagīratha, King Daśaratha, and Lord Rāma. Concurrently, through his daughter Ilā, Manu's lineage gave birth to King Purūravas and the Lunar Dynasty (*Candravaṃśa*), making Manu the supreme ancestor of every royal house in ancient Indian history.

3. The Cosmic Cycle of Manvantaras. In Purāṇic cosmology, time is measured across immense cyclical epochs known as Manvantaras (literally "the interval or lifespan of a Manu"):

  • The Fourteen Manus: A single daytime period of Brahmā (*Kalpa*, lasting 4.32 billion human years) is divided into fourteen successive Manvantaras, each lasting 71 Mahāyugas (approximately 306.72 million years).
  • The Present Epoch: Each Manvantara is presided over by a distinct Manu, accompanied by a new set of Saptarṣis, a new Indra, and new divinities. The first Manu of the present Kalpa was Svāyambhuva Manu ("the self-born"), while humanity currently dwells within the seventh Manvantara, presided over by Vaivasvata Manu. Seven more Manus are prophesied to rule in the future before cosmic dissolution (*Mahāpralaya*).

4. The Supreme Lawgiver: The *Manusmṛti* and Hindu Jurisprudence. In legal and ethical philosophy (*Dharmaśāstra*), Manu stands as the undisputed supreme lawgiver of ancient India. The foundational legal code, the Mānava Dharmaśāstra (commonly known as the Manusmṛti or "Laws of Manu"), is traditionally attributed to his divine revelation:

  • Codification of Dharma: The text comprehensively outlines the duties of the four societal orders (*varṇas*), the four life-stages (*āśramas*), civil and criminal law, the rules of evidence, taxation, and royal statecraft (*rājadharma*).
  • The Sovereign's Duty: Manu emphasized that the king is the servant of Dharma, wielding the rod of justice (*daṇḍa*) solely to protect the weak from the strong (eradicating the law of the fishes, *mātsyanyāya*). While modern sociology critically analyzes the rigid caste and gender hierarchies codified in the historical redaction of the text (c. 2nd c. BCE–3rd c. CE), Manu's name remains indelibly synonymous with foundational statutory law.

5. Polysemy, Cognates, and Semantic Bridges: The Thinking Creature. The extraordinary Indo-European heritage of Manu illustrates the profound linguistic link between consciousness, human identity, and the primal father. Rooted in PIE *men- ("to think"), Manu is the direct mythological and linguistic cousin of Proto-Germanic *Mannus (celebrated by Tacitus as the divine progenitor of the Germanic tribes) and the English noun man. The semantic bridge is glorious in its philosophical clarity: what distinguishes humanity from the beast (*mṛga*) is not claws or horns, but manas (the mind, intellect, reflection). Thus, the first man was named *Manu*—the Thinker—and his children were named *Mānavas*—the thinking race. Across millennia, *Manu* has endured as the eternal civilizational monument to the emergence of rational consciousness, organized urban life, and lawful human society.
