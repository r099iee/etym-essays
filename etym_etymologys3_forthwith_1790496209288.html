forthwith is a transparent Middle English compound whose two halves are among the oldest words in Germanic, and its history is therefore the story of how forth plus with fused into a single unanalysable command for now.

The first half begins in PIE *per-, meaning forward, through, in front of — one of the most prolific roots in Indo-European. It gives Sanskrit para (beyond), pura (before), Greek para (beside), peri (around), pro (before), Latin per (through), pro (forth), primus (first), and Germanic *fur-, *for-, *fra-. From that Germanic stock come English fore, before, first, from, far, forth, further, German vor, fort, für, and Dutch voor, voort. Old English forþ meant onward in space and onward in time: an army goes forþ, a tale goes forþ, time goes forþ. The vowel shows regular Germanic lowering of PIE e to u/o before certain consonants, and the final -þ is the old adverbial suffix also seen in with, smith formations. Phonologically forþ passed through Middle English forth with lengthening before the fricative to modern /fɔːθ/.

  • The with half and its reversal: Old English wiþ originally meant against, opposite, toward — the opposite of modern with. One fought wiþ an enemy, stood wiþ a wall, looked wiþ the south. The sense together with belonged to a different word, mid (cognate with German mit, Greek meta). Between 1200 and 1400, under Scandinavian influence and semantic bleaching, with absorbed mid and flipped to accompaniment: to be with someone went from standing opposite them to standing beside them. Withstand, withdraw, withhold preserve the old adversative sense; with child, with joy show the new comitative one. Proto-Germanic *wiþra- (toward, against, opposite), cognate with German wider (against) and wieder (again), is the secure ancestor; deeper PIE linkage is disputed and is therefore left open here rather than forced.
  • Fusion in Middle English (c. 1300): forth with written as two words meant forth along with (the action) — the going-forth and the commanded act occur together, with no gap. Chronicles write he went forth with to London and then he departed forthwith: the space between the words closes as the meaning shifts from spatial accompaniment to temporal immediacy. By Chaucer's lifetime the solid form forthwith is established as an adverb of instant succession, parallel to herewith, therewith, wherewith, all formed on the same with = with it, at the same time pattern.
  • Scripture and law fix the form: the King James translators (1611) use forthwith dozens of times — forthwith Jesus stretched forth his hand, forthwith the cock crew — giving it solemn narrative snap: one clause ends, the next begins with no pause. Parallel to this, Tudor and Stuart statutes adopt forthwith as the standard word for binding haste: to pay, deliver, appear, or execute forthwith. Once embedded in writs and orders, the word is protected from ordinary sound change and semantic drift; lawyers copy it verbatim for centuries.
  • Sound and spelling stability: unlike many Middle English compounds, forthwith has barely changed its shape since 1400: the th + w cluster resists assimilation, the stress settles on the second syllable in careful speech (/fɔːθˈwɪð/) though first-syllable stress (/ˈfɔːθwɪð/) is heard in rapid legal dictation. The voiced final -th (/ð/ as in with) is kept distinct from the voiceless th (/θ/) of forth, preserving the compound boundary inside a single word.

By Ruskin's day forthwith was already withdrawing from conversation into writs and sermons, which is exactly why Ruskin chooses it for a domestic tyrant: the grandfather speaks like a statute. Modern speakers who say right away, straight away, ASAP, immediately are saying the same Germanic thing with younger words; forthwith remains as the robed elder among them, kept upright by the courts.
