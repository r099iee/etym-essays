Clop is the sound of a shod hoof meeting hard ground, caught in four letters: hollow, round, and unhurried — the night-time metronome of horses on lanes, ponies on cobbles, donkeys on stone bridges. It is both noun (the sound itself, usually plural: the clop of hoofs) and verb (to go with that sound: hoofs clopping down the road), and the two have never been separable, since the word was born as an imitation that immediately became an action.

In the source sentence the hobbits lie wrapped against the dark listening past nearer noises — the creak of wheels, and then beneath it the slow clop of the ponies' hoofs. The adjective matters as much as the noun: slow stretches the intervals, and the reader hears exactly what the hobbits hear — unhurried hoofbeats approaching out of the night, each beat round and separate. Tolkien, with a philologist's ear for sound-words, sets creak against clop, wood against hoof, and the whole menace of the approaching riders arrives through rhythm before it arrives through sight.

Core meaning in detail: Phonetically clop is echoic engineering of rare neatness: the initial cl- cluster cracks like contact itself (compare clap, click, clash), the open -o- hollows the sound out, and the final -p stops it dead, exactly as the hoof stops against stone. Dictionaries record it from the early 20th century as noun and verb together, and horses have obliged ever since by continuing to make precisely the noise it names. The plural-of-sound construction — the clop of hoofs — treats it as a countable acoustic event: one clop per hoof-fall, a slow walker dealing them like cards.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The hoofbeat sound (primary noun sense, from c. 1920s): the clop of horses on tarmac, the slow clop of the ponies' hoofs. The bridge is direct imitation: the word sounds like what it names, and needs no further history.
  • To move with that sound (primary verb sense): hoofs clopping down the lane, a donkey clopping over the bridge. The bridge is the standard sound-to-motion transfer of echoic verbs — as thud and rattle also became ways of going.
  • The gait or approach announced by sound (literary extension): to hear the clop of pursuit, the clop of the night patrol. The bridge is metonymy: the sound stands for the traveller, so that clop can carry menace, homecoming, or routine depending on who listens.
  • Any similar hollow knock (occasional transferred use): the clop of wooden clogs, of loose horseshoes on stone floors. The bridge is acoustic likeness: whatever strikes hollow and round may borrow the hoof's own word.
  • Figurative plodding (rare, jocular): to clop along through a dull task. The bridge runs from the hoof's steady rhythm to any steady dull progress — work done one beat at a time.

Usage, grammar, and register: clop is informal, vivid, and rural — stable yards, bridle paths, period drama — and it lives mostly in the singular mass of sound (the clop of hoofs) or the progressive verb (clopping down the road). Collocations are acoustic: slow clop, steady clop, distant clop, muffled clop. Writers pair it with surfaces that ring — cobbles, tarmac, stone bridges, hard-packed lanes — and with night, fog, and distance, where hearing outruns sight. Onomatopoeic spelling variants (klop, cloppety) appear in children's books, but clop and its festive cousin clip-clop own the standard. Compare its sharper sibling clack (hard and high) and its heavier brother thud (soft and low): clop sits exactly between, hollow like the hoof itself.
