Devilry is a native English formation — "devil" plus the noun suffix -ry — but both halves carry deep histories, and the word itself was born in the turbulence of late medieval England. Its story runs from ancient Greek slander through the Latin Bible and Old English homilies, down to the playful modern sense of impish mischief.

The making of "devil"

  • The noun devil descends from Greek διάβολος (diabolos), "slanderer, accuser", a nominalisation of the verb διαβάλλειν (diaballein), "to throw across, to slander", built from διά "across, through" and βάλλειν "to throw" — the slanderer "throws words across" at someone. The underlying Indo-European root is *gʷelǝ-, "to throw, hurl, reach".
  • Greek diabolos, chosen in the Septuagint and the New Testament to translate Hebrew śāṭān ("adversary, accuser"), entered Latin as diabolus and from Latin passed into Old English as dēofol, recorded from the 9th century in the Vespasian Psalter and in homilies, then into Middle English as devel.
  • The route through Latin explains the English form: the loan happened during the Christianisation of Anglo-Saxon England, in the same wave that brought Gospel, bishop, and angel; Old English dēo- reflects Latin di- with native vowel development.

The suffix -ry

  • English -ry comes from Old French -erie, a suffix forming abstract and collective nouns (place, trade, practice, quality), itself built on Latin -ārius (the "doer" suffix) plus the feminine -ia. Old French -erie entered Middle English wholesale in the 13th–14th centuries and attached freely to native and borrowed bases alike.
  • Because devil was a native-established noun by the 13th century, the productive suffix could be welded directly to it: English did not need to import "devilry" — it made it, in parallel with coinages such as "roguery" and "knavery".

Birth and career of the word

  • Late 14th century: develrye appears meaning "the works of the devil", "diabolical conduct", in religious prose; homiletic and devotional texts use it for demonic agency in the world.
  • 16th century: the word reaches its darkest peak during the witch-panic era. In tracts, trials, and commentary, "devilry" names sorcery, trafficking with demons, and the alleged evil deeds of witches — a word of real fear in Reformation controversy.
  • 17th–18th centuries: the supernatural sense remains alive (Puritan and Gothic writing), but the word begins sliding toward roguish misconduct — "the devilry of mischievous boys", cheating at games, cunning stratagems — and Restoration theatre and essayists treat it as an amused, theatrical quality.
  • 19th century: both poles coexist: Gothic and horror fiction use "devilry" for diabolical villainy, while school stories, dialect writing, and journalism use it for harmless mischief and craftiness. The spelling "deviltry" appears in the 18th century and remains an elegant variant.
  • 20th–21st centuries: the ordinary sense is mischievous roguery or cruelly clever conduct; the magical sense survives in fantasy, horror, and religious writing; specialised uses add cricketing "devilry" (erratic bounce) and affectionate irony ("cheeky devilry").

Socio-cultural and literary attestations

  • Shakespeare's stage villains and tragic heroes repeatedly invoke the devil, and the abstraction in -ry fits the theatrical villain's register perfectly.
  • The witch-trial records of the 16th–17th centuries show "devilry" as a near-technical charge word bridging theology and law — evidence of how a folk noun could enter formal accusation.
  • In the Gothic novel (Radcliffe, Lewis), "devilry" supplies the colour of diabolism even when the plot explains the horror away rationally; the word keeps its supernatural charge in reserve.

In sum, devilry packages two millennia of history into one vivid noun: Greek accusations, Latin and Old English Christianity, medieval demonology, early-modern witch panic, and the modern taming of the devil into a byword for clever, reckless mischief. Its sliding register from horror to charm is a small mirror of how Europe's image of evil itself softened.
