The noun behind pawing is a thoroughly Germanic word of uncertain ultimate origin. It is reconstructed as Proto-Germanic *pautō, 'paw, foot of an animal,' and it surfaces independently across the West Germanic dialects — Middle Dutch pote (modern Dutch poot), Middle Low German pōt(e), Middle High German pfaute (modern German Pfote) — a distribution that proves the word was already native Germanic before the dialects split, even though no Indo-European ancestor can be securely reconstructed for it. The word may well be sound-symbolic: the blunt, padded syllable paut echoes the soft thud of a paw striking the ground, just as English pad and pat mime the same contact. Germanic languages keep the word purely for the foot of an animal; only French, in its own way, would nudge it toward the human hand.

English did not inherit the word directly but received it by a Romance detour. The Franks, whose speech was Germanic, carried *pauta into Gaul, and Old French took it as poue, 'paw, fist.' Notably the French word could already mean a human fist — a small step toward the human sense that English would later develop. From this French form Anglo-Norman paue and then Middle English paue followed, recorded around 1300. For three centuries the English word remained purely nominal — the foot of a cat, dog, or bear — neutral, descriptive, wholly animal, with no human application at all.

Then, in the early seventeenth century, the noun generated a verb. To paw meant, of an animal, 'to strike, scrape, or beat at the ground with the forefoot': a horse paws the stable floor in impatience, a dog paws at the door. Within a generation the verb extended to human beings — first meaning 'to handle roughly or clumsily,' the hand used as a blunt instrument rather than a tool, and then, by the eighteenth century, 'to fondle or maul with the hands,' a sense that remains current and is now chiefly pejorative.

  • The moral downgrade: the word's history is a steady transfer of animality onto the human hand. Where grope implies careful, deliberate human search, pawing keeps the whole flat forefoot — blunt, broad, unspecialized — so that to paw at a person is to touch them with the dignity of a beast; animality becomes accusation.
  • Fixed patterns: the phrasal verbs paw at, paw over, paw through, and paw about crystallize in the modern period; all are informal, vivid, and faintly demeaning, and formal prose prefers grope or fumble.
  • The cat's paw: the idiom 'cat's paw' (a dupe used by another) comes from the fable of the monkey who uses a cat's paw to pull chestnuts from the fire — the paw as the blind instrument of another's profit, the body doing the work the mind will not.
  • Technical life: in ethology, pawing is the technical word for the ground-scraping display of horses and cattle; in boxing, a pawing jab is a probing, uncommitted touch that tests range rather than landing force — the animal verb kept alive in two professional registers.

In the passage the participle is used in its animal sense, restored to full literalness: 'He went pawing about.' A creature reduced to forepaws, touching everything with the flat pad-like surfaces of its hands, moves blindly through the dark; the -ing form makes the search ongoing and open-ended. The word's long journey — from the padded Germanic syllable for an animal's foot, through Old French and Anglo-Norman, from noun to verb, from animal to human to moral accusation — is gathered into the single present participle, and the dehumanizing force of the sentence rests on the fact that the word has never quite let go of its animal origin. To go pawing about is to have surrendered one's hands, and one's standing, to the beast.
