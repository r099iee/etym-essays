Single root word (ACCURSED). Linear chain within the history of English; the noun curs has no secure PIE ancestry, so the chain begins with the earliest attested English form. → Old English curs 'a curse, malediction' (attested before 1000; origin unknown — no agreed PIE root, no secure Germanic cognates; proposals range from an ecclesiastical Latin source to Celtic contact, all contested) → Old English cursian 'to curse, to wish evil upon' (verbal derivative of curs) → Old English acursian 'to place under a curse, to curse thoroughly' (formed with the native intensive prefix a- 'thoroughly, completely' + cursian) → Middle English acursen / acursed 'under a curse' (participial adjective, 13th–14th c.) → ACCURSED 'under a curse; used to express strong dislike or anger; detestable, abominable' (Modern English; also the rare verb to accurse; often pronounced with three syllables /əˈkɜːsɪd/ as the participial form) Debate note: the pre-English origin of curse is genuinely unknown. Old English curs has no cognates in the other Germanic languages. Popular older explanations derived it from Latin cursus 'course' (reading a curse as a sentence or course of fate) or compared Celtic forms such as Old Irish cúrsagim 'I reprove'; modern handbooks (OED) record it as 'origin unknown'. No PIE root should therefore be asserted for the family.
