The story of TEMPERATE is the story of an image: the image of mixing things in due proportion. The word descends from Latin temperāre, “to mix in due proportion, to blend, to regulate, to restrain”, a verb whose root, according to the traditional etymology, goes back to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “to stretch, to span, to measure” — the same root that lies behind Latin tempus, “time”, understood as the due measure or season of something. This root stage is itself debated: modern scholars such as de Vaan are cautious, some preferring to derive temperāre from a separate sense of measuring or fitting, and no one treats the PIE ancestry as settled. What is certain is the classical Latin image: the temperate thing is the thing whose parts are blended in the right proportions, neither too much nor too little.

From blending to virtue.

  • In classical medical and philosophical language the verb moved from mixing metals or liquids to the blending of the four humours in the body — a body whose humours were in due proportion had a “temperate” or “tempered” constitution, and this is the root of temperament.
  • From the bodily mixture the word shifted to the passions: to temper the passions is to bring them into due proportion, and the temperate man is the self-restrained man who avoids excess in food, drink, anger, and desire.
  • Latin temperātus, the past participle, gave the adjective; Old French took it as temperé or tempré, and Middle English adopted it in the fourteenth century as temperat.

Sound changes and form. The change from Latin temperāre to English temperate is small: the loss of the final infinitive, the shortening and levelling of the internal vowels, and the suffix -ātus giving -ate. English adopted the word largely intact, which is typical of learned Romance borrowings that entered after the Norman Conquest through French.

The climatic sense and the doctrine of zones. The second great sense of the word comes from classical geography. Ancient geographers divided the inhabited world into zones: a torrid zone at the equator, frozen zones at the poles, and, between them, the “temperate” zones — so called because their heat was thought to be mixed in due proportion, moderate, neither burning nor freezing. English took up this sense in the scientific literature of the early modern period, and it survives in temperate climate, temperate zone, and temperate forest. The connection between the two modern senses is not accidental: both rest on the idea of a mixture kept between extremes.

Modern technical extensions.

  • In ecology and biology, “temperate” names the flora, fauna, forests, and woodlands of the mid-latitude zones — temperate deciduous forest, temperate rainforest.
  • In virology, a temperate bacteriophage is one that does not destroy its host but lies dormant within it, its genome integrated into the host’s — a modern jargon sense that preserves the ancient idea of a moderated, non-fatal relation.
  • In chemistry and materials, tempered (steel, glass) is a cognate: to temper a substance is to treat and regulate it, to bring it into the right condition.

Socio-cultural history. The moral sense of the word was given new weight by the Temperance movement of the nineteenth century, which preached moderation or outright abstinence in alcohol; temperance is the noun from the same root, and a “temperate” man came, in that context, to mean one who abstains. In humoral medicine the word had earlier carried scientific weight, and in everyday moral talk it still names the cardinal virtue of moderation, the golden mean between deficiency and excess.

Literary and everyday life. In the sentence “Ser Criston is not temperate,” the word works in its oldest moral sense: it says the man is unbalanced, given to excess of temper and violence, lacking the due proportion of passion. Across two thousand years, from a Roman image of mixing wine and water in the right measure to a fantasy novel’s judgment of a violent knight, the word has kept its core: the good mixture, the measured thing, the middle way.
