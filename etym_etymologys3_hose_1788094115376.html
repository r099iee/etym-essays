The history of hose is the history of a very old Germanic clothing word that changed its job description. Its earliest securely reconstructible stage is the Proto-Germanic noun *husǭ (stem *husōn-), meaning “a covering for the leg, a stocking”. The word was common property of the Germanic peoples: Old Norse hosa “stocking, leg covering”, Old Saxon and Middle Dutch hose, Old High German hosa “legging, knee-covering”, and modern German Hose “trousers, breeches” are all its descendants. The contrast between the two modern outcomes is instructive: in German, Hose rose to become the ordinary word for trousers, while in English hose was gradually squeezed out of the garment vocabulary by stocking and tights, and then reinvented itself as a word for rubber tubing.

The uncertain origin. No secure Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed for *husǭ. The standard dictionaries — the OED and Etymonline alike — label the origin unknown. Some scholars have floated a connection with a root meaning “to cover” (which would tie it to Latin casa “hut, shelter”), and others have suspected a pre-Germanic substrate word taken into the clothing vocabulary, but neither suggestion commands general assent. The honest summary is that hose is an ancient word whose deepest ancestry is lost; the chain of attested forms begins in Germanic and goes no further back with confidence.

Old English. In Old English the word appears as hosa, a masculine n-stem noun meaning “stocking, covering for the leg”, attested in glosses and prose from the earliest period of written English. Throughout the Old English period the word has exactly one job: naming the garment that covers the leg. It is a plain, workaday word of everyday dress, with no special register.

Middle English and the garment centuries. By the Middle English period the form hose (plural hosen) is the standard English word for leg coverings of every kind. Chaucer's Canterbury Tales offers a famous glimpse of the word in everyday use: the Wife of Bath wears “hosen of fyn scarlet reed, / Ful streite yteyd” — scarlet hose, tightly laced. In the same centuries the word entered the vocabulary of war as well as fashion: mail hose, plate hose and quilted hose protected the legs of knights from the 11th to the 16th century, and it is this armour sense that Tolkien revives in the source passage, where a dragon's legs are “covered with hose of a fine and flexible metal mesh”. The 14th–16th centuries were the golden age of the word's garment career: trunk hose, paned hose, cannon hose and nether stocks followed the fashions of the European courts, and the derivatives hosiery (the trade and its products) and hosier (the maker or seller) were formed from it.

The retreat from fashion. From the 16th century onwards, the knitted garment industry began to favour the word stocking for the close-fitting covering of the foot and lower leg, and hose steadily lost ground in the shops. By the 18th century the garment sense survives mainly in elevated, historical or technical use; it lingers in the compound pantyhose and in the collective hosiery, but as a living word for legwear it is finished.

The reinvention: the water tube. In 1807 the record shows the decisive semantic leap: hose is applied to a flexible tube for conveying liquids. The bridge is purely visual and functional — a hose is a long, hollow, flexible cylinder, exactly as a stocking is a long, hollow, flexible cylinder for the leg. The new sense rode the technological wave of the 19th century: fire brigades adopted canvas and rubber hose for delivering water under pressure, the domestic garden hose became a fixture of suburban life, and industry multiplied the word into air hose, fuel hose, radiator hose and hydraulic hose. The verb to hose (“to wash or spray with a hose”) follows in the same century, and British English coined the tautological hosepipe for the garden implement.

The slang plunge. In 20th-century American English the word took a second, entirely unexpected turn. To hose someone came to mean to cheat, swindle or defeat them harshly, and the participle hosed means ruined, broken or in serious trouble. The image is of being drenched and helpless, flattened by a jet one cannot resist. From this slang base, computing culture of the late 20th century adopted hosed for a system, file or configuration that is corrupted, unbootable or unrecoverable. A word that began as medieval legwear has thus ended up describing a broken hard drive, while its German cousin quietly became the word for trousers.
