To plummet is to fall or drop straight down, swiftly, steeply, and usually uncontrollably, from a considerable height to a much lower point. As an intransitive verb it foregrounds manner and trajectory rather than mere change of location: gravity, verticality, acceleration, and loss of control. A stone plummets into a well, an aircraft plummets toward earth, a hawk plummets onto prey with folded wings, temperature plummets overnight. The core image-schema is that of a heavy object released in air, falling true like a dropped lead weight, without tumbling, drifting, gliding, or recovering.

In its primary literal sense, plummet belongs to the physics of bodies in free fall. Dictionaries define it as to plunge straight down through air or water under gravity. Collocates reveal selectional preferences: cliffs, balconies, bridges, towers, aircraft, elevators, stones, bodies. Adverbial modification stresses perpendicularity and speed — plummet straight down, plummet vertically, plummet sickeningly, plummet hundreds of feet. Unlike fall, which is neutral and can be gentle, or tumble, which implies rotation, or plunge, which can be deliberate and into a medium, plummet entails helpless, arrow-straight, accelerating descent. The semantic bridge to the noun is direct metonymy: the original plummet was a small lead weight on a string, a plumb-bob, fisherman's sinker, or sounding lead. What lead does when dropped is fall fast and true, so to fall like a plummet is to plummet.

In its secondary and now statistically dominant sense, plummet is a figurative quantitative verb meaning to decrease abruptly and severely in value, amount, level, or esteem. Prices, stocks, profits, currencies, housing markets, oil demand, temperatures, barometric readings, poll numbers, ratings, test scores, attendance, and morale all plummet. In the source sentence — reading scores in the schools plummet — the sense is precisely this chart-metaphor: literacy measurements dropping precipitously as if traced by a line on a graph suddenly falling off a cliff. Journalism favours the verb because it dramatizes data, converting an abstract table into a physical event with alarm, failure, and loss of agency. Corpus evidence shows plummet strongly prefers downward subjects with negative evaluation and large magnitude: plummeted 30%, plummeted to a record low, plummeted for the third year. The polysemy bridge here is visual-conceptual metaphor of the kind Lakoff and Johnson describe as DOWN IS LESS and DOWN IS BAD, mapped onto a line-chart: numbers are objects arrayed vertically, less is lower, sudden less is sudden falling like lead.

In its tertiary extended senses, plummet covers aeronautical, emotional, and existential plunges where literal and figurative blur. Aircraft, helicopters, drones, and birds plummet when they lose lift; hearts, spirits, hopes, and stomachs plummet when news shocks the body. Her heart plummeted when she heard the results; his spirits plummeted after the defeat. The bridge is embodied simulation: sudden dread is felt as heaviness and dropping in the gut, as if an inner weight fell. Related idioms preserve the tool origin even where the verb is absent: plumb the depths to sound or explore deeply, plumb line for true vertical, out of plumb for crooked, plumb as adverb for exactly vertical and then exactly — plumb in the middle — and aplomb for self-possession, literally standing vertically true like a plumb-bob.

Domain usages differentiate the verb by register and expectation. In construction, surveying, masonry, and sailing, the noun persists: a plummet or plumb-bob hung on a cord tests verticality against gravity, a sounding plummet tests depth. In aviation and accident reporting, plummet encodes uncontrolled descent, triggering investigation into stall, engine failure, or weather. In finance and economics, it encodes sharp depreciation, typically worse than dip, slip, slide, slump and comparable to crash, collapse, nosedive but foregrounding trajectory rather than impact. In education and public policy, as in reading scores plummet, it encodes systemic failure and moral panic, often collocating with alarmingly, disastrously, precipitously. In meteorology, temperatures plummet encodes rapid cooling overnight. In everyday psychology, to plummet into depression, debt, chaos encodes loss of control. Across domains the constant is sudden vertical drop from high to low with connotations of weight, inevitability, and danger.

  • Literal physical fall: to drop perpendicularly under gravity — the vase plummeted from the balcony, the stone plummeted into the well. Semantic core is manner: straight, swift, heavy, accelerating. Polysemy bridge is metonymy from instrument to behaviour: the lead weight defines the manner of falling.
  • Figurative quantitative collapse: to decrease abruptly in value, amount, or level — shares plummeted 30%, house prices plummeted, reading scores plummeted. Bridge is chart-metaphor mapping numbers to height; reasoning is image-schematic: DOWN IS LESS, SUDDEN DOWN IS ALARMING LOSS.
  • Aeronautical plunge: aircraft or birds losing altitude catastrophically — the plane plummeted 10,000 feet in a minute. Bridge is literal application to heavier-than-air flight where lift fails and gravity resumes, blending physics with disaster narrative.
  • Emotional and psychological drop: hearts, hopes, confidence plummeting — her heart plummeted at the news. Bridge is embodiment and interoception: fear and disappointment felt as visceral sinking, internalizing the lead weight.
  • Noun: the lead weight itself: a plumb-bob, sinker, or sounding lead — a mason's plummet, to plumb the depths. This historically primary sense anchors all verbal senses; without the dense dull metal that falls true, the vivid verb would not exist. Bridge is etymological retention in plumb, plumb line, aplomb.
