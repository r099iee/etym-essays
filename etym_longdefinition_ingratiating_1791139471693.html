Ingratiating means deliberately charming, flattering, or pleasing in order to win favour, approval, or advantage, and by extension anything that wins favour because it is pleasing, whether calculated or not. An ingratiating person smiles too readily, agrees too quickly, remembers your birthday, laughs at your jokes, and hopes you will promote, forgive, or love them for it. The core is favour-seeking pleasantness.

The dominant active sense is pejorative: calculated sycophancy. An ingratiating manner, tone, letter, or grin implies insincerity, servility, and strategic warmth. The ingratiator is not kind for kindness's sake but performs kindness as investment. Hence the prompt's ingratiating centipedes: the many-legged creatures are imagined as fawning flatterers, wriggling pleasingly before they devour man. The adjective makes them morally low as well as physically low.

  • Deliberately flattering behaviour: ingratiating smile, laugh, compliment, phone call. Example: the intern's ingratiating eagerness annoyed the team. Implies ulterior motive.
  • Servile agreeableness: ingratiating tone, demeanour, apology. Example: an ingratiating interviewee who agrees with everything. Near-synonym: obsequious, sycophantic.
  • Pleasing / endearing quality (neutral-positive): ingratiating voice, simplicity, warmth, novel. Example: the singer's ingratiating huskiness won fans. Here calculation is absent; the thing naturally wins hearts. This use is common but purists hear a shadow of flattery.
  • Social-psychology tactic: ingratiation as impression management - flattery, favour-doing, opinion conformity to increase liking. Studied as distinct from self-promotion and exemplification.

Polysemy hinges on intention versus effect. All ingratiating things bring the agent into gratia, into grace or favour. If the bringing is deliberate, we condemn it as manipulative; if it is incidental, we may praise it as charming. Context and adverbials disambiguate: deliberately / cynically ingratiating versus naturally / genuinely ingratiating. Without markers, readers default to the cynical reading, so writers praising innocent charm often prefer endearing, winning, or engaging to avoid taint.

  • People: ingratiating colleague, student, salesman, politician. Collocates with grin, nod, bow, chuckle. Implies low status seeking high favour.
  • Animals (figurative): ingratiating dog, cat, centipede. A dog wagging for treats is affectionately ingratiating; the prompt's centipedes are sinisterly so.
  • Texts and speech: ingratiating letter, preface, dedication, speech. Flattering address to patron, reviewer, or electorate. Classic example: dedications to aristocrats in 18th-century books.
  • Aesthetic charm: ingratiating melody, prose style, actor. Easy, accessible pleasantness that critics may dismiss as crowd-pleasing.

Grammatically ingratiating is participial adjective, gradable (more / most ingratiating, very ingratiating) and progressive verb form (he is ingratiating himself with the boss). Typical frames: ingratiating with / to / towards someone. Register is formal-literate; in casual speech people say sucking up, brown-nosing, bootlicking for the same behaviour with coarser force. Opposite pole is aloof, abrasive, defiant, or genuinely gracious without strategy.

To master ingratiating is to hear gratia inside it: grace, favour, thanks. Every ingratiating act trades performed pleasantness for future grace, whether a promotion, pardon, or meal. The word reminds us that charm can be currency, and that audiences instinctively audit the exchange rate.
