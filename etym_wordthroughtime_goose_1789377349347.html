- Old English (pre-1100) - Attested as *gōs* (plural *gēs*), derived from Proto-Germanic *gans; central to Anglo-Saxon agriculture for meat, feathers, down, and quill pens; demonstrated early i-mutation plural.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) - Spelled *gos* / *goos* (plural *gees*); firmly rooted in village life, medieval fables (Reynard the Fox), and feast days (Michaelmas); developed its earliest proverbial associations with silliness and gossip.
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Elizabethan Era) - Shakespeare immortalized phrases like "wild-goose chase" (in *Romeo and Juliet*); tailors coined the term "tailor's goose" for their heavy, curved-handled pressing iron.
- 18th to 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Established as the quintessential British Christmas feast bird, immortalized in Charles Dickens's *A Christmas Carol* (1843); phrase "cook someone's goose" appeared in mid-nineteenth-century British slang.
- Early 20th Century - Developed the physical American slang verb "to goose" (to jab playfully in the buttocks), rapidly expanding in mechanical and financial registers to mean accelerating or invigorating a system.
- 21st Century - Continues as an essential biological classification, a timeless nursery figure ("Mother Goose"), and a universally recognized symbol of gentle foolishness and rustic resilience.
