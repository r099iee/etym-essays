• cheerio: similar because it is a British farewell and drinking toast, close in tone and period; differs because it is far more widely used, less class-marked and still current in ordinary British speech.
  • so long: similar because it is a casual farewell with the same throwaway cheerfulness; differs because it is international rather than British, neutral in register, and never a drinking toast.
  • goodbye: similar because it is the standard valediction; differs because it is neutral and unmarked, with none of the hearty upper-class flavour of bung-oh.
  • farewell: similar because it is a formal or literary goodbye; differs because of its solemn, elevated register and its suggestion of a longer or final parting.
  • toodle-oo: similar because it is a dated British upper-class farewell; differs because it is more playful and affected, and it has no toast sense.
  • toodle-pip: similar because it is an even more archaic posh farewell; differs because of its deliberately silly, music-hall flavour and its complete disappearance from serious use.
  • pip-pip: similar because it is a 1920s British greeting or farewell; differs because it can greet as well as part, and its clipped, cheerful tone is more neutral than the drinking-derived bung-oh.
  • chin-chin: similar because it is a dated British toast and farewell; differs because of its more openly jocular, slightly colonial flavour and its stronger association with drinking.
  • cheers: similar because it is a British toast that also serves as thanks and farewell; differs because it is fully current, multifunctional and not marked as dated.
  • bottoms up: similar because it is a drinking formula urging someone to empty the glass; differs because it is an imperative with no farewell use.
  • down the hatch: similar because it is a jocular drinking formula; differs because it focuses on swallowing and has no valedictory sense.
  • here’s how: similar because it is an older toast meaning “here’s to your health”; differs because it is specifically a toast and because of its American, slightly old-fashioned flavour.
  • skoal: similar because it is a Scandinavian-derived toast; differs because of its borrowed, ceremonial and slightly literary status.
  • prost: similar because it is a German toast; differs because it is foreign, beer-centred and never used for parting.
  • sláinte: similar because it is an Irish and Scottish Gaelic toast meaning “health”; differs because of its language and its formal, communal drinking context.
  • adieu: similar because it is a literary farewell; differs because of its French origin, formal register and sense of finality.
  • au revoir: similar because it is a French farewell meaning “until we see each other again”; differs because it is explicitly future-oriented and not a toast.
