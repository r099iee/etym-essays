Thyme's etymology is a fossil of Bronze-Age ritual. The word's story begins not in a garden but in a temple, with the smoke of sacrifice. The Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeu- means "to smoke, to rise in a cloud, to fume, to give off vapour", and its descendants across the family all cluster around air thickened with smoke or haze: Sanskrit dhūmá- "smoke", which passes straight into modern Hindi धुआँ (dhuā̃) and Odia ଧୂଆଁ (dhuā̃); Latin fūmus "smoke", whence Italian fumo, Spanish humo, French fumée, and the Latin-derived English fumigate; Lithuanian dū́mai and Old Church Slavonic dymŭ, the Russian дым (dym) "smoke"; and inside Germanic, Old English dūst — our word dust, the earth that rises in a cloud — Old English dox "dark", the ancestor of dusk, and Old High German tunst, the German Dunst "vapour, haze". The whole family is the vocabulary of burning and clouding, and it is from this smoke-world that the herb takes its name. In ancient Greek the root produced the verb θύειν (thýein), "to burn a sacrifice, to offer incense, to fill with smoke", and from the same stock Greek raised a remarkable family of words: θύος (thýos) "burnt offering, incense", θυμίαμα (thymíama) "incense", and — most famous — θυμός (thymós), "breath, spirit, soul, courage", the word at the centre of Greek psychology, which in Homer names the raging, smoking seat of emotion and courage in the chest. The link between smoke and spirit is exactly parallel to the Latin anima "breath, soul" and the English spirit from spirare "to breathe": breath, vapour, and smoke were the same substance, the stuff of life that rises and dissipates. From θύειν the Greeks formed θύμον (thýmon) for the aromatic herb — literally "the fumigating herb", the plant of the burnt offering, burned in temples to purify the air and honour the gods, its name fixed by its earliest use as incense rather than as food. The very fact that thyme's name is a verb turned into a noun, "the burning-plant", preserves a Bronze-Age practice of temple fumigation in the vocabulary of the kitchen; and the same ritual root surfaces again in the Greek-derived English word thymol, the antiseptic phenol of thyme oil, and in the medical term thymus, the gland so named by Galen for a supposed resemblance to the flower of thyme. Greek cookery and medicine carried θύμον into Latin as thymum, and the word then took the long Roman road north through the Romance vernaculars, appearing in Old French as thym, a learned-sounding borrowing from the Latin world. Middle English took it in the fourteenth century as tyme — spelled, and pronounced, exactly like our word time, a homophone that has enriched English wordplay for six hundred years. For two full centuries English speakers wrote and said "tyme" with no trace of the h. Then, in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, Renaissance humanists restored classical spellings across the whole learned vocabulary, and the h crept back into the written form — thyme — a purely orthographic resurrection modelled on Latin thymum and Greek θύμον, which left the pronunciation /taɪm/ untouched and created the celebrated mismatch between the word's spelling and its sound. The h of modern thyme is thus a learned ghost: it is the most visible trace, in a common kitchen word, of the entire scholarly apparatus of the Renaissance, the same movement that restored the h in throne, theatre, and Anthony. Once naturalised, thyme spread through the whole of European domestic life. In the medieval kitchen and still-room it was the sovereign seasoning and preservative, its thymol oil keeping meat and fighting infection; herbalists classed it among the antiseptic herbs, and its reputation as the herb of courage ran so deep that knights embroidered sprigs of it on their scarves and ladies wove it into favours. In the Mediterranean world its honey was already legendary: the bees of Mount Hymettus in Attica, feeding on wild thyme, produced the honey that antiquity prized above all others, and the plant itself carpeted the dry, sun-baked hillsides that modern ecology calls garigue and phrygana. Wild thyme (Thymus serpyllum, "mother of thyme") became in northern Europe the scent of chalk downs and sheep-walks — precisely the sensory world Tolkien evokes when he sets "the thyme and the sage and the marjoram" among the rabbit-cropped turf of the vanished high country. From the smoke of Greek altars to the thyme of an English roast, from the θυμός of Homeric courage to the antiseptic thymol of the modern pharmacy, the word has never lost its root's two meanings at once: it is the plant that burns, and the plant that breathes.
