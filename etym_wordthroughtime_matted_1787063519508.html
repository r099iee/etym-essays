- Late Old English / Middle English (1000–1400) - Noun *matte* (from Late Latin *matta*) referred to woven rush coverings; the verb *matten* arose to mean weaving rushes or pressing fibers into floor mats.
- 15th–16th c. (Fiber compaction & felting) - Extended to describe hair, fur, fleece, and vegetation that had grown naturally tangled, clotted, and compacted together like a rush mat.
- 17th–18th c. (Botanical & landscape description) - Adopted in landscape and botanical writings (Milton, Evelyn) to describe impenetrable thickets, root systems, and interwoven forest floors.
- 19th c. (Art framing & French homophonic convergence) - Influenced by the French borrowing *mat* (dull/lusterless), *matted* began to be used for framing pictures with cardboard borders (passe-partout) and dull, non-reflective finishes.
- Contemporary (Veterinary, Ecological, Styling) - Standard across veterinary medicine (matted fur/coats), ecology (matted root systems/algal mats), and visual arts/materials.
