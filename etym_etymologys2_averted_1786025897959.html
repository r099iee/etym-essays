AVERTED = Latin avertere "to turn away" (ab- "away" + vertere "to turn") + English past-tense suffix -ed.

PIE *h2epo
"away, off, from" - the adverb behind Latin ab- (also Greek apo, Sanskrit apa)

▼

Latin ab-
"away from, off" - preposition and verbal prefix (Classical Latin)

▼ (combines with the turning-verb branch below)

PIE *wert-
"to turn, to wind" - the root of Latin vertere

▼

Latin vertere
"to turn, to change, to convert" (1st c. BCE)

▼

Latin avertere
"to turn away, to avert" - formed as ab- + vertere (Classical Latin, attested in Caesar and Cicero)

▼

Latin aversus
"turned away, averted" - past participle of avertere (1st c. BCE)

▼

English avert
"to turn away; to ward off, prevent" - borrowed from Latin avertere in the late 15th century

▼

AVERTED
"turned away; prevented or warded off" - the modern English past-tense and past-participle form of avert

Note on a debated point: Modern French avertir ("to warn, inform") is NOT the source of English avert; it descends from Latin advertere ("to turn toward", hence "to direct attention to"). The English verb is a straight scholarly borrowing of Latin avertere, and the semantic shift from "turn away" (eyes) to "prevent" (danger) happened inside Latin and English, not in French.
