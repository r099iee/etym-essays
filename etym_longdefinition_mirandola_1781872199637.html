In Walter Pater's aesthetic catalogue, "Pico of Mirandola" names one of the exemplary 'engaging personalities' whom the sensitive observer values as a herb, a wine, or a gem is valued—for the unique virtue, the special impression of pleasure, each produces. Alongside La Gioconda and the hills of Carrara, Mirandola stands for the human-philosophical instance of Pater's principle of specialized pleasure: a brilliant, many-sided humanist character whose mind and presence affect the observer with a singular, unrepeatable impression. Pater is less concerned here with doctrinal detail than with Pico as an aesthetic-human phenomenon, a figure whose whole intellectual life has the quality of a rare object. The tone is appreciative and exemplary rather than biographical; the name functions as a cultured allusion that presupposes literacy in the cast of characters of Renaissance humanism. Who Pico was. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494), born at the castle of Mirandola near Modena, was an Italian Renaissance philosopher, philologist, and polymath. He studied canon law, philosophy, and the sacred languages; he is remembered above all for the Oration on the Dignity of Man (De hominis dignitate, composed c. 1486), a manifesto of Renaissance humanism in which man is defined by his freedom to choose his own nature and place in the cosmos—nowhere fixed, he may descend toward the brute or ascend toward the angelic. Pico's syncretic program sought to reconcile Plato and Aristotle, Scholasticism and Kabbalah, Hebrew, Christian, Greek, and Arab wisdom; his nine hundred theses, proposed for public debate in Rome in 1486, were censured, and his apology embroiled him in controversy with the papacy. He died young, at thirty-one, in 1494, leaving an unfinished legacy that the Romantic and Victorian imagination—Pater chief among them—transformed into the type of the brilliant, tragic, many-sided humanist. The name and its parts. Mirandola is a toponymic surname-element: the town of Mirandola in the Emilia-Romagna region of northern Italy, seat of the Pico counts and later dukes of Mirandola and Concordia. The full style Giovanni Pico della Mirandola means "Giovanni Pico of Mirandola": Pico is the family name, while della Mirandola marks lordship of the town and its territory. In English the name most often appears as Pico della Mirandola or Pico of Mirandola, the latter the form Pater uses. Mirandola should not be confused with the given name or with a common noun: although its Latin etymon (miranda, neuter plural of the gerundive mirandus, "to be wondered at, admirable") carries the sense of wonder, English treats the word purely as an onomastic element. Polysemy and modern usage:
  • Place name: the town and comune of Mirandola in Emilia-Romagna (province of Modena), seat of a medieval lordship and later a duchy; in Pater, "the hills of Carrara" is a landscape, but Mirandola is a town of the plain.
  • Dynastic and toponymic title: della Mirandola, borne by the Pico counts and dukes of Mirandola and Concordia, marking feudal lordship of the town.
  • Personal reference: Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, the Renaissance philosopher, and by extension his thought, his Oration on the Dignity of Man, or his syncretic program.
  • Cultural byword: "Pico of Mirandola" as a metonym for the brilliant, many-sided Renaissance humanist—polyglot, audacious, syncretizing—used in literary criticism, art history, and Renaissance studies, exactly as Pater uses it.
  • Caveat: Mirandola is not a common English noun meaning "wonder"; English uses it only as a place-name and title element.
Collocations and register. Collocations are almost entirely historical and onomastic: Pico della Mirandola, Pico of Mirandola, the Count of Mirandola, the Counts of Mirandola, the Oration of Pico della Mirandola, the Duchy of Mirandola. Register is scholarly, art-historical, and Renaissance-studies formal. Recognizing the allusion signals literacy in the Renaissance cast that Victorian aesthetic prose assumes; the name operates in Pater's list as the human counterpart to the artistic Gioconda and the geological Carrara, completing the triad of personality, art, and landscape as sources of unique virtue.
