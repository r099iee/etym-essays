SION (proper noun; coexisting form Zion). The Latin- and Vulgate-derived spelling of Zion used in early modern English Bibles, liturgy, and poetry — most famously by Milton in Paradise Lost, Book I (1667): "Or if Sion Hill / Delight thee more." It denotes the sacred hill of Jerusalem: historically the narrow southeastern ridge held by the Jebusites and taken by David (the City of David), then by synecdoche the whole holy city, then the people of God, the Church, and finally the heavenly Jerusalem. As a proper noun its polysemy is a ladder of escalating reference built over roughly three thousand years. Senses and uses:
  • The dry-height stronghold (oldest, topographical sense). Ṣiyyōn is first the Jebusite citadel on the southeastern ridge of Jerusalem, taken by David c. 1000 BCE (2 Samuel 5:7). The name probably means "dry height," from the Northwest Semitic root denoting dryness and parched ground (Hebrew ṣiyyā "drought, dry land"; Ugaritic ṣyw "to be dry").
  • The City of David / royal seat. After the conquest the name shifts from the mere fortress to the royal city David founded there — "the strong hold of Zion: the same is the city of David."
  • The sanctuary of God (cultic sense). With the translation of the Ark (2 Samuel 6) and the building of the Temple by Solomon (1 Kings 8), Zion becomes the seat of the divine presence: "the Lord dwelleth in Zion," "the mountain of the house of the Lord" (Micah 4; Isaiah 2).
  • The whole holy city (synecdochic sense). In the Psalms and Prophets Zion extends to the entire city of Jerusalem: "the gates of Zion," "the daughter of Zion," "beauty of Zion." The part stands for the whole.
  • The people of God (national sense). "Zion" becomes a name for the community of Israel itself — those who dwell in Zion, who sing the songs of Zion (Psalm 137:3), who return to Zion from exile.
  • The Church (Christian sense). The New Testament transfers the name to the heavenly and spiritual community: "ye are come unto mount Sion, and unto the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" (Hebrews 12:22). Hymns use "Zion" freely for the Church on earth.
  • The heavenly Jerusalem (eschatological sense). Revelation and apocalyptic tradition identify Zion with the perfected city of God, the dwelling of the redeemed: the New Jerusalem come down from heaven.
  • The Miltonic Sion Hill (17th-century literary sense). In Paradise Lost, Sion Hill is set beside Siloa's Brook and against the pagan Aonian Mount: the biblical hill of revelation is Milton's true source of divine song, the rival that outdoes Helicon.
  • The modern symbolic and political sense of the related form Zion. Since the 19th century, "Zion" (the standard modern spelling) has carried national and ideological weight — the Jewish homeland, the focus of Zionism — while the archaic spelling Sion remains confined to liturgy, poetry, and the King James tradition.
Historical bridges and reasoning:
  • The ladder of senses is a single climb: a dry fortress hill becomes a king's city, a king's city becomes God's sanctuary, God's sanctuary becomes the whole holy city, the city becomes the people, the people becomes the Church, and the Church becomes the heavenly Jerusalem. Each step is a synecdoche or spiritualization of the last.
  • Formal transmission: Hebrew Ṣiyyōn → Greek Σιών Siōn (Septuagint) → Latin Sion (Vulgate) → English Sion (early English Bibles, the Geneva Bible, KJV, Milton) beside the alternative Zion favored by later Protestant and modern usage.
  • Register split: Sion is the older Latin-derived, liturgical, poetic form; Zion is the modern standard spelling and the bearer of contemporary national symbolism.
Meaning today: Sion is archaic, essentially confined to the King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, hymnody, and early modern poetry, while Zion carries the full modern symbolic load — sanctuary, Church, heavenly Jerusalem, and national homeland.
