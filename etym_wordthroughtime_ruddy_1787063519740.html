- Old English (8th century): rudig meant "reddish," describing red coloring and complexions; the word's first home was the healthy, red face.
- Middle English (13th–15th centuries): extended from complexions to any red or reddish glow — cheeks, sunsets, firelight — a general warm-red descriptive adjective.
- 16th–18th centuries: a standard poetic and descriptive adjective for the glow of health, of fire, of morning light; also used for hearty, stout people.
- 19th–20th century Britain: developed a second life as a mild oath and intensifier standing in for "bloody" — from color adjective to euphemistic swear-substitute, still in use today.
- Today: in descriptive prose a reddish, glowing hue (especially faces and firelight); in British slang a mild expletive; in ornithology a taxonomic label (ruddy duck, ruddy turnstone).
