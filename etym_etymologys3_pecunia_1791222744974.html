Pecunia is the textbook case of how money gets its name: herd becomes coin, and the herd-word becomes the money-word.

Its prehistory is the Proto-Indo-European root *peku- meaning livestock as movable wealth. The root is pan-Indo-European: Sanskrit pacu-m, Avestan pasu-, Lithuanian pekus (cattle), Old English feoh (cattle, goods, fee), Gothic faihu, Old High German fihu (modern Vieh). Everywhere the semantic path cattle → goods → money → fee/payment recurs, proving the economic logic is not uniquely Roman but Indo-European pastoralism lexicalised.

  • Italic formation: In Proto-Italic *peku- yields Latin pecus, pecoris (herd). From this stem Latin derives an abstract noun with *-ya- / -ia: *peku-ya- > pecunia — literally cattle-ness, the state of having herds, herd-value. The morphological pattern parallels fortuna from fors, audacia from audax. The form is already attested in early Latin (Plautus, Cato) with fully monetary meaning, showing the shift predates literature.
  • Material history — the ox on the coin: Roman tradition held that wealth was first in cattle, then in unmarked bronze (aes rude), then in bronze bars marked with an ox (aes signatum with bos type, 5th–4th c. BCE), then in struck coins (aes grave, denarius from 211 BCE). Pliny, Varro and later antiquarians explicitly connect the ox device to pecus/pecunia. Modern numismatics confirms ox and cattle imagery on early Italic bronze. Whether every bar literally bore an ox matters less than that Romans believed it did — the belief kept the etymology alive as folk economics. The source sentence reproduces that antiquarian lore verbatim.
  • Classical elaboration: By Cicero's day pecunia is the unmarked word for money in oratory, historiography and law, generating a family: pecuniarius (monetary), pecuniosus (rich), peculium (private savings, originally a slave's allowed cattle), peculatus/peculari (embezzlement), peculiaris (private, one's own). Jurisprudence stretches pecunia to mean any valuable thing sued for. Moral discourse makes it the symbol of avarice and corruption (Sallust, Horace, Juvenal) and of civic euergetism and military pay.
  • Sound change: The phonology is straightforward: PIE labiovelar? No — plain *p-e-k-u- with regular preservation of p and k in Latin; *-u-ya- contracts to -unia with long u (pecunia scanned pe-cu-ni-a with u long). Stress in Classical Latin falls on the penult if heavy: pe-CU-ni-a. Medieval and English learned pronunciation palatalises -ni- and diphthongises vowels to give modern /pɪˈkjuːnɪə/.
  • Romance and English afterlife: Popular Romance replaces pecunia for everyday money with denarius > denaro/denier/dinero, argentum > argent, moneta > moneda/monnaie, but pecunia survives learned: Italian pecunia (literary), Spanish pecunia, pecuniario, French pecuniaire (14th c.), English pecuniary (c. 1500s, from Old French or Latin), impecunious (19th c., as if from im- + pecuniosus), pecuniously. Legal English still says pecuniary damages, pecuniary loss, pecuniary interest — money as distinct from bodily or emotional harm. Anthropologists cite pecunia alongside fee to illustrate commodity-money and cattle standards worldwide.

Literary attestations are dense: Plautus puns on pecus/pecunia; Varro (De Lingua Latina 5.95) derives pecunia from pecus; Cicero (De Officiis, Verrines) uses it hundreds of times for bribes and public funds; the Digest defines it expansively; Vespasian's pecunia non olet (Suetonius, Vespasian 23) becomes proverbial. The cultural context is Rome's transformation from pastoral chiefdom to Mediterranean monetary empire — and the conservatism of language that makes a global empire still count its sophisticated finances with a shepherd's word.
