Part I: The Etymological Evolution of SHAFTS

The biography of shaft begins with a cutting tool. Historical linguists trace the Germanic *skaftaz to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning to cut, scrape, or hew — reconstructed variously as *(s)kep-, *(s)kap-, or *skēp-. The root's presence across the branches is visible in Latin scāpus (the shaft or stem of a plant, the trunk of a column) and Greek skēptron (a staff, whence English sceptre) and skaptein (to dig). Everywhere the root appears it governs the same act: shaping a straight piece of wood or stone by cutting and scraping it down to a smooth, uniform rod. The s-mobile alternation (with and without the initial s-) and the exact vowel grade are matters of scholarly dispute, but the image is constant — a thing pared, hewn, and made straight.

2. The Germanic Rod and the Weapon World: *skaftaz

In Proto-Germanic the root produced *skaftaz, the shaved rod. Its first life was military: the shaft was the spear- or arrow-handle, the straight wooden spine of the hunter's and warrior's chief weapons. Old English sceaft carried precisely this range — the pole, the spear-shaft, the stock of a weapon — and the word stayed close to the carpenter's and fletcher's bench for centuries. In Middle English shaft still meant the spear or arrow itself, and the archer's shaft was the arrow shot from the bow. The weapon world is the semantic hearth of the entire word family: from the spear the word would later radiate into architecture, machinery, optics, and insult.

3. From Weapon to Building: The Built World

From the later Middle Ages the word extended outward into the built environment. A shaft became the stem of a column — the trunk between base and capital that carries the weight of an entablature — and the vertical passages that were dug or walled for practical purposes: the mine-shaft sunk into the earth, the lift-shaft and stairwell of the tall building, the ventilation shaft and chimney flue. The transfer is one of shape and direction: whatever is straight, long, and runs from one level to another is a shaft, whether it is a spear lying across a warrior's hand or a shaft of light falling across a forest floor. The optical sense, "a shaft of light," appears in English from the 16th century and is one of the most beautiful transfers in the language: light is felt as a solid, measurable, penetrating thing, a lance of brightness that slants, stabs, and falls.

4. The Mechanical Age: Drive Shafts and Power

The industrial age gave the word its most productive modern domain. A mechanical shaft is a rotating rod that transmits power and motion — the line-shaft that once ran the length of a factory, the drive-shaft and axle of the motor car, the crank-shaft and cam-shaft of the engine, the propeller-shaft of the ship. Engineers measure shaft horsepower, align shafts, and speak of shaft bearings; the word became as central to the machine age as the spear was to the age of bronze and iron. The continuity with the spear is real: the mechanical shaft is a rod made straight, exactly as the fletcher's shaft was made straight, but now turning to carry energy rather than flying to carry a point.

5. The Figurative Shaft: Wit, Satire, and the Verb

The weapon sense also survived in the moral and social registers. A "shaft of wit" or "shaft of satire" is a pointed attack that pierces like an arrow, and the verb to shaft — first recorded in American slang of the 20th century meaning to cheat or treat unfairly — carries the same image of being pierced or stabbed. On this card, the plural shafts is pure optics: the ruby shafts of sunset are lances of light, heavy and rich as wine, slanting through the archaic forest to reach the turfed floor. The word has travelled, in a single line, from the hunter's cut spear-rod of prehistoric Europe to the ruby columns of a dying sun — and every sense still holds the straight rod at its centre.

Part II: The Etymological Evolution of TURFED

The history of turfed is the history of the ground beneath our feet — a word that began as the cut sod of the peasant's hearth and grew into the name of a sport, a territory, and an act of ejection. Unlike most of the vocabulary on this card, turf has no securely established Indo-European pedigree: its earliest certain stage is the Germanic family itself, and honest etymology says so plainly.

1. The Germanic Base: *turbą and Its Continental Relatives

The Proto-Germanic noun *turbą (also reconstructed as *turba-) meant the sod — the surface layer of earth held together by the roots of grass — and by extension a cut block of such earth used as peat fuel. The word is peculiar to the Germanic languages and their neighbours: Old English turf, Old Norse torf, Old Frisian turf, Middle Dutch turf, and Old High German zurba (modern German Torf, peat). This tight Germanic distribution has led some scholars to propose an origin in the Germanic or a non-Indo-European substrate stratum — perhaps a pre-Indo-European word of northern Europe — while others have tried to connect the word to PIE roots meaning "to hold" or "to grasp," and one speculative proposal links it to Sanskrit darbhá (a tuft of sacred grass). None of these deeper connections commands scholarly agreement, and the OED and modern handbooks treat the further etymology as unknown. This is a genuine limit of knowledge: the Germanic stage and its meaning are secure, and everything earlier is open.

2. Old English turf: The Peasant's Hearth and the Upland Bog

In Old English, turf meant both the grass-covered surface of the ground and the cut block of peaty earth that could be lifted and used. For the Anglo-Saxon peasant, turf was a substance of the hearth: in the treeless uplands and fens, turf was dug from the bog in blocks, dried in the sun and wind, and stacked to burn through the winter. The turf cut was also the ground of home — the very surface on which the house stood, the sod of the threshold and the garden. The word's double life — the living grass surface and the fuel dug from beneath it — was established from the beginning, and both senses survive in modern English.

3. Middle English and Early Modern English: The Turf, the Racecourse, and the Adjective

Middle English kept turf in both senses, and the verb to turf — to cover ground with sod — produced the past participle turfed: a turfed lawn, a turfed bank, a turfed floor. The great semantic event of the early 18th century was the metonymy of "the Turf." Because the racecourse was a turfed track — the ground of racing was literally grass-covered — "the Turf" came to name the whole world of horse racing: its owners, jockeys, trainers, and bettors. By the 19th century, "on the Turf" was the standard phrase for being engaged in racing, and the word carried the glamour and the gambling of the sport into the drawing rooms of Victorian England.

4. Modern Senses: Territory and Ejection

The 20th century completed the word's spread. In colloquial and slang use, one's "turf" became one's own territory or area of influence — the home ground of the sod transferred to any defended ground, giving the language "on my turf," "turf war," and "turf rights," now standard in gang, political, and corporate speech. The British verb phrase "to turf out," to throw or eject someone, took the image of casting a sod (or a person) out onto the grass. And in modern sports and horticulture the word remains fully productive: artificial turf, turf management, to turf a stadium. In Vance's sentence the adjective stays close to the oldest layer — "the turfed forest floor" is ground upholstered in living grass, the horizontal, soft, rooted mat against which the ruby shafts of sunset strike — but the word carries within it the whole biography of the peat-cutter, the race-goer, and the gangster's claim.
