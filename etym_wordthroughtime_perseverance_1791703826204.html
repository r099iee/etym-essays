- 14th Century: Late Middle English perseveraunce - Borrowed from Old French and ecclesiastical Latin; perceived overwhelmingly as a cardinal theological virtue signifying steadfast constancy in Christian faith, prayer, and moral righteousness until physical death.
- 15th to 16th Century: Humanist and Renaissance moral philosophy - Expanded into secular ethics and political conduct; celebrated by humanists (such as Erasmus and Thomas More) as the essential virtue required to master classical learning and withstand royal political intrigue.
- 17th Century: Puritan theology and the English Civil War - Became the central battleground of English religious controversies over the Calvinist doctrine of the "perseverance of the saints" versus Arminian free-will theology; praised in John Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress as Christian's indispensable spiritual shield against despair.
- 18th to 19th Century: Enlightenment science and Victorian industrial ethos - Re-framed as the preeminent secular character trait of the self-made industrialist, scientist, and explorer; championed by Victorian writers (such as Samuel Smiles in Self-Help) as the engine of technological and imperial progress.
- 20th to 21st Century: Cognitive psychology and planetary exploration - Redefined scientifically through longitudinal psychology (grit, growth mindset); embraced as an aspirational archetype for space science, immortalized in NASA's Mars rover Perseverance.
