• Anger: Similar because both denote displeasure and displeasure’s heat; differs because anger is the general, value-neutral umbrella — displeasure without any judgment of justice — while indignation is specifically anger sparked by perceived injustice or unworthiness, anger with a moral verdict attached.
  • Resentment: Similar because both are strong forms of displeasure; differs because resentment is quiet, smoldering, and personal — the long-held sense of a wrong done to oneself — while indignation is dignified, articulate, and outward-looking, often about the disgrace of others rather than of oneself.
  • Wrath: Similar because both denote intense anger; differs because wrath is grand and punitive — the anger of God, kings, and nemesis — and implies the power to punish, while indignation implies the right to protest and can be felt by the powerless as well as the powerful.
  • Outrage: Similar because both denote strong moral anger; differs because outrage is sharper and more active — the anger spills into action and public outcry — while indignation is comparatively dignified, controlled, and articulate, anger at the threshold of speech rather than of force.
  • Rage: Similar because both are forms of intense anger; differs because rage is explosive, self-consuming, and often violent, judgment lost in the heat, while indignation keeps its judgment — the moral verdict is precisely what makes it indignation rather than rage.
  • Fury: Similar because both denote intense anger; differs because fury is wild, uncontrollable, destructive anger — the fury of a storm or a maddened animal — while indignation is composed, articulate, and morally elevated.
  • Pique: Similar because both are angry responses to a slight; differs because pique is petty wounded vanity at a personal snub — trivial and self-regarding — while indignation is principled anger at a wrong, directed outward at injustice or unworthiness.
  • Umbrage: Similar because both mean taking offense; differs because umbrage is offense taken at a slight or implication — etymologically a ‘shadow’ darkening the mood — personal and often unjustified, while indignation is public, principled anger at a genuine wrong.
  • Annoyance / Irritation: Similar because both are forms of displeasure; differs because annoyance and irritation are trivial, petty, everyday responses to minor irritants, while indignation is a grave, moral emotion responding to injustice or unworthiness.
  • Exasperation: Similar because both denote intense displeasure; differs because exasperation is anger at persistent frustration — being driven beyond patience — without the moral verdict of unworthiness that defines indignation.
  • Dudgeon: Similar because both mean a state of offended anger (‘in high dudgeon’); differs because dudgeon is archaic or literary and suggests a sulky, self-conscious taking of offense, while indignation is principled and morally elevated.
  • Ire: Similar because both denote anger; differs because ire is a formal, literary word for anger in general, with no necessary moral component, while indignation specifically names anger at injustice or unworthiness.
