all-but (adverbial phrase / compound): 'Very nearly; almost; everything short of the final stage.' In Peake's Gormenghast passage the phrase works as a single adverbial unit governing the participle 'forgotten': the 'outer' people are all-but forgotten, meaning they have passed through nearly every stage of oblivion and now hover on the final threshold of complete erasure. The nuance is a paradox held in tension — 'all' promises totality, while 'but' (etymologically 'on the outside, except') withholds the last step. These people are neither wholly forgotten nor securely remembered: they are everything-except-forgotten, a half-existence that is more haunting than total erasure, remembered 'with a start' or as the unreality of a dream. Peake's phrase marks the exact boundary where memory fails but has not yet ended — a linguistic asymptote the passage never lets the reader cross. Polysemy of 'all-but' — the senses it carries today
  • Adverbial 'almost' (core sense, Modern English): 'She was all but defeated', 'the room was all but empty' — the phrase qualifies verbs, participles and adjectives to mean 'so close to X that X is true in all but the last detail'.
  • Attributive hyphenated compound (19th–21st c.): 'all-but forgotten', 'an all-but-unbearable silence'; the hyphen binds the three words into a single prenominal adjective, a modern grammatical fossil showing the phrase hardening into a word.
  • Exceptive force retained (quantifier use): followed by a noun, 'all but' still means 'all except', as in 'all but one of the carvings were destroyed'; this literal, arithmetical sense is the historical springboard from which the 'almost' sense grew.
  • Formal, oratorical, near-legal register: the ceremonial pairing of 'all' with 'except' lends the phrase a dignity that neutral 'almost' lacks; it is common in journalism, legal drafting and formal prose ('all but certain', 'all but impossible').
  • Figurative asymptote in literary use: poets and novelists exploit the one-step-short image — a condition approached but never entered, a boundary crossed in everything but the crossing itself.
The historical bridge is a textbook case of semantic bleaching. The Old English ancestor of 'but' (būtan) meant 'on the outside, except'; joined to 'all' it produced the exact expression 'all except'. From the fourteenth to the fifteenth century the phrase 'all but' was reanalysed: instead of naming what is excluded (the one thing left out), it came to describe how far a state has been reached — everything up to the last step, everything short of the end. What began as spatial arithmetic ('the whole set minus one member') became a degree word for near-completeness. That is why 'all-but forgotten' is not the same as 'forgotten': it is forgotten in everything except the final step, and that un-taken step is exactly what the sentence keeps in reserve.
recrudescent (adjective): 'Breaking out afresh; becoming raw, active, or violent again after a period of dormancy or healing.' The word is a medical metaphor drawn from wounds: a recrudescent dream is one that has healed over and then tears open again, bleeding with its original intensity. In the passage the adjective colours the 'unreality of a dream' — the memory of the outer folk returns not gently but with the raw, reopened force of a fresh injury, overwhelming the mind as an old wound overwhelms the body. The tone is at once clinical and visceral: recrudescent lends a quasi-pathological precision to a gothic image of the mind, and the word's very rarity makes the dream's return feel diagnosable, as if the psyche itself had relapsed into fever. Polysemy of 'recrudescent' — the senses it carries today
  • Pathological sense (primary, 18th–19th c.): of a wound, ulcer, inflammation or fever that recurs with renewed severity after appearing to heal; also of a disease or epidemic 'breaking out' again after quiescence — the strict medical sense still current in clinical writing.
  • Extended sense of collective evils (19th–20th c.): of war, plague, rebellion, sectarian hatred, superstition or panic that breaks out afresh after seeming dormant ('the recrudescence of civil strife'); the image is the same wound tearing open.
  • Psychological and memorial sense (20th–21st c.): of suppressed thoughts, memories, desires or dreams surfacing again with raw force, as in Peake's 'recrudescent dream' — the mind relapsing into an unresolved state.
  • Register: rare, learned, formal, faintly archaic; the active verb is 'recrudesce' and the noun 'recrudescence'; the -ent form functions as an adjective meaning 'breaking out again'.
The semantic bridge is morphological and transparent. The root is Latin crūdus, 'raw, bloody, undressed'; the inchoative crūdēscere means 'to grow raw, to become sore'; the prefix re- adds return, giving recrūdēscere, 'to become raw again' — the exact medical verb for a wound reopening. Because the 'rawness' at the word's core is fleshly and bleeding, every later extension — an epidemic, a revolt, a memory — carries the same physical shock: whatever recrudesces does not merely return; it reopens a scar. That is the distinction Peake's sentence depends on, and the reason the word, though rare, retains its terrible precision.

Relation of the two words in the passage: the pair works by contrast. 'All-but forgotten' names a state held just short of completion — oblivion approached but not entered; 'recrudescent' names an active return — something re-entering the mind with the force of a wound. The first is an asymptote of erasure, the second an eruption. Together they describe the strange survival of the outer people: never fully present in memory, never fully absent either, but capable of suddenly tearing back into consciousness.
