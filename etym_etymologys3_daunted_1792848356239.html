The road from a Roman stable to an English traveller's courage runs through a single Latin verb for taming. The reconstructed PIE root *demh₂-, to tame or subdue, gave Latin domāre, to tame, whose frequentative domitāre meant to tame thoroughly or break in completely — the word of horse-breakers and conquerors. From it came Old French danter or donter, to tame or quell, and in the thirteenth century Middle English borrowed it as daunten, already stretching the stable sense toward the heart: to daunt a foe was to break his daring as one breaks a colt.

Medieval romance kept both strings of the bow. Knights daunted steeds and daunted hosts in the same breath, and the participle daunted could describe a broken horse or a quailing squire with equal ease. But as chivalry faded, the literal taming sense faded with it, leaving the psychological residue as the survivor: by the early modern period to be daunted was above all to feel courage ebb before something formidable. The negative undaunted, meanwhile, flourished beyond all expectation, becoming the set epithet of eulogy, epic, and citation — undaunted courage, undaunted devotion — until it effectively became an independent adjective.

Stages of the journey:

  • PIE taming root: *demh₂- underlies a whole family of subduing words across Indo-European, from Sanskrit damayati (he tames) to Greek damnēmi (I subdue).
  • Classical Latin horse-breaking: domāre and its intensive domitāre name the thorough mastery of beasts, extended naturally to peoples and passions.
  • Old French transmission: danter carries the taming sense into feudal vocabulary, where subduing rebels and breaking horses are neighbouring arts.
  • Middle English inward turn: daunten learns to subdue hearts, not just horses — the decisive softening from stable to spirit.
  • The triumph of the negative: from the sixteenth century undaunted becomes heroism's house adjective, while positive daunted settles into dignified introspection — daunted by distances, tasks, and odds.

Phonetically the journey is one of Norman compression: Latin domitāre loses its middle syllable through French mouths until only daunt remains, the broad vowel keeping something of the stable's breadth. Semantically the source sentence shows the finished product at its most characteristic: a traveller not daunted by distance, courage measured against miles, and an ancient word for breaking horses quietly breaking — or failing to break — a walking heart instead.

The negative's career deserves its own footnote: undaunted became a regimental motto word, emblazoned on colours and carved over school doors, and navies on both sides of the Atlantic have launched an Undaunted in nearly every generation. Citation language — undaunted courage, undaunted devotion to duty — keeps the Latinate participle in daily official use long after its positive retreated to literature. So when the source narrator says he was not daunted by the distance, he borrows, perhaps unknowingly, the cadence of commendations: the traveller commends himself in the idiom of medals.
