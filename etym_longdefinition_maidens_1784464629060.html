Maidens (the plural of maiden) are young unmarried women or girls, carrying in their oldest and most literary uses the colour of virginity, innocence, and marriageable youth. The word is more archaic and elevated than plain girls or young women; it belongs to ballad, romance, chronicle, and formal pathos, and in modern speech it survives mainly in fairy-tale, historical, or consciously old-fashioned writing. Etymologically it is a native Germanic word, the diminutive of an Old English word for a young woman, and its Germanic age is felt in the grave, old-fashioned dignity the word still carries. In the account of Dale's ruin in The Hobbit — "Later he used to crawl out of the great gate and come by night to Dale, and carry away people, especially maidens, to eat, until Dale was ruined, and all the people dead or gone" — the plural focuses the dragon's predation on the most defenceless and culturally valued victims. The word is stock of fairy-tale horror, the young women of legend, but Tolkien uses it inside a sober chronicle of a ruined town, so the horror becomes historical rather than merely picturesque. Tone: grim, archaic-epic; maidens is not flirtation but casualty class. The word marks age, sex, and social vulnerability at once, and its old literary register makes the atrocity read like an old song of a blighted land. Core senses.
  • Young unmarried woman (primary): a girl or woman who has not been married, especially one of marriageable age; in older use, a virgin.
  • Virgin, innocent (older moral and religious colour): the word historically asserts maidenly purity, as in the fixed phrase "maiden purity" and the religious figure of the maiden saint.
  • Adjective, "first, unused, untouched": maiden in attributive use means first or initial — a maiden voyage (a ship's first), a maiden speech (a parliamentarian's first), maiden land (never cultivated) — the transfer of "virgin" to anything in its first or unused state.
Polysemy: secondary, compound, and modern senses.
  • Maiden name: the family name a woman had before marriage.
  • Maiden voyage / maiden flight / maiden speech: the first journey, flight, or speech — the "first-time" sense, standard in modern English.
  • Maiden over (cricket): an over in which no runs are scored — the "untouched" sense applied to sport.
  • Maiden (in compounds, now rare): maiden aunt (an unmarried aunt), maiden lady (an unmarried woman, a spinster), where the word carries age and respectability.
  • Historical and dialectal: a maiden could also be a young female servant in older use; the machine sense — "the Maiden," a Scots beheading device of the 16th century — is a macabre historical quirk of the word.
Historical and semantic bridges. The bridge from "young unmarried woman" to "first, unused" is the idea of the untouched: a virgin is someone in their first, unspoiled state, and by extension a maiden voyage is the first, unspoiled run of a ship, a maiden over is one in which the batsman's score stays untouched. The Old English ancestor mægden, a diminutive of mægþ "young woman, virgin," already carried the twofold sense of a young girl and a virgin; the moral and religious colour grew in medieval Christianity, and the "first-time" sense is a later modern transfer. The shortened form maid developed a strong secondary sense "female servant," so the full form maiden remains the clearer literary word for the young unmarried woman without the servant association. Differentiation. A girl is age-general and modern-neutral; a young woman is plain adult description; a virgin stresses sexual status explicitly and clinically; a damsel is romance-register and often courtly and in distress; a lass is regional and familiar; a maid can mean servant as well as young woman; a spinster names an older unmarried woman with a modern social charge. Maidens keeps the literary young-unmarried-female sense without the servant reading. The card preserves the plural: many victims over time, the dragon's repeated night raids on Dale.
