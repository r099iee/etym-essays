- [Old English (c. 700–1100)] - þē served as the unambiguous dative and accusative singular second-person pronoun across all registers - [connotation: completely neutral, universal singular object pronoun]
- [Middle English (c. 1100–1500)] - thee adopted the French-influenced T/V social distinction alongside thou - [connotation: marked address of intimacy, familial affection, social equality, or downward hierarchy toward inferiors]
- [Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700)] - thee faced increasing competition from polite generalized you; addressing a social peer or superior as thee became an insult or sign of contempt - [connotation: socially dangerous, familiar, colloquial, or insulting among adults]
- [17th–18th c.] - Quaker plain speech adopted thee for universal equality; meanwhile, the King James Bible and Common Prayer Book fixed thee into sacred worship - [connotation: religious piety, sectarian plainness, and divine transcendence]
- [19th c.] - thee became a staple of Romantic and Victorian lyrical poetry, balladry, and fairy tales (as in Wilde) - [connotation: elevated aesthetic beauty, emotional pathos, and conscious historical archaism]
- [20th–21st c.] - obsolete in everyday standard English; preserved in Christian liturgy, historical fiction, poetry, and surviving Northern English dialects - [connotation: solemn, liturgical, nostalgic, and literary]
