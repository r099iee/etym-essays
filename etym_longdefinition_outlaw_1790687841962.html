Outlaw in the source sentence is a transitive verb (capitalized only because it opens the sentence) meaning to ban, prohibit, or declare illegal — to put something outside the protection or permission of the rules. “Outlaw them and the whole proceeding assumes an abiding air of flattery” means: if we banish those motives from consideration, criticism becomes mere flattery. The imperative has the full archaic weight of casting out, not merely discouraging.

Core modern senses must be distinguished because the same form functions as both noun and verb:

  • Transitive verb — to prohibit by law or rule: the dominant contemporary sense. Governments outlaw smoking in bars, outlaw discrimination, outlaw chemical weapons. A school outlaws mobile phones; a sport’s governing body outlaws a dangerous tackle. The object is an act, substance, or practice, and the result is illegality. Register is formal and stern, stronger than ban and more archaic than prohibit.
  • Transitive verb — historical: to deprive a person of legal protection, to declare an outlaw: the original verbal sense, now chiefly historical or journalistic. “He was outlawed and his lands forfeit.” Still used in histories of medieval England, the American West, or in metaphor (“the whistleblower was effectively outlawed from the industry”). Implies exile and loss of rights, not merely a fine.
  • Count noun — a fugitive from the law; a habitual criminal: “an outlaw on the run,” “the outlaws of the Wild West.” Retains the romantic ambivalence of Robin Hood and Jesse James: simultaneously dangerous and admirable. Extended to anyone living outside conventions.
  • Count noun / attributive adjective — rebel, nonconformist, outsider brand: “outlaw country,” “an outlaw poet,” “outlaw biker.” Here the illegality is stylistic independence. Marketing inverts the stigma into authenticity: the outlaw does not obey Nashville, Detroit, or the academy. Adjectival use (“outlaw values”) is common in journalism.

Domain-specific and extended usages:

  • Law: in older common law, outlawry was a formal judgment (judgment of outlawry) pronounced after five exigents when a defendant failed to appear. Civil outlawry entailed forfeiture of goods; criminal outlawry was equivalent to conviction. Abolished in England in the 19th–20th centuries but the verb survives in statutes as a synonym for criminalize.
  • Politics and international relations: “to outlaw war” (Kellogg-Briand Pact, 1928), “outlawed organizations,” “outlaw states / rogue states.” Implies moral condemnation beyond technical illegality; an “outlaw regime” is one that places itself outside international norms.
  • Sport and institutions: rule-books “outlaw” equipment (a swimsuit, an engine type, a tackle). The nuance is procedural exclusion to preserve fairness, exactly the rhetorical move in the source sentence: outlawing play and competition as motives distorts the game of style.
  • Figurative / interpersonal: “She outlawed all talk of dieting at the table,” “Humour was effectively outlawed in the meeting.” Hyperbolic extension meaning to forbid by authority or social pressure. Deliberately grandiose, borrowing legal gravity for domestic rule.

Semantic bridges explain the polysemy. The path runs: person cast outside the law’s protection → act of casting out → act of declaring anything impermissible → anything or anyone defiantly outside norms, admired for it. The first step is metonymy (person → sentence → action); the second is generalization (persons → practices); the third is amelioration/polarization (criminal → romantic rebel → brand). The capitalized verb in the context belongs to the second stage but borrows the severity of the first: to outlaw motives is not merely to overlook them but to strip them of critical citizenship, leaving only flattery.
