tempest is a noun denoting a violent, windy storm — a great or furious storm of wind, usually, in the literary and classical use, accompanied by rain, hail, thunder, or the upheaval of the sea. In the passage — "The wind became a tempest, the woods roared and creaked all round him" — the noun performs a sudden elevation of scale: what began as plain wind is promoted to a storm of violent, almost supernatural force, one that sets the woods roaring and fells a tree across the road. The word carries with it the dignity of great literature and the associations of classical epic and Renaissance drama.

Primary sense (meteorological): a tempest is not merely a strong wind; it is a storm that has become a protagonist, an upheaval in which wind, rain, and thunder conspire to remake the landscape. The word emphasizes violence, uproar, and transience: a tempest blows hard, rages, and passes. It is the classical word for the storm at sea that wrecks ships and for the land storm that strips the country:

  • At sea: the archetypal shipwrecking storm — "the ship was lost in a tempest" — the storm of ancient epic, of the Acts of the Apostles, and of the Renaissance stage.
  • On land: a tempest of wind and rain can fell trees, flood valleys, and strip the land, exactly as it does in our passage.
  • Archaic-weather sense: in older English, tempest could refer to the weather in general, any disturbance of the atmosphere — a direct survival of its Latin origin, where the word meant "weather."

Secondary sense — figurative uproar: the great majority of modern uses are metaphorical. A tempest of emotion, passion, or sound is a violent, uncontrollable outbreak: "a tempest of anger," "a tempest of applause." A political or social tempest is a violent uproar, turmoil, or agitation that shakes an institution or a society — "a political tempest erupted," as our example sentence has it. The bridge is natural: the storm at sea, which overwhelms all order and reason, becomes the image of any force that overwhelms composure, calm, or public order.

Tertiary senses and compounds:

  • Tempest in a teapot (chiefly American) / storm in a teacup (chiefly British): a great fuss, outrage, or commotion about something trivial — an ironic inversion of the word's grandeur, the violent storm confined to a kettle.
  • Tempest-tossed / tempest-tost: thrown about by storms, literally or figuratively; Emma Lazarus's "The New Colossus" (1883) fixes it forever as the emblem of the exile and the refugee — "the homeless, tempest-tost."
  • The Tempest: Shakespeare's late romance (1611) names the storm itself, and the title has made the word inseparable from magic, theatre, and the "brave new world" of the island — a tempest with a plot and a meaning, at once meteorological and magical.
  • Tempestuous: the everyday adjective — stormy, turbulent, violently emotional — used of weather, seas, passions, and relationships.

Register and nuance: tempest is a grand, bookish, deliberately literary word. In plain everyday speech English prefers storm; to call a storm a tempest is to insist on its scale, its classical heritage, and its drama. The word is mildly archaic in ordinary weather talk, which is why it thrives in poetry, epic, rhetorical journalism, and metaphor. It suggests not merely bad weather but weather with meaning — an upheaval that demands attention, an event in which nature itself loses its temper.

Historical semantic bridge: Latin tempestas originally meant "a period of time, a season" (from tempus "time"), then "the weather of a season," and finally, by specialization, "bad weather, a storm." The bridge is temporal: weather is what the sky does over a season, and the most noteworthy weather came to be the violent storm. English received the word already specialized to "storm," and the figurative leap from storm to emotional or social uproar is a constant of every European language that borrowed it — French tempête, Spanish tempestad, Italian tempesta — giving the word the same two lives, meteorological and metaphorical, wherever it is spoken.
