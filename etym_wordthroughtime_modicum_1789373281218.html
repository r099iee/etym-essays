- [Late 14th to 15th Century] - Borrowed directly into Late Middle English from Classical and Ecclesiastical Latin modicum (neuter substantive of modicus, "measured, moderate"), initially used in monastic and theological contexts to designate a small, measured allowance of food or drink, or a brief interval of time (drawing on the Vulgate Bible) - [connotation: monastic temperance, modest physical ration, biblical brevity]
- [16th to 17th Century] - Transitions during the English Renaissance from concrete physical rations to abstract intellectual and moral qualities; writers like Francis Bacon and Thomas Browne begin speaking of a "modicum of learning" or a "modicum of wit" - [connotation: modest intellectual capability, scholarly humility, measured portion]
- [18th to 19th Century] - Established in legal, parliamentary, and literary prose as the definitive term for a necessary baseline or minimum quantum of civil decency, justice, or common sense; frequently deployed in philosophical debates concerning natural rights - [connotation: irreducible minimum, civil dignity, measured requirement]
- [20th Century to Present] - Ubiquitous in educated English discourse and high-level journalism, almost exclusively paired with abstract uncountable nouns (goodwill, respect, common sense, dignity, privacy) to establish the bare minimum threshold required for civilized interaction or institutional legitimacy - [connotation: baseline decency, polite understatement, essential ethical quantum]
