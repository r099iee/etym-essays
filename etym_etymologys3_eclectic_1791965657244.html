Eclectic is a Greek loan of the Renaissance and the early Royal Society, and its history is unusually well documented at the point of entry: the word arrives in English in the 1680s as a label for a school of philosophy, and only later spreads into art, design, medicine, and ordinary speech.

The Indo-European root and the Greek verb

Greek λέγειν means “to gather, pick, choose,” and also “to say, tell,” the second sense developing from the first by way of “to gather words, to recount.” The verb descends from Proto-Indo-European *leǵ-, “to gather, collect,” whose Latin descendant legere means both “to gather” and “to read,” and whose English descendants include collect, select, elect, lecture, legend, and lexicon. Greek adds the prefix ἐκ-, “out of,” producing ἐκλέγειν, “to pick out, select,” and the adjective ἐκλεκτικός, “fond of selecting, able to select.”

  • PIE *leǵ- — “to gather, collect,” one of the most productive roots in the classical languages.
  • Greek λέγειν — “to gather, pick,” hence “to say, recount”; the source of logos, logic, and -logy.
  • Greek ἐκλέγειν — “to pick out,” with the prefix ἐκ-, “out of.”
  • Greek ἐκλεκτικός — “selecting,” and by extension the name of a philosophical tendency.
  • Latin legere — the parallel Italic descendant, “to gather, read,” source of elect, select, collect, lecture.

The philosophical school

The adjective became a sect label in the Hellenistic period. Diogenes Laertius, in his prologue on the schools, names an “Eclectic sect” founded by Potamo of Alexandria, described as one who chose doctrines from every school. The label was applied to thinkers who refused loyalty to a single authority and instead assembled a position from Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, and the Sceptics. Alexandrian eclecticism, especially the reconciliation of Plato and Aristotle, fed directly into Neoplatonism, and Roman writers of the late Republic and Empire practised a practical version of the same method.

  • Potamo of Alexandria — 1st c. BCE, traditionally the founder of the eclectic sect.
  • Alexandrian synthesis — the attempt to harmonise Plato and Aristotle, a major current in later ancient philosophy.
  • Roman practice — Cicero, Plutarch, and Galen all combine doctrines from different schools while professing allegiance to one.
  • Christian appropriation — patristic writers used eclectic method to take what was useful from pagan philosophy.

From Greek to French to English

The word reached early modern Europe through humanist Latin and French. French éclectique is recorded in the seventeenth century, and English eclectic appears in the 1680s, probably from the French, though a direct borrowing from Greek is also possible. The first English users are philosophers and historians of philosophy, and the noun is earlier and commoner than the adjective: an eclectic is a member of the eclectic sect before eclectic becomes a general adjective of style.

  • Orthography: Greek ἐκλεκτικός → French éclectique → English eclectic, with the Greek rough breathing and accents lost.
  • Pronunciation: English keeps the Greek stress pattern in the weakened form /ɪˈklek.tɪk/, with the first syllable reduced.
  • Early sense: “of or belonging to the eclectic school”; the general sense “drawing from many sources” follows in the eighteenth century.

Nineteenth-century expansion

The nineteenth century is the word’s great age of expansion. In architecture, Eclecticism names the deliberate recombination of historical styles, visible in Garnier’s Paris Opéra, in Victorian Gothic revival, and in the Beaux-Arts buildings of the New World. In education, McGuffey’s Eclectic Readers (from the 1830s) took their name from the selection of extracts, and the word became a selling point for anthologies and compendia. In American medicine, the Eclectic movement, founded by Wooster Beach, combined botanical remedies with regular practice and established its own schools and journals.

  • Architectural Eclecticism: the mixing of historical styles, sometimes called historicism or revivalism.
  • Educational publishing: “eclectic readers” and “eclectic series” advertised breadth of selection.
  • Eclectic medicine: a 19th-c. American sect using botanical remedies, now studied as part of medical history.

Modern usage and the two valuations

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the adjective has become a term of praise in matters of taste — eclectic music, eclectic interiors, an eclectic guest list — while remaining suspect in matters of thought. The double valuation is ancient: selection is a virtue when it displays discrimination and a vice when it disguises incoherence. The source passage exploits the second possibility with the phrase “a shallow eclectic,” where the adjective measures the absence of any organising principle. Modern usage has not resolved the ambiguity, and probably cannot, because the word names a method rather than a doctrine.
