kindred

1. Primary sense (the collective noun). Kindred is one's family and relations, considered as a whole — the body of people bound to one another by blood, ancestry, and shared origin. It is a collective noun: not a list of relatives but the entire circle of kin, "his kindred," "the kindred of the tribe," "my kindred." Unlike "relatives," which is neutral and modern, kindred is warmer, more organic, more solemn; it names a people bound by blood and origin rather than a set of family members. It carries a tribal, poetic, almost archaic weight, which is why it suits the half-Dwarf Doctor Cornelius when he calls the true Dwarfs "my kindred."

2. The adjectival sense. Kindred is also an adjective meaning "alike in nature or character," "of the same kind": a "kindred spirit" is a person whose mind and temperament are akin to one's own; "kindred souls" are people who recognise each other across a room as being cut from the same cloth. The adjective grew naturally out of the noun — those who share a kin are of one kind, and by extension anything sharing an essential nature is "kindred." This is the sense behind "a kindred language," "kindred arts," and the modern idiom "kindred spirit," and it is the bridge from blood to affinity: the word moved from kinship by birth to kinship by nature.

3. Secondary and technical senses. In anthropology and kinship studies, a "kindred" is the bilateral network of a person's kin traced through both parents — the set of relatives who gather at a rite of passage or stand behind a legal claim — a technical sense the old collective noun serves perfectly. In law, "kindred" appears in older probate and inheritance language for blood relatives (as distinct from affines, relatives by marriage). The word also feeds compounds and phrases: "kith and kin" (with kith, "known acquaintances," from a different root meaning "to know"), "next of kin," "kinfolk," "kinsman."

4. The historical bridge. The word is built from two Old English pieces: cynn "kin, family, race" and the suffix -rǣden "condition, state," so cynrǣden meant literally "the condition of being kin." By Middle English the compound had fused into kynrede, "kinship," and then — the crucial semantic step — the word slid from the abstract condition to the people who share it: "my kindred" means "those joined to me by the condition of kin." The same suffix appears in hatred (hate + -red, "the condition of hating"); the second element is unrelated to "red" the colour, a frequent false friend.

5. Tone and register. Kindred is decidedly literary, formal, and warm. It has none of the legal coldness of "relations," none of the bureaucratic neutrality of "relatives," none of the narrow domesticity of the modern "family." It gathers a whole people into a single intimate noun — "these are his people" — which is precisely the effect the passage needs: the speaker claims kinship with "the true Dwarfs" in the very breath in which he admits they would despise him for his human blood. The word is also a favourite of fantasy and fairy-tale prose, where races are bound by blood and origin, and of religious and poetic diction ("the kindred of the Lord").

6. In this context. Doctor Cornelius says, "if any of my kindred, the true Dwarfs, are still alive anywhere in the world, doubtless they would despise me and call me a traitor." Here kindred does double work: it asserts a bond of shared ancestry even while anticipating rejection. The tragedy is that, because of his mixed blood, he cannot be claimed by them — he is of their kindred by descent, but not of their kind. The word's warmth makes the anticipated contempt cut deeper, and its collective, tribal gravity makes "his people" feel like a whole lost nation.

  • Collective noun: one's family and relations as a whole — the circle of kin.
  • Adjective: alike in nature or character — "a kindred spirit."
  • Technical use: in anthropology, the bilateral network of kin through both parents; in law, blood relatives.
  • Etymology: Old English cynn "kin" + -rǣden "condition, state" → cynrǣden → kynrede → kindred.
  • Register: warm, solemn, literary, tribal — warmer than "relatives," narrower and more organic than "family."
