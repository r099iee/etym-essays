Sulla is a Latin cognomen, and like most cognomina it is a name first and a word second. Roman names of this type were not chosen from a dictionary; they accumulated in families, were inherited, and often became opaque even to the Romans who bore them. The gens Cornelia was among the oldest and proudest patrician clans, and its branches carried a remarkable series of such names: Scipio (“staff”), Lentulus (“slow” or perhaps “lentil”), Cethegus, Dolabella, Cinna, Merula, Rufus and Sulla. Several of these are not Latin at all, or not explicable from Latin, which is the first clue to the name's origin.

The Etruscan hypothesis. A large proportion of the oldest Roman gentilicia and cognomina are thought to be Etruscan, reflecting the Etruscan dominance of early Rome and the frequency with which Roman families absorbed or descended from Etruscan ones. Names such as Caecina, Perperna, Spurinna and Cethegus are transparently Etruscan. Sulla is usually placed in the same group on the grounds that it has no Latin etymology, that its phonology (the geminate -ll- and the un-Latin-looking stem) is compatible with Etruscan, and that the Cornelii had deep Etruscan connections. None of this is proof. No Etruscan inscription gives the name, and the hypothesis rests on typology rather than on a documented transfer. It should be reported as probable, not certain.

Ancient explanations. Roman writers were fond of explaining cognomina from physical peculiarities, and the tradition offers one for Sulla: a disfiguring mark on the face, variously described, from which the name is said to derive. This is exactly the kind of story that grows up around an opaque name, and it has no linguistic value; the word is not attested as a common noun meaning “blemish”. Plutarch, who records the tradition, is sceptical in his own way, and modern etymologists set it aside. The moral is general: for Roman cognomina, ancient glosses are evidence of how the name was understood, not of how it arose.

Sound and spelling. Latin Sulla has a short u and a geminate ll, and is pronounced in English either in the Latin manner, /ˈsʊl.la/, or in the anglicised manner, /ˈsʌl.ə/, which is standard in histories and biographies. The spelling has never varied: Sulla in Latin, Italian, French, German and English alike. The feminine form Sulla is also used as a modern given name in Italy, independently of the Roman.

The agnomen Felix. Sulla's adoption of Felix is a separate lexical event. Felix is a genuine Latin adjective, “fruitful, lucky, blessed”, from felix in the agricultural sense of a fertile field; applied to a man it claims that fortune favours him. Sulla used it publicly, associated himself with Venus and with Fortuna, and gave his twin children the significant names Faustus and Fausta, “auspicious”. The full style Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix is thus a political programme in three words — clan, branch, and divine luck — and later writers often drop the Felix precisely because the luck became a bitter joke.

The name in history and literature.

  • Late Republic: Sallust, Cicero and the contemporary sources treat Sulla as the man who first turned Roman arms against Rome and made proscription a technique of government.
  • Early Empire: Velleius, Appian and Plutarch write full accounts; Plutarch's Life is the source of most later characterisations, including the famous description of his face and his brutality.
  • Medieval and Renaissance: known mainly through Plutarch, Livy's epitomes and the digest of Roman law; Machiavelli cites him among those who won power by arms and then failed to keep it securely.
  • 18th–19th c.: the age of revolutions makes him a stock example of the military usurper; school histories dwell on the proscriptions and the abdication.
  • Modern: the name appears in scholarship on the constitution, on the proscriptions and on late Republican politics, and in general prose as a shorthand for ruthless, legally dressed dictatorship.

The name as a type. Unlike most Roman cognomina, Sulla has acquired a second life as a common figurative reference. To call a politician “a Sulla” is to accuse him of proscription, purge and the use of the army against citizens; to speak of “Sullan methods” is to invoke the lists posted in the Forum. This figurative currency is not etymology, but it is part of the word's modern meaning, and any account of the name that stops at the man misses how it is actually used.
