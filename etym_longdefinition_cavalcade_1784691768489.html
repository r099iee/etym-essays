Cavalcade (noun) denotes a procession or company of people riding on horseback, or by extension on ponies, in carts, carriages, or vehicles, especially one of a formal, ceremonial, or conspicuous character. The word is at once practical and grand: it describes the fact of mounted travel while lending it a theatrical, almost pageant-like dignity. The full sense range can be set out as follows: - A ride on horseback (historical sense, late 16th century): the earliest English meaning, recorded in the closing years of Elizabeth I's reign, referring to a single ride on horseback or a train of riders. This sense is now obsolete or historical. - A formal or ceremonial procession of riders (the core modern sense): a royal entry, a noble arrival, a military review, a hunt, or any mounted company moving in a dignified, public line. A cavalcade is something to be watched — the word insists on spectacle and on an audience. - Any line or company of mounted travellers, whether or not ceremonial: a cavalcade may be grand or merely long, but it is always public rather than a private mode of transport. - By extension, a procession of vehicles: modern usage readily applies cavalcade to a line of motorcars or lorries, though the more technical term for a ceremonial vehicle procession is motorcade (a 20th-century back-formation from cavalcade). - Figurative (rare, literary): any continuous and impressive succession, as in "a cavalcade of events" or "the cavalcade of history", where the image of riders passing in review is transferred to the passage of time or of experience. The word's register is its most distinctive feature. Cavalcade does not simply describe riders: it dignifies them. To call a group of riders a cavalcade is to frame them as a pageant, to give their travel an occasion and an audience. This elevation is built into the word's history — it descends from Latin caballus "work-horse, nag", a deliberately humble word that nevertheless produced, through Italian cavalcata and French cavalcade, the grandest of the English horse-family words. The same stock produced cavalier, cavalry, chevalier, and chivalry, all of which entered English in the same centuries and all of which share the irony of descending from a word for a common nag. In the passage from The Hobbit, chapter 2, the word performs a delicate act of perspective-shifting. The narrator has just reported Bilbo's sniffing complaint about "all this dwarvish racket", and then adds, in an urbane, conspiratorial aside, that he does not suppose the reader "would have noticed anything at all on a windy night, not if the whole cavalcade had passed two feet off." The word is chosen for its elevation: the thirteen dwarves and one reluctant hobbit, striding through the dark with their gear and ponies, are suddenly dignified into a mounted procession, a courtly image that sits in comic contrast with the "racket" of the preceding sentence. The humour works by incongruity — Bilbo hears a clatter, while the narrator calls the same company a cavalcade — and the grandeur of the word measures how far the reader's ordinary human senses fall short of the hobbit's. It also quietly underlines the theme of the chapter, in which the respectable Bilbo is being drawn, against his will, into an adventure far more impressive and dangerous than he imagines. Key distinctions from near-synonyms:
  • Procession: similar because both are formal lines of people moving together; differs because a procession can be on foot and is usually religious or ceremonial, while a cavalcade specifically involves riders on horseback.
  • Parade: similar because both are formal public displays; differs because a parade is held for display or celebration (often with an audience and a route), while a cavalcade is a dignified company of riders that may simply be travelling.
  • Convoy: similar because both are groups travelling together; differs because a convoy travels for mutual protection (ships, lorries, military vehicles), while a cavalcade moves for ceremony and spectacle.
  • Retinue: similar because both are companies accompanying someone; differs because a retinue or train is the following of a great person, emphasising service and dependence, while a cavalcade emphasises the mounted spectacle itself.
  • Cortège: similar because both are formal processions; differs because cortège is most commonly used of funeral processions, while a cavalcade is festive or ceremonial in a wider sense.
  • Motorcade: similar because both are processions of vehicles; differs because a motorcade specifically involves motor vehicles and was coined in the 20th century as a back-formation from cavalcade.
  • Column: similar because both are lines of marchers or riders; differs because "column" is a neutral military or marching term without pageantry, while a cavalcade carries ceremonial grandeur.
  • Pageant: similar because both are public spectacles; differs because a pageant is the display itself (with floats, costumes, tableaux), while a cavalcade is a procession of riders that may be part of a pageant but is not identical with it.
