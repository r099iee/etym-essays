Single root word: RECHERCHÉ (English adjective, borrowed from French). The chain runs from a turn-and-bend root of the Indo-European stock, through the Greek word for "ring," into Latin and the Romance verbs of seeking, and finally into French and English. Proto-Indo-European *(s)ker- "to turn, bend, curve" (reconstructed base; ~4500–3000 BCE; the initial s- is a mobile s-, and the base is also written *ker-) → Greek κίρκος (kírkos) "circle, ring" (attested from the archaic period; the form underlies Latin circus; its earlier etymology within Greek is debated — some link it to the same turning-root, others treat it as a loan of uncertain origin) → Latin circus "circle, ring; arena" (Classical Latin, attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Late Latin circāre "to go around, to traverse, to search about" (post-classical verb built on circus; recorded from roughly the 3rd–4th c. CE; unattested intermediate Romance steps are reconstructed) → Old French cerchier "to seek, to search" (attested from the 9th–10th c., continuing Gallo-Romance developments of circāre) → Middle French chercher "to seek" (13th–16th c.), and with the prefix re- "again, thoroughly" the verb rechercher "to seek out again, to search closely" (attested from the medieval period) → French recherché, past participle of rechercher, "sought out, choice, rare, exquisite" (used as an adjective from the 17th c.) → English RECHERCHÉ (borrowed in the 18th century, pronounced after the French fashion and often printed in italics). Note on debate: the stages between Latin circus and Old French cerchier are reconstructed developments of Gallo-Romance, not individually attested forms; the palatalized -ch- of French chercher and English recherché reflects the standard, inferred palatalization of Latin -c- before -i-/-yod in the northern Gallo-Romance dialects, an orthodox but unattested sound change. The deeper root is equally nuanced: the Greek kírkos "ring" is connected by many scholars with a PIE base *(s)ker- "to turn, bend," but the word is also classed as of uncertain origin, and the mobile s- means the root may be written with or without the initial consonant. The semantic path from "circle" to "seek" runs through the physical idea of going around, of making circuits while searching — the oldest sense of circāre is literally "to go about."
