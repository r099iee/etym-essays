The life of “twanged” begins not in an ancient root but in the human ear. Where most English words descend from Proto-Indo-European through millennia of regular sound-change, “twang” was coined from scratch in the sixteenth century as a direct acoustic imitation — the lexicographer’s term is “echoic” or “onomatopoeic,” and the OED labels the word “imitative.” When a bowstring or a banjo string is plucked, the ear hears two things fused into one: the sharp percussive attack of the snap and the ringing, nasal after-vibration as the string wobbles at high frequency. The sound-symbolic skeleton “tw-” (heard also in “twist,” “twitch,” “twirl,” all built on a lip-pursed, spread onset) plus the nasal “-ng” that reproduces the hum of the string gave the word its shape: /twæŋ/. There is no ancestor to reconstruct; the word’s entire biography is the roughly five hundred years it has spent being its own noise.

The written record shows the coinage hardening from a bare exclamation into a full verb. Around 1550 the noun appears, meaning the sound itself, and by 1553 “to twang” is a verb meaning “to make or emit that sound.” English speakers at once began building a small family on the model: “twangy” (19th c.) for the quality, “twanged” for the past — the very form Susan’s bowstring produces in the Narnia passage — and, later, a striking metaphorical transfer: a “twang” in the voice, the sharp, drawn, nasal accent heard in certain dialects. That vocal sense is itself pure sound-symbolism, since a nasal voice “twangs” like a slackened string; the jump from bowstring to accent is the only true semantic shift the word has ever made. Sister formations show English treating this corner of its vocabulary as a playground of ringing sounds: “tang” (a metallic ring, and by extension a sharp taste), “ting,” “ding,” “clang,” and “jangle” all arise in the same centuries, each shading the same basic snap-and-ring into a different timbre.

Cross-linguistically the pattern repeats itself wherever strings are plucked. Every culture that possesses a stringed instrument invents its own echo for the noise — English “twang,” for example, versus the French “vibraille” of a string or the German “zupfen” which names the plucking action rather than the sound; the fact that languages differ so wildly in their echoic words is itself proof that “twang” was invented, not inherited. In the twentieth century the banjo and the electric guitar gave the word a new homeland. The “twang” of country and bluegrass banjo, the “Telecaster twang” of rockabilly and surf guitar, and the “Nashville twang” of country singing all made the word a marker of American regional and musical identity; in narrative prose it stayed percussive and precise — the one-syllable report of a weapon or instrument being released.

Contemporary usage keeps every strand alive at once: “twanged” still names the sharp resonance of a plucked string, exactly one syllable long and exactly as long as the sound; the figurative “twanged at the heartstrings” keeps the pluck inside the body; and the “Southern twang” keeps the word in the voice. C. S. Lewis’s sentence — “It twanged: a chirruping twang that vibrated through the whole room” — is a small masterpiece of the word’s whole history: verb, then noun, then the after-ring, all in one breath.

Timeline:
  • c. 1530–1550 — echoic coinage; the noun “twang” is recorded by 1550 and the verb “to twang” by 1553.
  • 17th–19th c. — the family grows (“twangy”); the vocal sense appears and hardens (“nasal twang,” a regional accent), gaining a faintly rustic, comic flavour.
  • 20th c. — banjo and guitar make “twang” a word of popular music and of American regional character; figurative use (“heartstrings”) is common.
  • Contemporary — “twanged” still names the sharp, brief, nasal ring of a plucked string; brevity is the point, and the word remains its own sound.
