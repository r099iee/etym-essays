Lassos is the regular English plural of lasso, a rope with a running noose for catching cattle, horses and, in older warfare and hunting, men and wild beasts. Its lineage is a textbook Romance borrowing: classical Latin snare-word to Iberian ranching tool to Anglo-American cowboy staple. Each stage preserves the loop while widening its world from trap to livelihood to sport to software metaphor.

The starting point is Latin laqueus (also laqueum), meaning noose, snare, gin, trap and by transfer bond, tie or embarrassment — to fall into a laqueus is to be caught in words as well as cords. Classical authors use it for bird snares, judicial strangling and rhetorical traps. Etymologists dispute its deeper root: de Vaan calls it isolated within Latin, possibly related to lacere to entice or to a Mediterranean substrate; Pokorny-era links to PIE *leq- to bend are now treated as speculative. What is secure is the Vulgar Latin remodelling to *laceum, with loss of -qu- and simplification of the diphthong, yielding Old French las, lacs (source of English lace and lash) and Ibero-Romance lazo (Spanish) and laço (Portuguese), both first attested in the thirteenth century as noose and snare.

In Spanish lazo broadened from any tie or knot to the specific vaquero implement: a long rawhide or hemp reata with a honda knot forming a sliding loop, thrown to catch the horns, neck or legs of半-wild cattle. The lazo criollo of Argentina, Chile and Venezuela, the Mexican reata and lazo, and the Colombian rejo are regional variants of one technique perfected on haciendas where mounted herdsmen could not approach horned stock on foot. Reliefs of Pharaoh Seti I at Abydos (c. 1280 BCE) show lassoing already ancient in Egypt, and Indigenous Latin Americans used lassos as weapons before European contact, but the Spanish word and the mounted cattle-ranching system together created the modern institution.

Sound and spelling shifts into English are diagnostic of borrowing:

  • Spanish to English: lazo /ˈlaθo, ˈlaso/ enters American English c. 1807–1808 as lasso with double -ss- to fix the /s/ pronunciation and final -o retained as exotic marker; variant plural lassoes mimics potatoes, while lassos follows the normal -s rule and now dominates.
  • Pronunciation: British /læˈsuː, ləˈsuː/ versus American /ˈlæsoʊ, læˈsuː/; the verb to lasso, lassoed, lassoing keeps stress on the second syllable in Britain and first in America, reflecting different nativisation paths.
  • Kinship: lariat from Spanish la reata the braided thong is the doublet sibling, borrowed through Mexican vaquero speech into US cowboy jargon alongside lasso.

Semantic expansion in English follows the frontier. First attestations (1807 verb to lasso, 1808 noun in narratives of General Craufurds expedition) denote Mexican cattle-roping witnessed by Anglo travellers. By mid-century the cowboy adoption makes lasso the emblem of the US West: rodeo calf-roping, dude-ranch tricks, Wild West shows. Twentieth-century metaphor extends it to anything that encircles and captures — to lasso votes, customers, a promotion — and to biology (lasso cells of cnidarians that sting with coiled threads) and computing (Photoshop lasso tool that draws a freehand loop to select pixels). The museum list in this passage preserves the oldest pole: lasso as primitive weapon alongside clubs, spears and bolas in the war of the species for possession of the land.

Literary and material attestations anchor the arc:

  • 13th-c. Castilian: lazo as noose and snare in legal and hunting texts.
  • 1807–1808 English: earliest OED evidence for verb and noun in expedition narratives.
  • 19th-c. cowboy lexicon: reata, riata, lariat, lasso, lazo as a cluster of loans marking Mexican vaquero origin of US roping.
  • Webster 1913: Lasso, n. pl. Lassos. A rope or long thong of leather with a running noose, used for catching horses, cattle, etc. with verb to lasso.
  • Modern computing: lasso select, magnetic lasso, polygonal lasso as standard UI tools, completing the journey from rawhide to pixel loop.

In this card the plural matters because it is one item in a catalogue of relics: knives, clubs, spears, arrows, lassos, bolas, lures, traps, boomerangs, slings. The list moves from cutting to striking to piercing to ensnaring to deceiving, presenting ensnaring as a distinct Stone Age strategy. Lassos here are thus not ranch tools but ancestral snares — thrown loops that made men masters of beasts — whose museum survival lets the author claim modern security descends from primitive cunning.
