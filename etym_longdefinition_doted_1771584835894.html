Doted is the past tense of the verb dote, and on this card it is used intransitively with the preposition on: "Grandmother Jia doted on him so much, no one ever dared to correct him." To dote on someone is to be extremely and uncritically fond of them - to love with a love so excessive and so uncritical that judgment is suspended, affection becomes indulgence, and the beloved is shielded from every reproach. The tone is affectionate and protective on the grandmother's side, yet quietly critical from the narrator's vantage point, for dote always carries a faint warning of foolishness, of love gone soft at the edges. Within the novel's family politics, the grandmother's doting is the decisive counterweight to the father's severity: it is the emotional force that keeps Bao-yu safe inside the women's apartments and lets his "queer nature" flourish uncorrected. The word thus names a tenderness that is also a strategy, and a love that is at once the boy's shelter and, some would say, the family's blindness. The senses and history of the word can be set out:
  • Modern core sense (with on/upon): to be foolishly and excessively fond of someone; to love them without discrimination and without criticism - e.g. "She doted on her grandchildren, spoiling them with gifts."
  • Absolute use (rare, archaic): to be silly or weak in mind, especially from age - to be in one's second childhood. This is the earliest English sense, attested from the 13th century; it survives in the noun dotage "senile feebleness" and dotard "a senile fool".
  • Participial adjective doting: "foolishly fond; weak in mind with age" - now chiefly the affectionate sense, as in "a doting mother"; the doting parent is the canonical image of uncritical devotion.
  • Grammatical note: dote is almost always intransitive with on or upon; the direct transitive construction is rare and obsolete. The past tense doted and the present dotes are the ordinary inflected forms, and the noun dote is obsolete.
  • Register: the word is mild, literary, and slightly old-fashioned; it sits between affection and warning, and in modern English it is used almost exclusively of older people's love for children or grandchildren, or of passionate devotion between partners.
Boundary comparisons map the word's territory. "Loved" is the neutral, encompassing term; it says nothing about excess or loss of judgment, whereas dote is love that has crossed a boundary into unreasonableness. "Adored" suggests reverent, admiring love - closer, but adoration retains a note of worship and distance, while doting is familiar, sheltering, and physically indulgent. "Spoiled" and "indulged" describe the effect and the behaviour - the overpermissive treatment that softens the child - rather than the emotion itself; dote is the feeling from which spoiling flows. "Worshipped" brings in religious awe, far too solemn for a grandmother's coddling. "Idolized" implies public admiration and heroic status, quite wrong for the private, domestic register of the passage. "Pampered" emphasises comfort and luxury rather than affection. The distinctive core of dote is emotional excess shading into folly - a love that blinds - which is why the same root that produced dotage, the folly of the aged, produced the doting mother: the word has always known that too much love looks, from outside, like a kind of doting madness.
