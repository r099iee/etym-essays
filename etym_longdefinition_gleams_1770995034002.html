gleams is the third-person singular present-tense form of the verb gleam — to shine with a soft, gentle, typically reflected or intermittent light — and it is also the plural of the noun gleam, 'soft flashes or rays of light.' In this card's context, 'The cottage casement with choice muslin gleams': the muslin curtain at the window catches and returns the light softly, a fragile brightness set against the poem's cobwebs, decay, and death. The word is chosen for its delicacy — this is not a blaze but a gleam.

The verb senses:

  • Shine softly: to emit or reflect a faint, calm, and often intermittent light; to glow gently, as moonlight does on water or as a polished surface does in dim light.
  • Shine by reflection: specifically, to catch and return light from a smooth surface — a sword, a wet road, a windowpane, a muslin curtain 'gleams.'
  • Appear briefly: in literary use, to show a short flash or ray of light, close to glimmer but a little brighter and clearer.
  • Figurative sense: of a quality, feeling, or hope, to show itself momentarily or faintly — 'courage gleamed in his eye,' 'hope gleamed for an instant.'

The noun senses:

  • A ray or beam of soft light: 'a gleam of light under the door.'
  • A brief flash: 'the gleam of a distant lighthouse.'
  • Figurative trace: 'a gleam of hope,' 'a gleam of intelligence,' 'a gleam in his eye' (a hint of amusement, mischief, or intention).
  • Plural 'gleams': several soft lights or flashes; used poetically of scattered, faint, or intermittent lights, and in this card as the inflected verb form agreeing with a singular subject.

Semantic distinctions:

  • A gleam is softer and calmer than a glare or a flash, and it is usually reflected rather than self-generated — contrast glow, which suggests a warm, internal emission such as an ember or a firefly.
  • It is gentle or intermittent rather than blinding: a gleam catches the eye without overwhelming it, which is why the word carries a delicate, poetic register in both literal and figurative use.
  • In modern technical usage 'gleam' is essentially descriptive rather than a scientific term; its home is literature, journalism, and everyday metaphor, especially in the fixed phrases 'a gleam of hope' and 'a gleam in his eye.'

Register and collocations:

  • Common collocations: 'gleam of hope,' 'gleam in his eye,' 'the moon gleams,' 'the water gleamed,' 'a faint gleam,' 'the last gleam of daylight.'
  • The verb is usually intransitive ('the silver gleamed'), occasionally with a light source or surface implied ('moonlight gleamed on the lake').
  • The word belongs to the large Germanic family of gl- light words — glow, glimmer, glisten, glitter, glint — and retains a distinctively English, almost old-fashioned poetic quality.
