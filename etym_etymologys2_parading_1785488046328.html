1. PIE *perə- "to produce, to procure, to bring forward" (reconstructed; written *perh₁- in modern laryngeal notation) — the base of a large family of words for preparing, producing, and procuring. 2. Latin parāre "to make ready, to prepare, to arrange, to furnish, to equip" (attested from c. 200 BCE) — the productive verb of preparation; its sibling verb is parere, "to bring forth, to give birth". 3. Latin parātus "made ready, prepared" (past participle of parāre; attested from c. 100 BCE) — the base of the military vocabulary of readiness. 4. Vulgar Latin *parāta "a halting, a standing ready; troops drawn up in formation" (reconstructed; feminine of parātus used as a noun in soldiers' Latin) — the inferred ancestor of the Romance nouns. 5. French parade "a review of troops; an ostentatious display, a show" (16th century; from the same Romance stock as Spanish parada, "a halt, troops drawn up", and Italian parata, "a show, a parry") — the direct source of the English word. 6. English parade (noun) "a military review; a pompous public show" (c. 1656; also "the place where troops assemble", a parade-ground). 7. English parade (verb) "to march in review; to display showily, to strut" (18th century). 8. English PARADING (present participle / gerund) — this card: "marching out in ceremonial display; showing off conspicuously". Note on debate: the intermediate *parāta is reconstructed, not directly attested; the Romance forms (French parade, Spanish parada, Italian parata) are clear derivatives of parātus but preserve different semantic fragments — Spanish stresses the halt and standing-place, Italian the show and the parry (a warding-off stroke in fencing), French the review and the display. English borrowed chiefly from French, reinforced by Spanish parada in the phrase parade-ground. The PIE root is written *perə- (Pokorny) or *perh₁- (modern notation); the traditional linkage of Latin parāre with Sanskrit pṛṇāti "he bestows, grants" and Lithuanian pir̃kti "to buy, to procure" (both cited by Pokorny) is treated with caution by modern scholars.
