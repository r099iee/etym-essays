whirled — past tense of whirl: to move, or be moved, in a rapid circular course; to spin around at speed. In “The cloud whirled down; far up and away he was snatched,” the verb does double work: the cloud descends not in a straight fall but in a rotary plunge, and the whirling is what seizes Turjan — the rotation of the cloud is a vortex, a living drain in the sky whose spin reaches out and snatches him into its centre. The literary tone is vertiginous and impersonal: the world does not move past him; he is unwound into it, caught in the centripetal machine of the magic. Whirl is the most energetic of the spin-words, carrying in its sound the feel of its origins in Old Norse hvirfla — a word that itself sounds like what it means, the hiss and blur of rapid turning.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. Whirl descends from the PIE root *kwer-, “to turn, whirl, move around,” the root of rotation itself, through Proto-Germanic *hwerbaną and Old Norse hverfa, “to turn,” whose iterative form hvirfla, “to turn around and around,” was borrowed into Middle English in the early 14th century. The semantic bridge from turning to being-seized is simple and ancient: anything that whirls — a vortex, a whirlpool, a whirlwind — draws things into its centre, and the word therefore carries not only circular motion but envelopment, the sense that the spinner is being sucked into the spin.

Primary senses of whirled.
  • Moved, or was moved, in a rapid circular course (the cloud whirled down).
  • Span around and around rapidly about an axis (she whirled the rope).
  • Was carried or drawn along by a whirling motion (whirled up and away).
  • By metaphor, of the mind: reeling, dizzying, or rapid mental or social activity (her head whirled; the whirl of society).

flung — past tense of fling: thrown with sudden, violent, and careless force; released from the hand or from a machine without regard for how or where the object lands. In “far up and away he was snatched, flung head over heels into incalculable distance,” the word performs the second stage of the transport: first the whirling cloud seizes him, then it releases him — and the release is a fling, not a throw. He is projected like the tip of a whip when it is cracked, like a stone shaken from a sling: the energy ignores his weight, his dignity, and his destination.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. Fling probably descends from PIE *plak-, “to strike, beat,” through a Germanic s-extension that produced Old Norse flengja, “to flog, whip, beat,” and Old English flingan, “to whip, scourge.” The semantic bridge is the whip: a flogging stroke is a sudden, violent, arcing motion, and the throwing sense develops in Middle English from the late 14th century — to fling is to release a thing the way a whip releases its tip, with careless, lashing force. The distinct past form flung (modelled on ring–rang–rung) is unique to this verb.

Primary senses of flung.
  • Thrown with great force and careless disregard (flung head over heels).
  • Propelled suddenly and violently, as by a blow or a machine (flung open the door; flung from the saddle).
  • Placed, pushed, or dropped hastily and without ceremony (flung himself into a chair; flung his coat on the sofa).
  • Directed with force, especially of words: flung accusations, flung insults — and the noun fling, a brief period of wild or reckless indulgence (a last fling).

How the pair works in context. The two verbs are the two halves of the transport: whirled is the seizure (rotation reaching out and taking hold), flung is the release (violent, careless projection). Together they give the sentence its vertigo — the wizard is wound into the vortex, then whipped out of it, head over heels, into incalculable distance.
