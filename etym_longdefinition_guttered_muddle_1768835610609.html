guttered — Guttered is a candle-maker’s and candle-user’s word: a candle is said to gutter when its flame sputters, flickers unsteadily, and sends melted wax streaming down its sides in little channels (“gutters”), burning low and fitfully as it nears its end. The participle therefore carries an image of uneven, wasting combustion — a flame that has lost its steady heart and is being strangled by its own melted substance. In the passage being corrected, the word is applied, with devastating indirectness, to human life: “once their days of love had guttered” — the carvers’ youth and their capacity for love have burned down like candle-ends, sputtering and choked in their own wax, leaving only channels of congealed memory. The metaphor quietly governs the whole sentence: men whose love has guttered pour their sole remaining passion into carving, and the wooden figures they produce are, in a sense, the wax-hardened residue of their lives.
  • Primary literal sense (of candles): to burn unevenly, with the melted wax running down the sides in channels and the flame sputtering and sinking — the candle “gutters” as it dies.
  • Extended sense (of light and flame): any flame or lamp guttering — flickering fitfully, near extinction; the dying of the light itself.
  • Figurative sense (of life, passions, hope): to dwindle, sputter, and die out unevenly, strangled by one’s own residue — days of love, youth, ambition that “gutter” like candle-ends.
  • Related noun senses for context: “gutter” as the channel that carries water off a roof or down a street; the degraded “gutter” of the gutter press, life in the gutter — the same root-word now naming both drainage and degradation.
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: flickered stresses the trembling of the light but not the melting and dying; sputtered stresses the spitting of the flame; guttered uniquely combines sputter, melt, run, and sinking death — a slow, messy, self-consuming extinction.
muddle — A muddle is a confused, disorganised jumble — a state of disorder in which things are mixed up and unclear; to be in a muddle is to be befuddled, at sea, unable to sort matters out. The word began as a verb meaning literally “to make muddy, to stir up mud,” and that physical origin still shows through: a muddle is not a neat chaos but a muddy one — things stirred together until the water is clouded and nothing can be seen distinctly. In the passage being corrected, the “muddle of huts” at the foot of the outer wall is exactly that: an irregular, tangled, disorganised cluster of dwellings, jumbled together as if by an accidental stirring, so that no clear order or plan shows through the huddle of the poor quarter.
  • Primary sense (state of confusion): a confused or disordered mixture or state — a muddle of clues, to be in a muddle, to get into a muddle.
  • Verbal senses: to muddle = (1) to make muddy or turbid (muddle the water), (2) to mix up, jumble, confuse (muddle the papers), (3) to befuddle the mind (muddled with drink), (4) to bungle or manage badly (muddle through).
  • Figurative and idiomatic uses: “muddle through” (to get by clumsily, by trial and error), “muddled thinking” (confused, unclear), a “muddle-headed” person (befuddled).
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: confusion is general mental disorder; jumble stresses a physical mixture of disorganised things; tangle suggests knotted interconnection; muddle uniquely preserves the image of stirred mud and clouded water — disorder that hides what lies beneath.
Read together, the two words give the passage its atmosphere of spent energy and disorder: the days of love have guttered — burned down messily into wax and memory — and the lives of the carvers are set among a muddle of huts, a muddy, unplanned jumble at the foot of the wall, out of which, nonetheless, a score of skilled craftsmen still emerge to carve.
