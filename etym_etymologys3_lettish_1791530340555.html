The history of Lettish is the history of two different naming systems meeting: an external, German-mediated name for the eastern Baltic coast, and an internal Latvian name that eventually became the modern national norm. The English word did not emerge from the language itself. It came through German Lettisch, the adjective of Lette "a Lett", which in turn rested on a Latin and Middle Low German tradition of writing about the tribes between the Daugava and the Gauja. To trace the word is therefore to trace the consolidation of Latvia as a political and cultural idea, as well as the ordinary sound changes of an East Baltic language.

Medieval attestations. The earliest documentary layer belongs to the crusading and ecclesiastical Latin of the thirteenth century. Henry of Livonia's chronicle (c. 1220s) uses forms such as Letthi and Lettigalli for the Latgalian tribes; papal and imperial documents mention terra Letthorum (1208), Letthia (1209), and Lettia (1210). These Latin forms were not yet the name of a unified nation. They referred first to the Latgalians and then, by extension, to the various Baltic-speaking peoples who were being incorporated into the Livonian Confederation. German merchants and clerics rendered the same stem as Lettland for the territory and Lette for a person. Russian chronicles preserve parallel forms such as Lotygola, showing that the naming of the region was a multilingual affair long before modern nationalism.

The Latvian endonym. The internal name is different. Latvian latvis "a Latvian" is recorded in the seventeenth century (1648), and latvietis, the modern form, is attested at least as early as 1625. Most scholars derive these from an older latavis or latuva, which is probably a river name: Latvian Latuva or Latava, with relatives in Lithuanian Latava and Latuva. The root has been connected with a Proto-Indo-European lat- "moist, liquid; swamp, puddle", so that the ethnonym would originally have meant "people living by the Lata or Latava river", a muddy or marshy stream. This hydronymic explanation is the mainstream view associated with Kazimieras Būga, Jānis Endzelīns, and Konstantīns Karulis, but the exact river and the semantic starting point remain disputed. A competing, now largely rejected folk etymology connects the name with German Leute "people" (Old High German liuti); the similarity is suggestive but historically secondary.

From Lettish to Latvian. English first records Lettish in 1794 and Lett in 1831, both under German influence. During the nineteenth century the word became part of the comparative grammar of the Baltic languages. August Schleicher, Adalbert Bezzenberger, and other philologists described "Lettish" alongside Lithuanian and Old Prussian, and the term acquired a scholarly, slightly archival flavour. At the same time the Latvian national awakening, beginning in the 1850s, promoted a standard written language and a national history. Juris Alunāns proposed Latva in 1857; the modern state name Latvija and the ethnic name latvieši became standard. After 1918 the international style Latvia and Latvian displaced Lettland and Lettish in English. The older term survived among Baltic German writers, emigre scholars, and librarians, but it no longer functioned as the ordinary name of the language.

Language history behind the label. The language to which the label was attached emerged over many centuries. East Baltic split from West Baltic, and the ancestors of Latvian and Lithuanian diverged after about 800 CE, with transitional dialects persisting into the fifteenth or sixteenth century. Latvian as a distinct language grew out of the speech of the Latgalians as they assimilated Curonian, Semigallian, and Selonian communities after the Livonian Crusade. Livonian, a Finnic language, supplied a substrate and many loanwords. Characteristic Latvian developments include the loss of final short vowels, the change of Proto-Baltic an and am to uo, the assibilation of k and g to c and dz, and a fixed initial stress that later became a defining feature of the standard language. None of these changes created the name, but they made the speech that English speakers called Lettish audibly distinct from Lithuanian.

Literary and cultural attestations. The first printed Latvian is the Lord's Prayer in Sebastian Münster's Cosmographia universalis (1544). A Catholic catechism followed in 1585, and the Gluck Bible (1685-1694) gave the language a substantial prose monument. Nineteenth-century collectors, above all Krišjānis Barons, assembled the Dainas, the vast corpus of folk songs that became a symbol of national identity. The poets Rainis and Aspazija, the novelist Rūdolfs Blaumanis, and later Soviet-era and emigre writers carried the language into modernity. English readers usually meet the word Lettish in older translations, bibliographies, and histories of the Baltic; modern scholarship says Latvian.

  • Name type - English exonym mediated by German, not a Latvian self-designation.
  • First English record - Lettish 1794; Lett 1831.
  • Older meaning - the language and people of the eastern Baltic, especially in philology.
  • Modern replacement - Latvian, after the national state and endonymic standardization.
  • Key nuance - dated but not derogatory; best used only in historical or quoted contexts.
