Hula is a complex, sacred indigenous Polynesian performative dance and narrative art form developed in the Hawaiian archipelago, traditionally accompanied by chanted poetry (mele) or song. Far more than an aesthetic choreography or form of secular entertainment, traditional hula serves as an embodied oral archive—a living repository of genealogical lineage (moʻokūʻauhau), cosmological mythology, botanical taxonomy, historical events, and royal court protocol. Every physical motion in hula carries explicit linguistic and symbolic meaning: hand and arm gestures delineate natural phenomena such as the drifting of rain, the swell of ocean waves, the swaying of palm fronds, or emotional states of longing and grief, while grounded, rhythmic footwork and swaying hips anchor the performer to the spiritual energy (mana) of the land (ʻāina).

The art form is historically and culturally bifurcated into two primary traditions:

  • Hula Kahiko (Ancient / Traditional Hula): The classical, sacred form performed prior to Western cultural hegemony and the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy. Hula kahiko is characterized by unamplified acoustic percussion and chanted vocal recitation (oli and mele). Performers utilize indigenous percussion instruments including the ipu or ipu heke (single or double gourd drum), pahu (sacred sharkskin drum carved from breadfruit wood), ʻiliʻili (smooth, water-worn river basalt stones clicked like castanets), pūʻili (split bamboo rattle sticks), and kālaʻau (rhythm sticks). Performers train rigorously within a traditional school (hālau hula) under the strict disciplinary and spiritual direction of a master teacher (kumu hula), adhering to ancient sacred protocols (kapu) and dedicating performances to ancestral deities, particularly Laka (the benevolent goddess of dance, rejuvenation, and forest growth) and Pele (the volcanic goddess of fire and creation).
  • Hula ʻAuana (Modern / Wandering Hula): Developed during the late 19th and early 20th centuries under Western musical, harmonic, and religious influence. The term ʻauana literally signifies "to wander" or "to drift," referencing the dance's departure from rigid sacred ritual into secular, melodic storytelling. Hula ʻauana incorporates Western-introduced stringed instruments—principally the ukulele, slack-key steel guitar, acoustic guitar, and upright bass. Movements are softer, flowing, and lyrical, accompanied by harmonized vocal singing rather than solemn percussive chanting, with costumes evolving from traditional bark cloth (kapa) and native ti-leaf skirts to flowing Victorian-influenced satin or modern floral garments.

Polysemy, Idiomatic Usage, and Commercial Derivatives:

  • The Hula Hoop (Recreational Fad / Toy): In 1958, toy company Wham-O (founded by Arthur Melin and Richard Knerr) patented and mass-produced the polyethylene plastic hoop that became an international recreational sensation. The toy was explicitly christened the "hula hoop" because the rhythmic, circular gyrations of the hips and abdomen required to keep the spinning hoop aloft directly mirrored popular American perceptions of Hawaiian dancing.
  • Figurative / Metaphorical Idiom ("To Do the Hula"): In journalistic, legal, and political discourse, "doing the hula" or "dancing the hula" is occasionally employed as a metaphor for evasiveness, posturing, or deftly maneuvering around a controversial dilemma to distract an audience while avoiding substantive commitment.
  • Commercial Kitsch and Pop Culture Stereotypes: Throughout the mid-20th century, the term became associated with Hollywood cinematic tropes, Waikiki tourist luaus, plastic grass skirts, and kitsch souvenir "hula girl" bobbleheads mounted on vehicle dashboards—commercial reductions that stripped the sacred indigenous tradition down to an exoticized spectacle of tropical leisure.
  • Botanical and Floricultural Designations: Applied colloquially to several ornamental plants featuring pendulous, layered, or fringed foliage reminiscent of ceremonial ti-leaf dance skirts, such as the "hula skirt fern" or specific ruffled cultivars of Cordyline fruticosa.

Semantic Evolution and Cultural Reclamation: The semantic trajectory of hula reflects Hawaii's turbulent modern history. Once a solemn theological liturgy, it was demonized and legally suppressed by Calvinist missionaries in the 1820s and 1830s as licentious paganism. Driven underground, it resurfaced in the late 19th century under King David Kalākaua as an emblem of royal nationalism, only to be vulgarized by 20th-century Western commercial tourism. Following the Hawaiian Renaissance of the 1970s and the establishment of premier cultural competitions such as the Merrie Monarch Festival in Hilo, the word has been thoroughly re-anchored in global discourse as an intellectual, sacred, and world-class theatrical art form of indigenous cultural sovereignty.
