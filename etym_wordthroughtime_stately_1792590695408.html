- [Late Middle English (c. late 14th c.; Chaucer)] — formed as a hybrid compound of Old French estat and Germanic -ly (statelich), describing noble standing, high aristocratic rank, and opulent courtly display — [perception: regarded as a mark of elevated social status, wealth, and aristocratic magnificence]
- [Early Modern English & Renaissance (c. 16th–17th c.; Marlowe, Shakespeare)] — flourished in blank verse and Elizabethan drama, defining monumental palaces, sovereign kingship, and royal ships of war — [perception: celebrated as the premier epithet for royal splendour, military majesty, and heroic poetry]
- [18th Century Augustan Age & Architecture (c. 18th c.)] — applied to neoclassical architecture, balanced landscape design, and deliberate musical tempi (pavanes, sarabandes) — [perception: admired as an ideal of harmonious proportion, balanced symmetry, and classical restraint]
- [19th Century Romanticism & Victorian Landscapes (c. 19th c.; Hemans, Bryant)] — immortalized in British culture via "the stately homes of England" and transferred to nature's towering oak forests and mountains — [perception: revered as a symbol of ancestral heritage, sublime natural dignity, and serene moral elevation]
- [20th–21st Century Usage] — preserved in formal English, literary reviews, and historical descriptions to denote graceful composure, imposing architecture, and dignified bearing — [perception: universally recognized as an indicator of enduring quality, majestic presence, and dignified elegance]
