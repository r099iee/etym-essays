- Classical Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — Verb incidere meant literally to fall into or upon, strike, happen by chance, or occur unexpectedly (from in- + cadere).
- Medieval Scholastic Latin (12th–14th c.) — Adjective incidentalis arose in legal jurisprudence and scholastic logic to describe arguments, legal pleas, or properties that were non-essential (accidental) rather than substance (essential), debated in Thomistic philosophy.
- Early Modern English (c. 1600) — Adopted into English philosophical and legal prose (e.g., Francis Bacon) to distinguish primary causes from incidental consequences; Shakespeare and Jacobean playwrights used it for secondary dramatic plot points.
- 18th–19th c. — Coined in theatrical arts as "incidental music" for orchestral pieces accompanying stage dialogue; adopted in accounting and travel ledgers as plural noun "incidentals" for petty expenses.
- 20th–21st c. — Firmly codified in contract law and commercial codes ("incidental damages", "incidental beneficiary"), while remaining standard across sociology, science, and everyday English to contrast minor byproducts with fundamental principles.
