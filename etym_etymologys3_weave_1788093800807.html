I. The Indo-European inheritance (c. 4500–2500 BCE). The verb descends from the Indo-European root *webʰ-, 'to weave, plait', one of the best-attested craft roots of the family: Greek hyphaínein 'to weave' (with hyphos 'web'), Sanskrit ūrṇavābhí 'spider', literally 'the wool-weaver', Tocharian wäp- 'to weave', and the Germanic set — Old Norse vefa, Old High German weban, Old English wefan. Textiles follow the speakers: the same root that made a loom-word in the mother speech travelled to India, Greece and the North Sea, evidence of how deeply weaving was woven into Indo-European life and language. Already at this depth the root carried fate: Indo-European poetry makes weaving the image of ordering the world — the nymphs and goddesses who weave destinies, the sisters who spin human lots — so the seed of the word's later magic is present from the start.

II. Old English (7th–11th centuries). Old English wefan is a common verb of the craft: it makes cloth, webs, baskets and nets, and its compounds and derivatives ('webb' web, 'webba' weaver) are ordinary household words. The poetry of the period already uses it figuratively — the 'word-weaver' as a poet, the 'weaving' of a battle or a fate; the Anglo-Saxon world, like its Indo-European ancestors, thought of making as weaving. The weaver was an everyday figure: the loom stood in the hall or the workshop, and 'weaver' later becomes one of the oldest English surnames, fossilising a trade that had been a millstone of families for centuries.

III. Middle English and the guild age (12th–15th centuries). Middle English weven continues the verb unbroken. The craft settles into the great urban guilds — the weavers of London, York and the cloth towns — and the verb accumulates social weight: weaving is now a regulated profession with apprentices, standards and strikes, the engine of England's principal medieval export, wool cloth. Figurative use thickens in the same period: Wycliffite and devotional prose 'weave' arguments and sins and consolations; the romance tradition weaves adventures; the proverb of the Three Weird Sisters, the weavers of destiny at the cradle, spreads through folklore. The Fates, classical and Norse, fuse in the popular image of the weaver of each man's thread.

IV. The Renaissance literary weave (16th–17th centuries). Spenser's Faerie Queene is self-consciously a 'weaving' of legend and allegory; Shakespeare exploits the metaphor constantly — 'weave a plot', 'weave a web', and in A Midsummer Night's Dream the mechanic Bottom is a weaver, punning through the play on 'web' and 'thread' while he is himself woven into the fairy plot. The period also fixes the idiom of the spider's web of deceit and the web of fate. Weaving becomes the standard Renaissance image for the poet's art: the poem as a tapestry, the plot as a loom, the multiple strands of story 'interwoven' in the interlaced plot structure of the age — a technical term of literary criticism that survives today (interlaced plots, interwoven narratives).

V. The Industrial Revolution (18th–19th centuries). Between Kay's flying shuttle (1733), Hargreaves' spinning jenny (1764) and the power loom of Cartwright (1785), weaving passes from the cottage to the factory. The verb's centre of gravity shifts: 'to weave' now summons mills, machines and operatives; the weaver becomes the archetypal factory worker, and the Luddite protests against the stocking-frames and looms give the word its first class-war associations. Meanwhile the new political economy seizes the metaphor — 'the fabric of society', threads 'woven into' the nation — and 'web' begins its career as a term for interconnected systems, from railway webs to bureaucratic webs, a career that culminates in the twentieth century's World Wide Web.

VI. The motion sense, hair weaves and the modern word (19th century – today). Beginning in the nineteenth century, 'weave' acquired a second life as an intransitive verb of movement: ships and rivers 'weave' their winding courses, and by the twentieth century cyclists, skaters and crowds weave through traffic. The OED treats this as probably a different word — related to Old Norse veifa 'to wave, swing' and to 'wave' — but popular feeling has merged the two weaves, since the course of a weaving craft zigzags like the path of a weaver's shuttle. In the late twentieth century 'weave' entered fashion as a noun for woven-in hair extensions. The old textile verb, meanwhile, remains standard in craft, industry and metaphor alike: we still weave cloth, weave stories, weave spells and weave through cities, and every web — the spider's, the world's — remembers the ancient loom-word.
