The city behind the word The biography of damask begins not with a fabric but with a city — and the city is one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on earth. Damascus's name is first written in Akkadian cuneiform as Dimašqa in the Amarna letters of the fourteenth century BC, the diplomatic archive of Egypt's heretic pharaoh; from that Semitic base descend the biblical Hebrew Damméśeq, the Syriac Darmśq (with its characteristic intrusive -r-), the Arabic Dimashq of today, and the Greek Damaskós and Latin Damascus through which the name reached Europe. The inner meaning of the name is lost; an old tradition connected it with the Syriac word for the sycamore fig, šeqem, making the city "the place of sycamores", but modern scholars treat that as folk-etymology. The essential fact for this word is that the lineage is entirely Semitic: there is no PIE root anywhere in the ancestry of the name, and no Indo-European language contributed to it. From city to commodity For more than a thousand years Damascus was the western terminus of the Silk Road and the workshop city of the Levant — famous for its figured silks, its perfumes, its swordsmiths, and its roses. A city-name became a brand. From the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the Latin damascus, Italian damasco, and Old French damas were used elliptically for "the fine figured silk of Damascus", and English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century. The same city-name, travelling by different routes, produced the damask rose (Rosa × damascena, the fragrant rose of the Damascus plain from which attar of roses is distilled), the damson plum (from Latin damascena, "the Damascus plum"), and damascene metalwork — the art of inlaying gold and silver into steel, whose finest expression is Damascus steel, the legendary patterned wootz blades of Syrian smiths. Sound and form
  • The Greek Damaskós shows the transfer of the Semitic sibilant to Greek sigma; the Latin Damascus preserves the form Europe carried.
  • The English damask (14th c.) comes through Old French damas and Italian damasco; Modern French damas and English damask show the regular loss of the unstressed final vowel, while Spanish damasco and Italian damasco keep it.
  • The medieval Latin adjective damascus, "of Damascus", was used elliptically — damascus (pannus), "Damascus (cloth)" — and this ellipsis is the precise moment the city-name became a fabric-name.
Semantic drift and the fabric itself The fabric deserves a paragraph. Damask is a single-colour fabric — silk in the classical age, later linen and cotton — in which the pattern is woven into the ground by reversing the direction of the weave: where the ground is satin, the pattern is plain weave, and vice versa, so that pattern and ground differ only in how they catch the light. Because the pattern is made by the threads themselves, damask is reversible — the pattern appears in negative on the other side — a quality that made it the supreme fabric of grand table linen, since a damask tablecloth shows an elaborate design simply by lying on a table. It is deliberately austere compared with brocade, whose raised patterns are made by extra gold- and silver-laden weft threads; where brocade glitters, damask shimmers. This technical drift — from a named imported silk to a generic weave technique executable in any fibre — is the word's most important semantic development: damask became the name of a method, not merely of a commodity. Literary and cultural life By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries damask was the cloth of courts, churches, and the great houses of the Mediterranean — the stuff of bishops' vestments and noble tablecloths. Its register in English has always carried that aristocratic weight, and the collocations red damask, damask silk, damask napery, damask tablecloth all preserve the air of heirloom luxury. In The Story of the Stone, the red damask of the maid's dress does on the level of a single sentence what the whole first tour of the mansion does on the level of a chapter: it establishes that the Jia household's wealth is so vast that luxury has become the clothing of a servant. Red — the colour of celebration, luck, and vitality in Chinese culture — worn by a smiling maid as she brings tea, frames the house as a place where festival colours are everyday wear and where even the margins of the household are dressed in the finest silk the Silk Road's western capital had to offer. A fragment of four thousand years of a city's history has come to rest on a servant's skirt, and the word damask carries that entire history with it.
