LINEATION: the arrangement, division, or marking of lines — in its most specific modern use, the deliberate placement of line-breaks in poetry (the way a text is divided into verse-lines), and in the earth sciences, the alignment of linear features in rock. The word unites the abstract idea of "the pattern made by lines" with the concrete act of "drawing or ruling lines". It entered English in the 17th century as a learned term for drawing, measuring, and layout, and has since been specialised by poets, geologists, cartographers, and anatomists. In the source context it is used of poetry: enjambment breaks rigid metrical boundaries, creating "dynamic tension between syntactic structure and metrical lineation" — that is, between where a sentence ends and where the verse-line breaks.

Core senses and current polysemy:
  • Poetry and poetics (the dominant modern sense): lineation is the division of a poem into lines — the placement of line breaks that creates rhythm, emphasis, pace, enjambment, and visual shape on the page. Free verse, in particular, is defined by its lineation: the poet decides where each line ends, and those decisions carry meaning (a line that ends on "silence" before the next line begins "the word" makes the silence visible). The term is standard in literary criticism: "the lineation of this poem enacts its hesitation", "the poem's irregular lineation mirrors its disrupted speech". In metrical verse, lineation answers to metre — the lines are metrical units — and enjambment, the running of a sentence over a line-end, is precisely the point where syntax and lineation pull against each other.
  • Structural geology and petrology: lineation is a linear fabric in rock — the preferred alignment of elongate minerals, crystals, pebbles, or fold axes within a deformed metamorphic or igneous body, produced by shearing, stretching, or flow. Geologists distinguish stretching lineations, mineral lineations, intersection lineations, and slickenside lineations; they record the direction of ancient strain and flow, and are read like compass needles in the rock. The plural "lineations" is the norm in the sciences.
  • Drawing, cartography, and technical illustration: lineation is the tracing of lines — the ruled or hatched markings on a map, plan, or diagram (the lineation of a coastline, the lineation of a building's elevation), and the act of making such lines. In the 17th century, when the word entered English, this was its whole meaning: the drawing of straight lines and the marking of boundaries on a surface.
  • Anatomy and biology: the lineation of a palm (its life- and head-lines), the lineation of a leaf (its venation), the fine lineation of muscle fibres or of a shell's surface — linear markings or striations on a body or organ.
  • General and figurative use: any pattern of lines or furrows — the lineation of waves on sand, the lineation of a printed page, the lineation of a furrowed brow. These everyday senses are now rare, displaced by "line", "ruling", or "striation", but they remain legitimate uses of the word.

The historical bridge: All senses flow from Latin līnea, "a line", which originally meant "a thread of flax" — the string a builder or surveyor stretched, dusted with chalk or held taut, to mark a straight line — and līnea itself comes from līnum, "flax". The physical string became the drawn mark; the drawn mark became the written line of verse; and the arrangement of such lines became lineation. The 17th-century English word was about drawing and measuring (the act of producing lines, the plan of a building — as in the ground-line of Vitruvius); 19th-century science borrowed it for the linear structures of the natural world (rock fabrics, crystal alignments, leaf veins); and 20th-century criticism narrowed it to the art of the poetic line. Every stage keeps the root image of "thread" and "line", moving from the mason's string to the poet's page and the geologist's compass.

Register: In poetics the word is precise and technical, with no everyday equivalent — it names the specific craft of the line-break, and no informal synonym exists. In geology it is a standard field term. Elsewhere it is bookish and slightly archaic. The plural "lineations" is common in the sciences for a set of parallel linear features, and in literary criticism "lineation" is always singular, naming the whole system of a poem's line-breaks. The word is thus a fine example of a single term serving two almost unrelated disciplines — poetry and geology — each of which thinks it owns the word.
