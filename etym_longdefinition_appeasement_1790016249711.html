Appeasement is the noun for the act of appeasing — of making calm, quiet, or satisfied someone who is angry, aggrieved, or threatening, by yielding, conciliating, propitiating, or granting what is demanded. In its most neutral sense it means pacification: soothing temper, allaying fear, settling a quarrel. A parent's appeasement of a crying child with a story, a manager's appeasement of customers with refunds, a government's appeasement of a restive province with reforms — all share the logic that a small concession now prevents a larger rupture later. The word carries no intrinsic moral judgment in this general use; it can be prudent, kind, or merely expedient.

What makes appeasement lexicographically rich is that one historical episode — British and French diplomacy toward Nazi Germany in the 1930s, culminating in the Munich Agreement of 1938 — so stained the word that its political sense reversed polarity from virtue to vice. Since 1939 appeasement in political discourse almost always means craven, short-sighted concession to an aggressor that invites further aggression, the opposite of deterrence. This pejorative overlay coexists with the older neutral sense and with technical senses in law, religion, and biology, forcing speakers to signal by context whether they mean wise peacemaking or shameful surrender. Your context — The appeasement of Earth justice would be nothing compared to the acuteness of the girl's distress — uses the older, neutral-legal sense: satisfaction or propitiation of justice, the idea that justice must be appeased, given its due, before mercy can operate.

  • 1. General pacification and conciliation: calming anger, quieting discontent, restoring peace between persons or groups. Example: His apology was a transparent appeasement. Bridge: direct from appease (to bring to peace) + -ment (act of). The core image is bringing to peace, from Old French pais peace.
  • 2. Satisfaction of claims, appetites, or demands: giving what is asked to still hunger or ambition. Example: appeasement of hunger, appeasement of curiosity. Bridge: metaphorical extension from calming a person to quieting a need; what is fed ceases to clamour. Slightly literary today, often replaced by satisfaction.
  • 3. Religious / moral propitiation (older, literary): appeasing God, heaven, conscience, or justice by atonement, sacrifice, or punishment. Example: appeasement of divine wrath, appeasement of justice in your passage. Bridge: medieval theology treats anger — human or divine — as needing to be pacified by due payment. This sense preserves the gravity of justice as a personified power that must be satisfied.
  • 4. Political-diplomatic concession (neutral descriptive, then pejorative after 1938): a policy of granting concessions to a potentially hostile power to avoid war. Before Munich, appeasement was respectable statecraft — The Times praised it. After Munich and the invasion of Poland, appeasement became synonymous with Chamberlain's umbrella, Munich betrayal, cowardice. Example: Critics called the deal appeasement. Bridge: the general logic of concession applied to dictators; failure discredited not just the policy but the word. Modern politicians therefore avoid the noun except as an accusation against opponents.
  • 5. Legal-historical satisfaction: In older law, appeasement could mean settlement of a suit or feud by compensation. Bridge: peace (pais) as legal order; to appease is to restore the king's peace. Now obsolete, replaced by settlement.
  • 6. Ethology / behavioural biology (modern technical): appeasement behaviour or gestures — submissive signals by which animals inhibit aggression in a dominant conspecific. Example: dogs show appeasement by licking, crouching. Bridge: 20th-century ethologists (Lorenz, Tinbergen) borrowed the human political term ironically and then neutrally for animal submission; after human politics poisoned the word, animals gave it a neutral home.

Collocation and register: appeasement collocates with policy, strategy, hunger, anger, wrath, conscience, justice, demands. It is formal, slightly bookish; everyday speech prefers calming down, giving in, papering over. Its emotional temperature depends entirely on domain: warm and prudent in parenting, cold and contemptuous in foreign-policy debate, solemn in theology, clinical in ethology.

For learners: do not confuse appeasement (calming by concession) with contentment (inner satisfaction), atonement (reparation for sin, often without concession to a threat), or détente (mutual easing between equals). Appeasement is asymmetric: the appeaser yields, the appeased threatens.
