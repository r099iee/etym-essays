No word reaches further back than physic, for it descends from one of the oldest and most fundamental verbs of the Indo-European family — the root *bʰuH-, "to become, to be, to grow." In its Greek daughter this root kept its full generative force: the verb phúō means "to bring forth, to produce, to grow," and from it Greek built the noun physis, "nature, growth, the natural constitution of a thing." For the Greeks, everything that grows and lives — plants, animals, bodies, the world itself — participates in physis, and so physis became the Greeks' word for nature as an active, living force rather than a mere collection of objects. From physis the philosophers of Ionia and Athens named the study of that force: physikē, "the knowledge of nature," the science we still call physics. The physician and the physicist are, etymologically, siblings: both are students of physis, both work upon the growing, living world.

The word then travelled to Rome and into the medieval learned world. Latin physica rendered the Greek science of nature, and Old French fisique carried it into English as fisyk and phisik — our PHYSIC. At this stage the word was wonderfully broad: for centuries it could mean natural philosophy in general — the whole study of the natural world — and, by a natural narrowing, the medical art in particular, since medicine was the branch of natural philosophy most concerned with the human body. In early modern England a "doctor of physic" was a learned physician, and "to take one's physic" was to take a medicine — often, in the old pharmacopoeia, a purgative, so that "physic" acquired the specific colloquial sense of a laxative dose.

The family went on reproducing long after the medieval period:

  • physician — the practitioner of the art (from Old French fisicien);
  • physical — "of the body," also "of nature," from Greek physikós, "natural";
  • physique — the bodily frame, re-borrowed from French in the nineteenth century;
  • physiology, physiognomy — both from the same physis stock;
  • physics — see the great split below.

The most important event in the word's later history is the great eighteenth-century split. When Newton and his contemporaries still wrote of "natural philosophy," the old word physic covered the whole enquiry. But as the modern science of matter and energy crystallized, scholars re-imported the Greek-Latin physica in the form physics to name it, and the two words — once one — became different words. Since then physic has been stranded with its medical senses alone, and even those have been largely taken over by medicine (from Latin medicina). The modern physics of a laboratory and the archaic physic of an old physician share a single root and a single ancient meaning — "the study of nature" — divided only in the eighteenth century.

Today physic survives as a deliberate archaism. C. S. Lewis counts on its antique ring when he lists "Law, Physic, Alchemy, and Astronomy" among Caspian's princely studies — the word is chosen precisely because it belongs to an older world, alongside alchemy, before medicine and physics went their separate ways. Every use of physic, even the modern proverb "laughter is the best physic," is a quiet reminder that the healing art and the science of nature were once one pursuit, rooted in a six-thousand-year-old verb meaning simply "to grow, to become, to be."
