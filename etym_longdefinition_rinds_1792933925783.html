Rinds, the plural of rind, are in the most everyday sense the tough outer skins that nature or the cook puts between us and the tender inside — the peel of an orange or a melon, the waxed or bloomy coat of a cheese, the crisped skin of bacon — layers that protect, preserve, and resist teeth before yielding to knife, grater, or long patient gnawing.

In the source sentence the word is at its most humble and most ancient: dogs lying by the fire gnawing rinds and cracked bones — scraps from the farmhouse table, bacon rinds and crusts too tough for human teeth but exactly right for patient canine jaws. The scene reaches back through every kitchen hearth where nothing edible was wasted and the dogs got the rinds, and it fixes the word in its oldest social setting: the chewy leftover, the reward for waiting.

Core meaning in detail: A rind is a comparatively hard, thick, or bitter outer covering that is usually removed or worked around before eating, though a whole cuisine has grown up around prizing rinds themselves. Botanically the rind of citrus combines the oil-rich coloured flavedo with the spongy white albedo beneath; grating only the outermost layer gives zest, while the whole rind candied becomes peel. In cheesemaking the rind is the firm ripened exterior formed by pressing, salting, and ageing, regulating moisture and carrying flavour — bloomy on a brie, clothbound on a farmhouse cheddar, waxed on an edam. In butchery pork rind roasted becomes crackling and fried becomes scratchings, the crisp glory of the Sunday joint.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Tough peel of fruit (primary culinary sense): orange, lemon, lime, melon, and watermelon rinds; zest comes from grating the outermost rind and marmalade from slicing it. The bridge is protective function: the rind is what shields the juicy flesh, so the word gathers every thick peel under one roof.
  • Hardened outer coat of cheese (from the 15th c.): the parmesan or cheddar rind formed by pressing, salting, and ageing, simmered into broth by thrifty cooks. The bridge is analogy: the cheese is dressed in its own protective skin just as the fruit wears peel, and affineurs speak of tending rinds exactly as gardeners speak of skins.
  • Skin of bacon, pork, or ham (the hearth sense): bacon rinds gnawed by fireside dogs, pork crackling at the roast, ham rinds scored and glazed. The bridge carries the same tough-cover logic from orchard to farmhouse table — whatever must be chewed longest or cut away first.
  • Bark of a tree (earliest sense, now archaic and poetic): Old English rinde first of all meant the bark of a tree, a sense still met in Spenser and country speech. The bridge is the original sense itself: bark is the tree's rind, and the word later narrowed toward food coverings while keeping the bark in its memory.
  • Any hard outer crust (figurative extension): the rind of the earth, a rind of ice on a pond, the thin rind of snow over fields. The bridge is pure metaphor: any thin hard shell over a softer interior borrows the fruit's anatomy, and geologists and poets alike reach for it.
  • A hardened shell of manner or feeling (rare, literary): a rind of indifference, a rind of reserve around a shy person. The bridge runs through the crust image: feelings protected behind something chewy and hard to get through, exactly as teeth must work through bacon rind.

Usage, grammar, and register: rinds is plain, Anglo-Saxon, slightly rustic — market stalls, cheese counters, farmhouse floors — and it is almost always plural in living use, since rinds come by the handful, the paring bowl, the fireside scrap. Collocations run in trades: citrus rinds with marmalade makers, bacon rinds with dogs and butchers, cheese rinds with affineurs, watermelon-rind pickle with thrifty preserving traditions from the American South to Bengal. Unlike the elegant French zest or the neutral peel, rind frankly admits toughness and refusal — the part you must work at, candy, simmer, or give to the dogs — and even as chefs ennoble it with candied rind and rind-washed cheeses, the word keeps its honest kitchen growl.
