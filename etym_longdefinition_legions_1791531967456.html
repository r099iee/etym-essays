Legions is the plural of legion, a word that has travelled from the muster-grounds of the Roman Republic to the language of biblical demons, military pageantry and modern fandom. Its core meaning is a large body of soldiers, but its power comes from scale: a legion is not merely many soldiers but an organised, named, disciplined many, and the plural legions readily shades into “countless multitudes.”

The Roman legion

The legio was the principal unit of the Roman army, and the word itself means “a levying” or “a choosing,” from legere “to gather, pick out.” Polybius, writing in the second century BCE, describes a legion of 4,200 infantry and 300 cavalry in the mid-Republic, raised to 5,000 in emergencies. It was drawn up in three lines — hastati, principes and triarii — preceded by light-armed velites, and divided into maniples of 120 men under two centurions. The eagle standard, the aquila, became the legion’s religious centre; to lose it was a catastrophe, as the disaster of Carrhae in 53 BCE showed.

From maniple to cohort

The reforms attributed to Gaius Marius around 107 BCE turned the legion into a professional standing unit, recruited from the landless poor and equipped at state expense. The cohort of roughly 480 men replaced the maniple as the tactical building block, and a legion became ten cohorts, the first of double strength, supported by auxilia of non-citizen troops. Augustus fixed the army at 28 legions; after the loss of three under Varus in 9 CE, 25 remained, and the number hovered around thirty for the rest of the Principate. Each had a number, a name and a history: Gemina, Victrix, Ferrata, Rapax, Hispana.

Numbers, names and lost legions

Legions were numbered I to XXX and often nicknamed for a battle, a deity or an emperor. The disappearance of Legio IX Hispana from the record after the second century generated centuries of speculation, including the modern legend that it was destroyed in Scotland; the evidence is inconclusive, and the unit may simply have been disbanded or transferred. The Notitia Dignitatum, a late Roman register of offices, shows the word still in use for units of very different size and character.

Beyond Rome: the word in later military usage

After the fall of the Western Empire the word survived in Latin chronicles and passed into Old French and Middle English, usually in accounts of Roman history or of the heavenly host. It was revived for new formations in the modern era: the Polish Legions raised by Jan Henryk Dąbrowski in 1797, the French Foreign Legion founded in 1831, the Czech Legions of the First World War, and the Spanish Legion created in 1920. In each case the name borrows Rome’s prestige: a legion sounds disciplined, elite and expeditionary.

Figurative and biblical multitude

The most influential non-military use comes from the New Testament. In Mark 5:9 the Gerasene demoniac, asked his name, answers, “My name is Legion, for we are many” (Vulgate Legio nomen mihi est, quia multi sumus). The verse turned the word into a byword for a host of demons or evils, and the phrase “their name is legion” entered English as a formal way of saying “they are countless.” The archaic predicate use — “their troubles are legion” — preserves this sense.

Institutions and eponyms

  • The Royal British Legion (1921) — the veterans’ charity responsible for the poppy appeal.
  • The American Legion (1919) — the American veterans’ organisation; its 1976 convention in Philadelphia gave its name to Legionnaires’ disease.
  • Legion of Honour (Légion d’honneur, 1802) — the French order of merit established by Napoleon.
  • Legion of Mary and Legion of Christ — Catholic lay and religious organisations, again borrowing the military image.
  • Popular culture — the Space Marine legions of Warhammer 40,000, the mutant Legion of the X-Men, and countless game factions.

Biological and medical

Legionella pneumophila, the bacterium responsible for Legionnaires’ disease, was named after the 1976 outbreak among American Legion members in Philadelphia. The genus name is a direct modern echo of the Roman word, and “legionellosis” is now a standard public-health term.

Semantic bridges

  • Levy → standing unit: the Roman shift from a temporary draft to a permanent professional army.
  • Unit → army → any large force: the word generalises upward in scale.
  • Large force → multitude: the biblical and rhetorical sense, where the military image supplies vividness rather than literal soldiery.
  • Discipline and prestige → the modern taste for “legion” in elite and expeditionary names, as opposed to the disorder of a “horde.”
