SINGLE ROOT CHAIN: Proto-Indo-European *wes- "to clothe, dress, be clothed in" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; one of the most widely attested roots of the family, from Anatolia to India) → Latin vestis (f.) "garment, clothing" (attested 3rd century BCE) → Latin vestīre "to clothe, dress" (denominative verb built on vestis, attested 1st century BCE) → Late Latin dēvestīre "to unclothe, strip" (compound dē- "off, away from" + vestīre; attested in late Latin) → Old French desvestir / Middle French desvestir "to undress, strip, divest" (9th–14th c.; also Anglo-Norman desvestir) → English devest "to undress, to strip" (borrowed 16th c.), respelled divest (17th c., under the influence of Latin divestīre and the pattern of invest) → divested (past tense and past participle; in use as "stripped of clothing, property, rights, or office" from the 16th–17th centuries). NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: The chain from PIE *wes- to Latin vestis is secure and uncontested; the root is attested in Hittite waššiya- "to clothe oneself," Vedic Sanskrit vaste "he wears" and vastra (वस्त्र) "cloth, garment," Greek (h)eímata "garments," and Germanic *wazjaną (English wear). The only nuance concerns the intermediate preverb: English divest passed through Old French desvestir (from Latin dē- + vestīre), while the spelling divest reflects influence from medieval Latin divestīre and from the established English pair invest/investiture; both forms, devest and divest, coexisted in the 17th century before divest won. The finance sense (to sell off assets; divestment, divestiture) is a 20th-century extension of the same verb, transferring the clothing image of stripping from a person to an enterprise.
