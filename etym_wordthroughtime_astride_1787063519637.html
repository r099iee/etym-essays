- Late 16th century: Positioned with legs separated on opposite sides of a horse, bench, or mount - Coined as a locative adverb/preposition for equestrian and seated postures.
- 17th century: Extended to standing firmly with feet spread wide across a ditch, stream, or fallen object - Conveyed physical balance, protective stance, or readiness in combat.
- 18th to 19th century: Applied to geographical features, fortresses, and settlements spanning rivers, trade routes, or mountain passes - Became a staple of military cartography and travel literature.
- Late 19th to 20th century: Adopted into intellectual and cultural commentary to describe towering figures spanning two historical epochs, philosophical paradigms, or cultural traditions - Acquired monumental, Colossus-like metaphorical gravity.
- 21st century: Continues in both literal physical/equestrian usage and high-register figurative descriptions of institutions spanning global divides.
