A wander-word from the south. Unlike nearly every word in the BasicVocab deck, beryl has no Indo-European root at all: it is a "wander-word", a gem's name that travelled from language to language along the trade roads, keeping its shape as it went. The Indian end is Sanskrit vaiḍūrya, attested in the great epics, naming a precious stone of the south associated in Indian texts with the cat's-eye. Sanskrit vaiḍūrya is itself, in the standard view of Indo-Aryan scholarship (Mayrhofer), a loan from a Dravidian source — built on a base like *veḷ-, "white, bright" — though details remain debated, and a link to a place-name has also been guessed. From Sanskrit the word entered the Middle Indic languages as veḷuriya (Pali), and from the Indian trade it reached the eastern Mediterranean, where the gem was known as a product of the voyages down the Red Sea and across the Indian Ocean.

Greek and Latin. Greek takes the stone as bēryllos — the odd double -ll- betrays the loan — and uses it for a sea-green gem of India, the colour of shallow water over white sand. The Septuagint and the Greek Bible tradition use it for Hebrew tarshish, the stone of Ezekiel's vision, in which the wheels of the divine chariot gleam "like the appearance of beryl", so the word gains a scriptural seat in the first centuries of the Hellenistic Bible. Rome inherits it as beryllus: Pliny (Natural History 37.20) groups the beryls with the emeralds, locates them in India, describes their pale sea-green tint, and records that the Indians cut the longer crystals into cylinders, pierced them and strung them — a fashion the Roman jewellers copied wholesale; his catalogue of Indian gems fixed "beryl" for a thousand years as the name of the greenish stone of the East. The spelling is stable across the Romance languages: Old French beril, English berel and beryl by the 13th–14th centuries.

Medieval and early modern. In the medieval lapidaries, beryl is a stone of purity and clear water, associated with clarity of intellect, set in bishops' rings, and blurred in daily use with ordinary crystal: Middle English "berel" can mean a bright glass, and the diviner's "beryl" was a ball of clear stone or glass — the ancestor of the crystal ball, and the source of the learned word "beryllomancy" for crystal-gazing. The medieval confusion of beryl, aquamarine and crystal was only resolved by chemistry. In 1798, working on beryl and emerald together, Vauquelin isolated a new "earth": sweet-tasting, so named glucina; and in 1828 Wöhler (with Bussy) isolated the element itself, beryllium — so mineral and metal came to share one name, and the gem became an ore. The 19th-century gem trade, supplied from Colombia, Madagascar, Brazil, Siberia and the Urals, established the modern colour scheme: emerald for the chromium-green, aquamarine for the iron-blue, morganite for the pink — while "beryl" itself slid down-market to the plain yellowish-green and colourless grades.

Modern constellation. Today beryl is at once an industrial commodity and a gem family. Beryllium's lightness, stiffness and transparency to X-rays make it essential to aerospace, nuclear and optical engineering, and berylliosis guards its industrial frontier. In jewellery, aquamarine (March) and emerald (May) are official birthstones, morganite carries the Art-Deco market, and the word "beryl" survives in literature as the ancient sea-green — the colour of Wilde's lion eyes, of the mystic's wheels, of Pliny's Indian cylinders. One word, then, has carried a gemstone from the Tamil coast, through the epics of India, the Bible, Rome, the medieval glasshouse and the periodic table, onto the modern birthstone list: a complete biography in a noun, and BERYLS its plural, the many green eyes of the mineral world.
