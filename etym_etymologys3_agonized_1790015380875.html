Agonized descends from the Greek stadium via the Garden of Gethsemane. Its deepest root is PIE *h₂eǵ- to drive, lead, move — source of Greek agein /ˈa.geːn/ to lead, drive, bring, Latin agere /ˈa.ge.re/ to drive, do, act (whence agent, act, agitate), Old Norse aka to drive, Sanskrit ajati drives. Greek builds on agein the noun agon /aˈɡɔːn/ (genitive agonos): originally a gathering, place of assembly, then contest, struggle, trial held there — athletic games, dramatic competitions, lawsuits, battles. Adjective agonios of contest yields verb agonizesthai to contend, wrestle, and noun agonia /a.ɡoːˈni.aː/ the exertion, anxiety, mental struggle of contending. Classical agonia is secular-nervous: athletes', orators', soldiers' strain before and in effort.

Hellenistic and New Testament Greek sacralise the strain. Luke 22:44 describes Christ in Gethsemane en agonia in agony — sweat as blood wrestling with coming death — and patristic writers (Origen, Chrysostom, Augustine via Latin) universalise agonia as soul-throe, martyrdom, death-pang. Late Latin borrows agonia (feminine, 1st declension, /aˈɡoː.ni.a/) for mental anguish and death-struggle; Old French agonie /a.ɡoˈniə/ (13th c.) narrows to death-throe, last pangs. Middle English agonye, agonie (c.1380, Chaucer, Wyclif) means anguish of mind, pangs of death, Christ's agony. Pronunciation anglicises to /ˈæɡəni/ with initial stress and reduced vowels; spelling stabilises agony by Caxton.

  • Verb creation agonize 1620s: As with many Latinate nouns, English verbalises: to agonize (OED 1623, Cockeram) first transitively to torment, afflict with agony (agonized by gout, agonized conscience), then intransitively to suffer agony, to wrestle (agonizing on the cross, agonizing in prayer). Formation is agony plus Greek-verbal suffix -ize (via French -iser, Latin -izare, Greek -izein to act like), regular: agonize, agonizes, agonized, agonizing. Early Modern stress /ˈæɡənaɪz/ keeps agon- plus diphthongal -ize. Theological and medical texts dominate: martyrs agonized, patients agonized, souls agonized.
  • Participial adjective agonized 17th–18th c.: Past participle agonized /ˈæɡənaɪzd/ lexicalises as adjective for sufferers and their signs: agonized features, agonized accents, agonized Saviour (Milton, Bunyan, Richardson). It competes with agonizing (present participle as adjective for causes) and agonical (rare). 18th-century sentimental novels spread agonized look, agonized voice as bodily legibility of feeling — torment must show. The construction agonized by plus cause (agonized by doubts, by wounds) fixes passive-transitive grammar.
  • 19th century secularisation and intensification: Romantic and Victorian melodrama, war correspondence (Crimea, American Civil War), and Gothic horror make agonized the standard high-style synonym for tortured, anguished, harrowed. Agonized cries echoed, agonized faces turned, agonized suspense fill Dickens, Brontë, Stowe, newspapers. Simultaneously American English develops intransitive-deliberative to agonize over (to wrestle anxiously with a decision), recorded mid-19th c. and now dominant in US business and personal advice: agonized over college choice. The stadium returns as metaphor: choosing is contending.
  • Modern horror-science-fiction use: Wells, Lovecraft, and pulp successors need a verb for monsters that suffer without dying. Agonized slime, agonized writhing, seemed only agonized is textbook: human weapons that kill men merely agonize the inhuman, implying alien physiology (distributed nerves, регенерация) and moral inversion (death as privilege). The adverb only plus agonized becomes a measure of failure and dread. Parallelly sports and awards discourse trivialises: agonizing defeat, agonized fans for last-minute losses — contest-sense resurfacing as fandom.

Modern form is stable: verb agonize (US) / agonise (UK) /ˈæɡənaɪz/, past/adjective agonized/agonised /ˈæɡənaɪzd/, noun agony, agonizing. Cognates sharing *h₂eǵ- are legion — act, agent, agile, agitate, axiom? no, protagonist (first contender), antagonist — but agonized alone preserves the stadium's pain as personal state. To be agonized remains, etymologically, to be driven as in a contest — driven to the edge of endurance.
