• wretch: Similar because both denote a pitiable or contemptible person, and both can express sympathy for the unfortunate; differs because wretch is literary and grave, stressing misery and suffering, while blighter is colloquial and jocular, mingling pity with amusement.
  • scoundrel: Similar because both can name a disreputable person; differs because scoundrel is a genuine moral condemnation of a rogue's character, while blighter is a mild, often affectionate word for a nuisance or unfortunate fellow.
  • rascal: Similar because both name a person whose behaviour is mischievous but not really criminal; differs because rascal suggests playful, forgivable roguery with affection, while blighter can also carry real contempt and pity, and has none of rascal's cherubic charm.
  • bounder: Similar as an old British slang term for a shabby, ill-bred man; differs because bounder targets vulgar social climbing and pretension, while blighter simply names a blighted, unfortunate, or annoying fellow.
  • beggar (in "you lucky beggar"): Similar because both are old colloquial terms that have softened into jocular affection; differs because beggar literally names a person who begs and so stresses poverty, while blighter stresses the idea of being blighted or cursed by ill luck.
  • nuisance: Similar because both denote an annoying, troublesome person; differs because nuisance is neutral and administrative (a legal term for annoyance), with no colour of pity or affection, while blighter is vivid, colloquial, and emotionally double-edged.
  • pest: Similar because both name an irritating person who is hard to shake off; differs because pest is plain and humourless, while blighter wraps the irritation in mock-pity and old-fashioned British charm.
  • fellow / chap: Similar because in its softened sense blighter means simply "a fellow" ("poor fellow"); differs because fellow and chap are neutral or friendly and carry no residue of blight or misfortune, whereas blighter always retains a hint of the blighted, pitiable, or troublesome.
  • rogue: Similar because both name a person of doubtful morals treated with amused tolerance; differs because rogue suggests an adventurous or charming scamp, while blighter suggests a luckless or blighting nuisance, with more pity and less glamour.
  • cad: Similar because both are British terms of social disparagement for a low or ill-bred man; differs because cad is a precise term for one who behaves dishonourably to women (especially in the gentleman's code), while blighter is a general, lighter, and more affectionate insult.
