Art is the archaic second-person singular present of the verb to be: thou art means you are. It belongs to the old familiar singular pronoun thou/thee/thy, used for intimates, inferiors, God, and Nature, and it survives today in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, hymns, wedding vows, and fantasy dialogue such as the source sentence, where a speaker demands Who ______ thou?, meaning Who are you?

In the source sentence the word carries interrogation and authority: Who ______ thou? is no polite modern Who are you? but a challenge flung at a stranger, with the archaic verb lending the question the weight of another age. Fairy-tale translators and fantasy authors reach for art precisely for this effect, signalling that the speaker stands outside modern time, whether a witch, a tsar, or a talking beast.

Core meaning in detail: The old English second person distinguished singular from plural: thou art (one familiar person) against ye are / you are (many, or one honoured person). Art therefore encodes both number and intimacy: singular, and either loving (thou art my love), contemptuous (thou art a villain), or divine (thou art holy). Its grammar is fossilised: modern speakers never conjugate it anew but quote whole formulae, thou art, wherefore art thou, how great thou art.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Second singular of be (primary grammatical sense, from Old English): thou art king, thou art deceived. The bridge from the PIE root of being is direct inheritance: the form descends unbroken from Old English eart through Middle English art to Shakespeare's stage.
  • The verb of prayer and liturgy (sacral sense): Our Father, which art in heaven, how great thou art. The bridge is the Reformation's English Bible, which froze thou art in worship just as speech was abandoning it, so that addressing God in modern you are still feels thin to many believers.
  • The verb of love poetry (intimate sense): sonneteers tell the beloved thou art more lovely, Juliet asks wherefore art thou Romeo. The bridge is the lover's singular: thou names the one irreplaceable person, and art seals the intimacy that you are cannot carry.
  • The verb of insult and indictment (contemptuous sense): thou art a knave, thou art weighed in the balance. The bridge is the same singularity turned downward: to thou a stranger in Shakespeare's London was to deny him respect, and art delivers the verdict personally.
  • Confusion with the noun art (modern false sense): learners meeting Who art thou? sometimes parse art as skill, craft, cunning. The bridge is pure homonymy: the noun art (from Latin ars) and the verb art (from Germanic being) share nothing but sound, and context, the pronoun thou beside it, is the only guard against the mix-up.

Register and connotation: art is wholly archaic and therefore wholly marked: no one says it by accident. Used straight, it ennobles (scripture, hymns, vows); used playfully, it jokes (thou art grounded, young man); used in fantasy, it world-builds, telling the reader within two words that magic is afoot. Its solemnity is its meaning: art never merely states existence, it performs it.
