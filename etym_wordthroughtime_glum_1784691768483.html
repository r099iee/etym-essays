- 14th century - "glum" begins with a face treated as weather: the Middle English verb "gloumen" (c. 1300) meant "to become dark", and by the late 14th century "gloumben" had shifted to "to look gloomy or sullen" — a dejected expression is a lowering sky made flesh.
- 16th-18th century - from that darkness of countenance the adjective emerged: "glum" (c. 1540) meant sullen, moody, frowning, with the related noun "gloom" (1590, originally Scottish) meaning a sullen look before it took on physical darkness in Milton and melancholy from 1744.
- Modern English - "glum" is the quiet, self-contained form of unhappiness: silent, passive, brooding dejection with a darkened brow and downcast eyes, a temporary mood of discomfort rather than despair.
