Single root word: ranch (English noun and verb, borrowed from Mexican Spanish rancho). The chain runs from an Indo-European root for bending, through a Germanic word for a ring, through a Frankish word for a circle of men, into French military and social vocabulary, and finally into Spanish and the Americas. Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *(s)ker-, "to turn, bend, curve" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. NOTE: this root has many apophonic grades and is very widespread (Greek kirkos "ring," Latin circus, English curve, cradle, shrink); the Germanic form relevant here is the zero-grade with a nasal. 2. Proto-Germanic *hringaz, "a ring, a circle, something bent round; a circular assembly of men" — reconstructed, Proto-Germanic era, c. 500 BCE–c. 200 CE; attested descendants: Old English hring (modern English ring), Old High German hring (German Ring), Old Norse hringr. 3. Frankish *hring, "ring, circle, a group of men arranged in a circle" — Frankish (West Germanic) of the 5th–9th c.; borrowed into Old French. NOTE: the exact Frankish form is reconstructed; it is the Germanic ancestor of the French words for "row, rank, order" below. 4. Old French renc / ranc / rang, "a row, a line, a rank; a group of men arranged in order" — attested from the 11th–12th c.; from the Frankish "ring of men" the meaning shifted to "ordered line, row, rank" (source of modern English rank and range). The semantic bridge: a ring of warriors or companions arranged in order became any ordered row or rank of men. 5. Old French rangier / rancher, "to arrange, put in order, line up" — attested in medieval French; the verb of arranging rows and ranks. 6. Spanish rancho, "a group of people (soldiers, sailors, labourers) who eat together; a mess; a rough camp or hut where such a group lodges" — attested from the 15th–16th c., borrowed from the French verb rancher ("to arrange") via the sense "a mess arranged to eat together"; the OED and the Real Academia give the military mess as the early sense. 7. Spanish (Mexican) rancho, "a small rural settlement or farm; a cattle-raising estate" — attested in the Americas from the 16th–17th c. onward; the sense expanded from the camp and mess to the whole rural property, distinguishing the smaller rancho from the great hacienda. 8. American English ranch, "a large farm or estate for raising livestock, especially cattle, in the western US and Canada" — borrowed from Mexican Spanish rancho in the 1830s (first English attestation c. 1831); the English spelling drops the final -o. Final node: ranch. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The Spanish and Mexican stages are well documented; the only genuinely reconstructed stages are the PIE root *(s)ker- and the Proto-Germanic and Frankish forms, which are standard reconstructions. The shift from "group arranged to eat together" (the mess) to "the rural property where such groups lodged" to "the livestock estate" is the accepted historical bridge, though the exact point at which the mess-sense gave way to the farm-sense in New Spain is not precisely datable. No other stage is seriously in dispute.
