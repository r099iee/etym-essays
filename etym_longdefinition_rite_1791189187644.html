Rite is a noun denoting a solemn, prescribed ceremonial act or set of actions performed according to custom, especially for religious, spiritual, or deeply significant social purposes. A rite is not any habit but a habit lifted into meaning: words said, gestures made, substances shared in a fixed order because tradition, faith, or community says this is how it must be done. Baptisms, weddings, funerals, initiations, sacrifices, and oath-takings are prototypical rites.

  • Core religious sense: A formal act of worship or sacred observance, e.g. baptismal rite, Eucharistic rite, funerary rite, rite of exorcism. Here rite stresses authorised form; to perform the rite validly is to follow the book, whether missal, Torah, Qur'an commentary, or oral elders' instruction.
  • Rite as church tradition (capitalised): In Christianity, a Rite is an entire liturgical, theological, and disciplinary heritage: the Roman Rite, Byzantine Rite, Ambrosian Rite, Maronite Rite. To belong to a Rite is to inherit a calendar, chant, canon law, and spirituality, not just a single ceremony.
  • Anthropological sense: rule-governed symbolic action: Since Tylor and Frazer, anthropology calls any culturally standardised symbolic procedure a rite: puberty rites, mortuary rites, rain-making rites, blood-drinking rites. Durant's use — sometimes as medicine, sometimes as a rite — contrasts pragmatic cure with symbolic communion; as rite, drinking aims to bind drinkers to victim, ancestors, or gods through prescribed repetition.
  • Rites of passage: Following Arnold van Gennep (1909), rites that mark transition between statuses: birth, puberty, marriage, death, graduation, enlistment. They classically unfold in three phases — separation, liminality, incorporation — and often involve ordeal, exchange, or shared substance like blood or food.
  • Secular and personal rites: By metaphoric extension, any solemn or inviolable routine: the morning coffee rite, the bedtime rite, the Sunday match rite. The extension both honours the routine (it matters) and gently ironises it (it is not really sacred).
  • Rite vs. ritual vs. ceremony vs. observance: A rite is a single prescribed act (a marriage rite); a ritual is the script or system (the ritual of the Mass) or any repeated symbolic behaviour; a ceremony stresses public pomp and spectators; an observance stresses dutiful keeping. One may speak of the ritual for a rite performed with great ceremony.
  • Adjectival and verbal kin: Ritual (adjective and noun), ritualistic, ritualise, ritualism all derive from rite. To ritualise a quarrel is to freeze it into form; ritualism can praise devotion or mock empty formalism, depending on tone.
  • Connotation: solemnity without automatic approval: Rite is descriptively neutral but stylistically elevated. It dignifies insiders' practice and can either respect or exoticise outsiders' practice. Calling blood-drinking a rite neither endorses nor condemns; it classifies as meaningful order rather than random thirst.

In Durant's sentence, as a rite or covenant captures two overlapping but distinct ritual logics. As rite, blood-drinking is liturgical: done in set manner, at set time, for communion with life-force. As covenant, it is contractual: binding parties by shared substance. Both assume belief that blood carries vital force transferable by ingestion. For modern readers, rite here should trigger not horror-movie exoticism but recognition: every culture, including contemporary secular ones, maintains acts whose power lies in correct performance rather than chemistry — handshakes, anthems, toasts, minutes of silence — and blood-rites are that universal impulse in its starkest form.
