A lituus is the curved wooden staff carried by a Roman augur as the badge of his priesthood and the instrument with which he marked out the sacred region of the sky for observing omens; by extension the word also denotes a curved Etruscan-Roman bronze trumpet with a similar crook at the end.

The augural staff is the primary sense and the one in your passage. Made of wood, without knots, curved at the top like a question mark or bishop's crozier, the lituus was held in the right hand when the augur defined the templum in the sky, designated the quarters for watching birds or lightning, and rendered the division ritually binding. Romulus himself is depicted as using a lituus at the foundation of Rome when he took auspices on the Palatine. The object thus concentrates Roman ideas of lawful foundation: no magistrate's act, election, assembly, or military departure was fully valid without correctly taken auspices, and the crook that traced the sky made that correctness visible.

The trumpet sense shares only shape. The military lituus or liticines instrument was a long bronze tube bent at the far end into a flared bell, producing a shrill blast distinct from the straight tuba and the coiled c Cornu. It appears on Trajan's Column, in processions, and in funerary contexts. Writers from Cicero to Ovid play on the double meaning, and antiquarians preserved both in museums and on coins, where the lituus staff appears as a terse symbol of augurate and foundation alongside the capis, tripod, and sacrificial knife.

  • Augural crook (priestly staff): curved wand of the augurs for inaugurating space, taking auspices, blessing foundations; e.g. the lituus of Romulus, the lituus on Augustan coinage signalling restored religion. This is the passage sense.
  • Curved war trumpet: Etruscan-Roman cavalry and ceremonial trumpet with bent bell; used for signals, triumphs, funerals; distinct timbre from tuba and cornu.
  • Antiquarian and archaeological term: in modern excavation reports and museum labels, any surviving curved staff-head or trumpet fragment of that type; plural litui.
  • Figurative symbol of divination and authority: poets and historians use lituus metonymically for the augurate itself, for foresight, or for the union of religion and power, as when Cicero contrasts lituus with sword.
  • Scientific transference (rare): in older natural history the name was borrowed for curved fossils or shells fancied to resemble the crook; now obsolete except in taxonomy history.

Polysemy is bridged entirely by visual analogy: a stick bent at the end. Romans did not confuse the uses; they recognized the same Etruscan-derived curved form serving religion in wood and war-music in bronze. The passage lists the lituus among wands, rods, canes, and maces precisely because all are straight power made eloquent by a distinctive head: fairy wand by magic, shepherd's staff by care, Moses' rod by miracle, consul's ivory cane by law, lituus by divine approval, mace by coercion.

Usage notes: pronunciation stresses the first syllable with a Latinate second vowel; plural is litui in scholarly writing, lituuses rarely. The word is rare, literary, and technical, appearing in Roman history, archaeology, numismatics, and historical fiction. It is not to be confused with litus, shore, or liturgy. Capitalization is unnecessary except at sentence start. Register is solemn and antiquarian; using it correctly signals knowledge of Roman religion rather than generic antiquity.
