tramps is the plural of the English noun tramp, and it means "vagrants or wanderers who travel from place to place on foot, living rough and usually without regular work." The word began as a name for the heavy, rhythmic act of walking — the tramp of feet — and shifted to name the people who live by walking the roads. In the context card, "tramps" are the weary foot-travellers who come down from the highway to camp by the water, part of the itinerant labouring world of Depression-era America.

PRIMARY SENSE — vagrants and foot-travellers (the noun)

  • Tramps (persons) — people who travel on foot from place to place, often homeless and without regular employment, living rough and relying on casual work, charity, or begging. The plural names the group, as in the context card: tramps who "come wearily down from the highway in the evening to jungle-up near water."
  • Historical register — in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American English, "tramp" named the itinerant wanderer who walked the roads and rode the rails; after the American Civil War and again in the Great Depression, large numbers of displaced men took to the road and were called tramps. The sympathetic, weary, heavy-footed colouring of the Steinbeck context belongs to this era.
  • The verb behind the noun — to tramp is to walk with heavy, regular steps over long distances; "tramps" are so called because they tramp the roads. The word retains this bodily, acoustic root: a tramp is literally a "walker," identified by the sound and rhythm of the walk.

SECONDARY SENSES — the word family

  • The tramp of feet / marching — the noun also names the act or sound of heavy rhythmic walking: "the tramp of marching soldiers," "the tramp of boots on the wooden bridge." This is the sense from which the person-word derives.
  • A long walk / hike — "a tramp through the woods" is a long, tiring walk or hike, usually on foot over rough country; this is a neutral, physical sense common in British English ("to go for a tramp in the hills").
  • Tramp steamer / tramp ship — a cargo ship that carries freight between any ports as cargo offers, with no fixed route or schedule, as opposed to a liner on a scheduled run. The name transfers the wanderer's lack of fixed path to a vessel that goes wherever business takes it.
  • Offensive modern slang (of a person) — in twentieth-century slang, "tramp" developed a derogatory sense for a promiscuous woman; this sense is offensive, gender-specific, and now widely avoided in polite usage.

MODERN AND EXTENDED USES

  • Sociology and history — "tramp" is a historical and sociological category for the itinerant poor; historians distinguish the "tramp" (who wanders, often seeking work) from the "hobo" (who travels by rail and takes seasonal labour) and the "bum" (who is stationary and does not work).
  • Literature — the word is a powerful literary marker of Depression-era America, as in John Steinbeck's fiction; it also names the stock figure of the "tramp" in Chaplin-era silent comedy and vaudeville — a ragged, comic, but sympathetic wanderer.
  • Register — applied to people the word is now dated or insulting and is rarely neutral; the physical senses (heavy walking, a long hike, a tramp steamer) remain in use. The plural tramps in the context card is deliberately old-fashioned and sympathetic, evoking the weary dignity of the road.
