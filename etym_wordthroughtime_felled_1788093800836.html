- [Old English: "struck down in battle"] - The causative of "fall": warriors fell enemies, kings fell rebels, executioners fell criminals; a verb of violence, killing and toppling in the battlefield and law-court.
- [Medieval forestry] - "Fellen" extends to timber: felling trees becomes the daily, practical act of woodsmen; the word acquires its double life — hewing wood and striking foes.
- [16th–18th centuries: the georgic age] - Husbandry manuals and rural poetry make "felling timber" a familiar, dignified labour; the participle hardens into "felled timber", "felled oaks".
- [19th century: industrial logging] - Railways and the world timber trade turn felling into scale industry; "felled" becomes the paperwork word of timbermen — measured, commercial, vast.
- [20th–21st centuries: the figurative blow] - "Felled by a heart attack", "felled by scandal", "felled by grief" — impersonal forces fell people as trees fall to the axe; sports reportage keeps the physical sense vivid; and Tolkien's dragon, crashing "from on high in ruin", felled trees with his weight — the word spanning the whole history from battle-axe to obituary page.
