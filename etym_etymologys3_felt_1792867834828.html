The felt on Sam's head carries one of the oldest technologies on earth, because matted wool predates the loom by millennia. Long before anyone spun thread, the herders of the steppe discovered that wetted wool, beaten and pressed with heat, locks into a firm sheet: each fibre carries microscopic scales that ratchet past one another under pressure but refuse to slide back, so the mass shrinks, densifies, and becomes cloth without a single woven crossing. Yurt walls, saddle blankets, nomad cloaks, and the mythic carpets of the Scythians all came from this fulling work, and the Germanic noun *filtiz, preserved in Old English as felt, names exactly that beaten mat. Handbook etymology links *filtiz to PIE *pel-, to beat or drive, through the pounding of the fuller's hands and feet, a connection as physically honest as it is linguistically plausible, though cautious scholars admit the Germanic word may stand without a certain deeper root.

Medieval England turned the mat into an industry of the head. Rough fulled hoods called felts were imported, dyed, stiffened, and blocked over wooden forms into hats, and hatters became a great urban guild, with mercury, steam, and fashion in uneasy partnership. The beaver trade of the 1600s made beaver-felt the luxury fibre of Europe: pelts trapped in Canada were matted into waterproof hoods that became the towering beavers and toppers of gentlemen, until silk plush and the collapse of the trade ended the reign. Beneath fashion, common felt kept its humble posts: soldiers padded doublets with it, printers damped it, table-makers covered billiard slate with its woven cousin baize, and piano-makers voiced hammers with it, the soft touch between string and note.

Living with this cloth-word, indistinguishable in sound and spelling, is a total stranger: the past tense of feel. That verb descends from PIE *pal-, to touch or stroke, kin to Latin palpare, to stroke, which gives English palpable, what can be touched. Germanic *foljan meant to perceive by handling, Old English felan already reached from fingertips to heart, and Middle English felte settled the dental past that modern spelling writes as felt. The two felts have eyed each other across the dictionary ever since, never merging, merely coinciding, like two walkers in identical coats. Puns practically write themselves: the hatter felts the felt, and Sam, feeling faithful, wears felt on his feeling head.

The twentieth century scattered the cloth into a hundred niches. Felt seals the gaps in pianos and the draughts under doors, lines jewellery boxes, quiets billiard cushions, and carries paper sheets through mills on endless felt aprons. The craft revival brought yurts to festival fields and slippers to Christmas stockings, while the classroom made the felt-tip pen, with its wicking fibre nib, the instrument of a billion doodles. Sam's tall shapeless bag belongs at the homely end of this spectrum: unblocked, untrimmed, unabashed, the cloth before tailoring, worn with the pride of a gardener who packed himself.

Landmarks of the journey:

  • Steppes matting: nameless herders beat wool into the first cloth, tents and cloaks without looms.
  • Germanic naming: *filtiz and Old English felt give the beaten mat its surviving name.
  • Hatters' guild: medieval blockers turn fulled hoods into hats, crowning towns and judges.
  • Beaver century: Canadian pelts become European toppers, fashion built on matted fur.
  • Stranger twin: feel from PIE *pal- yields its own past felt, touch meeting textile by chance.
  • Industrial felt: pianos, mills, tables, and doors all padded, sealed, and quieted with matting.
  • Shire wearing: Sam's bag-hat returns the word to its humblest glory, cloth before craft.

From Scythian tents to Sam's sham hat, felt has stayed what beating made it: the people's cloth, warm, cheap, and honest, with a twin in the dictionary that feels everything.
