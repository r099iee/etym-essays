Pasquinades (the plural form of pasquinade) in the source excerpt from Laurence Sterne's A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy (1768) refers to anonymous, biting, and satirical lampoons, scurrilous verses, or mocking squibs publicly affixed to walls and doors to ridicule corrupt authorities, pretentious hosts, or rival factions. Sterne creates a delightful rhetorical symmetry between Panegyricks (flattering praises pasted at one door) and Pasquinades (scathing satires pasted at that door), capturing the volatile, duplicitous atmosphere of eighteenth-century Continental social and political life, where excessive public adulation was constantly undercut by anonymous mockery.

In classical and modern lexicography, a pasquinade is a satirical or lampooning squib, poem, or prose broadside—traditionally anonymous and posted in a conspicuous public place—directed against prominent individuals, religious hierarchies, oppressive governments, or social follies. The term derives historically from Pasquino, a famous mutilated Hellenistic statue in Rome upon which citizens secretly attached witty, subversive Latin and Italian verses attacking the Pope, cardinals, and aristocracy.

  • Core Historical Sense: Anonymous satires attached to public statues or walls: Short, caustic, and witty verses or caricatures posted during the night upon public monuments (specifically the 'Talking Statues of Rome' like Pasquino and Marforio) to evade papal censorship and voice popular outrage (e.g., the morning after the papal decree, fresh pasquinades covered the base of the statue; Renaissance Romans delighted in the caustic wit of nightly pasquinades). The semantic bridge is direct: the physical act of affixing subversive texts to the stone monument gave birth to the literary genre.
  • Literary and Journalistic Sense: Biting lampoons, squibs, or satirical broadsides: Any published or circulated satire, burlesque article, or verse that aggressively targets and ridicules a specific person's character, vices, hypocrisy, or policies (e.g., eighteenth-century pamphleteers exchanged vicious pasquinades during the election; the minister was stung by a series of anonymous pasquinades in the press).
  • Theatrical and Dramatic Sense: Coarse or farcical personal satires: In seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Italian and French theater, short satirical sketches, commedia dell'arte improvisations, or interludes filled with mocking personal references and topical mockery aimed at local authorities.
  • General Metaphorical Sense: Scathing mockery or ridicule: In modern prose, any severe, witty, and barbed attack on an institution, artistic work, or public figure that employs ridicule as its chief weapon (e.g., the editorial was less a reasoned critique than a bitter pasquinade against the administration).

Stylistically, Pasquinades is an erudite, literary, and historically rich term. It conveys far more specific historical texture than general words like insults or criticisms: it immediately evokes the underground street wit of Renaissance and Enlightenment Europe, where the pen was used as a public weapon against tyrannical authority.
