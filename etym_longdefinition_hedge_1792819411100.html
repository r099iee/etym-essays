Hedge is in its first and most concrete sense a dense row of closely planted shrubs, bushes, or small trees — hawthorn, privet, beech, yew — grown and clipped into a continuous living wall that marks a boundary, shelters fields from wind, and gives birds and small mammals a home. The English countryside is stitched together by such hedges, some of them older than the farms they enclose.

In the source sentence the word carries exactly this rural sense: a fugitive jumps over a low place in the hedge at the bottom of a field and escapes into the meadows beyond, passing into the night like a rustle of wind in the grass. The hedge is the boundary between enclosure and freedom, and its one low gap is the doorway out — a moment every country reader recognises, of vaulting the hawthorn where it sags and running free.

Core meaning in detail: A true hedge is planted, not wild: quickset hawthorn or blackthorn is set in a banked ditch, laid and pleached by the hedger’s billhook, and trimmed yearly so it thickens into a stock-proof barrier that no sheep, cow, or casual trespasser can push through. Ancient hedges grow rich — botanists count woody species along a stretch to estimate its age — and become linear woodlands in miniature, loud with wrens, dunnocks, and overwintering butterflies.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A living boundary of shrubs (primary sense, from Old English): the field hedge, the garden hedge, the hedgerow. The bridge is the thing itself, unchanged for a thousand years: a planted barrier of thorn and leaf.
  • Any protective barrier or safeguard (figurative): a hedge against inflation, a hedge of secrecy round the talks. The bridge is functional analogy: as the thorn hedge keeps cattle out of the corn, so the policy or clause keeps harm away from the asset.
  • A financial hedge (technical, 20th c.): an investment taken to offset the risk of another — options, futures, and the famous hedge funds. The bridge runs through the safeguard sense: the investor plants protective positions around the portfolio exactly as a farmer plants thorns around a field.
  • To hedge — to evade or equivocate (verb, from the 16th c.): to dodge a direct answer, to sit on the fence with ifs and buts. The bridge is the image of sheltering behind the hedge: the speaker takes cover in qualifications instead of standing in the open.
  • To hedge a bet (verb phrase, from 1672 in horse-racing): to back both sides so as to limit loss. The bridge combines barrier and evasion: the gambler builds a fence around the wager so that no single outcome can ruin him.
  • The verge or margin of a field (dialectal, literary): poets write of violets beneath the hedge meaning the shadowed strip at its foot. The bridge is place-for-thing: the hedge lends its name to the little wilderness it shelters.

Register and connotation: hedge is earthy and English — thatch, loam, and hawthorn rather than marble or steel. As a noun it comforts: gardens, nests, blackberries. As a verb it faintly accuses: the hedging politician, the hedged answer. The financial sense has lent it a sleek modern suit, but the thorns still show through — every hedge, whether of quickset or of derivatives, is at bottom a bet against a dangerous world.
