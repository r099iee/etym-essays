PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*(s)ker-
"to cut, cleave, divide, shear"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*skurō- / *skurōn-
"a cut piece of timber, prop, stay, support"
↓
Middle Dutch / Middle Low German (c. 12th–13th c.)
schore
"a prop, stay, wooden stake, oblique strut"
↓
Middle English (noun) (c. 1330)
schore
"a prop, support, leaning timber"
↓
Middle English (verb) (c. 1390)
schoren
"to prop, support, set props under"
↓
English (modern)
shore
"to prop, support, brace (especially shore up)"

Note: English shore (meaning a prop or to prop up) was borrowed into Middle English from Middle Dutch / Middle Low German schore, reflecting Proto-Germanic *skurō- from the PIE root *(s)ker- ("to cut"), originally denoting a timber hewn or cut to size to serve as a brace. It is etymologically related to native English shear, score, and shard. It is homographic with the noun shore meaning "coastline" (from Proto-Germanic *skuro-, also from *(s)ker- as the division where land is sheared off by the sea).
