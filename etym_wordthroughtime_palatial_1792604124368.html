- [Late 18th c. (c. 1750–1780s)] - coined in British English during the Neoclassical Enlightenment and the height of the Grand Tour, derived from Latin palātium to describe monumental royal residences, ducal country houses, and classical Roman villas - [perception: admired in aristocratic and artistic circles as an erudite architectural term denoting neoclassical symmetry, noble proportions, and imperial grandeur]
- [19th c. Victorian Era] - expanded rapidly alongside the Industrial Revolution to describe grand public and commercial projects (such as luxury railway hotels, transatlantic ocean liners, and department stores) that made royal luxury accessible to the wealthy bourgeoisie - [perception: celebrated as the hallmark of industrial progress, civic pride, and commercial triumph]
- [Early to Mid-20th c.] - adopted by Hollywood and architectural entertainment to designate the spectacular Beaux-Arts and Art Deco 'movie palaces' and Gilded Age mansions of Newport and Long Island - [perception: associated with intoxicating luxury, glamorous escapism, and the immense private fortunes of corporate barons]
- [Late 20th–21st c.] - widely naturalized in luxury real estate, hospitality marketing, and everyday prose, while frequently employed with playful irony to characterize surprisingly spacious domestic apartments - [perception: universally recognized as the definitive adjective for awe-inspiring architectural scale, luxurious design, and unconstrained spatial abundance]
