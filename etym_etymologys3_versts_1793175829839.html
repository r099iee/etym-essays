The deep root of versts is motion itself: Proto-Indo-European *wert-, to turn, wind, revolve, the ancestor of English versus (the turned furrow), verse, convert, universe, German werden (to become, literally to turn into), and Latin vertere (to turn). From turning comes the furrow: what the plough turns in one pass is a versus in Latin and, by the same farming logic far to the east, a *vьrsta in Slavic, first a turned row in the field, then any row or line, then a stage or age of life (a turn of years), and finally a measured length of road.

Common Slavic *vьrsta carried all these senses together: Old Church Slavonic vrьsta means age, generation, row, and Old Russian vьrsta adds stature and measure of distance. The semantic path is the surveyor's walk: rows in a field become lines on a map, lines on a map become miles on a road. By the Muscovite period the road-sense had standardised at 500 sazhens (about 1,067 metres), marked by the famous striped verst-posts, and Peter the Great's empire counted its vastness in these units, from Petersburg to Siberia.

Stages of the change:

  • Prehistoric turning: PIE *wert- spins off turning-words across the family, from Latin vertere to English worsen (to turn worse) and worth (to turn out, become).
  • Slavic rows and ages: *vьrsta names furrow-rows, ranks of men, and generations, uniting farming, society, and time under one turning image, with cognates in Lithuanian varstis and Latin versus as distant cousins.
  • Russian standardisation (17th–18th c.): the verst fixes at 500 sazhens for roads (with a longer 1,000-sazhen boundary verst for land), the striped posts go up, and the word enters every itinerary, from merchants' reckonings to the Decembrists' exile-marches.
  • English borrowing (16th–19th c.): Hakluyt's voyagers, Napoleon's officers, and finally Constance Garnett's translations naturalise verst/versts in English, keeping the foreign word where miles would flatten the Russianness out.
  • Abolition and afterlife (1924–today): the Soviets metricate the verst away, but Tolstoy's battlefields, Chekhov's steppes, and fairy-tale Eagles keep counting in it, so that many versts to Koshchei's castle still measures dread in no metric at all.

Note on nuance: the exact chain from turning to mile passes through several Slavic senses (furrow, row, age, stature), and scholars trace them through farming practice rather than documents, since early Slavic left few written measures. What is certain is the endpoint: by the time English met it, the verst was Russia's mile entire, and the Eagle of the source sentence uses it exactly as a peasant informant would, to say that the hero's road is long, counted, and terrible. The striped posts are gone from the highways, but the word still paces them: every English reader who meets the Eagle's warning hears, behind the number, the sledge-bells, the snow, and the slow arithmetic of versts ticking away toward Koshchei's gate.
