The biography of tenour begins at the loom and the stretched string. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch, to extend" — a root of the taut line, the drawn bow, the spread cloth — and one of the most productive roots in the entire family. In Sanskrit it produced tanoti, "he stretches," and from it the vocabulary of weaving: tántu, the thread stretched on the loom (still Hindi tantu and Odia tantu), and tantra, the warp and framework, which came to mean a system or doctrine and reached English as tantra. In Greek it produced teínō, "to stretch," with tónos, "a stretching, the tension and pitch of a string," the ancestor of tone and tonic, and ténōn, "a sinew," the stretched cord of the body, the ancestor of tendon. In Germanic it produced the words for thin — Old English þynne, German dünn, Dutch dun — for what is stretched out is spread thin. The root's central image is constant: something held taut between two points, giving shape by tension.

Latin: the two great verbs of the root

Latin built two great verbs on this single root, and the pair is the key to our word. Tendere, "to stretch," is the active face: it gave tension, extend, intend, attend, contend, distend, portend, pretend, and the frequentative tentāre, "to stretch toward, to try," the ancestor of attempt, tempt, tentative, and even tentacle, the stretching feeler. Tenēre, "to hold," is the completed face — the thing stretched and then kept fast — and it gave tenacious, tenant, tenement, contain, obtain, retain, sustain, pertain, detain, and abstain. From tenēre Latin formed the noun tenor, "a holding on, a continuous course, an uninterrupted thread" — the state of being held steady. In medieval church music the word acquired its most famous sense: the tenor was the voice that held the plainchant melody in a steady line while other voices wove around it, and from that "holding" part the term passed into Italian as tenore and into English as the name of the male voice.

The journey into English: tenour and tenor

The noun crossed into English by two routes, producing two spellings. Through Old French teneur and Anglo-Norman tenour, English took the word in the fourteenth century as tenour, meaning "the purport or drift of a document" and "a continuous course." This was the legal and formal word: the tenour of a will or a deed is what the instrument actually says, read as a whole, and the phrase "the tenor of the document" long remained the language of the courts. Through the musical Latin tenor, via Italian tenore, English took the word in the fifteenth century as the name of the singing voice — always spelled tenor. For three centuries the two spellings lived side by side, tenour being the older, more literary and legalistic form, tenor the standard modern one. Ruskin writes "tenour," and the choice is deliberate: it gives the word an old-fashioned, bookish dignity exactly suited to the formal, gently defensive register of his Preface.

Semantic development

  • The legal/documentary sense: "the tenour of the work" in Ruskin is the book's settled character and intention as a whole — not any single passage but the continuous direction of the whole. This is the oldest English sense, inherited from Anglo-Norman legal usage, where the tenour of an instrument was its exact verbal purport.
  • The ethical/life sense: "the even tenour of his life" — a steady, unbroken course of conduct. This extends the holding-image from documents to biography: a life held on an even line, without jerks or swerves. The phrase became proverbial in the eighteenth century (and is echoed in Gray's "the noiseless tenour of their way").
  • The general drift sense: "the tenour of the conversation," "the tenour of public opinion" — the prevailing tendency or direction of a series of remarks or of a period. Here the word shades into tone and tendency, always retaining the idea of a consistent direction against which individual items can be measured.
  • The musical sense: the voice that holds the melody. Etymologically the same image — a held, continuous line — but in English it belongs to the spelling tenor, and in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word also denoted a general prevailing drift or character, so that the two branches reunited semantically while parting orthographically.

Ruskin's usage and the retreat of the -our form

In the Preface to Modern Painters, Ruskin uses the word to deflect a charge of partiality: "Whatever may seem partial in my task is dependent not so much on the tenour of the work, as on its incompleteness." The argument turns on exactly the word's semantic content: the tenour of the work is its settled, continuous direction, the design that governs every part; and Ruskin insists that any seeming bias is not in that design but in the accident of its unfinished execution. The word carries the feel of a thread running through a fabric, and it is precisely the steady inner bearing of the book that he invokes to separate intention from defect. In the twentieth century the -our spelling fell out of general use: tenor became standard for all senses, and tenour survives mainly as period flavour — the deliberate old-world gesture of a writer reaching for the language of the Victorian literary past, or of modern prose invoking that register.

The word's whole history is thus the history of one image — holding steady — moving from the taut string of the loom to the held melody of the choir, the continuous course of a life, and the settled direction of a book.
