The biography of depreciation begins in the marketplace, not in the counting-house. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *per-, "to hand over, to sell, to traffic" — a root of exchange, of the giving of one thing for another. It must be carefully separated from the homophonous PIE root *per- "through, across" that underlies English per, forth, and percent; the two roots happen to share the same three-letter shape in our reconstructions but are distinct families, and linguists keep them strictly apart. The selling-*per- appears in Greek as pérnēmi, "to sell, to dispose of," and in the Baltic languages as Lithuanian pelnas, "gain, profit" — the same market-idea of a thing passing from one hand to another in return for value. This much is uncontroversial. What is debated is whether the Roman word for "price" actually comes from this root: the mainstream view (Pokorny's) derives Latin pretium from the selling-*per-, but the OED and several modern handbooks treat the ultimate source as uncertain, and alternative origins have been floated, including an Italic or substrate source. No intermediate steps are invented; the classical Latin noun is simply taken as the secure starting point of the Roman side of the chain.

Latin: the birth of the value-verbs

From Latin pretium, "price, worth, value," the Romans built an entire workshop of valuation. The base verb pretiāre, "to price, to value," was combined with prefixes to produce a matched pair that still rules our economics today. With the upward prefix ad- ("to, toward") came appretiāre, "to set a price on, to appraise," which yielded Old French apprécier and English appreciate — to raise in value, to esteem. With the downward prefix dē- ("down, away") came dēpretiāre, "to lower the price of, to undervalue," which yielded French déprécier and English depreciate. The two verbs are twins: the same market-action performed in opposite directions, and the pair mirrors the two directions in which value can travel. From the past-participial stem of dēpretiāre Latin formed the abstract noun dēpretiātiō, "a lowering of value," in Late Antiquity — the direct ancestor of the English word.

The journey through French

As Latin dissolved into the Romance vernaculars, the noun crossed into Old French as dépréciation, "a lowering of value," used in trade, in law, and in the moral vocabulary of the time. English, in its customary way, took the word on two fronts. The noun depreciacioun appears in Middle English by the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, chiefly in legal and mercantile contexts, where the fall of the price of goods or the worth of a claim mattered in the courts. The verb depreciate arrived in the seventeenth century, formed more directly from the Latin participial stem dēpretiāt-, so that English owns both a French-borrowed noun and a Latin-shaped verb — a common double inheritance. From its first appearance the word carried the double sense that still defines it: a fall in monetary value and a fall in esteem. Seventeenth-century writers already used it of reputation, so that depreciate could mean both "to reduce the market value of" and "to speak slightingly of."

The eighteenth century: currency and theory

In the eighteenth century the word was taken up with new precision by political economy. Writers on money applied depreciation to the debasement of coin and the fall of paper currency — the shrinkage of value that follows over-issue or loss of confidence. It was a technical word of the pamphleteers and the early theorists of exchange, and it is in this fiscal sense that the noun appears in the economic literature of Adam Smith's generation. Here the word kept its cold, ledger-like neutrality: a depreciation is a fact about prices, stated without moral colouring.

Ruskin and the critical turn

The nineteenth century supplied the great literary demonstration of the word's metaphoric reach. In the Preface to Modern Painters, Ruskin writes: "In three instances only have I spoken in direct depreciation of the works of living artists." The accountant's word is deliberately pressed into the service of art criticism. Ruskin is defending the moderation of his attack — he has censured living artists in only three cases — and the financial metaphor is exact: to depreciate a work of art is to mark its worth down, as a trader marks down stock, and the sentence has the air of a man stating his liabilities with precision. The tone is measured, defensive, and slightly cold; depreciation here is a clinical judgment of value, deliberately drained of personal malice, which is precisely what makes it more damning than abuse — the artist is not insulted but devalued, and the devaluation is stated as a matter of bookkeeping.

The modern twin lives

  • Accounting: In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, depreciation became a technical pillar of accounting — the systematic, non-cash allocation of a fixed asset's cost over its useful life, matching expense to the value consumed in production. This is now the word's most common professional sense, so firmly institutionalized that depreciation is a line item in every company's accounts.
  • Currency markets: In a floating exchange-rate system, depreciation is the fall of one currency against another under market pressure — distinct from devaluation, the deliberate official lowering of a fixed rate.
  • Goods and property: Everyday financial speech uses the word of anything that loses resale value — a new car depreciates the moment it leaves the showroom.
  • Reputation and rhetoric: The critical sense Ruskin used remains alive, now applied to reputations, achievements, and public standing being "depreciated" in print — a word that is at once a technical formality and a quiet insult.

The word's history is thus a single continuous movement from the market stall to the ledger to the review page: value, once conceived as the price of goods, became the standard metaphor for worth of every kind, and depreciation travels across all those fields while keeping its root sense of a downward mark.
