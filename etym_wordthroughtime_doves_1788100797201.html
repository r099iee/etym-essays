- [Old English dūfe] - [a plain farm bird in glosses and prose; with the conversion of England the dove becomes the Spirit’s bird and the word begins to glow with sanctity]
- [Middle English douve / dove] - [in religious lyric the dove is the Holy Ghost; in secular verse the turtle-dove is faithful love; the word is now sacred and tender at once]
- [Early Modern English] - [Tyndale and the King James Bible fix the dove of Noah and the dove of the Jordan; Shakespeare makes ‘dove’ a term of endearment — the bird-word softens into affection and innocence]
- [Victorian England] - [‘dove’ turns sentimental: valentines, wedding doves, peace-society emblems, and ‘dove-grey’ in the fashion magazines — the bird of chaste domestic virtue]
- [October 1962] - [Alsop and Bartlett’s crisis article sorts White House advisers into ‘hawks’ and ‘doves’; the plural becomes a political faction — those who counsel negotiation over war]
- [Later 20th century] - [Picasso’s peace dove, white-dove releases at ceremonies, protest iconography, and a soap brand named Dove — the bird multiplies into icon, ritual and commodity]
- [Today] - [DOVES still coo on the rooftops, but the word also flies through peace conferences and press columns; and its homograph ‘dove/dived’ keeps it entangled with diving — one spelling, two birds]
