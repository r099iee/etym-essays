Gurgling is the present participle of the verb gurgle, and its entire biography is governed by one fact: the word is born of imitation. Its oldest stratum is not a PIE root at all but the reduplicative, sound-symbolic base *gurg- — the pattern g-r-g — which human throats seem to produce spontaneously for the sounds of swallowing, bubbling, and throat-water, and which appears independently in language after language. In Latin this base split into two branches that together prefigure the whole modern meaning of gurgle. The first branch named the throat itself: gurgulio “gullet, throat, windpipe,” a word of medical and colloquial Latin from the 1st century BCE. The second named agitated water: gurges “whirlpool, flood, abyss,” with its derivative ingurgitare “to flood, swallow greedily” (the ancestor of modern English ingurgitate). Already, a thousand years before English, the same phonetic core meant both the throat and swirling water — the two poles between which the modern word still oscillates. From Latin the verbal line descends as gurgulare “to gargle, gurgle,” a denominative verb built on gurgulio and attested in Late Latin and medieval glossaries. How exactly it reached English is debated: Middle English gurgelen is first recorded in the early fifteenth century, and the Oxford English Dictionary treats it as probably a native imitative formation, one reinforced by the parallel Germanic echoic verbs Middle Dutch gorgelen and Middle High German gurgeln. The word may therefore be a home-grown sound-coinage that happened to rhyme with its continental cousins, or a loan that spread through the same onomatopoeic instinct; either way, no speaker ever needed to learn it — the sound spelled itself. Key stages of the semantic history:
  • 15th century — birth: gurgelen appears in Middle English meaning to make a bubbling, throaty noise, applied to liquids churning in the throat or in a narrow channel.
  • Late 16th century — the modern verb settles: gurgle is established in its two poles — the human gullet and swirling water — and begins to take transitive and nominal uses.
  • 17th–19th century — the pleasant pole grows: applied to streams, brooks, and drains, gurgling becomes a gentle, musical, almost affectionate sound; by the nineteenth century happy babies gurgle in their cribs, cementing the merry register.
  • 19th–20th century — the dark pole persists: the ominous side never fades — a gurgling throat, a gurgling drain, the wet bubbling of a body half-choked with liquid; both registers coexist, one playful, one horrible.
  • Modern medical and mechanical jargon: clinical auscultation uses “gurgling” for coarse, bubbling airway sounds and bowel noises, and domestic technology has “gurgling pipes” and “gurgling traps” — the word has quietly professionalized without losing its echoic core.
Socio-cultural forces shaped the split. The eighteenth-century taste for the picturesque made babbling brooks and gurgling rills a stock of landscape poetry, attaching a sentimental, musical value to the word. The nineteenth-century nursery — with its literature of infant joy — annexed the word for the happy bubbling of babies, giving English the cheerful gurgle of contentment. Neither register displaced the other, because both answer to the same physics: a throat or a pipe half-filled with liquid makes the same noise whether the feeling behind it is joy or drowning. Tolkien’s use in The Hobbit depends on exactly this tension. When Gollum’s weeping is heard as “a whistling and gurgling sound horrible to listen to,” the word drags its whole double history into the sentence at once: the reader feels the musical gurgle of streams being perverted into the wet, choked bubbling of grief, and the collocation with “horrible” forces the dark pole to win. The gurgle of the brook and the gurgle of the dying throat are the same word, and that is precisely why it is so potent here. Collaterally, the same echoic family produced gargle (a controlled throat-rinsing), gargoyle (from Old French gargouille, a throat-shaped drain-spout that “gurgles” rainwater from a cathedral roof), and gorge (the throat, via Latin gurges) — a whole lexicon of gullets and waters that still betrays its bubbling origin.
