dwellings (plural of dwelling): A dwelling is a place where human beings (or, by extension, any settled organism) live — a residence, abode, or habitation, considered less as a bare building than as a place inhabited, occupied, and returned to. The word's core meaning is "a place of residence," and it carries a formal, almost legal neutrality: it is the noun of census forms, housing surveys, and planning regulations (residential dwelling, dwelling house), the neutral counterpart of warm, personal home. In the source passage — "That other smell as of wild beasts and Aihai dwellings, also tainted the ... with its clinging ..." — that neutrality is exactly what makes the word uncanny: these are "Aihai dwellings," the habitations of a pale underground folk, yoked in one phrase to "wild beasts," so that the noun recognizes the form of a home while repelling what inhabits it. The plural form is significant: dwellings suggests a whole settlement, a cluster of inhabited structures, a community of lairs, and the anthropological dread of the narrator arises precisely because intelligence has clearly settled here — built, occupied, remained — and that settledness is what taints the air with its clinging smell. In technical and historical usage the word extends beyond the living to the dead and the non-human: archaeologists speak of cave dwellings, pit dwellings, cliff dwellings, and lake dwellings (prehistoric pile-buildings), and zoologists of animal dwellings (burrows, nests, dens), so that the modern word happily serves "dwellings of the dead" — tombs and grave-sites treated as residences. Polysemy summary: (i) the act of residing or inhabiting; (ii) a specific residence, house, or abode; (iii) in the plural, a settlement or group of inhabited structures; (iv) by extension, any structure or place that houses an organism, including the lairs of beasts and the tombs of the dead; (v) in fixed phrases such as dwelling house and dwelling unit, a legally defined unit of housing.

gloom: At its root gloom means "darkness, dimness, partial obscurity of light" — a twilight state in which objects are visible but not bright, in which the light is failing or heavily overcast. In the passage it fills the dim rooms of the haunted house of the mnemonic, and its tone is atmospheric: a heaviness of shadow, a quality of the air that hangs over a space rather than a mere absence of light. But the word's modern range is far wider, and the historical bridge from light to feeling is one of the oldest in the language. By an extension already complete by the 18th century, gloom also means "deep sadness, melancholy, despondency, a darkening of the spirits" — a mood in which the mind is dimmed as a room is dimmed, so that a man in gloom is one whose inner light has failed. This dual sense yields the adjectives gloomy ("dark, dim" and "melancholy, sullen") and the verb to gloom ("to look sullen or dark"; "to darken, make obscure"). Secondary and technical senses include: (i) "twilight, dusk" — the gloaming of the evening, when the light is dimmest; (ii) the "gloom" of a forest or a cellar, a dimness that is felt as oppressive or threatening; (iii) the emotional gloom of grief or pessimism, often pluralized ("the glooms of winter"); (iv) in early modern use, a sullen look or scowl — to gloom was to look darkly at someone; (v) dialectal senses of "murk, smog, mist". The semantic bridge is metaphor by analogy: darkness of the physical world and darkness of the spirit are felt as one, and the word passes freely between the two, so that a "gloomy" day and a "gloomy" man are described by the same quality of dimness.

fetor: Fetor is the strong, offensive, unpleasant smell that rises from something rotten, corrupt, or foul — a stench, a reek, a bad odor that is so powerful it can be said to "hang in the air" and cling to a place. It is the noun corresponding to the adjective fetid ("stinking, having a foul odor"), and in the passage it is the clinging smell that taints the dwellings of the Aihai. Its register is literary and semi-technical: in everyday speech one says stench or stink, while fetor keeps a clinical, medical, almost Latinate dignity, which is why it survives in medicine (e.g. fetor oris, "halitosis," literally "foulness of the mouth"; fetor hepaticus, the sweetish stench of liver failure; fetor uremicus, the ammonia odor of kidney failure). The variant spelling foetor reflects a Latin hypercorrect orthography and is now chiefly seen in older or British medical writing. Polysemy is thin but present: (i) a stench, a strong foul smell; (ii) specifically in medicine, a diagnostic bad breath or body odor; (iii) by extension, moral corruption — a "fetor" of dishonesty or decadence rising from an institution, using the physical sense metaphorically. Summary of the triad in context: The three words work as one image. The dwellings name the place of settled habitation and betray its animal character; the gloom supplies the atmospheric dimness in which the scene is seen; the fetor supplies the olfactory proof of corruption. Together they convert a description of architecture into an experience of dread, and each word's polysemy (residence ↔ lair; dim light ↔ melancholy; stench ↔ corruption) deepens the passage's uncanny, moral, and sensory weight.
