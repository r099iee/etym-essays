PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*sem-
"one, together, as one, like, same"

Proto-Italic (c. 800 BCE)
*semeli-
"like, resembling, similar"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE)
similis
"like, resembling, of the same kind"
↓
Proto-Romance (c. 4th c. CE)
*similare
"to resemble, make like, appear"
↓

sembler
"to seem, appear, resemble"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
semblance
"appearance, likeness, outward show"
↓
Middle English (c. 1300)
semblance / semblaunt
"appearance, demeanor, likeness"
↓
Modern English (modern)
semblance
"outward appearance, likeness, show"

Note: Traces to PIE *sem- ("one, together, like", which also gave English same and seem). In Gallo-Romance, Latin similis led to the verb *similare, which underwent syncope of the unstressed vowel (*sim'lare) and developed an epenthetic /b/ between the bilabial nasal /m/ and the liquid /l/, yielding Old French sembler. The abstract noun of action suffix -ance formed semblance ("outward appearance"), which entered Middle English around 1300 via Anglo-Norman.
