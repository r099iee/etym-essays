1. Homer and the birth of the name. “Pygmies” enters literature in the Iliad (3.3–6, c. 8th century BCE): the clamour of the Trojan host is likened to the cry of cranes fleeing winter rain, cranes that “bring slaughter and fate to the Pygmy men”. The name Πυγμαῖοι (pygmaîoi) was regularly explained by the Greeks as “one πυγμή pygmē tall” — the pygmē being the measure from elbow to knuckles, roughly a fist’s length (about 13 inches): a people of fist-height, whose whole existence was the size of a fist. The word is thus a Greek coinage from a native Greek measure, and the “race” itself begins as a simile — a poetic contrast of the tiny with the airborne — which Greek curiosity then hardened into fact.

2. The crane-war and the ancient ethnography. On Homer’s single image classical authors built a full people. The pygmies dwelt at the world’s edge — by the Nile’s sources, or in India, or in Thrace according to some — and each spring, when the cranes migrated, they mounted rams and goats and fought them; the geranomachía (“crane-battle”) became a stock scene in Greek art, from the François Vase (c. 570 BCE), whose lowest frieze shows pygmies wrestling cranes, through gems, coins and mosaics for a millennium. Aristotle (History of Animals 8.12) records the pygmy-crane war as sober fact of the Upper Nile; Herodotus (2.32) reports short-statured marsh-dwellers through the Nasamonians; Ctesias of Cnidus (5th century BCE) placed “three-span” men in India; and Pliny (Natural History 7.26–27) catalogues “the Pygmaei, who are not more than three spans high”, with their mountain battles against cranes. For Greeks and Romans the word was simultaneously geography, myth and a proverb of littleness.

  • 3. Medieval transmission. The Latin encyclopaedic tradition (Isidore of Seville’s Etymologies, 11.3.26, deriving the name from πυγμή and recording the three-span height) and the travel-wonder tradition (Mandeville’s Travels, mid-14th century, places three-span pygmies in India) carry the word into Middle English: pigmei appears in English texts of the 14th–15th centuries as a race of fabled dwarfs at the edge of the map. Pliny’s prose, not Homer’s poetry, is the Middle Ages’ authority, and the pygmies become standard baggage of the marvellous East.
  • 4. Renaissance and early modernity. Milton gives the pygmies their most famous English lines in Paradise Lost (1.575–578): the fallen angels strewn on the burning lake are compared, in their multitudes, to “that small infantry / Warred on by cranes” — the pygmy army against the birds — in the same breath as the giants of Phlegra. In Renaissance prose the word is proverbial for tiny combatants and for disproportionate wars: littleness raised to a military legend.
  • 5. The 19th century: from fable to ethnicity. With the exploration of central Africa the ancient name attached itself to real people. Stanley’s and Schweinfurth’s expeditions of the 1870s encountered the short-statured hunter-gatherers of the Ituri forest and its neighbours; anthropologists adopted “Pygmies” (capitalised) as an ethnonym for the forest peoples — Mbuti, Bambuti, Aka, Baka, Twa — and, loosely, for short-statured peoples of Southeast Asia and Oceania (the Negritos, the Aeta). The word thus made its strangest historical jump: from mythic fist-high fable to a living racial category of Victorian anthropology, sometimes carrying the old camel-load of condescension.
  • 6. The word in science and in the streets. Zoology borrowed the adjective wholesale: the pygmy hippopotamus of Liberia (first described in the 1840s), the pygmy marmoset, pygmy owl, pygmy shrew, pygmy sperm whale, pygmy goat, pygmy elephant — the smallest of any kind became “the pygmy of its group”. Biology even named a human pattern: the “pygmy phenotype” of short adult stature with normal infancy, now studied as convergent adaptation to forest life, linked to growth-hormone and IGF-1 insensitivity. At the same time “pygmy” as an epithet for a small or contemptible person (“a pygmy of a man”, “intellectual pygmies”) hardened into routine insult, and modern usage guides urge the specific names (Baka, Mbuti, Aka), with “Pygmy” reserved, capitalised, for the group — a word that is simultaneously an ancient ethnonym, a taxonomic prefix and a still-live slur.
  • 7. Today. PYGMIES in the plural still summons at once Homer’s crane-beset infantry, the classical world’s pliable ethnography, the Ituri forest’s living peoples (Colin Turnbull’s The Forest People, 1961, made the Mbuti world-famous), and the zoo’s smallest hippopotamus. The thread that holds all four together is size — fist, span, species, esteem — one ancient measurement driving four millennia of meaning, from the highest poetry to the smallest cage.
