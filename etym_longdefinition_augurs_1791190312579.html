Augurs were members of the Roman priestly college charged with interpreting the will of the gods by observing birds, lightning, the feeding of sacred chickens, and other auspices; in modern English the plural most often refers to those priests collectively, and by extension to any prophets or prognosticators.

Roman augurs did not foretell the future in the popular sense. Their task was juridical-religious: to declare whether the gods approved a proposed public act. Before elections, legislation, military departures, or the founding of temples and cities, magistrates acting auspicato consulted augurs who defined the templum, watched the sky, and announced aves admittere or alites and omens as favorable or unfavorable. An error in auspices (vitium) could vitiate an election and required abdication. The college, originally three, then nine, then fifteen under Sulla and sixteen under Caesar, was a coveted elite priesthood held by men like Cicero and Caesar himself. Insignia included the lituus and striped toga. Your passage places the lituus of the augurs among power-staves precisely because without their sanction Roman power was illegitimate.

In contemporary usage augurs survives in three layers. The historical plural names the college; the transferred singular and plural name any diviner or expert who reads signs, from weather prophets to political pundits; and the verb to augur with adjective augural generalizes the act of portending. Shakespeare's augurs and auguries helped naturalize the transferred sense: augurs as gloomy interpreters of fate.

  • Roman priests of auspices (historical plural): members of the collegium augurum; observers of birds (auspicia ex avibus), lightning (ex caelo), chickens (ex tripudiis); guardians of inauguration procedure.
  • Diviners generally (literary extension): soothsayers, seers, oracle-mongers in any culture; e.g. the augurs of Babylon, political augurs predicting elections.
  • Figurative experts and pundits (modern journalistic): economic augurs, media augurs who read markets or polls as signs; often ironic, implying pretentious certainty.
  • Singular augur as verb base: to augur well or ill for something, to portend; augury as omen itself and as practice; inaugural and inauguration derive from augural founding rites, preserving the root in everyday democratic language.
  • Attributive and idiomatic: augural law, augural books (libri augurales), augural staff (lituus); phrase to smile like augurs from Cato via Cicero, meaning conspiratorial recognition of shared humbug.

The semantic bridge from birds to prediction to punditry rests on Roman etymological folk-theory itself: avis, bird, plus a speaking root, yielding one who speaks from birds. Even if modern linguists debate the exact formation, the cultural logic held: birds moving in a gods-ordered sky were legible signs, and legitimacy depended on correct reading. Hence inauguration still means installation made valid by correct rites, and auspicious still means favored by signs, now merely fortunate beginnings.

Grammar: augurs is the regular English plural of augur; Latin plural was augures. Adjective augural, noun augury, auguration, verb augur. Pronunciation stresses the first syllable. Register is elevated and literary outside Roman history; in journalism it is mildly arch. Do not confuse with augers, boring tools, though the homophony is complete in non-rhotic speech except for spelling.
