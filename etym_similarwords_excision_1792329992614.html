• deletion: Similar because both involve the elimination or removal of written words, passages, or digital content; differs because deletion is an everyday, generic term that can describe striking out a single typographical error or pressing a backspace key, whereas excision carries formal philological and scholarly gravity, implying a deliberate, surgical removal of substantial material based on rigorous critical criteria.
  • expunction: Similar because both denote the authoritative removal or blotting out of textual elements from a formal document or legal record; differs because expunction emphasizes legal expungement, erasure, or rendering a record legally void as if it never existed, whereas excision emphasizes the physical or editorial act of cutting out spurious parts while preserving the surrounding authentic structure.
  • resection: Similar because both are precise surgical terms denoting the operative removal of biological tissue; differs because resection typically refers specifically to cutting out a section or segment of a long continuous organ (such as an intestinal resection or partial liver resection) and reattaching the remaining ends (anastomosis), whereas excision refers to cutting out a discrete lesion, tumor, or localized structure entirely.
  • ablation: Similar because both describe the removal or destruction of tissue or surface material; differs because ablation often involves non-mechanical destruction through thermal energy, radiofrequency, laser vaporization, or erosive melting (e.g., cardiac catheter ablation or glacial ablation), whereas excision strictly involves sharp surgical incising and cutting away.
  • extirpation: Similar because both denote total removal; differs because extirpation connotes aggressive, complete, root-and-branch eradication or uprooting (e.g., extirpation of a cancer or extirpation of a heresy), carrying a more destructive and ruthless emotional intensity than the controlled, clinical act of excision.
  • omission: Similar because both result in textual material not appearing in a published edition; differs because omission can be accidental, careless, or passive (a scribe simply overlooking a line), whereas excision is always an intentional, active, and principled editorial decision to cut something out.
  • expurgation: Similar because both describe removing sections from a literary or dramatic text; differs because expurgation (or bowdlerization) is specifically motivated by moral, prudish, religious, or political censorship to remove offensive, obscene, or controversial material, whereas textual excision is motivated by philological authenticity to eliminate historically spurious interpolations.
  • censorship: Similar because both involve suppressing or cutting out parts of a text; differs because censorship is an external, coercive political or ideological intervention imposed by authorities to control thought, whereas editorial excision is an internal scholarly methodology aimed at recovering the original pristine archetype.
