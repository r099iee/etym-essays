The evolution of BEREFT is the story of a robbery word learning to mourn. Its deepest proposed ancestor is PIE *reup- to snatch, tear, break, reconstructed by Pokorny from Baltic and Slavic verbs for plucking and breaking, with cognates in Lithuanian raupti and related forms. Whether the root began with a laryngeal *Hreup- is a technical debate among Indo-Europeanists and does not affect the Germanic story. What matters is the semantic seed: violent seizing and tearing away. From this base Proto-Germanic built *raub- robbery, booty, and the verb *raubojanan to rob, continued directly in Gothic biraubon to despoil, Old High German biroubon, Old Saxon birobhon, and Old Norse raufa.

Old English possessed the simplex reafian to rob, plunder, seize garments or goods, and the prefixed bereafian with be- completely or around, meaning to deprive of, to strip, to despoil. The be- prefix is the same completive seen in besiege, bedeck, bereave itself: it turns taking into taking-away-from. Charters and homilies use bereafian for dispossession of land and treasure; the past participle bereafod meant dispossessed. Phonologically the stem vowel follows regular Germanic ablaut, and the f/v alternation between reafian and reave reflects Verner's law voicing in different positions.

  • Middle English depredation to desolation: after the Conquest bereven, bireven continues for robbery and ravishment, but devotional writers extend it to spiritual and emotional stripping: bereft of grace, bereft of comfort. Chaucer and Langland use it for persons despoiled by fortune. The strong -t participle bereft emerges alongside bereaved by analogy with left, cleft, dealt, giving the clipped modern form. OED citations show reft alone surviving as a poetic twin.
  • Early modern poetic range: Shakespeare uses bereft for swords taken in battle, for senses drowned, and for widowed mothers, holding literal and figurative together. The Authorised Version's bereaved of children fixes the funereal association for centuries. Eighteenth-century sensibility prefers bereft of hope and bereft of reason, moving the robbery inward to the mind.
  • Victorian solemnity and modern lightness: Victorian mourning culture makes bereaved the noun for mourners and bereft the adjective of dignified loss, printed on memorial cards. Twentieth-century journalism then bleaches it productively: towns bereft of industry, broadcasts bereft of ideas. The bus-stop use in the Baker anecdote is this late, affectionate stage, where a grand word for widowhood tenderly dignifies a small mishap before a bicycle rescue.

Throughout, sound change is modest: loss of the infinitive ending, reduction of unstressed vowels, devoicing of final -ved to -ft in one variant while the other keeps bereaved. The survival of both bereaved and bereft lets English distinguish the mourner from the emptiness itself, a division of labour no other Germanic language quite keeps. Beekes and de Vaan add nothing here, the word being purely Germanic, but OED, Etymonline, and Pokorny agree on the chain from snatching through robbing to mourning.
