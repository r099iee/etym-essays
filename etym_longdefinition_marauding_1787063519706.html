marauding is the present participle and adjectival form of the verb maraud, and in the source text — Tolkien's The Hobbit — it describes the trail of the dragon Smaug: "On this western side there were fewer signs of the dragon's marauding feet." The word means to rove or roam about in search of plunder or prey, raiding and looting as one goes; a marauding creature is one that moves across a territory leaving a trail of theft and destruction behind it. Applied to the dragon's feet, the word carries a fierce, predatory energy: the feet themselves are agents of the dragon's wide-ranging depredations across the countryside and the mountain.

PRIMARY SENSE — ROAMING TO PLUNDER

  • To move about through a region in search of things to steal or destroy: "marauding bands attacked the village at dawn", "wolves went marauding among the sheep-folds".
  • The verb implies motion and reach: a marauder is not a stationary thief but one who covers ground, striking here and there and then vanishing.
  • It applies equally to people, animals, and even natural forces: marauding soldiers, marauding foxes, marauding pirates, marauding storms — anything that ranges abroad leaving ruin.
  • The noun marauder names the agent — one who marauds, a raider or plunderer; marauding also serves as an abstract noun for the activity itself ("a life of marauding and pillage").
  • In the source text, the dragon's feet are "marauding" because Smaug has roamed the Mountain and its flanks, smashing, scorching, and plundering Dale and the lands around — the prints of his ranging depredation remain to warn the travellers.

SECONDARY SENSE — ADJECTIVAL USE: GIVEN TO RAIDING

  • As an attributive adjective, marauding qualifies any agent that roams destructively: "marauding armies", "marauding insects", "marauding gangs".
  • The adjectival use is frequent in narrative prose, journalism, and natural history, where it evokes mobility, menace, and sudden violence in equal measure.
  • Register: marauding is more colourful and literary than plain "raiding"; it stresses the wandering, ranging character of the threat rather than a single, fixed assault — the raider comes, strikes, and moves on.
  • Because of its French pedigree (see the etymologies), the word carries a faint exotic, military-romantic colouring that plain "thieving" or "looting" lacks.

ETYMOLOGICAL AND SEMANTIC BRIDGES

  • The word entered English in the early eighteenth century from French marauder "to rove in quest of plunder", itself from maraud "a rogue, vagabond, rascal" — see etymologyS2 and etymologyS3 for the debated deeper origin.
  • The bridge from "rogue or vagabond" to "one who roams to plunder" is natural: a vagabond is a wanderer without fixed abode, and a wandering stranger is easily suspected — and often guilty — of theft; the rogue's restless wandering became the plunderer's ranging raids.
  • By the nineteenth century the word was entrenched in military and colonial vocabulary, applied to bandits, brigands, and irregular raiders from India to Mexico, gaining a distinctly menacing and exotic tone in dispatches and adventure fiction.
  • In modern usage marauding keeps that menacing romance: it is the word for pirates, wolves, alien invaders, and — in Tolkien — for a dragon, all creatures that range abroad and leave ruin in their wake; even "marauding" news headlines about mobs and criminals draw on the same predatory image.

GRAMMAR AND FORMS

  • Base verb: maraud; third-person singular: marauds; past tense and past participle: marauded; present participle and gerund: marauding.
  • The noun marauder and the abstract noun marauding (the activity) are formed regularly; the adjective marauding is identical in form to the participle.
  • In the source text marauding is an attributive adjective qualifying "feet": the dragon's marauding feet — the feet that do the ranging and the destroying, a metonymy for the dragon himself.

SUMMARY

  • marauding = roaming or ranging about in search of plunder or prey; raiding and looting across a territory.
  • It describes predatory movement with a trail of destruction, and in the source text it lends the dragon's very feet the agency of violence and greed, leaving "signs" of his passage for the dwarves to read.
