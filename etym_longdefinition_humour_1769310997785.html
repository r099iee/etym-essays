humour (British spelling; American humor) — the word has a double modern life: it names both the quality of being amusing or comic and a person's state of mind or mood. Behind both senses stands the medieval doctrine of the four humours — the bodily fluids that were thought to determine health, character, and temper. English humour is therefore one of the few words that spans laughter, mood, and medicine. 1. The comic sense: the quality of being amusing (primary modern sense)
  • The quality of being funny, comical, or amusing in what people say, write, or do: "a story full of humour".
  • The capacity to perceive or appreciate what is funny: "a good sense of humour" — the ability to find amusement, to laugh at the absurd, to take life's contradictions lightly.
  • A rich vocabulary of compounds and phrases descends from this sense: "black humour" (morbid comedy), "gallows humour" (joking in the face of death), "dry humour" (deadpan), "self-deprecating humour", "bathroom humour" (crude comedy).
  • In British usage the spelling is humour; American usage drops the "u" to humor, as it does in colour/color, favour/favor.
2. The mood sense: a state of mind, temper, or disposition (older, still living sense)
  • A person's prevailing state of mind or frame of temper: "in a good humour", "in an ill humour", "out of humour".
  • In this sense it is close to mood, temper, and spirits, but carries the historical flavour of temperament determined by the bodily humours.
  • This is the sense at work in the word's historical core: Mr. Bennet's "sarcastic humour" in Pride and Prejudice is the cast of mind that finds the world ridiculous — a temperament poised exactly between mood and comic inclination.
3. The verb: to humour someone
  • To humour a person is to comply with their wishes, whims, or moods in order to keep them calm or happy: "to humour a difficult patient", "to humour the child".
  • The verb developed from the mood sense: to go along with another's humour (mood) is to indulge it rather than oppose it.
  • It shades from kindly indulgence to appeasement — humouring someone can be an act of tact or of cynical management.
4. The historical medical sense: the four humours (ancient and medieval medicine)
  • In the physiology inherited from Hippocrates and Galen, the body was held to contain four humours — fluids: blood, phlegm, yellow bile (choler), and black bile (melancholy).
  • Health and character alike were thought to depend on the balance of these fluids: an excess of black bile produced a melancholy temperament, of blood a sanguine one, of phlegm a phlegmatic one, of yellow bile a choleric one.
  • From this medical theory the word expanded to mean the temperament or state of mind produced by the fluids — the bridge from "fluid" to "mood".
  • The theory survives in modern words: sanguine, phlegmatic, melancholy, and choleric are all humoral adjectives; "good humour" and "ill humour" are humoral phrases.
5. Technical modern sense: the fluids of the eye
  • In modern anatomy the word survives literally: the aqueous humour and vitreous humour are the fluids that fill the chambers of the eyeball.
  • This is the direct descendant of the medieval "humour = bodily fluid" sense, preserved by scientific terminology.
  • It shows that the word's "fluid" meaning never died; it simply retreated from the whole body into the specific fluids of the eye.
6. The historical bridge from fluid to laughter
  • The chain runs: fluid (Latin humor) → bodily fluid (medieval medicine) → the temperament the fluids determine → a person's state of mind or whim → indulgence of that whim (the verb) → the disposition that finds the world amusing → the comic quality itself.
  • The "sense of humour" is, etymologically, the temperamental cast that makes one amused by things — a mood that has become a talent.
  • The complete journey — from the moisture of the earth to the laughter of the drawing-room — is one of the most celebrated semantic drift-chains in the English lexicon.
Register and usage notes
  • Humour is the British and Commonwealth spelling, humor the American; both are pronounced identically.
  • As a comic word it ranges from everyday ("sense of humour") to literary ("dark humour"); as a mood word it is formal and slightly old-fashioned, surviving in phrases like "in good humour" and in literary character description.
  • Do not confuse with whim (a sudden passing impulse) or caprice (an unpredictable change of mind), though the historical mood sense of humour includes both.
