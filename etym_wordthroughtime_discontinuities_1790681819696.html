- 17th-century learned formation: discontinuity as a logical-physical term meaning want of continuity, used by philosophers and mathematicians in a neutral descriptive sense
- 18th–early 19th c.: extended into natural history and geology to name unconformities and faunal breaks — perception shifts from logical fault to empirical fact about nature's record
- Late 19th–early 20th c.: rigorous mathematisation (Cauchy, Weierstrass, Baire classification of discontinuities) — connotation becomes precise, technical, value-neutral, a property to classify rather than deplore
- Mid-20th c.: human sciences appropriation — Kuhn, Foucault, Lévi-Strauss make discontinuity a celebrated anti-continuist slogan; perception splits: for traditionalists it signals failure to integrate, for radicals it signals honesty about rupture
- Contemporary plural discontinuities: routine analytic plural in interdisciplinary critique — connotation diagnostic and mildly accusatory, as in the source: to expose manifest discontinuities is to charge a school with isolation from neighbouring inquiry
