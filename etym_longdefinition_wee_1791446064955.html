Wee is an adjective meaning "very small, little, tiny". It is chiefly Scottish, Irish, Northern English and New Zealand usage, but it is widely understood and often used self-consciously in British English for a gently affectionate or diminutive effect. The comparative is weer and the superlative weest, though these are rare outside dialect writing. The word is also used adverbially in the phrase a wee bit, meaning "a little", and in the fixed expression the wee hours, meaning the early hours after midnight.

Primary sense: small in size. The core meaning is physical smallness, often with an overtone of endearment or mild condescension: a wee house, a wee lassie, a wee dram. Unlike tiny, which can be neutral or even clinical, wee usually sounds warm, familiar and slightly informal. It is the kind of word a speaker uses to soften a statement or to make a small thing charming rather than insignificant.

Secondary sense: small in quantity or degree. Wee extends from size to amount: a wee bit of sugar, a wee drop, a wee while, wait a wee minute. In these phrases it functions as a minimiser, often with a polite or coaxing tone. The construction a wee bit is especially common in Scottish and Irish English and has spread into general British usage, sometimes as a euphemism for "rather" or "slightly".

Temporal sense: the wee hours. The phrase the wee hours (or the wee small hours) means the early hours of the morning, roughly from midnight to dawn. The logic is that the hours are "small" in the sense of being few or slight, or perhaps because the night is at its quietest and most diminished. The expression is now standard in British and American English, even among speakers who would not otherwise use wee.

Regional and social distribution. Wee is strongly associated with Scotland, where it is normal colloquial vocabulary, and with Ireland, Northern England and New Zealand. In Scottish English it can be used with a range of affective nuances, from tenderness to irony. In England it is marked as Scottish or Irish, or as a consciously cosy, old-fashioned word. In North America it is familiar chiefly through Scottish and Irish immigration, the phrase wee hours, and literary representations of Celtic speech.

Grammatical behaviour. Wee is usually attributive (a wee boy) but can be predicative (the boy is wee). It is gradable, with weer and weest, though periphrastic more wee and most wee also occur. It does not normally take very in traditional dialect (a wee wee thing is possible for emphasis), and it can be reduplicated for a nursery effect. The adverb wee occurs in phrases such as wee bit and wee while.

Semantic neighbours. Wee overlaps with little, small, tiny, minute, diminutive and petite, but it is distinctive in register: it is warmer and more colloquial than small, less extreme than tiny, and more affectionate than diminutive. It can also imply youth (a wee child) or a short duration (a wee while), senses that pure size adjectives do not always carry.

Figurative and idiomatic uses. Beyond the fixed phrases, wee can be used ironically: a wee problem may mean a problem that is anything but small. It can also signal intimacy or local identity, as in the title of a Scottish song or the name of a small business. In children's language and in speech to children, wee is a common diminutive marker, comparable to Italian piccolo or Spanish pequeño in affectionate use.

Technical note. The word is not related to the verb wee meaning "to urinate" (a nursery euphemism of uncertain origin, probably imitative), nor to we the pronoun. It is a homograph and homophone of the urine verb, which is why the phrase a wee bit can occasionally be played on for humour. Etymologically, wee comes from a noun meaning "weight, a measured quantity", not from a word for smallness.
