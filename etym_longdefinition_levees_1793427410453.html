Levees (singular: levee, historically also spelled levée) is a noun displaying two major, historically prominent branches of meaning in the English language: first, a formal morning reception or assembly held by a monarch, head of state, or high political minister upon rising from bed; and second, an embankment, dike, or natural ridge constructed along the edge of a river or waterfront to prevent flooding or to serve as a landing wharf.

In the source passage from Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels (Gulliver's letter to his cousin Sympson), the word is deployed strictly in its primary historical political sense: Gulliver bitterly complains that his writings have failed to reform the nation, noting that he had expected to see ‘the young nobility’s education entirely changed; the physicians banished; the female Yahoos abounding in virtue, honour, truth, and good sense; courts and levees of great ministers thoroughly weeded and swept; wit, merit, and learning rewarded.’ Here, levees refers to the notorious morning receptions held by powerful 18th-century British cabinet ministers (such as Lord Godolphin and Lord Oxford), which were daily besieged by swarms of sycophants, flatterers, place-hunters, and supplicants desperate for political appointments, royal pensions, or court favors.

Core Meaning and Etymological Coherence: Both the aristocratic reception and the civil engineering flood-barrier share a single etymological root: French lever (‘to raise, lift, or rise’), from Latin levāre (‘to lighten, raise up’). The courtly levee signifies the physical rising of a ruler from bed into public power; the geographical levee signifies the physical raising of an earthen bank against the flood of waters.

Primary, Secondary, and Modern Senses:

  • Courtly Morning Assembly & Patronage Gathering (Primary Historical Sense): A formal early morning reception held by a sovereign, prince, or minister of state to receive courtiers, diplomats, and office-seekers while dressing or immediately after rising. Originating in the elaborate rituals of the French court at Versailles, it became in Augustan Britain the chief venue for ministerial patronage and political corruption.
  • River Embankment & Flood Control Barrier (Primary North American Civil Engineering Sense): An artificial dike, earthen embankment, or reinforced masonry wall constructed along the margin of a river, lake, or delta to confine floodwaters within the river channel (most famously exemplified by the Mississippi River levee system). The semantic bridge connects the act of raising earth with protecting settlements from seasonal deluge.
  • Geomorphological Natural Levee (Physical Geography): A low, broad ridge of sediment deposited naturally along the banks of a river during overbank flood events, formed when coarse silt drops out of suspension as the floodwaters decelerate. The bridge highlights nature’s own method of raising river boundaries through recurring inundation.
  • Official State Receptions & Vice-Regal Assemblies (Diplomatic & Civic Protocol): In modern diplomatic and commonwealth protocol, particularly in Canada, an official public reception hosted on New Year’s Day by the Governor General, provincial lieutenant governors, and military commanders to receive citizens and military officers. The bridge preserves the ceremonial greeting of the public by high state officials while stripping away the monarchical bedchamber intimacy.
  • Riverboat Landing Wharf (Historical 19th-Century American Commerce): In the 19th-century American steamboat era, a paved or reinforced waterfront landing along navigable rivers (such as at New Orleans, St. Louis, or Natchez) where paddlewheel steamers docked to unload cotton bales and passenger cargo. The bridge identifies the raised embankment with the commercial life of the riverfront.
  • Satirical Trope of Flattery and Sinecure Hunting (Literary Nuance): In 18th-century satire (Swift, Pope, and William Hogarth’s Marriage A-la-Mode), the minister’s levee symbolizes the ultimate theater of vanity, political servility, and corrupt patronage, where dignity is sacrificed for petty titles and bribes.

Register and Connotation: In British, European, and historical literary contexts, levees evokes aristocratic ceremony, royal intimacy, and political intrigue; in North American vernacular and geography, it immediately evokes civil engineering, river deltas, the mighty Mississippi, and the heroic or tragic struggles against devastating floods.
