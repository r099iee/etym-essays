Single root word: FLINT. The chain runs from a well-attested Indo-European root of "splitting" to the modern noun, and the whole history is remarkably compact and secure. Proto-Indo-European *(s)plei- "to split, cleave, splice" (reconstructed root; the meaning fits flint's conchoidal fracture — the stone that splits into sharp shards) → Proto-Germanic *flintaz "hard stone, rock shard, flint" (noun; reconstructed; the cluster *fl- plus -nt- is characteristic of Germanic; the PIE initial *splei- > PGmc *fl- shows the loss of s- in the Germanic offshoot, a debated but standard reconstruction) → Old English flint "flint, a rock noted for hardness and for giving off sparks when struck" (noun; recorded from c. 8th–9th c.; also already transferred to "a hard, unyielding person") → Middle English flint "hard quartz used for fire-striking, weapons, and tools" (noun; 12th–15th c.) → Early Modern English flint "spark-striking rock; symbol of hardness" (16th–17th c.; flintlock recorded 1680s; skinflint c. 1700; flint-glass 17th c.) → Modern English FLINT (noun; the stone itself, its tools, its sparking role, and all figurative senses of hardness). Note on debate: the PIE root is usually written *(s)plei- with the mobile s-, and the Germanic initial *fl- is derived by dropping the s- (cf. Latin plico "fold" but English flint). Some scholars prefer to treat the Germanic word as an independent Germanic coinage, but the root's widespread "split" semantics across Indo-European (Greek plínthos "brick," a split-off lump) make the *(s)plei- connection the standard, if not absolutely certain, account.
