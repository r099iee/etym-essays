English holds two separate words spelled patter, and their histories could hardly be more different: one is a sound made by rain, feet, and frightened hearts; the other is a prayer that turned into a salesman’s spiel. Tracing the pair together is a small lesson in how onomatopoeia and religious practice can each feed the same English word-shape.

Chain 1 — the sound of patting. The sound word begins with pat, an echoic verb first recorded in Middle English in the 14th century, imitating the light impact of a hand or object. Pat sits in a crowded family of expressive verbs built on a labial consonant (p-/b-) followed by a short vowel and a stop or liquid, all imitating small, quick contacts: tap, rap, thump, clip, clap. Middle and early modern English then used the frequentative suffix -er — which marks action repeated or continued, as in chatter, flicker, dawdle — to derive patter, “to strike lightly again and again.” The first recorded senses, from the early 17th century, are of quick repeated impacts: the pattering of hail or rain, the patter of small feet, the rapid beat of a pulse. The noun followed naturally from the verb in the same century: a patter is the sound of patting made substantive, a stream of light taps perceived as one texture of sound.

The sound family has remained astonishingly stable over four centuries because it is grounded in the physical world and in sound symbolism. The vowel a in pat is an open, bright vowel, and the sequence p-a-t gives a short, dry, percussive feel; English has used this sound-shape for light impacts since the 14th century and shows no sign of abandoning it. The family also generated a rich set of reduplicative twins — pit-a-pat, pitter-patter, pitty-pat — in which the vowel alternation i/a mimics a double, uneven beat, the classic template of English echo-words (compare tick-tock, ding-dong). In this idiom, pit-a-pat (16th century) was the heart’s own rhythm: “his heart went pit-a-pat.” When Tolkien writes in The Hobbit (1937) of a heart “all of a patter and a pitter,” he is drawing on three centuries of this cozy, nursery-shaped vocabulary to render fear as something small and tender rather than monstrous.

Chain 2 — the prayer that learned to talk fast. The second patter has a documented and famous origin. The Lord’s Prayer opens in the Latin Vulgate with the words Pater noster, “Our Father.” The phrase entered English very early as paternoster or simply pater, naming the prayer itself; prayer-beads were “paternoster beads.” Because illiterate congregations recited the prayer by rote, and because it was often mumbled quickly, the verb to patter arose in the 14th century meaning “to say one’s paternoster,” then “to mumble prayers,” and by the 16th century simply “to talk rapidly or indistinctly.” The leap from praying to talking fast is the semantic core of the story: mechanical, habitual recitation became the type of rapid, unthinking speech. By the 17th century the noun patter meant glib, fast talk, and in particular the cant of thieves and rogues — flash patter or canting patter was the secret fast-talk of the criminal underworld, so called because rogues “patronized” or “pattered” their jargon at speed. Street sellers, showmen, and conjurors picked up the word for their smooth running commentary, and in the 19th century the theatre gave it a lasting home: the patter song, a comic aria delivered at breakneck speed, became a trademark of Gilbert and Sullivan’s operettas, most famously “I Am the Very Model of a Modern Major-General” in The Pirates of Penzance (1879). A modern sales patter is the direct descendant of that fast, practiced flow.

The two families in the modern word. By coincidence or sound-symbolic attraction, the two words converged on the identical form: both are quick, light, and rapid, and both suggest a machine-like, near-automatic repetition — raindrops falling one after another, a salesman’s phrases rolling out one after another. English speakers have never found this homonymy confusing, because the context — weather, heart, feet, or speech — settles the meaning instantly. The sound sense remains the older, humbler, purely English creation; the speech sense carries a Latin and ecclesiastical pedigree. Modern dictionaries therefore treat them as two separate words, and etymologists point to the paternoster chain as one of the most colourful semantic drifts in the language — from the first words of Christian prayer to the fast talk of a fairground barker.

In summary:

  • 14th c.: echoic patten “to tap lightly” (sound family); to patter “to recite the paternoster, mumble prayers” (speech family).
  • 16th c.: pit-a-pat for the trembling heart; patter shifts to rapid, indistinct talk.
  • 17th c.: noun patter (sound) “rapid light taps”; noun patter (speech) “glib talk, thieves’ cant”; pitter-patter reduplicative established.
  • 19th c.: the patter song and the showman’s patter; the sound sense stays cozy and domestic.
  • 20th–21st c.: both senses coexist; Tolkien revives the sound sense for the heart “all of a patter,” keeping the nursery-jingle fear alive.
