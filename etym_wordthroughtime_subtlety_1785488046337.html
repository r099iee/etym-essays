- [Middle English sotil (13th–14th c., via Old French subtilite)] - Borrowed meaning "thin, fine-textured, delicate" — of cloth, threads, and bodies — but already applied to persons as "shrewd, wily," so the word balanced admiration for sharp wits against suspicion of cunning.
- [16th–17th century: the Latinising spelling] - The learned respelling subtle (with silent b) overwrites the older sotil; the sense moves toward refined, penetrating intelligence — a subtle argument is one of fine discernment, though subtlety still courts the charge of craftiness or sophistry.
- [18th–19th century: the scholarly age] - Subtlety becomes the word for fine distinctions that escape coarse instruments: philosophical nicety, delicate reasoning, perceptive delicacy; the craftiness sense recedes to the margins.
- [Modern register] - Subtlety carries an approving, almost aesthetic tone — refinement, understatement, fine-grained nuance (of humour, taste, metrical artistry); the sly-trickery sense survives mainly in phrases like "subtle manipulation," and the silent-b spelling marks it as a bookish word.
