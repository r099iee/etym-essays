Compound word — TWO independent roots, TWO chains. CHAIN 1 — the first element: jewel (PIE *yek- “to speak, utter”). Variable stages = 6. 1. PIE *yek- “to speak, utter” — a root of speech; attested in Celtic (Welsh iaith “language”, from *yek-t-), Germanic (Old Saxon gehan “to say, confess”), and Italic (c. 4500–3000 BCE). 2. → Proto-Italic *joko- “a jest, a spoken thing” — the speech-root specialised as “jest, play on words”. 3. → Latin iocus “jest, joke, sport, pastime” (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero and Catullus). 4. → Vulgar Latin *iocale “that which causes joy; a plaything” — a neuter adjective meaning “sportive, amusing”, substantivised as “plaything”. 5. → Old French jouel “ornament, jewel, gem” (12th c.) — the plaything became the treasure; the meaning glided from “toy” to “precious ornament”. 6. → Middle English juel / iuel “jewel” (13th c.) → English jewel — Final node of chain 1: English jewel (the first element of the compound). CHAIN 2 — the second element: encrusted (PIE *kreus- “to freeze, congeal, form a crust”). Variable stages = 7. 1. PIE *kreus- “to freeze, congeal, form a crust” — a root of cold and hardening (Pokorny's *kreus-), also reflected in Germanic words for ice and rime (c. 4500–3000 BCE). 2. → Latin crusta “a hard outer covering, shell, rind; a crust” (attested 1st c. BCE) — the nominal core of the family. 3. → Latin incrustāre “to cover with a crust, to overlay, to incrust” (Late Latin, from in- “on, into” + crusta). 4. → Old French encroster “to incrust, to cover with a crust” (12th c.). 5. → Middle English encrusten “to cover with a crust” (14th–15th c.). 6. → English encrust (verb) “to cover with or form a crust” (16th c.); the participle encrusted “covered with a crust” (adjective). 7. → English jewel-encrusted (compound adjective, 19th c.) — Final node: English JEWEL-ENCRUSTED (rootWord), the compound joining chain 1's jewel to chain 2's encrusted. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The derivation of Old French jouel from Latin iocus is the majority view, but a minority etymology derives jouel from Latin gaudium “joy” (the root of French joie and English joy); the OED favours the iocus route, and the debate is noted but not resolved here. (2) The PIE root *kreus- is reconstructed with the standard methods; its Germanic reflexes (Old English hrūse “earth, soil”, the rime and hoar-frost words) are usually connected, though some handbooks keep them separate. (3) The compound itself is modern (19th c.) and transparent: it is a simple juxtaposition of the noun jewel with the past participle encrusted, and no further derivation is involved.
