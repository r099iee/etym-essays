Derivation chain for FLUE — the immediate ancestor of English flue is contested. Two candidate lineages are presented separately, as scholarship does not yet allow a single secure path; the modern English word's first attested sense is 1580s. === CANDIDATE LINEAGE — Romance ("the flowing") === 1. Proto-Indo-European *bʰlew- "to flow, swell, run over" — the root of flowing and running water, ancestor of Latin fluere; Hellenic (Greek phlúō "to boil over, abound") and Indo-Iranian cognates exist but are NOT ancestors of English flue. 2. Latin fluere "to flow, run, stream" (attested throughout Classical Latin) — from *bʰlew- with the regular development; a large family grew from it in Latin itself: fluctus "a wave" (English fluctuation), fluidus "flowing" (English fluid), fluxus "a flowing" (English flux), fluens "flowing" (English fluent). 3. Old French fluer "to flow" — the regular development of Latin fluere in Gallo-Romance; and from it the noun flue / fluie "a flowing, a stream, a current" (attested in Old French). Note: French fleuve "river" (from Latin fluvius) is a related but distinct formation. 4. English FLUE "smoke-passage of a chimney" (1580s) — a sixteenth-century borrowing from the Old French noun, applied by analogy from running water to running smoke — IF this lineage is correct. === CANDIDATE LINEAGE — Germanic ("the pipe, the tube") === 1. Middle English flewe "mouthpiece of a hunting horn; a pipe, a tube" (c. 1400) — the older English conduit-word; no secure PIE ancestry is claimed for it, and it may be onomatopoeic (the sound of blowing). 2. English FLUE "chimney duct; conduit for smoke" (1580s) — an extension of the pipe/tube sense to the tubular passage of a chimney — IF this lineage is correct. === REINFORCEMENT AND DEBATED POINTS === - Whatever the immediate source, the modern technical sense — "the smoke-passage of a chimney" — is first attested in English in the 1580s, the great age of the enclosed English chimney. - The two lineages may well have converged: the Romance "flow" (Latin fluere) supports the chimney sense (smoke flowing upward), while the Germanic "tube" (Middle English flewe) supports the pipe sense. Most dictionaries describe the etymology as uncertain rather than decided. - The Latin fluere lineage does genuinely descend from PIE *bʰlew-, whose Hellenic cognate phlúō is noted for completeness; Sanskrit plávate "swims, floats" belongs to the parallel root *pleu- and is therefore NOT an ancestor of flue. - Flue is a late, isolated English formation: no word in Sanskrit, Hindi, French, Italian, or German descends from the English noun flue itself. German uses Rauchfang and Schornstein, Dutch uses schoorsteen, Hindi uses dhūmranāl / chhimnī — all unrelated to the English word. The French and Italian forms cited above (fluer, fleuve, fiume) are cognates of the candidate Latin fluere, not of the English noun flue.
