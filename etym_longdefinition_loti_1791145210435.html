Loti in the source passage refers to Pierre Loti, pen name of French naval officer and novelist Louis Marie-Julien Viaud (1850–1923), famous for exotic novels and travel sketches set in Tahiti, Japan, Turkey, and elsewhere; more generally Loti alone evokes that author and his lush, melancholic, orientalist style.

As a proper noun Loti functions as a surname/pseudonym: Pierre Loti explained his diet — i.e. the Tahitian chief spoke to the French writer during his 1872 stay in Tahiti that furnished Le Mariage de Loti (1880). In literary discussion Loti is shorthand for fin-de-siècle exoticism, naval travel writing, and early French Japonisme/Tahitianism. The Académie française elected him in 1891; his house in Rochefort is now a museum.

Other senses and uses to disambiguate:

  • Pierre Loti (author): core sense. Example: Loti's Tahiti novels. Attributes: impressionistic, mournful, colonial gaze; works include Aziyadé (1879), Madame Chrysanthème (1887, source for Madame Butterfly), Pêcheur d'Islande (1886).
  • Loti as common noun in other languages: In Sesotho loti is a banknote / Lesotho loti (plural maloti), the currency of Lesotho since 1980 (from Sesotho plural? mountain?). Entirely unrelated homograph; capitalised Loti for author vs lower-case loti for currency in running text; context disambiguates.
  • Loti as given name / surname elsewhere: Loti occurs as a South Asian and Pacific given name/surname (e.g. Hindi loti = small vessel? lota/loti water pot). In the Pacific anecdote, Tahitian loti also means rose (loan from English rose via loti?). These are coincidences, not the French author, but explain why schoolmates could nickname Viaud Loti after a flower.
  • Loti flower anecdote: biographers say young Viaud was called Loti after le loti, an Indian flower (lotus?), or Tahitian rose. The pseudonym thus carries floral-exotic connotations deliberately cultivated by the author.
  • Attributive uses: lotiesque, lotisme in French criticism mean in the manner of Loti — dreamy exotic melancholy. Streets, squares, a hill in Istanbul (Pierre Loti Hill), and a cake? bear his name.

Grammar: singular proper noun, invariable; with Pierre usually full Pierre Loti on first mention, Loti thereafter. Pronounced lo-TEE in English, lo-TI in French. Do not confuse with Lot (Biblical figure, English county lottery, French département) or lotus. Capital L always for the author.

Register is literary-historical; modern criticism reads Loti with postcolonial caution — admired for style and ethnographic detail, critiqued for orientalism, eroticisation, and the cannibal cliché exemplified by the source quotation (white man tastes like banana). Knowing Loti signals familiarity with French popular exoticism and its influence on opera, travel writing, and early anthropology.

Reading Loti today requires double vision: admiration for his musical, sense-saturated prose and early photographic/ethnographic curiosity, alongside recognition that works like Le Mariage de Loti stage Tahitians as backdrop for European melancholy and erotic fantasy. The cannibal joke in the source — a chief teasing Loti that roast white man tastes like ripe banana — is characteristic: half-reported speech, half self-dramatisation of the traveller who has gone deliciously native yet remains edible outsider. Scholars therefore pair Loti with Conrad and Stevenson to teach how popular exoticism shaped anthropology's questions and tourism's expectations, while Pacific writers answer back from the other side of the beach.
