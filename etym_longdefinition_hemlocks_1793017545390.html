Hemlocks, the plural of hemlock, are in their primary Old World sense tall poisonous weeds of the carrot family, above all poison hemlock, Conium maculatum, with hollow purple-spotted stems, fern-like leaves, and flat white clusters of tiny flowers that fade into ribbed grey seed.

In the source sentence they stand faded among the rank plants of a treeless waste: stalky and faded hemlocks and wood-parsley. The pairing is botanically exact, since hemlock and wood-parsley are both wayside umbellifers, and faded catches them past flowering, dry seed-heads on spotted poles. This is the plant of ditches, churchyards, and waste ground across Britain, beautiful at a distance and deadly at close quarters.

Core meaning in detail: Poison hemlock is a biennial that spends its first year as a low rosette and its second shooting to two metres or more, smooth hollow stems blotched with purple, leaves finely divided like parsley, and a mousy unpleasant smell when bruised. Every part carries the alkaloid coniine and its kin, which paralyse the muscles upward until the lungs stop; a few grams of leaf or a single parsnip-like root can kill an adult. The danger is sharpened by resemblance: to the careless eye hemlock is cow parsley, chervil, or wild carrot, and foragers have died of the likeness.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Poison hemlock, Conium maculatum (primary British sense, from Old English): the wayside poison of the source sentence. The bridge to fame is history itself: this is the kōneion that killed Socrates in 399 BCE, the state poison of Athens, and the plant has carried judicial menace ever since.
  • Any of several poisonous umbellifers, loosely: country speech sometimes stretches the name to cowbane, water dropwort, and fool's parsley, fellow white-flowered killers of wet places. The bridge is guilt by association: plants that look alike and kill alike share a name.
  • The hemlock tree, Tsuga, especially eastern and western hemlock (American sense, from the 18th c.): great evergreen conifers of North America, wholly unrelated botanically. The bridge is smell: crushed foliage of the young trees reminded settlers of the Old World poison plant, and the sinister name crossed the Atlantic onto a harmless timber tree.
  • The poison itself, metonymically: as in to drink the hemlock, meaning to accept a death sentence or a fatal doctrine. The bridge runs from plant to cup to fate: Socrates lifted the cup, and every later draught is his.
  • Literary emblem of treachery and witchcraft: from Shakespeare's witches to country tales, hemlock stands for corrupt beauty and secret killing. The bridge is the plant's own character: lacy white flowers on a poisoned stem, the perfect emblem of fair-seeming evil.

Register and connotation: hemlocks in the plural usually means the plants in the landscape, slightly literary, the botanist's or novelist's word rather than the farmer's curse, which prefers duller names. The singular carries the historical chill: to say hemlock is to summon Socrates, Athens, and the cup. American ears hear the tree first, a shade and timber conifer of cathedral forests, while British ears hear the poison first; the source sentence, with its faded ditch-side weeds, is unmistakably the poison. Grand and gloomy, the word never sounds cosy: even the tree named for it broods.
