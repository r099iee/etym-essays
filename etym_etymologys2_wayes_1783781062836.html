Chain for wayes: 1. PIE *weǵʰ- ("to convey, carry, transport, travel, move in a vehicle"; reconstructed; the ancestor of Latin vehō "I carry," Sanskrit váhati "he carries," Greek ókhos "carriage," and the Germanic family; c. 4500–3500 BCE) → 2. Proto-Germanic *wegaz ("course of travel, path, road", the o-grade noun built on *weǵʰ-; attested by its descendants Old English weġ, Old Saxon weg, Old Frisian wei, Old Norse vegr, Old High German weg; reconstructed for c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → 3. Old English weġ ("way, road, path, course of life, manner of doing"; attested from c. 8th–9th c. CE; the plural is wegas, "ways") → 4. Middle English wei / way ("way, path, manner, method"; c. 1100–1500; plural forms wais, waies, wayes) → 5. Early Modern English wayes (plural spelled with -es in 16th–17th c. orthography, which commonly wrote the plural -s as -es or -ies; also waies, weyes; Milton, Paradise Lost 1667, prints "the wayes of God") → 6. Modern English wayes (the archaic -es spelling preserved on the card; the standard modern plural is "ways"). Debated / nuanced stages: (a) the Latin noun via "road, way," sometimes cited as a sister form, has a contested derivation — many connect it to vehō (from *weǵʰ-), but some scholars derive it from a separate root meaning "to bend, turn" or from the idea of a side road, so it should not be treated as an uncontroversial member of this chain; (b) the plural orthography wayes is a spelling convention of early modern English, not a distinct morphological form — the -es writing of the ordinary plural -s is well attested (eyes, daies, wayes), and modern "ways" simply regularized the spelling; (c) the o-grade noun *wegaz is the standard reconstruction, but the exact grade and suffix (compare the o-grade *wóǵʰ-o-) are a matter of reconstruction from the attested Germanic forms rather than direct record.
