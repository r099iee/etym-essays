• Carnation: Similar because both refer to Dianthus caryophyllus; differs because carnation typically designates the modern, heavily cultivated, large double-flowered florist varieties that have often lost their natural fragrance, whereas clove-pink specifically highlights the heirloom, single or semi-double wild type noted for its intense, spicy clove perfume.
  • Sweet William: Similar because both are beloved, fragrant garden perennials of the genus Dianthus; differs because Sweet William (Dianthus barbatus) produces dense, flat-topped terminal clusters (corymbs) of many small bicolored flowers, whereas the clove-pink bears individual or loosely paired, larger blossoms on branching stems.
  • Gillyflower: Similar because historically gillyflower was the common Elizabethan name for the clove-pink; differs because gillyflower (from French giroflée) was a broad, ambiguous historical label that also encompassed wallflowers (Erysimum) and stocks (Matthiola), whereas clove-pink is botanically precise to Dianthus.
  • Campion: Similar because both belong to the pink family (Caryophyllaceae) with five-petaled notched corollas; differs because campions (genus Silene) typically lack the rich spicy clove scent of the clove-pink and frequently feature inflated, bladder-like calyces or open only at dusk.
  • Corncockle: Similar because both are traditional European wild flowers in the Caryophyllaceae family; differs because the corncockle (Agrostemma githago) is a scentless, magenta-petaled field weed containing toxic saponins, lacking both the eugenol fragrance and the jagged frilled petal margins of the clove-pink.
  • Wallflower: Similar because both were grown in English cottage gardens for their powerful, sweet-spicy spring aroma; differs because the wallflower belongs to the mustard family (Brassicaceae, Erysimum cheiri) and bears four cross-shaped yellow or orange-brown petals without the serrated fringes characteristic of the clove-pink.
  • Damask Rose: Similar because both were cultivated historically for their powerful aromatic oils used in perfumery and confectionery; differs because the damask rose is a woody, thorny shrub of the Rosaceae family producing multi-layered layered petals yielding rose-oxide and geraniol, entirely different from the herbaceous clove-scented Dianthus.
