The geography of gullies begins in the human throat. Latin had the noun gula, 'throat, gullet', extended by metaphor to appetite and greed, with the verb gluttire, 'to gulp down', and the noun glutto, 'a glutton'. The Indo-European prehistory is thin but suggestive: comparativists from de Vaan onward have read the family as a reduplicated, onomatopoeic formation of the shape *gul-/*glu- — the gurgle of swallowing made into words — which Watkins and others gather under the PIE root *gwel-, 'to swallow'. Cognates fan out across the ancient world: Old English ceole 'throat', Old Church Slavonic glutu 'gullet', Russian glot 'gulp', Old Irish gelim 'I devour'. Whether the root is a true PIE etymon or, as de Vaan argues, an onomatopoeic base reinvented independently in several branches, the family's centre of gravity is unmistakable: the throat, and the action of swallowing.

From throat to landscape. From Latin the word passed into Gallo-Romance as Old French gole (modern gueule, 'maw'), and its diminutive golet was formed with the familiar -et suffix: originally the 'little throat', hence the neck of a bottle, a narrow sea channel or bay, and a gutter. English borrowed golet about 1300 as gullet, 'the passage from the mouth to the stomach'. The geological career began when the same word was turned from anatomy to terrain: a goulet was a narrow channel, and by the mid-seventeenth century English had taken it up as gully, 'a channel in the earth made by running water'. The semantic logic is vivid — a gully is the throat the earth opens to swallow water, and the water, in turn, swallows the soil, scouring a gullet down the slope. The plural gullies appears in precisely this topographical sense in travel writing and adventure fiction.

Sound and form. The form shows the French diminutive stripped of its ending: Old French golet/goulet lost its final -t and adopted the English spelling gully/gulley with the common -y ending for nouns, while the doublet gullet preserved the French form intact. The vowel — French o from Latin short u — was shortened to English /ʌ/, so that gully rhymes with dully, not with gooly. The plural gullies is the ordinary English plural of a -y noun after a consonant.

Semantic and cultural life. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries gully became a workhorse of colonial and frontier vocabulary: American pioneers and surveyors used it for the steep little water-courses of the new territories, and beside it grew the New World variant gulch. American English stretched the image further, coining gully-washer by 1887 for the sudden downpour that scours such channels bare. Meanwhile geology fixed the word as the mid-scale landform, larger than a groove and smaller than a ravine, carved by runoff rather than by a permanent river. In The Hobbit the word earns its deep, wet menace: these are throats of the mountainside, narrow at the lip but 'very deep', still swallowing the streams whose 'waterfalls' fall within them — a perfect fusion of the word's anatomical origin and its topographical destiny, chosen, like all Tolkien's vocabulary, with a philologist's sense of the buried image in every word.
