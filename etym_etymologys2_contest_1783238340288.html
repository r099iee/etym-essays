PIE *trei- 'three' (English three, Greek treîs, Sanskrit tráyas, Hindi tīn) + PIE *steh₂- 'to stand' (Latin stāre 'to stand', Sanskrit sthā-, Greek hístēmi, English stand) → pre-Italic compound *tri-stā- 'standing as a third person', i.e. the disinterested bystander who can witness (the two roots fused before the Italic stage) → Proto-Italic *terstis / *tristi- 'a third one standing by, a bystander' (Old Latin terstis preserves the *tris- grade) → Latin testis (m./f.) 'a witness, one who stands by' → testārī 'to bear witness, to make a will' → con- 'together' + testārī = contestārī 'to call witnesses together; to make a solemn joint declaration' → contestārī lītem 'to join issue in a lawsuit' (the formal act by which both parties summoned witnesses and entered the dispute) → Late Latin contestāre / contestō 'to dispute, oppose' → Old French contester 'to dispute, litigate, oppose' (13th century) → English contest (verb, c. 1604) 'to dispute, oppose, strive for' → English contest (noun, 1670s) 'a struggle, rivalry, dispute'. Note on the debate: the traditional derivation of testis from *tri-stā- 'third-stander' is accepted by the OED and by Etymonline but has been questioned by some modern philologists, who treat it as probable rather than certain; the Old Latin form terstis is the main evidence for the *tris- element. The ancient jest that testis means 'testicle' because it witnesses virility is folk-etymology (the diminutive testiculus borrowed the word playfully). English test is an unrelated word from Old French test, an earthen vessel used to assay metal.
