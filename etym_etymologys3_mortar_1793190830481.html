The evolution of mortar is a tale of one vessel lending its name to everything made or fired inside it, so that kitchenware, masonry, and artillery ended up sharing a single word with perfect historical logic.

Roman kitchens and workshops all held a mortarium: a thick gritty bowl, often with a spout, in which cooks and pharmacists crushed grain, herbs, and pigments with a stone or wooden pounder. Archaeologists dig them up by the thousand across the empire, sometimes stamped with the maker's name, and the Latin word already bundled vessel and contents together — the bowl and the crushed mixture within it. That bundling would echo for two thousand years.

When the word crossed into French as mortier and into Middle English as morter, it carried both halves of the bundle. Monks ground medicines in morters, cooks ground mustard and spices, and builders ground burnt lime with sand and water into the bedding paste of walls — and since the paste was mixed and worked with the same tools, it took the vessel's name. By Chaucer's century the mason's mortar was standard English, and the wall's hidden skeleton of lime carried the memory of the apothecary's bowl in every joint.

Landmarks in the word's march:

  • Antiquity — the imperial bowl: mortaria mass-produced in Roman potteries serve kitchens, pharmacies, and building sites alike, already spanning food, medicine, and craft.
  • Early Middle Ages — monastic medicine: leeches and apothecaries keep the vessel central to healing, grinding simples for remedies, while masons raise abbeys with its namesake paste.
  • Late Middle Ages — the paste confirmed: building accounts record mortar by the cartload, and the mixture sense stands as an independent meaning with its own trade and tools.
  • 17th c. — gunpowder borrows the bowl: squat siege pieces with stubby barrels resemble an apothecary's mortar in profile, and gunners name the mortar-piece for it; the kitchen word learns to thunder.
  • 19th–20th c. — folklore and trenches: tale-collectors illustrate Baba Yaga riding her iron mortar, fixing the flying bowl in the world's imagination, while two world wars make the infantry mortar the foot soldier's own artillery.

Sound change along the way was gentle: Latin mortarium lost its ending in French mortier, and English vacillated between morter and mortar before settling on the -ar spelling under Latin influence. The three senses never confused speakers in practice, because trade context disambiguates instantly: the kitchen, the scaffold, and the battery each claim their mortar without contest.

Culturally the word's richest hour may be the folktale one. Baba Yaga's iron mortar — in which she flies, sweeping away her track with a broom and driving with the pestle — turns the humblest kitchenware into a vehicle of wild freedom, and illustrators from Bilibin to modern picture books have kept it clattering across the sky. That a word could simultaneously grind the pharmacist's pills, bed the bricklayer's courses, lob the soldier's shells, and carry the witch through the forest is a fair measure of how much history a single stout bowl can hold.
