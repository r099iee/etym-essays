follies is the plural of the countable noun folly, meaning acts, ideas, or habits that show a lack of good sense, prudence, or foresight; foolish or imprudent conduct; and, more broadly, the quality of being foolish. In the specific literary context of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Elizabeth Bennet praises her sister Jane for being "honestly blind to the follies and nonsense of others." Here the word is used in its satirical, affectionate Regency sense: the "follies" are the vain, absurd, and silly behaviors of the people around them — the affectation of candour, the empty pride of the Bingley sisters, the ridiculous deference of Mr. Collins, the pompous vanity of Lady Catherine — all the petty irrationalities of polite society that a sharp-eyed observer would normally notice and mock. Elizabeth's point is that Jane, through a singular sweetness of temper, sees past these weaknesses and even improves upon people's virtues, while saying nothing of their faults. The tone is not harshly moralistic: follies here denotes comic human weakness rather than vice or wickedness, a distinction Austen's narrator repeatedly draws between the two.

The core meaning (primary sense). At its heart, a folly is a failure of practical wisdom. Unlike an error of mere fact, a folly involves judgment: the agent should have known better, and the mistake arises from vanity, haste, passion, or self-deception rather than from ignorance or malice. Classic examples include the "youthful follies" of a prodigal son, "the follies of the age," and the proverbial foolishness of those who "commit follies" or "indulge their follies" — harmless or costly blunders that expose a want of sense.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The word has acquired several distinct senses, each with a traceable historical bridge:
  • Lack of good sense; foolishness (abstract). The oldest and broadest sense, e.g. "the folly of such an enterprise," "it would be folly to try." This abstracts the quality from any single act and treats it as a general trait.
  • A foolish act, mistake, or venture (count noun). "The youth's follies were many," "to commit a folly," "to indulge in follies." A single misguided action regarded as a breach of prudence.
  • Architectural folly (from the late 17th century). A costly, extravagant, and deliberately non-functional building — a sham ruin, a whimsical tower, a Chinese pagoda, a grotto — erected on a great estate purely for ornament and pleasure. The semantic bridge is the idea of an extravagance that only a fool would commission; the term came into English from French folie "madness, a mad fancy," which was also used of such pleasure-buildings (e.g. the folies around Paris).
  • Theatrical "Follies" (from the early 20th century). Lavish musical revues — above all the Ziegfeld Follies (1907–1931) — combining glamour, spectacle, music, and comedy. The bridge is the older sense of "a fanciful, extravagant entertainment": something costly and playful, indulged for its own sake.
  • Horticultural and landscape sense. Closely tied to the architectural sense: any ornamental garden structure or planting laid out to amuse the eye, such as a hermitage, sham temple, or "folly" tower.
  • Fossilized legal and proverbial residue. In older moralizing texts "folly" pairs with "extravagance" and "vice" to denote imprudence with consequences, as in "the follies of youth" that ruin a fortune.

Register, collocation, and grammar. Follies is fully countable ("his follies," "a youthful folly") though it often appears in the plural as a sweeping collective. It collocates with "commit," "indulge in," "the follies of," "youthful," "human," and pairs naturally with "vices" ("follies and vices"), "nonsense," and "absurdities." It belongs to a formal-to-mid register: elevated enough for satire and moral philosophy, plain enough for conversation. In Austen's sentence the plural carries a gently ironic, affectionate-collective force, gathering all the silliness of society under one indulgent word — exactly the register her narrator uses to mock without malice.
