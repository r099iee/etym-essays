• proverb: the closest everyday word; similar because both are traditional sayings passed on; differs in being the plainer, more democratic term, used of folk wisdom and of any pithy sentence, whereas adage is learned, literary and now usually dismissive.
  • maxim: similar in being a concise statement of a general truth; differs in implying a deliberate moral or practical rule formulated by someone, whereas an adage is inherited rather than invented.
  • aphorism: similar in being a short, pointed saying; differs in being characteristically the work of a named author and prized for its compression and novelty, whereas adage is anonymous and valued for age.
  • axiom: similar in being a self-evident proposition; differs in belonging to a system of reasoning and being either foundational or optional, whereas an adage belongs to no system and is merely old.
  • dictum: similar in being an authoritative utterance; differs in being attributed to a specific speaker, often a judge, and in being a statement of a principle rather than a proverb.
  • saw and old saw: similar in being the older English equivalent; differs in being rustic, folksy and now comic, and in being almost always paired with old.
  • truism: the nearest synonym in the modern dismissive sense; similar because both name a statement that is true but says nothing; differs in being analytically blunt and slightly technical, whereas adage keeps an old-world literary flavour.
  • platitude: very close; similar in naming a stale commonplace; differs in being more openly critical and more often used of a speaker’s remark as a rebuke, whereas adage can be used admiringly of age.
  • cliché: similar in being a familiar formula; differs in being a modern term equally at home with images, phrases and plot devices, and in carrying no implication of age or authority.
  • commonplace: similar in being an overused generalisation; differs in being a somewhat literary noun or a familiar adjective of banality, and in being less clearly about a wording.
  • tagline, slogan and catchphrase: similar in being memorable short formulations; differ in being modern commercial coinages referring to a specific utterance with an author, whereas an adage is anonymous and unowned.
  • motto: similar in being a short memorable sentence attached to something; differs in being attached to a person, family or institution as a declaration, and in not implying age or staleness.
  • gnome and gnomic: similar in referring to terse, cryptic wisdom; differ in being more learned, more often applied to verse, and not carrying the modern contempt of adage.
  • apophthegm: similar in being a compressed piece of wisdom; differs in being deliberately startling, paradoxical and attributed to a speaker, the opposite of the banal.
  • byword: similar in being a saying associated with a person or place; differs in usually carrying a negative reputation, as in a byword for sloth.
  • oracle: comparable at the outer edge, in that both can be a saying received with authority; differs in implying divine or prophetic source, which is precisely what an adage lacks.
  • epigram: similar in being a short pointed saying; differs in being a witty, quotable literary performance with a sting, not a piece of transmitted wisdom.
  • adages in the plural, contrasted with sayings and proverbs in bulk: the same words may be described as any of the three, and the choice registers the speaker’s attitude — neutral, learned, or dismissive — rather than any real difference in the words themselves.
  • proverbial, used as in the proverbial bad penny: similar because it marks a saying as traditional; differs in functioning as an adjective inside a phrase rather than as a noun naming the saying, and in often being jocular.
