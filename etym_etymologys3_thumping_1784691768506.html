Thumping belongs to that ancient class of words which English forged directly out of sound — the echoic, or onomatopoeic, stratum of the vocabulary. Its history is not the story of an Indo-European root travelling through centuries of sound change; it is the story of a language inventing a word to match the noise it names, and then watching that word proliferate into metaphor, idiom and political cliché. 1. The echoic stratum and the question of a PIE root The pattern *thump-, imitating the heavy, dull impact of a blunt mass against a solid surface, appears across the North-Western Germanic dialects. Scandinavian speech has tumpa, "to stamp or walk heavily," and English itself has built a whole family on the same acoustic model — thud, thwack, bump, dump, stump, stomp. There is no Indo-European root to trace here, because the word was not inherited but invented, and its invention is roughly datable: thump first appears in English records of the sixteenth century, when the verb "to strike with a heavy, dull blow" and the noun for such a blow enter the language together. Its persistence over four and a half centuries testifies to the accuracy of its sound: the voiceless th, the low short vowel, and the closing plosive group -mp reproduce with peculiar fidelity the sound of something massive, ungraceful, and final hitting the earth. 2. The sixteenth century: the word is born The earliest records show the word already complete in both parts of speech. The verb thump meant to strike or beat with a heavy, dull blow — the blow of a fist, a cudgel, or a heavy fall — and the noun named both the blow and the dull sound it produced. The participle thumping appeared alongside, doing double duty from the start as a verb form for continuous striking and as a verbal adjective. In Tudor and Stuart English the word was a vigorous, plain, muscular verb of everyday life, the natural word for the sound of a fist on a trestle table, a cobbler's hammer, or a fall from a horse. It carried no elegance and needed none; its bluntness was its meaning. 3. The semantic ladder: from blows to magnitude The interesting history is the upward climb of the adjective. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the participial thumping took a comic semantic step from "striking heavily" to "strikingly large or emphatic." The bridge is the logic of weight: a thumping blow is a heavy one, and heavy things are big, so thumping came to mean "big, emphatic, resounding" without any physical blow at all. By the nineteenth century one could tell a thumping lie, receive a thumping fine, and — in the twentieth-century British political idiom that has fixed the word in modern speech — win a thumping majority, a victory so large that it resounds through the house like a heavy fist on a table. Meanwhile the heart, in figurative and sentimental prose from the eighteenth century on, began to thump — to beat hard with fear, joy, or exertion — so the word acquired an emotional register as well as a physical one, while the agent noun thumper (twentieth century) came to denote anything remarkably large, a "whopper." 4. The senses in summary
  • Physical (16th century–present): heavy, dull, repeated striking; the sound of it.
  • Of the heart and pulse (18th century–present): beating hard and violently with emotion or exertion.
  • Intensive adjective (18th–19th centuries–present): very large, emphatic, striking — chiefly British colloquial.
  • Adverbial intensive (19th century–present): thumping great, thumping good.
  • Agent noun thumper (20th century): a remarkably large specimen.
5. Socio-cultural and literary life The word's social register has never risen above the homely, and that is its strength. It belongs to taverns, barracks, kitchens and sports fields rather than to pulpits or courts; its comic weight made it a natural favourite of novelists of common life, and its acoustic clarity makes it instantly vivid in the mouth. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2, Tolkien uses it with exact discrimination: the two wrestling trolls roll "kicking and thumping" — the dull, monotonous, ungraceful music of great weight — against which "whacked," the bright crack of Tom's branch, sounds in precise acoustic contrast. The thump is the sound of bodies, the whack the sound of a stick; the comic failure of the rescue ("and that of course only made them madder than ever") is carried entirely by the sound-painting of the two verbs. Four centuries of echoic life are compressed into a single clause.
