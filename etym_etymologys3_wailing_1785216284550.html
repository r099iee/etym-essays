The history of wailing is, literally, the history of a cry. Unlike most words, which begin as names for things, this one begins as a sound — a primal exclamation of grief so basic that it is reconstructed as the Proto-Indo-European interjection *wai-, "ah! alas!" The word is onomatopoeic in character: it is the natural human vocalisation of distress, and precisely because it is a cry rather than a concept, it survived the great sound-shifts of Indo-European without being reshaped. The famous shift described by Grimm's Law — which changed PIE *p to Germanic *f, *t to *θ, and *k to *h — simply had nothing to work on here, because an interjection of woe does not belong to the regular consonant system. The result is that the same cry appears, almost unchanged, in every branch of the family: Latin vae (cried by the Romans in "vae victis," woe to the vanquished, when the Gauls took the Capitol), Welsh gwae, Old Irish fāe, and, in Germanic, Old English wā, the ancestor of modern woe and its family woeful and woebegone.

What turned the word into a verb was the Norse genius for building speech-acts out of exclamations. In Old Norse the same cry appeared as vei, and Norse speakers did something remarkable: they derived from it a denominative verb, væla, "to utter woe," turning the bare cry into an action that could be performed aloud — an activity with a beginning and an end. Around 1300, Middle English borrowed this verb as waylen: to cry aloud in sorrow, to lament with a sustained, piercing voice. As the Middle English diphthong -ay- smoothed to the modern long vowel, the spelling and sound settled as wail. From the very beginning the word was bound to grief and refused the milder uses of weep or moan. The fourteenth-century translation of the Bible fixed its register forever: "there shall be wailing and gnashing of teeth" — the phrase from the Gospels naming the sound of the damned — made wailing the archetypal noise of utter hopelessness. Throughout the Middle Ages and the early modern period the word dominated scenes of funeral lament, prophecy, and disaster; it stayed elevated and intense, the public, piercing cry of despair rather than private tears.

The present participle wailing converted the single cry into a state and a continuing action. The -ing suffix is not incidental: to wail is an act, but to be wailing is to be caught in the act, to be unable to stop, the sound going on and on. It is this continuous quality that makes the participle so suited to Tolkien's sentence. Gollum is "cursing and wailing away in the gloom" — the cursing gives him articulate abuse, the wailing shows the raw, keening misery beneath it, and the -ing makes the sound unending, a lament that drifts through the dark toward the listening Bilbo. The word is never used lightly: a person wails only in extremity, and the reader's ear registers the sound as shrill, self-pitying, and horrible.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries extended the cry beyond the human voice. Anything that produces a high, sustained, mournful, piercing sound may now be said to wail: sirens wail through a city at night, the wind wails through a desolate valley, bagpipes wail, trains wail at a crossing. The transfer preserved the emotional signature of the word — the quality of longing and loss — while detaching it from the mourner's throat. In the same period music adopted the word: a wailing blues guitar or saxophone "cries" with bent, high notes, and wailing became a term for a style of impassioned, mournful instrumental or vocal performance. Yet the word has never softened. It still names the prolonged, high-pitched cry of grief or pain — largely wordless, desperate, dramatic — and it sits precisely between whine and howl: higher and more sustained than a moan, more piercing and less articulate than a lament. Four thousand years after the first *wai- was cried in grief, English still names the same sound with the same word, and Gollum's wailing is that ancient cry still echoing in the dark.
