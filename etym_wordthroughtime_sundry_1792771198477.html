- [Old English c. 9th–11th c.] — Attested as syndrig (derived from adverb sundor, "asunder"), signifying separate, private, peculiar, distinct, or individual — [perception: neutral administrative and legal descriptor of distinct property or private persons].
- [Middle English 12th–15th c.] — Shifted phonologically to sondry / sundry, broadening from "separate" to "various, several, diverse kinds"; immortalized in Chaucer's 1387 General Prologue to The Canterbury Tales ("of sondry folk") — [perception: universal, vibrant descriptor of social variety and diverse human assemblies].
- [Early Modern English 16th–17th c.] — Codified in legal formulas and civic proclamations, giving birth to the enduring fixed legal and popular idiom "all and sundry" (meaning everyone collectively and each person individually) — [perception: authoritative juridical and colloquial catch-all phrase].
- [18th–19th c. Industrial & Victorian Commerce] — Nominalized into the commercial bookkeeping plural sundries (meaning minor, incidental expenses or unclassified trade goods that do not warrant individual account ledgers) — [perception: pragmatic, utilitarian accounting term for miscellaneous trivia].
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Relegated largely to stylized, archaic, humorous, or nostalgic prose, while surviving robustly in the idiom "all and sundry" and in hotel / retail charge sheets ("sundries") — [perception: warm, slightly whimsical literary word evocative of traditional English storytelling].
