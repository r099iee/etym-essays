zealous (adjective) — full of or characterized by zeal; intensely eager, passionate, and devoted in pursuit of a cause, belief, religion, or objective; marked by fervent, whole-hearted commitment that shows itself in sustained effort and advocacy. CORE MEANING At its core, zealous describes the quality of acting with zeal: an energetic, fervent, and unflagging devotion to something one values — a faith, a political programme, a duty, a person, or a cause. It is a word of intensity: the zealous person does not merely support or approve but pursues, promotes, and defends with ardour. In the passage under study Macaulay uses it of the Germanic kings Alaric, Theodoric, Clovis, and Alboin, calling them 'zealous Christians' — passionate, newly-kindled converts for whom the new religion was still a novelty and therefore a burning devotion, in contrast to the merely habitual or political Christianity of established courts. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Religious sense — zealous faith, zealous believers, zealous preaching; historically the primary sense, from the Reformation onward, and the sense Macaulay exploits. The semantic bridge: religious fervour is the classic object of zeal, and the word has always been at home in sermons and devotional writing.
  • Historical sectarian sense — the English zealous shares its ultimate root with the Zealots (Greek Zēlōtai), the 1st-century Jewish faction that resisted Roman rule with armed militancy; through the New Testament and the Church Fathers, 'zealot' hardened into 'fanatical partisan', and zealous can still carry that militant edge when used of political or religious partisanship.
  • Legal sense — in American professional ethics, 'zealous representation' (or 'zealous advocacy') is the duty of an attorney to advocate vigorously for a client within the bounds of the law; a technical-jargon sense in which zealous denotes the expected standard of committed professional service.
  • Political and social sense — zealous advocates, zealous reformers, zealous campaigners; the word is standard for committed partisanship in public life, retaining approval while its cousin overzealous marks the point where fervour tips into excess.
  • Personal-hobby sense — a zealous collector, a zealous gardener, a zealous fan; used colloquially for intense personal enthusiasm that may be slightly comic in disproportion.
  • Negative / pejorative shading — zealous can shade toward censure when the cause itself is questionable: 'zealous persecution', 'zealous censorship', 'zealous book-burning'; the pejorative pole is fully institutionalized in overzealous, zealot, and fanatical.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGES The semantic bridge from the Greek root is one of the most striking in the language. Greek ζῆλος (zēlos) meant 'ardour, eager rivalry, emulation, jealousy', traditionally derived from an Indo-European root meaning 'to boil, seethe, foam' — the earliest sense of the Greek word is a passionate heat that rises and bubbles over, transferred from the boiling of a pot to the ferment of desire and ambition. From this image of boiling passion, Latin borrowed zēlus, and through Old French the word split into the doublets zealous and jealous: jealous drifted toward 'possessive suspicion, envy' while zealous kept the older sense of devoted ardor. English zealous itself is a bookish coinage of the sixteenth century, formed from Late Latin zēlōsus with the native suffix -ous, and it has carried ever since the double potential of the root — admiration for fervour on the one hand, suspicion of excess on the other. Modern English preserves both poles in 'zealous advocate' (laudatory) and 'overzealous' (reproachful).
