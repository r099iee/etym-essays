YELPING is the present participle of the verb yelp, meaning "uttering short, sharp, high-pitched cries," typically of pain, surprise, or distress. The word is used of the involuntary vocal spasm that breaks from a creature in sudden discomfort or alarm, a brief, staccato, single-syllable cry repeated, the voice of a dog trodden on, of a child startled, of an animal struck. In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses the participle in the middle of the dwarves' quarrel after their escape from the goblins' tunnels: Bilbo's blinding flash sends the goblins "running back yelping" into the dark. The word strips the enemy of menace by giving them the vocal behaviour of frightened dogs, making the rout comically undignified. The register is observational, slightly derisive, the language of the kennel applied to goblins.

Etymological and historical note. The modern sense is the end-point of a long descent. The Old English ancestor gielpan meant "to boast, to exult, to glory in oneself," a proud, self-regarding cry of human triumph, closer to bragging than to pain. Only gradually, across Middle English and the early modern period, did the word shed its boastful sense and settle on the sharp, high-pitched cry of animals and of humans reduced to an animal register. The word has literally fallen, in both register and meaning, from the speech of praise to the mere noise of the creature.

  • Sense 1 (core modern sense, of animals): "to utter a short, sharp, high-pitched cry, especially of pain, alarm, or distress," of dogs, puppies, wolves, and other animals: "the dog yelped when its tail was trodden on." This is the dominant and default sense.
  • Sense 2 (of persons): "to cry out suddenly in a sharp, high-pitched voice from pain or surprise": "he yelped as the nettles stung his hand." Applied to humans the word is deliberately belittling, placing the speaker in the register of the kennel, the farmyard, or the nursery.
  • Sense 3 (noun): "a short, sharp, high-pitched cry": "a yelp of pain", "a yelp of triumph." The noun derives from the verb and names the single cry; the present participle names the repeated uttering of them.
  • Sense 4 (obsolete and historical, the boast): "to boast, to exult, to speak proudly," the original Old English sense, gielpan, extinct by the modern period, preserved in the poetic record including Beowulf, and essential to understanding the word's trajectory.
  • Sense 5 (figurative and onomatopoeic extensions): by extension, of any short, sharp, high-pitched sound that mimics the cry, a yelping radio, a yelping laugh, the yelp of a hinge; and, informally, of a sharp complaint or protest, a political yelp of protest, preserving the idea of a sudden, brief, public cry.

Register and stylistic bridge. The participle yelping is observational and deflationary: it describes what a creature sounds like in pain or terror, and to apply it to any creature, human or goblin, is to compare it to a dog. Its historical bridge runs from pride to pain: the boastful gielpan of Old English, the loud shouting of Middle English, the dog's cry of the seventeenth century onward, a word that descended the social ladder from the speech of praise to the mere noise of the creature, and is now so firmly tied to the animal cry that its boastful past is nearly forgotten. Onomatopoeia and semantics conspired: the sharp, yelping sound of the modern cry helped the word shed its old boastful meaning, because a word that now sounded like a dog's cry could no longer plausibly name a hero's boasting.
