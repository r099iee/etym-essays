The biography of "mule" is the biography of a hybrid that arrived in Europe together with the animal itself, and the word is one of the clearest cases of a cultural loan in the Indo-European lexicon. Latin mūlus, attested already in Plautus in the early second century BCE, has no transparent Indo-European ancestry: most scholars treat it as a loanword from a pre-Roman Mediterranean or Aegean substrate, a word that entered Latin together with the practice of breeding the sterile jack × mare cross that the Italic peoples learned from their neighbors. Because the mule itself was a domestic innovation of the eastern Mediterranean, its name traveled as a cultural artifact, and nothing in Sanskrit, Greek, Germanic, Celtic, or Balto-Slavic offers a secure PIE etymon. (A connection with Greek mýklos, "lewd beast; mule," is sometimes proposed but remains uncertain.)

Spread and sound change. From Latin the word radiated in two directions.

  • Northward into Germanic. The beast of burden was known to the Anglo-Saxons by the eighth century, and Old English borrowed mūl "mule" directly from Latin — one thread of the modern form. The long back vowel survives in modern /mjuːl/.
  • West and south into Romance. Old French produced mul and mule, Italian mulo, Spanish and Portuguese mulo/mula, Romanian mul — all direct descendants of Latin mūlus; modern French reformed the word as mulet with a diminutive suffix, and from muletier English borrowed muleteer in the 18th century.
  • The Norman channel. It was from Old French and Anglo-Norman that Middle English re-borrowed mule around 1300, and that Norman line is the direct ancestor of the word McCarthy uses.
  • Germanic compounds. The Germanic dialects that lacked a simple noun built analytic compounds — German Maulesel ("mule-ass") and Maultier ("mule-animal"), Dutch muildier, Danish muldyr — testimony that the mule was an import conceptually as well as linguistically.

Semantic career and socio-cultural role. The word's meanings follow the animal's role through history.

  • Medieval workhorse. From the later Middle Ages the mule was the great pack animal of pilgrims, armies, and trade routes; the vocabulary around it grew accordingly — muleteer, mulet (a young mule).
  • The American frontier. In the nineteenth century the mule became the freight machinery of the West: mule trains, pack mules, and mule skinners hauled supplies across Texas, the gold fields (including the 1849 rush in which the passage is set), and the wagon roads. The animal was, in effect, the industrial engine of westward expansion.
  • Character transfer. Out of the animal's notorious refusal to be moved came mulish (1570s), transferring obstinacy fused with laboring power onto human temperament — "stubborn as a mule," "mule-headed."
  • Literary use. Writers have always used the mule to deflate equestrian glamour; McCarthy's "aged mule" accepted as wages is the endpoint of that tradition, a spent machine of the very traffic that built the West.

False friends and modern extensions.

  • The fashionable mule (a backless slipper) is a separate word from Latin mulleus, the red-dyed shoe of Roman patrician magistrates — not a cognate of the animal.
  • The student verb to mule ("to cheat on an exam") is a nineteenth-century American slang coinage of its own.
  • The modern criminal "mule" (a drug courier) and the "spinning mule" (Crompton's hybrid textile machine, 1779) are fresh metaphors that revive the animal's hybrid, load-carrying identity.

In the passage the noun sits at the very bottom of the register of mounts: payment in kind, an exhausted biological tool, and the plainness of the word does the work. The mule is a loan, a hybrid, a word with no homeland — exactly matching the creature it names.

LATTERDAY — a marriage of two independent PIE lineages that met only in modern English; each half deserves its own biography.

Latter. The word begins with the PIE root *lē- (also cited as *lēd-), "to let go, slacken, grow weary," a root whose semantic energy in other branches went toward tiredness: Greek lēdeîn, "to be weary" (glossed in Hesychius), Latin lassus, "weary" (whence English lassitude), and Lithuanian lėtas, "slow, calm." In Germanic the same "slackening" took a temporal turn: Proto-Germanic *lataz, "slow, lazy," yielded Old English læt, "slow, late," and its comparative lætra produced latter, "the later or second of two," with an exact Gothic match in laþiza. The drift is from physical slackness to lateness: what is loose, released, dragging becomes what comes late, and what comes late comes at the end. By Middle English, lattere is fixed as "the second of two; the last-mentioned," and modern latter still carries that strict comparative force.

Day. English day descends from the PIE root *dʰegʷʰ-, "to burn": Proto-Germanic *dagaz names the day as the burning or hot time of the sun — the equation is standard but not beyond dispute, as the OED notes. The same root flames on in Vedic Sanskrit dáhati, "burns," and dāha, "burning heat" (whence Hindi and Odia dāh, "burning, pyre"), Lithuanian dègti, "to burn," and Old Irish daig, "fire." The Germanic *dagaz became Old English dæġ, Middle English day, and modern day. Crucially, Germanic "day" and Latin diēs are not cognates — Latin's day comes from *dyew-, the sky — so the Romance day-words (jour, giorno) are properly kept out of this family.

The compounding and its fortunes. The two words met in nineteenth-century English.

  • The adjective latter-day is attested from the 1840s in the general sense "modern, recent, of a later period."
  • Its most famous fixed use is Latter-day Saints, the name adopted by the Mormon church in 1830 to claim the final dispensation of history.
  • The eschatological aura of "the latter days" — the closing era of the world in biblical prophecy — is built into the compound, and that is exactly what McCarthy turns to irony.
  • Journalism then made the productive formula "a latter-day X": a present figure standing at the close of a longer story, a diminished reincarnation of something greater — a latter-day Robin Hood, a latter-day Caesar.

In the passage. Fredonia was a genuinely short-lived breakaway state proclaimed at Nacogdoches in 1826–27; by the time the Reverend rides through in 1849 it is already a memory, a republic that came late in the historical sequence and could not last. Calling it "latterday" does two things at once: it dates it as recent — a product of the current age — and it condemns it as terminal, a conclusion rather than a beginning. The word's low comic grandeur is the compound's gift: solemn eschatology shrunk to the size of a frontier swindle, and the worn epithet "latterday" carries the whole deflation.
