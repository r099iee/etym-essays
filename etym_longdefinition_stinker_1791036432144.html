stinker is a versatile informal noun built on stink + -er, whose literal sense — something or someone that stinks, emitting a foul smell (a stinker of a cheese, drains, cigar) — has ramified into a family of figurative insults and complaints. In the Wodehouse source, to come a stinker means for love's frail craft to suffer a foul-up, crash, nasty turn, or rotten outcome — a stinking mess, a bad failure. The speaker laments that romance has gone rotten when a few manly words of contrition might have saved it. The tone is rueful, upper-class, and comic-euphemistic: a coarse smell-word softened into gentlemanly slang.

As a personal insult, stinker means a rotten, contemptible, mean, or treacherous person — a cad, rotter, swine in mild form. Bertie Wooster's world is full of stinkers: rivals, aunts, moneylenders, and former friends who let one down. The force is moral-olfactory: bad character is figured as bad smell, an ancient metaphor (something fishy, rotten to the core, stinking conduct). Unlike squirt, which belittles size, stinker condemns quality — secret meanness, betrayal, or surprising nastiness. It can be affectionately grumbled (you old stinker) or genuinely venomous (lying stinker), with intonation deciding.

Beyond persons, stinker rates anything foul, hard, or failed. A stinker of an exam, problem, cold, hangover, review, or day is one that stinks in the sense of being nasty, unfairly hard, or rottenly unpleasant. Theatrical and sporting slang adds the flop sense: a play, film, or match that is a stinker has bombed, stunk up the joint. American and British school slang converge here: a real stinker of a question is the one that sinks everyone.

  • Primary literal sense: that which emits a stink — stinker of a pipe, drains, factory farm. Still current but less frequent than figurative uses; often with of: a stinker of a cigar.
  • Personal insult sense: a despicable, untrustworthy, mean person; a rotter, cad, swine-lite. E.g. selfish stinker, sneaky stinker, poor stinker (the last sympathetic: someone in a stinking situation).
  • Event / failure sense (source sense): a nasty turn, foul-up, fiasco — to come a stinker / come a cropper region. Love comes a stinker = romance crashes foully. Bridge: failure figured as a bad smell that lingers.
  • Difficulty sense: a hard problem or ordeal — a stinker of an exam question, a stinker of a cold. The unpleasantness of smell maps to unpleasantness of effort or suffering.
  • Domain usages: school/university (stinker of a test); theatre/film reviews (a stinker = flop); sport (a stinker of a match, decision); workplace (a stinker of a deal, commute); Wodehousian society comedy (moral stinkers vs comic stinkers).
  • Collocations and register: informal, slangy, chiefly British but widely understood in America. Patterns: dirty / rotten / sneaky / poor / lucky stinker; a real stinker; what a stinker; to pull / come a stinker. Softer than bastard, swine, shit; ruder than rotter, bounder in some ears, milder in others. Playful when said with a grin.
  • Grammar: countable noun; attributive in compounds like stinkerette (rare, jocular). Verb base stink, adjective stinking as intensifier (stinking rich, stinking cold) shares the evaluative flip.

Mastering stinker means hearing smell as judgement. English constantly moralises odour — sweet deal, fishy story, rotten behaviour, stinking attitude — and stinker is the noun that bottles that equation: what smells bad is bad, what behaves badly smells. In the source, love itself does not smell; its wreck does — a stinker of an outcome that offends the nose of romance.
