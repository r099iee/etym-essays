• Futile: Similar because both denote effort or speculation that produces no result; differs because futile stresses failure to attain an end and is more technical, close to 'vain', while idle also covers inactivity, unemployment, and physical emptiness of occupation.
  • Vain: Similar because both mean empty of effect or result (a vain effort, an idle threat); differs because vain also carries a separate sense of excessive pride in appearance, and stresses emptiness of outcome, while idle stresses emptiness of foundation and of occupation.
  • Fruitless: Similar because both mean producing no result; differs because fruitless is a vivid, concrete agricultural metaphor (a ground that yields no crop), while idle is more abstract and ranges over claims, machines, and persons as well as efforts.
  • Pointless: Similar because both mean lacking purpose or effect; differs because pointless is casual and colloquial, focused on absence of point or goal, while idle is older, more formal, and rooted in the physical idea of emptiness.
  • Lazy / Indolent: Similar because both describe a person not working; differ because lazy and indolent judge character and habit, implying disinclination to effort, while idle may be purely descriptive of temporary inactivity (an idle machine, an idle worker) without moral judgement.
  • Inactive / Dormant: Similar because both mean not currently active or in use; differ because inactive and dormant are neutral, technical terms (a dormant volcano, an inactive account), while idle carries the older connotation of emptiness and sometimes of wasted capacity.
  • Unemployed: Similar because both describe a person without work; differs because unemployed is the modern, neutral, economic term for a jobless person, while idle is broader (machines, time, claims, threats) and carries older moral and literary overtones.
  • Empty / Hollow (etymological kin in sense): Similar because idle originally meant 'empty' and still means 'empty of foundation or value'; differ because empty and hollow are physical and fully general, while idle has been specialised to the abstract senses of groundlessness, futility, and inactivity.
