Sprang is the past tense of the irregular (strong) verb spring, one of the most richly polysemous verbs in English. Because it is a past tense, its exact meaning is inherited from spring, and it can express any of the verb's senses as a completed action in the past. Tolkien's sentence — "the waters of the Running River sprang" — uses the sense "to issue forth, to burst or flow out," describing water bursting from the mountain as a spring bursts from the ground. The choice of a strong, monosyllabic past tense suits the elevated, mythic register of the passage.

The senses of spring, and therefore of sprang.

  • To leap, jump, or move suddenly: the oldest sense. "The cat sprang onto the table," "he sprang to his feet" (stood up suddenly), "to spring forward/back."
  • To burst forth, to issue suddenly: "the water sprang from the rock," "the flame sprang up," "she sprang to mind" (came suddenly into consciousness). This is the sense in the card.
  • To come into being, to arise, to originate: "the custom sprang up in the Middle Ages," "the idea sprang from an old letter," "to spring from a family" (to be descended from). In this sense spring is followed by from, out of, or up.
  • To appear suddenly: "wherever he went, hotels sprang up," "the truth suddenly sprang into view."
  • Of plants: to grow, shoot up, or sprout — the sense behind the noun spring as the season of growth ("the grass sprang again").
  • To develop or grow rapidly: "his business sprang from a stall to a chain."
  • Mechanical senses: to activate a trap or release a mechanism ("to spring a trap," "to spring a lock"), and of a coiled device to release ("the spring sprang back").
  • Of wood or material: to warp, crack, or split ("the door sprang in the damp").
  • Nautical sense: of a vessel, to swing or be turned about by a rope or the tide.
  • To pay for / treat (informal): "to spring for" dinner (20th-century American), or "to spring" someone out of prison (to free suddenly).

Grammatical character. Spring is a class 3 strong verb in English, with the ablaut pattern spring – sprang – sprung: the past participle is sprung (used with have/had). The past tense sprang descends directly from Old English sprang, the singular past of springan, and has remained the standard literary past tense for over a thousand years, although in some American dialects sprung is used colloquially for the past ("he sprung up") — a form regarded as nonstandard in careful writing. The form sprang also appears in such fixed collocations as "sprang to life," "sprang to mind," and "sprang into action."

Register and nuance. As a monosyllabic strong past tense, sprang is vivid, abrupt, and emphatic: it names a sudden, energetic, or surprising event. In literary prose it is used of leaps, outbursts, origins, and revelations, and it is particularly at home in fantasy and adventure narrative (as in The Hobbit), where sudden motion and sudden emergence are constantly in demand. It is also the standard past tense of several common idioms — "sprang to attention," "sprang to her defence," "sprang a surprise on them" — and so remains very much alive in ordinary English. A speaker who chooses sprang over the simple jumped or came is choosing force and suddenness, and usually a hint of the elemental: water that "sprang" from rock is water that was there, hidden, and then was not, and then was everywhere.
