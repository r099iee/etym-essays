In Pater's aesthetic theory, adjuncts are the non-essential, secondary, or accidental accompaniments that surround a work of art or a beautiful personality—historical circumstance, medium constraints, biographical chatter, moral afterthoughts, or any outer husk that is joined to the core impression without being that impression. The critic's function is to 'distinguish, analyse, and separate from its adjuncts' the pure virtue by which a picture, landscape, or person produces a special pleasure. The tone is analytical and almost chemical: adjuncts are real and often interesting, but they are joined-on matter, not the element sought. Pater is not scorning context as worthless; he is ranking it as subordinate to the unique aesthetic property he wants isolated. Polysemy: the senses the word carries today.
  • Aesthetic-critical sense (Pater): the secondary, accidental qualities or circumstances joined to a beautiful object, to be stripped away so the essential virtue may be known.
  • General noun: anything added or joined to something else as supplementary rather than essential—'the luxurious furniture was a mere adjunct to the architecture.'
  • Grammatical sense: an adjunct is a word, phrase, or clause that modifies a sentence element without being grammatically obligatory (e.g., an adverb of time or place); a technical term in linguistics and grammar.
  • Academic rank: 'adjunct professor'—faculty appointed on a non-tenure, attached-but-not-core basis; the word names a structurally subordinate position.
  • Adjective use: 'adjunct duties,' 'adjunct services'—helping, supplementary, added-on.
  • Collocations of power and ceremony: 'adjuncts of power,' 'adjuncts of ceremony,' 'mere adjuncts'—the trappings that surround a ruler or ritual.
  • Philosophical resonance: the essence-versus-accident distinction; adjuncts are the accidents, the joined-on properties that are not constitutive.
  • Historical Latinism: from Latin adiungere 'to join to,' the word entered English in the late sixteenth century as a learned borrowing for 'something joined on.'
Semantic boundaries with near-synonyms:
  • Accessory: likewise secondary, but often optional equipment chosen for use or style; adjunct is more structural or scholarly.
  • Appendage: stresses physical attachment and can sound slightly pejorative or grotesque; adjunct is cooler and more Latinate.
  • Supplement: adds what was missing to complete a whole; an adjunct may never have been needed for completeness.
  • Attribute: can be an essential property (in logic or philosophy); Pater's adjuncts are precisely not the essential virtue.
  • Circumstance: a surrounding condition; adjuncts include circumstances but also any joined secondary quality the critic must peel away.
  • Accompaniment: something that goes along with another thing; accompaniment is neutral and pleasant, while adjunct implies attached-but-inessential status.
Collocations: 'adjunct to' (something), 'mere adjuncts,' 'adjunct professor,' 'adjuncts of power/ceremony,' 'strip away the adjuncts.' Register is formal, academic, and critical; the plural form in aesthetic prose marks a technical vocabulary of essence versus accident. Historical bridge: the Latin compound adiungere, 'to join to' (ad- 'to' + iungere 'to join'), produced the past participle adjunctus and the noun adjunctum 'a thing joined on'; from the physical yoking of animals the root extended to abstract attachment and to the ranked idea of the secondary—the grammarian's optional modifier and the critic's detachable husk both descend from that same act of joining.
