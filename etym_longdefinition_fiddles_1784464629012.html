Fiddles (plural of fiddle) are bowed string instruments of the violin family—physically the same four-string, hourglass-bodied design as the classical violin, but named fiddle when the register is folk, traditional, dance, or informal rather than concert-classical. The distinction is cultural and stylistic—repertoire, bow hold, and often a flatter bridge for double-stops—not a different species of instrument. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, Kili and Fili rush for their bags and bring back ‘little fiddles’: portable folk-scale instruments that fit a travelling dwarf's pack, ready to join flutes, drum, and clarinets. Tone: homely-musical, slightly rustic or traditional beside later ‘viols’ and Thorin's harp—the younger dwarves' practical music gear, not courtly heirlooms alone.

Fiddle and violin. A fiddle and a violin are one instrument: four strings tuned in fifths, a hollow wooden body with a waisted outline, a fingerboard, and a horsehair bow. The name chosen signals social and musical context. Violin is the Italianate concert word, used of classical technique, orchestras, and solo recitals; fiddle is the native, informal word, used of folk, dance, and traditional playing. Historically the same split ran between courtly and tavern music, and it hardened in the 19th–20th centuries into repertoire and technique: a fiddler often uses a different bow hold and a flatter bridge to play double-stops and driving dance rhythms.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. A viol (elsewhere in the chapter) is the older fretted Renaissance consort family with a flat back and a different hold, not a synonym for the violin-shaped fiddle. A viola is the larger alto instrument of the same family. A cello is the large bass-voice instrument held between the knees. A lyre or harp is plucked, not bowed. Fiddles here keeps bowed folk/traditional strings, plural and ‘little’—the word the text uses for the sudden jam in a hobbit-hole before the deeper song begins.

Polysemy and modern usages. The word's semantic range, past and present, includes the following:

  • The instrument (primary sense, here plural): a violin, especially played in folk, traditional, or informal style. The sense fixed by the context.
  • To fiddle (verb): to play the fiddle; by extension, to fidget, toy, or tinker with something—‘fiddle with a knob’, ‘stop fiddling’—from the rapid fidgety movement of a fiddler's hands.
  • To fiddle = to cheat or falsify: British informal, ‘to fiddle the books’ or ‘to fiddle one's expenses’, i.e. to manipulate accounts dishonestly; the semantic bridge runs from quick, small, deceptive hand-movements to petty fraud.
  • Idioms: ‘fit as a fiddle’ (in excellent health), ‘to play second fiddle’ (to take a subordinate role, from orchestral hierarchy), ‘fiddle-faddle’ (trivial nonsense, a reduplication of the old sound-word), ‘fiddlesticks’ (an interjection of dismissal).
  • Financial jargon: a ‘fiddle’ is a fraud or a piece of dishonest accounting—‘a tax fiddle’.

Historical bridge. The semantic reasoning runs: rapid, fidgety movement of the bowing hand → restless hand movements and tinkering → minor swindling and false accounting; and the instrument's place at the foot of the orchestral hierarchy → ‘second fiddle’ for any subordinate role. The plural fiddles is ordinary inventory language: several instruments, as in a band.
