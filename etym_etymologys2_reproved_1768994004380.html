ROOT WORD: reproved — the past tense and past participle of the verb to reprove, “to reprimand, censure; (archaic) to disprove, refute.” Single root: a direct, well-attested linear chain.
Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE): the roots *pro- “before, in front, forward” and *bʰuH- “to become, to be” combine as the compound *pro-bʰw-o-s, literally “being in front, prominent, standing out,” which underlies Proto-Italic *proβwos “good, upright.”
Stage 2 — Latin probus “good, upright, honest, virtuous” (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE; Plautus, Terence): from Proto-Italic *proβwos, itself the reflex of the PIE compound. The same Latin word gives English probity and prove’s whole family.
Stage 3 — Latin probāre “to test, examine, prove; to approve” (attested 1st c. BCE; Cicero, Caesar): a denominative verb built on probus with the verbal suffix -āre; the source of English prove, proof, probation, approbation.
Stage 4 — Latin reprobāre “to reject, condemn, disapprove; to prove to be false” (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE): re- “back, against” + probāre, literally “to prove against, to test and find wanting.” The past participle reprobātus gives English reprobate.
Stage 5 — Old French / Anglo-Norman reprover “to accuse, blame, censure” (attested c. 11th–13th c.): inherited from Latin reprobāre with the intervocalic -b- softening toward -v-.
Stage 6 — Middle English repreven / reproven “to rebuke, censure; to disprove, refute” (attested c. 1300; Chaucer’s contemporaries): borrowed from Anglo-Norman, entering English with both the rebuking and the disproving senses.
Stage 7 — Modern English reprove (16th c.) and its past tense / past participle REPROVED (the form on this card): the “disprove, refute” sense recedes to archaic and theological usage; the censuring sense becomes the standard one.
Notes on debate and nuance: The derivation of Latin probus from PIE *pro-bʰw-o-s is the standard account (accepted by de Vaan) but depends on a reconstructed compound. English reprove reaches the language via Old French from Latin reprobāre, yet its spelling in -v- was reinforced by the native verb prove (from Old French prover, from Latin probāre) — a case of two French descendants of one Latin root merging in spelling. The “disprove/refute” sense, dominant in Middle English and the Renaissance, is now archaic and survives mainly in theology and the phrase “reprove error.” The Latin prefix re- functions here as “back, against” rather than simply “again,” though the “again” force (“prove once more”) is also present in the tradition.”
