A ladle is a large, long-handled spoon with a deep cup-shaped bowl, used for drawing liquids — soup, stew, punch, sauce — out of a large vessel and serving them. Its defining anatomy sets it apart from every other spoon: a handle long enough to reach the bottom of a pot without burning the hand, and a bowl deep and rounded enough to hold a generous portion of liquid while it is carried from vessel to plate. The word names the great serving implement of the communal meal, the utensil that stands between the pot and the table, and it has been a fixture of the English kitchen in essentially this form for well over a thousand years.

The word is genuinely polysemous, and its senses radiate outward from that central picture of drawing and distributing liquid:

  • The kitchen serving utensil (core sense): a large spoon, commonly of metal or wood, for transferring soup, broth, stew, gravy, punch, or mulled wine from a pot, tureen, or bowl into individual portions. This is the everyday sense, and it is the sense carried by the noun in almost all modern usage.
  • The ritual and ceremonial ladle: in archaeology and in older domestic and religious practice, the same shape appears as a great bronze or silver ladle used for offering wine and broth in ancestral and sacrificial rites. In the Dream of the Red Chamber passage the bronze ladle of Bao-yu's study belongs to this hieratic register — it is the descendant of the Shang and Zhou ritual ladles, a ceremonial object rather than a kitchen tool, and its bronze body signals permanence, status, and continuity.
  • The verb to ladle: to serve or distribute with a ladle, and by extension to pour out or distribute anything in a free, generous, or often unmeasured way. One ladles soup into bowls; one "ladles out" praise, blame, information, or propaganda — the abstract object is dispensed as generously and mechanically as liquid from a pot.
  • Ladle out (idiomatic and figurative): to hand out something — usually something plentiful or overdone — in large quantities, e.g. "he ladled out advice by the bucketful." The image is of generous, almost indiscriminate distribution.
  • The water-drawing implement: in older English, and in the history of the word family (OE hladan "to draw up water"), a ladle was also a scoop or vessel for drawing water out of a well, barrel, or boat — the sense preserved in the Old English gloss of Latin antlia.
  • Regional and technical uses: in some dialects and trades the word names any large spoon or scoop of the general shape; in metallurgy and crafts a ladle is a long-handled vessel for pouring molten metal, and a foundry ladle transfers hot metal from furnace to mould — the sense that most starkly keeps the ancient image of drawing a heavy fluid out of a burning vessel.

In this scene the ladle is part of the careful inventory of Bao-yu's study: "On the left-hand one was a small, square, four-legged ding, together with a bronze ladle, metal chopsticks, and an incense container." Everything here is antique and ceremonial — the ding is a ritual bronze vessel of archaic Chinese type, the chopsticks are of metal, the incense container completes the sacrificial triad — and the bronze ladle is not a kitchen tool but a ritual implement. The nuance of the word here is material and hieratic: the ladle is the practical link between storage and service, the object that "loads" a substance from the vessel into the moment of offering, and its bronze body signals permanence, status, and continuity rather than mere utility.
