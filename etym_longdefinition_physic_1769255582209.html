Physic (noun; also verb): the art or practice of healing — medical science — and, historically, a medicine, potion, or especially a cathartic (purgative) taken to restore health.

1. Primary senses — the healing art and the medicine

  • The art of healing: physic is the science and practice of medicine — the knowledge and treatment of bodily illness. In the source context, "Law, Physic, Alchemy, and Astronomy" are the subjects of a princely education, where Physic names the medical art (Greek-derived, deliberately archaic).
  • A medicine or dose: "to take one's physic" — a medicinal preparation, potion, or purge. In the older pharmacopoeia physic was often specifically a cathartic or laxative draught.
Key features:
  • Learned register: "a doctor of physic" was the standard early-modern title of a qualified physician.
  • Archaic/historical in modern English: physic has been largely displaced by medicine; it survives in period prose, in fixed phrases, and as a deliberate literary archaism.
  • Verb (rare, transitive): "to physic someone" — to dose with medicine; "to physic oneself" — to take a purge.

2. Related words in the same family

  • physician — the practitioner of physic;
  • physical — "of the body," and "of nature" (from Greek physikos, "natural");
  • physique — the bodily frame and build (re-borrowed from French in the nineteenth century);
  • physics — the modern science of matter and energy — the same Greek word physikē, rebracketed as a distinct noun (see below);
  • physiology — the study of the body's functions; physiognomy — reading character from the face; both from the same physis root.

3. Polysemy — the great split between physic and physics

Greek physis means "nature" — the growing, living constitution of things. The science of nature was physikē (epistēmē), "the knowledge of nature." In the medieval learned world, physic was wonderfully broad: it meant natural philosophy in general and, by natural narrowing, the medical art in particular, since medicine was the branch of natural knowledge most concerned with the human body. In the eighteenth century the scientific sense was re-imported in the form physics to name the new science of matter and energy, splitting the once-unified word in two:
  • physic (medical senses only) — the archaic word on this card;
  • physics (the science of nature) — the modern technical term.
Semantic bridge and reasoning: both descend from physis, "nature," because for the Greeks medicine and natural philosophy were one study of the living, growing world; the split happened in the eighteenth century, leaving physic stranded with the healing art and the dose.

4. Register, collocations, and usage notes

  • Classic collocations: "doctor of physic," "to take one's physic," "physic garden" (a garden of medicinal plants), "Physic" as a subject name (as in C. S. Lewis's list of Caspian's studies).
  • Register: archaic, formal, literary; deliberately antique in modern use — "laughter is the best physic" is a proverb-like modern extension meaning "the best medicine."
  • Etymology note: from Greek physis, "nature," through Latin physica and Old French fisique; not related only to the physical body, but to nature and growth at the root.

5. Polysemy summary

  • (1) The art of healing / medical science — primary historical sense.
  • (2) A medicine, potion, or cathartic dose — narrowed from the art to the preparation.
  • (3) Family member of physis — physician, physical, physique, physics, physiology.
  • The unifying bridge is nature: the healer and the physicist are both students of physis, the growing living world — a unity lost when physics split off in the 18th century.
