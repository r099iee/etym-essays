scrupulous (adjective) — diligent, thorough, and extremely attentive to details; very concerned to avoid doing wrong. A scrupulous person gives the closest possible attention to every small point while holding himself or herself to a strict moral standard, refusing to cut corners, overlook an error, or countenance a dubious action. The word is at once a badge of craftsmanship and a badge of conscience. Core meaning and how it behaves in sentences. The adjective attaches itself to two great realms of human care — the intellectual and the moral. In the intellectual realm it describes exact, painstaking workmanship: a scrupulous editor corrects every typo, scrupulous research verifies every citation, a scrupulous accountant reconciles every entry. In the moral realm it describes a conscience that will not rest easy: scrupulous honesty means refusing even a small lie, scrupulous fairness means giving every side its due. Because it unites the two realms, the word is a bridge between precision and principle — to be scrupulous about one's work is to treat accuracy as a matter of integrity. Primary senses:
  • Careful, exact, and thorough in attending to details — "scrupulous attention to detail," "a scrupulous examination of the accounts," "scrupulous cleanliness."
  • Strictly conscientious in moral matters — "scrupulous in keeping promises," "a scrupulous refusal to profit from another's loss," "scrupulous honesty."
  • Full of scruples; given to hesitation or anxiety about what is right — "a scrupulous conscience that weighs every motive," "a scrupulous fear of doing wrong."
Secondary and modern senses — the polysemy in full:
  • Over-scrupulous: when concern for correctness or propriety goes too far, the word takes on a gentle reproach. In the source passage Mrs. Bennet calls someone "over-_____," meaning almost too fastidious and morality-conscious — worrying a great deal about making the correct, respectable choice. This is the excess of the very virtue the base word names, and it shows the adjective sliding from pure praise into amused tolerance.
  • Professional jargon of quality: in law, medicine, science, and management the word denotes exacting, verifiable thoroughness — "scrupulous compliance," "scrupulous documentation," "scrupulous handling of evidence" — a technical register meaning total, audit-proof care.
  • The noun scruple: a qualm of conscience, a small doubt whether an action is right — "he had no scruples about lying." In the plural the noun names moral standards themselves ("a man of few scruples"), and the verb "to scruple" means to hesitate on moral grounds ("she did not scruple to deceive").
  • The apothecary's scruple (historical technical sense): in old pharmacy and coinage, a scruple was the smallest standard unit of weight — 1/24 of an ounce — showing the same root lending its name to minute precision in weighing medicine and precious metal. The phrase "not a scruple" once meant "not a grain, not a jot."
  • Metaphoric drift into modern speech: because the root literally means "a sharp little pebble," the word always carries a whisper of something small and sharp that makes you stop and check your step — hesitation, pricking, a stone in the shoe of the careless. That image still animates modern uses such as "scrupulously honest" (checking every step) and "without scruple" (trampling over the pebbles), and it is why the word feels both clinical and moral at once.
Historical semantic bridge. The chain runs from a literal stone to an abstract standard. Latin scrupus meant a sharp stone; its diminutive scrupulus was a tiny pebble that slipped into a Roman sandal and made walking painful — a traveller had to stop, empty his shoe, and examine his step. Roman moralists (Plautus, Cicero, Seneca) seized on the image: a nagging doubt is a pebble in the conscience, a small impediment that forces you to hesitate before proceeding. From "a small doubt that stops you" the word slid to "a small standard of exactness" (the apothecary's weight) and then to "the habit of respecting the small points" — which is precisely scrupulousness. The moral and the meticulous are one, because both are ways of refusing to step carelessly over the tiny things that the careless always miss.
