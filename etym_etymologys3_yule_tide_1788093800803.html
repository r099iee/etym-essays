1. The mystery of “Yule”

“Yule” is one of the oldest attested festival-words of Germanic, and one of the most opaque. It is confined to the Germanic branches — there is no agreed Indo-European etymon — and the reconstructed Proto-Germanic form is usually given as *jehwlą (with scholarly variants *jehwla-, *jegwla-, *jehulą). The origin is genuinely unknown: proposals include a connection to Latin iocus “jest, sport” via a speculative PIE root *yek- “to speak”, and a link to the month-name system of the old Germanic year; the OED and modern handbooks prudently mark the etymology as uncertain. What is certain is the festival’s antiquity and its fixity at the winter solstice: by the time the Germanic peoples appear in the written record, the word already names the turning-point feast of the year.

2. The Gothic witness

The oldest explicit attestation is Gothic jiuleis, a month-name in the Gothic calendar preserved in the Codex Ambrosianus (4th–6th century CE): the compound months fruma jiuleis “first Yule-month” and aftuma jiuleis “second Yule-month” divide the boundary between the old year and the new around November–December. The fact that a festival name could name whole calendar months proves how central the feast was to East Germanic life by the first millennium.

3. The Old English record and Bede

Old English had geōl / geōla (“Yule, Christmas”), and, as in Gothic, the name governed the calendar: Bede, in his De temporum ratione (c. 725), records the old English year in which the months we call December and January were ǣrra geōla (“former Yule”) and æftera geōla (“latter Yule”), given in Latin dress as Giuli. With Christianisation, geōl simply transferred to the new festival: “Yule” became the English word for Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and the Christmas season, while the pre-Christian midwinter rites — the great log, the boar, the fires, the drinking — attached themselves, often uneasily, to the Christian feast.

4. Old Norse and the great feast

The Old Norse plural jól names the greatest feast of the year: a twelve-day midwinter celebration involving sacrifice (jólablót), heavy drinking (jóladrykkja), feasting, and the renewal of oaths and peace — the midwinter blot offered “til árs” (“for a good year”), i.e. for the harvest and prosperity to come. Snorri Sturluson and the kings’ sagas describe it in detail, and the Christian kings of Norway consciously operated with it: as Hákonar saga góða relates, Hákon the Good (10th century) set Yule at the same time as the Christian Christmas and enacted the law that every householder must brew ale and keep Yule holy for as long as the ale lasted. The festival thus survived inside the Christian calendar with its name and its drinking untouched. The Scandinavian and English branches mutually confirm the antiquity and the solstice-fixity of the feast.

5. Middle English: yole, yule, and the making of “Yule-tide”

Middle English yole / yule continues the word: the change from g-spelling to y-spelling reflects the palatal pronunciation of the initial consonant (geōl → yōl → yule), with the late-medieval vowel shift giving the modern /juːl/. English began early to compound the festival-word with tīd “time, season”: alongside “Yule-day” (Christmas Day) and the church-derived “Christmastide” we find Yule-tide, used in English from the medieval–early modern period and established in print by the early modern era. The -tide element itself descends from PIE *dā-/*dī- “to divide, allot” → Proto-Germanic *tīdiz “time” → Old English tīd “time, season, hour, festival” — time, in the old Germanic view, being the divided, allotted portion of the year; the same family yields German Zeit “time” and English time itself.

6. Early modern lows and the Puritan suppression

After the Reformation, “Yule” retreated in educated southern English into dialect and the north (it remained standard in Scots), partly because the Puritan party attacked the whole Christmas festival as a heathen-papist corruption: Parliament’s ban on Christmas celebrations in 1647–60 made “Yule” and its revels politically suspect. The word survived in proverbs, carols, the countryside and the “Yule log” — the great block dragged in on Christmas Eve, still called the “Yule clog” in northern dialect into the nineteenth century.

7. The Victorian revival and modern “Yuletide”

The nineteenth-century reinvention of Christmas as a family and gift-giving festival resurrected the old word: “Yuletide” and “Yule-tide” flooded the new Christmas cards, carols and annuals, and “Yule” reappeared in revived seasonal customs (Yule log, Yule candle, Yule songs). In the twentieth century “Yuletide” became the standard festive-season word of advertising and greeting-card English, while “Yule-tide” with the hyphen remained the consciously antique spelling. Tolkien, steeped in the old north, chose the hyphenated form for the close of The Hobbit (1937) — “Yule-tide was warm and merry there” in Beorn’s hall — and neopagan and historical revivals have since reclaimed “Yule” for the solstice festival itself.

8. The drift in one sweep

  • Pagan solstice feast (Germanic antiquity; the Gothic month-name; the Old Norse twelve-day feast).
  • Christianisation: Yule = Christmas; the English year-month names; Bede’s Giuli.
  • Reformation and Puritan era: demoted to dialect, Scots and the countryside; briefly outlawed with Christmas itself.
  • Victorian revival: “Yuletide” as the warm, nostalgic face of the new Christmas — cards, carols, log-burning.
  • Today: “Yuletide” is the standard festive word; “Yule” is reclaimed by neopagans; “Yule-tide” with the hyphen is the archaic, literary and Tolkienian form.
