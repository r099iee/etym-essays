- [16th century: the literal sense] - Coined as benight ('to overtake or envelop with the darkness of night'), 'benighted' first meant physically caught out of doors after nightfall — stranded, lost, or exposed to peril on a journey, with night as an active agent.
- [17th–18th century: the figurative leap] - The word extended from darkness of night to darkness of unreason: 'benighted' heathens, pagans, and minds were those plunged into moral or spiritual ignorance, and the word took on a pitying, condescending warmth in missionary and Enlightenment prose.
- [19th century: the peak of the metaphor] - 'Benighted' became the standard epithet for ages, peoples, and places deemed backward or unenlightened; its tone hardened toward smug superiority even while it retained an undertone of tragic helplessness.
- [Contemporary sense] - The literal sense is now largely archaic, and figurative use survives chiefly as literary, mock-archaic, or ironic colouring; today it reads as a consciously old-fashioned way of saying 'lost in the dark' or 'hopelessly ignorant'.
