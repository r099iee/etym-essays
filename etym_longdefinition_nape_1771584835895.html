NAPE is the noun for the back of the neck — the narrow, slightly hollowed strip of flesh, muscle and skin that runs from the base of the skull down to the top of the shoulders, flanked on each side by the trapezius and splenius muscles and fronted by the cervical spine. It is the region where the head joins the neck and where the lower edge of the hairline gathers; in hairdressing the "nape area" or "nape zone" is the short, fine hair that grows at the bottom of the skull and is routinely trimmed when a haircut is "cleaned up at the nape." In the passage from David Hawkes's translation of Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng (The Story of the Stone), the word anchors an opulent dressing scene: Bao-yu's braided hair is "fastened at intervals from the nape downwards with four enormous pearls and ending in a jewelled gold clasp." The nape is here the anatomical starting-point of a vertical cascade of ornament — the pearls descend "from the nape downwards" like a ladder of wealth — and at the same time a pivot of intimacy, for in the classical Chinese imagination the nape is where hair begins, where a neck curves, where a kiss may land. The word thus quietly imports physical delicacy and beauty into what might otherwise be a mere inventory of jewels, and from this single pivot hair, pearls, and gold all flow.

The core senses of NAPE are few but must be enumerated with care:
  • Anatomical sense: the back of the neck, from the occiput (the back of the skull) down to the shoulders — the standard, primary and overwhelmingly dominant sense in modern English.
  • Idiomatic full phrase: "nape of the neck," almost always used with "of the neck," so that "the nape" alone is elliptical for "the nape of the neck."
  • Hairdressing and barbering: the "nape," "nape line" or "nape area" is the lower hairline at the back of the head, and "nape-length" describes a haircut that ends at that point.
  • Technical anatomy: the nucha, the nuchal region — the scientific term for the nape that shadows the everyday word.
  • Zoological and informal sense: the "scruff" of an animal's neck is the loose skin at the nape by which mothers carry young and handlers grip dogs.
  • Figurative and literary sense: the nape as the site of vulnerability and tenderness — the exposed, unguarded place that a breeze strikes, a kiss lands, or a blade finds; to feel the nape prickle is to feel a shiver of apprehension or desire.
  • Metonymic use in clothing: "nape" names the back-neck point used in dressmaking and tailoring as a measuring landmark.

The word's semantic history is strikingly stable: from its first recorded appearance in Middle English (c. 1300) it has meant precisely the back of the neck, and it has never drifted from that single anatomical site. What has changed is not the denotation but the connotation and the register. In ordinary use "nape" is the neutral, precise noun for the back of the neck — the default term in medical, zoological and everyday description. But because the nape is an exposed and relatively defenceless part of the body, the word has accumulated a persistent charge of intimacy and vulnerability in literary and romantic prose: one feels a cold breeze on the nape, one kisses the nape of a neck, a villain's knife presses against a victim's nape. This dual character — neutral in the clinic, tender on the page — is the semantic bridge that explains how a plain anatomical noun can carry so much emotional weight, and it is exactly this reserve of tenderness that Hawkes's sentence taps when he strings the pearls "from the nape downwards."

Register distinctions among the neighbouring words sharpen the boundary of NAPE:
  • "Neck" names the whole cervical region and is the neutral anatomical default; "nape" selects only the back of it and adds a note of vulnerability.
  • "Scruff" is informal and used chiefly of animals, carrying a graceless, disciplinary charge that no ornate or romantic prose would tolerate.
  • "Nucha" is the technical anatomical term, from medieval Latin, confined to scientific writing.
  • "Occiput" names the back of the skull itself, not the neck, and would be anatomically wrong for this context.
  • "Cervix" is wholly anatomical and, in modern English, hopelessly ambiguous with the uterine cervix.
  • "Back of the neck" is a plain descriptive phrase, serviceable but flat, lacking the single precise noun the translator needs for rhythm.

In short, NAPE alone occupies a rare middle ground: precise enough for the anatomist, tender enough for the lover, and economical enough for the poet — which is exactly why Hawkes can build the whole image of pearls and gold upon a single word.
