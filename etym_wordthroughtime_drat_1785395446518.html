- [c. 1800: a disguised oath] - drat enters colloquial English as a clipped, softened “God rot (him)!” — an oath bleached of its religious force, a euphemism that gestures at damnation while committing to none; it is from birth an evasion, energetic on the surface, harmless beneath.
- [19th century: the comic stage] - the word becomes the stock exclamation of Victorian farce, the comic novel, and the music hall — folksy, dated, faintly theatrical irritation; “drat it!” is the vexation of the inconvenienced uncle or the blustering father, never real rage.
- [20th century: the quaintness sets] - as spoken profanity loosens, drat only grows more old-fashioned and literary; writers, Tolkien among them in 1937, use it for bluff, hearth-side, period grumbling, so that its datedness becomes its charm — a safe swear for bluff characters who mean nothing by it.
- [Contemporary sense] - drat survives as deliberately quaint mild irritation, euphemistic and comic — the phonetic ghost of a curse — confined to the historical novel, the period drama, and the self-consciously gentle swearer who vents feeling while keeping it deliberately small.
