Wrought is the archaic past tense and past participle of the verb work — the strong-form survivor of the Old English past worhte, meaning "worked, made, fashioned, produced, or caused." In the sentence "a small coat of mail, wrought for some young elf-prince long ago," the word means "made, crafted, fashioned": the mail-shirt was worked by a smith — hammered, linked and shaped — for a prince of the Elves. The word's whole atmosphere is deliberate: wrought is chosen because it sounds ancient and skilled, the past tense of a workshop where things are made by hand, with effort and art.

Primary senses.

  • Made, fashioned, crafted: "wrought of gold", "a sword wrought by the finest smith", "wrought with great skill" — the central sense; the object is made by working, usually by hand, and the word carries a charge of craftsmanship.
  • Worked (of materials): "wrought iron" — iron that has been shaped by hammering and working while hot, as opposed to cast iron poured into moulds; "wrought gold/silver" — metal worked into shape rather than merely mined or melted.
  • Produced, caused, brought about (literary): "the storm wrought havoc", "the disease wrought great changes", "time has wrought its damage" — to work in the sense of to effect or produce; this sense survives strongly in fixed phrases.
  • Worked, agitated (in wrought-up): "wrought up" means excited, overstimulated, tense — "she was wrought up about the exam" — the idea of being worked, stirred, wound tight like a spring.

Grammar and distribution.

  • Wrought is the old strong past participle of work; in Middle English it was the ordinary past form (wroghte, wrouȝt), but from the 16th century the weak form worked took over the everyday job, and wrought retreated into literary, biblical and technical registers.
  • It survives in fixed compounds and phrases: wrought iron, wrought-up, hand-wrought, "wrought havoc", "wrought changes", "wrought miracles", "what hath God wrought", "wrought with skill".
  • It is used attributively before nouns (a wrought-iron gate, a wrought silver cup) and predicatively in elevated prose (the cup was wrought of silver).
  • Pronunciation: the gh is silent; wrought rhymes with bought, thought, caught (/rɔːt/).

Domain and technical uses.

  • Metallurgy and engineering: "wrought iron" is the classic material of blacksmithing — fibrous, tough, corrosion-resistant iron shaped by hammering; it built Victorian bridges, anchors, and ornamental gates before steel replaced it; "wrought steel" and "wrought alloys" are still technical terms for worked (rolled, forged, extruded) rather than cast metal.
  • Craft and art: "wrought" describes objects of skilled handwork — wrought silver, wrought glass, wrought-ironwork — and in art history, any material "wrought" by the maker's hand.
  • Literary and biblical English: the King James Bible uses wrought scores of times ("what hath God wrought", "I have wrought a great work"); the phrase was famously echoed in the first Morse telegraph message (1844), and wrought remains the standard archaic participle in hymns and solemn prose.
  • Phrase-fossils: "wrought havoc", "wrought havoc on", "wrought changes" — in journalism and history writing, wrought is the participle of choice for large impersonal effects (war, time, weather) precisely because it sounds weighty and complete.

Secondary and figurative extensions.

  • Wrought-up: emotionally worked, agitated, tense — from the 19th century; the image is of a person wound up tight as a worked spring.
  • Overwrought: the adjective meaning (1) elaborately over-decorated (of art and writing) and (2) over-agitated (of people) — literally "over-worked", over-elaborated or over-excited; a modern and common word built from the same participle.
  • Wrought as "caused": "the flood wrought ruin on the valley" — work in its oldest causative sense of producing an effect; the semantic bridge is that making and causing are the same act seen from two sides (a smith works iron into a sword; the storm works ruin into a town).
  • Wrought as "done, performed": in scriptural style, "the works he wrought" — deeds performed; the bridge to "made" is that a deed is a work performed, a thing brought about by effort.

Why the word means all this. Wrought is simply the old past of work, and work in Old English already meant both "to make or fashion" (wyrcan a sword) and "to perform, do, cause" (wyrcan a deed, a miracle, damage). English lost the strong participle for everyday use, but kept it alive in exactly the contexts where making is serious: metalwork, scripture, and solemn description of cause and effect. Every modern sense — a wrought-iron gate, a wrought-up temper, havoc wrought by a storm — is the same Old English verb, preserved like a hammered blade: still sharp, still shaped by hand, and deliberately archaic.
