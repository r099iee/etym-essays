Justifie is a compound, so its etymology is two separate chains: the chain of the "just" element (Latin iūstus, from PIE *yewes-) and the chain of the "-fie" element (Latin -ficāre, from PIE *dʰeh₁-). Each is given in full below. CHAIN 1 — the "just" element: 1. PIE *yewes- ("law, rule, what is binding, right order, what is sound"; reconstructed; the source of Sanskrit yóḥ "health, welfare" in the Rigveda and Avestan yaoš "health, vigor," showing the root's original sense of sound, lawful, proper condition; c. 4500–3500 BCE) → 2. Proto-Italic *iowes- ("law, right"; the Italic reflex of *yewes-) → 3. Latin iūs (iūris, "law, right, justice, legal right"; attested from c. 3rd c. BCE; the basis of iūstus and iūstitia) → 4. Latin iūstus ("just, righteous, lawful, according to law"; formed with the adjectival suffix -tus; attested from the classical period, c. 1st c. BCE) → 5. Latin iūstificāre ("to make just, to justify, to declare righteous"; Christian Latin compound of iūstus + -ficāre, translating Greek dikaióō; attested from c. 2nd–3rd c. CE in Tertullian and the Vulgate) → 6. Old French justifier ("to justify, to make lawful"; attested from c. 1080 in the Chanson de Roland) → 7. Middle English justifien ("to make just, to declare righteous, to show to be right"; first attested c. 1350) → 8. Early Modern English justifie ("to show to be right, to declare righteous"; the -ie spelling of Milton's Paradise Lost, 1667; modern standard spelling justify). CHAIN 2 — the "-fie" (making) element: 1. PIE *dʰeh₁- ("to put, place, set, make"; one of the most productive roots of Indo-European; source of Greek títhēmi "I put" and thésis, Vedic Sanskrit dadhāti "he places" and dharma-, and the Germanic root of English do, did, done, deed; c. 4500–3500 BCE) → 2. Proto-Italic *fakiō ("to make, do"; the Italic reflex of *dʰeh₁- with an added *-k- extension) → 3. Latin faciō (facere, "I make, do"; attested from c. 3rd c. BCE) → 4. Latin combining form -ficāre ("to make [into]", the suffixal shape of facere in denominal and de-adjectival compounds such as iūstificāre "to make just," māgnificāre "to make great"; classical and Christian Latin) → 5. Old French -fier ("to make", the verb-forming suffix descended from -ficāre, as in justifier; attested from the 11th c.) → 6. English -fy (the productive English verb-forming suffix in justify, magnify, edify, simplify; the -ie spelling in justifie reflects 16th–17th c. orthography before -y was standardized). Debated / nuanced stages: (a) the PIE reconstruction *yewes- for "law" is standard, but its precise relation to the Indo-Iranian "health/welfare" words is nuanced — Latin iūs shows a semantic shift from "sound, lawful condition" to "law, right," and the match to Sanskrit yóḥ is morphological rather than word-for-word; (b) the Latin -tus in iūstus is a common adjectival suffix, and iūstus is uncontroversially built on iūs; (c) the exact date and authorship of iūstificāre are not directly recorded — it belongs to the Latin of Christian writers (attested in Tertullian and the Vulgate) rather than classical Latin; (d) the final vowel spelling -ie (justifie) vs -y (justify) is a purely orthographic, not etymological, difference reflecting early modern practice.
