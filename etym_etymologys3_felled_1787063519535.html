The historical evolution of felled showcases the enduring power of Proto-Germanic causative verb morphology, demonstrating how subtle grammatical suffixes created powerful lexical distinctions that have survived intact into modern English for over two millennia.

1. Indo-European Origins and the Causative Mechanism:

  • Proto-Indo-European Base: The verb ascends to the Proto-Indo-European root *pōl- / *pʰel- ("to fall, drop, slip"), which produced primary verbs denoting downward kinetic motion across the western branches of the Indo-European family (including Old Armenian p'ul, "collapse, fall").
  • Causative Derivation: In Proto-Germanic, the causative formation was constructed by adding the causative/factitive suffix *-janą to the o-grade stem of the strong root *fallaną ("to fall"). This yielded the weak causative verb *fallijaną, literally meaning "to make fall, cause to drop, or knock down".

2. Germanic Dialectal Proliferation and Old English Fyllan:

  • Pan-Germanic Concordance: The causative distinction was preserved across all Germanic languages: Old Saxon fellian, Old Frisian fella, Old High German fellen (modern German fällen, distinct from fallen), Old Norse fella (modern Swedish fälla), and Middle Dutch vellen.
  • Old English I-Mutation (Umlaut): In Anglo-Saxon England, the Proto-Germanic suffix *-janą triggered i-mutation of the root vowel /a/ to /e/ or /y/, producing Old English fellan / fyllan (Class I weak verb; past tense fylde / felde, past participle gefylled / gefelled). Anglo-Saxon scribes used fellan to record the clearing of primeval British forests as well as the heroic slaughter of warriors in battle poetry (e.g., in Beowulf: he his feond gefylde, "he felled his foe").

3. Middle English Levelling and Weak Regularization:

  • Vocalic Merging: During the Middle English period (c. 1150–1500), the verbal forms levelled into fellen, taking the past participle felled / y-felled. Crucially, Middle English speakers maintained the strict distinction between the intransitive strong verb fallen (past: fel / fell, participle: fallen) and the transitive weak verb fellen (past: felled, participle: felled).
  • Forestry and Chivalric Documentation: In medieval England, Royal Forest Law heavily regulated the felling of oak, ash, and beech. Court rolls from the 13th and 14th centuries frequently record legal penalties against peasants who had felled timber within the King's royal demesne without a forester's warrant.

4. Technical Specialization and Tolkien's Literary Woodland:

  • Early Modern Tailoring and Metaphor: By the 16th and 17th centuries, fell entered tailoring terminology for folding down seams. In medicine and drama, playwrights like Shakespeare utilized felled to evoke sudden catastrophe (e.g., Macduff in Macbeth: "What, all my pretty chickens and their dam / At one fell swoop?").
  • Tolkien's Attestation in The Hobbit (1937): In chapter 8 of The Hobbit, Tolkien describes Bilbo and the dwarves discovering an elusive clearing within Mirkwood: "where some trees had been felled and the ground levelled." Here, felled immediately indicates past intelligent cultivation and habitation within an otherwise wild, primordial forest, setting up the ethereal feast of the Wood-elves.
