Bowers is the regular plural of bower, whose story begins with being itself. PIE *bheu- meant to be, exist, grow, and by extension to dwell, being somewhere. It produced Sanskrit bhavati he becomes, Greek phyein to grow, Latin fui I was, English be, been, booth, build, boor, and Germanic *buanan to dwell with noun *buraz a dwelling. In Old English this gave bur, also spelled bour: an inner room, a lady chamber, a hut or cottage, any dwelt-in enclosure, as in Beowulf bride-bower passages and saints lives.

In Middle English bour and bower split along two paths: inward to a private chamber, especially a lady bower in romance, and outward to a leafy shady shelter woven of branches, an arbour. The latter dominates after 1500 as garden culture grows: Tudor and Georgian bowers of honeysuckle and vine, Shakespeare bank where wild thyme blows, Milton blissful bower, Pope garden bower. The plural bowers thus evokes both architecture and nature, built huts and grown arbours, exactly why the source sentence pairs birds making nests and bowers: animal architecture as model for human huts, with chimpanzee shelters completing the scale.

  • Old English bur: lived-in room or hut; legal and poetic word for dwelling, without necessary greenery.
  • Medieval romance bower: lady private chamber with bower-maid; chivalric, feminine, secluded, in Chaucer and ballads.
  • Early Modern garden bower: shady recess of trees or vines; pastoral ideal from Spenser through Pope to Keats.
  • Specialized survivals: nautical bower anchor carried at the bow; card-game bower or jack from German Bauer; Australian bowerbird bower in ornithology from the nineteenth century.
  • Modern plural bowers: chiefly poetic-literary for shady nooks; in ecology, male bowerbirds build and decorate bowers to court females.
  • Sound change: Old English short vowel lengthened and diphthongized in the Great Vowel Shift to modern ow sound, with plural -s.

Literary attestations are dense: Shakespeare Titania bower, Milton Adam and Eve bower, Keats bowers of bliss, Tennyson, and Darwin descriptions of bowerbirds building playhouses. The source anthropology, man learning from beavers, birds, and chimps, uses bowers in its oldest dwelling sense returned through nature: birds as builders of leafy huts prefiguring human architecture. The garden bower and the forest hut meet in one plural, and civilization begins, the author suggests, by copying nests and bowers glimpsed in the woods.

Nineteenth century naturalists gave the word fresh precision when they described satinbirds and great grey bowerbirds clearing courts, raising stick avenues, and sorting blue feathers, berries, and snail shells by colour. Gould and Darwin quoted these accounts as evidence that beauty itself motivates building, and modern field experiments confirm that tidier, better decorated bowers win more mates. The etymological circle thus closes: the Germanic dwelling-word, borrowed back from the forest through ornithology, now names animal houses whose symmetry and ornament rival human garden design.
