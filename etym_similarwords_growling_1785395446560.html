• Snarling: Similar because both are throaty vocal expressions of hostility; differs because snarling is sharper, more explosive, and made with bared teeth — a sound of the muzzle rather than of the deep chest, often the closer-range, more overtly aggressive counterpart of the growl.
  • Grumbling: Similar because both are low, continuous, rumbling sounds; differs because grumbling is a subdued murmur of discontent that complains rather than attacks — it belongs to people, stomachs, and thunder, and lacks the deliberate menace of the growl.
  • Grunting: Similar because both are low guttural sounds made deep in the throat; differs because grunting is short, abrupt, and closed-throat, a single percussive expulsion (of a pig, of a man straining) with no sustained, vibrating menace.
  • Roaring: Similar because both are loud animal vocalisations of aggression; differs because roaring is far louder and more open-mouthed, a bellow at full stretch, whereas a growl is a locked-jawed mutter kept deliberately low.
  • Barking: Similar because both are canine vocalisations of warning; differs because barking is a percussive, repeated, open-mouthed call of alarm or attention, rhythmic and deliberate, while growling is a continuous low rumble of threat.
  • Whining: Similar because both are continuous dog vocalisations; differs because whining is high-pitched, drawn-out, and submissive — a sound of distress, desire, or pleading, the opposite emotional pole of the low, dominant growl.
  • Whimpering: Similar because both are low, continuous sounds of a creature; differs because whimpering is soft, weak, and pathetic, expressing fear or pain rather than the confident threat of a growl.
  • Rumbling: Similar because both denote low, continuous, rolling sound; differs because rumbling is acoustically descriptive and emotionally neutral (of thunder, stomachs, distant traffic), while growling carries the animal register of threat and hostility.
