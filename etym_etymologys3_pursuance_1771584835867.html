The ancestry of pursuance is the ancestral verb of companionship. The Proto-Indo-European root *sekʷ- meant "to follow, to accompany," and its oldest daughters preserve the image of two beings walking together. In Sanskrit, sácate (सचते) means "follows, accompanies," and from the same root comes sákhi (सखि) "friend, companion" - literally "one who follows alongside" - which survives in Hindi as sakhā (सखा) and in Odia as sakhā (ସଖା), both still meaning "friend." Greek softens the initial s- to h- and gives hépomai (ἕπομαι) "I follow," from which comes akólouthos (ἀκόλουθος) "attendant," the ancestor of English acolyte - the one who follows the priest. The root is thus, from its very beginning, a word about trust and fellowship: to follow is not merely to trail behind but to keep company with, to attend, to belong to the train of another. From the same source come the Latin socius "ally, companion," and with it society, social, and associate - the whole family of words in which following and fellowship are one. Latin refines the root into an instrument of law and purpose. From sequor, sequī, "to follow," the Romans built prosequī - pro- "forward" plus sequī - "to follow forward, to accompany, to escort, to pursue," a verb at home both in the law courts (to follow up a suit) and on the road (to escort a departing guest). The derived family is enormous: secta "a following" gives sect; sequentia "what follows" gives sequence and consequence; exsequī "to follow out" gives execute; and the participle prosecūtus gives prosecute, the learned doublet that English took directly from Latin. Two paths then carry the verb into the vernacular world. First, Vulgar Latin *prosequere passes into Old French as poursuivre / poursivre, "to chase, pursue, continue, carry on," and from Anglo-French pursuer English takes pursue about 1275 - initially "to follow with hostile intent," a hunting word, but by the late fourteenth century already "to follow a course of action," the moral sense that had existed in Latin all along. Second, the French noun - poursuite "pursuit," and, beside it, poursuiance "the act of pursuing" - carries the verb's action into noun form; English borrows poursuiance as pursuance in the 1590s. The recorded history of the English noun is a history of hardening. In its first century pursuance is an ordinary action noun: the pursuance of a claim, the pursuance of a course, the pursuance of a design. But very early, and decisively in the seventeenth century, it is captured by the fixed prepositional formula "in pursuance of," which becomes the standard connective of legal deeds, wills, statutes, parliamentary orders, and official correspondence - the phrase by which an action is declared to be the carrying out of an earlier instruction. From this legal-archival habitat the word never escaped. Its bare sense, "the act of pursuing," faded into the background as pursuit, the livelier sibling, claimed the energetic chasing of goals and quarries; and in the twentieth century the administrative phrase "pursuant to" began to compete with and largely replace "in pursuance of" in statutes and regulations, so that modern pursuance survives chiefly as a dignified, slightly archaic legalism. Its meaning, however, never lost the moral colour of its root: to act in pursuance of a principle is to follow that principle like a path, step by step, in faithful execution of what has been adopted. The word's literary life is thin but telling - it lives almost entirely in documents, which is precisely why a translator of The Story of the Stone can use it to dignify a daughter's obedience. When Dai-yu's parents bring her up to believe that good health is founded on careful habits, "and in pursuance of this principle, had always insisted that after a meal one should allow a certain interval to elapse before taking tea," the legal-administrative formula imports its gravity into the domestic scene: a rule is being carried out with the fidelity of a will being executed, and the orphaned daughter who still waits before taking tea is following her parents' teaching as a clerk follows an instruction - and as a friend, etymologically, walks alongside. The evolution of the word, from the Indo-European image of two beings walking together, through the Roman law court and the Old French chase, to the frozen formula of the modern statute, is a single unbroken story of following - and pursuance is the noun that kept the faith.
