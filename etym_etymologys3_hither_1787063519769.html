From a prehistoric pointing finger to a literary relic

The story of hither begins not with a word for a place but with a gesture: a pointing finger. Its ancestor is a Proto-Indo-European deictic element *ke- / *ki-, the "this / here (near the speaker)" stem, whose only job was to anchor speech to the speaker's own here-and-now. This is the same ancient deictic base that later gave Latin cis "on this side," and, within Germanic, the words for "here" (he-forms) across the whole family. In the parent language the opposition was simple: *ke- "this (by me)" versus the distal stem that yielded English that and there.

In Proto-Germanic this pointing stem was extended with a directional suffix into *hidrē, an adverb meaning "to this place, toward here." Germanic had a systematic set of such directional adverbs — three here/that/which-way forms — and the reader can still see their skeleton in Old English: hider "hither," þider "thither," hwider "whither." These three travelled together through the entire history of the language, and they reshaped each other's spelling and form at every stage.

Old English: hider, an everyday word

Old English hider (also spelled hīder) is attested from the earliest period of the language, c. 8th–9th centuries, in prose and verse alike. It was a completely ordinary word of motion: chronicles report that messengers came hider; homilies urge the faithful to turn hider to the truth; gospel translations preserve Christ's "come to me" as an imperative of coming hider. Nothing about it was elevated or archaic; it was simply "to here." Alongside it, the system hider/þider/hwider (with the locative set hēr/þǣr/hwǣr "here/there/where") gave Old English a clean four-way map of space: where you are, where you go, and whence you come.

Middle English: the great spelling change

In Middle English the word appears as hider, hidder, hithir, hyder, hithere — a period of free spelling. The significant development is phonetic and analogical: the final consonant of the stem was gradually reshaped from -d- to -th- under the pressure of its siblings thither and whither. Because the three words so often occurred together ("hither and thither," "whether he comes thither or whither..."), speakers levelled the consonant pattern across the set. The -th- spelling is already found in the 13th–14th centuries and became the norm in the 16th century; the conservative form hider survived in dialects (it is recorded in northern English and Scots into the modern period). Meanwhile the language's goal-construction was changing: instead of a bare directional adverb, English increasingly said "come here" / "go there" without marking the goal separately, squeezing the whole family of -der directionals.

Early Modern English: the golden age of "hither"

The 16th–17th centuries are the great literary age of hither. It is the standard word for approach in the King James Bible — "Bring him hither to me" (Matthew 17:17), "Come hither" resounding through its pages — and it is everywhere in Shakespeare, where "come hither" is the stock summons of the stage, and where the word carries both physical and emotional approach. In this period hither was still normal educated speech, but the first signs of retreat had appeared: in casual, colloquial talk, "here" with a verb of motion was winning. The word was drifting upward in register, carried on the currents of print, preaching, and the stage.

The 18th–19th centuries: fossilising in idioms

By the 18th century hither had lost the everyday spoken field to "here"; it survived in set pairs and formal prose. The pair "hither and thither" (for scattered, restless, or aimless movement) became proverbial. The temporal compound hitherto ("up to this time") was already fixed and remains standard in formal and legal writing today. Writers of the Romantic and Victorian periods pressed the word into service for elevation and archaism; it began to read as consciously poetic or old-fashioned, a badge of high style rather than a neutral adverb.

The 20th–21st centuries: a word reborn as a phrase

In the 20th century hither completed its migration from vocabulary to idiom. Its living modern uses are almost entirely fixed:

  • "hither and thither" — still the most common modern survival, used for scattering or indecisive movement.
  • "hither and yon" — the folksy variant with yon.
  • "come-hither look/glance" — since the mid-1800s, the flirtatious invitation; the word's one genuine new meaning.
  • "hitherto" — the temporal adverb, fully alive in formal registers.
  • Mock-archaic and fantasy register — the word thrives in fantasy and period fiction (including, fittingly, the very scene in The Hobbit from which this card's example is drawn), in headlines seeking gravitas, and in comedy.

Hither is thus a perfect case-study of register change: the same form, the same meaning ("to this place"), for fourteen hundred years — while its social position travelled from core everyday speech, through the heights of biblical and Shakespearean prose, to a fixed phrase kept alive by idiom, tradition, and the taste for a dignified archaism.
