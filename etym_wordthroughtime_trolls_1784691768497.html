- 17th-18th century introduction - troll enters English from Scandinavian tale and saga as a being of the untamed wilderness: a huge mountain giant or a cave-haunting magical creature of either sex, feared and treacherous
- 19th-century folkloric sense - the folk beliefs of Sweden, Norway and Denmark are collected and retold: trolls dwell in hills and caves, work magic and shape-shift, and cannot endure daylight, turning to stone when the sun rises; the word gains its fixed picture of the stony, lumpish giant
- 20th-century literary sense - Tolkien and the fantasy revival fix the modern troll: a huge, slow-witted, flesh-eating brute who speaks in rustic English and bickers like a kitchen-boy; the connotation is comic-menacing rather than purely dreadful
- Contemporary sense - the noun has acquired a second, very different life online, where a troll is one who posts provocative or malicious messages to stir up conflict; the old giant and the new nuisance now share the word, the modern sense carrying a sneaky, disruptive charge
