- 17th Century: Refers specifically to a plain-woven, unbleached cotton fabric imported from Calicut, India. - Connotation: Exotic, affordable, utilitarian.
- Late 17th - 18th Century: Expands to include printed cotton fabrics, often with colorful patterns, whether imported or domestically produced. - Connotation: Fashionable, decorative, subject of trade disputes and protectionist laws (Calico Acts), still relatively affordable.
- 19th Century: Becomes a more general term for any plain-woven cotton cloth, often used for everyday items, linings, or as a base for printing. The term for printed fabric remains strong. - Connotation: Common, versatile, foundational textile.
- Late 19th - 20th Century: Emerges as a descriptor for a specific multi-colored (orange, black, white) pattern, particularly in cats. - Connotation: Distinctive, charming (for cats), associated with female animals due to genetics.
- Contemporary Usage: Continues to refer to the plain cotton fabric (especially in crafts), the printed cotton fabric, and the tri-color pattern in animals. The firearm usage is niche. - Connotation: Traditional, natural (for plain fabric), vibrant (for printed), unique (for animal patterns).
