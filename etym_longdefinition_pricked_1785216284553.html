PRICKED is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb to prick: to pierce or mark with a sharp point. In its most familiar idiomatic use it means "to point something sharply erect," as in the fixed phrase pricked up his ears — the ears suddenly lift and swivel forward, sharp and alert, as hearing strains toward a sound. The idiom imagines the animal (or person) setting its ears upright like a needle-point rising from cloth; the sharpness of the needle lends the gesture its quality of sudden, reflexive attention.

In The Hobbit, the word captures Bilbo alone in the wilds of the Misty Mountains: "Bilbo pricked up his ears." The word does not denote any literal stabbing. It records an instinctive, animal-quick gesture of attention — the hobbit's ears lift and turn as his hearing catches an unexpected sound. The tone is reflexive and bestial rather than intellectual: Bilbo does not decide to listen; his body simply pricks up like a dog's or a horse's at a distant noise. Tolkien deliberately transfers a phrase applied since the 1590s to hunting animals onto his comfortable, homely hobbit, and the transference quietly suggests that out in the wild even a hobbit's instincts sharpen into something almost feral. Attention itself becomes a needle standing suddenly upright.

The word's senses, past and present:

  • Literal (primary): to pierce, puncture, or mark with a sharp point — to prick a finger with a needle, to prick a blister, to prick the soil.
  • Idiomatic "prick up one's ears": to raise the ears sharply and attentively, as an animal does; by extension, to become suddenly and sharply attentive — "he pricked up his ears at the mention of gold."
  • To spur or goad (archaic/technical): to prick a horse — to urge it on with the spur; by metaphor, to prick someone to action, to incite, to goad. "Pricked forward" meant urged on.
  • Moral/psychological "prick of conscience": a sharp, sudden pang of guilt or unease — the conscience pricks like a needle; a "prick of conscience" is a sudden stabbing sense of wrongdoing.
  • Of plants: a "prick" is a thorn or spine; "prickly" means full of thorns, and by metaphor, irritable or sensitive.
  • Gardening: to "prick out" seedlings is to transplant them by lifting them with a pointed dibber.
  • Vulgar slang (modern, taboo): the noun "prick" is a coarse slang term for the penis and, by extension, an insult for a contemptible man; this register shadows the word's coarse, physical history and is kept separate in polite use.

Etymologically the word is Germanic-only and of uncertain, likely expressive origin. The Proto-Germanic base *prik- denoted a point or pointed object; Old English had the noun prica ("point, puncture, prickle, sting") and the verb prician ("to prick, sting, pierce"), which developed through Middle English prikken into modern prick. The sharp pr- onset is probably echoic — the sound of a sudden jab — and the OED treats the family as of uncertain origin with no demonstrated PIE root. The decisive moment for the modern idiom came in the 1590s, when "to prick up one's ears" was first applied to dogs, horses, and hares raising their ears to listen — an image that has remained vivid ever since.
