- 15th c. — English jurists adopt the Latin phrase lex talionis, glossing it as the law of retaliation; the genitive is felt as part of a fixed citation.
- 16th–17th c. — the phrase becomes standard in arguments about Mosaic and Roman law, and the Authorized Version of 1611 fixes “eye for eye” in English.
- 18th–19th c. — Enlightenment penology debates proportionality; Beccaria attacks literal talion as barbarous while Kant defends a strict equality of crime and punishment.
- 20th c. — international law and just-war theory invoke proportionality, anthropologists use the talion to describe feuding societies, and the phrase becomes a learned allusion in journalism.
- 21st c. — mostly allusive: “lex talionis” in commentary on tit-for-tat politics and online retribution; the bare genitive Talionis survives almost only inside the phrase.
