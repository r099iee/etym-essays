exultation — /ɛɡzəltˈeɪʃən/ — a feeling or display of triumphant, exuberant joy; the state of rejoicing so powerfully that it bursts outward into shouts, cries, or bodily motion; in classical and literary usage, specifically the act of leaping or springing up for joy — the visible and audible eruption of victory. In this passage from Jack Vance’s Tales of the Dying Earth, the ancient air of the world is imagined as an archive of every human utterance it has ever carried: “cries of pain … sighs, laughs, war shouts, cries of exultation, gasps.” Within that catalogue of the voice’s extremes, exultation names the loudest of the bright emotions. It is the cry of triumph, the upper register of the human soul, set in deliberate opposition to the cries of pain that open the list. The literary tone is archaic, formal, and oratorical: Vance selects the most Latinate, ceremonial term available rather than plain joy, so that the shout seems to issue from a classical or biblical world — the jubilant cry of a victor, a people, or a godlike wizard. It is a sound of boundless self-assertion: where sighs and gasps are involuntary emissions of weakness, a cry of exultation is an instrument of power, a war-trumpet of the spirit. The air has experienced it as a distinct species of sound, the acoustic signature of victory.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. The word carries its physical history inside it. It descends from Latin exsultāre, a frequentative verb meaning “to leap up again and again, to bound and prance,” built from ex- (“out, up”) and saltāre (“to leap, to dance”), itself the frequentative of salīre, “to leap, to spring.” The image is a body springing repeatedly into the air, unable to contain its energy — and that leap is the semantic bridge to the emotion: joy so strong that the body jumps is exultation. Modern English retains the residue of this physical sense in older and poetic usage, where to exult is still literally to spring up for joy.

Primary senses.
  • Intense triumphant joy bursting outward into shouts, cries, or movement — the dominant modern sense (the crowd exulted; cries of exultation).
  • The act of leaping or springing up for joy — the classical and physical sense retained in poetry and older dictionaries.
  • The jubilant outcry itself — exultation as the sound of triumph, the noun for a particular kind of utterance.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings.
  • Religious / biblical register: rejoicing in divine favour or salvation; the Latin Vulgate uses exsultāre for the exulting soul (exsultabo et laetabor, “I will exult and be glad”), and devotional prose keeps a reverent, psalm-like colour that plain joy lacks.
  • Oratorical / rhetorical register: a formal, ceremonial noun for public rejoicing, favoured by orators, poets, and scripture for its Latinate weight.
  • Literary-archaic register: deliberately chosen in modern fiction to sound classical and heroic — exactly how Vance deploys it here.
  • Nominal and adjectival derivatives: exultant, exultantly, exultancy, exulting — all sharing the leap-root and the same triumphant colouring.
  • Technical residue in Latin scientific names: the epithet exultans (“leaping, springing”) appears in some binomials, preserving the physical root in taxonomy.

How exultation differs from its neighbours.
  • Elation (Latin elatus, “lifted up”) — calm, interior buoyancy; silent, private, and still, with no leap and no cry.
  • Jubilation (Latin jubilāre, echoing a Semitic shout; cf. Hebrew yovel, “jubilee”) — communal, carnivalesque celebrating, a festival of many voices rather than a single victor’s cry.
  • Triumph (Greek thriambos, the procession honouring a victorious general) — the public narrative of victory itself, the parade rather than the cry.
  • Rapture (Latin rapere, “to seize”) — being carried out of oneself, passive and ecstatic; exultation is active, self-assertive leaping.
  • Ecstasy (Greek ekstasis, “standing outside”) — a trance-like loss of self; exultation is a gain of self, the fullest possible self-assertion.

In a single sentence: exultation is joy that cannot stay inside the body — it leaps, it cries out, and it names victory aloud, which is precisely the note Vance strikes when the ancient air of the dying world is made to have heard, among all human sounds, cries of exultation.
