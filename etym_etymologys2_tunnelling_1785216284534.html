The etymology of TUNNELLING is a story of metaphor that begins not with excavation but with barrels; no Proto-Indo-European root can be assigned to the line. Medieval Latin tunna "cask, barrel" (9th century; a North Sea Germanic cask-word of uncertain origin, possibly Celtic — cf. Old Irish toun "hide, skin," a covering stretched over a frame; also found in Old English tunne "tun, cask" and Old High German tunna, ancestor of German Tonne) → Old French tonne "tun, cask" → Old French tonnelle (diminutive of tonne, literally "small cask"; also used of a long funnel- or barrel-shaped net used to decoy birds) → Middle English tonnel, tonel "funnel-shaped wire net into which birds were decoyed" (early 15th century) → English extension "tube, pipe" (1540; anything long, hollow, and tubular reminded English speakers of the net) → English "underground passage" (1660s; the passage cut under a river or through a mountain; re-borrowed into French as tunnel in 1878) → English verb to tunnel "to excavate underground" (intransitive, 1795; transitive, 1856) → English tunnelling, present participle (with the British doubled -ll-; American English writes tunneling with one -l-) → English tunnelling (present participle in the Hobbit passage: "when they were tunnelling down long ago"). NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: the oldest stage is uncertain. Medieval Latin tunna and Old English tunne are a North Sea Germanic maritime cask-word whose deeper ancestry is unknown; a Celtic origin (cf. Old Irish toun "hide, skin") has been suggested but is not proven, and no Proto-Indo-European root can be assigned. The leap from "bird-decoy net" to "tube" to "underground passage" is a purely metaphorical chain of shape-association — the word never meant "to dig" until the English noun was well established. The native English word for an underground passage before tunnel was mine.
