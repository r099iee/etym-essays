SULLY: To soil, stain, or tarnish — literally to make physically dirty, and, far more often in modern use, to mar, taint, or tarnish the purity, honour, reputation, or integrity of a person, a name, an ideal, or an institution. Sense in this context. The card's sentence — "Will you so easily __________ our ancient honor?" — uses the verb in its dominant figurative register. "Honor" is here conceived as a precious substance, a bright surface that can be dirtied or dimmed, and to sully it is to tarnish an inherited, ancestral reputation: the word implies that honour is a treasure handed down over generations ("ancient honor") and that the speaker is horrified at how easily it might be degraded by a single act of cowardice, betrayal, or dishonesty. The rhetorical question — "Will you so easily...?" — sharpens the sense of impending defilement: sullying is presented as something quick and cheap, an act that stains what it took generations to build. The elevated, slightly archaic tone suits chivalric or tragic drama, where the abstract "honor" and the verb "sully" belong to the language of oaths and reputation. Primary literal sense — to make dirty. At its root, sully means to soil physically: to smudge, begrime, or dirty a surface. This is the sense that survives in descriptions of white cloth, bright metal, clear water, or polished objects being stained. In the card's example sentences both registers are shown — "He refused to __________ his reputation with lies" is figurative, while "Mud had __________ her white dress" is literal. The literal sense is now the rarer one (most speakers would say "soiled" or "stained"), but it is the historical base from which everything else descends. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings.
  • Figurative — tarnishing reputation or honour (dominant sense): the moral use, as in "a sullied reputation," "a sullied name," "sullied honour," "to sully the memory of the dead." Here sully means to bring shame, scandal, or disgrace upon something formerly pure or admired; it is the verb of scandal and infamy.
  • Moral and religious defilement: to sully purity, virtue, chastity, or innocence — the language of defilement and corruption, as in "a sullied soul" or the "sullied" maiden of older literature. This shades into the religious sense of ritual pollution.
  • Political and journalistic use: a reputation, record, office, or cause is said to be sullied by corruption, scandal, or hypocrisy; "sullied" functions as a powerful adjective of condemnation in political prose ("a sullied legacy," "a sullied administration").
  • "Unsullied" — the negated form: perhaps the most frequent modern surface, used as a positive epithet for what remains untarnished, spotless, or pristine: "an unsullied reputation," "unsullied purity," "her unsullied character." The word's emotional weight lives largely in this negated adjective, which praises by denying any stain.
  • Environmental and aesthetic use: to sully the air, water, or a landscape is to defile a clean natural space; a view or vista "sullied" by ugly construction. This bridges the literal and figurative senses.
The historical bridge. The English verb comes from Old French souiller "to soil, stain, make dirty," which in turn descends from the Latin noun sus "swine, pig" through suīllus "pertaining to swine": the French souil was the muddy pool where pigs wallowed, and souiller originally described wallowing in filth like a pig. The leap from "make muddy" to "tarnish reputation" is an ancient and universal metaphor: purity is light and whiteness, and anything that dims or dirties it is a stain. English borrowed the verb in the 16th century at the height of this figurative extension, so that "sully" entered the language already carrying the moral meaning of defilement alongside the physical one. The word's lasting niche is precisely this moral one: it is less common than "soil" or "stain" in literal use but remains the preferred dignified verb for the tarnishing of honour, virtue, and reputation. Collocations and register. Sully collocates with nouns of reputation and purity — "reputation," "name," "honour," "character," "record," "purity," "innocence," "memory," "legacy" — and with the adverbial intensifiers "easily," "never," "forever," "wantonly." It is a formal, literary word, at home in tragic drama, political rhetoric, and moral essay rather than casual speech; it carries a consistently grave, condemnatory tone. Its derived adjective "unsullied" is a fixed part of the vocabulary of praise. Synonyms in descending formality include defile, besmirch, tarnish, debase, and pollute, while the lighter everyday verbs are soil, stain, and dirty.
