uncovetous is a transparent English negation — un- (not) + covetous (greedy) — but each half has a long voyage, one Germanic, one Latin-French, meeting only in Tudor England.

The negation un- is among the oldest morphemes in the language. It continues PIE negative *ne, *n- (not), seen in Latin ne-, in-, non, Greek a-, an-, Sanskrit a-, an-, via Proto-Germanic *un- (not, as in Gothic un-, Old High German un-) to Old English un- (unhealthy, unwise, unclean). It attaches freely to adjectives and participles without changing the base sound: covetous to uncovetous by simple prefixation, stress staying on cov- /ʌnˈkʌvətəs/. No phonetic shift occurs at the boundary; the history lies entirely in the base.

  • Latin desire (cupere to cupiditas): Classical Latin cupere (to desire, wish, long for — cupio, cupis, cupit) is neutral to passionate, giving cupidus (desirous, fond of, then greedy) and cupiditas (desire, ambition, avarice). Cupido personified is Desire himself. PIE *kup- (to desire, quiver with excitement) links Latin to Sanskrit kupyati (gets agitated) and Old Church Slavonic kypeti (to boil). Latin keeps desire broad; moral narrowing comes later.
  • Vulgar Latin intensification (*cupidietare): Late speakers form a frequentative-desiderative *cupidietare (to keep desiring eagerly), unattested on stone but demanded by Romance outcomes: Old French coveitier, Provençal covezar, Italian cupidare in glosses. The change of Latin u to French o before labials, loss of middle d, and palatalisation of t before i to /ts/ then /s/ are regular Gallo-Romance sound laws.
  • Old French moralisation (coveitier to coveitous): by the 12th century coveitier no longer means to desire neutrally but to lust after what is another's, under Church pressure from the Decalogue and avarice allegory. Anglo-Norman carries it to England after 1066 as coveiter, cuveter. The adjective coveitous (full of illicit desire, from coveit- + -ous, full of) is formed in French and borrowed whole: Middle English coveitous (Chaucer's Friar is not covetous of money alone but of souls). Pronunciation anglicises French /kuveit-ia/ to /ˈkʌvɪtəs/ with reduction of the French diphthong and voicing of t region.
  • English fixation and negation (1500-1700): Tyndale and the Authorised Version render Exodus 20:17 as thou shalt not covet, fixing covet as the Bible word for illicit desire and covetous as the vice (Luke 12:15, beware of covetousness). Preachers then need its opposite and prefix Germanic un-: uncovetous, uncovetously, uncovetousness appear in sermons, Baxter, Bunyan, and Johnson's Dictionary (1755, not covetous, not greedy). Ruskin inherits the pulpit word and makes it aesthetic: to look uncovetously is to keep the Commandment with the eyes.

There is no serious debate about the main chain — Latin cupere to French coveitier to English covet is textbook — only the routine caveat that Vulgar Latin *cupidietare is reconstructed rather than inscribed, and that PIE *kup- glosses waver between desiring and quivering. The English prefix un- is wholly regular. What is remarkable is cultural, not phonetic: a Germanic not cancelling a Latin-French greed to translate a Hebrew commandment, then teaching a Victorian boy how to look at castles.
