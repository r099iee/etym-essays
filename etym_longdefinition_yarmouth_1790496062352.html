Yarmouth is a historic seaport town in Norfolk, eastern England, standing on a narrow spit between the North Sea and the tidal River Yare, formally distinguished as Great Yarmouth from the smaller Yarmouth on the Isle of Wight. In the source sentence, John Ruskin in Praeterita recalling his maternal grandfather, a sailor who used to embark, like Robinson Crusoe, at Yarmouth and come back at rare intervals making himself very delightful at home, the word names the working port from which a Croydon-connected mariner would ship for the North Sea, the Baltic, or the coastal trade: herring fleets, colliers, and packet boats. The Robinson Crusoe comparison seals the romance: Yarmouth is York and Hull in Defoe, the Humber and Wash ports from which a restless son runs to sea.

Physically and historically, Great Yarmouth grew on sand: a shingle spit thrown up at the Yare's mouth, first settled by Cerdic's Saxons, chartered by King John in 1208, walled in the 13th century, and rich by the 14th on herring. Its grid of narrow Rows (more than 140 alleys, some under a metre wide), its two piers, Britannia and Wellington, its naval hospital and Nelson's Column, its vast sandy beach, and its herring curing yards and smokehouses made it at once a fishing capital, a naval station, and later a seaside resort. Daniel Defoe in his Tour (1724) calls it the finest quay in England after London; Dickens lodges there for David Copperfield's Yarmouth chapters (Peggotty's boat-house, the storm and wreck); 19th-century railways bring London holidaymakers for donkeys, piers, and bloater paste.

Linguistically, Yarmouth is transparent: Yare plus mouth, the mouth of the Yare. The river name Yare is pre-English, probably Brittonic, of debated sense (babbling, eddying, or yew river have all been proposed); the second element mouth is Old English mutha, river mouth, harbour. Domesday Book (1086) writes Gernemutha and Gernemua, medieval rolls Jernemuth, Yernemouth, then Yarmouth by the 15th century. The initial change from Gerne- and Yerne- to Yar- reflects standardisation on the modern river spelling Yare. Great was added to mark size and importance (Great Yarmouth versus Yarmouth, Isle of Wight, and Little Yarmouth across the river at Gorleston).

  • Core toponymic sense (proper noun, the Norfolk port): the town and borough itself; for example to embark at Yarmouth, Yarmouth herring, Yarmouth Rows, the Yarmouth Blitz. Capitalised, usually definite in older use (the Yarmouth), now bare. This is the source sense and dictionary head sense.
  • Administrative and parliamentary senses: the Borough of Great Yarmouth, the parliamentary constituency, the diocesan and naval districts; for example Member for Yarmouth, Port of Yarmouth. The bridge is jurisdiction: the town name extends to the authority governing it.
  • Economic metonymies: Yarmouth for its products and fleets; for example Yarmouth herring (cured red herring), Yarmouth bloater (lightly smoked whole herring), Yarmouth Roads (the anchorage). Here the port stands for what sails from it or is cured in it, exactly as Ruskin's grandfather's herring business depends on Yarmouth.
  • Isle of Wight homonym: Yarmouth, a small ferry port at the mouth of the Western Yar, Isle of Wight. The bridge is parallel formation (Yar plus mouth) on a different river. Context disambiguates: Great signals Norfolk, bare Yarmouth in a Wight context signals the island.
  • Literary and idiomatic aura: Crusoe's Yarmouth Roads where his first ship is wrecked, Dickens's storm, Borrow's Lavengro Yarmouth scenes. The name carries salt, storm, and return: to sail from Yarmouth is to risk the North Sea, to come back to Yarmouth is home and delight, as Ruskin's grandfather did.

Pronounced in Received Pronunciation as YAR-muth, with first-syllable stress and reduced second vowel, locally often YAR-muth with a Norfolk broad vowel. Spelt Yarmouth, pronounced as if Yarmuth; the -mouth element reduces to muth in most English river-mouth names (Plymouth, Dartmouth, Portsmouth). It must not be confused with Yare alone (the river from Norwich to the sea), Yar (the Wight river), or Yarm (a Teeside town of different origin). In Ruskin the word is affectionate and salt-stained: the doorway to Robinson Crusoe adventure through which a genial sailor-grandfather disappears and reappears.
