In this tragic turn of Dream of the Red Chamber, accursed brands a stroke of bad luck so malevolent and perfectly timed that it feels supernaturally doomed. Ying-lian's near-escape collapses the day after hope rises: she is sold to the cruel Xue Pan by a misfortune 'which no one could possibly have foreseen.' The adjective's tone is charged, archaic-literary, and emotionally absolute — not mild 'unfortunate,' but luck as if under a curse. It moralises chance: the universe (or fate) seems to have pronounced evil upon the girl just when deliverance seemed near. Doom, injustice, and bitter irony colour the word in this Red Chamber context. The core meaning of accursed is 'under a curse; subject to a malediction; doomed or ill-omened by supernatural or divine force.' Grammatically it is a participial adjective: the fossilised past participle of the now rare verb to accurse ('to curse, to place under a curse'), which was formed in Old English from the intensive prefix a- plus the verb to curse. As an adjective it can be attributive ('an accursed day') or predicative ('the place is accursed'), and in older or poetic use it is often pronounced with three syllables, /əˈkɜːsɪd/, matching the participial spelling. The word is polysemous, and each sense is a stage on the same historical road:
  • Religious / biblical sense (oldest): under divine curse or anathema; excommunicated, God-forsaken, reprobate (e.g., 'the accursed sinner'). This is the sense carried over from Old English religious vocabulary, where acursian named the church's solemn act of anathematising.
  • Intensifier of strong dislike: used to express anger, contempt, or exasperation toward a person or thing considered detestable — 'Get that accursed dog out of my garden!' Here it is roughly equivalent to 'damned' or 'confounded,' with extra literary weight.
  • Narrative / literary sense of ill fortune: applied to events, strokes of luck, or fates that seem malignantly timed, as in this card's 'the most accursed stroke of bad luck'; the misfortune feels personally cursed rather than merely random.
  • Old rhetorical formula: 'accursed be...' — a solemn imprecation calling a curse down, common in archaic and literary English (e.g., 'accursed be he who moves my bones').
  • Rare verbal use: to accurse survives as an archaic or literary verb meaning to execrate or lay a curse upon; its participle is the adjective on this card.
The semantic development runs from a precise religious act to a general emotional intensifier. In Old English, acursian meant to place something under a solemn curse — originally the church's action of anathematising, the strongest condemnation the culture knew. Because the religious curse was absolute, the participial adjective accursed naturally came to mean 'worthless, detestable, abominable,' and from there to a general intensifier of anger and loathing, exactly like damned or confounded. In the Red Chamber sentence the word does double duty: it registers supernatural malevolence (a stroke of luck as if cursed) and the narrator's bitter, moralising anger at the injustice. It is the intensity built into the word's very structure — the Old English a- prefix meant 'thoroughly, completely' — that keeps accursed heavier than plain cursed to this day. Register is literary, religious, or rhetorically elevated; in casual modern speech speakers are far more likely to say 'damned' or 'wretched.' Common collocations: accursed fate/luck/day, the accursed one, accursed villain, an accursed place, and archaic accursed be he who.... Note the spelling: accursed has a double -c- reflecting its formation (a- + cursed), and it should not be confused with the unrelated adjective cursed used as a plain swear-intensifier, though the two share the verb curse.
