Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *kewh₂- 'to strike, hew, cut, forge' (reconstructed root; also cited as *kau- in older literature; the laryngeal-final reconstruction *kewh₂- accounts for the Greek and Germanic vowel outcomes). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hawwaną 'to hew, cut, strike' (reconstructed class VI strong verb; cognates include Old English hēawan 'to hew', Old Norse höggva, Old High German houwan, German hauen). Stage 3 — Proto-Germanic *hawją (variant *haujam) 'that which is cut down; mown grass; hay' (reconstructed neuter ja-stem noun derived from *hawwaną; literally 'the cut thing'). Stage 4 — Old English hīeġ (West Saxon) / hēg, hīg (Anglian) 'hay, mown and dried grass' (attested from the Old English period, c. 8th–11th centuries; developed from *hawją by i-umlaut). Stage 5 — Middle English hei, heye, hay 'hay, dried grass fodder' (attested from the 13th century onward). Stage 6 — Modern English HAY 'grass or other herbaceous plants cut, dried, and stored as fodder for livestock' (16th century onward). Note on the derivation: the account from PIE *kewh₂- via Proto-Germanic *hawwaną and *hawją is the standard and secure one — the same root yields Latin caedō 'to cut' (via a *-d- extension) and its descendants (caesura, precise, concise, excise, -cide), and the nominalization 'cut thing' > 'mown grass' is transparent across Germanic: Old Norse hey, Old High German hewi / houwi, German Heu, Dutch hooi. No stage in the Germanic-to-English line is seriously disputed.
