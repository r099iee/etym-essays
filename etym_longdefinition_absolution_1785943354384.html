ABSOLUTION is the formal release from guilt, obligation, sin, or punishment - specifically, in religious usage, the remission of sins pronounced by a priest over a penitent who has confessed, and in legal usage, the complete acquittal or discharge of a person from a debt, liability, or accusation. The word's image is that of something tied being unbound: absolution loosens the knots of guilt and obligation. In the sentence behind this card - 'One who seeks absolution' - the word names what the seeker wants: a formal, external act of release from wrongdoing, granted rather than achieved, and the phrase has the shape of a definition or a motto, pointing to a person defined by the search for pardon.

CORE SENSES
  • Sacramental absolution: the formal forgiveness of sins pronounced by a priest in the sacrament of penance (confession), after contrition and confession; a rite of the Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican churches, and in Catholic teaching the act by which God's pardon is declared and the soul released from sin.
  • Legal acquittal: the complete discharge of an accused person from a charge or accusation - in Roman law the absolute release of a defendant, the formal verdict that sets them free.
  • Civil release from debt or obligation: the discharge of a debtor, the cancellation of a liability, the freeing of a person from a contract or duty.
  • General and figurative use: the freeing of a person from blame, guilt, or responsibility by the judgment or forgiveness of another - to seek absolution for a mistake, to grant absolution from blame.
  • Literary and psychological metaphor: release from the inner burden of guilt, the peace that follows confession and pardon.
POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY MODERN MEANINGS IN DETAIL
  • Theology and liturgy - 'absolution of sins', 'form of absolution': the technical term of the sacrament of penance; the priest's words 'I absolve you from your sins' are the form of absolution, and in Catholic doctrine the rite 'absolves' by declaring the pardon God has granted. The word also names the prayer of forgiveness said over the sick or the dying in various rites.
  • Law - 'acquittal', 'absolute discharge': the legal sense inherited from Roman law, where absolutio was the formal release of a defendant; in modern usage the near-synonym acquittal has largely taken this role, but absolution still appears for formal release from liability.
  • Civil and commercial use - 'absolution from debt', 'absolution of a contract': the discharge or release of a person from a financial or contractual obligation, the old legal sense surviving in formal contexts.
  • Everyday figurative use - 'to seek absolution', 'to grant absolution': pardon for a mistake, an offence, or a lapse, granted by an aggrieved party, a boss, a friend, or a community; the word is frequently used lightly ('she wanted absolution for forgetting the appointment') while keeping a hint of religious weight.
  • Related forms - the adjective absolute (Latin absolutus, 'loosened from, finished, complete') and the verb absolve grew from the same stem, so that absolution is one of a family (absolve, absolute, absolutism, absolute zero) all descending from the Latin idea of setting free.
HISTORICAL AND SEMANTIC BRIDGE
The bridge runs from the Roman courtroom to the confessional. Latin absolvere was formed from ab- 'away from' and solvere 'to loosen, to unbind': to loosen something away, to set it free. In Roman law the verb and its noun absolutio were the standard terms for acquittal - the formal verdict that loosed the defendant from the charge, that untied the accusation. The Latin Church, speaking the legal language of the empire, took the same word into the sacrament of penance: the priest absolved the penitent, loosening the bonds of sin exactly as the court had loosed the accused. The English word, borrowed in the thirteenth century, is thus a courtroom term that became a confessional term - the legal image of untying a binding carried over whole into the moral and spiritual sphere.

REGISTER AND USAGE
  • Absolution is formal and institutional: in religious use it names a defined sacrament or rite, in legal use a defined formal act, and only in lighter figurative use does it become a general word for pardon.
  • It collocates with verbs of giving and seeking (grant, pronounce, receive, seek, obtain) and with prepositions of the domain (absolution for, absolution from).
  • The related verb absolve (from Latin absolvere) is more versatile - one can absolve a person from blame, from a promise, from a duty - while the noun absolution keeps the religious and legal flavour.
  • Absolution differs from forgiveness in being external and formal: forgiveness is a personal state, absolution is an act that releases.
