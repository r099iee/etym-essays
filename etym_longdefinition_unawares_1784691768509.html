Unawares is an adverb meaning without awareness or warning, in a manner that catches one unprepared: to be taken unawares is to be surprised, ambushed or overtaken at a moment of inattention. The word is the negative of the old adjective aware with the adverbial genitive suffix -s (the same ending hardened in always, needs and towards), and it carries a stronger sense of suddenness than the plain adjective unaware: one may be unaware of a fact for years, but one is caught unawares in a single moment. The idiom to catch (or take) someone unawares has been fixed in the language since the sixteenth century and belongs to the vocabulary of ambush, mishap and miscalculation — of vigilance tested and found wanting. The word is almost exclusively adverbial in modern English and survives chiefly inside that fixed phrase, which is its true home: taken unawares, caught unawares, come upon unawares. The sense in Tolkien's The Hobbit, Chapter 2. The phrase stands at the climax of the sack-catching sequence. Each of the twelve dwarves has been taken with humiliating ease: as each one comes up to the fire, a smelly sack snaps over his head and he is down. Then "Thorin came last — and he was not caught unawares." Tolkien marks the distinction sharply. The other dwarves walked into the trap blind; Thorin comes expecting mischief, hears the trolls' voices, sees the fire and the sacks, and understands the situation before he is seen. He is not surprised, not off guard, not ambushed — and so, alone among his company, he keeps his wits, feigns helplessness as he is thrust among the sacks, reaches the fire and smites one of the trolls across the nose with a burning branch. The word unawares is the hinge of the whole scene: the narrative builds a rhythm of helpless surprise — caught unawares, again and again — only to break it with Thorin's alertness, and with the sudden violence that follows. Primary senses of unawares
  • Adverb (the card's core sense) — without awareness, warning or preparation; in an unsuspecting manner, so that one is surprised or caught off guard.
  • In the fixed phrase to catch / take / come upon someone unawares — to surprise someone at a moment of inattention; the word's principal modern use.
  • Historically also "of oneself, inadvertently" — doing something unawares is doing it unintentionally or without noticing (chiefly literary, now rare).
  • As a noun (rare, archaic) in the phrase "at unawares" — in a state of being off guard, unprepared.
Polysemy: how the senses developed
  • The base is the Germanic adjective meaning "watchful, conscious": Old English wær, intensified as gewær, and in Middle English iwar / aware, "watchful, conscious, informed." The negative prefix un- produced unware / unwar, "ignorant, off one's guard."
  • The adverbial sense grew directly from the adjective by the old adverbial genitive -s: in the sixteenth century unaware became unawares, "in an unsuspecting manner," exactly as always, needs and towards fixed the same ending. The word was a favourite of the Elizabethans, used of ambushes, falls, and death arriving without notice.
  • The semantic bridge between "ignorant" and "surprised" is vigilance: to be unaware of a threat is to be unready for it, and to be unready is to be caught unawares. The word thus moved from a static state (not knowing) to a sudden event (being taken by surprise).
  • In the eighteenth century the bare adjective unaware was re-formed from the adverb and settled into its modern role as a common predicate adjective ("I was unaware of that"), leaving unawares to survive chiefly in the fossilised phrase catch unawares.
Distinctions from near-synonyms Unawares must be distinguished from its close relatives. Unaware is a state adjective (ignorant of a fact, for any length of time); unwary implies a lack of caution rather than of knowledge; by surprise stresses the suddenness of the event rather than the victim's state of mind; unexpectedly is neutral and descriptive; abruptly stresses sharp, unceremonious change; off guard names the state of being unprepared, of which being caught unawares is the consequence. Unawares alone fuses the victim's ignorance with the suddenness of the ambush in a single word.
