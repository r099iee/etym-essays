A word of smoke. The English noun reek is one of the oldest words for smoke in the language, and its history is a textbook case of a word changing its reference from one sense organ to another — from what is seen to what is smelled. Its root goes back to a Proto-Indo-European form reconstructed as *h₁rewg-, "to belch out, to emit, to discharge," the same root that appears in Greek ereúgesthai "to belch, to disgorge" and Latin ructāre "to belch" (English eructation). The underlying image is of something forcibly ejected from within — a burst of smoke from a chimney, of steam from the earth, of breath from a mouth. In Proto-Germanic this produced the noun *raukiz, "smoke, steam, vapor," which the Germanic languages spread everywhere: Old Norse reykr (still Icelandic reykur "smoke," and the source of the place-name Reykjavík, "Smoky Bay"), Old High German rouh, modern German Rauch, Dutch rook, Swedish rök, Danish røg — and Old English rēc, which is the direct ancestor of modern reek. The word is thus part of the common Germanic inheritance, not a borrowing, and for the oldest Germanic world it was simply the word for smoke.

Old and Middle English: smoke and holiness. In Old English, rēc (later rēoc, rīec) meant "smoke, vapor, steam," entirely neutrally. It appears in the Old English gospels and devotional prose for the smoke of the altar and the cloud of incense, in the poetry for the smoke of hall-fires and of burned dwellings, and in the scientific and astronomical texts (such as the Old English Prognostics and computus) for vapor and mist. The verb rēocan, "to smoke, to steam," matches the noun. Middle English keeps the form reke (13th–15th centuries) with the same visual meaning, and the Wyclif Bible uses it for smoke rising from the earth and from the altar. At no point in its first six hundred years in English does the word have anything to do with smell.

Scotland keeps the old word alive. In the North of England and above all in Scotland, reek remained the living, everyday word for smoke well into the modern era — as it still does in Scots and Northern English dialect. The most famous monument to this is the nickname of Edinburgh, Auld Reekie, "Old Smoky," a name recorded from at least the 16th century for the city shrouded in the coal-smoke of its countless chimney-stalks. Robert Burns, Walter Scott, and Robert Louis Stevenson all used the word in its Scottish, visual sense ("the reek of the town," "a reek of smoke"), and the word carried into standard English literature a poetic charge of hearth and home, of peat fires and winter evenings, that it has never entirely lost.

The great shift: from smoke to stench. Sometime in the 17th and 18th centuries the word began to drift from the visual to the olfactory. The reason is easy to see: smoke in a lived-in world is rarely clean, and a word for "smoke" constantly used of chimneys, kitchens, and burning refuse inevitably began to imply the smell that goes with it. Early signs appear in the 16th century (a "foul reek" of burning), and by the 1830s–1860s the dictionaries record the new sense as established: reek, noun, "a strong, disagreeable smell"; reek, verb, "to emit a strong bad smell." The old "smoke" sense did not die — it survived in dialect, in Scots, and in poets — but it receded from the center, and the word crossed decisively from the eye to the nose. This is one of the cleanest documented examples in English of a word migrating between the senses, driven not by metaphor but by the simple physical fact that smoke smells.

The modern figurative life. In the 20th and 21st centuries the verb, in the construction reek of, has become the word's most vigorous growth. "To reek of" something means to be soaked in, saturated with, or strongly suggestive of it — almost always something bad: a scandal reeks of corruption, a deal reeks of hypocrisy, a confession reeks of fabrication. The construction is a metaphorical transfer from the physical (a room reeking of tobacco) to the moral (a scheme reeking of fraud): where the eye cannot see, the mind "smells" the dishonesty. Meanwhile the noun keeps both faces — a writer can still speak of "the reek of a peat fire" (old, visual, literary) and "the reek of stale beer" (new, olfactory, everyday) in the same paragraph, and a dragon's "foul reek," as Tolkien has it in The Hobbit (1937), is magnificently both: smoke that stinks. It is this layeredness — a thousand-year-old word of Germanic smoke wearing its modern, moralized odor — that makes reek such a powerful instrument in the hands of a philologist-novelist like Tolkien, who knew exactly the double etymology he was putting into Balin's mouth.
