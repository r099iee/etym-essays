1. Pre-English origins (obscure by record, narrow by family). Unlike most everyday verbs, prick has no securely reconstructed Indo-European etymology. The word is attested only within Germanic: a West Germanic root *prikōn “to prick, jab” underlies Middle Dutch prikken, Low German pricken and English prick. Beyond Germanic no agreed cognates exist, and the root is widely judged to be expressive or echoic — a sound-symbolic gesture of a tiny sharp action, of the kind Germanic word-stock is rich in (compare “pin”, “pick”, “pike” as a family of point-words). Nineteenth-century dictionaries toyed with a link to an Indo-European root meaning “to break”, but no modern authority accepts this. The honest record is: no certain PIE root; the ancestors beyond Proto-Germanic are unknown, and no stage should be invented to fill the gap.


2. Old English: the plain physical verb (9th–11th centuries). Old English prician “to prick, pierce, sting” is attested in glosses rendering Latin pungere and stimulare (“to prick, goad, sting”), in psalter glosses, in prose and in the compound noun stock; the related noun pricca means “a sharp point, a goad”. In Anglo-Saxon usage the word is entirely physical: the point of a thorn, the goad of an ox, the prick of a needle. The psalters’ habit of glossing stimulare with prician is significant: the Latin word is the favourite verb of the biblical “sting” — most famously the sting of conscience — so the seed of figurative use was planted in the very earliest English records.


3. Middle English: opening out (13th–15th centuries). In Middle English the verb prikken keeps the piercing sense and adds a riding register: to prick a horse is to spur it, and “pricking” comes to mean riding fast across country. Chaucer’s world treats the spur as the emblem of haste: the Knight in the Canterbury Tales has returned from his wars, and elsewhere in Middle English romance knights and messengers “prike” their horses over field and forest. The noun “prik” names any point or mark — a dot, a spot, the point of a compass — and the archery sense (“the prick” of the target’s centre) appears around 1500. The semantic bridge from physical to moral is already audible: to be “pricked” is to be goaded and stung, and the goad transfers naturally from the horse’s flank to the mind.



  • Prick-song (16th century): written music — notes pricked or dotted on the page — as opposed to extemporised plain-song; the sense “to write down with pricked dots” is alive in Elizabethan music books and church records.

  • The exact point: “prick” names the precise moment or place from the 16th century — “the very prick of” something, “prick of noon” — a usage that survives in poetic and archaic prose.


4. Early Modern English: the conscience is fixed (16th–17th centuries). The King James Bible (1611) gives the figurative sense its canonical form: at Pentecost the hearers of Peter’s sermon “were pricked in their heart” (Acts 2:37). The Greek original uses katanyssēnai, rendered in the Vulgate as compuncti sunt; the KJV translators chose the plain Anglo-Saxon verb “pricked”, and from that moment “pricked conscience” and “pricked by remorse” are fixed English idioms, part of every sermon and every moral tale for three centuries. The same period records “to prick up one’s ears” (an animal points its ears at a sound; by extension, sudden attention — Shakespeare’s age loves the phrase), and the noun gains its vulgar sense: “prick” for the penis, recorded from the 1590s, the “point” again, now plainly phallic.


5. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: domestication. The verb softens into the daily world of sewing, gardening, medicine and the sea: pricked fingers from needles and pins; gardeners “pricking out” seedlings with a dibber (the term remains standard); sailors pricking their position on a chart with dividers; the medical “prick” of a lancet, later of a hypodermic needle. In Victorian philanthropy the pricked fingers of the needlewoman become a social symbol: Wilde’s seamstress with hands “all pricked by the needle” belongs to a whole literature of sweated labour, from parliamentary reports on the “sweating system” to Thomas Hood’s “The Song of the Shirt” (1843). The word is simultaneously at its most domestic and most charged with social meaning.


6. The twentieth century and after. The noun “prick” hardens into a general term of abuse (recorded from the early twentieth century), which makes speakers wary of the word in casual speech; the verb, however, keeps its old dignity in writing. “Pricked” now lives chiefly in set phrases — a pricked conscience, pricked ears, pricked by regret, curiosity or ambition — and in the literal registers of needle, nursery and physician. The allergy “prick test” keeps the verb current in clinics, and the participle remains available whenever a sharp point meets a vulnerable surface: the exact, full-blooded literal sense that the language has never lost is the one the seamstress’s fingers exemplify.
