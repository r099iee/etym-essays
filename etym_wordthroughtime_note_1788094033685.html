- (1200s) - Note enters English from Old French meaning "a mark, sign, token" — and immediately also the musical note, the written mark of a tone.
- (1300s–1400s) - "Take note of" = pay attention to (mark in the mind); "of note" = distinguished; note = reputation — the mark sense moralised.
- (1500s) - A note becomes a brief written record and a short letter; "note of hand" appears — a written promise to pay, the ancestor of paper money.
- (1600s–1700s) - Banknotes spread as paper currency (the "note" promises payment); footnotes fill scholarship; a "note" also names a bird's or instrument's characteristic sound.
- (1800s) - Diplomatic notes between states; "a note of [emotion]" — a perceptible quality in voice or atmosphere — becomes a standard literary device.
- (1900s–) - All senses flourish at once: musical notes, banknotes, lecture notes, sticky notes, "take note"; the sound-character sense lets Tolkien write "the rumble of his snoring changed its note" — the dragon's identifying sound shifts, and Bilbo flees.
- (today) - Note is fully alive in music, money, memory, and mood; its root meaning — the mark by which you recognise a thing — still quietly organises every sense.
