FROM WORK TO WEALTH TO WORDS

Copiously is a word about abundance, and its deepest ancestor is a Proto-Indo-European root about production: *h₃ep- (written *op- in older handbooks), meaning 'to work, to produce, to be in abundance'. The root is well attested across the family — Sanskrit ápas means 'work, the sacred act', and Latin builds from it both opus, 'work', and ops, 'wealth, power, resources, help' — so the original mental picture is of abundance as the product of work: the wealth that is gathered, the supply that is made. In classical Latin ops was already a word of real resources — the opēs of a family or a state were its riches, its means, its armed forces — and the noun of wealth and the verb of helping grew side by side in the same family.

COPIA: PLENTY GATHERED TOGETHER

From ops Latin made the great noun copia, with the prefix co- ('together') and an abstract ending: copia is literally 'resources gathered together', plenty concentrated in one place. The word was everywhere in Roman life: copiae (plural) were supplies and troops — the abundance of provisions and soldiers that made an army viable; copia in the singular meant plenty, abundance, a full supply of anything; and the adjective copiosus (from copia + -ōsus, 'full of') meant rich, well-supplied, abounding. The Romans personified this plenty as the goddess Copia, companion of the personified Abundance, and it is from her that the cornucopia — the 'horn of plenty' — takes its name, the overflowing horn that pours out wealth without end.

THE RHETORIC OF COPIA

The most interesting Roman development was rhetorical. For Cicero and the later writers on oratory, copia verborum — 'abundance of words' — was the mark of a full, rich, eloquent style, and the Renaissance inherited this ideal wholesale: Erasmus's enormously influential textbook of 1512 was titled De Utraque Verborum ac Rerum Copia ('On the Abundance of Words and Things'), training schoolboys to produce, from a single sentence, endless rich variations. This rhetorical sense — abundance of language as a virtue of expression — is the historical root of the modern phrase 'copious notes' and 'copiously annotated', and it shows how a word of material wealth was always also a word of verbal wealth: the rich man has copia of goods, the eloquent man has copia of words.

INTO ENGLISH

English took the adjective copious in the fourteenth century, borrowed from Latin copiosus and reinforced by Old French copieux; the adverb copiously followed in the fifteenth century as a purely English formation, copious plus the adverbial suffix -ly. From the beginning the word carried the double Latin inheritance: plenty of material things and plenty of words. Through the Renaissance it flourished in exactly those two domains — natural philosophy and medicine described copious rains, copious exhalations, copious humours, while rhetoricians and divines praised copious style and copious preaching. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries spread the adverb across the physical and mental landscape: writers sweated copiously, bled copiously, took copious notes, and produced copiously illustrated books of travel and natural history.

THE MODERN WORD

  • Sound and form: the pronunciation /ˈkəʊ.pi.əs.li/ shows the Latin vowel o as English /əʊ/; the spelling copiously has been stable since the 15th century.
  • Socio-cultural pressure: the rhetorical ideal of copia gave the word its prestige sense of verbal abundance; the scientific and medical prose of the Enlightenment and Victorian eras spread it to literal volume — sweating, bleeding, raining, noting.
  • Literary attestations: 'copiously' appears throughout Early Modern religious, scientific, and travel writing; the phrase 'copious notes' has been a schoolroom and scholarly fixture since the 19th century.
  • Register today: the adverb is still a shade more formal and literary than 'a lot' or 'abundantly' in casual speech; it is the natural choice when the writer wants to stress the sheer volume and generosity of an output or a flow.
