An entreaty is an earnest, humble, and heartfelt request or plea; a solemn asking for something, often repeated, made to someone who is free to grant or refuse it. To yield to an entreaty is to be moved by sincerity; to be deaf or closed to entreaty, as Dickens says of pelting rain and of Scrooge, is to be unmoved by even the most touching appeal. The word is formal and literary, carrying more dignity and emotional weight than everyday request.

The core distinguishes entreaty from nearby asking words by three features: earnestness, humility, and persistence. A request may be casual or official; an entreaty is emotionally charged. A demand asserts a right; an entreaty acknowledges dependence on another's goodwill. An order expects obedience; an entreaty seeks to persuade the will. Begging can imply desperation or inferiority; entreaty implies respectful urgency without loss of dignity. In short, entreaty is pleading that honours both the asker and the asked.

  • Earnest personal plea (central sense): a heartfelt appeal from one person to another. Example: her entreaty for forgiveness moved him to tears. Implies eye-contact, sincerity, often repetition. Stronger than ask, softer than beg. Common in fiction, letters, and solemn speech.
  • Formal petition or prayer: a written or spoken appeal to authority — court, king, governor, God. Example: the condemned man's entreaty for clemency; entreaties poured in after the disaster. Often plural. Implies procedure and deference. Survives in legal and religious register.
  • Diplomatic or collective appeal: nations, groups, or movements make entreaties for peace, aid, action. Example: repeated entreaties to the UN went unanswered. Formal, public, documented. Near-synonym to appeal but more emotive.
  • What it is not: not a casual favour, not a command, not extortion. You would not call asking to pass the salt an entreaty unless joking; you would not call a ransom demand an entreaty because it coerces. Entreaty requires voluntariness on both sides: the asker freely humbles himself, the granter freely chooses.
  • Countability and collocations: countable noun: one entreaty, many entreaties. Typical verbs: make, offer, present, press, renew, ignore, yield to, turn a deaf ear to entreaty. Typical adjectives: earnest, humble, tearful, passionate, moving, repeated, fervent, solemn. To be open or closed to entreaty measures moral responsiveness.

Historically entreaty belonged to hierarchical societies where inferiors petitioned superiors as a matter of form: subjects to monarchs, children to parents, suitors to ladies, defendants to judges. The word therefore carries an old-world courtesy; to entreat is to observe decorum even in distress. Modern democratic speech prefers appeal, plea, or request, leaving entreaty for novels, ceremonies, and deliberate elevation. Using it today signals that the matter is grave and the feeling deep; used lightly it sounds arch or ironic.

Dickens's use shows the moral scale. Wind, snow, and pelting rain are less open to entreaty than Scrooge is bitter: nature cannot listen, but Scrooge will not. The comparison makes pitiless weather the baseline and finds the man colder. To grasp entreaty here is to grasp Victorian charity: to be human is to be open to being entreated.
