EMBERS descends from an Indo-European root meaning 'to burn' or 'glow', through a Germanic compound meaning 'glowing ashes'. CHAIN: - PIE *h₂eys- / *ās- 'to burn, glow' (reconstructed; c. 4500-3500 BCE) — source also of Sanskrit édhas- 'fuel', Ancient Greek αἴθω (aíthō) 'I kindle, burn', and Latin aestus 'heat, glow' → Proto-Germanic *aima- 'vapour, smoke, glowing heat' (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-500 CE) → Proto-Germanic compound *aimunjō- 'glowing ashes, hot coals' (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-500 CE) — built on *aima- with an ash-element; exact compounding debated (see note) → Old English æmyrge 'glowing ashes, smouldering coals' (c. 700-1100) → Middle English emeris / ambris 'embers' (c. 1300-1450; intrusive -b- inserted by epenthesis, cf. bramble, thimble) → Modern English embers (1450-present, plural) → EMBERS NOTE ON DEBATE: the Proto-Germanic compound is reconstructed rather than directly attested; its exact shape (*aimunjō- vs alternative vocalisms) is debated, but the attested Old Norse eimyrja and Old High German eimuria confirm that a compound 'glowing-ash' word existed across Germanic. The Middle English intrusive -b- (ambris) is the same epenthetic insertion that gave bramble and thimble. The word has been almost exclusively plural in English since Middle English times.
