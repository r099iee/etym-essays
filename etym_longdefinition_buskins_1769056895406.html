buskins (plural of buskin) are calf-high or knee-high soft boots, typically of leather, tied or laced about the leg — warm, supple, close-fitting foot-and-lower-leg coverings. In the passage from C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian that generated this card, they are the "warm, soft buskins" that Doctor Cornelius hands to Caspian as he dresses for the secret midnight escape from Miraz's castle. The word is a deliberate archaism: "buskin" is not the modern English word for a boot, and Lewis chooses it precisely because it sounds like something from a fairy-tale wardrobe or a Renaissance stage, gently signalling the romance-world of Narnia where a scholar-fugitive and a prince slip through moonlit corridors dressed like wanderers of an older, grander age.

Principal senses (polysemy). Buskin is a rare word with a rich double life — a plain dress-word and a towering theatrical symbol:

  • Concrete footwear sense (primary): a soft leather half-boot reaching to the calf, worn by hunters, travellers, and labourers, and in the passage given to Caspian as quiet, warm night-footwear. In this sense the plural "buskins" is the ordinary count-noun form for a pair.
  • The theatrical sense (tragedy by metonymy): since the sixteenth century "the buskin" has been the standard English name for the high, thick-soled boot worn by tragic actors on the ancient Greek stage (the Greek kóthornos). By a famous metonymy it came to stand for tragedy itself, exactly as "the sock" stood for comedy. The fixed pair "sock and buskin" (comedy and tragedy) entered the common vocabulary of criticism; "to assume the buskin" or "to write in buskins" means to write or act tragedy.
  • Figurative "tragic style or mood": in literary criticism the word is used for the lofty, elevated register of tragic drama — Milton speaks of the "buskin'd stage," Dryden of the buskin of tragedy — and by extension for anything stately, dignified, or solemnly tragic in manner.
  • Rare transferred uses: in old texts "buskin" is also applied loosely to any high boot or foot-covering rising above the ankle, including the rough half-boots of rustics; the adjective "buskined" (wearing buskins; tragic) is recorded from the seventeenth century.

Function in the passage. In Prince Caspian the buskins are the opposite of formal footwear: warm, soft, and quiet. Their function in the scene is tactical rather than ceremonial. Hard-soled court shoes would click and clatter on the stone corridors, so Cornelius equips the boy with soft buskins whose yielding soles permit the two muffled figures to move with "almost no noise." The word therefore carries a hushed, conspiratorial tone — footwear as stealth technology — while its archaism keeps the whole escape inside the fairy-tale register of Narnia. The softness of the leather does the work of silence, and the word itself does the work of enchantment.

Near-synonyms and boundaries.

  • Boots is the plain modern sibling; it lacks the softness and the archaic glamour — modern boots are often heavy, rigid, and military, whereas buskins are supple and theatrical.
  • Ankle-boots / half-boots name the same cut but are strictly descriptive, modern, and neutral, carrying none of the stage history or romance of buskin.
  • Moccasins are soft and supple but cover only the foot, not the calf, and carry associations of Native American footgear rather than of the Greek or Renaissance stage.
  • Shoes is far too general and modern; a buskin always suggests a boot rising above the ankle.
  • Gaiters cover the lower leg but are worn over shoes and do not cover the feet themselves — leg-coverings rather than footwear proper.
  • Cothurnus is the learned Latin name for the Greek tragic boot itself, the cultural object behind the theatrical sense; the English word buskin is the common, naturalized name of the same object.

The decisive boundary is metonymic: since the sixteenth century "the buskin" has also been the standard term for the high boot of Greek tragedy, so that it became a standing figure for tragedy itself, opposed to "the sock" of comedy. No synonym — boot, half-boot, moccasin — carries that theatrical cargo; when Lewis writes "buskins," a learned reader hears at once the echo of the tragic stage behind the soft night-footwear of a prince in flight.
