Albanian is the English adjective and noun for the language, people, and culture of Albania, Kosovo, and the wider Albanian-speaking world. It is also the name of an independent branch of the Indo-European family, with no close living relatives. Albanians call themselves shqiptarë (singular shqiptar), their country Shqipëri, and their language shqip. The English word comes through Latin and Greek from the ancient tribe Albanoi, and its history is therefore an exonymic one, parallel to Armenian and Greek.

Primary sense: the language. Albanian is spoken by perhaps seven to eight million people in Albania, Kosovo, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Serbia, Greece, Italy, and the diaspora. It is the official language of Albania and Kosovo and a co-official language in parts of North Macedonia. The two main dialect groups are Gheg in the north and Tosk in the south, divided roughly by the Shkumbin River. The modern standard, based largely on Tosk, was fixed at the Congress of Orthography in 1972. Albanian has a rich case system, a developed system of verb forms, and a lexicon layered with Latin, Greek, Slavic, Turkish, Italian, and French borrowings. Its oldest surviving written text is the Formula e Pagëzimit ("Baptismal Formula") of 1462; the first printed book is Gjon Buzuku's Meshari (1555).

Secondary senses: people and adjective. An Albanian is a member of the Albanian people or a citizen of Albania; the Albanians are the ethnic group. The adjective describes nationality, language, and culture: Albanian literature, Albanian music, Albanian cuisine, the Albanian Alps, Albanian sworn virgins. In the diaspora, Albanian can be an identity label independent of citizenship, used by people in Italy (Arbëreshë), Greece (Arvanites), Turkey, Switzerland, Germany, the United States, and elsewhere. The word also appears in the names of political movements and regional organizations.

Tertiary and technical senses. In linguistics, Albanian is a branch of Indo-European whose exact position is debated; it is often linked to Illyrian, sometimes to Dacian or Thracian, but no ancestor language is securely identified. The dialects form a continuum, and the old Tosk varieties of Italy and Greece preserve archaic features. In history, Albanian can refer to the medieval Arbanon or Albanon region, to the Albanenses of Latin documents, and to the Ottoman Arnavut population. In material culture, Albanian describes the qeleshe (white felt cap), the xhubleta (women's dress), and the kanun customary law. In English, Albanian is occasionally confused with Caucasian Albanian, the extinct language of the Caucasian Albanians in modern Azerbaijan, which is unrelated.

Modern usage and semantic bridge. The semantic bridge runs from an ancient tribal name to a medieval regional name to a modern national and linguistic label. Greek Albanoi and Albanopolis were local names in Illyria; Byzantine writers revived Albanoi and Arbanitai in the eleventh century; Latin and Italian chanceries used Albanenses and Albanesi; English adopted Albanian in the sixteenth century. The endonym shqip is first attested in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and gradually replaced arbërisht as the national name. The coexistence of Albanian, Arberesh, Arvanitika, and Shqip reflects the layered history of the Balkans. In modern English, Albanian is neutral, though it can be coloured by debates over Kosovo, the Albanian mafia stereotype, and the country's communist past.

  • Language - an independent Indo-European branch; Gheg and Tosk dialects; standard since 1972.
  • Person - a member of the Albanian people or a citizen of Albania.
  • Adjective - of Albania, Kosovo, or Albanian culture.
  • Historical - related to the ancient Albanoi, medieval Arbanon, and Ottoman Arnavut.
  • Caution - not to be confused with Caucasian Albanian, an unrelated extinct language.
