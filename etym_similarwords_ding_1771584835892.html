• Tripod: Similar in being a three-legged stand or vessel; differs because tripod is purely geometrical and utilitarian - a camera or chemistry tripod - with none of the ding's ritual or dynastic charge, and it does not cover the four-legged forms the ding also takes.
  • Cauldron: Similar in being a large metal cooking vessel; differs because cauldron emphasises the huge pot and drags in associations of witch's brew and iron kettle - domestic, folkloric, and profane - whereas the ding is sacred and hierarchical.
  • Censer: Similar in being a bronze vessel of ceremony; differs because a censer is a small incense-burner used in temples and altars, whereas the ding is a large vessel for presenting ancestral food offerings and a symbol of dynastic mandate.
  • Urn: Similar in being a large vessel of bronze or pottery; differs because an urn is closed, legless, and used for holding ashes or liquids, while the ding stands on legs and serves sacrifice and rank.
  • Brazier: Similar in being a metal vessel on legs for holding burning fuel; differs because a brazier is a practical fire-holder for warmth or cooking, with no ritual or political meaning.
  • Kettle: Similar in being a metal pot; differs because a kettle is a domestic boiler of water, mundane and profane, while the ding is a sacrificial and ceremonial bronze of a ruling house.
  • Vessel: Similar in being a hollow container; differs because vessel is a generic, neutral term for any container, whereas ding names a specific historical ritual bronze type with legs and handles.
  • Altar: Similar in being a focus of sacrifice and ancestral offering; differs because an altar is the table or platform upon or near which offerings are laid, not itself a vessel, whereas the ding is the vessel that holds the offerings.
  • Bowl: Similar in being a round container; differs because a bowl is a plain open dish without legs or ritual status, while the ding is a leg-bearing ceremonial cauldron invested with dynastic meaning.
