FUSSY is an adjective meaning fastidious, overly detailed, or excessively concerned with trifles and minor details; it characterises a person, thing, or process that makes a disproportionate fuss over small matters. In the literary context supplied — "Sometimes the footnotes disagree with the text above them; this may seem a FUSSY process, but I did not want to cut too much" — the word characterises a process that feels disproportionately cluttered, exacting, and burdened by minor qualifications. The tone is gently self-deprecating and apologetic: the speaker admits that his own scholarly procedure may strike the reader as annoyingly finicky, yet insists that this laborious precision was a deliberate safeguard against over-cutting the material. The word here does not mean decorative or dandified; rather, it captures the anxious, footnote-laden fussiness of a mind that cannot let a single cross-reference, emendation, or scholarly quibble go unnoted, even when the marginalia threaten to outweigh the main argument. The polysemy of fussy is wider than this single context suggests, and each sense branches from the same core image of disproportionate concern. Applied to persons, fussy means hard to please, finicky, habitually anxious over small matters — a fussy eater, a fussy old lady, a fussy customer; here the word is mildly critical but warm, the complaint of exasperated affection rather than of contempt. Applied to babies and children, fussy names the fretful, restless, easily displeased state that makes them cry without obvious cause. Applied to things and processes, fussy means over-elaborate, cluttered with unnecessary detail — a fussy design, fussy ornament, fussy prose, fussy footnotes; in this sense it describes work that tries too hard, sacrificing clarity or simplicity to excessive refinement. Applied to tastes and standards, fussy stresses an exacting, discriminating fastidiousness that may be aesthetic or hygienic. And in modern editorial and scholarly usage, fussy describes apparatus or procedure burdened with minor qualifications, precisely the sense of this card. Senses at a glance:
  • Of persons: finicky, hard to please, habitually anxious over small matters (a fussy eater, a fussy old lady). Bridge: one who makes a fuss — disproportionate commotion — over trifles.
  • Of babies and children: fretful, restless, easily displeased (a fussy baby). Bridge: the small creature's disproportionate fuss over discomfort or want.
  • Of things and processes: over-elaborate, cluttered with unnecessary detail (a fussy design, fussy ornament). Bridge: the maker's excessive attention transferred to the made object.
  • Of standards and taste: exacting, discriminating, fastidious, often aesthetic or hygienic. Bridge: refinement pushed past comfort into anxious excess.
  • Editorial / scholarly: burdened with minor qualifications, pedantically over-qualified (fussy footnotes, a fussy process). Bridge: the annotator's disproportionate care applied to marginal detail.
Register is informal to neutral, warmer and more homely than fastidious or meticulous, and distinctly less condemnatory than pedantic. Fussy collocates with nouns denoting fine-grained objects of attention — eater, baby, details, design, clothes, footnotes — and with the prepositions about (fussy about his food), with, and over. Derived forms include fussiness (the abstract noun), fussily (the adverb), fusser (one who fusses), and the American fussbudget, a person who fusses excessively. The historical bridge is the noun fuss, "needless commotion or bustle over trifles," a word first recorded around 1701 in colloquial English, whose sound seems to imitate the puffing, blowing, fidgety energy of needless excitement; the adjective fussy, formed with the native suffix -y, is recorded from the 1770s. The word's whole family is expressive rather than Indo-European — fuss, fussy, and their kin are classed by lexicographers as imitative or sound-symbolic formations, coined again and again to capture the sound of restless agitation, and never traced to a PIE root.
