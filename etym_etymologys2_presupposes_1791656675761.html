Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*per- + *upo + *po-s(i)nere
"before; under; to place,
lay down, put aside"
↓
Latin (Classical era)
prae- + sub- + pōnere
"before; beneath; to put,
place, or lay down"
↓
Medieval Latin (c. 12th c. CE)
praesuppōnere
"to place under beforehand;
assume as a prior premise"
↓
Old French (14th c.)
presupposer
"to take for granted beforehand,
assume as an antecedent"
↓
Late Middle English (c. 1420s)
presupposen
"to assume beforehand in logic,
require as a prior condition"
↓
Early Modern English (16th c.)
presuppose
"to assume beforehand; require
as an antecedent condition"
↓
Modern English (3rd pers. sing. pres.)
presupposes
"requires or assumes as an
antecedent condition or truth"

Note: Latin 'praesupponere' is a complex scholastic compound of 'prae-' ('before'), 'sub-' ('under'), and 'ponere' ('to put, place'). In Late Latin and Romance, 'ponere' blended phonologically and semantically with Late Latin 'pausare' ('to cease, halt, pause', from Greek 'pausis'), yielding Old French 'poser' and English 'pose'. The 3rd-person singular present form 'presupposes' reflects standard Early Modern English verbal inflection replacing archaic '-eth'.
