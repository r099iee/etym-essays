relinquish (verb): to let go, give up, or part with — to surrender a hold, a right, a claim, or a possession, especially reluctantly or by deliberate act. In the Gormenghast passage the verb is used of Rottcodd's grip on the feather duster: 'he had never been known to relinquish the feather duster from his grasp.' The choice of relinquish rather than drop or let go is crucial: it elevates the physical act of holding a duster into a formal, almost legal renunciation. To relinquish is to release something only by a conscious act of the will, and Rottcodd's fingers, keeping their grip even through 'all his dozing', make his hold a habit so deep it has become involuntary. His post is his identity, and the duster is its emblem. The tone is comically grand: the ceremonial word is lavished on the smallest of objects, and the sleeping man's grip becomes a parody and yet a vindication of duty — the one thing never relinquished in a life given to sleep. Polysemy of 'relinquish' — the senses it carries today
  • Physical sense (core): to release or let go of a hold or grasp — 'he relinquished her hand', 'she refused to relinquish the duster'; the original and simplest sense, of parting with a grip.
  • Legal / property sense (prominent): to surrender a right, title, claim, interest or possession formally — 'to relinquish one's claim to the estate', 'to relinquish custody'; the courtroom and conveyancing register of the word.
  • Military / command sense: to give up a post, command, office or responsibility — 'the general relinquished his command'; formal surrender of authority, usually voluntary and orderly.
  • Personal / emotional sense (reluctant giving up): to part with a hope, ambition, dream or attachment, usually with regret — 'she relinquished her dream of dancing'; the word carries an ache of reluctance, the sense of giving up what one would rather keep.
  • Abstract / intellectual sense: to abandon a belief, position, hope or assumption — 'they relinquished the theory after the new evidence'; deliberate surrender of a mental hold.
  • Register: formal, deliberate, slightly ceremonial; unlike 'drop' it is never accidental, unlike 'abandon' it implies awareness and usually regret, and unlike 'surrender' it requires no adversary.
The semantic bridge is the will. Relinquish is the quiet, formal act of parting with something by a conscious decision — its Latin ancestor relinquō means literally 'to leave behind' (re-, 'back', + linquō, 'to leave'). Every sense — the grip, the right, the command, the dream — is the same act of deliberate, willed separation. The word sits at the centre of a large field of giving-up verbs, neither as moralising as abandon, nor as combative as surrender, nor as public as renounce — and that central, reluctant quality is exactly why Peake chooses it for Rottcodd: the sleeping man's grip on the duster is the body's refusal to perform the act that names his whole situation, a terminal commitment in which the leaving is forever postponed.
