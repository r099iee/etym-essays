- Classical Latin (*atrocitas*) - Signified a fierce, grim quality of mind, savage ferocity, or the horrifying, unrelenting harshness of a crime, disaster, or physical pain (derived from *ater*, "dark, black, gloomy").
- 16th Century (Renaissance English) - Adopted directly from Latin/French as an abstract singular quality (*atrocity*), denoting the supreme monstrousness, heinousness, or cruelty of an offense or treasonous plot.
- Late 18th Century (French Revolution & Enlightenment) - Began pluralizing (*atrocities*) in diplomatic, journalistic, and literary writing to describe specific, concrete events of organized butchery, terror, and mob savagery.
- 19th to Early 20th Century - Cemented in international law, military reporting, and literature (e.g. Tolstoy, British parliamentary reports) as the definitive term for war crimes, civilian massacres, and colonial abuses.
- Mid-20th Century to Present - Codified in humanitarian law (the Geneva Conventions, Rome Statute of the ICC) within "atrocity crimes" (genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity), while retaining a colloquial hyperbole in everyday speech for terribly offensive aesthetic or culinary failures.
