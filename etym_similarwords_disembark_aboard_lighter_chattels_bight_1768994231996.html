• Land: Similar to disembark in arriving on shore; differs because land is the general verb that can apply to the ship or the people alike, while disembark refers specifically to passengers leaving a vessel.
  • Debark: Similar to disembark in leaving a ship; differs because debark is a learned seventeenth-century synonym now mostly technical, while disembark remains the standard formal word.
  • Alight: Similar in getting down from a conveyance; differs because alight stresses the descending motion from a vehicle (or, archaically, a ship), while disembark marks the formal threshold of leaving a vessel for land.
  • Unload: Similar in removing from a vessel; differs because unload applies to cargo rather than people, whereas disembark is agent-focused — the passengers themselves stepping ashore.
  • Go ashore: Similar to disembark in leaving a ship for land; differs because go ashore is the plain, warm, spoken idiom, while disembark is the formal, bureaucratic, logbook word.
  • On board: Similar to aboard in meaning on a vessel; differs because on board is the analytic, slightly more formal equivalent, while aboard is the fused, traditional, more compact and nautical form.
  • On: Similar to aboard in indicating position; differs because on is the neutral general preposition, while aboard specifically names being on a vessel or vehicle.
  • Embarked: Similar to aboard as the boarding act; differs because embarked names the act of going onto a ship, while aboard is the resulting state or location — the difference between the verb and the preposition.
  • Barge: Similar to lighter in being a flat-bottomed cargo boat; differs because barge is the general term for large flat-bottomed freight boats, while lighter is the specific type that transfers cargo between ship and shore.
  • Ferry: Similar to lighter in being a boat for transfer; differs because a ferry carries people across a fixed route, while a lighter moves freight between a deep-draft ship and the quay.
  • Dinghy: Similar to lighter in being a small boat; differs because a dinghy is a small tender for passengers or ship's business, not a cargo flat for lightening a vessel's load.
  • Punt: Similar to lighter in being a flat-bottomed boat; differs because a punt is a shallow river boat poled along, not a harbor cargo vessel working ship-to-shore.
  • Belongings: Similar to chattels in being personal possessions; differs because belongings is the plain everyday word, while chattels is the technical legal term for movable property.
  • Possessions: Similar to chattels in things owned; differs because possessions is neutral and general, while chattels specifically distinguishes movable property from real property (land and buildings).
  • Effects: Similar to chattels in legal-administrative register; differs because personal effects is cooler and more bureaucratic, while chattels retains the old property-law and livestock history.
  • Goods: Similar to chattels in things of value; differs because goods is the commercial word for merchandise, while chattels is the legal word for movable personal property.
  • Assets: Similar to chattels in items of value; differs because assets implies value toward paying debts — a financial view — while chattels is about personal property and possession, not solvency.
  • Bay: Similar to bight in an indentation of the coastline; differs because a bay is larger and more enclosed, a body of water partly landlocked, while a bight is a shallower, more open curve of the coast itself.
  • Cove: Similar to bight in a coastal curve; differs because a cove is small, sheltered, and circular, while a bight is wide, shallow, and open to the sea.
  • Gulf: Similar to bight in a large coastal indentation; differs because a gulf is large, deep, and more enclosed, while a bight is broad, shallow, and sweeping.
  • Inlet: Similar to bight in the sea entering the land; differs because inlet emphasizes the opening and entry of water, while bight emphasizes the curvature of the coastline itself.
  • Loop: Similar to the nautical bight in a rope's curve; differs because loop is the general word for any closed curve, while the bight is the specialist seaman's term for the slack middle of a line.
