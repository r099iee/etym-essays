• Unerring: Similar because both mean never missing the mark or never making a mistake; differs because unerring stays close to practical performance (an unerring aim, an unerring instinct), while infallible makes the categorical claim that the person or principle cannot be wrong about truth and judgment.
  • Impeccable: Similar because both suggest a standard that cannot be faulted; differs because impeccable belongs to manners, taste, and style (impeccable breeding, impeccable timing) and says nothing about cognitive error.
  • Foolproof: Similar because both mean that failure is impossible; differs because foolproof applies to mechanisms and procedures so simple they cannot be misused, and rarely to persons or judgments.
  • Omniscient: Similar because both claim freedom from error; differs because omniscient is theologically reserved for a being who knows everything, whereas infallible only asserts that one cannot be wrong, not that one knows all.
  • Dependable: Similar because both describe that which can be relied on; differs because dependable is a modest, practical word for reliable behaviour, while infallible is an absolute claim to the impossibility of error.
  • Reliable: Similar because both describe something that can be trusted; differs because reliability admits occasional failure while infallibility denies it — an infallible thing is more than reliable.
  • Trustworthy: Similar because both justify trust; differs because trustworthy concerns honesty and character, while infallible concerns the impossibility of being wrong.
  • Certain / sure: Similar because both express absence of doubt; differs because these describe a state of mind or a high degree of probability, while infallible is an objective claim about the impossibility of error.
  • Inerrable: Similar because both are Latin-derived adjectives meaning incapable of error; differs because inerrable is the rarer, more technical doublet (chiefly theological), while infallible is the common word.
  • Fallible (antonym): Similar because both are formed on the same Latin stem and describe the capacity for error; differs because fallible means liable to err — the exact negation of infallible, and the word Mill saves with his “almost.”
  • Invulnerable: Similar because both deny the possibility of being harmed or refuted; differs because invulnerable is physical (cannot be wounded) or logical (cannot be attacked), while infallible is epistemic (cannot be wrong).
  • Perfect: Similar because both denote a state with no flaw; differs because perfect is far broader and often aesthetic or moral, while infallible is specific to error and judgment.
