- 4th–3rd c. BCE — Latin legio means “a levying,” the annual draft of Roman citizens; it is an administrative, seasonal word rather than a name for a permanent institution.
- 2nd–1st c. BCE — The manipular and then cohort legion becomes the backbone of Roman conquest; “legion” acquires prestige, discipline and the eagle standard.
- 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE — The professional imperial legion is a long-service unit with a number, a name and a provincial posting; to provincials the word means Rome itself.
- 4th–5th c. — Late Roman legions shrink and multiply; the word survives in the Notitia Dignitatum but the institution is transformed.
- 5th–15th c. — Latin legio passes into Old French and Middle English, chiefly in religious and historical writing; the Vulgate’s “Legion” of demons gives it a sinister, supernatural charge.
- 16th–18th c. — Renaissance historiography and the Authorized Version keep both senses alive: the Roman army and the countless host; “their name is legion” becomes a proverb.
- 19th c. — New formations borrow the name for prestige: the Polish Legions (1797), the French Foreign Legion (1831), and various volunteer legions in the wars of unification.
- 20th c. — The word serves veterans’ organisations (American Legion 1919, Royal British Legion 1921), a French order of merit, and, after 1976, a bacterium and a disease.
- 21st c. — “Legions” is common in fandom and gaming (“legions of fans,” Space Marine legions); the tone is grandiose and slightly archaic, and the Roman reference is often purely decorative.
