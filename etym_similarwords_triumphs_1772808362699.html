• Victories: Similar because both denote successes, especially military ones; differs because "victory" names the outcome of a battle or contest, while "triumph" names either the Roman ceremonial procession or, by extension, a success of special brilliance and exultation.
  • Conquests: Similar because both belong to the language of military success; differs because "conquest" stresses territorial acquisition and subjugation, while "triumph" stresses the celebratory ceremony and the glory of the return.
  • Ovation: Similar because both are Roman victory ceremonies granted to victorious generals; differs because the ovation was the lesser, diminished rite (on foot or horseback, without the full pomp), while a triumph was the supreme honour - hence "ovation" also has the modern meaning of sustained applause, which "triumph" does not.
  • Celebrations: Similar because both denote festive rejoicing; differs because "celebration" is a general term for any festive act, while "triumph" names a specific institutional ceremony and its atmosphere of victorious exultation.
  • Processions: Similar because both denote formal ceremonial parades; differs because "procession" is generic and may be religious, funereal, or civic, while "triumph" names the specifically military-Roman victory procession.
  • Pageants: Similar because both denote elaborate public spectacles; differs because "pageant" stresses theatrical display and may be fictional or civic, while "triumph" is a real historical ceremony with religious and political meaning.
  • Successes: Similar because both denote favourable outcomes; differs because "success" is colourless and general, while "triumph" implies a brilliant, glorious, or hard-won success that invites celebration.
  • Achievements: Similar because both denote things accomplished by effort; differs because "achievement" is neutral and everyday, while "triumph" implies a spectacular, celebrated accomplishment.
  • Exultations: Similar because both denote great joy at success; differs because "exultation" names the feeling itself, while "triumph" names the event or ceremony, though the "feeling" sense of triumph ("a note of triumph") overlaps.
  • Glory: Similar because both capture the martial grandeur of Roman expansion; differs because "glory" is an abstract state of honour and renown, while "triumph" is a concrete ritual and its celebration.
  • Coronation: Similar because both are grand ceremonies of supreme public honour; differs because "coronation" marks the accession of a monarch with a crown, while "triumph" celebrates a victorious general's return and involves no crown.
  • Laurels: Similar because both symbolise victory and its honours (the triumphing general wore laurel); differs because "laurels" is a metaphor for accumulated honours, while "triumphs" names the actual ceremonies and victories.
