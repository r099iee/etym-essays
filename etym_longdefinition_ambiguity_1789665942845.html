Ambiguity names the state in which a word, sentence, image, action or situation admits two or more distinct readings so that meaning cannot be settled without further context or decision. It is both a property of language and a condition of experience: a contract clause, a poem, a glance or a political promise can all harbour ambiguity. Unlike a simple mistake, ambiguity is structural; the form itself supports multiple construals, and the uncertainty troubles literal-minded readers precisely because no single paraphrase exhausts it.

In the source sentence the device created not just the layer of ambiguity troubling literal-minded readers but also a remarkable universality. Here ambiguity is productive, not defective. By refusing single reference, the text stays open to different times and places, allowing many readers to enter. That literary sense depends on the core linguistic sense but adds an aesthetic valuation: ambiguity as richness rather than failure.

  • Primary linguistic sense: lexical and structural multiple meaning. Lexical ambiguity where one word has two senses, as in bank or light; syntactic ambiguity where structure branches, as in visiting relatives can be boring; semantic-pragmatic ambiguity where reference or scope is indeterminate. Bridge: from Latin ambiguus wavering, going both ways, to the image of language itself wavering between paths.
  • Secondary senses: vagueness, equivocation and strategic opacity. In law and bureaucracy ambiguity voids or endangers meaning and must be construed against the drafter. In diplomacy and management it can be deliberate constructive ambiguity that lets parties agree while disagreeing. In psychology and organisational theory tolerance or intolerance of ambiguity describes comfort with uncertain situations. Bridge: extension from form supporting two readings to agents exploiting or enduring that openness.
  • Tertiary literary, philosophical and scientific senses. Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity in 1930 canonised ambiguity as the engine of poetic density. Existentialism and post-structuralism treat ambiguity as ontological, as in de Beauvoir and Merleau-Ponty. In information theory and computing, ambiguity contrasts with entropy and with underspecification that a parser must resolve. Queer and cultural theory reclaim ambiguity as resistance to binaries. Bridge: generalisation from local double meaning to a worldview in which meaning is constitutively plural.
  • Related distinctions. Ambiguity differs from vagueness where borderlines are fuzzy rather than branching, from polysemy which names the lexical capacity that creates ambiguity in use, from ambivalence which is divided feeling rather than divided meaning, and from obscurity which blocks meaning altogether. Skilled usage keeps these apart while recognising their family resemblance.
  • Collocation and register. Neutral to formal, countable and uncountable: an ambiguity, layers of ambiguity, fraught with ambiguity, riddled with ambiguity, mired in ambiguity. Verbs that govern it reveal attitude: exploit, preserve and cherish versus remove, resolve, eliminate and clarify. Moral colour depends on project: science and law seek to reduce it, poetry and diplomacy often cultivate it.

To grasp ambiguity fully is to see why the same property can trouble one reader and liberate another. For the literal-minded it breaks the contract that words should point one way; for the literary it keeps the work alive across contexts, conferring the universality the source sentence celebrates.
