- [15th-century 'seize wrongfully'] - In its current form since Middle English (from Old French encrochier, 'to seize'), 'encroachment' first meant wrongful seizure, especially the gradual taking of another's land by creeping beyond bounds.
- [Legal and agrarian sense] - In law and rural life it named the stealthy extension of a fence, wall or claim over a neighbour's land - a measured, provable overstepping.
- [Constitutional metaphor] - In 17th and 19th century political prose it became the key word for the gradual, illegitimate extension of royal or state power - encroachments on liberty - the archetypal abuse of the Revolution settlement.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'encroachments' still denotes both the physical and the political creeping advance, its sense of slow, nibbling illegitimacy fully intact.
