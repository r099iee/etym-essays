SNOBBERY is a noun denoting the attitude, behaviour, or disposition of a snob: the assumption of social, intellectual, or cultural superiority, expressed as disdain, condescension, or exclusion towards those judged inferior in rank, taste, education, wealth, or refinement. It is an abstract formation — snob + the suffix -ery (“the behaviour or practice of”) — coined in the early nineteenth century and generalized in the wake of Thackeray’s The Book of Snobs (1848). In the sentence “His sheer snobbery was evident to all,” the word labels an observable, outward demeanour: an unmistakable air of looking down on others, apparent at a glance rather than inferred from any single act.

Primary senses.

  • Social snobbery: contempt and condescension based on class, birth, wealth, title, or position; the classic sense, in which a person esteems themselves above others by rank and despises those below.
  • Intellectual snobbery: condescension grounded in education, learning, or perceived mental ability; the intellectual snob despises what it judges ignorant or vulgar in the world of ideas.
  • Aesthetic / cultural snobbery: disdain rooted in taste — in art, music, literature, theatre, food, and wine — where the snob asserts a superior refinement.
  • Taste-based snobbery in general: the modern dominant use, extended to nearly every area of discernment: coffee snobbery, craft-beer snobbery, film snobbery, cycling snobbery, and so on.

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Sociological jargon: snobbery as a mechanism of class distinction and boundary-marking; in sociology it is studied as the assertion of status and the exclusion of outsiders, closely linked to the idea of cultural capital and distinction.
  • Inverted / reverse snobbery: pride in one’s own plainness and contempt for the refined; the reverse snob despises the posh and the cultivated for their refinement itself.
  • Institutional snobbery: the exclusiveness of an organization — a club, a college, a firm — which cultivates an air of superiority to outsiders.
  • Attributive and idiomatic use: “pure snobbery”, “dreadful snobbery”, “middle-class snobbery”, “the snobbery of the old school”; also snobbishness (the state) and snobbism (the doctrine).

Nuances of register and tone. The word is clinical in sociology, mocking in satire, and indignant in polemic; it frequently collocates with sheer, pure, and mere, which intensify its contemptuous force, and it can be turned against oneself in self-deprecating humour.

Historical semantic bridge. An 18th-century dialect word snob (“a shoemaker; a cobbler’s apprentice”) shifted in Cambridge slang to mean “a townsman, a non-university man”, then in the early nineteenth century to “a person who vulgarly admires and apes the gentry”, and finally to “one who looks down on those considered inferior in taste or class”. The abstract noun snobbery generalized the last sense, and its rise tracked the anxieties of an aspirational middle class defining itself against both the aristocracy above and the working class below.
