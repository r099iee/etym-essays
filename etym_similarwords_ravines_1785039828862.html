• Gorge: Similar because both are steep-sided gaps in the land; differs because a gorge is wider and usually carries a stream along its floor, while a ravine is a sheer, torrent-cut cleft that may be dry.
  • Canyon: Similar because both are deep, steep-walled valleys; differs because a canyon is grander still and carries American Southwestern associations (from Spanish cañón, 'tube'), a scale beyond the ravine.
  • Gully: Similar because both are water-carved channels; differs because a gully is smaller and more modest — often leapable — whereas a ravine is too wide to jump and too sheer to climb.
  • Chasm: Similar because both are deep gaps in the earth; differs because a chasm is a broad rift, more geological than water-made, with no necessary connection to running water.
  • Defile: Similar because both are narrow passes through difficult terrain; differs because a defile is a pass that armies must traverse single-file, implying a route through, while a ravine is an impassable barrier.
  • Crevasse: Similar because both are deep, steep-sided fissures; differs because a crevasse is strictly a fissure in a glacier, not a water-cut gorge in rock.
  • Gulch: Similar because both are narrow water-worn channels; differs because gulch is a New World (American frontier) term, generally smaller and less sheer than a ravine.
  • Couloir: Similar because both are steep, narrow, channel-like features in mountain terrain; differs because a couloir (French, 'corridor') is a deep mountain gully, often snow-filled, a mountaineering term rather than a general word.
  • Arroyo: Similar because both are channels cut by water in dry country; differs because an arroyo is a desert watercourse, usually dry and broad-floored, with none of the ravine's steepness or depth.
  • Gulf: Similar because both descend from the root meaning 'to seize' (gulf via Greek kolpos; ravine via Latin rapere) and both name large openings; differs because 'gulf' is an arm of the sea or a wide gap, on a far grander scale and not water-cut in rock.
