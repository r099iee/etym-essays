Ancient Greek (c. 4th c. BCE)
ἐπιτομή (epitomḗ)
"a cutting short; abridgment, summary"
↓
Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
epitomē
"literary abridgment, digest"
↓
Middle French (15th c.)
epitome
"summary, compendium"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1520s–present)
epitome
"perfect embodiment or archetype;"
"a concise summary or abridgment"

Note: Greek ἐπιτομή combines ἐπί (epí, "upon, at, besides") and τέμνειν (témnein, "to cut"). The semantic transition from "cutting short a text" (abridgment) to "the quintessential embodiment of a type" occurred because an abridgment extracts and concentrates the pure essence of a sprawling volume into a single compact form.
