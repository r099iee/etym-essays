descent names both blood moving down through time and bodies moving down through space: lineage, ancestry, extraction on the one hand; a going down, slope, fall, raid or moral decline on the other. In the source sentence — Ruskin, I have never been able to trace these prejudices to any royalty of descent — it is the genealogical sense, pure and pointed. He cannot derive his Tory royalism from royal blood: of his father’s ancestors he knows nothing, of his mother’s only a Croydon inn-keeping grandmother. Royalty of descent would be kingship by pedigree; his royalty is by affection, reading and yearning. The phrase turns pedigree inside out — prejudice without descent, loyalty without lineage.

Outside genealogy descent remains one of English’s great vertical words. Parachutists make descents, rivers descend in rapids, roads descend steeply, property descends to heirs, manners suffer a descent into vulgarity, armies make descents (raids) on coasts. The noun mirrors the verb descend in all its branches, while keeping a dignity the verb sometimes lacks: a descent can be stately (the descent of the choir from the rood-loft), technical (rate of descent, angle of descent), or fateful (the Descent of Man, Christ’s descent into Hell).

  • Core genealogical sense: lineage, ancestry, extraction: e.g. of Norman descent, to trace one’s descent from Vikings, royalty of descent. This is the source sense and a head sense. The semantic bridge from Latin descendere (climb down) is genealogical diagram: generations are steps downward from founder to child, as on a family tree read top to bottom. Adjectives specify: direct, lineal, maternal, paternal, royal, humble descent. Law distinguishes descent (inheritance by blood, heir-at-law) from purchase or devise; biology speaks of descent with modification (Darwin).
  • Spatial downward-movement sense: going down, slope, drop: e.g. a steep descent to the village, the balloon’s descent, a descent of 500 metres. Countable, often with measurements. The bridge is literal Latin: de- (down) + scandere (climb) — to climb down. Aviation, mountaineering and hydrology quantify it (rate, angle, gradient); tourism waymarks it (descent to the beach, descent from the pass).
  • Military-raid sense: sudden attack from the sea (a descent upon): e.g. to make a descent on the coast, Viking descents on England. Now literary-historical. The bridge is downward from ships to shore, then suddenness: troops descend on a beach as hawks descend on prey. Chronicles use it for Vikings, French and Spaniards; modern prose prefers landing, raid, invasion.
  • Moral-intellectual decline sense: fall, degeneration, bathos: e.g. a descent into chaos, his descent from scholar to propagandist, a descent from the sublime to the ridiculous. Often with into. The bridge is vertical metaphor: good is up, bad is down, so worsening is going down. Theology (the Fall, descent into sin), literary criticism (bathos as descent) and journalism (descent into recession) share it.
  • Theological and literary fossils: the Descent (of Christ) into Hell / Hades (the Harrowing of Hell between Crucifixion and Resurrection); Darwin’s Descent of Man (1871, man’s derivation by evolution); descent of the Holy Ghost at Pentecost. Each keeps capital-letter dignity and shows how high doctrine borrows a plain down-word.
  • Contrasts and kin: ascent is the upward opposite; descendant is the person descended (note -ant vs -ent); descend is the verb; descender is a climber’s device and a typographic tail (g, p, y). To be descended from is blood; to descend to may be condescension (he descended to abuse) or inheritance (the estate descended to her).
