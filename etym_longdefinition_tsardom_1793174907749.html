Tsardom is the realm, reign, or dominion of a tsar: in the strict historical sense the Russian state under its autocratic sovereigns, from Ivan IV's coronation as tsar in 1547 to the empire Peter the Great proclaimed in 1721, and by extension any autocratic domain ruled with tsar-like absoluteness. The word fuses the Slavic sovereign-title tsar with the Germanic domain-suffix -dom, the same ending that builds kingdom and Christendom, so that it parses transparently as the tsar's domain, a kingdom with a Russian crown on it.

In the source sentence — the newly married pair set out for the Hawk's Tsardom — the sense is frankly fabulous rather than constitutional. The Hawk, a shape-shifting bridegroom of the wonder-tale, rules his own named realm beyond the mortal maps, and his tsardom is one of those glittering elsewhere-kingdoms of folklore: reached by quest, bounded by enchantment, and governed by beings who wear bird-shape as easily as robes. Here tsardom does world-building work, telling the listener that the Hawk is no mere bird but a sovereign, with a court, a territory, and a princess-bride to carry home to it.

Core meaning in detail: Historians use Tsardom — often capitalised as the Tsardom of Russia or Tsardom of Muscovy — for the centralised Muscovite state that shook off the Mongol yoke, gathered the Russian lands under Moscow, and expanded across Siberia under an ideology of autocracy and Orthodoxy, the so-called Third Rome. In this usage it denotes both a period (1547–1721) and a polity: the chancelleries, the streltsy musketeers, the prikaz offices, the bearded boyars, the whole machinery of early modern Russian rule before Petrine Westernisation renamed it an empire.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The Russian state under the tsars (primary historical sense, 1547–1721, extended to 1917): the Muscovite and imperial polity as a unit of rule — its territory, administration, and ideology of autocracy. The bridge is compositional: tsar plus -dom, exactly as king plus -dom makes kingdom.
  • A fairy-tale realm ruled by a tsar-like sovereign (literary sense, the tale's own): the Hawk's tsardom, the thrice-ninth kingdom, any enchanted elsewhere with a crowned head. The bridge runs through folklore's habit of dignifying every wonderland-king with the highest title the audience knows.
  • The period or condition of a tsar's reign (temporal sense): as in during Ivan's fearsome tsardom, meaning his rule, his time on the throne. The bridge is the same one that lets reign mean both the ruling and the years ruled.
  • Any absolute or oppressive regime (figurative, journalistic): as in the little tsardom he runs at the office, a petty autocracy of memos and moods. The bridge is caricature: the historical absoluteness of the tsars becomes a template for every unchecked boss.
  • The sphere or world of Russian autocracy as a cultural idea (abstract sense): writers speak of the tsardom the way they speak of Christendom, a whole civilisation under one sign. The bridge is the -dom suffix at its most expansive, naming not acres but a way of life.

Register and connotation: Tsardom is historical and literary, faintly exotic in English mouths — historians use it precisely, journalists use it to conjure onion domes and secret police, and storytellers use it to gild a realm in a single word. Its connotations split with its referent: the fairy-tale tsardom glitters, the historical one broods with knouts, exiles, and Siberian snows. To call a modern office a tsardom is never praise; it accuses the ruler of backward, personal, unaccountable power.
