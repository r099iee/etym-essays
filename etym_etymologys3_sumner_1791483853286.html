The history of Sumner is the history of an office that became a surname and then a doctrine. Its earliest recoverable layer is Latin. The verb monēre, “to warn, remind, advise”, belongs to a large Indo-European family built on the reconstructed root *men-, “to think”; the same root underlies English mind, mental and comment. With the prefix sub-, Latin formed submonēre, “to remind quietly, put in mind, suggest”, and in legal and administrative usage “to cite, summon”. The agent noun passed into Gallo-Romance and Anglo-Norman as somoneor, the officer who summoned parties to an ecclesiastical court.

The medieval office. In England the summoner was an apparitor: a lay official of the consistory court who delivered citations, collected fees and reported offenders against morals. The role was necessary and widely detested. Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (c. 1387–1400) give the type its permanent literary portrait: the Summoner with his fire-red cherub's face, his few Latin tags, his readiness to take wine and pennies in exchange for silence. The General Prologue's famous line, “A somonour was ther with us in that place”, fixed the spelling somonour, while everyday Middle English shortened it to sumner. The surname arose exactly as other occupational surnames did: a man was called “William the sumner” and his children inherited the label.

  • Spelling and distribution. Medieval records show Sumner, Sumnour, Somonour and Somner. The form Sumner concentrated in East Anglia and the Midlands, and it is from this stock that the New England families descend.
  • Social connotation. Because the office was associated with spying, fees and moral policing, the name began life with a satirical edge. That edge is invisible today, but it explains why the word never became a neutral occupational noun like Baker or Smith.
  • Transatlantic transfer. Puritan and later emigration carried the surname to Massachusetts, Connecticut and Maine in the seventeenth century, where it lost its English administrative associations and became simply a respectable family name.

Charles Sumner and the moralisation of the name. The first American bearer to achieve world fame was Charles Sumner (1811–1874), the Massachusetts senator, orator and abolitionist. His speech “The Crime against Kansas” (1856) provoked Representative Preston Brooks's cane attack in the Senate chamber, an assault that turned Sumner into a martyr of the antislavery cause. During Reconstruction he led the Radical Republicans and fought for civil rights legislation. After his death the name Sumner in American public life connoted militant moral principle, and several towns and counties were named for him.

William Graham Sumner and the invention of a vocabulary. The second and more durable fame belongs to Charles's unrelated namesake, William Graham Sumner. Born in Paterson, New Jersey, in 1840, he was educated at Yale and in Europe, ordained an Episcopal priest, and then abandoned the ministry for social science. From 1872 until 1909 he taught at Yale, where his lectures on currency, protection, land tenure and custom became legendary. His early reputation rested on economic orthodoxy: A History of American Currency (1874), What Social Classes Owe to Each Other (1883), and the essay “The Forgotten Man” (1883), which argued that organised charity and protective legislation transfer burdens to the unnoticed, self-supporting citizen.

  • “The forgotten man.” The phrase is Sumner's most lasting coinage in public rhetoric. It has been borrowed by politicians of very different persuasions, usually with the meaning reversed: where Sumner meant the sober taxpayer, later users meant the poor and excluded.
  • Folkways (1906). His masterpiece studies how repeated solutions to the problems of hunger, sex, fear and vanity become habit, then custom, then coercive morality. From this analysis come the terms folkways, mores, in-group, out-group and ethnocentrism — a vocabulary that entered sociology, anthropology and ordinary educated speech.
  • Anti-imperialism. In “The Conquest of the United States by Spain” (1898) Sumner turned his individualism against the Spanish-American War, arguing that empire would corrupt the republic at home. The essay complicates the stock portrait of him as a mere apologist for power.
  • Posthumous system. His notes were edited by Albert Galloway Keller as The Science of Society (1927–28), a four-volume monument that kept his name before a new generation.

Reputation and semantic drift. The phrase “social Darwinism” was not Sumner's own; it became attached to him in the twentieth century, above all through Richard Hofstadter's Social Darwinism in American Thought (1944). Hofstadter's portrait made “Sumner” a byword for the claim that the poor are biologically unfit and that charity is therefore harmful. Later historians and libertarian writers have argued that this reading flattens a more complex thinker: Sumner distinguished the struggle against nature from competition among persons, condemned militarism, and described custom with genuine relativism. The dispute is itself part of the word's modern meaning. To invoke Sumner is to invoke a quarrel about how far society should be engineered.

Literary and commemorative afterlife. Sumner's own prose — epigrammatic, combative, fond of the second person — survives in quotation dictionaries, especially the line that “the forgotten man” pays for every scheme. His name appears in the toponymy of at least five American states, in the titles of sociology courses, and in the given names of children whose parents never read him. The singer Sting, born Gordon Sumner, gave the word a wholly new, pop-cultural resonance in the late twentieth century. In all these uses the medieval summoner is invisible, yet the long descent from a hated court officer to a contested intellectual brand is exactly what makes the name a small model of semantic history.
