- Late 19th Century (1880s–1890s): Emerged in Late Victorian British colloquial and public-school slang from the dialectal verb *footle* (to potter about, trifle, bungle), describing silly, futile, or trivial actions.
- Early 20th Century (Edwardian & Interwar Era): Rose to widespread popularity in British literary prose, drama, and detective fiction (e.g., Arthur Conan Doyle, P.G. Wodehouse, Dorothy L. Sayers), describing clumsy investigative bumbling, foolish bureaucracy, or petty social squabbles.
- Mid-20th Century: Maintained a strong presence in British satirical journalism and upper-middle-class vernacular as an expressive term of exasperated dismissiveness toward bureaucratic timewasting.
- Late 20th to 21st Century: Preserved primarily in British, Commonwealth, and literary English as a charming, sharply expressive descriptor for petty bureaucratic interference, trivial administrative debates, and hopelessly inept management.
