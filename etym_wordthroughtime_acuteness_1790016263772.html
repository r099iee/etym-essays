- Antiquity–1300s: sharpened point — acutus means whetted, pointed; needles, swords, stakes; perception is craft and war, the whetstone virtue.
- Late 1300s–1600s: learned borrowing — English acute enters medicine and geometry for sharp disease and sharp angle; acuteness names the property; perception is scholastic, Latinate, physicians and schoolmen distinguishing acute from chronic, obtuse from acute.
- 1600s–1800s: wit and sense — acuteness of wit, of hearing, of sight becomes praise; Enlightenment optics, acoustics, and essayists celebrate penetrating minds and keen senses; perception is complimentary, the sharp man sees furthest.
- 1800s–1900s: pain and crisis — Romantic and Victorian prose fixes acuteness of suffering, acuteness of distress, acuteness of want; medicine formalises acute care; perception is sympathetic and urgent, sharpness as something to relieve.
- Contemporary: analytic intensity — acuteness of crisis, of shortage, of competition dominates news and social science; alongside acuteness of mind as cognitive praise; perception is diagnostic, measuring how pointed and pressing a condition is.
