Dr Attridge is being credited with bringing "a well-practised expertise" to a daunting enterprise - an editorial and scholarly undertaking so large, exacting, or intellectually forbidding that it would intimidate most academics before they even began. The adjective does not merely say "difficult"; it measures the task's power to frighten or deter the would-be labourer, so that the praise that follows ("well-practised expertise") acquires real force: only practised skill can tame something that would daunt lesser hands. The tone is admiring, even slightly awed - the author's achievement is staged as a victory over an obstacle that appears overwhelming at first sight. The word carries a hidden metaphor preserved from its Latin ancestry. Daunt descends from Latin domitare, "to tame, to break in, to keep under control" - the verb used of taming wild animals. A daunting task, in the deepest etymological life of the word, is a wild thing that must be broken in and subdued; that is precisely what Attridge's practised expertise does. PRIMARY SENSES OF DAUNTING TODAY:
  • Intimidating and discouraging, of tasks and prospects: a daunting task, challenge, prospect, enterprise or undertaking is one so large, hard, or forbidding that it deters people from attempting it - the central modern sense, and the sense of the present passage.
  • Of situations and environments: a daunting landscape, a daunting crowd, a daunting array of data - the external scene or quantity that imposes itself and discourages action.
  • In business, sport, and organisational life: a "daunting target" or "daunting goal" is an ambitious objective that demands high skill and self-discipline; a "daunting schedule" or "daunting itinerary" is one that appears overwhelming in its demands. In leadership and sports psychology the word is used of challenges that elicit rational hesitation and demand preparation rather than fear.
  • Of quantities and volumes: "a daunting amount of work", "a daunting volume of material" - the sense of being dwarfed by sheer quantity (as in our context's editorial enterprise).
  • Of persons and qualities: a daunting opponent, a daunting reputation, a daunting intellect - someone or something so commanding that others hesitate to engage with them; the focus is on the respect and hesitation produced rather than on any threat of harm.
  • Mild or joking use: in everyday speech the word is often softened - "a daunting list of chores", "a daunting menu" - where the intimidation is trivial but the shape of the meaning (a thing that makes you hesitate before beginning) is preserved.
  • The related verb daunt: "nothing daunted" (idiom, "undismayed"), "to daunt someone" (to discourage, intimidate, or make fearful) - and the archaic positive senses of the verb family, "to subdue, to tame, to overcome" (now largely lost except in the negative adjective dauntless, "fearless, unsubduable").
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE - FROM TAMING BEASTS TO DAUNTING TASKS: The semantic path runs from the stable to the mind. Latin domitare (frequentative of domare, "to tame") meant "to keep on taming, to subdue again and again" - the dogged, iterative struggle of breaking a wild animal's will. Old French danter/dompter carried this into the medieval world with the senses "to tame, conquer, quell", and Middle English da(u)nten (c. 1300) meant "to subdue, vanquish, overawe". The decisive shift came when the object of the verb changed from beasts to human spirits: to "daunt" a person came to mean to bring their courage down, to overawe or intimidate them. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries daunt meant "to frighten, discourage, dishearten", and the participle daunting (first recorded c. 1656) settled into its modern adjectival role: that which daunts. A daunting thing is therefore, by etymology, a thing that must be broken in - a task wild enough to resist, needing a practised hand to tame it. Nuance against near-synonyms: "intimidating" stresses the fear or loss of confidence a person or situation provokes, often with a personal, almost threatening colour; "formidable" emphasises the respect and dread commanded by sheer power, excellence, or magnitude; "overwhelming" suggests being flooded, swamped, or engulfed by sheer volume or emotion; "discouraging" focuses on the effect of sapping hope or heart; "frightening" is blunter and more visceral. Daunting is distinct in combining genuine difficulty with a calm, rational hesitation - it makes you pause and steel yourself rather than flee. Success against a daunting task, as in our passage, is therefore a genuine achievement of skill: the practised expert who can "tame" what would daunt lesser hands.
