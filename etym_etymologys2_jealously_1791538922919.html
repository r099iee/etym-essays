Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*yā- / *yeh₂-
"to be eager, seek eagerly, revere"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th c. BCE)
ζῆλος (zēlos)
"ardor, fervent zeal, eager rivalry"
↓
Hellenistic Greek (c. 3rd c. BCE)
ζηλόω (zēloō)
"to emulate, envy, be zealous for"
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th c. CE)
zelosus
"full of zeal, ardent, passionate"
↓
Gallo-Romance / Old French (c. 12th c.)
gelos / jalous
"keen, zealous, possessively watchful"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th c.)
ielous / jelous
"protective, suspicious of rivalry"
↓
Middle English (c. late 14th c.)
ielousliche / jelously
"zealously, with watchful vigilance"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
jealously
"with possessive vigilance or envy"
↓
Modern English (present)
jealously
"with fierce protective vigilance,
or with envious resentment"

Note: 'Jealous' and 'zealous' form an etymological doublet. 'Jealous' entered English via Norman French in the 13th century, undergoing the palatalization of initial /z/ to /dʒ/, whereas 'zealous' was borrowed directly from Late Latin 'zelosus' during the 16th-century Renaissance.
