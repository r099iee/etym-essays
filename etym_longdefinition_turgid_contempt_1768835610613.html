turgid — swollen, distended, or puffed up beyond the natural measure, as if with fluid or internal pressure; literally describing a part of the body (a vein, an organ, a limb) abnormally and tautly engorged; and metaphorically describing anything that has become thick, heavy, and pressurised — an atmosphere, a body of water, a style, an utterance. The word descends from Latin turgidus, 'swollen,' from the verb turgēre, 'to be swollen, to swell,' and it carries with it the physics of engorgement: a turgid thing is taut with content, stretched tight from within, at the very point of strain.

Senses of turgid

  • Physical — of bodies and parts: a turgid vein, a turgid limb, a turgid organ — distended, tautly swollen with fluid; the literal sense, still the first meaning in medical contexts.
  • Botanical / scientific: plant cells are said to be turgid when filled with water to the point of firmness; turgor (from Latin turgor, 'a swelling') names the internal fluid pressure that keeps a plant upright — a key technical term in botany and cell biology.
  • Of water and weather: a turgid river or stream is swollen, in full flood.
  • Figurative — of language and style (the modern, dominant sense): turgid prose, a turgid speech, turgid rhetoric — bombastic, inflated, pompously ornate, swollen with words beyond their content; the stylistic sense treats style as a body puffed up beyond natural measure.
  • Figurative — of atmosphere and feeling: an air, a silence, or a mood 'turgid with' emotion — thickened, distended, saturated with suppressed feeling, on the verge of bursting; the sense used in the passage ('the air between them was turgid with contempt and jealousy').
  • Historical bridge: from the body's swelling to anything puffed up beyond natural measure — the shift runs from physical engorgement to stylistic inflation to atmospheric pressure; all three share the image of a thing stretched taut from within.
contempt — the feeling or attitude of regarding something as worthless, beneath consideration, or beneath notice; a settled, often cold and superior disregard that cuts the object off from serious value. The etymology is a vivid cut: Latin contemnere, 'to scorn, despise,' was formed from the intensive prefix con- and temnere, 'to slight, to despise' — a verb traditionally connected (though not without debate) with the Greek root for 'to cut' (τέμνω, témnō), so that to hold in contempt is to cut a thing off from consideration, to sever its worth.

Senses of contempt

  • Emotional / attitudinal: the settled judgement of worthlessness — 'she looked at him with utter contempt'; colder and more judicial than mere dislike, quieter and more final than open scorn.
  • Legal (contempt of court): the institutionalised sense — conduct that defies or insults the authority of a court, punishable as an offence; contempt becomes not only private feeling but public judgement, a recorded verdict.
  • Beneath contempt: the idiom pushes the idea to its floor — so worthless that even contempt is too honourable a response.
  • Derivatives: contemptuous (feeling or showing contempt), contemptible (deserving contempt), contemptibly; the family keeps the sharp cut of the root.
  • Historical bridge: from the Latin image of cutting off (a person cut off from consideration), the word hardened into the formal attitude of the Roman courtroom and the Christian moral tradition, then into the legal English phrase 'contempt of court'; the modern sense still carries the cold finality of a sentence passed.

In the passage

  • The two words fuse: the air is turgid — swollen, distended, pressurised — with contempt and jealousy. The craftsmen 'stood about like beggars, their families clustered in silent groups,' and the turgidity is the accumulated pressure of that silent, resentful crowd: an atmosphere so full of unspoken contempt that it has become a substance, a taut body on the verge of bursting. Turgid gives the feeling a body; contempt gives it its cold, cutting edge.
