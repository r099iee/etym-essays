Covenant is a noun denoting a solemn, binding agreement, promise, or pact, especially one undertaken with gravity, formality, and moral or sacred weight. To covenant is to do more than contract; it is to pledge oneself, often before God, community, or honour, to a course that constrains future choice. Marriages, divine promises, constitutional foundings, and land obligations are all called covenants when speakers wish to stress that breaking them would be betrayal, not mere breach.

  • Core general sense: solemn agreement: A mutual undertaking sealed by oath, signature, or ritual, e.g. a covenant of peace, a covenant to protect the forest. It implies deliberation, publicity, and endurance; covenants are made to last generations, not to expire next quarter.
  • Biblical-theological sense: In Judaism and Christianity, God's pledged relationship with humanity: the Noahic covenant (never again to flood), Abrahamic covenant (descendants and land), Mosaic/Sinaitic covenant (law and obedience), Davidic covenant (eternal dynasty), New Covenant in Christ's blood. Here covenant translates Hebrew berith (bond, cutting — from cutting sacrificial animals) and Greek diatheke (disposition, testament). It is unilateral in grace yet bilateral in obligation.
  • Legal-property sense: In common law, a promise under seal in a deed, enforceable by action: restrictive covenant (limiting land use), affirmative covenant (requiring action), covenant of quiet enjoyment, covenant not to compete, financial covenants in loans. Breach sounds in damages and injunction. Modern conveyancing still runs on covenants that bind successors, not just signatories.
  • Historical-political sense: Covenanter movement: Capitalised Covenant refers to the Scottish National Covenant (1638) and Solemn League and Covenant (1643), and by extension their signatories, the Covenanters, who suffered persecution under the Stuarts. To invoke covenant in Scottish or Ulster memory is to invoke martyrdom for conscience.
  • Blood-covenant and kinship sense: In anthropology, a rite in which parties become kin by mingling, drinking, or smearing blood, as in Durant's a rite or covenant. Blood-covenant creates artificial brotherhood: of one blood, mutually bound to avenge and succour. Frazer and others saw in it the origin of all oath-taking.
  • Covenant vs. contract vs. treaty vs. vow vs. testament: Contract is commercial and enforceable, cool and reciprocal; treaty is between states; vow is personal and often religious, usually unilateral; testament is a disposition of will or witness; covenant uniquely joins legal force with sacred kinship, implying enduring loyalty beyond consideration.
  • Verb use: to covenant: Formal and somewhat archaic as a verb (they covenanted to pay yearly), now largely replaced by agree, undertake, promise, but retained in deeds (the lessee covenants to repair) and liturgy (we covenant together). Past forms covenanted, covenanting carry solemn stiffness.
  • Register and collocations: Elevated, biblical-legal register. Collocates with solemn, sacred, binding, breach, keep, enter into, sign, seal. Adjectives like ancient, everlasting, new signal theology; restrictive, affirmative signal property law.

Durant's pairing — sometimes as medicine, sometimes as a rite or covenant — maps three primitive theories of blood-drinking: as physic (it heals), as worship (it connects to powers), as social bond (it makes kin). As covenant, the draught is not about nutrition or even communion with gods but about horizontal obligation: drinkers share substance and therefore share fate. The victim's vital force becomes common property, and with it mutual liability. Modern readers who sign mortgages with covenants, join churches with covenants, or hear politicians invoke covenants with voters are participating, in attenuated form, in the same logic: shared words, shared substance (ink, bread, blood), shared future. Covenant dignifies agreement into kinship.
