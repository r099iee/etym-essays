- 16th to 17th Century - Originates in Early Modern French as a diminutive of *papier* (*paperolle*); primarily utilized in convent handicrafts to describe narrow strips of gilded paper rolled around quills to imitate precious metal filigree in reliquaries (quilling).
- 18th to 19th Century - Develops a colloquial, slightly pejorative domestic register across France; used by domestic servants, clerks, and housekeepers to dismiss small scraps of paper, notes, wastepaper slips, or annoying household clutter.
- 1913 to 1927 - Immortalized by Marcel Proust in *À la recherche du temps perdu* (published across these years); fictionalized as the housekeeper Françoise’s affectionate yet dismissive name for the narrator’s glued manuscript strips.
- Mid-20th Century - Adopted by French literary scholars, philologists, and editors (notably Pierre Clarac, André Ferré, and Jean-Yves Tadié) preparing the definitive Pléiade editions of Proust's works, elevating the word into an essential scholarly term.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Institutionalized within international genetic criticism (*critique génétique*) and BnF archival conservation; *paperoles* becomes a celebrated universal emblem of modular, collage-like modernist literary composition.
