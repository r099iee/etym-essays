[No PIE root — the origin is unknown, probably expressive or imitative; standard authorities (OED, Etymonline) mark "baffle" as of uncertain origin, and the chain therefore begins at the earliest attested material] Expressive / onomatopoeic core: Old French baffe "a slap, a blow" (attested 12th–13th c.; imitative, from the sound of a blow) → Old French bafouer "to mock, deride, abuse" (verbal derivative of the blow-word: to mock someone is to strike them verbally) → Scots / early modern English baffeil, baffell, bauchle "to disgrace publicly, to baffle" (15th–16th c.; borrowing of the mocking-verb family) → English to baffle "to subject to public disgrace or infamy" (c. 1540s; the ritual degradation of a perjured knight) → English to baffle "to bewilder, confound, frustrate" (sense-shift attested by 1649) → baffled (past participle, Modern English).

DEBATE / NUANCE NOTE: There is NO established PIE etymology. The link to Old French baffe / bafouer via Scottish intermediaries (baffeil, bauchle) is the leading hypothesis but is not certain; the OED records the Scottish forms without fully committing. A parallel technical line — 17th-c. nautical "to baffle the wind" (to waste the wind's force by trimming the sails) — produced the mechanical noun "baffle," a plate that checks or deflects a current; this line shares the verb's "check, defeat" sense and is documented alongside the moral one.
