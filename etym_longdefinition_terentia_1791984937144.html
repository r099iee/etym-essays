Terentia (c. 98 BCE – 4 CE; the year of her death is given variously as 4 or 6 CE) is a Latin feminine proper noun used in English as the name of the Roman woman who married Marcus Tullius Cicero, probably in 80 or 79 BCE, and who bore his daughter Tullia in 78 BCE. In the source passage the name fills the slot “married to —”: it identifies Cicero's wife at the moment when, aged thirty, he had returned to Rome and was establishing himself in the courts. The word is therefore not a common noun with a lexical meaning but a historical proper name, and all that it “means” in the sentence is carried by the biography behind it.

The person denoted

  • Family and status: Terentia belonged to the plebeian but long-established gens Terentia. The sources present her as wealthy in her own right; her dowry was one of the practical foundations of Cicero's equestrian household, and her half-sister Fabia was a Vestal Virgin, a distinction that placed the family close to the religious centre of the state.
  • The marriage: The union with Cicero, contracted about 79 BCE, was a conventional aristocratic alliance — money, connections and children rather than romance. It lasted some thirty-three years and produced Tullia and Marcus, the future consul suffect of 30 BCE.
  • Public role: Like other matrons of the senatorial class, Terentia held no office and cast no vote, yet she ran a large household, cultivated patronage and acted as her husband's agent during his exile of 58–57 BCE, when she lobbied senators and, by Cicero's own account in the letters, faced danger and financial loss.
  • Divorce: Cicero divorced her in 46 BCE, apparently over money and the neglect of his affairs during the civil war, and soon afterwards married the young Publilia, a match that scandalised contemporaries and was quickly dissolved. Terentia is reported to have outlived him by decades, dying at about a hundred and three, and to have remarried — a detail resting on late and hostile gossip.

What the name means as a name

  • Feminine of a gentilicium: Terentia is the grammatically feminine form of the Roman family name Terentius. Women of the upper classes normally bore no praenomen; the feminine of the nomen served as their entire official name, occasionally differentiated by maior, minor or tertia when sisters shared it.
  • “She of the Terentii”: The force of the word in Latin is possessive and clan-based, closer to “a Terentia” than to a modern first name. It announces descent, and through descent, political alignment.
  • Male counterparts: The same stem yields the masculine Terentius, borne by the playwright Publius Terentius Afer (Terence) and by the polymath Marcus Terentius Varro, and it survives in Italian Terenzio and Terenzia and in the English given name Terence.
  • No common-noun senses: English has never lexicalised “Terentia” as a word for a wife, a widow or a shrewd estate manager; its only meaning is the person. Allusive use (“another Terentia”) is a literary gesture, not a dictionary sense.

Register, connotation and collocations

The name belongs to the register of classical biography and Ciceronian scholarship. It collocates with dowry, household, exile, divorce, letters to Atticus and Tullia. In Cicero's correspondence the tone surrounding her moves from affectionate practicality to exasperation and finally to cold dismissal; in Plutarch and in the later moralising tradition she is flattened into a type, the managing wife. Modern historians, reading the same letters more carefully, present her instead as a competent manager and political operator whose reputation was written by a man who had reasons to be unkind.

Why the name matters in this reading

The sentence is a career summary: Cicero is thirty, he is back in Rome, he is pleading in the courts, and he is married to Terentia. Each clause is a piece of the same ascent. The marriage supplied the money and the family dignity that a new man from Arpinum needed; the courts supplied the fame. Knowing who Terentia was turns a bare name into evidence about how Roman political life actually worked — through households, dowries and alliances quite as much as through speeches.
