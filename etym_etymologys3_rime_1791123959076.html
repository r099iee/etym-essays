Rime meaning frost is one of English's most purely Germanic weather words, surviving almost unchanged for fifteen centuries while its homophone rhyme arrived separately from Greek via French. Its ancestor is Proto-Germanic *hrimaz meaning hoarfrost, the white crystalline coating on branches, beards, and eyebrows. Cognates crowd the North Sea: Old Norse hrim, Old Frisian rim, Dutch rijm, German Reif (with sound shift), all meaning frost. The deeper Indo-European source is usually reconstructed as PIE *krey- or *ghrim- frost, though the exact laryngeal shape is uncertain; what is secure is the Germanic hr- onset with voiceless h before r, later simplified in English.

Old English hrim, hryme appears in poetry and glossaries for the white frost that crowns winter mornings. Beowulf knows hrim on the mere; the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle notes bitter winters with hrim. Pronunciation then was /hriːm/ with audible h. Middle English, after Norman influence, lost initial /h/ before /r/ (as in hring → ring), giving rime /riːm/. Spelling kept i for the long vowel, later diphthongized to modern /raɪm/ by the Great Vowel Shift.

Semantic stability is remarkable: from OE to Dickens to modern meteorology, rime never strayed far from granular frost formed when supercooled fog droplets freeze on contact. Science now distinguishes it precisely:

  • Soft rime / hard rime: feathery vs dense ice accretion on masts, aircraft, trees.
  • Rime vs hoarfrost vs glaze: rime forms from freezing fog with wind, hoarfrost from direct deposition on still nights, glaze from freezing rain. Dickens's frosty rime on head and eyebrows is technically rime ice from breath and fog.
  • Rime vs rhyme: by the 17th century printers respelled poetic rime (verse) as rhyme under Greek rhythmos influence. Frost-rime kept old spelling. Samuel Johnson already separates them.

Literary attestation made rime immortal. Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner (1798, archaically spelled Rime) puns on both frost and tale: a mariner rimed with polar ice telling a rhymed tale. Scott, Keats (rime of frost-work), and Dickens use it for venerable wintry whiteness — frost as age. Dickens's Scrooge with frosty rime on head, eyebrows, and chin turns meteorological accretion into moral physiognomy: the man's inner cold has precipitated outward, whitening him like an old tree.

Socio-cultural context: before central heating, rime was daily winter experience. Rimed windows, beards, thatch, rigging signalled hard frost to sailors, coachmen, and the poor. To be rimed was to be exposed. Victorian Christmas cards aestheticized it into beauty, but Dickens retains its bite: rime stiffens gait and nips noses.

Modern usage is split between poetic survival and technical precision. Poets still write rimed fields for atmosphere; the Met Office, aviation, and mountaineering use rime icing as hazard terminology. As a verb, to rime (frost rimed the branches) remains current though rarer than frosted. The noun is countable and uncountable: a rime, much rime.

Debate note: derivation from PIE is less certain than the Germanic stage. Pokorny proposed *ghrim-, others *krey- cold. OED marks the pre-Germanic origin as unknown/uncertain. What is undisputed is PGmc *hrimaz → OE hrim → ME rime → ModE rime, and complete separation from verse-rhyme from OF rime from Latin rhythmus from Greek rhythmos.
