Picayune functions primarily in modern English as an adjective characterizing something as of negligible monetary value, trivial, paltry, or contemptibly small-minded and nitpicking in attitude. Historically, it was an essential numismatic noun in the southern United States, denoting a small Spanish colonial silver coin—the half-real—valued at six and a quarter cents (one-sixteenth of a Spanish milled silver dollar or "piece of eight"), and subsequently applied to the United States half-dime and other minor five-cent coinage during the nineteenth century.

The word exhibits distinct nuances across historical economics, sociolinguistics, and contemporary rhetorical registers:

  • Adjectival Sense (Trivial and Insignificant): Describing material objects, sums of money, or issues that are unworthy of serious consideration due to their minuscule scale or negligible worth (e.g., "dismissing a multi-million dollar lawsuit over a picayune bookkeeping discrepancy").
  • Adjectival Sense (Small-Minded and Nitpicking): Characterizing people, mentalities, bureaucratic regulations, or behaviors that are overly fastidious, meanly captious, petty, or preoccupied with minute, inconsequential technicalities (e.g., "a picayune office supervisor who disciplined employees for arriving thirty seconds late"). In this register, it is often expressed through the derived American adjective picayunish.
  • Historical Numismatic Noun (The Spanish Half-Real): In colonial and antebellum Louisiana and the Mississippi Valley, a picayune designated the Spanish silver half-real coin. Because the fledgling United States suffered from a chronic shortage of domestic small-denomination silver coins until the mid-nineteenth century, foreign silver specie remained legal tender until the Coinage Act of 1857, and the picayune served as the standard currency for casual market purchases, tavern drinks, and newspapers.
  • Print Culture and Journalism (*The Times-Picayune*): In 1837, journalists George Wilkins Kendall and Francis Lumsden established a groundbreaking daily newspaper in New Orleans named The Picayune. They deliberately chose the title because each issue cost exactly one picayune (six and a quarter cents)—a price significantly cheaper than established aristocratic journals that charged ten cents or required annual subscriptions. The newspaper became one of the most celebrated investigative organs in American journalism, immortalizing the word in journalistic history as *The Times-Picayune*.
  • Regional Geographic Place Names: Surviving as a prominent toponym in the American South, most notably the city of Picayune, Mississippi, founded in the late nineteenth century and named in honor of the New Orleans newspaper by its founder, Eliza Jane Poitevent Nicholson (the first female publisher of a major American newspaper, who wrote under the pen name "Pearl Rivers").

The semantic bridge from coin to adjective represents an organic psychological transition: when a coin is the smallest piece of silver in everyday circulation, anyone who argues tenaciously over it, or any concern deemed worth no more than that coin, is easily branded as small-minded, cheap, or picayune.
