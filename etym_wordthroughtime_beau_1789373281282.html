- 12th to 16th Century - In Old and Middle French (*bel* / *beau*), functions as the universal adjective for handsome, fair, noble, and fine, derived from Latin *bellus*.
- Late 17th Century (Restoration England) - Borrowed into English as a noun during the reign of Charles II to designate an aristocratic man of fashion, elegance, and gallantry obsessed with French manners, dress, and polite courtship.
- 18th to Early 19th Century (Augustan & Regency Eras) - Solidified as both a historical archetype of dandyism (Beau Nash, Beau Brummell) and the standard informal noun for a young lady's sweetheart, suitor, or courting companion.
- 19th to Mid-20th Century - The noun *beau* becomes a warm staple of Victorian and Southern American vernacular; simultaneously, literary and intellectual prose adopts French adjectival phrases (*beau rôle*, *beau geste*, *beau idéal*, *beau monde*) to express sophisticated psychological and aesthetic nuances.
- 21st Century - Continues in literary and translation criticism as the definitive label for taking the flattering, unearned high ground (*le beau rôle*), while surviving nostalgically in popular parlance as an affectionate term for an escort or boyfriend.
