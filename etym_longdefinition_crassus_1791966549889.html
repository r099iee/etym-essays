Crassus is a Latin cognomen — the hereditary third element of the Roman name — whose literal sense is "thick, dense, solid, fat," and, by an easy metaphor, "stout, coarse, dull, obtuse." In the present reading the word names Lucius Licinius Crassus (140–91 BCE), consul in 95 BCE, censor in 92 BCE, and the most admired Roman orator of the generation before Cicero. He is the principal speaker of Cicero's De Oratore (composed c. 55 BCE, set in 91 BCE), and the quoted passage asks whether the opinions Crassus voices in that dialogue can be identified straightforwardly with Cicero's own.

Who is denoted

  • Lucius Licinius Crassus (140–91 BCE): a member of the plebeian gens Licinia, son of Marcus Licinius Crassus and brother of Publius Licinius Crassus (consul 97 BCE). He was quaestor in Asia, consul in 95 with Quintus Mucius Scaevola the Pontifex, and censor in 92 with Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus.
  • The censor's edict: in 92 BCE the two censors banned the recently founded Latin schools of rhetoric, often regarded as the first official intervention of the Roman state in rhetorical education — a fact that sharpens the paradox of Crassus, the greatest orator of his day, distrusting professional training in eloquence.
  • The orator: Cicero, who as a teenager may have heard him, ranks Crassus and Marcus Antonius as the twin peaks of pre-Ciceronian eloquence in the Brutus; Crassus is praised for consummate Latinity, dignity, wit, and a style at once ample and restrained.
  • The character in De Oratore: Crassus argues that the true orator must master the whole of knowledge and philosophy, that eloquence divorced from wisdom is a weapon in the hands of madmen, and that technique alone cannot produce the ideal speaker. He is urbane, commanding, sometimes ironic, and closer to Cicero's own ideal than any other speaker in the dialogue.
  • Not the triumvir: the name is now far more familiar from Marcus Licinius Crassus (c. 115–53 BCE), the triumvir, reputedly the richest Roman of his age, who crushed Spartacus, allied with Caesar and Pompey, and died with his army at Carrhae in 53 BCE. He was the orator's nephew — son of the orator's brother Publius — a distinction routinely lost in popular writing.

The cognomen: from body to mind

  • Descriptive origin: Roman cognomina frequently began as nicknames — Rufus "red-haired", Longus "tall", Naso "nose", Paullus "small", Balbus "stammering". Crassus "stout, thick-set" distinguished a solidly built branch of the Licinii, and the nickname became hereditary within that line.
  • Material senses: in classical Latin crassus describes things physically thick or dense — thick air, thick blood, solid ground, dense shade, coarse wool, fat meat.
  • Mental extension: from "dense" comes "dull, stupid, crass," exactly parallel to English thick and dense used as insults. Latin already had this pejorative use, so the modern adjective is not a semantic novelty but a survival.
  • English descendants: crass (16th century) "grossly stupid or insensitive"; crassly, crassness, and the rare crassitude (17th century); the medical Latin crassamentum "clot" belongs to the same family of thickness.

Register, ambiguity and modern reference

  • As a proper noun in English, Crassus is a bookish classical borrowing with no everyday currency; it appears in translations of Cicero, Plutarch and Appian, in histories of Rome, and in the phrase "as rich as Crassus," which alludes to the triumvir's fortune, not to the orator.
  • In classical scholarship the bare name is context-dependent: rhetoric and Cicero point to the orator; politics, money and Parthia point to the triumvir. Editors often write "Crassus the Orator" or "L. Licinius Crassus" precisely to prevent confusion.
  • The cognomen itself carries no insult in Latin usage: a Roman named Crassus was not being called stupid, any more than a modern Mr Strong is being called a strongman. The pejorative charge lives only in the adjective.
  • In this passage the name is a key to authorial voice: because Crassus is Cicero's most authoritative speaker, the scholarly question of whether his words represent Cicero's own views determines how the whole dialogue is read.

Why the distinction matters here: the quotation is about the social power of eloquence and about who speaks for the author. Confusing the orator with the triumvir would replace a debate about wisdom, education and civic speech with a story about wealth and military disaster. Reading Crassus as the orator keeps the argument where Cicero placed it: in the mind of a cultivated statesman who doubts that rhetoric alone can save a commonwealth.
