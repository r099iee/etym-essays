paralytic primarily denotes relation to paralysis: affected with loss of muscular function or sensation, or causing such loss. Medically it describes strokes, lesions, agents, or states that produce immobility — a paralytic attack, paralytic shellfish poisoning, paralytic ileus. As a noun it can denote a person suffering paralysis (now often considered dated and clinical, replaced by person with paralysis). In the source sentence — self-consciousness can be paralytic, as Hamlet found out — the word is used in its celebrated figurative extension: so excessively self-aware, reflective, or fearful that thought inhibits action, leaving the agent inwardly mobile but outwardly frozen.

The Hamlet allusion fixes the sense: the prince who thinks too precisely on the event, whose conscience makes cowards, whose native hue of resolution is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought. To call self-consciousness paralytic is thus to diagnose hyper-reflection as a functional lesion of the will. The adjective bridges neurology and moral psychology in a single stroke, implying that excessive monitoring acts like a severed nerve between intention and execution.

  • Strict medical-neurological sense: pertaining to paralysis — loss of voluntary movement due to neural injury or disease. Examples: paralytic poliomyelitis (motor-neuron destruction), paralytic strabismus (ocular muscle palsy), paralytic agents (curare-like blockers). Objective, diagnostic, non-metaphorical.
  • Pharmacological-toxicological sense: causing paralysis — paralytic shellfish toxins, paralytic venoms. Here the adjective is active-causative rather than passive-affected, a regular ambivalence in English -ic adjectives (compare cathartic, narcotic).
  • General incapacitating sense: rendering helpless or immobile by any means — a paralytic fear gripped the city, paralytic bureaucracy. Bridge from medicine via metaphor: fear acts like a lesion, institutions act like damaged nerves.
  • Psychological-reflective sense (source sense): inhibiting action through over-analysis, self-monitoring, or scrupulosity — paralytic perfectionism, analysis paralysis, paralytic self-consciousness. Implies intact competence coupled with blocked execution; the sufferer knows what to do but cannot release the act.
  • Archaic noun sense: a paralytic person — the paralytic lowered through the roof (Gospel translations). Now largely superseded in polite use by person-first language, but retained in historical and biblical contexts.
  • Collocational and gradable profile: gradable (almost, nearly, completely, partially paralytic); collocates with fear, dread, shyness, self-consciousness, indecision, perfectionism in figurative use; formal-literary in figurative register, clinical in literal register; antonyms mobilising, liberating, enabling, bracing rather than a single opposite.

Understanding paralytic therefore requires holding literal and figurative together: a nerve lesion and a Hamlet-like hesitation share the same structure — signal generated centrally, failing to reach muscle — differing only in whether the block is organic or attentional. The source exploits this identity to warn that introspection, valuable in measure, can become pathogenic in excess.
