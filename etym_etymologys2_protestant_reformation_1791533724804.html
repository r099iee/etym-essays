Reformation

PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*mer-bh- / *mor-gʷ- (or non-PIE substrate)
"shape, form, appearance"
↓
Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE)
forma
"shape, figure, outline, mold"
↓
Latin (1st c. BCE)
reformare
"to form anew, reshape, restore"
↓
Latin (noun of action) (c. 2nd c. CE)
reformatio (gen. reformationis)
"a transformation, renewal, restoration"
↓
Old French (13th c.)
reformation
"restoration, repair, moral renewal"
↓
Middle English (14th c.)
reformacioun
"improvement, amendment of abuses, correction"
↓
English (16th c.–modern)
Protestant Reformation
"the 16th-century religious transformation of Western Europe"

Note: The word Protestant originally bore an affirmative legal sense ("bearing solemn public witness to one's faith"), not merely a negative dissent against authority. The noun reformatio had been a core institutional phrase in late medieval canon law for centuries (e.g. reformatio in capite et in membris, "reform in head and members") before becoming uniquely identified with the break from the Roman hierarchy.
