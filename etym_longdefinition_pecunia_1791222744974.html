pecunia is a Classical Latin feminine noun meaning money, wealth, property or a sum of money, etymologically cattle-wealth — money insofar as it descends from livestock.

Formed from pecus (cattle) + the abstract/collective suffix -ia, pecunia literally means the value embodied in a herd, then by natural extension any fungible store of value: coined bronze, silver and gold, credit, estate, fortune. In Cicero, Caesar, Sallust and the jurists it is the ordinary prose word for money in all registers — cash in hand, public funds (pecunia publica), bribes, debts, dowries, fines and revenues.

The word encodes a complete economic history: pastoralists who counted cattle came to count bronze stamped with cattle, then to count abstract units called by the old cattle name. English preserves it only in learned adjectives — pecuniary — but Roman law and finance built an entire vocabulary around it.

  • Core monetary sense — coined money, cash, currency: Pecuniam solvere, numerare, dare, accipere — to pay, count, give, receive money. Pecunia signata is struck coin; pecunia numerata is ready cash. This is the default meaning in commerce, soldier's pay, tax collection and daily life from the 3rd c. BCE onward.
  • Extended wealth sense — property, fortune, estate, means: Pecunia frequently means total wealth, not just pocket cash: homo pecuniosus (a moneyed man), pecuniae amplificatio (increase of fortune), pecuniae cupidus (greedy for wealth). The bridge is synecdoche: the cash portion stands for the whole estate, just as herd once stood for whole substance.
  • Legal-technical senses: In Roman jurisprudence pecunia becomes a term of art for res in general — anything valuable that can be claimed: pecuniae petitio (action for debt/property), pecunia certa, incerta. Ulpian notes pecuniae covers not only coin but all things, because all things were once reckoned as cattle-value. Related compounds proliferate: pecuniarius (pertaining to money, monetary penalty), pecuniosus (wealthy), peculatus (embezzlement of public money, from peculari).
  • Moral and rhetorical colouring: Roman moralists load pecunia with ambivalence: pecuniae studium (love of money) as root of corruption versus pecunia as legitimate reward and civic foundation. Sallust's pecuniae cupiditas, Horace's pecunia donat omnia (money gives everything), Juvenal's satires — money as power, temptation and measure of worth. Proverbs crystallise: pecunia non olet (money does not stink — Vespasian's urine-tax defence).
  • Medieval and modern learned survival: Pecunia does not become French argent or Italian soldi/denaro in popular speech, but survives as a learned legal and ecclesiastical term (pecunia in canon law, scholastic economics) and passes into English as pecuniary (relating to money, as in pecuniary interest, pecuniary damages, pecuniary motive), impecunious (having no money), pecunious (rare). In economics and anthropology pecuniary still signals the cattle-origin theory of money.
  • Distinction from neighbours: Aes is bronze as material; nummus is a specific coin; argentum is silver/money as metal; moneta is mint/coinage as institution; stips, merces, pretium are payment, wages, price. Pecunia alone carries the abstract-universal sense wealth-as-such with its pastoral etymology audible to every Roman ear.

Understanding pecunia therefore requires holding two images at once: the lowing herd on the Campagna and the clinking bronze in the forum purse. The secondary senses — fortune, claim, bribe, public fund, moral corruption — all flow from asking what happens when a society stops counting heads of cattle and starts counting stamped discs, but keeps the old barnyard name for the new abstraction.
