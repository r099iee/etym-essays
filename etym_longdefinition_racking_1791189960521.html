Racking is the present participle and participial adjective of rack, meaning straining, stretching, or tormenting to the utmost: exerting a body, mind, or mechanism with violent, painful effort. In the source sentence the primitive brain was racking itself, stretching its own powers and straining every faculty, to invent mechanical answers to survival problems. This is the familiar idiom to rack one's brains, often spelled wrack by confusion: to think extremely hard, to force the mind as if on a torture frame until it yields an idea. More generally racking describes any agonizing strain: a racking pain convulses the body; a racking cough shakes the chest; racking sobs shake the shoulders; racking guilt torments the conscience.

The torture-frame origin explains the violence. To rack was to stretch limbs on the engine until joints parted, so any racking effort feels dangerously maximal, wringing out the last drop. The mind-racking branch pictures ideas as confessions extracted under pressure; the pain branch pictures suffering as judicial torment inflicted by disease or remorse; the shaking branch pictures coughs and sobs as wrenches of the bodily frame. All share excess: ordinary trying is insufficient, so the language reaches for the Tower. Medical writing prefers racking for paroxysmal symptoms; moral writing prefers racked with guilt or doubt; everyday speech prefers racking my brains before exams, puzzles, and names on the tip of the tongue.

  • mental exertion: racking brains, wits, memory, invention; thinking with painful intensity to extract an answer.
  • physical torment: racking pain, fever, labour; severe suffering feeling like stretching or tearing.
  • emotional anguish: racked with doubt, racked by remorse; conscience stretched on an inner frame.
  • violent shaking: racking cough, sobs, shudders; paroxysmal wrenching of the frame.
  • economic extraction: racking rents, rack-rent; extorting the maximum, historical landlord use.
  • technological stretching: racking cloth on frames, drawing wine off lees; artisanal senses now rare.
  • spelling crux: racking versus wracking; purists reserve rack for strain and wrack for ruin, most dictionaries accept both for brains and pain.
  • tone: intense, graphic, slightly literary; stronger than straining, more violent than trying.

The idiom governs reflexives and possessives: racking his brains, racking itself, racked with pain, racking to remember. It collocates with superlatives and absolutes: racking hard, racking desperately, racking in vain. Its opposite is ease: to recall effortlessly, to rest untroubled. The source redeems the violence positively: the primitive brain torturing itself to invent tools, traps, and machines is heroic, not pathological, and the reader is invited to admire the strain that produced civilization rather than flinch from it.
