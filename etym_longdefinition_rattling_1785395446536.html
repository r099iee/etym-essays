RATTLING is the present participle of the verb "to rattle". It denotes the producing of a rapid, unbroken succession of short, sharp, percussive sounds — a series of knocks, clacks, and clicks so closely spaced that they fuse into a single chattering din. The participial form carries an insistently continuous, mechanical quality: a rattling noise does not happen once; it keeps happening, again and again, without pause, like hail on a roof, a chain dragged over stone, or a loose window banging in the wind. The word is onomatopoeic, built on the echoing skeleton r-...-t-...-l, which imitates the staccato repetition of hard bodies knocking together. It is a word of pure sound-symbolism: no Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed for it, and it was coined in Middle English directly out of the noise it names. PRIMARY SENSES
  • Emitting a rapid series of short, sharp sounds — the core physical sense, of chains, windows, gunfire, hailstones, engines, or loose parts shaking together.
  • Moving with a rattling noise — a cart rattling over cobbles, a train rattling over points: the motion sense, in which the sound stands metonymically for the thing producing it.
  • Shaking something until it rattles (transitive) — to rattle a box, to rattle the bars of a cage, to rattle a door.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • Figurative, "to disconcert, alarm, unnerve" (16th century) — to shake a person's composure as a chain is shaken: the news rattled her, the witness was rattled; the modern idiom "to rattle someone's cage" keeps the image of shaking bars.
  • Of rapid glib speech — "to rattle on" or "to rattle off" means to talk fast, fluently, and at length, usually of routine or rehearsed material; the mouth rattles like a shaken instrument.
  • Noun senses (16th century) — the instrument that rattles: a baby's toy rattle; a watchman's or alarm rattle (a ratchet that made a warning clatter); the rattlesnake (17th century), named from the horny rings of its tail; the death-rattle (17th century), the terminal throat-sound of a dying person.
  • Intensifier use (16th century, now dated) — "a rattling good yarn", "a rattling pace": brisk, lively, excellent, from the vigour of rapid motion.
  • Mechanical and technical use — rattling machinery, a rattling engine, valve rattle, the rattle of loose bearings: the word is standard in engineering and mechanics for abnormal, loose, repetitive noise.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES The figurative sense "to disconcert" grows directly out of the physical shaking: to rattle someone is to shake their composure as a chain is shaken, and the transfer was so natural that it needed no literary hero to establish it. The death-rattle extends the sound to the body's last mechanical noise, the involuntary rattle of breath in the throat. The rattlesnake's name is pure metonymy from its warning organ, the horny rings it vibrates. The intensifier use springs from the speed and vigour of a rattling motion — a fast-moving, rattling thing is a lively thing, and from the 16th to the 19th century "rattling" meant excellent or brisk. In every case the semantic history is a single chain of metonymy and metaphor radiating from one acoustic core, and the modern word still carries all these strands. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In The Hobbit, rattling is the auditory core of the landslide catastrophe. The whole mountain-side is in motion, and rattling captures the overlapping percussion of countless stones striking one another and the rock, while slipping gives the movement and cracking gives the fracture. The three participles work as one: things slip, they rattle as they strike, and they crack as they give way. Rattling here is not one noise but many, layered and unceasing, and it gives the chaos an almost mechanical, metallic texture — the sound of the earth shaking itself apart. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • Clatter — a lighter, more hollow noise made by loose hard objects knocking together; clatter suggests mere disorder, while rattle suggests a tighter, more mechanical and even rhythm.
  • Clank — a duller, heavier sound, specifically of metal striking metal.
  • Clang — a single loud, resonant, metallic ring, prolonged rather than rapid.
  • Click — a single short, sharp, light sound, the smallest member of the family.
  • Clack — a single sharp dry sound, or the loose chattering of a mechanism.
  • Rustle — a soft, dry, swishing sound of light things moving (leaves, paper, silk), entirely without the hardness of a rattle.
  • Crash — a loud, shattering, sudden impact, one great noise rather than a rapid series.
GRAMMATICAL AND FORMAL NOTES Rattle is both intransitive (the window rattles) and transitive (he rattled the gate). The present participle rattling works as a continuous-verb form, as an adjective (a rattling noise, rattling chains), and as a gerund (the rattling of the train). The noun rattle is countable (a toy rattle) and uncountable (the rattle of gunfire). Register: plain, vigorous, and everyday; the word is at home in speech and prose alike, and its imitative force has kept it fresh through seven centuries of continuous use.
