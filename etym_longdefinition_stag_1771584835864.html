Stag is the adult male of a deer, especially the red deer (Cervus elaphus), distinguished by its size and by its full head of branching antlers. In the context of this card it is used by a translator to form an architectural compound, "stag's head roofing," for the device by which "the big building at the head of the courtyard was connected at each end to galleries running through the length of the side buildings." The phrase imports an animal into the grammar of building: the juncture of the roofs is presumably so called because it resembles the antlered head of a stag branching outward from a central ridge to the galleries on either side. The noun therefore does double duty in the passage - denotatively it identifies a species and sex of deer, while connotatively it projects an image of branching, thrusting, natural force onto the built environment, endowing the mansion with a subtle zoomorphic energy: each corner sprouts an antler-like projection. The tone is that of architectural description touched by the poetic, a technical term that retains, in full vigour, the vivid animal it evokes. The choice is culturally telling, for in English the stag is an emblem of nobility, virility, and the hunt, and its head is a familiar motif of heraldry and country-house decoration; a translator reaches for it to give a Chinese building a resonant English image of strength. Core senses.
  • The adult male deer, especially the red deer. The primary, defining sense. A stag is a fully grown male with a head of antlers; the hind is the female, the calf or fawn the young. In British hunting usage a "stag" is specifically the male red deer with a full head of antlers.
  • The male of other large Old World deer. By extension, any large male deer of species such as the sika or sambar; in North America the word is sometimes loosely applied to male deer generally, though the wapiti (American elk) male is more usually a "bull."
Secondary, slang, and technical senses - and the bridges that connect them.
  • Stag party / stag night / stag do (British). A celebration held for a man about to be married, attended only by men. Bridge: "stag" as a male-only gathering, the male of the species - the Victorian "stag party" or "smoker," an entertainment for men only; the image is of the unpaired male, the buck standing apart from the herd.
  • To go stag (American). Of a man, to attend a social event without a female partner; a "stag dance" was one to which men came alone. Bridge: the lone male animal, unattached and unpaired.
  • Stag (stock-market slang, from the 1840s). An investor who buys shares in a new issue hoping to sell them at an immediate profit; also the verb "to stag" a new issue. Bridge: one who is "outside the herd," taking a quick speculative leap rather than investing for the long term; the sense dates from the railway speculation boom of the 1840s-50s and is peculiar to English.
  • Stagflation (economics, 1960s). Stagnation plus inflation; the coinage of the politician Iain Macleod in 1965. Here the "stag-" element is stagnation, not the deer, though the accidental pun on the slang "stag" investor is often noticed.
  • Stag beetle. The large beetle Lucanus cervus, so called because the male's enormous mandibles resemble antlers. Bridge: a folk-etymology transfer - the deer's horns become the beetle's horns, the word travelling from the animal to a lookalike insect.
  • Stag as a hunted and table animal. The stag of the hunt, whose flesh is venison; "stag" also denotes the meat of a mature male deer, and its head as a trophy or heraldic charge. The "stag's head caboshed" (a head shown facing with no neck) is the heraldic image that the architectural "stag's head roofing" directly evokes.
  • Obsolete verb senses. To "stag" in older slang could mean "to spy" or "to inform" - a stag as a man who stands watch - now obsolete; and in modern student slang "stag" survives in the verb of attending stag, i.e. alone.
Why the word means what it means. English stag appears in Middle English around 1200 as stagge, "an adult male deer," behind which stands an Old English *stagga that survives chiefly in place-names. Its deeper origin is uncertain: the Oxford English Dictionary calls the etymology "obscure," pointing to a probable Scandinavian connection (Old Norse steggr, "a male animal, a male bird") and a possible membership in the Indo-European family of *stegh-, "to stick, to prick, to pierce." If that membership is right, the stag is "the pricking animal" - the beast defined by its antlers, the sharp sticks growing from its head. The word would then be a distant cousin of English stick, sting, stake, and stitch, and of Latin stimulus, "a goad." The naming logic is widespread: animals are frequently named for their most salient physical feature, and an antlered deer is, above all, a sharp and branching thing. Whatever its exact origin, the word's core sense - the male deer with the grand rack of antlers - has remained stable for eight centuries, while its glamorous secondary senses have all grown out of that single image of the powerful, solitary male.
