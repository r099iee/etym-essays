The history of VALE begins in Latin, but at a point where the family tree grows dim. Latin vallis (also written valles) "valley" is first attested around the 2nd century BCE in the epic poet Ennius and is perfectly regular in classical prose and verse — Caesar's Gallic War uses it for the valleys of Gaul, Vergil for the pastoral dales of Arcadia. Yet when scholars try to push the word further back into Proto-Indo-European, the trail vanishes. Modern handbooks (de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin, 2008) mark the etymology "uncertain"; older authorities such as Pokorny proposed a link to the PIE root *wel- "to roll, to turn," the valley conceived as a winding or rolling landform, while others have toyed with a connection to vallus "stake, palisade," as if the valley were an "enclosed" place. None of these proposals commands agreement, no attested PIE form can be reconstructed, and honesty requires saying so: the etymology of vallis is simply not recoverable beyond Latin, and the word may even be a substrate or loanword. The chain from Latin onward, however, is fully documented and can be told with confidence. What is certain is the Latin word's immense cultural afterlife. In the Vulgate, vallis rendered the Hebrew emeq and ge, the low places of scripture, and the "valley of the shadow of death" of Psalm 23 (vallis umbrae mortis) made the valley the archetypal place of peril and consolation at once. The liturgy of the medieval Church added the most famous phrase of all: the Salve Regina, sung to the Virgin Mary, calls this world a lacrimarum valle, "a vale of tears." Every later English use of "vale of tears" descends directly from this Latin devotional coinage, and it is the deep reason the word feels sacred. The Romance path to English ran through Old French val, which inherited the Latin stem directly (vallem), while a separate derivative valee produced the ordinary French word for valley and, ultimately, the English doublet valley. English received vale in the 13th century — attested around 1300 — as a deliberately literary borrowing, at a time when the native word was dale (Old English dael). From the start, then, vale was the slightly exotic, bookish member of the family: the word of chronicles, romances, and devotional prose rather than of farm talk. Its history in English is the history of its register:
  • 14th-15th centuries: Chaucer uses vale for the pastoral settings of his tales; the word appears in religious and romantic verse.
  • 16th century: Spenser's Faerie Queene is full of vales in its allegorical landscapes; Shakespeare, in Othello, speaks of "the vale of years," the downhill stretch of life, showing how early the valley became a metaphor for human mortality and humility.
  • 17th century: The phrase "this vale of tears" (calqued from the Vulgate's lacrimarum valle) and the "vale of death" give the word its solemn, elegiac cast; Milton uses vale for both paradise and the fallen earth in Paradise Lost, and the King James Bible, although it generally prefers "valley," keeps "vale" in poetic passages.
  • 18th century: The word is fixed as the poetic, pastoral, and devotional term — the word of the graveyard poets, of hymnody, and of landscape poetry; Gray's country churchyard is set among vales and uplands.
  • 19th century (Romantic era): The Prelude's opening exploits the word's register exactly. Wordsworth asks "in what vale / Shall be my harbour?" — converting a mere topographic fact into a sheltered, hallowed retreat where the homeless spirit can anchor, the "vale of rest" answering the "vast city" from which he has fled.
The sound changes in the chain are modest. Latin vallis was pronounced with /w/ in Classical Latin (v as in "wine"); by the late Empire the /w/ became the voiced labio-dental /v/ of the modern languages, and the stem vallem gave Old French val with the loss of the final syllables typical of Gallo-Romance. When English borrowed the word, it kept the French and Latin spelling vale, and its long /e'/ reflects the Middle English pronunciation of the open syllable. The most interesting phonetic fact, however, is the doublet: vale and valley both descend from Latin vallis but entered English by different routes — vale from the Latin stem or Old French val, valley from Old French valee — and the two spellings now divide the same geography between the poetic and the practical. In modern English the specialization is complete:
  • Ordinary speech uses valley.
  • Vale survives as a poetic archaism, in set phrases ("the vale of tears," "the vale of years"), and in consciously literary prose and verse.
  • It survives most importantly fossilized in place-names: the Vale of Evesham, the Vale of York, the Vale of Glamorgan, the Vale of Tempe, and countless lesser vales. In these names the word is not archaic at all but a living administrative and geographical term, and its persistence testifies to the deep territorial meaning the word always carried.
Etymologically, vale and valley are doublets — the same Latin word entering English twice by different routes — and their split in register is a miniature lesson in how a word's social life can diverge from its etymology. An ordinary Latin noun, elevated by the Vulgate and the liturgy, borrowed as a literary word, narrowed to poetry and fixed phrases, and finally preserved in the place-names of the English landscape: that is the whole career of VALE, a word that has spent two thousand years meaning "valley" while meaning, in its poetry and prayer, far more — the shelter, the sorrow, the resting place of the human spirit.
