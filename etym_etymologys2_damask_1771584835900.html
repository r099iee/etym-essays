Damask is a toponym turned into a fabric name; it has NO Indo-European (PIE) ancestry. The chain below traces the name of the city, then the transfer of that name to the silk. (8 attested stages.) 1. Akkadian Dimašqa "Damascus" — attested in cuneiform in the Amarna letters (14th c. BCE), the earliest written form of the city's name. The inner-Semitic meaning of the name is not securely known; an old tradition connected it with the word for the sycamore fig (šeqem), but modern scholarship treats this as folk-etymology. 2. → Syriac / Aramaic Darmśq "Damascus" — with the intrusive -r- typical of Aramaic adaptation of the name. 3. → Biblical Hebrew Damméśeq (דַּמֶּשֶׂק) "Damascus" — the Israelite form of the city's name. 4. → Greek Damaskós (Δαμασκός) "Damascus" — the form in which the city's name entered the European world. 5. → Latin Damascus "Damascus" — the Roman and medieval Latin name of the city. 6. → Medieval Latin damascus "of Damascus; fabric of Damascus" — elliptical use of the adjective for the city's famous figured silk (12th–13th c.); this is the exact moment the city-name became a fabric-name. 7. → Italian damasco "damask" and Old French damas "damask silk" (13th c.) — the trade name of the fabric in the languages through which it reached northern Europe. 8. → Middle English damask "figured silk fabric" (14th c.), borrowed through Old French — the direct ancestor of the modern word. Final node: English DAMASK (rootWord) — "figured fabric of one colour, classically the silk of Damascus", 14th century to present. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The meaning of the Semitic city-name itself is not securely known; the šeqem "sycamore" etymology is widely rejected as folk-etymology. (2) The transfer of the name from city to fabric (stages 6–7) is well attested, not debated. (3) Sister words from the same city-name — the damask rose (Medieval Latin rosa damascena), the damson plum (Latin damascena pruna), and Damascus steel — are separate borrowings from the same toponym, not different roots.
