encroachments (noun, plural) — In this passage from Macaulay's History of England, the word denotes the slow, cumulative, and illegitimate extension of royal power beyond its lawful limits. The context is constitutional: the "system" — the network of statutes, precedents, and parliamentary checks built after the Revolution of 1688 — is what "effectually secured our liberties against the encroachments of kingly power". The key tone is one of incremental creep: these are not sudden coups or open invasions but a patient, nibbling expansion of prerogative, a pressure applied at the edges until a boundary is crossed. Macaulay's syntax reinforces the stealth: the encroachments are "of kingly power", a phrase that turns the king's government into a force of nature pressing upon a fragile liberty. Crucially, the sentence pivots to irony — the very constitutional machinery that blocked royal encroachment "gave birth to a new class of abuses". The word thus carries a judicial, almost fiscal exactness: an encroachment is a measured, provable overstepping, and the passage treats it as the archetypal abuse that the Revolution settlement was designed to contain.

Core sense: an encroachment is an intrusion on a person's territory, rights, or authority — a gradual, stealthy advance beyond proper limits. The word's full modern range is surprisingly wide:

  • Property / land encroachment. The oldest and most concrete sense: a fence, wall, building, or growth creeping over a neighbour's boundary line. In law it is a real-property matter — a physical intrusion onto another's land, remediable by removal or damages, and a staple of surveying disputes.
  • Political / constitutional encroachment. The gradual seizure of power, rights, or privileges by a state, monarch, or institution — "encroachments on civil liberties", "encroachments of the executive", "encroachment upon parliamentary privilege". This is Macaulay's sense and the dominant one in constitutional history.
  • Ecological / vegetative encroachment. In environmental science, vegetation creeping into a habitat: "woody encroachment" (trees invading grassland), "desert encroachment", "scrub encroachment" — the advance of a plant community over another, often a consequence of fire suppression or grazing change.
  • American football. A defensive penalty: encroachment occurs when a defender crosses the line of scrimmage and touches an offensive player before the snap — an offside-adjacent infraction that stops the play. This is the word's vivid modern sports sense.
  • Figurative / general encroachment. Anything that gradually invades or appropriates: "encroachment on my time", "encroachment of technology on privacy", "encroachment of the sea on the coast".
  • Medical / spatial encroachment. In anatomy and surgery, the gradual extension of a structure into a space not its own (for example, the encroachment of a herniated disc on a nerve root) — a specialist technical extension of the spatial sense.

The historical bridge is the hook. Old French encrochier meant "to catch with a hook, to seize, fasten on, grab hold of", formed from en- "in, into" and croc "a hook". A hooked instrument seizes gradually: it grips what is not its own, snags it, and drags it in — the slow possessive grab rather than the open assault. The word moved from the physical seizure of land (the medieval and agrarian sense) to the legal sense of creeping over boundaries, and then, in seventeenth- and nineteenth-century political prose, to the creeping seizure of rights and powers. The hook-image never fully disappears: an encroachment is always something caught, held, and pulled across a line, inch by inch.

Register and collocation: encroachment is a word of formal, legal, and political prose. It typically takes a possessive or an of-phrase naming the aggressor ("encroachments of kingly power", "encroachments of the executive") and is almost always the object of verbs like check, resist, guard against, prevent, block. Collocations: territorial encroachment, encroachment on civil liberties, to resist encroachment, gradual encroachment. Its register is dignified, slightly Latinate, and analytic rather than emotional. The plural encroachments in Macaulay does real work, suggesting a whole series of accumulated transgressions rather than a single event — a history of creeping overreach that the "system" was built to stop.
