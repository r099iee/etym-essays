1. Two prefixes, one marriage.Unintelligible is a late coinage (attested from around the 1610s) that welds two very different histories. The first is native: un-, the old Germanic negative, descended from the Indo-European syllabic nasal *n̥-, "not", which also gave Latin in- and Greek a-/an- — the same ancient negative atom that produced English untrue, Latin incredible, and Greek an-archy. The second is Latin: intelligibilis, "that may be understood", the adjective of intellegere, "to understand". The verb is itself a composite: inter, "between", plus legere, "to gather, choose, read" — understanding as an act of gathering scattered things into one meaning. The same root *leǵ- (PIE "to gather") underlies lecture (a reading), legend (what must be read), legion (a "chosen" levy), and diligent (one who "picks out" persistently).

2. The Latin career of intelligibilis. In classical Latin, intellegibilis described what can be perceived by the mind as opposed to the senses. Late antique philosophy gave the pair its precise weight: Boethius, translating and glossing Aristotle's logical works, systematically opposes the intelligibilis (grasped by intellect) to the sensibilis (grasped by sense) — the boundary along which "understandable" acquires a metaphysical flavour. English absorbed intelligible in the fourteenth century, and the negative unintelligible followed in the seventeenth, when philosophy, law, and theology all needed a precise word for the failure of meaning.

3. The seventeenth-century career. The early uses are learned: unintelligible scripture, unintelligible nature, unintelligible metaphysics. The word arrived exactly as the new science and the new philosophy were arguing that reality must be legible — that the Book of Nature is written in mathematics — and its dark double was indispensable: what resisted reading was declared unintelligible. John Locke, in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), repeatedly turns the word against jargon: much obscure erudition is, he charges, "unintelligible", signs without sense. The forensic use followed: courts refused to enforce contracts whose terms were unintelligible, and the word settled into legal and administrative prose as the standard description of a document that cannot be obeyed because it cannot be understood.

4. Philology, pathology, and the machine: 19th-20th centuries. The nineteenth century gave the word new homes. Comparative philology described unknown and unrelated tongues as unintelligible to Europeans; the new speech pathology used it of aphasic utterance; telegraphy and telephony applied it to corrupted transmissions. Modernism then rehabilitated the word as a badge: "unintelligible" poetry and painting defied the demand for ready sense, and critics fought over whether the difficulty was a virtue or a vice. By the late twentieth century the word had spread to the bureaucratic and the computational — unintelligible error messages, unintelligible fine print, unintelligible data — so that today it serves as the exact, unflustered descriptor for every transaction of meaning that fails: the stutter, the dead channel, the document that cannot be obeyed.

  • Sound:legere → intellegere (assimilated form) → Middle English intelligible; the English negative un- joins without a pause: un-in-telligible.
  • Meaning: "not graspable by the mind" (1610s) → "not hearable/readable in practice" (tech and law) → "not interpretable" (code, data, art).
  • Register: from metaphysics to forensics to tech support — while keeping its cool neutrality; unlike nonsense, it never insults the thing it describes, only the possibility of reading it.
