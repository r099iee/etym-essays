- c. 1200 — the packer's bundle: Middle English trusse names the packed bundle of a traveller or pedlar, and trussen the act of making it, cloth, straw, and cord against the road.
- 14th c. — hay and hernias: the truss of hay feeds armies' horses while the surgical truss supports ruptured bodies, the same strapped pressure serving stable and sickroom.
- 15th–16th c. — kitchen and scaffold: cooks truss fowls for the spit and gaolers truss prisoners for the cart, neatness serving appetite and authority alike.
- 17th–18th c. — engineers triangulate it: builders name the braced timber framework a truss, and bridges, roofs, and ships' masts are trussed into triangles that cannot rack.
- 19th c. — empire's luggage: soldiers, emigrants, and missionaries truss kit across five continents, and the trussed pack becomes an emblem of readiness.
- Today — kitchens and campsites: cooks still truss birds, walkers still truss packs, and surgeons have retired the hernia truss to museums, leaving the word to the competent bundle.
