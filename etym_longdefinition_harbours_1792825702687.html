Harbours, the plural of harbour (American spelling harbor), names in its primary sense sheltered stretches of coastal water — bays, coves, estuaries, or walled basins — where ships can anchor, load, and ride out weather in safety. A harbour is the coast's promise that the sea, elsewhere a highway and a hazard, has at least some addresses where it agrees to behave.

In the source sentence the word wears its most mythic dress: the elves are going to the ______, out away beyond the White Towers — the Grey Havens of Tolkien's map, the elven ports of the far west from which ships sail out of the world. Sam's vague waving arm says everything about mortal geography here: nobody in the Shire knows how far it is to the Sea, past the old towers on the western borders, but everyone knows the harbours are where journeys end and the greatest journey begins. The everyday noun is thus lifted, without changing its meaning by a hair, into eschatology — a port like any port, except that its ships do not come back.

Core meaning in detail: Physically a harbour needs three things: shelter from prevailing wind and swell (a headland, an island, or a breakwater), water deep enough to float hulls at low tide, and holding ground where anchors bite. Nature supplies the first two in estuaries and drowned valleys; engineers supply the third kind with granite arms like those of St Ives or Dover. Great harbours — Sydney, Rio, Valletta, Scapa Flow — are accidents of geology that became accidents of history: navies, merchants, and migrants all followed the shelter, and cities grew around the anchorage like limpets round a rock.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Sheltered anchorage or port (primary noun sense, from the 14th c.): the walled basin, the fishing harbour, the harbour lights. The bridge from the older lodging sense is functional analogy: as an inn shelters travellers, so a harbour shelters ships — refuge translated from land to water, keeping its promise of rest and safety.
  • Any place of refuge or asylum (figurative noun, from the 16th c.): a harbour of rest, a safe harbour in a storm of life, the financial safe harbour clauses of modern law. The bridge is metaphorical shelter: the breakwater becomes any protection — walls, statutes, friendships — behind which the vulnerable ride out trouble, and central bankers now speak of safe harbour assets exactly as pilots once spoke of safe anchorages.
  • To give shelter to, to house (verb, transitive): to harbour fugitives, to harbour travellers for the night. The bridge runs backwards from noun to verb: because a harbour is shelter, to harbour is to shelter — first ships and crews, then by natural extension outlaws, refugees, and anyone needing a roof and silence.
  • To keep in the mind, to cherish or nurse (verb of thoughts and feelings): to harbour doubts, to harbour a grudge, to harbour suspicions. The bridge is inward metaphor: the mind becomes the harbour basin, and the resentment a ship that refuses to sail — kept, fed, and hidden behind the breakwater of a smiling face. No other English verb says quite this: harbouring a grudge means tending it in shelter, not merely feeling it.
  • To shelter criminals or the enemy (verb, legal and hostile sense): harbouring a fugitive is a crime in most jurisdictions, and nations accuse each other of harbouring terrorists. The bridge extends the sheltering verb into law and war: the same roof that is mercy in one light is complicity in another, and the word carries both verdicts at once — warmth toward the sheltered, frost toward the shelterer.

Register and connotation: harbours is a warm word in a cold element — safety, lamplight, coiled ropes, gulls arguing over fish boxes. Even its grimmest compounds (harbouring enemies, harbouring resentment) keep the image of shelter; the menace lies in what is sheltered, never in the sheltering itself. Tolkien leans on exactly this warmth: the elven harbours are the final kindness of a world full of roads, the last lit windows before the dark water. Few English nouns manage to be at once so practical (pilotage dues, dredging schedules) and so tender (home, haven, rest) without either sense thinning the other.
