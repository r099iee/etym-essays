Boughs are the large, main branches of a tree — the principal limbs springing directly from the trunk that bear the lesser twigs, foliage, and fruit; the word carries an archaic, literary dignity that plain 'branches' lacks. In The Hobbit, Dwalin and Balin swarm up a tall slender fir 'trying to find a place to sit in the greenery of the topmost boughs': the plural form evokes a broad, interlocked canopy, the very picture of refuge from the wolves below. The tone is deliberately storybook — boughs belong to folk tales, carols, and the language of trees in their full majesty — and the detail of the 'tall slender fir' sharpens the irony, since a fir carries little foliage and its topmost boughs can hardly be a comfortable seat. Tolkien lets the dignity of the word do one thing and the facts of the tree do another: the narrator speaks of greenery and high refuge, while the reader pictures two old dwarves precariously wedged in a scanty, swaying crown.

Senses and contexts (polysemy). Though the word is a single, stable tree-term, it recurs in several contexts with distinct colour:

  • Principal branch (core) — a large limb springing directly from the trunk, as opposed to twigs and lesser growth; the sense in the Tolkien context, and the meaning the word has carried since Old English.
  • Literary and poetic use — the word of carols, folk song, and pastoral verse ('under the greenwood tree'), where boughs name shelter, canopy, and the architecture of the whole tree in its majesty.
  • Festive use — boughs of holly, evergreen boughs at Christmas, 'deck the halls with boughs of holly'; the tree-limb as decoration and welcome.
  • Emblematic use — the olive bough as an emblem of peace, from classical and biblical imagery (the dove returning with the olive leaf), where 'bough' carries ceremonial weight.
  • Homographic confusion — bough rhymes with cow and must be distinguished from the homograph bow (to bend, the archer's bow), a spelling collision deliberately created in the 15th century.

Register and imagery. As a literary word, bough implies the architecture of the whole tree — strength, antiquity, and sheltering spread — not mere twiggy growth, and it quietly lends the fleeing dwarves a touch of dignity even in their comic scramble. In ordinary modern speech bough has largely yielded to the neutral branch, surviving only where dignity or tradition is wanted — poetry, folk song, carol, and the storybook canopy. Its etymology is a body-word: the name descends from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning 'arm', so a bough is, etymologically, the arm of a tree — a metaphor preserved across the Germanic languages and, in Tolkien's hands, still doing its ancient work of making the tree a living, sheltering body. Distinguished from its neighbours: branches is the neutral, inclusive everyday word for any limb of any size; twigs are small terminal shoots — the opposite end of the scale from the great bough that bears them; limbs is chiefly American and colloquial, the word of the climbing child rather than of the poet; sprays are light flowering or leafy shoots; and foliage names leaves, not wood. Only bough insists on size, on growth directly from the trunk, and on a poetic, almost archaic elevation — the word of the Christmas carol and of 'under the greenwood tree' — which is exactly the register Tolkien's narrator commands.
