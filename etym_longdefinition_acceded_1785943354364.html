ACCEDED is the past tense and past participle of the verb accede — most commonly "to agree to a demand, request, or proposal" (especially an official one), and, in a second distinct sense, "to assume or enter upon an office or dignity", above all "to accede to the throne" (of a monarch). The verb is formal and literary, at home in diplomacy, law, and constitutional writing. In the context sentence of this card ("And you acceded to this prank") it carries the "agreed to" sense: you gave in to the prank.

THE MODERN SENSES IN FULL

  • "Agree to a demand, request, or proposal": "the king acceded to the barons' demands", "she acceded to his request"; usually with "to", and implying consent given after consideration or pressure.
  • "Join an international agreement": in law, "to accede to a treaty" means to formally become a party to a treaty already negotiated and signed by other states — a technical term of international law (hence the noun "accession").
  • "Assume an office or dignity": "acceded to the throne" (became monarch), "acceded to the presidency"; the official language of succession and inauguration.
  • Everyday-diplomatic extension: "acceded to a request" in business and formal correspondence, i.e. granted or yielded to it gracefully.
  • The noun "accession": both "agreement, assent" and "the act of taking office" ("the accession of Queen Victoria"); in libraries, "accession" also means the adding of a book to a collection.
  • Related but distinct words: "access" (a way of approaching; from the same Latin accedere), "accessory", "accession"; "acceded" also works as a participle adjective describing a state of having agreed.
  • Register: "acceded" is formal, bureaucratic, and ceremonial — a word of treaties, royal successions, and diplomatic minutes; it is not used in casual speech, where "agreed", "gave in", or "went along with" are normal.

THE HISTORICAL BRIDGE

The Latin verb accēdere means "to go to, to come near, to approach" — ad- "to" + cēdere "to go, move, yield". The literal, spatial sense of walking up to someone — "to go to join" — was extended in Latin itself to "to come to agree with", since to join a person's side is to fall in with their proposal. The same movement supplied the sense "to enter upon": you step up onto the throne. English borrowed the verb in the 15th century, and by the 17th the two modern senses — agreeing to terms and entering upon an office — were established, with the international-law sense of "acceding to a treaty" crystallising in the 18th–19th centuries. The semantic bridge runs, in short, from "approach" (a movement in space) to "assent and entry" (a movement in agreement and in office).

THE PAST TENSE AND ITS USE

As a past tense, "acceded" reports the completed act of formal agreement or entry and takes the preposition "to" ("acceded to the demands", "acceded to the throne", "acceded to the treaty"). It is the word of minutes, proclamations, and constitutional history, and its register is exactly what gives it weight: acceding is not merely agreeing but formally, officially, and finally agreeing — or formally stepping into a position of supreme authority.
