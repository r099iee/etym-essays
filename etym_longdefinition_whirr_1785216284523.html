Whirr is a noun denoting a continuous, low-pitched buzzing or humming sound made by something moving or rotating very rapidly — the blur of a bird’s or bat’s wings, the spin of a fan, the vibration of a machine — and it is also a verb meaning to make or move with such a sound. The word is onomatopoeic through and through: its own shape, whirr, with the breathy wh, the burring vowel, and the trilled rr, mimics the continuous, blurred, vibratory noise it names. As a noun, whirr names the sound itself; as a verb, to whirr is to produce it; and the participle whirring renders a moving object — bat’s wings, a fan blade, a spinning top — as one continuous vibration rather than a series of distinct flaps or turns.

In the literary context preserved on this card — “still he heard no sound of anything except the occasional whirr of a bat by his ears, which startled him at first, till it became too frequent to bother about” — whirr is J.R.R. Tolkien’s word, from The Hobbit (1937), for the sound of a bat’s wings beating the air close to a listener’s ear. The passage describes a traveller groping through the darkness of an underground passage, and the whirr is a small, vivid acoustic detail in a dead-silent world: sudden enough to startle him at first, yet so repetitive that it soon “became too frequent to bother about.” The word thus captures both the rapid, mechanical quality of the sound and its brief, almost insect-like character — a faint, busy noise that punctures silence rather than filling it. Nothing in the sentence suggests danger; the whirr is a nuisance, an interruption of the dark, and its homely, mechanical ring (like a spinning wheel or a clock) makes the underground world feel inhabited rather than utterly dead.

The word is polysemous, and its senses fall into a single family with clear branches:

  • Sound of rapid rotation: the continuous buzz of a fan, a spinning wheel, a motor, a disk, or any rapidly revolving thing — “the whirr of machinery.”
  • Sound of rapid vibration: a low, continuous buzzing vibration, as of a dentist’s drill, a sewing machine, or a hard drive (in older computers) — a tremulous drone rather than discrete beats.
  • Sound of wings: the blurred, rapid beating of bird or bat wings, especially in flight close by — “the whirr of a hummingbird.”
  • Verb, intransitive: to make such a sound — “the fan whirred all night.”
  • Verb, transitive / with motion: to move or carry something with such a sound — “the blades whirred through the air.”
  • Figurative, modern: of a busy place or mind, “to be whirring” with activity or thought — a humming, buzzing state of constant motion, as in “a whirr of activity.”
  • Spelling variants: whirr and whir (also older whurr) are both current; whir is the more common modern spelling, whirr the more emphatic, atmospheric form, which is why Tolkien and many literary writers prefer it.

The semantic bridge from sound to sense is direct: any rapidly moving or rotating object produces the same continuous vibratory blur, so the word moves easily from spinning wheels (its earliest uses) to wings, fans, machines, and finally, by metaphor, to any scene or mind in rapid motion. Unlike a buzz, which is insect-like and tonal, or a hum, which is musical and steady, a whirr is specifically the sound of rapid mechanical or physical movement — quick, blurred, and continuous, and slightly relentless. It is the noise of things that are fast, busy, and in a hurry.
