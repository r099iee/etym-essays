- [Arabic badawī, "dweller in the desert or steppe"] - [in Arabic the word contrasts with the settled townsman; a plain geographical label, neither flattering nor rude]
- [16th–17th c. entry into English via French bédouin] - [an outlandish, exotic word for an "Arab of the desert," wrapped in curiosity about the East]
- [18th–19th c. Orientalist romanticisation] - [perception hardens into the "noble Bedouin": free, fiercely hospitable, poetic, unchanging — an image European travellers both admired and patronised]
- [19th–20th c. loose extension] - [writers stretch the word to any wanderer or gypsy-like figure, diluting its ethnic precision]
- [late 20th–21st c. modern reality] - [most Bedouin are now settled or semi-settled citizens who keep the identity proudly; UNESCO (2008) inscribes Bedouin oral poetry as intangible heritage; the word is used by Bedouin themselves as an ethnonym of belonging]
