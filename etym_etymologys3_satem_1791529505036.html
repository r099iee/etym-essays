1. The Avestan corpus. Avestan is the language of the Avesta, the scripture of Zoroastrianism. Its oldest layer, the Gathas attributed to Zarathushtra, is composed in Old Avestan; the Younger Avesta, including the Yasna, Visperad, Vendidad and the Khordeh Avesta, is in a later form of the same language. The texts were transmitted orally for centuries and written down much later, in a script devised for the purpose in the Sasanian period. Avestan is an Old Iranian language, closely related to Old Persian, the language of the Achaemenid royal inscriptions, and more distantly to Vedic Sanskrit. It is not Middle Persian, the language of the later Zoroastrian books, and it is not modern Persian.

2. The numeral and its sound history. The word satəm descends from PIE *ḱm̥tóm "hundred". In Proto-Indo-Iranian the palatovelar becomes an affricate, reconstructed as *ć, giving *ćatám. In Iranian the affricate develops into a sibilant, and Avestan shows s-: satəm. Sanskrit, the other great Old Indo-Aryan language, shows śatám, with a palatal sibilant. Old Church Slavonic sъto, Lithuanian šimtas and Latvian simts show the same broad pattern in Balto-Slavic. The differences among s, ś and š are the result of separate later developments within the satem branches, not evidence of a single original sound.

3. The nineteenth-century discovery. The division between the two great groups was noticed gradually as comparative philology assembled the Indo-European family. August Schleicher's 1871 Compendium still assumed a single series of velars. Karl Brugmann's 1886 Grundriss distinguished palatal and plain velars and grouped languages by the treatment of labiovelars. In 1890 Peter von Bradke proposed the division in the form that became canonical, taking the Latin and Avestan words for "hundred" as the type specimens. Brugmann adopted the terms in the 1897 edition, and they passed into English usage in the 1890s. The labels were thus coined as pedagogical aids, not as reconstructible proto-forms.

4. The theoretical collapse. The twentieth century destroyed the idea that centum and satem were the two oldest branches of the family. The decipherment of Hittite and the discovery of Tocharian showed that the picture was more complex: Tocharian is the easternmost branch but behaves as centum, and Anatolian preserves a three-way dorsal distinction. Albanian and Armenian show mixed or debated patterns. The modern consensus treats the division as an areal isogloss, a wave of assibilation that spread across part of the late Indo-European dialect continuum. The word satem therefore shifted from "name of a branch" to "name of a sound change", and its theoretical status is now explicitly provisional.

5. Literature and attestation. The Avesta is the primary attestation of satəm, and the Gathas are its most studied layer. The Sanskrit Rigveda provides the parallel śatám; the Old Church Slavonic corpus provides sъto; Lithuanian and Latvian provide šimtas and simts. These texts are not merely data points for a sound law: they are the literary and religious monuments of the languages in question. The word for "hundred" appears in hymns, legal formulas, inventories and everyday counting, which is precisely why it is such a robust diagnostic. Its survival across millennia and scripts makes it a small monument to the continuity of Indo-European numerals.

6. Sound changes and debates.

  • Palatovelar assibilation: PIE *ḱ, *ǵ, *ǵʰ become sibilants or affricates in satem languages; the exact outputs differ by branch.
  • Ruki law: in Indo-Iranian, *s becomes *š after *r, *u, *k or *i, which complicates the simple sibilant picture.
  • Labiovelar merger: satem languages generally merge labiovelars with plain velars, while centum languages keep them distinct.
  • Incomplete satemization: Balto-Slavic and Indo-Iranian show residue of labial elements and alternating forms, suggesting a gradual spread rather than a single event.
  • Albanian and Armenian: both are often called satem, but their dorsal systems preserve distinctions that complicate the binary; Albanian may never have merged all three series.
  • Dating: the assibilation is dated to the late third or early second millennium BCE in the traditional account, but the dating is inferential and debated.

7. Historiographical caution. The centum–satem pair was sometimes used in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries to construct narratives of an "Aryan" east and a "western" civilization. Those narratives were scientifically unsound and politically dangerous. Modern Indo-European studies treats the division as one isogloss among many and rejects racial or evaluative readings. The word satem is now a neutral technical term, but its history is a warning about how linguistic classifications can be recruited for ideological purposes. Its survival in the discipline is due to its convenience, not to any claim of superiority or purity.

8. Modern usage. In contemporary writing, satem appears mainly in historical linguistics, in the phrase "centum–satem", and in descriptions of Indo-Iranian and Balto-Slavic. It is usually lower-case as an adjective and capitalized in branch names. It is not the name of a people or a language, and it should not be used as a synonym for "Aryan" or "eastern". The derivative satemization names the process, and satemize is a rare verb. For a general reader, the safest gloss is: satem = the "hundred"-with-s type, the eastern counterpart of centum.
