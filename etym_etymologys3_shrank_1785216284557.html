Shrank is the preterite of the strong verb shrink, whose Old English form is scrincan “to shrivel, contract, wither, draw together,” recorded in the earliest period of the language in literal use — of fruit, skin, and cloth drawing together — and soon after of a person “shrinking” from something in fear or repugnance. Old English scrincan belongs to the third class of strong verbs, with the paradigm scrincan — scranc — scruncon — (ge)scruncen: it is precisely the archaic singular preterite scranc that survives as modern shrank, while the old plural scruncon was levelled away in Middle English, leaving shrunk for the past participle and shrunken for the participial adjective. Behind the Old English verb stands Proto-Germanic *skrinkwaną “to contract, shrivel,” with attested cognates in Middle Dutch schrinken and Middle Low German schrinken “to shrink,” and probably related — through an ablaut variant *skremp-/skrump- — to German schrumpfen “to shrivel, shrink.” Beyond Germanic the etymology goes dark: the OED judges the ultimate origin uncertain, and scholars are inclined to see an expressive, perhaps echoic formation whose skr- cluster mimics the wrinkling, rasping, contracting motion itself; the frequently repeated link to a PIE root *sker- “to turn, bend” is suggestive but not secure, and no Indo-Aryan or Romance cognates can be demonstrated. In English the sense “to draw back, recoil from something frightening or distasteful” is old — attested from the thirteenth century in the idiom “to shrink from” danger, duty, or the touch of the unclean — and it grows directly out of the physical meaning: the flesh that shrivels and contracts when it dries is the same flesh that contracts and draws the whole body small in fear. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the participle “a shrinking nature,” “she shrank from publicity” painted the timid, the bashful, the faint-hearted — modest fear rather than dramatic panic — and the word became a standard vocabulary of moral and social timidity. In the twentieth century two further developments extended the family. In American slang, “head-shrinker” — from the practice of shrinking severed heads as trophies — was clipped to “shrink” (1966) as a jocular term for a psychiatrist, “one who compresses the troubled mind.” And the material sense turned economic and quotidian: shrinking economies, shrinking budgets, attention spans, and retail “shrinkage,” the modern coinage “shrinkflation” (products shrinking in size while prices hold), and “shrink-wrap” in packaging. Meanwhile the verb’s grammar has shown a long-noted drift: many speakers now use shrunk for the past tense (“he shrunk back”) and even shrunken, but standard literary English — and Tolkien — keeps the conservative shrank, a form that has survived unchanged from the Anglo-Saxon paradigm for over a thousand years. Key stages of the semantic history:
  • Old English — physical contraction: scrincan “to shrivel, contract, wither,” of cloth, fruit, and skin; the singular preterite scranc is the direct ancestor of shrank.
  • 13th century — figurative recoil: the idiom “to shrink from” transfers the body’s contraction to the mind’s withdrawal; fear shrinks the body as frost shrinks fruit.
  • 17th–19th century — timidity register: “a shrinking nature,” “shrinking violet”: the participle paints the timid and bashful — modest fear rather than dramatic panic.
  • 20th century — slang ‘shrink’: American English clips “head-shrinker” to “shrink,” a noun for the psychiatrist who compresses the troubled mind.
  • Contemporary — contraction everywhere: the physical sense turns economic and quotidian — shrinking economies, budgets, attention spans, retail shrinkage, shrinkflation — while “to shrink back” still preserves the primal, reflexive drawing-together of the body in fear.
Socio-cultural forces explain the range. The early-modern moralists’ love of psychological portraiture made “shrink” a word for conscience and shame — the soul that shrinks from sin — so the physical verb grew a whole moral vocabulary. The twentieth-century market economy turned contraction into a statistical phenomenon — shrinking GDP, shrinkflation — and the psychotherapeutic culture of mid-century America turned the head-shrinker into a familiar comic figure, giving the noun its modern slang life. All of these senses are the same image at different scales: things drawing together into smaller compass. In The Hobbit, Tolkien’s shrank back sits at the very oldest point of this spectrum — the reflexive, serpent-quick drawing-together of the whole body in fear — and the word’s thousand-year continuity from Anglo-Saxon scranc to Gollum’s cowering gives the sentence its strange antiquity: the reader hears, in one archaic preterite, the oldest English instinct of the flesh drawing itself small before the dark.
