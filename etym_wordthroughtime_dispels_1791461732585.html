- Latin, 1st c. BCE - dispellere is a forceful, physical verb: to drive apart a body of men, a crowd or a cloudbank; the tone is military and concrete
- Middle English, c. 1400 - borrowed through Old French, the verb keeps its literal force: knights dispel a host, wind dispels smoke, light dispels darkness; no abstraction yet
- 16th–17th c. - the metaphor of fog and darkness takes over: Protestant polemic and humanist prose speak of dispelling the darkness of error and ignorance; the word becomes a weapon of enlightenment rhetoric
- 18th–19th c. - dispel settles into polite educated prose; fears, doubts and rumours are dispelled; the physical sense survives mainly in nature writing about mist
- 20th c. - journalism, public relations and politics make "dispel rumours" and "dispel the myth" formulas; the word becomes a reassuring, institutional verb
- 21st c. - public health and fact-checking give "dispel misinformation" and "dispel vaccine myths"; the word now signals the authoritative correction of false belief, and its optimism is often quietly ironic
