Evolution of STUMPED. The past-tense form stumped rests on the verb stump, itself transferred from the noun stump: the short residual base left when a tree is felled, or by extension any truncated remainder — a limb, a tooth, a candle, a pencil, a cricket wicket. That noun is solidly Germanic. It enters Middle English in the late thirteenth century as stump, aligned with Middle Low German stump, Middle Dutch stomp, and Middle High German stum(p)f, and is routinely traced to a Proto-Germanic adjective *stumpaz carrying the sense ‘blunt, mutilated, cut short’ — the semantic core of something reduced to a stub. Cognate material across West Germanic keeps the same blunt-remainder image, while the North Germanic branch preferred the parallel formation stubbr (whence Swedish stubbe, Danish stub, Icelandic stubbi). No secure Proto-Indo-European equation for *stump- is generally accepted: handbooks sometimes compare it speculatively to PIE *(s)teu- ‘to push, knock, strike’ (the stock behind stow, stun, stupid, and Latin tundere ‘to beat’), but the equation is not firmly established, and the honest routing keeps the tree at the Germanic level.

By the early seventeenth century (attested around 1600–1610), English speakers verbalized the noun: to stump was to walk heavily or clumsily, as if on a wooden leg or on residual stumps, the footfall thudding and the gait stiff. The metaphor is concrete: the wooden prosthesis or the truncated base becomes the model for a noisy, rigid stride, and the verb immediately generates its inflected forms stumps, stumping, and stumped. The sound is part of the sense — the hard final cluster /-mp/ lands like a foot hitting the floor. In this walking sense the verb has a vivid literary history: it appears in English fiction whenever a character with a wooden leg or a stiff, offended gait crosses a room, from seventeenth-century stage comedy to Dickens and on to Tolkien, whose Bilbo “stumped off to the pantries” in exactly this register of comically dignified irritation.

From this same stock, further senses branched off in the nineteenth century without disturbing the walking verb. American stump-oratory (campaigning from a tree-stump platform, hence on the stump and stump speech) grew out of the practice of frontier orators haranguing crowds from a felled tree-trunk — a use recorded in American English from the early 1800s and still alive in political reporting. The idiom stump up (‘to pay reluctantly’, British) extended the image of digging money out of a pocket with a heavy thud. And the transferred sense ‘to baffle, nonplus, reduce to silence’ — as when an audience stumps a speaker, or a question leaves a student stumped — extends the same image of stopping someone short: a person halted as abruptly as if they had run hard against a tree-stump. This baffle-sense, attested from the early nineteenth century, has become the dominant everyday meaning of stumped, so completely that many modern speakers no longer associate the word with walking at all. In cricket, the specialised sense ‘dismissed by the wicketkeeper breaking the wicket while the batter is out of the crease’ (from the wooden stumps of the wicket, 18th–19th century) gives the word a technical sporting home that further multiplies its senses.

Tolkien's line requires only the literal walking sense: Bilbo is not baffled (that is the very next emotion, on a different word); he is walking — and his walking says what politeness forbids him to say. The word's long career from a Germanic blunt-remainder adjective to a comic gait verb to a metaphor for intellectual blockage is a textbook case of semantic layering: the oldest image — a thing cut short — is still faintly audible in every modern sense, from the stump of a tree to the stump of a leg to the stumped candidate frozen at the wicket.

  • Proto-Germanic *stumpaz ‘blunt, cut short’ → Middle English stump (13th c.) → English stump → verb to stump ‘walk heavily’ (c. 1600) → stumped.
  • Branches: baffle-sense (19th c.), cricket (18th–19th c.), American stump politics (early 19th c.), stump up (British).
  • Sound-symbolic core: the hard /st-/ onset and snapped /-mp/ coda enact the heavy footfall.
  • Debate: no secure PIE equation; the speculative *(s)teu- link is not firmly established — the chain stops at the Germanic level.
