The history of jaded is the biography of a tired horse that learned to describe tired souls. It begins in the late medieval stable with jade, a contemptuous word for a worn-out nag, the kind of broken-winded mare a miser would sell and a gentleman would mock, extended by the usual cruelty of slang to a worthless woman. No pedigree for this noun has ever been proved: Victorian scholars pointed hopefully at Old Norse jalda, a mare, but the phonology and the record are both too thin to carry conviction, and honest etymologists mark the origin unknown. What matters is the image: an animal ridden past its strength, dull-eyed and unresponsive, and a whole future of metaphor standing saddled and waiting.

Around 1600 the noun foaled a verb, to jade, meaning to ride a horse hard and by extension to exhaust anyone: Shakespeare's contemporaries speak of jaded travellers, jaded soldiers, jaded wits, all ridden out by effort. The past participle jaded from the 1630s simply named the result: used up, spent, done in. For a century and a half it stayed in the muscles, a word of roads and battlefields, until the 1770s, when polite society discovered a new use for it. The age of novels, grand tours, and rich tables needed a word for the fatigue of pleasure rather than of pain, and jaded was promoted from the stable to the salon: the jaded palate that rich sauces could no longer tempt, the jaded beau whom no ball could amuse.

Stages of the shift:

  • Stable contempt, 14th–16th c.: jade as nag and insult, low slang with a sneer built in; the horse too tired to trot becomes the emblem of worthlessness.
  • Physical exhaustion, 1600–1750: to jade and jaded spread through plays, sermons, and military memoirs for bodies worn by work, age, or dissipation; Samuel Johnson's Dictionary knows the verb well.
  • The turn inward, 1770s–1800: letter-writers and novelists begin applying jaded to appetites, sensibilities, and hearts; tiredness migrates from the legs to the capacity for joy.
  • Romantic and Victorian fashion, 19th c.: Byron's heroes, Thackeray's clubmen, and French-influenced talk of being blasé make jadedness the badge of the man about town who has tasted everything; meanwhile the literal horse sense fades from city streets as horsepower gives way to steam.
  • Modern psychology, 20th–21st c.: the word absorbs new clinical neighbours, burnout, compassion fatigue, and overstimulation, and becomes the everyday name for media-age exhaustion: jaded viewers, jaded electorates, jaded feeds, all prodded, as in the source sentence, only by wonders large enough to pierce the callus.

Phonetically the word barely moved across four centuries: jade plus the participial -ed, pronounced with the long modern vowel, needed no sound shift to do its new work. Semantically it travelled from hoof to heart, from the horse that will not gallop to the critic that will not applaud, and that journey explains its peculiar moral colour. Jaded never quite blames the sufferer the way cynical does, nor pities them the way exhausted does; it remembers the stable, where the fault lay as much with the rider who demanded too much as with the beast that could give no more. To call modern attention jaded is thus a quiet accusation against the age of excess itself: we have been ridden hard by plenty, and the spur of novelty draws less and less blood.
