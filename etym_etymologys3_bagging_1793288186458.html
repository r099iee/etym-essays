The etymology of bagging opens with a puzzle, because the innocent noun bag at its root has no certain ancestry beyond the Middle Ages. It appears in Middle English around 1200 as bagge, a sack of cloth or hide, with close cousins in Old Norse baggi, a pack or bundle, and the word feels thoroughly Norse in its hard double g and its workaday setting of markets, mills, and ships. Whether the Norsemen brought it or merely reinforced a native word of the same shape is one of those small debates that dictionaries note and cannot settle, since sacks are humble things whose names travel by mouth rather than by manuscript.

Older etymological handbooks bravely connected bag with a Proto-Germanic root *balg- meaning a swollen skin or bellows, which would make it kin to belly, bellows, bulge, and billow, all descendants of the idea of blowing up and swelling. The image is seductive: a bag is a belly of cloth, bellows are leather bags that breathe fire, and bulging is what full bags do. Modern scholarship, however, urges caution, pointing out that the sound correspondences are irregular and that trade words for containers are borrowed so freely across languages that a tidy family tree may be an illusion. The honest statement is that bag is first clearly seen in the Norse-influenced north and spreads south with commerce, its deeper roots obscure.

What is certain is the verb's steady growth once the noun was established. By the 15th century to bag meant to put corn or merchandise into bags, a miller's and maltster's term priced by the sack. The hop economy that Cobbett describes gave the verb some of its heaviest employment: Kent and Sussex hop-grounds picked their sticky cones in September, dried them in oast-houses over charcoal, and trod them into tall jute pockets for the London brewers, and each stage had its gang, its wage, and its grumble. Picking, drying, and bagging were the three toll-gates between bine and beer, and the fifty shillings Cobbett reckons covered sweat as much as sacking.

The descriptive sense, where things themselves bag without any sack in view, grows in the 18th century alongside looser tailoring and bigger sails. Breeches bag at the knee, stockings bag at the ankle, and sails bag with wind, each use transferring the sack's physics of flexible fullness onto new materials. The sporting sense follows in the 19th century through the game-bag, the canvas creel slung over the shooter's shoulder: birds in the bag are birds secured, and soon cricketers bag wickets, anglers bag salmon, and schoolboys bag the best apples in the orchard by the same cheerful extension.

Semantic and social landmarks:

  • Medieval markets: bagge holds grain, coins, and tools, the portable warehouse of pedlars and pilgrims.
  • Tudor mills: to bag becomes a priced operation in milling, malting, and hopping accounts.
  • Georgian seas and sails: canvas that bellies is said to bag, and tailors adopt the word for loose-hanging dress.
  • Victorian sport: the full game-bag crowns the shooting party, and a good bag enters the language of trophies.
  • Twentieth-century playgrounds: children bag seats, sweets, and bargains, turning harvest slang into everyday claiming.
  • Modern supermarkets: bagging becomes the checkout routine and the paper-versus-plastic debate, the ancient sack reborn at every till.

Literary attestations are correspondingly earthy. Agricultural writers from Tusser to Cobbett price bagging alongside threshing and carting; sporting memoirists boast of bags of grouse and bags of trout; novelists dress tramps and schoolboys in baggy clothes that signal poverty or comfort; and Australian newspapers record the rougher slang of bagging out, where words become weapons. The hop-pocket itself has become heritage, demonstrated at open-air museums where visitors may still tread hops into the tall sack and stitch its mouth, exactly the labour Cobbett costed at fifty shillings.

Today bagging holds all its layers without embarrassment. The combine harvester bags grain in plastic, the sailor reefs a bagging sail, the shopper watches groceries bagged at the till, and the teenager in baggy jeans wears the sack as fashion. From an obscure Norse bundle to a universal image of soft fullness, the word proves that humble tools make the hardiest metaphors, because every generation refills the bag and rediscovers the bulge.
