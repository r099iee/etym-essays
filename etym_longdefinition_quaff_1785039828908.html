QUAFF is the literary verb for drinking heartily, copiously, and with relish — drinking deep in large draughts, noisily and with evident pleasure. It is a word of robust festivity: the drinker of a quaff is not merely quenching thirst but celebrating, raising a great tankard or goblet and drinking it down with gusto. In the goblins' song, While Goblins quaff, and Goblins laugh, the verb paints the captors' gluttonous carousal in their dark feast-hall — drinking deep and laughing loud while their captives labor or await torment far underground. Its nuance is coarse, boisterous, and triumphant, and its old-fashioned flavor makes the celebration feel both merry and ominous.

The word enters English in the early sixteenth century (the verb by c. 1510) from an obscure origin — probably imitative of the glugging sound of rapid swallowing, or borrowed from Low German quassen, "to overindulge in food and drink." Its meaning has stayed remarkably stable for five centuries: from the first attestation it signified copious, hearty, relishful drinking.

Core senses in detail:

  • Primary — to drink deeply (verb, transitive): To drink (a liquid) in large draughts with relish. "They quaffed dark ale from crude iron tankards."
  • Primary — to drink deeply (verb, intransitive): To drink copiously and heartily. "The revellers quaffed till dawn."
  • Extended — a deep draught (noun): A single large, hearty swallow, or the drink so taken; recorded from the 1570s. "He took a long quaff of the wine."
  • Extended — a hearty drinker (agent noun, quaffer): One who drinks deep; attested 1510, now chiefly humorous or archaic.
  • Extended — fig. to drink in (rare/poetic): To take in eagerly, as of air, or by analogy an experience; a poetic extension of the physical act.
  • Modern coinage — quaffable: Of a beverage, especially wine or beer, pleasantly easy to drink in quantity; a 20th-century marketing and reviewing term.

Semantic bridge and rationale:

  • From sound to act: If the word is onomatopoeic, the glugging, gurgling noise of deep swallowing became the verb for the act itself; the same imitative logic underlies many drinking words.
  • Volume plus pleasure: Unlike gulp (a single swallow) or swig (quick and utilitarian), quaff uniquely combines copiousness with joy, ceremony, and relish — drinking deep gladly, usually in festivity.
  • Register: The word is now literary, archaic, and slightly comic; it evokes tavern scenes, feasts, and mock-heroic revelry rather than everyday drinking.

Modern usage contexts:

  • Literary prose and verse: Shakespeare, Milton, and later poets used quaff for hearty ceremonial drinking; modern writers borrow it for playful, old-fashioned effect.
  • Wine and beer writing: "quaffable" is a standard reviewer's term for an easy-drinking, undemanding wine or ale.
  • Comic and mock-heroic register: Modern speakers "quaff" a mug of ale in jest, deliberately sounding Elizabethan or jolly.
  • Rare literal use: The noun "a quaff" still names a hearty drink taken with pleasure.

In short, QUAFF uniquely joins copiousness with joy and vigor — drinking deep and gladly, often in festivity — which is exactly the goblins' carousing mood as they toast their own savage laughter round and round far underground.
