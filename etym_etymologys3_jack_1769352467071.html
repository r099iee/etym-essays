The biography of jack is one of the strangest in English, because it begins not with a Proto-Indo-European root but with a personal name — and a Hebrew one at that. The story opens with the Biblical name Yəhōchānān, "Yahweh is gracious," which the Greek-speaking world Hellenised as Iōánnēs and Rome Latinised as Iōannēs. Christianity carried the name across Europe, and in Old French it became Jehan and Jan, the common baptismal name of the Middle Ages. The pet-forms of John — Jankin, Jakin, Jacke — were the nursery and street versions of the name, the affectionate diminutives that parents actually shouted across the yard. By the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Jakke was so overwhelmingly common among English peasants and servants that it stopped being a particular person and became a word for any man at all: the generic Jack, the common fellow, the hireling, the servant-boy who turned the roasting-spit, hauled the water, and ran the errands. This is the pivot on which the whole history turns: the name of the most ordinary man in England became the word for ordinariness itself.

From "the common man" the word radiated outward in every direction. Because a medieval household depended on boys doing tedious manual work, the first mechanical devices that replaced those boys inherited the name: the turnspit jack (a clockwork device that rotated meat over the fire instead of a boy), the bootjack (which pulled off riding boots), the lifting jack (which raised heavy loads), and later the screwjack, the jack-plane, the jack-hammer, and the lumberjack's whole tool-world. Each "jack" is literally a substitute for a human Jack. The card sense arrived in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries when the court card showing a servant or page — formerly the "knave" — was renamed the Jack, and the maritime world named the small flag flown at a ship's bow the "jack," most famously in the "Union Jack" of Britain. The male animals took the name too: the jackass (male ass), the jackrabbit, the jack — the male of the species, once again the common male. Every sense preserves the original equation: Jack = the ordinary male who does the work.

The money sense, which is the point of this card, emerged at the meeting of two centuries and two continents. In late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century America, working-class speech took the generic "jack" — the fellow, the labourer — and turned it into the word for the thing a labourer earns: cash, wages, savings, stakes. John Steinbeck's Depression-era farmhands in Of Mice and Men give the sense its most famous literary form: "we're gonna get the jack together and we're gonna have a little house and a couple of acres an' a cow and some pigs." The phrase "get the jack together" is the dream of accumulated savings, the pool of earned money that will buy the future; it is money as the common man's stake, not as spending-cash. The word is perfect for the characters because it carries the whole democratic history of its own name — money earned by the man called Jack, saved by the man called Jack, spent by the man called Jack.

Contemporary English keeps all these layers alive at once. A mechanic reaches for a jack to lift a car; a gambler lays down his jack as the card; an engineer plugs a cable into a jack; a ship flies the jack; a vet treats a jack rabbit; and a labourer, in vanishing dialect, still wants to "get some jack." The name that began as a Hebrew prayer for divine grace, became the most common Christian name of Europe, degraded into a generic noun for the common man, then into the machines that replaced him and the money he earned, is now a whole family of homonyms — a living demonstration that a word's biography is a social history. Jack in its money sense is not just slang; it is the ghost of every labouring man who ever saved his wages, named after himself.
