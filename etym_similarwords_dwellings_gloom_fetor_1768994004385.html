• Home: Similar in being a place where one lives; differs because home is warm, personal, and emotional — the locus of belonging — while dwelling is formal, neutral, and structural, usable of beasts and of the dead.
  • House: Similar in being an inhabited structure; differs because house names the physical building as such, with no regard to whether anyone lives in it, while dwelling insists on occupation and habitation.
  • Residence: Similar in being a place of settled living; differs because residence is bureaucratic and administrative, the word of tax rolls and census forms, while dwelling, though formal, keeps a domestic and architectural concreteness.
  • Abode: Similar in being a dwelling place; differs because abode is poetic and slightly archaic, suggesting permanence and dignity, while dwelling is plainer and more neutral in register.
  • Habitation: Similar in being a place of habitation; differs because habitation is broad and can mean either the act of inhabiting or the place, and is usable of men or mice with no distinction, while dwelling is more specifically a human residence (or, ironically, a humanlike lair).
  • Domicile: Similar in being a legal place of residence; differs because domicile is a technical legal term for the jurisdiction where one has permanent residence for legal purposes, far more abstract than dwelling.
  • Lodgings: Similar in being a place where one stays; differs because lodgings suggests temporary, rented, or furnished accommodation, while dwelling implies a settled and usually permanent home.
  • Quarters: Similar in being a place of accommodation; differs because quarters suggests assigned or military/official housing, shared and impersonal, while dwelling implies a private, occupied home.
  • Accommodation: Similar in being shelter for living; differs because accommodation is an abstract, collective noun for housing provision, without the concrete image of a single inhabited building.
  • Shelter: Similar in being a place that protects; differs because shelter is any cover against danger or weather, temporary and minimal, while dwelling is a settled, built, and continuously inhabited residence.
  • Lair: Similar in being the abode of a creature; differs because lair is the den of a wild animal, deliberately set against dwelling as the bestial counterpart — the word the passage makes "Aihai dwellings" evoke.
  • Nest: Similar in being a place built to live in; differs because nest is the small, woven, non-human structure of birds or insects, and in metaphor suggests a home grown by instinct rather than architecture.
  • Darkness: Similar in being absence or near-absence of light; differs because darkness is total blackness, while gloom is partial dimness — a twilight or overcast obscurity in which forms are still faintly visible.
  • Dimness: Similar in being reduced light; differs because dimness is a neutral, physical description of weak illumination, without atmosphere, while gloom adds a heaviness, a felt oppressiveness of the air.
  • Dusk: Similar in being the dim light of evening; differs because dusk names the specific time of twilight, an hour of the day, while gloom is any dimness regardless of time and leans toward mood as well as light.
  • Twilight: Similar in being partial darkness; differs because twilight is the transitional light before dawn or after sunset, gentle and often beautiful, while gloom is thicker, duller, and more threatening — the light gone grey.
  • Shadow: Similar in being darkening of a space; differs because shadow is cast by an object blocking light, has a shape and source, while gloom is a general dimness of the whole atmosphere with no single cause.
  • Murk: Similar in being thick darkness; differs because murk is opaque, impenetrable blackness, usually foggy or sooty, while gloom keeps some visibility and often carries the emotional sense murk lacks.
  • Melancholy: Similar in being deep sadness; differs because melancholy is a clinical and literary word for sadness as a temperament, while gloom is the everyday word for a dark, heavy, despondent mood.
  • Sadness: Similar in being unhappiness; differs because sadness is a broad, mild, transparent emotion, while gloom is a heavy, brooding, obscuring sadness — one that dims perception as it dims spirits.
  • Despondency: Similar in being loss of hope; differs because despondency is a state of lost courage and low spirits, more passive than gloom, which pictures the mind darkened and oppressed.
  • Dejection: Similar in being low spirits; differs because dejection is a cast-down, dispirited state, while gloom emphasizes heaviness and dark color rather than mere lowness.
  • Stench: Similar in being a strong unpleasant smell; differs because stench is blunt, popular, and immediate — the everyday word — while fetor is literary, Latinate, and semi-technical.
  • Stink: Similar in being a bad smell; differs because stink is informal and can be a verb ("to stink"), while fetor is a formal noun naming the emanation itself, not the act of smelling.
  • Reek: Similar in being a strong offensive smell; differs because reek emphasizes the smoke-like, penetrating quality of the smell rising and spreading, while fetor is the static, clinging stench of corruption.
  • Odor: Similar in being a smell; differs because odor is entirely neutral — good or bad — while fetor is always foul and offensive.
  • Malodor: Similar in being an unpleasant smell; differs because malodor is a polite, euphemistic, technical word for bad smell, while fetor is stronger, franker, and more literary.
  • Effluvium: Similar in being a foul emanation; differs because effluvium is a noxious exhalation or outflow, often invisible and gaseous, while fetor focuses on the smell itself as perceived.
  • Miasma: Similar in being a foul atmosphere; differs because miasma is the ancient theory of disease-bearing bad air, a poisoning of the environment, while fetor is simply the odor, with no necessary theory attached.
