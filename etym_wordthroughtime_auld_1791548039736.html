- 7th–11th Century: Anglian Regional Retention - Maintained in the Anglian dialect of Old English as *ald*, preserving the original unbroken low-back vowel sound against the West Saxon vowel fracture into *eald*.
- 12th–14th Century: Northern Middle English & Early Scots Divergence - While Southern Middle English rounded long /aː/ into open-mid /ɔː/ (producing modern 'old'), Northern Middle English and Early Scots retained the unrounded vowel, spelling it *ald* and later *auld*.
- 15th–16th Century: Middle Scots Literary Standard - Thrived as the prestigious, official literary form in the golden age of Scottish court poetry (the Scottish Chaucerians including Robert Henryson and William Dunbar).
- 17th–18th Century: Post-Union Vernacular Revival - Reinvigorated following the Union of the Crowns and the 1707 Acts of Union as a passionate symbol of Scottish national heritage, celebrated in pastoral verse by Allan Ramsay, Robert Fergusson, and Robert Burns.
- 1788–19th Century: Global Festive & Nostalgic Icon - Robert Burns's immortal setting of 'Auld Lang Syne' catapulted the word into international usage as the universal expression of enduring friendship and New Year's commemoration.
- 20th–21st Century: Dialectological Paradigm & Cultural Heritage - Functions in linguistics textbooks as the primary exemplar of dialectal phonological divergence, while serving as a beloved marker of Scottish institutional identity.
