- 16th c. - English borrows dubious directly from Latin dubiosus, "wavering, doubtful," built on dubius, literally "of two minds"; the adverb dubiously, "in a doubtful manner," appears as the learned doublet of the everyday, French-derived doubt.
- 16th–18th c. - dubiously keeps a narrow manner-sense, attaching to verbs of judgment and speech; it carries the Latin sheen that plain doubtfully lacks, marking uncertainty as considered rather than reflexive.
- 19th–20th c. - the adjective dubious extends to "of questionable quality or honesty" (a dubious bargain), and dubiously shares that extension (dubiously acquired); the word now splits between a manner - acting with doubt - and a verdict - being of doubtful worth.
- 20th–21st c. - in narrative prose dubiously almost always modifies a bodily gesture - a look, a sniff, a shrug - performing doubt rather than stating it; it paints a mind literally divided between trusting and distrusting, which is exactly the work it does on Maspic's sniff.
