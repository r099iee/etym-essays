TUNNELLING — driving a road beneath the ground At its plainest, TUNNELLING is the present participle of the verb tunnel: to excavate a long underground passage, cutting a tunnel through rock, earth, or mountain. The word denotes deliberate, laborious, subsurface engineering — the slow extension of a traversable corridor through the material of the earth, whether by miners, moles, engineers, or prisoners. It is the British spelling, with the doubled -l-; American English writes tunneling with a single -l-. The action always implies depth, length, and the making of a passage that something can pass through. SENSE 1 — the verb of excavation (the core sense). To tunnel is to dig an underground passage: engineers tunnelling through granite, moles tunnelling under a lawn, the army tunnelling beneath enemy fortifications. The object may be the ground (to tunnel the mountain) or the passage (to tunnel a route). Since 1795 for the intransitive and 1856 for the transitive, the verb has named the deliberate creation of a horizontal, traversable corridor — a road beneath the ground. SENSE 2 — the noun: a tunnel. The passage itself: an underground or underwater corridor, as in a railway tunnel, a road tunnel, a mine adit, an escape tunnel. The noun, from the 1660s, is the modern sense of a word that earlier meant "tube, pipe," and still earlier "a bird-decoy net." A tunnel is distinguished from a mine by purpose (passage rather than ore) and from a trench by being roofed over. SENSE 3 — quantum tunnelling (physics jargon). In quantum mechanics, tunnelling (or tunneling) is the phenomenon by which a particle passes through a potential-energy barrier it does not have the energy to surmount classically — the particle "tunnels" through the wall instead of climbing over it. Named in the late 1920s as the new quantum theory was being worked out; it underlies radioactive decay, the scanning tunnelling microscope, and semiconductor physics. SENSE 4 — network tunnelling (computing jargon). In networking, tunnelling is the encapsulation of one protocol inside another so that data can travel over an incompatible network — VPN tunnelling, SSH tunnelling, GRE tunnels. The image is exact: one data stream is carried invisibly inside another, as through a pipe or passage. The tunnel is the private road driven through the public network. SENSE 5 — covert tunnelling. Because tunnels are secret by nature, the verb specializes to hidden, illegal digging: smuggling tunnels under borders, prison-escape tunnels, tunnels dug by burglars into bank vaults. To tunnel here is to undermine the surface unnoticed. SENSE 6 — idioms and derived uses. Tunnel vision is a narrowing of sight and attention to a single channel, as if looking through a tunnel (20th century, from the clinical loss of peripheral vision); the light at the end of the tunnel is the hope at the end of a long ordeal. Wind tunnel names the tube through which air is driven for testing models; carpal tunnel is the anatomical passage for nerves in the wrist (and carpal tunnel syndrome its affliction); tunnel of love is a fairground ride. POLYSEMY SUMMARY. The word's senses radiate from one root image: a long, hollow, tube-like thing, and the act of making or using it. A cask became a net, a net became a pipe, a pipe became a passage cut under a river or through a mountain — and from the passage came the quantum and the network tunnels of the twentieth century, every one of them a road, conduit, or path beneath or through a barrier. IN THE HOBBIT. Tolkien writes that the goblins of the Misty Mountains "had come on the lake, when they were tunnelling down long ago, and they found they could go no further; so there their road ended in that direction." The word carries the whole history of goblin engineering: Tolkien's goblins are miners and tunnelers by nature, enlarging ancient caves and joining them up with passages. Here tunnelling conveys deliberate, laborious, underground excavation on a vast scale — the slow gnawing of passage after passage through the mountain's bones — and its matter-of-fact placement in the narrator's geography lesson gives the goblins' ancient work a legendary, geological depth, as if the mountain itself were honeycombed by generations of unseen diggers. It is also the word that underlines the scene's irony: the goblins' proudest engineering feat simply dead-ends at an underground lake, for their road "ended in that direction." NEAR-SYNONYM BOUNDARIES (the excavation field):
  • Dig: the general term for any hollowing, from a garden hole to a mine — tunnelling is the specific making of a long, traversable passage.
  • Burrow: a small animal's cramped, irregular scratching, often furtive — tunnelling is deliberate, horizontal, engineering-scale.
  • Excavate: the formal, technical word for removing earth in construction or archaeology — tunnelling names the specific creation of a passageway, not any digging out.
  • Mine: stresses the extraction of minerals rather than the making of a passage — tunnelling drives a road that can be walked.
  • Bore: mechanical, circular drilling for shafts or blast-holes — tunnelling cuts a passage you can traverse.
  • Drill: to make a hole with a rotating tool — tunnelling is a larger, linear excavation, not a circular bore.
  • Undermine: to excavate beneath something so as to weaken it — tunnelling may undermine, but its purpose is passage, not collapse.
