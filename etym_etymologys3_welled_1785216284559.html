The biography of welled begins in the ancient Proto-Indo-European root *wel-, whose core meaning was "to turn, roll" — the fundamental motion of anything that wheels or swirls, a root with a vast descendant family across the Indo-European languages. In the Germanic branch this rolling motion attached itself to water: the Proto-Germanic strong verb *wallaną meant "to boil, bubble up, surge", the churning of liquid heated or stirred into motion, and its cognates span the old Germanic world — Old Norse vella "to boil, bubble", Old High German wallan "to surge, seethe" (modern German wallen, from which, with a suffix, comes Welle "a wave"), and Middle Dutch wellen "to swell, spring up". Old English inherited two related verbs: the strong verb weallan, "to boil, bubble, surge; to well up", used of seas, fountains, and of blood pouring from a wound, and the weak verb wellan, "to make boil". Beside them stood the derived noun wella, "a spring of water" — the very spot where water wells out of the earth — which passed through Middle English welle into modern English well. Middle English levelled the verbal system into a single wellen, "to rise up and flow forth", and the modern verb well, almost always followed by up, has kept that intransitive sense of spontaneous upwelling ever since.

Sound change explains the form. From the PIE root, Proto-Germanic built the strong verb *wallaną with the characteristic Germanic ablaut (well-forms in the present, wall-forms in the past, woll- in the participle), and Old English weallan displayed the familiar apophony weallan–wēoll–wollen ("to surge–surged–surged"), while the derivative noun rested on the o-grade. As Old English's unstressed endings levelled in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, weallan and wellan merged into a single Middle English wellen, and the spelling settled on well in early modern English with the vowel stable at /wɛl/. The past tense, originally strong (wēoll), was regularised to a weak welled with the dental suffix -ed — the normal path by which English verbs of motion, weather, and natural process (boil, well, melt, flow) drifted into the majority weak class — so that today welled is an entirely regular formation, its strong ancestor visible only in archaic and dialectal survivals.

Semantically, the career of the verb is a steady march from the earth to the heart. In Old English the sense is wholly literal and elemental: seas and springs well, fountains rise, blood wells from a wound. The sixteenth century widened the word to tears and bodily fluids — "tears welled in her eyes" is recorded from this period — with the image of liquid rising to the rim and gathering there under internal pressure. The great transfer came in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when the Romantic and Victorian imagination of the emotions as a hidden reservoir within the person allowed pity, gratitude, understanding, and memory to "well up" in the heart and mind. The key literary witness is the tradition of sensibility — the novel of feeling — in which the involuntary upwelling of tears and sympathy is the signature of a morally responsive soul; words like "well", "brim", and "overflow" became the vocabulary of that cult of the heart. By the twentieth century the emotional use had become the dominant one, and in the sentence above Tolkien, a philologist of genius, deploys it with full historical awareness: the physical spring is the root image, but what wells is now compassion itself.

In society and literature the word has always carried the associations of purity, origin, and blessing. A wellspring is the source of nourishment, and "to well up" retains that aura of the pure, the unbidden, and the life-giving — the opposite of the forced, the artificial, or the violent. It appears in the King James Bible ("the well of life"), in the poetry of Wordsworth and the prose of Dickens and Hardy for tears of sympathy and memory, and in countless modern contexts for emotion rising involuntarily. The noun has drifted its own way: from "a spring" to "a shaft sunk to water", to the oil well of the petroleum age, to the stairwell and the wheel-well of modern engineering, always preserving the notion of a vertical channel through which something rises from beneath — and in the stairwell, the thing that rises is the traveller himself. Across the family the image is constant: a turning, surging motion; but it is in the Germanic stream, and in Old English weallan above all, that the sense of gentle, abundant, unstoppable upwelling — the sense Tolkien so exactly uses — was born and has been preserved for over a thousand years.
