Bay (noun) in its primary modern sense is a broad indentation of a coastline — or of the shore of a lake or river — a curve of water lying between two projecting headlands or points of land, typically sheltered and therefore a natural anchorage. In the source context — "the entering river by a promontory of rock which formed a calm bay" — the word denotes exactly this: the river's mouth curving behind a rocky headland into still water. Geography orders the sizes loosely: a cove is smaller than a bay, a gulf larger; a bight is a wide, gentle curve; a sound is a long broad channel. But the word's centre is not precision: it is shelter.

The polysemy of "bay": five words in one form

English "bay" is a famous case of homonymy — five unrelated words that happen to share a spelling and pronunciation. Dictionaries number them, and the numbers matter for this card because the context's "bay" is only the first:

  • 1. Bay, the body of water (the present card's sense): an inlet of the sea, lake, or river, from Late Latin baia via Old French baie; the note's "calm bay" behind a promontory is the classic early sense — a sailor's sheltered place.
  • 2. Bay, a recess or compartment of a building: from Old French baee "opening" (baer "to gape"), so a bay is an open recess in a wall or structure — bay window, sick bay, loading bay, cargo bay, bomb bay. The popular merging of this "opening" sense with the water sense is a folk-etymological confusion (the sea also seems to "open" into the land), but the two are distinct in origin.
  • 3. Bay, the laurel tree (and its leaf and berry): from Old French baie "berry" (Latin baca), the laurel's berries; "bay leaf" in cooking; poetically, "bays" are the laurel wreath of victory — "to win one's bays" means to earn honour.
  • 4. Bay, the horse's colour: a reddish-brown coat, from Old French bai, Latin badius "chestnut-brown" — "a bay mare".
  • 5. Bay, the baying of hounds: from Old French abaiier "to bark" (imitative); "to hold at bay" and "to bring to bay" come from the hunt, where a stag or boar turns to face the baying pack — hence "to keep someone (or something) at bay", to hold off an attacker or threat.

The semantic logic and bridges

The water sense has no metaphor in it: a bay is what the sea does to the land, and the word simply names the shape. The other senses arrived by different roads and settled into the same sound: the "gape" family gave the architectural bay; the berry gave the laurel; the horse's colour came from Latin equestrian vocabulary; the hounds' chorus came from an imitation of barking. English speakers have long felt a covert kinship between the water bay and the "open" bay — both are openings or recesses — and popular etymology has fused them for centuries, but the written record keeps them apart. For lexicography, the five bays are a standing lesson that identical forms can conceal five separate histories.

Place-names and modern uses

  • Water: Hudson Bay, Chesapeake Bay, the Bay of Biscay, Monterey Bay, Botany Bay; adjectivally in "bay shore", "bay water", "bay area".
  • Metonymy of place: "the Bay" can mean a whole region — the San Francisco Bay Area (and, in shorthand, its technology economy), Tampa Bay, Green Bay; "the Bay State" is the standard nickname of Massachusetts, from the Massachusetts Bay Colony.
  • Architecture, engineering, computing: bay window, sick bay, loading bay, docking bay, engine bay, server bay — the "opening" sense alive and productive in modern technical language.
  • Biology and cookery: bay tree, bay leaf; "bay rum" is a fragrant lotion distilled from the leaves of a West Indian bay tree (Pimenta racemosa), a 19th-century colonial product.

Grammar and register

As a water noun, "bay" is countable (a bay, bays), takes of-phrases freely ("the Bay of Bengal"), and is common in both technical geography and everyday speech; its register is plain and serviceable, with a permanent place in nautical prose and in romantic landscape description alike. The phrase structures of the other senses are equally productive ("at bay", "bay window", "bay leaf", "bay mare"), so the single form "bay" is one of the most polymorphous little words in the dictionary — a water shape, an opening, a tree, a colour, and a chorus of hounds.
