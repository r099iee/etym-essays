- Ancient Greek to Classical Latin (c. 5th c. BCE–1st c. CE) - Greek *kōnōpeion* denoted a couch or bed fitted with fine netting to protect against mosquitoes (*kōnōps*); borrowed into Latin as *conopeum* with Egyptian luxury connotations.
- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed via Anglo-Norman French *canapé* into English as *canope*, designating a rich fabric covering suspended over a bed or altar to shield against dust and signify high status.
- 16th to 17th Century (Elizabethan & Jacobean Eras) - Broadened dramatically to ceremonial state processions and royal thrones (velvet canopies carried over monarchs); famously used by Shakespeare in *Hamlet* to describe the starry firmament ("this most excellent canopy, the air").
- 18th to 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Solidified in royal fairy-tale wedding pageantry (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*) as an emblem of imperial luxury; applied in architecture to cantilevered porticos.
- 20th Century (Modern Science & Aviation) - Acquired crucial specialized scientific senses: the forest canopy in tropical ecology, the transparent acrylic cockpit bubble in fighter jets, and the deployed nylon fabric wing in parachuting.
- 21st Century (Contemporary Usage) - Universally used across ecology, architecture, aerospace, and everyday design, spanning from urban tree canopies to sleek glass transit shelters.
