INSISTENT is a Latin word built on the most basic verb of position in the Indo-European family: to stand. At the root core is PIE *steh₂-, 'to stand, be standing', a root so ancient and so widespread that it produced the stand-words of nearly every daughter language — English stand, German stehen, Latin stāre, Greek ἵστημι, Sanskrit sthā-. From this root the Italic branch made a special, reduplicated present, *stí-steh₂-, 'to cause to stand, to set, to place', which Latin inherited as sistō, sistere. The verb sistere means to make something stand up, to set it in position; and out of it Latin built a whole family of compounds, each one a stance: resistere (to stand against), exsistere (to stand forth, to exist), consistere (to stand together), dēsistere (to stand off, to desist).

The particular compound that produced insistent is īnsistere, formed with the prefix in- in its sense of 'on, upon': to īnsistere is to stand upon something — to plant one's foot on a point, to set foot on a path, and from that literal treading, to press upon a matter, to insist on it. Latin turned this verb into its present participle, īnsistēns, īnsistent-, 'standing on, pressing, urgent', and this participle is the direct ancestor of the English adjective, adopted in the seventeenth century at the height of English's borrowing from Latin.

  • Sound shifts and forms: the Latin prefix in- and the reduced vowel of sistēns combine as īnsistēns; the cluster /ns/ was preserved in the English spelling, and the final Latin participial ending -ent- survives intact in insistent, exactly as in consequent, dependent, and latent. The verb insist (1580s) came into English at nearly the same time, straight from īnsistere, and the nouns insistence and insistency were formed on it in the following decades.

Once in English, insistent split along two lines of pressure. The physical, spatial sense — standing on, resting on — faded into obscurity; what remained was the figurative sense of pressing demand: the standing-on of a claim, a plea, a belief. By the nineteenth century the word was thoroughly naturalized for persons and signals alike — the insistent salesman, the insistent knocking — and in technical registers it found new homes: an insistent symptom in medicine, an insistent motif in music, an insistent claim in law.

  • Semantic phases: (1) PIE *steh₂- 'stand'; (2) reduplicated *stí-steh₂- 'cause to stand'; (3) Latin sistō 'set, place'; (4) īnsistere 'stand upon, press upon'; (5) īnsistēns 'pressing, urgent'; (6) English insistent (1620s) — from literal standing-on to unrelenting demand.

Today insistent is the word for pressure that refuses to break off. Its Latin anatomy is perfectly legible — in- 'on' and sistere 'to stand' — so that to be insistent is, quite literally, to keep standing on the thing one wants, a two-thousand-year-old image of planted feet and unyielding will.
