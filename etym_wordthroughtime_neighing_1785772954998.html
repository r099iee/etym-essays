- Old English hnægan — Perceived as the natural verb for the horse's cry in a culture of warhorses, plow animals, and status steeds; the hn- onset still audible in careful reconstruction of early pronunciation.
- Middle English neyen / neighen — Spelling and sound modernize as h- drops; common in agricultural and chivalric contexts; the form is ordinary, not elevated.
- Early Modern–18th century — Standard verb/participle for horse calls in husbandry, travel writing, and drama; little stigma or metaphor yet dominant.
- 19th century literary peak — Neighing becomes a stock acoustic detail of rural idylls, storm-panicked stables, and cavalry charges; Romantic and Victorian ears "hear" landscape through it.
- 20th–21st century — Still the default English word for the sound; ethology may prefer finer terms (nicker, squeal, whinny subtypes), but public perception of neighing remains the loud classic horse call. Metaphorical "neighing with laughter" survives as mildly comic or unflattering. The exact form neighing is transparent, non-taboo, and stably denotative.
