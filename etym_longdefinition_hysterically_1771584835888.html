HYSTERICALLY, in the adverbial form, describes behaviour overwhelmed by wild, uncontrolled emotion — laughter, sobbing, or panic that has broken free of all restraint. In this scene from The Story of the Stone, Bao-yu, his face "streaming with tears," is "sobbing hysterically" because he has just learned that none of his girl cousins possesses a jade like the one that defines his own special existence. The tone of the word here is violent and convulsive: it conveys a grief that shakes the whole "delicate frame," a cry that is not dignified or controlled but helpless, spasmodic, almost animal in its intensity. Because of its deep history — hysteria takes its name from the Greek hystera, "womb" — the adverb also carries a ghost of ancient medical doctrine, in which violent emotion in women was blamed on a wandering womb. Deploying "hysterically" of a young man is therefore quietly subversive: it applies to Bao-yu the vocabulary of the "female malady," aligning his excessive, emotional nature with the feminine, and suggesting that in his character the ordered, courtly masculinity of his household is repeatedly broken through by a deeper, womblike, primal feeling.

Polysemy and the full range of uses:

  • Of genuine convulsive emotion — sobbing, laughing, or panic beyond control: "she was sobbing hysterically"; the sense at work in the context sentence.
  • As an everyday intensifier — the weakened modern use: "hysterically funny," "hysterically loud," "hysterically busy" — meaning extremely or to an absurd degree, with no implication of actual hysteria.
  • Historical medical sense — behaviour attributed to the old diagnosis of hysteria, once believed to arise from the womb (Greek hystera); a sense now confined to historical or diagnostic writing.
  • Gendered and cultural freight — the adverb still carries, faintly, the charge of the nineteenth-century "female malady," so that applying it to a man is often pointed or subversive.

Boundary comparisons sharpen the word's exact force. Uncontrollably is the broad, neutral adverb for any loss of control and lacks both the convulsive quality and the medical-historical freight. Frantically stresses desperation and urgent, agitated action — one rushes about frantically, but one does not necessarily sob frantically; it is more panicked than overcome. Convulsively emphasizes the physical jerking and spasming of the body, the mechanical twitching, without the emotional wildness that hysterically implies. Desperately points to hopeless urgency and a kind of grim purposefulness, a despair that is goal-directed rather than dissolved in feeling. Wildly is the loose, everyday intensifier for any lack of control, with none of the clinical history. Manically and frenziedly stress agitation and excitation rather than the overflow of grief or mirth. Hysterically is distinguished precisely by combining a total loss of emotional command with a specific, quasi-medical history — the word that turns an excess of feeling into a diagnosis.
