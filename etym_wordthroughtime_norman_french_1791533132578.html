- 1066–1100 — The speech of the conquerors: a northern French dialect brought by a small ruling elite, unwritten as a standard, associated with lordship, land and the court. To speak it is to be on the winning side.
- 12th c. — Anglo-Norman becomes a literary language in its own right: Wace, Gaimar and Marie de France write for an Anglo-Norman public. The name suggests a confident, cross-Channel aristocracy.
- 13th c. — French is the language of law, school and fashion, but English is the language of the majority. “Norman-French” begins to be felt as the speech of a caste, and bilingualism becomes ordinary among the middling sort.
- 1204–1258 — The loss of Normandy and the Provisions of Oxford weaken the continental tie; the French of England is increasingly an inheritance rather than a living link.
- 14th c. — The Statute of Pleading (1362) and the rise of English literature reduce its official role. Chaucer’s Prioress speaks French “after the scole of Stratford atte Bowe,” not of Paris: the insular variety now sounds provincial.
- 15th–17th c. — Anglo-Norman survives mainly as Law French, a technical jargon of the inns of court, and as a marker of old family prestige. The phrase becomes antiquarian.
- 18th c. — Law French is abolished in 1731, and “Norman-French” becomes a historical-linguistic label for the medieval language and its legal fossils.
- 19th c. — Philology classifies Norman as a langue d’oïl and traces English doublets to it; the term acquires a scholarly, slightly romantic aura of the “Norman yoke.”
- 20th–21st c. — “Norman-French” usually means Anglo-Norman in academic writing, while the modern dialects are called Norman, Jèrriais or Guernésiais. The phrase now carries the memory of conquest, law and linguistic fusion.
