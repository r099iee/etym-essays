• Clotted: Similar because both mean thickened into lumps or clots; differs because clotted covers blood and cream alike (clotted blood, clotted cream as a delicacy), while curdled stresses sour separation into curds and whey.
  • Coagulated: Similar because both describe liquids setting into solids; differs because coagulated is the clinical term (coagulated blood, coagulated protein), while curdled is the kitchen word with a sour smell.
  • Congealed: Similar because both mean thickened from fluid to stiff; differs because congealed is cooling and stillness (congealed gravy, congealed opinions), while curdled is acid and separation — lumps plus liquid.
  • Soured: Similar because both describe milk turned and sweetness lost; differs because soured names the taste and temper (soured milk, soured relations), while curdled names the lumpy texture that souring produces.
  • Split: Similar because both describe sauces and mixtures separating; differs because a split sauce is cooks' neutral jargon (the mayonnaise split), while curdled dramatises the disaster with dairy imagery.
  • Separated: Similar because both mean a mixture parted into its components; differs because separated is neutral and often deliberate (separated whey in cheese-making), while curdled implies unwanted ruin.
  • Thickened: Similar because both involve liquid growing dense; differs because thickened is usually success (thickened gravy, a thickening plot), while curdled is thickening gone wrong — lumpy instead of smooth.
  • Fermented: Similar because both are milk transformed by microbes; differs because fermented is controlled craft (fermented yoghurt, fermented beer), while curdled is accident — or cheese, which is accident tamed.
  • Chilled (in curdle the blood): Similar because both freeze the veins in horror idioms; differs because chilled is cold air on skin (chilled to the marrow), while curdled imagines the blood itself clotting with fear.
