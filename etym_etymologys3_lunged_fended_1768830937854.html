THE EVOLUTION OF LUNGE The word of lengthening. 'Lunge' is the English form of the French noun allonge, 'a lengthening, an extension,' from the verb allonger, 'to lengthen, stretch out,' itself from Latin longus, 'long,' and ultimately from the Indo-European root *dlengʰ-, 'long' — the same root as English long. The word's DNA is the idea of length: a lunge is an act of making the body long. It entered English in the eighteenth century through the fencing school, where the lunge is the fundamental attack — the deep, explosive extension of the front leg with the body's weight thrown behind the blade, the whole frame stretched to its farthest reach to drive the point home. The term was a precise, athletic piece of technical vocabulary: the fencing master's command, the length of the stretch measured in inches of reach. From the salle to the street. In the nineteenth century the word breaks out of the fencing hall and becomes the ordinary verb of sudden forward attack. Boxers lunge, tigers lunge, drowning men lunge; the whole body is thrown forward in one explosive, extended reach. The word keeps the geometry of the original: a lunge is linear, committed, and whole-bodied — it is not a casual step but the body's full investment in a single direction. The dark logic of commitment. Narrative prose of the twentieth century and after has kept the word's dangerous logic. Because a lunge commits everything to the attack, it is precisely the move a skilled opponent can punish: the extended arm cannot be recalled, the stretched body is exposed, the overcommitted fighter is the vulnerable fighter. The card's context is a perfect demonstration — 'when she lunged at him again, he fended off the blow and leaning forward, touched the point to her arm' — the lunge's very commitment is what lets the defender step inside it. The word thus carries its meaning and its consequence in a single gesture. The merged doublet. A separate borrowing, French longe, 'a long rope,' from Latin longa, gives the horse-training lunge: the circular exercise in which a horse is worked on a long rope. The two words — the fencing thrust and the training rope — merged in spelling in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and are now felt as one, both bound by the shared root idea of length. Modern English uses 'lunge' for both, and the fencing sense remains the word's historical heart. THE EVOLUTION OF FEND The clipped descendant. 'Fend' is an aphetic form of 'defend' — English simply dropped the initial syllable. The shortening happened in Middle English (by the fourteenth century), and the clipped verb kept its ancestor's full meaning: to ward off, to repel, to strike a blow away. The root reaches back through Latin defendere, 'to ward off, protect,' to fendere, 'to strike,' and finally to the Indo-European root *gʷʰen-, 'to strike, slay' — the same root that gives Sanskrit hanti and Greek theinein, both 'to strike.' Etymologically, to fend off a blow is to strike the blow away. The workmanlike life. From the sixteenth century 'fend' develops its own life alongside 'defend.' The reflexive 'to fend for oneself' appears — to manage, to get along, to supply one's own needs — the everyday self-reliance of the capable poor. To fend for oneself is to beat off hunger, cold, and need with one's own hands. The phrasal 'fend off' stays martial: blows are fended off, attacks are fended off, questions and reporters are fended off with a wave of the hand. The sharp deflection. In combat prose, fending is the active, economical deflection — one sharp movement that turns an attack into an opening. It is the opposite of passive guarding: to fend is to act, to strike away, to answer force with a precise, minimal counter-force. On the card, Turjan 'fended off the blow and leaning forward, touched the point to her arm' — the fend is not merely defensive; it is the pivot on which the whole exchange turns, converting the woman's attack into her own defeat. Fend today. Contemporary English keeps the word mostly in its two fixed phrases — 'fend for oneself' and 'fend off' — and its bare, muscular core is still felt: the immediate act of striking a threat away. Its evolution is the story of a prefix dropped and a meaning kept: two centuries after 'fend' was merely 'defend' without its first letter, it had become a word in its own right, leaner than its parent and, in the hands of a good writer, sharper.
