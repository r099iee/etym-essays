PARRICIDE — linear chain for parricide:
1. PIE *kh₂eyd- "to cut, strike, kill" (c. 4000–3000 BCE; probably, and debated) — the -cide root, reflected in Latin caedō; its deeper reconstruction is not universally accepted.
2. Latin caedō, caedere "to cut, hew, strike, kill" (classical, 1st c. BCE) — source of the combining forms -cīda "killer" and -cīdium "the killing" (cf. homicīdium "homicide," regicīdium "regicide").
3. Latin paricīda "one who kills a parent or near kin" (the older form; from pār "equal" + -cīda, "killer of an equal") → reshaped as parricīda, with the -rr- pulled by folk etymology toward pater "father".
4. Latin parricīdium "the crime of parricide" (Roman law, Republic and Principate) — extended also to treason against the state, the "fatherland."
5. Old French parricide (c. 12th–13th c.).
6. Middle English parricide (a. 1560s) → English parricide (TARGET) — also the person so called.
Note on the component pār: Latin par "equal" is itself from PIE *per- "to bring forth, allot, produce" (the root of "equal" and "parent"); the deeper chain for that element is a separate, secure line, but the word's operative components are pār + -cīda.

HAMLET — linear chain for hamlet:
1. PIE *tkei- "to settle, dwell, be at home" (zero-grade *tki-; c. 4000–3000 BCE) — Greek kept it in ktízō "to found, build, settle," and Indic may preserve it (disputedly) in Sanskrit kṣeti "to dwell."
2. Proto-Germanic *haimaz "home, dwelling, village" (c. 500 BCE–CE 500) — Gothic haims still meant "village," Old Norse heimr "home, world," Old English hām "home, village" (and the -ham of place-names like Nottingham).
3. Frankish / Gallo-Romance borrowing: *haim → Old French ham, hameau "small village" → Old French hamelet "small hamlet" (diminutive -el + -et, the ancestors of English -let).
4. Middle English hamlet (14th c.) "a small village, a cluster of houses without a church."
5. English hamlet (TARGET) — the settlement on this card; 1600 also Shakespeare's prince Hamlet, from the Danish legend of Amleth (a different but cognate branch of the same "home" root).
Debate/nuance note: English hamlet was borrowed from Old French, not descended directly from Old English hām, though both go back ultimately to the same Germanic stock *haimaz. The connection of the prince's name Amleth to the "home" root is traditional and widely repeated but not beyond dispute.
