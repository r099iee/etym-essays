The history of tussle is short, honest, and refreshingly free of conquest: it is a little word for little struggles, coined by adding a little ending to a rough verb. Its parent, tousle — to rumple, to pull about, to handle without ceremony — appears in Middle English around 1400 as touselen, describing what wind does to thatch and what playing children do to each other's clothes. The -le suffix is the frequentative, English's way of making an action small and repeated: as wrestle is to wrest and nuzzle to nose, so tussle is to tousle — a repeated, diminished tousling shared between two parties.

Phonetically the change from tousle to tussle is vowel-shortening under the light stress of a colloquial frequentative, the same levelling seen in pairs like douse and dussle in dialect. The spelling with u fixes the short vowel, and the hissing ss — really the same s doubled by scribes — gives the word its scuffling soundtrack. Say tussle slowly and the tongue wrestles the palate twice, a miniature of the action named. Eighteenth-century orthoepists tutted at such expressive coinages as low, which is precisely why the people kept them.

From tousling to headlines:

  • Middle English (c. 1400 – 1500): touselen handles things roughly — hair tousled by wind, clothes tousled in play — with no mutuality yet and no noun of contest.
  • Tudor and Stuart England (16th – early 17th c.): tousle and dialectal touze (to pull, to worry as a terrier worries a rat) circulate in speech; Shakespeare's world knows the action though not yet the noun.
  • 1620s — first record: tussle surfaces in print meaning a small struggle or scuffle, the frequentative fully formed and instantly at home in jest-books and country pastimes.
  • 18th c.: poets and letter-writers use it for mock-heroic effect — lapdogs tussle, beaux tussle over fans — trading on the gap between the word's roughness and its smallness.
  • 19th c.: the sporting press discovers it: rucks, scrums, and electoral contests become tussles, and the figurative sense (a tussle over a clause, a tussle for the title) detaches from actual bodies.
  • 20th – 21st c.: headline English makes it ubiquitous — cabinet tussles, transfer tussles, courtroom tussles — while the nursery keeps the literal sense warm, and Tolkien's pantry tussle shows the word doing what it has always done best: making conflict cuddly.

The debated fringe concerns the ultimate root of tousle. Nineteenth-century philologists, hungry for antiquity, proposed Old Norse tosa, to shred or tease wool, or a lost Proto-Germanic *tūs-, to tear — attractive guesses that would push the family back to Viking fingers in fleece. Modern etymologists, led by the OED's caution, mark the connection unproven: the Norse forms explain wool-working better than wrestling, and the sound correspondences wobble. What is certain is the English-internal story, frequentative -le on a native rough-handling verb, first attested when James I sat the throne. Tussle therefore belongs to that cheerful class of words — scuffle, bustle, hustle, wrestle — whose very shape, short vowel plus busy -le, performs the bustling action they name. Four centuries on it has never grown solemn, never entered statutes or liturgies, and never needed to: its job is the small brave business of the playground, the pantry, and the back page.
