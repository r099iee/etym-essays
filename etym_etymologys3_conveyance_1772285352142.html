The noun conveyance is a late, abstract formation on the verb convey, but its deeper history is the Proto-Indo-European root *weǵʰ-, whose meaning was "to go, to move, to carry, to travel in a vehicle." That root is extraordinarily productive across the family. Sanskrit vahati "carries, conveys" (with vāhana "vehicle" and vāhinī "a convoy, army train") preserves the verb alive; Greek formations built on ókhema/ókheios give words for carriage and conveyance; Germanic kept it in wegan "to carry, weigh," Old English weg "way," and English weigh, wagon, wain; and Latin re-deployed it in two directions at once — as vehere "to carry, convey" (source of vehicle, vehiculum, vector, and vex in its older senses) and as via "way, road." The "way" and the "vehicle that travels the way" are thus two faces of one ancient idea: motion along a path. Whether via itself descends from *weǵʰ- or from a distinct root *weyh₁- "to go, chase" is debated, but its connection to the "way" concept of the family is beyond dispute.

From via, Latin speakers formed compounds that keep the way in view:
  • viāticum "provisions for a journey" (English viaticum), deviāre "to turn from the way" (English deviate, devious), and obvius "in the way, meeting" (English obvious, via "standing in the path of sight").
  • Late or Vulgar Latin *conviāre — prefix com- "with, together" + via — meant "to go with along the way, to accompany, to escort." The form is reconstructed rather than directly attested; classical Latin preferred comitārī "to accompany," and the compound was likely reshaped within Gallo-Romance itself, but its existence is securely inferred from the Romance descendants.
  • Old French convoier / conveier retained the sense of conducting or escorting someone along a route — protection and guidance as much as pure transport; the same word gives modern English convoy (a group escorted along a way) and convey.
Middle English borrowed the verb as conveien / conveyen in the 14th century, and its meanings promptly branched. Physical transport of persons or goods; figurative transmission of news, feeling, or meaning ("to convey an idea"); and, in law, the formal transfer of property rights all developed side by side, driven by the same root image of "carrying over" from one place, hand, or mind to another. In the 15th century the noun conveyance (verb convey + the abstract suffix -ance) lexicalized the whole family of senses:
  • A means of transport — the carriage, ship, or "public conveyance" by which people and goods moved.
  • The act of carrying or communicating — the process itself.
  • A legal instrument that transfers title — the "deed of conveyance," a specialty of the English legal vocabulary that remains fully technical today.
The semantic drift can be charted as a series of extensions: shared travel on a road → escort and conduct → transport and transmission → abstract medium of ideas — and, in parallel, legal "carrying over" of ownership. The verb's history is social as much as linguistic: in the centuries of coaching and sailing, "conveyance" was the ordinary, dignified word for the carriage or vessel that moved a traveller along the road; in the legal chancery of early modern England it named the very document that moved property from hand to hand. The literary use in the card — "the artist is his conveyance, not his companion" — reactivates the oldest image: the artist is the vehicle on the way, not a fellow passenger. Mind or vision is the cargo; the artist is the means by which it is moved from one soul to another. The word's sister vocabulary — vehicle, via, deviate, obvious, wagon, weigh, way, convoy — keeps the motion-and-path complex visible across more than four millennia, from the PIE road to the artist's studio.
