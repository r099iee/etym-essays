Maiden is a native Germanic word, one of the oldest strata of English vocabulary, and its history shows how a language can keep a word for a thousand years while the society that uses it changes completely. From an Indo-European word for a young person, Germanic developed a specific word for the young unmarried woman; Old English shaped it into a diminutive; and modern English has kept it as the elevated, literary name for the virgin and the young bride-to-be — while also spinning it off into cricket, shipping, and parliamentary speeches. 1. The PIE root: *maghu- "young person." The chain begins with the Indo-European root *maghu- / *magʰu-, reconstructed as "young person, child, unmarried youth." The root appears in Germanic with the sense of a young male or female, and also in Celtic (Old Irish maug "young slave, boy"); its wider Indo-European connections are uncertain, and some scholars treat the material with caution, so the deep reconstruction should be flagged as plausible but not airtight. From this root Germanic builds the stem for the young woman, *magaþiz (seen in Gothic magaths "maiden," Old High German magad "maiden," Old Norse mær / mey from a related shape), alongside the male youth in *maguz (Gothic magus "boy, servant," Old English magu "son, boy, servant"). 2. Old English: mægþ and the diminutive mægden. Old English inherits the West Germanic stem as mægþ, "maiden, virgin, young woman," attested from the eighth century. Around it the language builds a diminutive with the -en/-in- suffix (the same suffix family seen in chicken, kitten): mægden (also spelled mæden, mæiden), "a young girl, a little maiden," attested from the ninth century. The diminutive is the direct ancestor of the modern word, and its "little" colour explains the word's persistent tenderness — maiden has always been a word that softens while it names. 3. Middle English: maiden between religion and romance. Middle English maiden / meiden means an unmarried young woman and, emphatically, a virgin. The word is central to medieval Christianity — the Virgin Mary is "the Maiden," maidens are the pure saints and martyrs of the calendar — and equally central to medieval romance, where the maiden is the object of courtly love, the endangered and rescued young woman of the narrative. The moral and romantic colour of the modern word is fixed in this period: the maiden is pure, defenceless, and valuable. 4. The split with maid. Middle English also uses the aphetic short form maid for the same young woman, and over time the two forms specialize. Maid develops the strong secondary sense "female servant" (a maidservant, a lady's maid), and in modern English the short form is at least as likely to mean a domestic worker as a young woman. Maiden, keeping the full form, retains the literary and elevated sense — the young unmarried woman, the virgin — so that the language has a plain word for the servant and an old-fashioned word for the daughter. 5. The "first, unused" transfer. From the virgin's "untouched" state, the adjective maiden develops the modern sense "first, initial, unused": maiden voyage (a ship's first journey), maiden speech (a first speech in a parliament), maiden land (never cultivated), maiden name (the family name before marriage), and, in cricket, a maiden over — an over in which the scoring remains untouched. The historical bridge is transparent: the untouched thing is a "virgin" run, passage, or score. 6. The semantic stages in summary.
  • PIE *maghu-: "young person" — a sex-general word for the young, shared with Celtic.
  • Proto-Germanic *magaþiz: the young woman specialized — the gender-specific maiden emerges from the age word.
  • Old English mægþ → mægden: the diminutive softens and genders the word, adding tenderness and "little" scale.
  • Middle English maiden: the word is fixed in the religious and romantic registers — the virgin, the pure and defenceless young woman.
  • The maid split: the short form drifts into service ("female servant"), leaving maiden the literary form.
  • Modern transfer: "first, unused, untouched" — maiden voyage, maiden speech, maiden over.
7. On this card. In The Hobbit, Smaug "used to crawl out of the great gate and come by night to Dale, and carry away people, especially maidens, to eat." Tolkien chooses the old, literary plural deliberately: not modern journalism's "young women," but the ballad and chronicle word, the word of the pure and defenceless daughters of a town. The archaism makes the dragon's appetite a legendary atrocity rather than a news report — and the word's ancient Germanic roots, its medieval religious and romantic history, and its modern "first-time" senses all hover behind the single grave noun. The card form is the plural of repeated night theft and slaughter.
