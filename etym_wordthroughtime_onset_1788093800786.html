- Armed attack — the noun first means 'an assault, a setting-on' (16th c.); 'the onset of the enemy' is a standard line of 16th–17th-century prose and verse.
- The slide to time — by the 1560s the same word means 'the coming-on of a period or condition' (onset of winter, of age, of night): the attacker vanishes and the event itself 'comes on'.
- Clinical capture — 19th-century medical writing fixes 'onset of fever / of disease' as a technical moment; the aggressive sense fades from ordinary speech into historical prose.
- Technical expansion — the 20th century adds the syllable 'onset' in linguistics and the monsoon 'onset' in meteorology, and 'onset detection' in music technology: always the beginning treated as an arrival.
- Today — 'onset' is the default word for a beginning one does not control (disease, war, winter), while the old attack-sense lives on in history and in deliberately archaic battle prose, as in Tolkien's 'his onset grew ever slower'.
