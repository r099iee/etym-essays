PENITENT: Feeling or expressing sincere sorrow and regret for having done wrong, together with a resolve to amend one's life; repentant. As a noun, a person who performs penance under the direction of a confessor. In the card's sentence — "The __________ offender knelt before the altar, seeking pardon for the wrongs he had done" — the word sets the offender kneeling before the altar, the posture of penance, so that "penitent" is not a cold intellectual admission but an embodied, devout contrition that seeks pardon in a sacred space. The tone is reverent and solemn, threading together guilt, humility, and the hope of forgiveness; the penitent does not merely regret, he performs his sorrow in the presence of God. This sacramental colouring distinguishes the word sharply from any secular confession, and it gives the prose a hush of church light, of Lent, and of absolution. Core features of the concept.
  • Sorrow for wrongdoing: the penitent feels genuine regret for sins or faults committed, not mere displeasure at their consequences.
  • Religious frame: the word is at home in the vocabulary of Christian practice — confession, penance, absolution, atonement — and carries a sacramental charge that secular synonyms lack.
  • Action and resolve: penitence is not passive; it involves a resolve to amend, and in the fullest sense the performance of penance, the outward act of the inner sorrow.
  • Register: reverent, literary, and now somewhat formal; the word has retreated from everyday speech, where "sorry" or "remorseful" would be used.
Polysemy and related senses. The word has two grammatical lives and a cluster of related senses:
  • Sense 1 — adjective, moral and religious (primary): feeling sincere sorrow for one's sins or faults and resolving to amend; "a penitent sinner," "penitent tears," "penitent heart." This is the sense of the card's sentence.
  • Sense 2 — noun, the person (sacramental): a person who performs penance under the direction of a confessor, especially in the sacrament of reconciliation; "the priest heard the penitent's confession." Here the word is a title in the church, not merely an emotion.
  • Sense 3 — noun, historical (ecclesiastical orders): in early and medieval Christianity, "penitents" named a class of persons publicly enrolled in penance — the penitent order of the early church, who did public penance before readmission to communion; the word is also applied to certain religious orders and confraternities (e.g., the Penitents in medieval Italian confraternities).
  • Sense 4 — transferred, secular (modern, rare): by extension the word is occasionally used of secular remorse, "a penitent apology," "a penitent tone," though the religious colouring almost always remains; the strictly secular word is "remorseful" or "contrite."
The family of the word — penitence, penance, penitentiary, impenitent, repent — all descend from the same Latin verb paenitēre, "to regret," and the senses are unified by the idea of sorrow that performs itself: the penitent's regret is made visible in the act of penance, and the whole cluster is built on the church's teaching that sorrow must be shown and worked out. Set against its near-synonyms.
  • Repentant is the closest moral twin, but it is more ethical and less sacramental — it names a change of mind about wrongdoing without necessarily implying the discipline of penance.
  • Contrite stresses the crushing of the heart, a more intense, inward, and private grief; a contrite person is broken, a penitent person is kneeling and at work on his amendment.
  • Remorseful is secular and often self-focused, describing the gnawing pang of conscience rather than any outward act of atonement.
  • Rueful is lighter and wistful, tinged with regret for a lost opportunity rather than for sin.
  • Apologetic is social and perfunctory, the register of the drawing-room, not of the confessional.
  • Sorry is the ordinary, weakened everyday word, covering everything from a spilled cup to a grave sin.
Only "penitent" implies visible, performed regret united with the resolve and the machinery of penance. Collocations and register. The word collocates richly with the vocabulary of the church: "penitent sinner," "penitent tears," the "penitent thief" of the Gospels (Saint Dismas, crucified beside Christ), "penitential psalms," "penitential discipline," and the noun's use in "the priest heard the penitent." Its register is reverent and literary: the word belongs to sermons, devotional prose, and solemn narrative, and in modern English it has acquired a slightly old-fashioned, churchly air. The noun "penitentiary" is the word's darker modern survival — it drifted from "place of penance" to "prison" in American usage, a striking example of semantic change.
