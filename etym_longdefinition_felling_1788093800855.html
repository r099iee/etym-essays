Felling is the present participle and verbal noun of the verb to fell, “to cause to fall, to strike down, to cut down”. The word is the causative partner of to fall: if a tree falls, someone fells it; the verb means “to make fall”. In the source context (“they busied themselves felling trees”) the word has its most familiar modern sense — cutting trees down at the base so that they fall — and it is the standard technical term of forestry and the timber trade. Tolkien's use — the Elvenking's people felling trees and gathering timber to rebuild Esgaroth after the dragon's attack — shows the word at work in its plain, useful sense: woodcraft, timber, and the business of a wounded town rebuilding itself.

The senses of felling and its base verb fall into a coherent family:

  • Tree-felling (core modern sense): cutting down a standing tree, by axe, saw or machine, so that it falls to the ground. In forestry, felling is the precise term for the whole operation — felling direction, felling season, clear felling, selective felling, thinning felling — and the word names both the act and, as a noun, the quantity of timber cut (“the year's felling”).
  • Striking down a person or animal: to fell an opponent is to knock him to the ground — “a felling blow”, “he felled the ox with one stroke”. This is the older, broader sense, applied to combat, hunting and execution; it survives in literary prose and in boxing and martial reporting (“a felling right hook”).
  • Figurative sense — to destroy, defeat, ruin: “the scandal felled the minister”, “fever felled half the crew”, “the news felled her”. The bridge is transparent: what is felled is brought low, as a tree is brought low. The participle is used of illness, grief, disaster and political downfall, and carries a note of sudden, complete collapse.
  • Technical sense in sewing: to fell a seam is to fold one edge over the other and stitch it flat — the “fell” of a seam; the same verb, in a specialised transferred sense of “to flatten down”. This is genuine polysemy of the same word, not a homograph.
  • Distinct homographs — must not be confused: the adjective fell (“cruel, fierce, deadly” — “one fell swoop”, from Old French fel), the noun fell (“an animal's hide”, from Old English fell, “skin”), and the noun fell (“a hill, a moor”, from Old Norse fjall) are entirely different words that happen to share the spelling. “In one fell swoop” (Shakespeare, Macbeth) means “in one cruel stroke” — it has nothing to do with cutting trees.

Grammatically, felling is the -ing form of the regular verb fell (fell, felled, felled), and it must not be confused with falling, the -ing form of fall. The distinction is obligatory: “the felling tree” (being cut down) and “the falling tree” (coming down by itself) are different images, and the pair is a classic illustration of the causative — one verb makes, the other happens. As a noun, felling is used in forestry, timber law and environmental discourse (“the felling of the ancient grove”, “a ban on felling”), where it is often paired with “logging” and “deforestation”. In modern usage the word is neutral-technical in the timber trade, but in environmental contexts it has acquired a negative colouring — felling is what protesters gather to stop — while in figurative use it remains a vivid literary verb for bringing the mighty low.
