- 13th c. — the lump-word arrives: Middle English cobbe means a rounded mass, a head, a chief — earth-lumps, loaf-lumps, and headmen share one blunt syllable.
- 14th–15th c. — compounds accumulate: cobnuts, cobbles, and loaf-cobs settle into farmhouse and street vocabulary; the lump-sense multiplies by shape.
- 16th c. — the horse joins the family: short, thick-set riding horses are nicknamed cobs for their barrel-round compactness; the stable-yard gains its tradesman.
- 17th c. — the swan is drafted: the knob-billed male swan becomes the cob, the lump on his beak promoting him to head of the pair.
- 18th–19th c. — corn and empire: American maize gives the world the corncob, and the parson's cob becomes the emblem of safe, sensible English horsemanship.
- Today — workaday and beloved: show cobs parade, corn on the cob steams, and cob loaves crust — the lump-word thrives wherever roundness needs a name.
