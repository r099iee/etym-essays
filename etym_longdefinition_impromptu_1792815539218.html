Impromptu describes anything said, sung, played, or done without preparation, rehearsal, or planning: an impromptu speech, an impromptu concert, an impromptu party in the kitchen that nobody arranged and everybody remembers. The word carries a distinctive flavour of cheerful readiness rather than mere accident, because an impromptu performance is not a mistake but a feat: it praises the speaker's or player's ability to produce something delightful out of nothing, on demand, with the materials of the moment. In the source sentence the young hobbits get up an impromptu orchestra, and the whole comedy and charm of the scene lies in that word: no rehearsal, no conductor, no music stands, only high spirits and whatever instruments come to hand, instantly converted into a merry dance-tune.

The word works as adjective, adverb, and noun without changing its shape, and each role shades the meaning slightly. As an adjective it classifies the unprepared thing: an impromptu visit, an impromptu press conference, where the emphasis falls on the absence of planning. As an adverb it describes the manner of doing: she spoke impromptu for twenty minutes, holding the floor with no notes, where the emphasis falls on the skill of improvising. As a noun it names either the unprepared performance itself, he tossed off a brilliant impromptu, or, in classical music, a specific composed genre piece that merely imitates improvisation, Schubert's and Chopin's Impromptus, short lyrical piano pieces written out in full yet styled to sound freshly invented. That last sense is the word's elegant paradox: music carefully composed to sound as if it were not composed at all.

Register and connotation repay attention, because impromptu is approving in a way its near-synonyms are not. To call a speech unprepared criticizes it; to call it impromptu forgives or even celebrates it, implying grace under pressure and a welcome spontaneity. Journalists reach for it to dignify chaos, hosts to excuse informality, and reviewers to praise artists who triumph over mishaps: the impromptu encore after a power cut becomes the emotional peak of the concert precisely because nobody planned it. The semantic bridge from the Latin original is readiness: what is done in promptu, in readiness, at hand, slides naturally into what is done from whatever is at hand, without fetching anything from elsewhere, and hence without preparation.

Modern extensions carry the word well beyond the concert hall. Business writing loves the impromptu meeting, the corridor conversation that solves in five minutes what the scheduled committee could not solve in five weeks, and the praise hidden in the adjective rebukes over-organization itself. Social life has its impromptu gatherings, picnics, and road trips, where spontaneity is the entire point and planning would spoil the pleasure. Even technology has borrowed the term: impromptu networks and impromptu sessions in software denote connections assembled on the fly from available parts, a metaphorical return to the word's deepest sense of making do with what is present and ready.

Learners should keep the word's boundaries crisp. Impromptu always implies performance before others, however informal: one can give an impromptu speech but not, strictly, feel an impromptu emotion, since feelings are not performed from readiness. It also implies success or at least completion: a speech that collapses after two helpless sentences is attempted or botched rather than truly impromptu, because the word's praise depends on the performer actually delivering. Held together, these conditions make impromptu one of English's most compact compliments: unprepared, public, and pulled off.

  • Unprepared performance (core adjectival sense): done with no rehearsal, as in an impromptu song; the central meaning, bridged from Latin in readiness by the idea of working only with what is already at hand.
  • Offhand manner (adverbial sense): speaking or acting without notes, as in to perform impromptu; bridged by shifting the emphasis from the thing produced to the skill of producing it.
  • Musical genre piece (noun sense): a short composed piece feigning improvisation, as in Schubert's Impromptus; bridged by imitation, since composers borrowed the prestige of true improvisation for fully written music.
  • Spontaneous gathering (social sense): arranged on the moment, as in an impromptu party; bridged by extending unpreparedness from performers to hosts and guests collectively.
  • Ad-hoc assembly (technical sense): networks or sessions formed on the fly; bridged by metaphor, mapping readiness-at-hand onto devices and connections.
  • Approving versus neutral neighbours: impromptu praises where unprepared blames and spontaneous merely describes; the compliment is built into the word's history of admired readiness.
