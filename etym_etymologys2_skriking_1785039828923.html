Imitative base *skrei- / *sker- "piercing, grating cry" (sound-symbolic root built from a sibilant /s/ + harsh velar /k/ + high vowel /r-j-/; the acoustic template for piercing cries across Indo-European; no regular inherited PIE pedigree is accepted — the root is the sound itself) → Proto-Germanic *skrīkōnan (v.) "to screech, cry out shrilly" (reconstructed on the strength of the Scandinavian and continental imitative verbs) → Old Norse skrækja / skríkja (v.) "to screech, twitter, cry out" (recorded in the Scandinavian period) → Middle English scriken / skriken (v.) "to screech, shriek" (c. 1200, formed under strong Scandinavian influence in the Danelaw) → Northern English & Scots dialect skrike (v.) "to shriek, weep loudly, complain" (the northern survivor, preserving the hard /k/ of the Old Norse form) → present participle skriking "shrieking, screeching, uttering sharp high-pitched cries" → Modern English SKRIKING (dialectal/archaic present participle) Parallel branch (standard form): Middle English scriken / skriken → 16th-century southern reshaping shriek (v.) "to shriek" → Modern English shrieking. Related noun: Old Norse skrikja (n.) "a shrieker; the shrike bird" → Old English scric (n.) "a shrike or thrush, the shrill-caller" → Modern English shrike (n., 1540s, the butcher-bird). West Germanic parallels (independent imitative formations of the same type): Old Frisian skrichta "to screech" → Middle English scritch / screech (1570s, alteration of scritch) → Modern English screech; Middle Dutch schrien "to shriek" → English screak (c. 1500). Note on debate: the *skrei-/*sker- grouping is impressionistic — a family of parallel sound-symbolic words rather than a single attested descent. The OED treats skrike, shriek, and screech as kindred imitative formations with Scandinavian and continental parallels but no inherited Indo-European ancestor. The exact route of the English form — direct loan from Old Norse skríkja/skrækja, or native Old English development influenced by Norse — is debated; no single written intermediate is attested between the Scandinavian verbs and the Middle English scriken, though the Danelaw context strongly favours Norse influence. The hard /k/ of skrike versus the soft /ʃ/ of shriek reflects two dialectal outcomes of the same /skr-/ cluster, northern versus southern.
