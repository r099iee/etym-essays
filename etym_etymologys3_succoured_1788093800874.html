The story of succoured begins with an ancient Indo-European verb of motion and ends in a distinctly English field of chivalry, religion and philanthropy. The Latin verb currere "to run" is usually traced to a PIE root *ḱers- "to run" — the reconstruction is standard since de Vaan, though the Indo-European comparanda are thin and partly disputed, Latin curro having few secure relatives outside Italic. From currere Latin built a large family of compounds, each joining the root "run" to a prepositional prefix: occurrere "to run against, meet", recurrere "to run back", discurrere "to run about". One member of the family, succurrere (sub- "up to, from below" + currere), acquired in classical usage the idiomatic sense "to run to the help of, to aid" — the vivid image is of someone sprinting up beneath or to the side of a fallen or besieged comrade. The compound is well attested in classical prose: Cicero and Livy both use succurrere of coming to the aid of persons, cities and causes, and the derived noun succursus "a running to help, relief" underlies the Romance forms. (The prefix sub- itself continues the PIE preposition *upo "under, up from under", as in Greek hypó, though Latin's s- is not fully explained.)

Romance transmission. In the Gallo-Romance of the early Middle Ages, succurrere passed into Old French as socorre / sucurre "to aid", with the noun secors "help, aid" (Modern French secours, verb secourir). English borrowed along both tracks during the great thirteenth-century influx of French vocabulary: the noun socour "aid, relief" is recorded from about 1225, and the verb socouren "to help, relieve" is recorded from the early fourteenth century. For roughly three centuries the word was ordinary, unmarked English: knights succour besieged castles, physicians succour the sick, and kings succour the poor in chronicles, romances and popular piety alike. This was the age of the word's greatest plainness — succour then did the work that help and aid do today.

The sixteenth-century re-Latinisation. In the 1500s, as English underwent its systematic Renaissance re-spelling, socour was written succour to display its Latin source succurrere: the double -cc- was restored and the French-style -our was retained on the model of favour, honour and colour. The Authorized Version of 1611 ratified the new spelling and gave the word its most famous attestation: quoting Isaiah, "in the day of salvation have I succoured thee" (2 Corinthians 6:2, following "Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation"). From the seventeenth century onward, succour carried a double charge: military relief — the noun and verb used of towns, garrisons and whole campaigns in the wars of religion and the Thirty Years' War — and divine or providential aid, in which sense it fills the sermons and devotional prose of the period.

The nineteenth-century humanitarian peak. The Victorian era turned succour into the standard verb of organised mercy. Newspaper appeals, charity reports and the reforming novels of Dickens and his contemporaries constantly speak of succouring the destitute, the fever-stricken and the shipwrecked; in this register succour glows with a philanthropic, almost sacramental warmth while remaining entirely concrete — food, blankets, medicine, safe passage. The nineteenth century was the last period in which the word was genuinely common in everyday prose.

The twentieth century and Tolkien. Since about 1920 succour has retreated from speech into literary and ceremonial English. Its final flourishing in popular fiction came with the deliberate archaism of fantasy and historical novelists: in J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), from which this card's sentence is taken, Bard answers Thorin's contempt for the Elvenking with the stately reproach that the Elvenking "has succoured the people of the Lake in their need, though they had no claim but friendship on him" — the choice of succour rather than help or aid is itself an act of style, evoking the chivalric chronicle prose Tolkien deliberately imitated. American English has meanwhile simplified the spelling to succor (as with all the -our words), and derivatives such as succourer "one who brings relief" (recorded from the sixteenth century) are now rare. The word's trajectory — from plain verb of aid, to military and divine relief, to philanthropic slogan, to literary archaism — is a compact model of how a perfectly ordinary word climbs into the ceremonial register as its everyday rivals (help, aid, support) take over its street-level work.
