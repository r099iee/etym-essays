The etymology of satirists is a tale of a full plate. Latin satura — earlier satura lanx, a dish heaped with mixed first-fruits offered to the gods — meant full, stuffed, sated, from satis, enough, itself from PIE *seh2-, to satisfy. Roman literary historians applied the food metaphor to a new kind of poem: Ennius’s and then Lucilius’s saturae were miscellanies, stuffings of varied measures and subjects, unified less by form than by a censorious gaze at contemporary vice. The spelling drifted to satira under Greek influence, and with the spelling came a persistent false friend — the Greek satyros, the lustful goat-man of the wilds — so that Renaissance printers cheerfully illustrated satires with satyrs, as if mockery were the braying of beasts. Quintilian already knew the dish derivation was correct, but the satyr confusion seasoned the word’s aura for a thousand years, lending satirists a whiff of the untamed.

What the genre kept from the dish was miscellaneousness with teeth. Horace refined Lucilius’s rough medley into urbane conversation — the smiling satirist chatting the reader out of avarice and ambition — while Juvenal, writing under emperors where frank speech was dangerous, forged the opposite pole: indignant, sublime denunciation of a city rotting from the head. Both stances travelled intact through the Middle Ages (where satire survived chiefly as a schoolroom label for Horace and Juvenal) into the vernacular explosion of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. English borrowed satire from Middle French around 1500 and satirist — formed with the Greek-derived agent suffix -ist (via Latin -ista, marking one who practises) — followed within a century: Nashe, Donne, Marston, and then Dryden, who Englished Juvenal and theorised the form. The plural satirists thus names a self-conscious guild, practitioners of a classical office in modern dress.

The Augustan age made that guild central to public life. Dryden, Pope, and Swift wielded the heroic couplet as a surgical instrument, and the Dunciad and Modest Proposal showed the form’s two extremes — mock-epic contempt and deadpan ironic enormity. Eighteenth-century law recognised the danger: seditious-libel prosecutions stalked satirists precisely because ridicule moves readers whom argument leaves cold. The Romantic period complicated the office, prizing sincerity over mockery, yet Byron, Peacock, and later Thackeray and Dickens kept the ironic scourge in hand. By the Victorian era the satirist had partly migrated into the novel and the illustrated press — Punch (1841) institutionalised weekly mockery — and the singular mechanistic censor became a profession: the staff satirist, the cartoonist, the parliamentary sketch-writer.

The twentieth century completed two extensions visible in the source sentence. First, satire escaped verse and fiction into scholarship itself: Thorstein Veblen’s Theory of the Leisure Class (1899) is economics written as sustained deadpan mockery — conspicuous leisure, vicarious consumption, the ceremonial lawn — and the passage’s phrase incessant mocking of status games recognises Veblen as satirist by method though economist by title. Second, the broadcast age made satirists of comedians, hosts, and filmmakers, from Chaplin and Kubrick to late-night television, so that the plural now naturally covers a mixed company of versifiers, novelists, cartoonists, and comics. Through every medium the word’s charge persists from its first dish: a full serving, many ingredients, one sating purpose — to stuff pretension until it bursts, and thereby, in the classical hope the sentence invokes, to straighten out those who spent their lives showing off.

  • PIE–Latin: *seh2- → satis → satura — satisfaction becomes satiety becomes the full dish.
  • c. 100 BCE–100 CE: Lucilius, Horace, Juvenal fix the genre’s smiling and savage poles.
  • c. 1500–1600s: English borrows satire, coins satirist; the guild naturalises.
  • 1660–1800: Augustan zenith — Dryden, Pope, Swift; prosecution proves the sting.
  • 1841–present: Punch, broadcast satire, scholar-satirists like Veblen; the plural covers every medium.
