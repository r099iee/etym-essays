- 14th–15th c. — Medical and surgical toxicity: Latin virulentia enters medieval surgical compendia, denoting the poisonous putrid matter and foul pus discharging from infected ulcers and serpent bites.
- 16th c. — Botanical and animal venom: Applied in Renaissance natural histories to the venomous secretions of vipers, scorpions, toads, and deadly nightshade.
- 17th c. — Figural transfer to political and religious speech: Pamphleteers during the English Civil War utilize it for the 'poisoned pens' and bitter malice of sectarian polemics.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment literary satire: Alexander Pope and Jonathan Swift deploy the term against envious Grub Street critics whose critiques are dripping with venom.
- 19th c. — Victorian aesthetic critique and microbiological dawn: John Ruskin attacks the virulence of insect-like critics in 1844; decades later, Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch formalize it as the scientific metric for microbial pathogenicity.
- 20th–21st c. — Dual scientific and cultural ubiquity: Essential in modern epidemiology for measuring viral pathogen severity, while universally applied in media analysis to describe toxic online discourse and political polarization.
