• Socrates: Similar because Socrates was Plato's teacher and the protagonist of nearly all his dialogues; differs because Socrates wrote nothing and left no school, whereas Plato is the author and founder who recorded, transformed, and systemised Socratic thought.
  • Aristotle: Similar because Aristotle was Plato's greatest student and, with him, one of the two pillars of Western philosophy; differs because Aristotle founded the Lyceum and pursued empirical science, logic, and natural history, whereas Plato stressed the transcendent Forms, the soul, and idealist metaphysics.
  • Plotinus: Similar because Plotinus (3rd century AD) founded Neoplatonism, the great later development of Plato's philosophy; differs because Plotinus lived six centuries after Plato and transformed the doctrine of Forms into a mystical system of emanations from the One.
  • Xenophon: Similar because Xenophon was another Athenian student of Socrates who wrote Socratic memoirs and history; differs because Xenophon was a practical soldier, historian, and essayist rather than a metaphysical philosopher, and founded no school.
  • Platonist / Platonism: Similar because they derive directly from the name and designate his philosophy and its followers; differs because Plato names the historical person, while Platonism is the evolving system of thought and Platonist its adherent.
  • Platonic (adjective): Similar because it descends from his name and doctrines; differs because platonic in modern usage means "non-sexual, spiritual love" (from the Symposium) or "ideal and abstract," a narrowed everyday sense of the philosophical tradition.
  • The Academy: Similar because the Academy is the school Plato founded and is metonymically attached to him; differs because the Academy is the institution and later its buildings and successors, while Plato is the person; "Academy" now means any learned institution.
  • Dialogue / Dialogues: Similar because Plato's works are written in dialogue form, with Socrates as chief speaker; differs because dialogue is the literary genre, used by many philosophers before and after, not the name of the philosopher himself.
