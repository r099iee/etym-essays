GAMMERS is the plural of gammer, an old dialectal English word for an old woman — specifically a garrulous, elderly woman of the village, the female counterpart of gaffer (an old man). The word is a contraction of godmother, formed by exactly the same process that turned godfather into gaffer: the two words were colloquial shortenings used first for the godparent relationship and then, by extension, as terms of address and reference for elderly village folk in general. In the Tolkien passage above, the "greybeards and gammers" are the old men and old women of the town who claim, from their youth, to have seen the dragon flying in the sky — and whom the younger people laugh at as credulous old gossips.

  • Primary sense (dialectal): an old woman; in traditional rural English dialect, the word for an elderly woman, especially one who is talkative and full of local lore; the plural gammers = "old women", almost always in contrast or parallel to the old men (gaffers).
  • Secondary sense (address): used as a respectful (later slightly condescending or affectionate) term of address to an old woman, equivalent to "old mother" — "good gammer, what news?"; the word carried the same plain, homely dignity as gaffer.
  • Folk and tale register: in folklore and rural writing, "gammer" is the stock old woman — the teller of old tales, the keeper of remedies, the village gossip; the pairing "gaffer and gammer" is a traditional formula for "the old folk" of the village.
  • By-extension sense (generation): in the context of Tolkien’s fantasy, "gammers" denotes the older generation generally — the elderly whose long memories reach back to a time the young dismiss as legend; the word’s old-fashioned, dialectal flavour suits Tolkien’s deliberately archaic, rural-sounding prose.
  • Comparative sense: unlike hag or crone, gammer is not hostile: it is plain, homely, and faintly affectionate, and its connection to "godmother" gives it an undertone of Christian sponsorship and neighbourly respect.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The historical bridge is phonetic and social. In the later Middle Ages, when godparents were the most important family connections outside blood kinship, godmother (Old English godmodor) and godfather (Old English godfæder) were everyday words. In colloquial speech these were compressed: the first syllable and the following consonant cluster ran together, producing dialectal gammer and gaffer — forms recorded from the 16th century. Because godparents were typically older and respected neighbours, the words then drifted from "godparent" to "old person": gaffer = old man, gammer = old woman, without requiring any actual godparent relationship. The pair became a stock rural formula, and "gammer" survives today chiefly in dialect literature, folk tales, and deliberately old-fashioned writing.

Register and nuances. Gammer is archaic and dialectal in modern English. It carries a distinctly rural, old-fashioned, slightly patronising-but-affectionate tone — the tone of a village, not a town. It is the word of nursery rhymes and folk-lore collectors. In contemporary speech it survives mainly in set contexts: in historical fiction, in dialect studies, and in "gaffer and gammer" formulas; a few related formations survive (e.g. "gammer" used jocularly). It is milder than hag (which is insulting and witch-like), gentler than crone (literary and faintly sinister), and more specific than "old woman". Tolkien’s choice of the word is deliberately folkloric: the gammers belong to the realm of legend, old tales, and dismissed memory.

  • Synonyms and near-synonyms: old women, old wives, crones, hags, beldams, dames, gossips, grandmothers, grannies, dowagers, matrons.
  • Paired opposite: gaffers (old men) — the masculine counterpart formed identically from godfather.
  • Antonyms: maidens, girls, lasses, young women.

Word-family and grammar. Gammer is a singular countable noun (a gammer, two gammers); gammers is the regular plural. The formation belongs to a small family of old colloquial contractions — gaffer (godfather), gammer (godmother), gossip (historically godsibb, "godparent", whence "old woman’s prattle" and finally "rumour-monger") — words in which the "god-" prefix was worn down in everyday speech.
