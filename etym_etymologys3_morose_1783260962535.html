MOROSE — Full of One's Own Ways Morose descends from one of the most inward of the Indo-European roots: *meh₁-, whose senses cluster around "exertion, will, spirit, mind" — the inner force that makes a person what he is — or, in the o-grade form *mō-, the same base viewed as "will, custom." From that nominal derivative, two great families were born on opposite sides of Europe. In the Germanic world it produced *mōdaz, "mind, heart, spirit, courage": Old English mōd, whose descendants include modern English mood and moody, German Mut, "courage," and Gemüt, "disposition," Gothic mōþs, "anger," and Old Norse móðr, "wrath." In the Italic world the same base produced Latin mōs, mōris, "habit, custom, manner" — a word that deserves pause, because in Roman thought mōs was nothing less than the foundation of society. The ancestral way was sanctified in the phrase mōs māiōrum, "the custom of the forefathers," which conservative Romans invoked as an unwritten constitution; the plural mōrēs, "character, morals," yielded through Cicero's coinage mōrālis the entire modern family of moral, morality, and morale; and from the same stock came mōrigerus, "one who follows another's humour," and our own word. The adjective morōsus was built from mōs with the suffix -ōsus, "full of" — the same suffix that gives cūriōsus, "full of care." Etymologically, then, a morose person is "full of his own customs": wholly governed by his own habits and self-will. In classical Latin the adjective ran a revealing course from the particular to the peevish. It could mean "self-willed, obstinate" — a man who will have things his own way; it could mean "fastidious, hard to please" — a morose critic of style, an old man affronted by novelty; and only in late and moralising usage did it harden into the modern sense of "sullen, ill-tempered, gloomily cross." The semantic engine is the idea of habit: a person so fixed in his own ways that he is irritated by everything outside them, and so turns sour, withdrawn, and resentful. This is why morose names a character rather than a mood — it is the disposition of the man who has so filled himself with his own customs that the world can no longer please him. The word reached English as a learned borrowing in the 1560s, in the flood of Latin vocabulary that the Renaissance carried into the language. Its first English uses were exact and Latinate: a "morose" man was self-willed, obstinate, humorless in his insistence on his own way. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the adjective settled into its modern value, describing an habitual, churlish gloom — the temperament of a man set against being pleased — and it became a stock word of character-portraiture in essays and moral prose. Samuel Johnson and the periodical essayists used it for the man who cannot enjoy society; the novelists of the nineteenth century gave it to brooding, unsociable, sourly discontented figures, and the psychologists of the same era took it up for pathologically withdrawn and melancholic states — "morose melancholia" is a phrase of the older psychiatric literature. Modern use has, if anything, broadened it slightly. Morose still denotes sullen, gloomy ill-temper in persons, but it is also applied to moods, faces, silences, weather, and landscapes — anything that seems to refuse cheerfulness. In Macaulay's sentence it is paired with desponding, and the pairing is precise: the desponding man has given up hope; the morose man refuses to be pleased. The first has surrendered expectation, the second is full of himself and of his own sour ways. Across three thousand years the etymology has never been forgotten by the meaning: from the Indo-European will to the Roman custom of the ancestors to the Renaissance "morose" man, the word has always been about the self closed in on itself — full of its own habits, and therefore hard to please.
