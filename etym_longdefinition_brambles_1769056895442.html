brambles (noun, plural) — prickly, woody, usually thorny shrubs, most typically the blackberry and raspberry canes of the genus Rubus, whose long arching stems root at the tips and form dense, impenetrable tangles. In the landscape they are the 'aggressive' vegetation — a physical barrier that must be forced through. In this passage the word does one sharp job: 'They panted up the steep bank, forced their way through some brambles.' It is a word of resistance. Brambles do not merely grow; they tangle, snag, and scratch. They seize clothing, rake exposed skin, and hide the fact that they are in fact edible — the very bush that tears your arm bears the blackberries you would be glad of later. The tone here is exertion and slight menace: the children are already panting when they meet the brambles, and what they find on the other side is the old apple tree 'heavy with large yellowish-golden apples.' The bramble is the wilderness's gatekeeper, the last scratchy obstacle between effort and reward. Primary sense. Botanically, 'bramble' denotes the rough, thorny, woody plants of the genus Rubus — blackberry, raspberry, dewberry — whose canes are armed with stout curved prickles. The word is singular for one such plant and collective in the plural for the tangled mass such plants form. Secondary and extended senses.
  • In British English the plural 'brambles' is the standard everyday word for the scratchy thicket of a hedgerow or abandoned field; 'the brambles' means the thorny undergrowth as a place.
  • By extension 'bramble' may loosely name any thorny wild bush, even outside Rubus, and poets use it for wild, uncultivated, hostile growth generally.
  • In food and rural vocabulary 'brambles' are the blackberry canes and their fruit-bushes: 'picking brambles' is a set phrase for gathering blackberries.
  • In figurative prose 'brambles' stand for difficulty, entangling complications, or the moral overgrowth of neglect — 'the brambles of bureaucracy' or 'a path choked with brambles' as an image of obstruction.
  • In landscape and gardening jargon brambles are the classic 'wilding' species that reclaim neglected ground, so the word carries an ecological sense of nature reasserting itself over cultivation.
The semantic bridge. The word descends from an ancient onomatopoeic root for a buzzing, growling noise; the thorn bush may have been named 'the buzzing one' for the bees in its blossom or 'the bristling one' for its projecting thorns. Either way, the name belongs to the plant's sharp, insistent character. Its sibling 'broom' (the same root without the diminutive) names the shrub whose twigs became a sweeping tool — so the whole family is built on one bristly, buzzing plant-idea. Nearest companions.
  • 'Briar' (or 'brier') is the prickly wild rose or heath — the sweetbriar of hedges — and in the smoking world the word was transferred to the heath-root from which tobacco pipes are carved; it is a rose, not a berry.
  • 'Thorns' names the sharp spines themselves and by metonymy any thorny plant — a religious and proverbial word ('crown of thorns', 'a thorn in the side') that points to the point, not the bush.
  • 'Undergrowth' is any low vegetation growing beneath trees — ferns, nettles, saplings — and is merely a layer of the forest, not necessarily prickly at all.
  • 'Scrub' is stunted, low, dry vegetation (often on poor land), with no implication of brambles.
