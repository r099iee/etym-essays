cosmogony (noun; plural: cosmogonies) designates a theory, narrative, myth, philosophical system, or astrophysical account concerning the origin, creation, and evolutionary birth of the universe, the solar system, or the cosmos as an ordered, coherent whole. Unlike disciplines such as observational cosmology (which studies the current large-scale structure, geometry, and dynamics of the universe) or eschatology (which speculates on its ultimate destruction or final state), cosmogony focuses specifically on the primordial transition from nothingness or undifferentiated chaos into an articulate, law-governed reality.

The word exhibits deep polysemic significance, scholarly nuance, and broad metaphorical reach across scientific, philosophical, mythological, and literary domains:

  • Mythological & Religious Accounts (Sacred Origin Myths): In anthropology, comparative religion, and folklore studies, a cosmogony is a culture's foundational sacred narrative explaining how reality, natural environments, and humanity were brought into being. Anthropologists categorize these traditional cosmogonies into recurring structural archetypes, including: creatio ex nihilo (creation out of nothingness by divine fiat or spoken thought, as in the Judeo-Christian Genesis or ancient Egyptian Memphite theology); creation from a primordial cosmic egg (as in Hindu, Chinese, and Orphic myths); earth-diver myths (in which an animal descends into primordial waters to retrieve mud that expands into dry land, common across Native North American cultures); and emergence myths (where humans and creatures ascend through subterranean wombs to populate the earth).
  • Astrophysical & Scientific Theories: In theoretical physics, astronomy, and planetary geology, cosmogony denotes the empirical, mathematical investigation of the physical mechanisms responsible for the origin of astronomical bodies and the spacetime fabric itself. It encompasses the standard Big Bang model (the hot, dense initial singularity 13.8 billion years ago), cosmic inflation theory (Alan Guth's exponential expansion model), quantum cosmogonies (such as the Hartle-Hawking "no-boundary" proposal, where time emerges smoothly from Euclidean space), and the nebular hypothesis for the gravitational accretion of proto-planetary discs into solar systems.
  • Literary, Artistic, & Aesthetic World-Building: In literary criticism, poetics, and cultural theory, cosmogony describes a vast, totalizing, self-contained fictional or poetic universe constructed by an author. When a work like Marcel Proust's In Search of Lost Time, Dante's Divine Comedy, James Joyce's Finnegans Wake, or J.R.R. Tolkien's The Silmarillion is characterized as a "cultural cosmogony," the term signifies that the work does not merely describe an existing external reality, but systematically constructs an autonomous macrocosm endowed with its own internal laws of time, memory, space, and spiritual ontology.
  • Philosophical Metaphysics & First Principles: In classical and modern metaphysics, cosmogony directly engages the foundational ontological dilemma: Why is there something rather than nothing? It explores first causes, the nature of prime matter (the Greek hyle), and whether the universe requires an uncaused prime mover (as in Aristotle's physics) or arose through spontaneous, unguided physical symmetry-breaking.
  • Psychological & Cognitive Development: In developmental psychology and psychoanalytic theory (particularly in the work of Carl Jung and Jean Piaget), the term is employed metaphorically to describe an infant's or individual's emerging cognitive model of reality. An individual's "internal cosmogony" represents the mental architecture through which the developing ego differentiates itself from the chaotic sensory blur of the outside world, creating a coherent subjective universe.

The historical and conceptual bridge connecting these diverse meanings is the ancient Greek compound structure of kósmos ("ordered, harmonious whole; beauty") and gonē ("generation, birth, begetting"). Whether articulated through archaic poetry chanting of Gaia emerging from Chaos, rigorous tensor equations modeling quantum fluctuations in the early universe, or the sweeping narrative architecture of a modernist novel reconstructing Parisian society through involuntary memory, a "cosmogony" consistently represents humanity's supreme imaginative and intellectual impulse: to explain how the total theater of existence came to be.
