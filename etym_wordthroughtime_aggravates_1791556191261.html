- Late 16th c. — Early Modern borrowing from Latin: Adopted into English from Latin aggravare meaning literally to make heavier, to burden, or to increase the moral or spiritual weight of an offense or sin.
- 17th c. — Legal and medical formalization: Established in common law and medical treatises as a precise technical verb indicating circumstances that augment the heinousness of a crime or factors that worsen a physiological wound or disease.
- 18th c. — Metaphoric expansion to human emotional states: Began appearing in literature applied to intangible states, such as 'aggravating one's grief' or 'aggravating an opponent's temper', laying the semantic groundwork for psychological application.
- 19th c. — Colloquial shift to personal annoyance & prescriptivist backlash: Shifted through metonymy to mean 'irritates or annoys a person'; fiercely denounced by Victorian and Edwardian style arbiters (such as H.W. Fowler) as an illiterate blunder to be rigorously eradicated.
- 20th–21st c. — Bifurcated dual standard: Achieved peaceful sociolinguistic coexistence; universally retained in formal, legal, and medical prose for the worsening of objective crises, while simultaneously embraced in everyday spoken English as a natural synonym for exasperating or annoying someone.
