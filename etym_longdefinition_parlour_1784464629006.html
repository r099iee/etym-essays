Parlour (British spelling; American parlor) is a sitting room in a private house, historically set aside for receiving guests, formal conversation, and the more presentable side of domestic life — not for cooking, sleeping, or rough work. In Bilbo’s Bag End the parlour is the social heart of hobbit comfort: upholstered civility, pipes, and polite space. When Balin, Dwalin, Fili, and Kili whisk trays and small tables into the parlour and set the feast out afresh there, they do not merely move furniture: they annex the room of conversation itself, converting Bilbo’s quiet sanctuary into a bustling dwarf banquet hall without leave. The comedy is a comic invasion of domestic dignity — the parlour’s very name (a place for speaking) is hijacked by the noisiest of company.

Polysemy — all senses and uses.

  • Domestic room, historical core: the private sitting room of a house or flat, reserved for family conversation and the reception of guests, distinct from kitchen, hall, bedroom, and (later) the more formal drawing-room.
  • Monastic room, earliest English sense: in medieval monasteries, the parlour was the room where monks were permitted to converse — an institutional exception to the rule of silence (Old French parloir).
  • Commercial establishment, modern extension: “parlour” names shops and service rooms that “receive” the public — beauty parlour, ice-cream parlour, funeral parlour, tattoo parlour, pizza parlour, massage parlour. The semantic thread is continuous: a place designated for talk and reception.
  • Historical sociolect: “the parlour” in Victorian and Edwardian Britain was the best-furnished guest room, where the parlour-maid served and families played parlour games; the sense survives in phrases such as “parlour games” and “parlour socialism” (socialism argued in comfortable drawing-rooms rather than on the streets).

Historical and semantic bridges. The word’s long walk from Greek to furniture passes through “speaking.” Greek parabolḗ meant “a throwing beside,” hence “a comparison” (and later the geometric parabola); Late Latin parabola came to mean “speech, word” (whence French parole); from that speech-noun a Vulgar Latin verb *parabolare / *parlare “to speak” developed (whence French parler); and Old French parloir named the monastery room where speech was allowed. Middle English took the word around 1200 as parlour, “a speaking room,” and it secularized from monastery to manor: the room of conversation became the room of guest-reception, and finally the modern parlour of domestic gentility and commercial front-rooms.

Register, collocations, and modern life.

  • Register: “parlour” is now old-fashioned or cosily period as a domestic term, and briskly commercial as a suffix (beauty parlour, ice-cream parlour).
  • Collocations: parlour-maid, parlour game, funeral parlour, beauty parlour, ice-cream parlour, tattoo parlour, pizza parlour, parlour palm; “the parlour” in Victorian fiction.
  • Spelling note: American English writes parlor; British English keeps parlour; the -our spelling was standard in American English until the mid-19th century, when Noah Webster’s reform shifted it.
  • Modern survival: as a domestic word it has largely yielded to living-room and sitting-room, but it survives strongly in commercial names and period fiction.

Synonym boundaries. A drawing-room is the later genteel “withdrawing” room to which the company retired after dinner — more formal than the parlour. A living-room or sitting-room is the modern everyday equivalent, less formal and without the parlour’s guest-facing Victorian connotations. A hall is larger, more public or transitional; a study or library is for private work and books. Parlour keeps older connotations of guest-facing conversation and middle-class or manor hospitality — precisely the civilized shell the dwarves cheerfully crack open.
