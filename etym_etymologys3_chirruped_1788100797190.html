"Chirruped" belongs to the smallest and oldest class of English vocabulary: words that imitate the sounds they name. Unlike nearly every other word in this batch, it has no Indo-European root, no Latin ancestor, no documented sound-law journey. Its history is entirely English and entirely onomatopoeic — a record of how a language, hearing the sparrow, builds a word out of the notes themselves, and then watches that word grow a life of its own.

1. The nature of the echoic word. Onomatopoeic verbs are coined again and again across the history of English — chirp, twitter, peep, cheep, cuckoo, croak, hiss, gurgle, trill, whistle — and they follow a characteristic pattern: they appear suddenly in the written record, already fully formed, because they do not descend from older words but from the sounds of the world. The family to which "chirruped" belongs begins with chirp, first attested in English in the mid-15th century (c. 1440) in the form chirpen, glossing the small cry of birds. The word is transparently built on the phonetic shape of the sound itself: the palatal burst ch- followed by the high front vowel -ir-, exactly the sharp small note of a sparrow. It was an everyday word almost at once — mid-15th-century English used it without gloss, certain that every hearer would recognise the bird in the word.

2. The birth of "chirrup" in the late 16th century. "Chirrup" appears in the written record in the 1570s as a lengthened, softened variant of "chirp". Two forces shaped it. First, the simple process of sound symbolism: the fuller form, with its rolled -rr- and its reduced second syllable (chirr-up), suggests a longer, more continuous, trilled sequence of notes — a chirrup is to a chirp what a run of descending trills is to a single staccato note. Second, and characteristically for the period, the form was understood — at least by folk etymology, and possibly in its actual origin — as "chirp" plus the particle "up", the same "up" that appears in "cheer up", "speak up", "pipe up": a chirruping bird is one that "chirps up", keeps its song going, cheers the scene with sound. Elizabethan and Jacobean writers exploited exactly this liveliness: the word appears in late 16th- and 17th-century verse and drama as the cheerful sound of birds and of vivacious human voices, always small, always bright, never threatening.

3. The horse-chirrup: a word acquires a job. The word's most interesting chapter is its career in the stables. From at least the 17th century, English coachmen, post-boys, ploughmen, and huntsmen used "chirrup" — as verb and as interjection — for the special sharp, clucking, trilling sound, made with tongue against the palate or through the teeth, used to encourage and urge on horses. The usage spread with the coaching system through the 18th century, and it is a measure of how ordinary the sound was that "chirrup" developed this second, purely human sense without losing a beat of its bird meaning: the same word now meant "to utter the sparrow's note" and "to make the coachman's cluck". The two senses ran side by side through the 19th century — Dickens, the great recorder of coaching life, uses "chirrup" for both horses and cheerful people — and the horse-sense died only with horse transport in the early 20th century, surviving today in historical novels and equestrian writing ("chirruping to the pony").

4. The Victorian gleam: cricket, hearth, and children. The 19th century gave "chirrup" its warmest literary weather. The image of the chirruping cricket on the hearth — the tiny, persistent, sociable note of domestic peace — became a Victorian fixture; Dickens's The Cricket on the Hearth (1845) made the chirruping of the hearth-cricket the very emblem of household cheer, and the word chimed perfectly with the age's taste for cosy naturalism. Simultaneously "chirrup" found its permanent home in writing for children: the chirruping sparrow, the chirruping chick, the chirruping canary populate Victorian and Edwardian nursery books, where the word's diminutive scale and unthreatening cheer made it the ideal sound of a safe world. Nature writers of the period (Richard Jefferies, W.H. Hudson) used it precisely and realistically, while novelists let their cheerful minor characters "chirrup" their greetings — the sound of small talk in the key of a bird.

5. The form "chirruped": the past tense as literary device. The specific form in the present card — "chirruped" — is the simple past, and its history is really the history of the verb's use in narration. In the narrative past tense the word gained a special function: the reporter's device for rendering the continuous, background sound of nature — "the sparrows chirruped in the eaves while the argument went on below" — one of those iterative-imperfective verbs (like "twittered", "twinkled", "gurgled") that prose uses to paint a scene's auditory texture. Because past-tense narration dominates fiction and memoir, "chirruped" has probably appeared in print more often than the present "chirrups": it is the standard form in which English literature lets a scene chirp; and in the source context the word does exactly this narrative work — the sparrows' continuous, sociable chirruping is the perfect audible background to a traveller's triumphant progress.

6. The modern word. In contemporary English "chirrup" survives at three levels: as a real, if literary, verb in nature writing (bird guides and essays); as a slightly antiquated gem in historical and children's fiction; and as an archaic specialty in equestrian contexts. Ordinary speech uses "chirped" or "twittered"; "chirruped" now confers a deliberate quaintness, a backwards glance toward the Victorian parlour — which is exactly why the sparrows of the source passage, and the writer who chose "chirruped" for them, can still make a modern reader hear a morning of contented birds. A word born from a sound in the 16th century has ended as a period adverb of cheer: proof that even the humblest onomatopoeia can retire with dignity.
