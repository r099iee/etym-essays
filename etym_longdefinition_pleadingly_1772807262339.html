pleadingly — adverb meaning 'in a manner that uses an emotional plea to persuade someone to do something'; earnestly, entreatingly, with a supplicating tone, look, or gesture. In the source text, Bao-yu addresses Grandmother Jia 'pleadingly': 'Dearest Grannie,' he says, 'I should be perfectly all right next to the summer-bed. There's no need to move me into your room. I should only keep you awake.'

The adverb paints a soft, affectionate, and strategically dependent manner of speech rather than a courtroom petition. Bao-yu is the spoiled favourite using emotional appeal — tone, posture of humility, and the intimacy of 'Dearest Grannie' — to keep from being moved into her room. The word captures domestic persuasion: an earnest entreaty designed to look like consideration for her sleep while actually securing his own preference. In the Jia household, pleading is a recognized social tool between doting elder and cherished heir — half sincere affection, half practised manipulation.

Polysemy — shades of meaning in current English:
  • Manner of speech (primary sense): said, asked, or spoken in an earnest, supplicating way ('said pleadingly', 'asked pleadingly'); the dominant modern use, almost always in narrative prose.
  • Look and gesture: of eyes, faces, or posture — a pleading look, a pleading glance, eyes raised pleadingly; the adverb extends from voice to the whole body's appeal.
  • Earnest request for a favour: 'to plead' in the personal sense of begging, coaxing, or entreating for something desired, short of desperation ('she looked at him pleadingly, hoping for a treat').
  • Emotional intensity and dependency: pleadingly implies vulnerability and need — the speaker places themselves at the mercy of the hearer's kindness, which is why it suits children, lovers, and dependents.
  • Legal echo (residual sense): through the verb plead ('to plead a case', 'plead guilty'), the adverb retains a faint formal echo of petition before a higher authority — in fiction this lends a delicate ceremonial colour to otherwise domestic scenes.
Boundaries. Beseechingly is more archaic and solemn, often too heavy for a grandson's coaxing; imploringly raises the emotional stakes toward desperation or crisis, which overstates Bao-yu's mild preference about bedding; entreatingly is formal and literary, stressing polite request without the slightly childlike colouring of pleadingly; wheedlingly or cajolingly imply crafty flattery and can sound more cynical than the text's affectionate dynamic; piteously focuses on exciting pity for suffering, not on winning a small domestic concession.

Collocations and register. 'said pleadingly', 'looked/asked/spoke pleadingly', often with eyes, voice, or gesture named in the same sentence. Register is standard narrative English, common in fiction for children, lovers, or dependents seeking favour. It is milder than legal 'plead' in court yet still carries a faint echo of formal petition — exactly the blend of emotional plea and social performance the scene requires. The word converts a request into a performance of need, and in Bao-yu's mouth that performance is precisely what makes it so effective on Grandmother Jia.
