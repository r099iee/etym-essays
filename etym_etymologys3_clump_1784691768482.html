The word clump begins its English life in the fourteenth century as a loan from the continental Germanic world: Middle Low German klump and Middle Dutch clumpe meant a lump, a compact heavy mass of earth, wood, or metal, and the English form clompe, clumpe, borrowed in the age of Hanseatic trade, carried exactly that sense. The base itself, Proto-Germanic *klumpa-, is one of that expressive family of Germanic words built on the consonant pattern kl- to suggest mass and cohesion — the same kin-group that produced clod, clot, cluster, and clog (a "lump of wood", and hence the wooden-soled shoe that the Dutch called a klomp). These are not refined, bookish words; they are the sounds of lumps hitting the ground, and their history in English is a history of heavy, material things. For three centuries the English clump stayed lumpish: a clump of earth, a clump of metal, a clump of dough. The word named inert mass, nothing more.

  • Sound-symbolism. The initial cluster kl- is the family's fingerprint. Germanic mass-words cluster around it — clod (from Old English clodd), clot (from Old English clott), cluster (from Old English clyster), clog, clump — because the contact of a heavy object with a surface makes a short, blunt, echoing noise that English speakers repeatedly chose to encode with kl-. The vowel kept its short u through Middle Low German and English, so the word stayed heavy and monosyllabic, a lump of a word for a lump of a thing.
  • From inert mass to living growth. Then, in the seventeenth century, the sense took a decisive turn from the inanimate to the living. A clump of trees — a dense, close-set cluster of growth standing together like a clot of vegetable matter — begins to appear, and the word's old weight is exactly what makes the new sense vivid: these are trees grown thickly together, massed, lumpish, distinct from the orderly grove or the deliberate planting. The extension is a pure metaphor of cohesion: the trees are stuck together into a single dense body, as earth is lumped into a clod.
  • The verb and the sound. The verb followed the noun into motion in the nineteenth century: to clump is to walk with the heavy, dull tread of a mass of weight, and the sound itself is also a clump. A clump of boots on stairs, a clump of hooves on turf — the verb names both the action and its noise, and it completes the semantic family: every sense of clump is a sense of something dense, heavy, and cohesive, whether it is a lump of earth, a knot of trees, or a heavy boot coming down on mud.
  • Socio-cultural and technical spread. From horticulture ("a clump of daffodils", "clumping bamboo") to the sciences (a clump of galaxies, clumped distribution in statistics), the word kept its core image of dense aggregation. In geology and soil science a clump is a coherent mass of soil; in physics and astronomy "clumping" describes matter gathering into knots; and in botany a clumping growth habit is one that sends up many stems from one base. The word never lost the accidental, unmanaged quality it had from the start: a clump is a chance gathering, not a designed one.
  • Literary attestations. In The Hobbit the word works by that inherited heaviness. The clump of trees to which the soaked company retreats is not a pleasant grove; it is a dense, accidental mass of growth, offering the one practical thing the travellers need — a patch of drier ground under a close-set canopy — but not enough canopy to defeat the wind, which shakes the rain off the leaves in an annoying drip, drip. The word's denseness is felt in the scene: these are trees grown close together, their boughs overlapping, offering a partial, leaky roof to travellers who have no better one. It is a homely, exact, slightly comic word, and it anchors the scene in the concrete detail of a night spent under trees in a storm.

Clump thus contains a complete little history of Germanic word-making: an expressive sound-symbolic root, a continental loan into medieval English, three centuries of inert, lumpish service, a seventeenth-century leap from mineral to vegetable, a nineteenth-century extension to heavy movement and its sound, and a modern technical career across horticulture, geology, astronomy, and statistics — all of it held together by the single picture of things stuck together in a dense, heavy, irregular mass.
