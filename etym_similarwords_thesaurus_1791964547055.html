• dictionary: Similar because both are alphabetical reference books about words; differs because a dictionary defines and explains, whereas a thesaurus groups words by meaning and offers equivalents without defining them.
  • lexicon: Similar because it can denote a dictionary or the whole word-stock of a language; differs because it has a scholarly, often ancient-language flavour and frequently means the vocabulary itself rather than a book.
  • glossary: Similar because it is a list of words with explanations; differs because it is restricted to the difficult or technical terms of a particular text or field.
  • vocabulary: Similar because it concerns the words available to a language or a person; differs because it usually names the stock itself, not an arranged book, and implies no grouping by meaning.
  • wordbook: Similar because it is literally a book of words; differs because it is a plain, old-fashioned term with no suggestion of synonymy or of treasure.
  • synonym dictionary: Similar because it names exactly the modern sense; differs because it is explicit and descriptive, while thesaurus is the traditional title and carries a claim to completeness and prestige.
  • gradus: Similar because it is a reference work for finding the right word; differs because a gradus is specifically a Latin dictionary arranged for verse composition, concerned with quantity and poetic idiom rather than general synonymy.
  • treasury: Similar because it is the literal original sense and the metaphor still felt inside the word; differs because it has no lexical content at all.
  • repository: Similar because both denote a place where things are stored and drawn on; differs because it is neutral and often digital, with no implication of wealth or arrangement by meaning.
  • controlled vocabulary: Similar because it is the information-science sense of the same word; differs because it prescribes indexing terms and their hierarchical relations for retrieval, not stylistic alternatives for a writer.
