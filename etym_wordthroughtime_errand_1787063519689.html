- Middle English “erende/errand” (13th–15th c.) — “message, tidings, a messenger's commission”; still names the mission and its content, used of both royal dispatches and humble letters — neutral-to-formal register.
- 16th–17th c. — “errand” increasingly names the journey itself (“to go on an errand”); the word slides down the social scale as errand-bearing becomes servants' work; “errand-boy” and “errand-girl” appear.
- 17th c. — “a fool's errand” (first recorded 1656) makes “errand” a vehicle of irony: a mission doomed to fail; the word gains a comic, deflating tone inside idioms.
- 18th–19th c. — the industrial city and the growth of retail make “running errands” a routine part of urban life; the word becomes everyday, faintly menial, pragmatic.
- 19th–20th c. — “errand of mercy” and similar literary collocations keep a dignified, humanitarian strand alive; “errand” is two-faced: humdrum chore or noble mission.
- 20th–21st c. — “errand” is ubiquitous in daily speech (“run an errand”), broadened to admin, shopping, and digital chores; the mission sense survives in formal and literary contexts, as in Tolkien's “the dwarves' errand”.
