LILAC is a classic long-distance colour-and-plant loan whose biography runs from the Indian subcontinent through Iranian and Arabic trade vocabularies into early modern European gardens and English. It is a word that travelled with goods and gardens, not with a single people's genetic family. The Indic starting point: nīla. The deepest secure form is Sanskrit nīla 'dark blue, indigo', attested already in the Rigveda (c. 1500–1000 BCE). This is the same base that yields Hindi nīlā 'blue' and the traditional indigo-dye terminology of South Asia—the rich, saturated blue of indigo that gave the world its first great natural blue dye. Beyond Sanskrit, the deeper Indo-European ancestry of nīla is uncertain; scholars do not reconstruct a confident PIE twin, and the honest treatment is to state that the root's pre-Sanskrit history is obscure rather than to invent one. What matters for the colour story is that the word begins in the deep blues of indigo, far from the pale violet that English now calls lilac. The Persian and Arabic relay. From Indic, the colour-root enters Persian as nīl 'indigo, blue', part of the great trade vocabulary that carried indigo, textiles, and dyestuffs across the Iranian plateau and into the Islamic world. Persian forms a derivative līlak / nīlak meaning roughly 'bluish' and applied to the lilac-like plant and its tint. Persian then loans the form into Arabic as līlak / līlāk. Crucially, this is not a native Semitic triliteral invention: Arabic is a carrier language on the Silk Road and Mediterranean botanical route, and the word's biography must say so rather than invent a Semitic root. The semantic lightening also begins here: the name moves from the deep blue of the dye to a bluish flowering plant and its paler blossom. Entry into Europe and English. European languages take the word from Arabic and Ottoman and Iberian contact zones. The shrub itself arrived in European gardens as an exotic from southeastern Europe and western Asia, and its name travelled with it: French lilas and Spanish lila / lilac establish the Romance forms from which English borrows in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as lilac (the first English attestations date to the 1620s). Italian lillà follows the same botanical fashion. The route of transmission—whether English took the word directly from French, from Spanish, or from the Turkish-Arabic forms via intermediaries—is slightly debated, but the family is one and the ultimate source is secure. The colour sense separates. Once naturalized, English splits the senses cleanly. Lilac first names the ornamental shrub (Syringa) newly fashionable in Western gardens; then, as the shrub's brief and fragrant blooming became a seasonal event in English gardens, the pale purple of its panicles became a colour name. The colour sense is thus secondary and culture-bound: what English speakers mean by 'lilac' is not the indigo of Sanskrit nīla but the washed violet of European lilac blossom. In the nineteenth century the colour acquired a heavy load of Victorian association—delicacy, spring, sentiment, and mourning-adjacent elegy—most memorably in Whitman's elegy for Lincoln, 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd', where the lilac's bloom and fragrance become symbols of memory and grief. Since then lilac has been a standard soft, greyed or pinkish purple in textiles, interiors, and design. The novel's context. In the Dream of the Red Chamber translation, 'lilac-coloured hangings' imports that European colour word into a Qing material world of brocade and satin. The linguistic irony is elegant: a term that began as the deep indigo of South Asian dye-making, travelled through Persian and Arabic markets, entered European gardens as an exotic shrub, and became the pale violet of Victorian sentiment, is now used to dress a Chinese heroine's bedchamber in a hue the translator judged delicate enough for her. The loan-chain biography and the novel's palette meet at the same point: colour words, like textiles, travel across the world and carry their histories with them.
