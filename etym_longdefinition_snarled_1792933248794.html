Snarled is the past tense of snarl in its animal sense: to growl viciously with bared teeth, the sound a dog makes when it means come closer and I will bite. It is a throat-sound given teeth — low, vibrating, and edged with the threat of the snap that may follow — and English hears menace in it instantly, whether it comes from a mastiff, a villain, or a queue of traffic described in metaphor.

In the source sentence two wolvish-looking farm dogs sniff the intruding hobbit suspiciously and snarled if he moved: the classic guard-dog bargain — freeze and you are sniffed, stir and you are warned. Tolkien, who loved dogs and knew farmyards intimately, stages the moment exactly as every walker knows it — the sudden stillness, the dropped head, the lip lifting off the canine teeth, the rumble that says movement will be punished. Sam does not move. The dogs have made their grammar perfectly clear.

Core meaning in detail: To snarl is to produce a sustained guttural growl while exposing the teeth, combining voice and face into a single warning display. Dogs do it over food, territory, and pups; wolves over carcasses; big cats in their own deeper register. Humans borrow it the moment civility slips — he snarled his refusal — and the verb then covers both the sound and the utterance delivered in that tone. Grammatically snarled works intransitively (the dogs snarled), transitively (she snarled abuse at them), and conditionally, as here (would snarl if he moved).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Growled with bared teeth (primary sense, of animals, from the 1590s): the dogs snarled over the bone. This is the living core — the guard-dog rumble of the source sentence — and every other sense radiates from it.
  • Spoke in a vicious, growling tone (of people): he snarled at the reporters to get out. The bridge is direct imitation: the angry human drops his voice, bares his teeth, and borrows the dog's acoustic threat, so that rudeness arrives with fangs.
  • Became entangled or knotted (the twin verb, snarl as tangle): traffic snarled for miles, the lines snarled in the wind. The bridge is felt rather than proven: a snarled rope, like a snarling dog, is twisted into something hostile that resists handling and punishes the fingers that touch it.
  • Complicated or obstructed affairs (figurative extension of the tangle): negotiations snarled over a single clause. The bridge carries the knotted rope into institutions: red tape tangles like fishing line, and progress growls to a halt.
  • Wore a snarling expression without sound (the facial sense): his face snarled with contempt. The bridge isolates the visual half of the display — the lifted lip, the wrinkled muzzle — from the sound, so that even silence can snarl.
  • Made a harsh grating sound (of machines and engines): the saws snarled through the timber. The bridge is pure acoustics: a chainsaw's aggressive roar resembles a giant dog's throat, and the verb lends the machine animal temper.

Register and connotation: snarled is unambiguously hostile — there is no friendly snarl, no playful snarl that is not a metaphor straining at the leash. Of dogs it is factual and alarming; of people it condemns, painting the speaker as brutish and out of control; of traffic and thread it complains with energy. Writers reach for it when they want threat made audible in a single syllable, and its harsh consonant cluster — sn- plus -arl — performs the very harshness it names, which is why four centuries have barely changed it.
