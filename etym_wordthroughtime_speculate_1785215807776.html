- 16th century - “Speculate” (from the Latin for watching and spying out) first means to observe closely, then, figuratively, to contemplate with the mind's eye.
- 17th–18th century (philosophy) - Solidifies as the dignified word for systematic reflection and theorising — “speculative” philosophy ranges beyond immediate evidence in the service of knowledge.
- 18th century (finance) - A second life is born: to speculate in stocks, goods, or property — to act on uncertain expectations; the philosophical and commercial senses now run in parallel.
- 19th–20th century - Mill uses it for fruitless counterfactual musing (“it would be idle to speculate”); everyday usage drifts toward guesswork without firm evidence, mildly depreciated beside the grand philosophical sense.
- Contemporary - Split between intellectual “speculate on/about” (conjecture) and financial “speculate in” (risky trading); both rest on watching for a sign, the former dignified, the latter tinged with gambling.
