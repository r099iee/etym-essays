Two words, one spelling. Modern English helm is really two separate old words that happen to share a form. The first, and the sense used in the source sentence (helms as helmets), descends from Proto-Germanic *helmaz, "helmet, protective covering," built on the Proto-Indo-European root *ḱel-, "to cover, conceal, protect." The second, the steering word (helma, the tiller), is a distinct Old English noun of uncertain ancestry, possibly connected to a Germanic root for "handle" or "to turn." Their convergence in spelling is an accident, but a productive one: English has long exploited the ambiguity between protective covering and navigational command.

Old English and the covering sense. Old English helm (from *helmaz) meant a helmet, but also any protective covering — a sense preserved in compounds and in the transferred use of kings and lords as the helm or protection of their people. The word is cognate across the Germanic family: Old Norse hjálmr, Old High German helm, and Gothic hilms all mean helmet. Even more strikingly, *ḱel-, the Indo-European root behind the family, is the same root that produces English hell (the concealed place), hall, and the -heal of "to heal" — a whole cluster of covering-and-protecting ideas.

Middle English chivalry and the great helm. In the Middle English period, helm was the standard word for the knight's helmet — the great helm of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, a heavy cylindrical headpiece, later the basinet, the armet, and the sallet. The literature of chivalry is full of helms: they are donned, doffed, struck, and cleft in the romances. At the same time the separate word helma (steering gear) continued independently, used of ships and, by extension, of the man in charge.

Renaissance divergence. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the two words began to blend in usage. As helmet (a diminutive, "little helm," borrowed from French) took over the everyday naming of head-armor, helm the covering-word drifted toward the archaic and poetic — the place where it has lived ever since. The steering word, meanwhile, kept growing: to take the helm meant to take command of a ship, and by the eighteenth century the phrase was freely applied to any leadership. The verb to helm (to steer, to command) arose in the same centuries and remains standard.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century partition. The nineteenth century fixed the modern split. Helm as "helmet" survived only as a consciously archaic or literary word — and fantasy literature of the twentieth century, above all Tolkien, gave it a new lease of life as the grand, old-fashioned word for a warrior's helmet. Helms hanging on an armory wall beside coats of mail is exactly the register the revived word inhabits. Helm as "position of command" became one of the most common figurative expressions in business and political English: at the helm, take the helm, a change at the helm. The verb to helm is now routine for leadership of organizations.

Summary. Two Old English words — helm "helmet, covering" (from PIE *ḱel-, to cover) and helma "tiller" — collided in a single spelling. The covering word retreated to archaic and fantasy registers while the steering word conquered the language of command. Modern helms is at once a knight's helmet and a captain's wheel: one word, two thousand years, two journeys.
