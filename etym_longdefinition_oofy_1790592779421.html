Oofy is a now-dated but vivid piece of British upper-class and theatrical slang meaning wealthy, moneyed, affluent, abounding in oof or ready cash. To call a man very oofy, as Wodehouse repeatedly does of plutocrats, uncles, and prospective fathers-in-law, is to say he is not merely comfortable but flush, rolling in money, capable of lavish expenditure, loans, and rescues without feeling the pinch. The term belongs to the comic register of the 1910s-1930s, conveying amused envy rather than sociological precision, and it almost always modifies men whose wealth makes them targets for borrowing, matchmaking, or gentle exploitation.

A secondary sense extends to things and atmospheres saturated with money. An oofy proposition, marriage, or clientele is one promising profit or displaying luxury. An oofy manner may suggest the confident ease of the rich, the freedom from counting shillings that allows generosity, eccentricity, or carelessness. Unlike neutral rich or wealthy, oofy foregrounds liquid, spendable, touchable cash rather than landed estate or invested capital, and it implies new, urban, often slightly vulgar money rather than ancient aristocratic lineage. It is wealth as experienced by outsiders hoping for a share.

Tertiary nuances include ironic and affectionate uses. To call a struggling actor oofy after a single success is teasing hyperbole. To describe a schoolboy as oofy for possessing five shillings is relative and comic. The diminutive -y ending softens envy into fondness, making oofiness seem like a personal characteristic akin to beefy or jolly rather than a structural economic position. In Wodehouse's world, oofy men are peculiar not because wealth corrupts but because it exposes temperament: generosity, suspiciousness, pomposity, or naivety become more visible when money removes constraint.

  • Class and comedy: Used by valets, clubmen, and ingenues to classify millionaires, uncles, and suitors as eligible or touchable for loans; essential to plots turning on subsidies, allowances, and inherited fortunes.
  • Cash versus capital: Implies ready oof in pocket rather than abstract net worth; an oofy man can produce banknotes, stand champagne, or write a cheque on the spot.
  • Period flavor: Strongly associated with Edwardian and interwar slang alongside oof, oof-bird, beans, dough, mazuma; modern use is deliberately retro, arch, or pastiche.
  • Moral coloring: Generally benign and envious rather than condemnatory; oofy people are to be cultivated, married, or gently mocked, not denounced as exploiters.
  • Gendered use: Overwhelmingly applied to men in original sources, reflecting patriarchal control of wealth; oofy women appear mainly as heiresses.

Polysemy bridges through metonymy: money called oof yields person characterized by money called oofy yields situations characterized by such persons. The -y adjectival suffix, as in filthy-richy, cashy, moneyed, personalizes economics into temperament. The peculiarity Wodehouse notes in very oofy men—their touchiness, suspicion, or eccentricity—follows logically: those constantly approached for money develop defensive habits, while those insulated by abundance develop oblivious ones. The word thus does sociological work disguised as slang, marking how wealth distorts ordinary reciprocity. It differs from rich by informality and liquidity, from opulent by lack of grandeur, from loaded by vintage charm, and from prosperous by focusing on spendable cash rather than sustained success. To be oofy is to be good for a touch, and everyone in the Drones Club knows it.

Today oofy survives mainly as a Wodehousian fossil, revived by aficionados to evoke a vanished world of sovereigns, fivers, and country-house weekends. Its very oddity—beginning with a grunt-like oof—makes it memorable, onomatopoeic of the wind knocked out by a large bill or the grunt of lifting a money-bag. That comic sound is central to its endurance in Anki decks and literary quizzes.
