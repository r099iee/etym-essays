Deep stage: no secure PIE root can be reconstructed for the tramp family. The Germanic verb is treated by historical linguists as expressive or imitative — coined out of the sound of heavy footsteps — so an honest chain begins at Proto-Germanic and does not manufacture an Indo-European etymon. [DEBATED — see note below] → Proto-Germanic *trampaną “to tread heavily, stamp, trample” (reconstructed; a class VI strong verb, continued by Old Norse trampa “to tread, trample,” Middle Low German trampen, Middle High German trampen, and the later German trampeln “to trample”; the whole set is sound-symbolic of heavy footfalls) → Middle English trampen “to tread heavily, to stamp, to walk heavily” (attested from the late 14th century CE) → Modern English tramp “to walk heavily; to trample; (noun) the sound of heavy, rhythmic footsteps; a long hard walk” (16th century CE onward), with the later social and nautical senses — “a tramp,” a vagrant (17th–18th centuries CE), and “a tramp steamer,” an irregular-route cargo ship (19th century CE) → Modern English TRAMP — the card's target form: the noun of heavy, rhythmic footfall (this card) NOTE on the debate: occasional handbooks float a connection between the tramp family and a proposed Indo-European root meaning “to step, run” (in the *drem- / *treb- area), but the evidence is thin and most specialists treat the Germanic verb as imitative of heavy feet, without a recoverable PIE ancestor. The Germanic-to-English line itself, however, is securely attested: Middle English trampen (14th century) and its continental relatives trace to *trampaną. The modern social senses (vagrant, tramp steamer) are late developments of the walking sense, not of the footfall sense, and trampoline, a French loan from Italian trampolino, is a distant relative through the same heavy-treading verb-family.
