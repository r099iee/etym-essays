• dive: Similar to duck in going head-first downward; differs because to dive is a deliberate, athletic, self-endangering commitment into water or air, whereas to duck is a quick compression of the body to avoid — always a dodging, never a plunge.
  • stoop: Similar in bending the body down; differs because stooping retains a measure of dignity and is often voluntary and deliberate, while ducking is the practiced, unheroic reflex of getting under cover.
  • crouch: Similar in lowering the whole body; differs because crouching bends the knees to hide, spring, or wait, while ducking is a single completed move — a compression to pass beneath or escape.
  • dip: Similar in lowering briefly; differs because dipping is a partial lowering with the implication of raising again, while ducking is a decisive, avoidance-driven motion.
  • dodge: Similar in evading something incoming; differs because dodging is lateral or positional evasion, while ducking is specifically the vertical lowering of head and shoulders.
  • evade / shirk: Similar in the figurative sense of "duck out"; differs because these are abstract and moral, while "duck" keeps the physical dodging gesture inside the metaphor.
  • shabby: Similar to ratty in meaning worn and run-down; differs because shabby is comparatively neutral and even pitiable, while ratty makes the object verminous — gnawed, filthy, infected — and can also name a snappish temper.
  • tattered: Similar in describing a worn-out thing; differs because tattered emphasizes rips and hanging rags, while ratty emphasizes dirt and vermin rather than tears.
  • mangy: Similar in carrying disease and patchiness; differs because mangy is literally scabby, of animals, while ratty ranges from fabric to temper.
  • dingy: Similar in describing a dirty, grimy state; differs because dingy emphasizes dullness of color and light, while ratty adds the animal odor of infestation.
  • scruffy: Similar in describing unkempt shabbiness; differs because scruffy is about untidiness and neglect, while ratty is about degradation to the level of vermin.
  • grubby: Similar in being soiled with dirt; differs because grubby is surface dirt, while ratty implies gnawed, filth-soaked decay.
  • seedy: Similar in squalor; differs because seedy points to moral tawdriness of places, while ratty points to actual vermin and physical decay.
  • irritable / testy: Similar to the British slang sense of "ratty" for a person; differs because these are neutral descriptions of bad temper, while ratty suggests a snappish, bitten, frazzled edge.
  • intoxicating: Similar to heady in acting on the mind like liquor; differs because intoxicating is plainly admiring and literal about alcohol, while heady can be sickening or thrilling and names the head as the organ affected.
  • potent: Similar in emphasizing power, especially of drink and drugs; differs because potent is general force, while heady specifies a dizzying, consciousness-altering effect.
  • pungent: Similar in a sharp sensory bite; differs because pungent is sharp and biting to nose or tongue, while heady is dense and overwhelming, going to the head.
  • overpowering: Similar in overwhelming the senses; differs because overpowering is neutral force, while heady carries the vocabulary of elevation — glamorous, intoxicating, dizzying.
  • acrid: Similar in a harsh, irritating smell; differs because acrid is bitter and stinging, especially of smoke, while heady implies a concentration that acts on consciousness.
  • exhilarating: Similar to the modern "heady days" sense; differs because exhilarating is purely positive, while heady always holds the dizziness of overload — glamour and nausea together.
  • sortie: Similar in being the military rush-out that gave "sally" its meaning; differs because sortie is the technical noun from French, while sally is the English form that also took on wit and excursion senses.
  • rush: Similar in moving out suddenly; differs because rushing is haste without ceremony, while sallying implies a deliberate, purposeful breaking-out against a besieger.
  • foray: Similar in a sudden incursion; differs because a foray is a raid into enemy territory to plunder, while a sally is a breaking-out from a besieged place.
  • dash: Similar in quick movement; differs because dashing is impetuous and excited, while sallying is gallant and deliberate, with siege overtones.
  • venture: Similar in going forward in the face of risk; differs because venturing is uncertain and commercial, while sallying is military and purposeful.
