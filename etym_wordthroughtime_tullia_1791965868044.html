- 1st c. BCE — Tullia is a Roman woman’s nomen; in her father’s letters she is Tulliola, and public knowledge of her is confined to her marriages and her death.
- Antiquity — Livy’s Tullia Minor fixes the name in literary memory as the type of the unnatural daughter, while Cicero’s Tullia survives mainly in his correspondence and in Plutarch’s Life.
- Middle Ages and Renaissance — Boccaccio’s catalogue of famous women and humanist reading of Cicero keep both Tullias visible, and the name carries an unresolved double association.
- 18th–19th c. — Middleton’s Life of Cicero (1741) and Melmoth’s translation of the letters (1753) make Cicero’s Tullia a familiar figure of pathos in English, and Victorian biographies sentimentalise her.
- 20th–21st c. — social history and women’s history recover her as an agent with her own marital strategies, while the name remains a scholarly identifier and a living Italian given name.
