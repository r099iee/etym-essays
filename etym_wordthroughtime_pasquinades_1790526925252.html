- Early 16th Century (Rome, c. 1501-1520s) - Roman citizens begin pasting anonymous Latin verses on the mutilated Hellenistic statue dubbed Pasquino; Italian terms pasquino, pasquinata emerge as expressions of anti-papal satire and street liberty.
- Late 16th Century (1580s-1590s) - French adapts pasquinade, English borrows pasquil and pasquinade; Elizabethan and Jacobean writers use it for polemical religious pamphlets and scurrilous court satires (e.g., the Marprelate controversy).
- Eighteenth Century (1700s) - Augustan and Enlightenment satire; Fielding (The Pasquin), Swift, and Sterne establish pasquinades as the quintessential foil to sycophantic panegyrics in European political and literary warfare.
- Nineteenth Century (1800s) - High literary historiography; Romantic and Victorian historians (Carlyle, Macaulay) use pasquinades to evoke the underground revolutionary wit of Renaissance Italy and pre-revolutionary France.
- Contemporary English (1900s-present) - Sophisticated historical and literary term; retained in educated prose to denote sharp, anonymous, or public satirical broadsides against political hypocrisy.
