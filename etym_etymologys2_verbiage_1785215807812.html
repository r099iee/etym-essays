Linear derivation chain for VERBIAGE (single root): 1. PIE *werh₁- ("to speak, say, utter") — the Indo-European root of speaking and saying, attested across the family (Latin, Germanic, Greek, Indo-Iranian). An extended variant *werdh- produced the Germanic word-word (Proto-Germanic *wurda- → Old English word → English word). 2. Latin verbum ("a word, an utterance; the verb") — the direct Latin outcome of *werh₁-; classical usage from the earliest Latin texts. Neutral in meaning: a word, a saying, a proverb (verbum sapienti sat est, "a word to the wise is enough"). The source of English verb, verbal, verbatim, proverb, adverb. 3. Old French verbe ("a word; the verb") — the Romance outcome of Latin verbum, carried into medieval French. 4. French verbiage ("an excess of words, wordiness") — coined in French from verbe + the suffix -iage, which descends from Latin -āticum and denotes a collection, quantity, or state (as in feuillage, "a mass of leaves"). Attested in French from the 16th–17th century; the pejorative "mass of words" sense is native to French. 5. English VERBIAGE ("superfluous or redundant words; wordiness") — borrowed from French in the early 18th century (attested c. 1720s), in an age of Enlightenment prose when critics and editors were turning against verbose official and scholarly writing. Note: classical Latin verbum is neutral and simply means "word"; the critical, dismissive colouring of verbiage was born in French. The English word is a direct borrowing from French — the only Romance development of this root that reached English in this form.
