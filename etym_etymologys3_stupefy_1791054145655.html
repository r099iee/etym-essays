Stupefy enters English as a learned Latin-French borrowing meaning to make stupid in the original sense: to strike senseless. Its Latin source is stupēre (to be stunned, be amazed, stand agape), seen in Cicero and Ovid for bodies rigid with shock and minds blank with wonder, and its causative compound stupefacere (stupē- + facere, to make), which gives stupefactus, stunned. Latin already contains the whole later range: physical stunning by a blow, mental stunning by marvels, and moral stunning into foolishness — hence stupor (numbness, amazement), stupidus (dazed, foolish), and stultus nearby. Whether stupēre continues PIE *steup- (to strike, to push, seen in English stoop, steep?) is debated. Pokorny proposed it; de Vaan marks it uncertain because initial st- + -up- clusters are often expressive or sound-symbolic rather than regularly inherited. The protocol therefore requires an explicit debate note, supplied in S2.

Old French stupefier (12th–14th century) carried the Latin range into vernacular medicine and marvel literature: to stupefy a limb with cold, to stupefy readers with wonders. Middle English borrowed it in the 15th century as stupefien/stupefy alongside stupid, stupor, stupendous. Early attestations are medical and theological: Caxton and later Elyot use stupefied for limbs benumbed by disease and minds dulled by sin. The spelling with -fy (French -fier from Latin -ficare) marks it as a causative — to make + stupor — parallel to petrify, mortify, clarify.

Semantic expansion in English proceeded through overlapping stages:

  • Physical stunning (15th–17th c.): To knock senseless with a blow, cold, or drug. Surgeons stupefy a patient with opium or dwarf-elder before amputation; the Tahitian passage preserves this sense for fish.
  • Pharmacological dulling (16th–19th c.): To benumb with narcotics or alcohol. Herbals warn that hemlock and nightshade stupefy; temperance tracts say gin stupefies the poor.
  • Emotional-cognitive overwhelming (16th c.–present): To amaze, astound, dumbfound. Shakespeare's contemporaries are stupefied with grief, joy, or prodigies. This becomes the dominant literary sense.
  • Boring into dullness (19th c.–present, colloquial): To dull by monotony. Dickens and later journalists write of stupefying routine, stupefying boredom — a weakened, frequentative sense where no drug is involved.

Phonetically, Latin stupefacere [stʊpɛˈfaːkɛrɛ] → Vulgar Latin shortening → Old French stupefier [stypəfje] with loss of intervocalic c and yod formation → Middle English stupefy with English stress on first syllable STU-pe-fy and French u [y] anglicised to /uː/. Received Pronunciation today is /ˈstjuːpɪfaɪ/, with yod /stj-/ retained (American often /ˈstuː-/). Inflections are regular: stupefies, stupefied, stupefying.

Literary attestations anchor each layer. The Geneva Bible and King James tradition use astonish where Latin has stupefacere, but sermons prefer stupefy for moral numbness. Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) warns that idleness stupefies the mind. Johnson's Dictionary (1755) defines To Stupefy as to make stupid, to deprive of sensibility, citing Boyle on opiates. Shelley and Keats are stupefied with delight; Conan Doyle's victims are stupefied with chloroform; Orwell's proles are stupefied by lotteries and gin. Modern headlines keep both poles: a drug stupefies a victim (crime), a comeback stupefies critics (sport).

In the source sentence, stupefy governs fish into cooperation — a wry Durant euphemism where poisoning is framed as persuasion. The verb thus bridges chemistry and psychology: plants that benumb gills are described as if they persuaded minds. That bridge is the word's whole history in miniature, from struck body to dulled mind.
