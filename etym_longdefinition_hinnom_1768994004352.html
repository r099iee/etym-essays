Hinnom (proper noun; also the Valley of Hinnom, biblical Hebrew Gēʾ-Hinnōm) — the valley south and southwest of Jerusalem that became, through Scripture, one of the great names of hell. In its literal sense it is a geographic place: a ravine on the boundary between the tribal allotments of Judah and Benjamin, named in the book of Joshua as the "Valley of Hinnom" and, in the fuller formula, "the valley of the son of Hinnom," indicating a family or clan that held land there. Historically it is a place of infamy: within it stood the Tophet, the cult-site where, in the days of Kings Ahaz and Manasseh, children were offered in fire to the god Molech, and which the prophet Jeremiah denounced as a coming "Valley of Slaughter." Theologically it is the ancestor of hell itself: from the Second Temple period the polluted valley became a figure of the final judgment, and its Aramaic contraction Gehannā passed into Greek as Gehenna, the New Testament's standard word for the place of eschatological fire, rendered "hell" in English Bibles.
  • Primary sense — the biblical toponym: Gēʾ-Hinnōm, "the Valley of Hinnom," a real ravine outside Jerusalem (Joshua 15:8; 18:16; Nehemiah 11:30). The name is a place-name, and in English (chiefly through the King James Version) Hinnom is the plain name of the valley.
  • Secondary sense — the site of abomination: the same valley contained the Tophet, the sacrificial fire-pit to Molech where children were passed through fire (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31–32; 19:6). This historical horror is what loaded the name: the valley of child-sacrifice became the valley of fire and death.
  • Tertiary sense — hell itself: through the Aramaic Gehannā and the Greek Gehenna, the valley's name became the New Testament's word for the place of eternal punishment (Matthew 5:22; Mark 9:43–48), and so Hinnom, by its very geography, is the historical origin of the word "hell" in its eschatological sense.
  • Figurative and modern sense: by extension, "Hinnom" (and "to go down through Hinnom") names any place or situation of fire, destruction, or suffering — a byword for catastrophe, damnation, and the place of no return. In the passage the earthmen, "with a fair chance of unlimited gold at the journey's end, would have gone down through Hinnom": they would have descended into the very valley of fire and damnation for the chance of wealth. The word is deliberately archaic and biblical, an Old Testament thunder-note in a science-fantasy landscape, and it measures greed by the scale of eternal punishment — no horror, not even the fires of the accursed valley, would turn them back.
  • Semantic boundaries: Gehenna is the Greek form of the same name, the theological place of eschatological fire; Tophet names the specific cult-site within the valley; Sheol is the Hebrew underworld — the shadowy common grave of all the dead, gloomy but not fiery; Hades is the Greek equivalent, the neutral realm of the shades; Tartarus is the Greek place of torment for the wicked; hell is the general English term, generic and doctrinal. Hinnom alone is the geography — the real ravine whose name became damnation.
The historical bridge is a valley that changed meaning. A routine place-name in the land registry of ancient Israel became the site of a horror, then the prophet's byword for judgment, then the very name of hell. Every modern use of Hinnom carries that whole descent in miniature: the word is a map of terror, the biography of a real place that became a theological place.
