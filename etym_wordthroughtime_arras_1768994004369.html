- 14th c. - arras enters Middle English as an eponym: the rich woven hangings made in the workshops of the city of Arras in Artois, northern France, then Europe's pre-eminent centre of the tapestry trade; the word simply names a fine wall tapestry and signals wealth and ceremony.
- 14th–15th c. - the great hangings become the most prized furnishings a household can own, listed in inventories and wills; Chaucer plants the word in the Knight's Tale, cementing its aristocratic, ceremonial register.
- 16th–17th c. - the turn toward concealment: heavy, opaque hangings become the natural hiding-places of doorways, alcoves, and eavesdroppers; Polonius hides "behind the arras" in Hamlet, fixing the word in the popular imagination as a veil that hides while appearing merely ornamental.
- 18th–19th c. - the Arras trade declines and the word recedes from daily speech, kept alive in historical fiction, stage directions, and Shakespearean allusion as a deliberately antique noun.
- 20th–21st c. - consciously archaic and literary; modern prose uses it for period flavour and atmosphere while ordinary speech has long retreated to "tapestry" or "wall hanging"; in metaphor it now names a thick, opaque, concealing covering - as in "the arras of cryptic silence."
