Sprouted is the weak past of the native English verb sprout, a word of Germanic ancestry whose root belongs to the deep Indo-European vocabulary of scattering, sowing, and pushing forth. The ultimate base is usually reconstructed as PIE *sper- "to strew, scatter, sow," sometimes extended as *spreu-d- with a dental element denoting a spreading or spurting motion. The family is large and conspicuous across the Indo-European world: Latin spargere "to scatter, sprinkle" lies behind English sparse, disperse, and asperse; Greek speirō "to sow" and its noun spóros "seed, sowing" lie behind modern scientific spore and sporophyte; and related strew-words appear in other branches. The precise shape of the root at the PIE level is debated — whether the base is simple *sper- or a set of extended forms *spreu-, *spreud-, *sprəu- — but the semantic core is agreed: the root denotes the action of flinging outward, strewing seeds, or pushing growth out from a point, and from that core the growth-meaning of sprout develops within Germanic. This is exactly the kind of root where the honest etymologist says "the exact reconstruction is debated" rather than inventing a single neat form. Proto-Germanic produced the verb *spreutaną "to sprout, spring up, shoot forth," built on the root with the Germanic growth-motion extension, together with the noun family that goes with it. The West Germanic reflexes are strikingly uniform in form and meaning: Old English sprūtan "to sprout, germinate" (attested chiefly in the derivatives and compounds of the Anglo-Saxon record), Middle Dutch spruten (modern Dutch spruiten), Middle Low German spruten, and Old High German spriozan with its modern descendant sprießen "to sprout," beside the noun German Spross "a shoot, sprout." Old Norse shows the same family in sproti "a sprout, shoot, twig" and the related verb spretta. The uniformity is the signature of a genuinely old Germanic word: every branch built the same verb and the same noun from the same inherited root, and the meaning "new growth pushing out" is common Germanic, not a loan from one branch to another. The Germanic word took the strewing root of the seeds and focused it on the plant's own response: the seed that the sower scatters then itself scatters shoots upward — a beautiful and ancient convergence of the sowing image with the shooting image. In Old English the verb sprūtan was part of the ordinary vocabulary of agriculture and woodcraft, though the surviving textual record preserves it mostly in compounds and derivatives — the language of the Anglo-Saxon field and forest rather than of the surviving prose. Middle English continues it as spruten, sprouten, recorded from the thirteenth century in the sense "to bud, germinate, put forth shoots." Modern English sprout is the direct descendant, used as both verb and noun. The noun development is interesting: "a sprout" is any young shoot, and in the vegetable garden and the kitchen the word specialized to the edible young growth — most famously the Brussels sprout, the small head of the cabbage-like plant, and the bean sprout of East Asian cuisine, where the sprout is the germinated seed eaten whole. The morphological behavior settled in the modern period as a regular weak verb — sprout, sprouted, sprouted — and the past form sprouted is therefore a recent and ordinary formation, the regular past of a verb whose root is thousands of years old. The weak past is a sign of the verb's everyday status: it did not keep a strong pattern but settled into the regular class that English uses for its most frequent, least archaic verbs. The figurative life of the verb is where the word does its modern work. To sprout is not merely to grow but to appear suddenly and in abundance, as shoots appear after rain: buildings "sprout up" in a city, rumors sprout, businesses sprout, ideas sprout. The phrasal verb sprout up adds the sense of rising into visibility from below ground, and the particle carries the whole movement from hidden to seen. The metaphor is ancient in principle — growth as emergence from a small beginning — and English has used sprout figuratively since the early modern period, long before the modern urban extensions. The verb keeps its botanical frame even in figurative use: things that sprout are imagined as small, vigorous, multiple, and sudden. In the nutrition trade of the twenty-first century the word gained a technical life — sprouted grains, sprouted flour, sprouted bread — that returns the verb to its oldest literal ground, the germination of the seed, even as the figurative senses continue to multiply in ordinary speech. Tolkien uses exactly the botanical frame in the prologue of The Hobbit: "Tales and adventures sprouted up all over the place wherever he went, in the most extraordinary fashion." The sentence is botanical comedy applied to legend-making. Gandalf walks through the world and, as if his footsteps were seeds, narrative growth springs from the ground around him — stories are volunteer growth, appearing "all over the place" and "in the most extraordinary fashion," the way weeds or spring barley come up where you least expect them. The past tense makes it a historical pattern, the completed flowering of the wizard's earlier wanderings, and the phrasal "sprouted up" gives the growth its vertical, visible motion. For a philologist-novelist who understood the ancient strewing-and-sowing root of the word, the joke is exact: the wizard sows, and stories sprout. The word's journey — from an Indo-European scattering of seed, through the Germanic shoot-verbs of every branch, to the sudden multiplication of adventure in a fantasy prologue — shows how deeply the oldest agricultural metaphors still organize the way English imagines creation and increase. Summary bullets: - PIE *sper- / *spreu-d- "to strew, scatter, sow" (shape debated; attested in Latin spargere, Greek speirō / spóros). - Proto-Germanic *spreutaną "to sprout, spring up": the growth-extension of the strew-root within Germanic. - West Germanic uniformity: Old English sprūtan, Middle Dutch spruten, German sprießen, Old Norse sproti. - Old English sprūtan: agricultural and woodcraft vocabulary, thinly attested in the simplex. - Middle English spruten / sprouten (13th c.): "to bud, germinate." - Modern sprout (v. / n.); regular weak past sprouted. - Noun specialization: bean sprouts, Brussels sprouts; modern sprouted grains and flours. - Figurative: "sprout up" — sudden abundant appearance (buildings, rumors, tales). - Tolkien: "Tales and adventures sprouted up" — botanical comedy, the wizard as sower.
