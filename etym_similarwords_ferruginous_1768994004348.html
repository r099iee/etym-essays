• Rusty: Similar because it names corroded iron and its reddish-brown colour; differs because it is everyday and colloquial, implying the neglect of manufactured objects, whereas ferruginous is formal and scientific, suggesting natural mineral content rather than human carelessness.
  • Russet: Similar because it names a dull reddish-brown hue; differs because it is chiefly a colour word of cloth, fruit, or potatoes, carrying no mineral chemistry at all.
  • Ochreous (ochraceous): Similar because it denotes an earthy reddish-yellow-brown tone; differs because it refers to a clay pigment (ochre) rather than to the iron oxides of rust.
  • Ferric: Similar because it is a chemical term built on the Latin root for iron; differs because it denotes specifically the trivalent Fe3+ oxidation state and carries no colour value.
  • Ferrous: Similar because it too is a chemical iron-term; differs because it denotes the divalent Fe2+ oxidation state, again with no colour meaning — and is easily confused with ferruginous precisely because both begin with "ferr".
  • Ferriferous: Similar because it means "iron-bearing"; differs because it is a pure composition word with none of the rust-colour sense that ferruginous acquired.
  • Ferreous: Similar because it derives from Latin ferrum "iron"; differs because it means "of, like, or made of iron" (even "iron-hearted"), an archaic and mostly figurative word, without the rust-hue.
  • Tawny: Similar because it names a warm brown-orange colour; differs because it is a general colour term of animals and cloth, with no connection to iron or oxidation.
  • Ruddy: Similar because it names a reddish complexion or tone; differs because it is a common, healthful word for skin, cheeks, or light, with no metallic or chemical sense.
  • Hematitic: Similar because it involves iron in mineral form; differs because it specifically references hematite (iron oxide ore) as a rock or pigment, a much rarer and more technical term than ferruginous.
  • Sideritic: Similar because it belongs to iron-mineral vocabulary (siderite); differs because it names a specific iron-carbonate mineral and is used almost only by mineralogists, without the colour sense.
