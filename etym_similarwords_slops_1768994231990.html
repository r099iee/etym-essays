• swill: Similar because it names liquid household waste, especially kitchen food-leavings; differs because swill is specifically the pig-swill of the farmyard — animal feed rather than the soiled water of a sickroom.
  • sewage: Similar because both denote liquid waste matter; differs because sewage is institutional and infrastructural, carried off by pipes and sewers on the scale of a city, wholly impersonal, while slops is domestic, intimate, and recurrent.
  • wastewater: Similar because both name used, discarded water; differs because wastewater is the clinical technical term of plumbing and treatment plants, odourless and abstract, while slops keeps the brute wet physicality of a basin carried downstairs.
  • effluent: Similar because both denote liquid waste discharged; differs because effluent is the technical discharge of a factory, treatment works, or industrial process, while slops is the organic refuse of a household body.
  • refuse: Similar because both denote discarded matter; differs because refuse is solid, dry, and often bulky general waste, lacking any liquid character and any sense of a body being nursed.
  • garbage: Similar because both denote waste thrown away; differs because garbage is solid kitchen and household rubbish, dry and often bagged, while slops is wet, liquid, and carried in open vessels.
  • trash: Similar because both denote things discarded; differs because trash is dry, light, and mixed waste, with no liquid implication at all, while slops names exactly the wet residue of living.
  • dishwater: Similar because both are dirty water from a kitchen; differs because dishwater is the specific wash-water of crockery, neutral and confined to the kitchen, while slops covers the full range of soiled water including the sickroom's.
  • bilge: Similar because both name dirty waste water collected in a lower space; differs because bilge is specifically the foul water that gathers in a ship's hold — nautical, enclosed, and stagnant — while slops is domestic and routinely emptied.
  • hogwash: Similar because both name liquid waste fed to pigs; differs because hogwash is swill by another name, now chiefly figurative for nonsense or worthless talk, while slops retains its literal household meaning.
  • leavings: Similar because both denote the remains of a meal; differs because leavings is what is left on plates, solid or mixed, while slops is the separately collected liquid waste of kitchen and sickroom.
  • slush: Similar because both denote soft, wet, semi-liquid matter; differs because slush is chiefly melting snow and ice — weather, not refuse — while slops is the deliberate collection of household liquid waste.
  • gruel: Similar because both can name thin, watery food for invalids; differs because gruel is genuinely a food (oatmeal boiled in water or milk), while the "slops" fed to convalescents are cast-offs of a thinner consistency, and the word keeps the taste of the waste-basin.
  • muck: Similar because both denote wet dirt and filth; differs because muck is generalized dirt and manure, often agricultural, while slops is specifically liquid household and sickroom waste.
  • filth: Similar because both denote offensive waste matter; differs because filth is a broad term for dirt and foulness of any kind — moral as well as physical — while slops names a particular domestic category of liquid refuse.
