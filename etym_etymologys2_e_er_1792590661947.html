PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500 BCE)
*h₂óyu- / *h₂eyw-
"vital energy, life force, lifetime, eternity"
(source of Latin aevum, Greek aiōn)
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*aiwō / *aiwaz
"age, eternity, ongoing law, always"
(extended with prepositional element *frī / *farinō)
↓
Old English (c. 8th c.; Beowulf, Alfredian prose)
ǣfre
"ever, at any time, perpetually, always"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.; Ormulum, Chaucer)
ever / euere / er
"ever; (in verse) contracted to er / euer"
↓
Early Modern English (poetic syncope with apostrophe) (c. late 16th c.; Shakespeare, Milton)
e’er
"ever (contracted monosyllabic poetic form)"

Note on typographical evolution and syncope: The apostrophe in e’er was introduced during the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras to indicate typographical elision (syncope) of the letter 'v', distinguishing this contraction from the unrelated word ere (meaning "before", from Old English ǣr). In Early Modern printing (such as the Shakespeare First Folio of 1623 and Milton’s 1667 Paradise Lost), compositors frequently alternated between e'er, e're, and eer to signal to the speaker or actor that the metric demanded a single syllable.
