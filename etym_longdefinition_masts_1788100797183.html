MASTS (plural of mast) are the tall, long, upright spars that rise from the deck of a sailing vessel and carry its sails, rigging, yards, flags, and — as in the sentence that supplies this note, from Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince — the lanterns that glow along a river at night: "He passed over the river, and saw the lanterns hanging to the masts of the ships." The word is one of the oldest in the seafaring vocabulary of the Germanic languages, and its senses radiate outward from the ship to land and sky.

Primary sense.

  • Ship's mast: the vertical wooden (historically) or metal (modernly) spar stepped into the keel or deck of a boat, to which the sails are bent by way of yards, booms, and gaffs, and from which the rigging — shrouds, stays, halyards — is spread. Vessels are classified by their masting: a single-masted sloop or cutter, a two-masted ketch or schooner, a three-masted ship or barque; the great sailing ships of the nineteenth century mounted fore, main, and mizzen masts, each built in several sections (lower mast, topmast, topgallant mast, royal mast).
  • Specific nautical roles: the mainmast is the tallest; the foremast stands forward; the mizzenmast astern; the jigger is the aftermost mast of a four-master. A mast also carries the crow's nest, the signal halyards, and the navigation lights that make ships visible at night — the very lanterns of the Wilde sentence.

Extended and transferred senses.

  • Radio, television, and antenna masts: since the early twentieth century, any tall vertical structure supporting transmitters or aerials, typically a latticed steel tower — the modern continuation of the word's "tall pole" core, now carrying signals instead of sails.
  • Derrick and crane masts: in engineering, the upright post of a derrick or the vertical member of certain cranes; in drilling, the mast supports the drill string.
  • Half-mast: the phrase for a flag flown below the peak of its staff as a sign of mourning; the flag is raised to the top and then lowered to "half-mast", a ceremony that has made the word a small emblem of public grief.
  • Before the mast: the old idiom for the life of the common sailor, who berthed in the forecastle forward of the mainmast, immortalised by Richard Henry Dana Jr.'s memoir Two Years Before the Mast (1840) — the phrase now means any humble, hard-working life at sea.
  • Figurative uses: a "mast" may be any tall, erect, columnar object; the idiom "nail one's colours to the mast" (never surrender) uses the mast as the pole of honour from which a ship's identity is flown.

Homonym note. English also has a second word mast, from Old English mæst, meaning "the fallen nuts and acorns on which pigs fatten" — a word of entirely different origin that happens to share the form. In woodland and farming contexts (mast year, beech mast) it is this homonym that is meant; the two words have never been confused in sailing usage but are a favourite trap of word-history.

Semantic bridge. The single bridge from ship to shore to sky is "vertical pole carrying something aloft": sails, then lanterns and flags, then radio waves, then antennas. Every modern sense is a re-application of the shipwright's most visible piece of timber.
