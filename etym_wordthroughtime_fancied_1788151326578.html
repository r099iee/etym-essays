- Late 14th century - "fantasy" (via Old French fantaisie, from Greek phantasia "appearance, imagination") is shortened in speech to "fancy"; the participle "fancied" meaning "imagined, devised by fancy" appears in the 15th-16th centuries.
- 16th-17th centuries - Shakespeare and his age use "fancy" for amorous inclination ("Tell me, where is fancy bred"); "fancied" gains the twin value "beloved, held in the heart's imagination" - love and imagination are one idea.
- 18th century - philosophical writers (Locke, Addison) treat fancy and imagination nearly interchangeably, making "fancied" a technical word of aesthetics: the fancied vision, the fancied pleasure - refined, mental, calm.
- 19th century - the Romantics re-split fancy from imagination (Coleridge's hierarchy), so "fancied" now can mean "merely invented, not genuinely imagined"; meanwhile the sporting use is born, and a "fancied horse" is one the public backs and hopes will win.
- 20th century - British colloquial "to fancy" = "to want" (fancy a drink, fancy him) makes "fancied" the participle of desire; in American English the verb withers, so the word splits regionally: imaginary in America, desired-and-backed in Britain.
- 21st century - "fancied" thrives in sports pages (the fancied favourite), in the reflexive idiom ("he fancies himself a poet"), and in literary prose for imagined slights and idealized scenes - a compact bridge between fantasy, desire, and judgement.
