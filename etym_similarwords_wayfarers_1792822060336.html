• Travellers: Similar because both journey from place to place; differs because travellers is the general modern word (cars, trains, planes), while wayfarers go specifically on foot along open roads.
  • Pilgrims: Similar because both fare along ways toward a goal, often holy; differs because pilgrims journey for faith to a shrine, while wayfarers may travel for any reason, sacred or not.
  • Wanderers: Similar because both roam without settled home; differs because wanderers drift aimlessly by nature, while wayfarers follow ways — roads and paths imply direction even when the destination is unknown.
  • Wayfaring strangers: Similar because the folk song names exactly this figure; differs because the song's stranger is one lonesome soul with a spiritual burden, while wayfarers is the everyday plural of all road-goers.
  • Hikers: Similar because both walk long distances with packs; differs because hikers walk for sport with gear and maps, while wayfarers walk as a condition of life, often from need.
  • Tramps: Similar because in the Shire the strange wayfarers look much like tramps; differs because tramp stresses destitution and vagrancy, while wayfarer dignifies the same figure with purpose and poetry.
  • Vagrants: Similar because both are homeless figures on the road; differs because vagrant is legalistic and disapproving (vagrancy laws), while wayfarer is romantic and charitable.
  • Nomads: Similar because both live in motion rather than settlement; differs because nomads move as peoples with herds and seasons, while wayfarers are solitary or small-group walkers on made roads.
  • Passers-by: Similar because both are strangers briefly seen and gone; differs because passers-by merely happen to pass (streets, stations), while wayfarers are committed to the road as their way of life.
