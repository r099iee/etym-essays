Rousing is in its dominant modern use an adjective meaning exciting, stirring, and full of vigorous spirit — anything that wakes an audience from indifference and sweeps it into shared enthusiasm: a rousing speech, a rousing chorus, a rousing finale, a rousing victory, a rousing reception.

In the source sentence the word colours a burst of defiant song on the very edge of danger: once the company reaches the edge they will turn and give their pursuers a rousing chorus — sung loudly, heartily, and all together, voices raised in full-throated fellowship. Here rousing does double work: the singing is hearty enough to stir the singers themselves, and pointed enough to startle or mock whoever hears it.

Grammar in brief: the form is the present participle of the verb rouse (to wake, to stir up), long since established as an independent adjective — first recorded in the adjectival sense in the late sixteenth century. Like stirring, thrilling, and electrifying, it keeps one foot in the verb (the act of waking or stirring) and one in pure description (the quality of being exciting).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Exciting and stirring, full of spirit (primary adjectival sense): a rousing anthem, a rousing performance, a rousing campaign speech. The bridge runs straight from the verb rouse meaning to wake: what wakes sleepers wakes audiences, lifting them from torpor into energy, applause, and action.
  • Waking or awakening someone, literally bringing out of sleep or rest (participial sense): rousing the household at dawn, rousing the crew for the night watch. This is the verb at its plainest — shaking, calling, or stirring sleepers into motion — and the source of every figurative extension.
  • Stirring up feelings, suspicions, or opposition (figurative verbal sense): to rouse anger, pity, curiosity, or suspicion; to rouse the nation to indignation. The bridge is metaphor: emotions are sleepers lying dormant in the breast, and an insult, a story, or a speech wakes them as surely as reveille wakes a soldier.
  • Vigorous, hearty, splendid — an intensifier of praise (colloquial adjectival sense): a rousing cheer, a rousing success, a rousing dinner, a rousing welcome. The bridge runs through energy itself: whatever is done with the full-blooded vigour of awakened people earns the adjective, so that rousing slides from describing the effect (it rouses us) to describing the quality (it is done with spirit).
  • Starting game from cover; rising to action (old sporting and hunting sense): the stag rouses from its lair, the hawk rouses its feathers. This preserves the word's earliest recorded habitat — falconry and the chase — where rouse first meant the shaking and ruffling by which a hawk settles its plumage before flight. The bridge to the modern word is the visible shiver of coming awake: feathers shaken out, sleepers stretching, crowds rising to their feet.
  • A waking or stirring-up, as a noun (rare, chiefly poetic): a rude rousing from dreams. The bridge is simple conversion of verb to noun, surviving in literary phrases where the shock of being wakened is itself the subject.

Register and connotation: rousing is almost entirely positive — journalists, sports writers, critics, and politicians reach for it to praise energy honestly felt and generously shared. A rousing speech moves even the sceptical; a rousing defeat of a rival delights the home crowd. The one shadow falls on rousing suspicions or rousing hatred, where the same waking power turns unwelcome — proof that to wake what sleeps is not always a kindness.
