FitzJames — in Scott usually hyphenated Fitz-James — is the name of the mysterious Knight of Snowdoun in Walter Scott's narrative poem The Lady of the Lake (1810), on whom Ruskin says he chiefly formed his boyhood notion of kinghood. A gallant hunter lost in the Trossachs, entertained by Ellen Douglas on Loch Katrine, waylaid by Roderick Dhu, victorious in single combat, and finally revealed in Stirling Castle as James V himself, the King, FitzJames is the chivalric incognito king: brave, courteous, merciful, and fond of guising among his people. For the child Ruskin, who paired him with the Douglas lords of The Lady of the Lake and Marmion, FitzJames meant what a king should be — hard-working, adventurous, just — before empty modern castles disappointed him.

As a name in the world, FitzJames is a Norman-French patronymic surname meaning son of James (Fitz = son + James). After 1066 Anglo-Norman scribes wrote illegitimate royal sons as FitzRoy (son of the king), FitzClarence, FitzCharles, FitzGeorge, and likewise FitzJames for sons of kings named James — above all James FitzJames, 1st Duke of Berwick (1670-1734), illegitimate son of James II by Arabella Churchill, Marshal of France, victor of Almanza (1707), philosopher-soldier praised by Montesquieu and buried in Paris. The surname survives in England, Ireland, and America (often Fitzjames), in colleges, streets, and ships named for the duke, and as a Christian-name echo (Fitzjames Stephen). Scott borrowed a real bastardy-tag and made it a king's sport-disguise.

  • Literary head sense: Scott's Knight of Snowdoun (source sense): Canto 1 (Chase) to Canto 6 (Guard-Room): James Fitz-James, the Knight of Snowdoun hunts a stag to Ellen's isle, trades a ring for hospitality, fights Roderick on the mountainside (thy name and birth demanded), is captured, and unveils himself to pardon Roderick, reunite Ellen with Malcolm Graeme, and reconcile Douglas. E.g. FitzJames's ring, FitzJames and Roderick's combat. Traits: archery, minstrelsy, mercy, love of disguise — the Goodman of Ballengeich legend of the historical James V (who roamed disguised) versified. The bridge to kinghood is moral: a king proves himself by labour and risk, not by throne.
  • Historical king behind the mask: James V of Scotland (1512-1542): the historical King of the Commons who indeed wandered disguised as the Gudeman of Ballengeich, dispensing rough justice in ballads (Christis Kirk, The Jolly Beggar). Scott collapses James IV's Flodden chivalry and James V's gaberlunzie adventures into one FitzJames. To know FitzJames historically is to know Stewart kingship as touring, spying, wenching, and pardoning — the very harder work than anybody else Ruskin admired.
  • Historical duke sense: James FitzJames, Duke of Berwick: e.g. Berwick's FitzJames at Almanza, FitzJames's Memoirs. Natural son of James II, nephew of Marlborough, Catholic exile commanding Louis XIV's and Philip V's armies, father of the Spanish Dukes of Liria and Fitz-James Stuart (ancestors of the Empress Eugenie). Here FitzJames means Jacobite exile valour and Franco-Spanish generalship — a different, adult, continental kingship-shadow beside Scott's Highland pageant.
  • Onomastic-pattern sense: Fitz- as son-of (often illegitimate): e.g. Fitzroy, Fitzgerald, Fitzwilliam, Fitzhenry, Fitzalan. Norman fitz (son) prefixes a father's Christian name; when the father is a king, discretion replaces the surname with Roy. FitzJames exemplifies the rule: to be a Fitz is to be acknowledged yet set apart. The semantic bridge is social: a name that confesses birth while claiming blood.
  • Ruskinian moral sense: the child-mind's kinghood test: having formed my notion of kinghood chiefly from the FitzJames of the Lady of the Lake — kings do, dare, forgive, and tramp the hills. When real castles prove always empty, wonder curdles: where are the working kings? The whole of Praeterita's Tory lament — Tydides and Idomeneus labouring, Scott's kings labouring, modern landlords absent — starts in this disappointment. FitzJames is thus a lost standard.
  • Modern surname and memorial sense: e.g. Captain Francis McClintock's lieutenant James Fitzjames (Franklin expedition, 1845-48), Fitzjames Avenue, FitzJames Stuart. Arctic, academic, and street names keep the patronymic alive without royalty. Spelling varies (FitzJames, Fitzjames, Fitz-James); pronunciation is always /fɪtsˈdʒeɪmz/ with stress on James; the capital J mid-word marks the compound.

Register is romantic-literary and historical. One writes FitzJames when Scott, Stewart kings, or Jacobite dukes are in play; otherwise the word sleeps in gazetteers. Ruskin wakes it to judge the present: a king should be a FitzJames — booted, bow in hand, merciful after combat — and an empty castle is a broken promise.
