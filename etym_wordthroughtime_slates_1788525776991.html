- [14th c.] - Middle English borrows *sclat / slat* from Old French *esclate*, designating thin flat roofing stones split from metamorphic rock quarries - [perception: durable, high-status roofing material for castles, abbeys, and civic halls]

- [15th–17th c.] - Welsh and English slate quarries expand; the spelling stabilizes as *slate / slates*, increasingly used for damp-proof courses and domestic building - [perception: standard, highly respected stonecraft and permanent weatherproofing]

- [18th–19th c.] - Mass-produced framed writing *slates* become the universal tool of primary education worldwide ('clean slate'); the political meaning of a candidate list emerges in American democracy, while British slang develops the verb 'to slate' (harshly criticize) - [perception: ubiquitous schoolroom apparatus, democratic voting tool, and lively theatrical review idiom]

- [20th c.] - Industrial quarrying peaks, providing tournament billiard beds and architectural restoration tiles; *clean slate* becomes an indelible philosophical and psychological idiom - [perception: classic architectural stone, symbol of fresh beginnings, and standard political list]

- [21st c.] - Retains premium status in high-end architectural roofing and heritage restoration, alongside pervasive digital metaphors in film production clapperboards and political candidate rosters - [perception: elite sustainable roofing material and timeless cultural idiom]
