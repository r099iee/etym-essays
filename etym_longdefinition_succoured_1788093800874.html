Succoured is the past tense and past participle of the verb succour, "to give aid or relief to someone who is in distress, danger, or need". It is the standard British and Commonwealth spelling; American English writes the same word succored, dropping the French-derived -u-. The word's pedigree is vivid: Latin succurrere literally means "to run up to" — sub- "up to, under" + currere "to run" — so that when you succour someone you are, etymologically, running to their side at the moment of crisis. In the source passage — "he has succoured the people of the Lake in their need, though they had no claim but friendship on him" — Bard defends the Elvenking against Thorin's accusations by citing precisely this unselfish aid: help extended to a people who had no legal claim to it, only friendship to plead.

Primary sense — relief in need.

  • To come to the assistance of a person or people in serious difficulty: the refugees were succoured with food and shelter; the wounded were succoured on the field. The related noun succour ("aid, help, relief") names the help itself, while the verb emphasises the act of bringing it.
  • Urgency and extremity: the word is characteristically used when the need is pressing — famine, siege, sickness, shipwreck — and when the help is decisive rather than incidental. One is scarcely "succoured" by a casual courtesy; succour is the help that arrives when help is truly needed.

Secondary senses and domains.

  • Military usage: to succour a besieged town or garrison is to bring it relief from outside — to break the blockade or to supply what the defenders lack. This is one of the oldest English applications, inherited from the chronicle vocabulary of the Middle Ages, and it is the exact register Bard invokes when he describes the Elvenking's relief of Lake-town's people.
  • Religious and theological usage: "divine succour" — aid sent by God — pervades prayers, sermons and the Authorized Version ("in the day of salvation have I succoured thee", 2 Corinthians 6:2). In this register succour shades toward grace, protection and providence; the verb describes God, saints or angels answering human need.
  • Humanitarian usage: from the eighteenth century onward, succouring the poor, the sick and the distressed becomes a stock phrase of charity discourse, journalism and the novel; the word acquires a mild philanthropic glow while remaining entirely concrete — food, blankets, medicine, safe passage.

Register, form and boundaries. In contemporary English succour and succoured are decidedly literary and slightly archaic: they belong to formal narration, historical fiction and ceremonial prose rather than to everyday speech. The pragmatic bridge from the physical sense (running to help) to the abstract (relief of any need) is one of the simplest in the lexicon, and it explains why the word compresses "aid, urgency and personal commitment" into its single stem. It differs from help in gravity, from rescue in completeness (rescue removes one from peril; succour relieves one within it), and from support in temporality (support is ongoing; succour is the decisive intervention). The British spelling preserves the historical -our on the model of favour and honour, and the double -cc- reflects Latin succurrere.
