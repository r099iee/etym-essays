- Early 17th Century - Borrowed into English Renaissance prose directly from Latin; used in natural philosophy and botany to describe the budding of plants, germination of seeds, and natural generation of animal offspring.
- Mid-to-Late 17th Century - Adopted into theological, political, and moral discourses (e.g., Jeremy Taylor, John Milton); applied metaphorically to the dangerous "pullulation" of heresies, sins, and unruly passions sprouting in the human heart or body politic.
- 18th to 19th Century - Elevated into high literary, historical, and sociological prose (Thomas Carlyle, John Ruskin, Victorian novelists); vividly depicts urban squalor, teeming crowds of immigrants, and buzzing industrial slums pullulating with life.
- Early 20th Century - Embraced by modernist critics, philologists, and bibliographers; applied to literary texts and editorial history (as in the Proustian context) where uncorrected drafts or corrupted editions pullulate with typographical blunders and chaotic revisions.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Retains its status as a sophisticated, high-register literary verb denoting fertile overproduction, viral spread of digital ideas, or unruly biological and textual proliferation.
