PIE *bʰleh₁- "to howl, weep, lament, cry out" (reconstructed; debated — de Vaan treats the prehistory of Latin flēre as uncertain) → Latin flēre, flēō "to weep, to cry, to lament, to shed tears" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, Plautus) → Latin flēbilis "tearful, weeping; lamentable, pitiable — that which draws tears" (attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Late Latin / early Romance shift: flēbilis "weak, infirm, wanting strength" (semantic weakening; the intermediate stage is inferred from the unanimity of the Romance reflexes and is not directly attested) → Old French feble / fleible "weak, frail, without force" (10th–12th c.; Latin fl- > Old French f-) → Anglo-Norman / Middle English feble "weak" (12th c.) — the source of English feeble (adjective) → Middle English feblely, English FEEBLY (adverb "in a feeble manner", 14th c.; feble + the native adverbial suffix -ly) Note (debated): The reconstruction *bʰleh₁- for the ancestor of flēre is standard but not certain; some scholars connect flēre with Latin flāre "to blow" (weeping conceived as "blowing out cries"), but most keep the two apart. The intermediate Late Latin sense "weak, infirm" is inferred rather than directly attested.
