- c. 9th–14th Century: Old and Middle English distinguished the strong verb acan (hard /k/) from the front-mutated noun æċe (soft /tʃ/), denoting physical pain, dental torment, and bodily illness. - Feared as an inevitable corporeal affliction of mortal life.
- 1500s–1600s: The noun was pronounced 'aitch' (generating famous Shakespearean puns on the letter H) while the verb was pronounced with /k/. - Widely used across Elizabethan drama to signify physical torment, bodily labor, and bitter venom.
- 1700s–1800s: Dr. Samuel Johnson imposed the classical pseudo-Greek spelling ache/aching, while the language unified the pronunciation around the verb's hard /k/. The Romantic poets embraced aching as a supreme emblem of sublime emotional longing and existential voids. - Reified as a sensitive badge of artistic melancholy and refined emotional depth.
- Early 20th Century: Satirized in British interwar humor (P.G. Wodehouse) as a melodramatic trope for lovesick youths nursing minor romantic tiffs in aristocratic estates. - Perceived with affectionate amusement as a comic cliché of self-dramatizing heartache.
- Late 20th–21st Century: Maintained clinical ubiquity in medicine to classify non-acute dull pain (myalgia, arthralgia) alongside its enduring emotional sense in literature and songwriting. - Perceived as a fundamental descriptor of both physical fatigue and heartfelt human longing.
