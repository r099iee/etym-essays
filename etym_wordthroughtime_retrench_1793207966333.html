- 16th c. — the knife and the ditch: to retrench is to cut off flesh or phrases, and to ditch a camp against the enemy; one verb for surgery, rhetoric, and war.
- Early 17th c. — the household adopts it: great families retrench superfluous expenses; cutting costs becomes a recognised virtue of stewardship.
- Late 17th–18th c. — the state learns it: after each war ministers retrench the establishments; the word enters budgets, debates, and the political economists.
- Early 19th c. — Cobbett's moral badge: to retrench in all sorts of ways is the honest gentleman's answer to taxation; plain living rebukes the stock-jobber's four-horse show.
- Victorian age — the empire of retrenchment: retrenchment joins peace and reform in the great Liberal slogan; Gladstone makes cutting expenditure a creed.
- Today — the formal announcement: ministers still retrench while everyone else cuts back; the verb survives in boardrooms and history books, its noun retrenchment commoner than itself.
