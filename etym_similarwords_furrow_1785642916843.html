• Wrinkle: Similar because both denote a crease of the skin; differs because a wrinkle is a general, often passive, fine and irregular crease of age or sun, whereas a furrow is a deeper, straighter, more deliberate line — cut, as it were, by emotion across the brow.
  • Crease: Similar because both denote a line in a surface; differs because a crease is a sharp fold made by pressure or folding (trousers, paper), while a furrow is a cut channel, usually in skin or soil, carrying the agricultural image.
  • Groove: Similar because both denote a long narrow channel; differs because groove is the neutral mechanical term for any long narrow cut in any material, lacking furrow's plough-origin and its facial use.
  • Trench: Similar because both denote a long narrow excavation; differs because a trench is deep and wide, dug for defence or drainage, while a furrow is a shallow cut made by a blade for planting or seeding.
  • Channel: Similar because both denote a long hollow passage; differs because a channel is typically a natural or constructed waterway, while a furrow is a dry cut in earth or skin.
  • Line: Similar because both denote a long thin mark; differs because line is completely general (ink, thought, telephony), while furrow is specific to cut channels and facial creases.
  • Ridge: Similar because it is the furrow's constant field-companion; differs because the ridge is the raised strip between furrows — the two are complementary opposites, not synonyms.
  • Frown: Similar because both express displeasure or thought on the face; differs because a frown is the whole expression, usually a drawing together of the brows, while a furrow is the specific crease-line it leaves.
  • Plough: Similar because both belong to the same agricultural act; differs because plough is the verb of the whole operation of turning soil, while furrow names the specific resulting line — and the face.
