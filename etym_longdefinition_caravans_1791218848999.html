caravans is the plural of caravan: in its oldest and most evocative sense, a company of travellers — merchants, pilgrims, migrants — journeying together over long distances through desert or dangerous country, typically with a train of pack animals such as camels, mules or horses moving in single file. In the source sentence the caravan crosses mountains and deserts guided by stars, the classic Silk Road and Saharan image of mutual protection and shared navigation.

From this human group the meaning extended to the means of movement and dwelling. Key modern senses form a coherent family:

  • Desert / trade caravan: The core historical sense. A large organized party with leader, guides, guards, animals and goods, travelling fixed routes between oases, bazaars and ports. Examples: trans-Saharan salt and gold caravans, Arabian incense caravans, Central Asian Silk Road caravans, American wagon-trains. Connotes endurance, exotic commerce, and safety in numbers.
  • File or procession (generalized): Any group of vehicles, animals or people moving together in a line: a caravan of trucks, jeeps, camels, or even camels of tourists. This preserves the file-formation idea without the desert context. Often used by NGOs and armies for aid convoys.
  • Covered vehicle / van: From the 17th century, a large covered cart for passengers, goods, freak-shows or menageries. This yields modern van by clipping. In 19th-century circus and Romany use, a brightly painted horse-drawn living wagon.
  • British trailer caravan / static caravan: The dominant UK sense today: a towed leisure trailer or mobile home parked on a caravan site for holidays. American equivalent is travel trailer. Connotes budget seaside holidays, retirement living, and Traveller culture. The verb to caravan means to holiday with such a trailer.

Further nuances include the caravanserai / caravansary (inn for caravans), caravan park, and metaphorical uses: a caravan of migrants in journalism, often politically charged, or a caravan of floats in a parade. Adjectival use appears in caravan route, caravan trade, caravan leader.

Polysemy is bridged by metonymy: people → line formation → vehicles → houses on wheels. The desert-traveller sense explains the vehicle sense (the vehicles that carry the company) and the British trailer sense (a house that travels with you). The protective, communal core — travelling together for safety and economy — persists even when the referent is a white box towed behind a car in Cornwall. Capitalized Caravan can also name the U.S. Army Lance missile system carrier and pop-cultural works, but these are secondary proper uses.

In register, caravan is neutral to romantic for desert travel, technical for logistics, and everyday for British holiday accommodation. It contrasts with convoy (military/nautical escort) and procession (ceremonial) by its implication of long, arduous, self-sufficient overland travel.
