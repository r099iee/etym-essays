- 9th-11th c. - Attested in Old English as ātorcoppe, serving as the standard native Anglo-Saxon name for a spider across medical leeches and glosses.
- 12th-14th c. - Evolved in Middle English into attercop / attercoppe, used in natural history texts and early theological homilies.
- 15th-16th c. - Standard southern English increasingly favored spider; attercop retreated into northern dialects and developed a sharp figurative insult sense for venomous, spiteful people.
- 17th-19th c. - Flourished in northern English and Scots regional speech (Yorkshire, Lowlands) as a dialectal term for spiders, webs, and ill-tempered shrews.
- 20th-21st c. - Immortalized globally in classic fantasy literature through J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, becoming one of the most famous philological insults in modern English fiction.
