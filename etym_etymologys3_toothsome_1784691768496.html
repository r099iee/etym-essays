The story of toothsome is really two stories braided together: the six-thousand-year career of the Indo-European word for "tooth", and the younger history of the English suffix -some that turns it into an adjective. Neither part is exotic; both are textbook Germanic. But put together, around 1565, they made one of the most mouth-fixated adjectives in the language.

The tooth. The tooth-word is among the oldest and most securely reconstructed words in Proto-Indo-European. The ancestral form *h₁dónt-, "tooth; that which bites or eats", is attested, by regular descent, in nearly every branch of the family. Latin dēns, dēntis gives Italian dente, French dent, Spanish diente; Greek odṓn (odṓnt-) gives the technical English words orthodontist, periodontal, and dentine; Sanskrit dánt survives in modern Hindi dāṁt (दांत), "tooth"; and Lithuanian dantis carries the old word on in Baltic. The very shape of the word, with its famous dental consonants at both ends, looks like the mouth it names; and some scholars have suggested that *h₁dónt- was itself built from the verb root *h₁ed- "to eat" — the tooth as "the eater" — an etymology that would make the word's whole history a small pun on itself. That derivation is attractive but not universally accepted; it is recorded in the handbooks as a plausible bridge rather than a certainty.

Germanic and Old English sound changes. In the Germanic branch the dental root underwent two characteristic sound shifts. The first was Grimm's Law, which moved the PIE stops: PIE *d became Germanic *t, and the cluster reshaped so that *h₁dónt- emerged as *tanþs — the shape behind every Germanic tooth-word. The second was Verner's Law, which voiced the *þ in certain positions and produced the alternating stem *tunþ- that lies behind Gothic tunþus and behind the English plural teeth, whose vowel is the result of i-umlaut: an early Germanic plural ending in -i fronted the back vowel ō to ē in the plural, so that the modern pair tooth / teeth is a living fossil of an ancient sound rule. In Old English the singular appears as tōþ, with the vowel raised from Germanic *a to ō before a nasal-plus-consonant cluster — the same rule that turned *gans into gōs (goose) and *manþ into mōnaþ (month). The modern spelling tooth, with the long vowel written -oo-, settled in Middle English, and the old dental letter thorn (þ) that wrote the final sound was gradually replaced by th as printing spread in the fifteenth century.

The suffix -some. The second ingredient is older in outline but younger in use. PIE *sem- meant "one, together, whole" — it is the root of English same and of Latin similis "like" and semel "once". Germanic built from it the adjective *samaz "same" and, by a familiar grammaticalization, the suffix *-samaz, which turned nouns and adjectives into adjectives meaning "sharing in the quality of, characterized by, tending to". Old English used the suffix freely: wynsum "joyful" (from wynn "joy") survives as winsome; hālsum "healthful" became wholesome; and the machinery stayed productive, so that handsome ("easy to handle", 15th c.), wearisome (15th c.), and quarrelsome (16th c.) could all be struck fresh. In the sixteenth century, when the pattern was still fully alive, English speakers attached it to tooth and produced toothsome, "pleasant to the tooth", first recorded about 1565.

  • Sound shifts. Grimm's Law (PIE *d → Germanic *t) and Verner's Law (Germanic *þ → *ð in certain positions) produced the alternating tanþ- / tunþ- stems; Anglo-Frisian raising gave Old English tōþ; i-umlaut gave the plural teeth; the replacement of þ by th shaped the modern spelling tooth. The suffix -some is phonetically conservative: Old English -sum → Middle English -som → Modern English -some, with only the vowel unrounding in late Middle English.
  • Semantic drift. The leap from "of the tooth" to "delicious" is metonymy: the tooth stands for eating and tasting, so what is pleasant to the tooth is pleasant to eat. In the 16th–17th centuries toothsome meant exactly that; in 17th–18th-century cookery writing it acquired the hearty, substantial warmth it still has; by the 19th century it was extended jocularly to people ("a toothsome lass") and to anything tempting; and in the 20th–21st centuries it became consciously literary and nostalgic.
  • Register and literary attestations. Toothsome appears in the early cookery and travel prose of the 1600s, in the hearty comic writing of the 18th and 19th centuries, and in modern fantasy narration most famously in Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937): "There was a fine toothsome smell." Tolkien's use of the archaic, homely adjective is deliberate register-play — the phrase is as cosy as a Shire pantry, and the joke is that it describes a troll camp.
  • Socio-cultural factors. The word declined in ordinary speech as modern food vocabulary multiplied (tasty, yummy, delicious, mouth-watering), but it never died: it is kept alive by historical fiction, food writing with a period flavour, and fantasy — the genres in which an old word for a rich mouthful still feels right.
  • Family and relatives. Toothsome sits in the great English tooth-family: tooth, toothache, toothpick, toothbrush, toothpaste, sweet tooth, toothy, toothed, and the archaic toothsome itself — and, far off, its Latin cousin dental, dentist, dentifrice, and orthodontist, all from the same PIE *h₁dónt-. The Germanic and Romance branches of one ancient root continue to furnish English with two whole vocabularies of the mouth.
