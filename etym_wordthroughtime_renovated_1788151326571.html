- [15th–16th c.] - Enters English from learned Latin as “renovate,” used of restoring buildings, health, and the mind; register formal, devotional, theological.
- [17th c.] - Physiological and devotional prose (“renovation of the blood,” “renovated heart”) make the word a technical term for renewal of body and soul.
- [18th–19th c.] - Romanticism gives “renovated spirit” its great literary moment (Wordsworth’s Prelude); the word carries weight of inner rebirth and recovered vocation.
- [20th c.] - The building sense explodes: renovation becomes an industry, a television genre, and a real-estate selling point; “renovated” enters everyday speech as praise for kitchens and homes.
- [21st c.] - Technical extension into software and systems modernisation and professional historic-preservation jargon; the word now sells kitchens as readily as it once described conversions of the heart.
