Jarred is the past tense of the verb jar. It has two closely related families of meaning, both rooted in the idea of a harsh, sudden disturbance. In its physical sense, to be jarred is to receive a sharp, unpleasant jolt or vibration through the body — the jarring of an arm when an axe or pick-handle strikes unyielding rock, exactly the sensation in Tolkien’s sentence. In its sensory and figurative senses, a jarring sound or sight is one that clashes harshly, grates on the ear, or rudely interrupts a mood — the word moves naturally from physical shock to emotional and aesthetic discord.

Primary senses

  • To jolt or shake with a shock (transitive): a hard impact transmitted through a limb or object — “the explosion jarred the windows”; “the fall jarred his spine”.
  • To receive such a shock (intransitive): “her arm jarred against the door-frame”.
  • To make a harsh, grating, discordant sound: “the keys jarred against the metal desk”.
  • To clash or be in conflict (figurative): “the modern building jarred with the old houses”; “her cheerful tone jarred on his nerves”.

Secondary and extended senses

  • To shock or unsettle emotionally: “the news jarred her out of her complacency” — a sudden, rough disruption of composure, less violent than “traumatised” but sharper than “surprised”.
  • Aesthetic discord: colours, designs, or styles that “jar” are those that clash unpleasantly; a “jarring note” in music, writing, or behaviour is one that breaks the harmony of the whole.
  • Older, rarer uses: in past centuries “to jar” could mean to quarrel or bicker (“jarring neighbours”); this sense survives mostly in “jarring” meaning discordant or incompatible.

Semantic bridge

  • The single underlying image is harsh contact: two things coming together with a rough, unpleasant, unmusical impact. A hard blow to a limb, a grating noise, a clash of opinions, and an offensive juxtaposition of colours are all cases of the same “jar”.
  • The word travels from the body outward: first you feel the jolt in your bones; then you hear the grating sound; then you recoil from the clash in taste, sight, or feeling. Every later sense is the physical jolt remembered and reapplied.
  • In Tolkien’s sentence — “the handles splintered and jarred their arms cruelly” — the word is at its most physical and violent: the shock of iron against stone runs up the wooden handles and into the arms of the workers, a hurt delivered by a surface that refuses to yield.

Register and usage

  • Standard vocabulary, common in both speech and writing; the physical sense is everyday, and the figurative sense is a favourite of criticism, design, and description.
  • Common collocations: “jarred his/her/their teeth”, “jarred the nerves”, “a jarring note”, “jarring colours”, “jarred awake”, “jarred from his reverie”.
  • As a preterite, “jarred” may be transitive (“jarred their arms”), intransitive (“the crate jarred on the cobbles”), or followed by “with”, “against”, or “on” for the clashing sense.
  • A false friend to avoid: the noun jar (“a glass container”, from French jarre and ultimately Arabic jarrah) is an entirely different word with a separate history; it has nothing to do with the verb jarred.
