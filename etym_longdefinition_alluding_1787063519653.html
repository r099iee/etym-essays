ALLUDING is the present participle of the verb allude, meaning "to refer to something indirectly or covertly, without naming it plainly". In the source sentence, the Lake-men are alluding to the dragon with a curse and a nod — that is, they are referring to him obliquely, half-said, with superstition and fear rather than in plain words.

  • Primary sense: to mention something indirectly; to hint at or refer to a person, event, or idea by implication rather than explicit statement. Always constructed with to: "allude to".
  • Rhetorical sense: in literary criticism, to make an allusion — a deliberate reference, often to another text, myth, or historical event, that the reader is expected to recognise. "The poem is alluding to Milton."
  • Conversational sense: to touch on a subject without going into detail, or to make a guarded reference that only insiders will fully understand.

Grammar and usage: alluding is the -ing participle, used in progressive constructions ("was alluding to"), as a verbal noun or gerund ("his alluding to the incident angered them"), and as a present participle in participial phrases ("alluding to his failure, she changed the subject"). It governs the preposition to invariably; "allude of" and "allude that" are ungrammatical.

Semantic bridge: the word descends from Latin allūdere, "to play with, jest with, refer to playfully", formed from ad- "toward" and lūdere "to play". The semantic development runs: "to play toward a target" → "to make playful, glancing reference" → "to refer to indirectly". The modern sense keeps the old lightness: an allusion is a glancing touch, a game of recognition, not a blunt statement. That historical playfulness is why alluding is milder than stating, declaring, or naming.

Secondary nuances today:

  • Literary theory: alluding is the act of creating intertextuality — a text gains depth by pointing to earlier works.
  • Political and journalistic speech: "alluding to" is used to report veiled references without confirming them, e.g. "the minister alluded to possible tax changes".
  • Evasive vs delicate: alluding can be either tactful (softening bad news) or evasive (avoiding accountability), depending on context.
  • Distinction from eluding and deluding: the word is often confused with eluding (escaping) and deluding (deceiving) because of sound similarity; the spelling all- marks "toward (a reference)".

Collocations: "alluding to his past", "the film keeps alluding to earlier classics", "without alluding to any names", "alluding darkly to a secret". The word sits in the same family as allusion (noun) and allusive (adjective).

Register: alluding is standard, slightly formal, and very common in essays, journalism, and literary analysis; in casual speech people more often say "hinting at" or "referring to".

Contrasts: stating, mentioning (plain and direct), quoting (verbatim), citing (formal, verifiable), implying (wordless suggestion from context), insinuating (negative, sly).
