Perchance is a small fossil of the Norman Conquest that learned to dream. It began as two French-flavoured words meaning by the fall of the dice and ended as English poetry's favourite way of saying who knows. Its whole evolution is the story of accident becoming attitude.

The Latin fall behind fortune. The deep root is the Latin verb cadere, to fall — whence also cadence, decadence, accident, and case in all its senses. Roman speakers already used falling for happening: things befall us in English for the same buried metaphor, events are falls of fate. Vulgar Latin nominalized the fall as cadentia, literally a falling, hence that which falls out or turns up — the ancestor not only of chance but of the musical cadence, the fall of the voice at a phrase's end. Old French palatalized the hard c before a into ch, giving cheance: luck, fortune, one's lot, the throw of the dice. Note the consonant music across the channel: where Latin had cadentia, French sang cheance and English, borrowing after 1066, wrote chance — the same softening that turned Latin camera into French chambre.

  • Dice and destiny: medieval chance kept a gambling flavour — the chance of the dice, the main chance — which is why hedging with perchance always faintly suggests that life is a game whose throw is hidden.
  • Cousins in falling: accident (a befalling), incident (a falling-upon), decadence (a falling-away), and cadence (a falling-close) all share the cadere root, making perchance kin to half the vocabulary of fate.

The preposition that steered it. Latin per — through, by, by means of — is one of the oldest and toughest of prepositions, surviving intact into French par and English per (as in per annum, per capita). In Old French par cheance or per chance meant straightforwardly by luck or as fortune wills, and Middle English borrowed the whole phrase ready-made in the 1300s, initially as two words. Chaucer's generation wrote per chance where they meant if it so happens: a condition laid on fortune, not yet a confession of ignorance.

  • Fusion and drift: across the 1400s the two words fused in spelling and the meaning slid from if fortune brings it to maybe — the classic path from describing the world to describing the speaker's mind, which linguists call subjectification.
  • The peradventure parallel: French also gave per aventure, by adventure, which English fused as peradventure; the two adverbs have walked side by side for six centuries, peradventure always a half-step grander.

Literary crowning and gentle retirement. The 1500s and 1600s made perchance immortal. Shakespeare uses it some dozens of times, culminating in Hamlet's to sleep, perchance to dream — the very hinge of the most famous meditation on uncertainty in English. The King James Bible (1611) deploys peradventure and perhaps more often, but perchance colours the era's sermons and sonnets alike. Then, across the 1700s, the plainer perhaps — itself once a similar per + haps (chances, plural of hap) formation — won the contest for neutral prose, helped by sounding slightly less French in an age of plain-style ideals. Perchance withdrew with honour to verse, liturgy, and deliberate period voice. That is why it feels so at home in the Russian wonder tale of the source sentence: translators of Afanasyev and his kin reach for it to keep the English as timeless as the Russian, letting fugitives from Baba Yaga or the Firebird hope in the diction of old story. What began as a Norman gambler's shrug now opens story-time itself — three soft syllables in which fortune, poetry, and doubt are still falling together.
