candelabra (noun, singular — originally the Latin neuter plural of candelabrum): a branched support, standing on a base or suspended from above, designed to hold several candles or lamps at once. In Titus Groan, Peake deploys the word in the plural of abundance: seven great candelabra hung at nine-foot intervals turn the Hall of the Bright Carvings into a cathedral of artificial light, “illuminated night and day.” Every detail is significant. Seven is the number of the classical planets and of ritual perfection; suspension from the ceiling makes each candelabrum a hanging constellation rather than a piece of standing furniture; and the fixed nine-foot spacing converts illumination itself into architecture — the light is laid out in a measured grid, exactly like the carvings it reveals. The word's faintly Latinate, ceremonial ring — candelabra rather than “candle-stands” — matches the Byzantine, decrepit grandeur of Gormenghast: these are church-like fixtures, and Rottcodd serves them with the devotion of a sacristan, replenishing them before retiring at nine o'clock. The tone is one of sacred maintenance: constant flame against the perpetual dimness of a windowless loft.

The word's grammatical history is as interesting as its object, and a complete definition must set out its unusual senses and forms:
  • candelabrum (the strict Latin singular): the term of art for one branched candle-stand — a pedestal, table stand, or hanging frame with several arms for candles or lamps.
  • candelabra (classical plural): the Latin neuter plural of candelabrum, “the branched stands”, used in English from the 16th century to name multiple fixtures — this is the form Peake uses for his seven hanging lights.
  • candelabra (modern English singular): from the 19th century, speakers reanalysed the -a ending as a feminine singular (as in umbrella, arena), so that candelabra now names a single branched stand; this is now the standard colloquial singular.
  • candelabras (modern English plural): the back-formed plural of the reanalysed singular, now common in ordinary usage alongside the classical candelabra.
  • Extended and figurative senses: in anatomy, a “candelabra” or candelabra-like arrangement names a branching system of blood vessels or nerves (e.g. the candelabra cells of the retina); in electrical engineering a “candelabra base” or “candelabra screw” is the small fitting used for chandelier bulbs — the word survives inside the industry that replaced the flame it once served.

The semantic and historical bridge is visible in every sense. Latin formed candelabrum from candēla, “a candle”, with the instrumental suffix -brum, so the word literally means “instrument for candles”: at first a simple stick, then, under Hellenistic and Roman luxury, a branched pedestal or hanging frame for many flames at once — the more flames, the more light, the more power. English borrowed the plural in the sixteenth century as a learned, ceremonial word of classical antiquity, and modern usage now holds three forms in uneasy coexistence: the classical singular candelabrum, the vernacular singular candelabra, and its new plural candelabras. Peake knowingly exploits this instability when he writes of “seven great candelabra suspended from the ceiling”: the form is classically plural, ceremonially Latinate, and yet reads naturally as a modern singular collective — the seven fixtures become almost one great hanging constellation of light.
