- 14th c. – Borrowed into Middle English (as suffisen, soffisen) from Old French suffire (stem suffiss-) and Latin sufficere; used both transitively ("to satisfy, supply fully") and intransitively ("to be adequate, be enough") in Chaucer and Wycliffite texts.
- 15th–16th c. – Established in Early Modern English drama, biblical translation, and philosophical rhetoric (frequent in Shakespeare and the King James Bible); developed the characteristic imperative and subjunctive formulas (e.g., "let it suffice", "sufficeth it").
- 17th–18th c. – Solidified in Enlightenment philosophy, legal jurisprudence, and mathematical deduction to define the boundaries of necessary and sufficient conditions; established the modern rhetorical transition formula "suffice it to say".
- 19th c. – Retained in high Victorian prose, parliamentary oratory, and academic criticism as an elevated verb of adequacy, frequently utilized to delineate what an argument, resource, or theory can and cannot accomplish.
- 20th–21st c. – Preserved in formal, scholarly, and analytical English (e.g., Rudolf Arnheim's diagnostic critique that exposure to masterworks does not suffice); occasionally appears in digital databases in corrupted OCR forms like suflice due to ligature misrecognition.
