VEXED is the past participle of the verb vex used as an adjective, meaning annoyed, irritated, or mildly frustrated — a state of mind ruffled by annoyance rather than consumed by anger. In the passage from The Hobbit, after Elrond has explained the moon-letters and declared that they can only be read by "a moon of the same shape and season" as the day they were written, Gandalf and Thorin ask "What do they say?" together, "a bit vexed perhaps that even Elrond should have found this out first." The qualifiers are carefully chosen: "a bit" and "perhaps" soften the word, so the tone is comic and rueful rather than hostile — a small puncture of dwarven pride at having the secret of their own people's rune-lore unravelled by an elf before they could do so themselves. Vexed here is the light, almost affectionate register of mild irritation, not the strong, archaic sense of torment or affliction. Core senses (exhaustive)
  • Annoyed, irritated, mildly angered: the everyday modern sense — a person ruffled by a small frustration, as in "he was vexed by the traffic."
  • Frustrated, thwarted: vexation caused by obstacles, delays, or unreachable goals, closer to the emotional sense in the Tolkien passage.
  • Tormented, harassed, afflicted (archaic and strong): the older force of the Latin ancestor vexare, "to shake, jolt, harass"; in older English vexed could mean severely distressed or persecuted, as in "vexed with pains."
  • Disturbed, troubled, agitated (of water or sea): "the vexed waters" — a sea churned by wind, a poetic survival of the root sense of tossing and shaking about.
  • Disputed, debated, much contested (of questions): the collocation "a vexed question" or "vexed matter" means a point of controversy that has been repeatedly troubled by argument; this sense runs from the 17th century into modern journalism and scholarship.
  • Afflicted by something persistent (with): "vexed with doubt," "vexed by the memory" — a lingering, recurring trouble that will not go away.
Historical and semantic bridges The thread running through every sense is the Latin verb vexare, a frequentative of vehere "to carry, convey," which originally meant to shake or jolt something about — literally to toss a load back and forth in transit. From this physical jolting the word extended to mental shaking: to vex a person became to buffet them with trouble, to worry and harass them. English inherited the full spectrum in the 15th century, and for three centuries vexed could mean anything from "persecuted" to "perturbed." The modern weakening to "mildly annoyed" is a late drift: as the strong senses of torment retreated, the word settled into polite understatement, which is exactly why it is available for comic use in modern prose — a "bit vexed" is almost cosy. The intellectual sense of "a vexed question" preserves a different metaphor: a question that has been shaken and worried so often that it refuses to settle. Register, grammar, and idioms Vexed is chiefly predicative or attributive (a vexed expression; he looked vexed), and it takes about with the cause (vexed about the delay) or by and with with the agent (vexed with the interruption). It belongs to a literary and slightly old-fashioned register; in speech it often carries a half-smile. Related formations are vex (verb), vexation (the state, or an annoying cause), vexatious (adjective, especially in law, "vexatious litigation" — a suit brought to harass a defendant), and vexedly (rare adverb). The phrase "a vexed question" is the most famous idiom and is used to this day in academic and journalistic prose for an unresolved controversy. Near synonyms in brief Annoyed and irritated are the plain modern equivalents; frustrated stresses the thwarting of a goal; exasperated is stronger, close to the point of losing patience; peeved and nettled are lighter and more colloquial; aggrieved adds a sense of unjust treatment; harried, pestered, and harassed belong to the older, stronger strain of vexare. Vexed keeps a distinctly civilised, faintly bookish tint that the others lack.
