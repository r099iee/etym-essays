Moose is one of the earliest and most transparent Native American loans in English, and unlike most BasicVocab words it has no Indo-European pedigree at all. It enters colonial records in the early seventeenth century as English fur-traders, fishermen, and settlers in New England encounter an animal unknown in Europe: Alces alces, the largest member of the deer family, called in Proto-Algonquian *mo·swa, literally the twig-eater or bark-stripper, from the verb *mo- to strip bark, to eat off, plus an animal suffix. Eastern Abenaki moz, Penobscot muns, Narragansett moos, and Massachusett moos all name the animal for its habit of stripping willow, birch, and maple shoots in winter.

Captain John Smith notes moos in 1616, and Roger Williams in his 1643 Key into the Language of America glosses moos as the great beast. Early spellings vary — moose, moos, mus, muse — before moose standardises by the late 1600s. English takes both the sound and, unknowingly, the description: every time we say moose we are calling it the stripper in Algonquian. The plural remains moose (one moose, two moose), like sheep and deer, by analogy with native animal names, though jocular mooses appears.

Phonetically the borrowing is simple: Algonquian /moːs/ maps neatly onto English /muːs/, spelled oo for the long vowel. No Latin or French intermediary intervenes, so there are no great consonant shifts to track — only the regular English lengthening and the Great Vowel Shift already completed for this vowel class. The stability of the form for four centuries shows how loans for new fauna freeze quickly once printed in travel books and natural histories.

Semantically the word barely drifts, because the referent is zoologically fixed, but cultural connotations accrete:
  • Colonial wonder and sustenance: moose as giant meat, hide, and danger — larger than an elk, bellowing in rut, capable of killing wolves. Early accounts stress size and strangeness.
  • Frontier humour and Canadian emblem: the moose as the solemn, slightly absurd northerner — long legs, drooping muzzle, majestic yet goofy. Tourist kitsch, hockey mascots, and Bullwinkle reinforce comedy.
  • British comic property: for Wodehouse and his London audience who never met a live moose, a stuffed moose is an exotic trophy of empire and travel, funnier than a stag because more unwieldy and foreign. Its quiet stolidity is instantly visual: heavy, glassy-eyed, dignity without thought.
  • Conservation and ecology: modern moose as indicator species for boreal forest health, declining with ticks and warming. Scientific prose keeps Alces alces alongside moose, with cow, bull, and calf for sex and age.
Note the transatlantic split with elk: in Britain elk means Alces alces, while in America elk means wapiti (Cervus canadensis) and moose means Alces alces — a classic colonial confusion of large deer that persists.

Socio-culturally, moose marks North Americanness. To put a stuffed moose in an English country house, as Wodehouse does by simile, is to signal well-travelled wealth and hunting prowess while inviting laughter at solemnity. The animal that strips twigs in Algonquian becomes, in Edwardian simile, the perfect emblem of a man who will not strip off his mask: large, northern, unmoved, faintly ridiculous, and impossible to hurry.
