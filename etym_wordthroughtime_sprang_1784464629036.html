The verb “spring,” with its strong past “sprang,” has meant to leap or burst upward with sudden energy since earliest English — the verb of coiled bodies released into the air.
“Spring” from the same rising image gave the water that “springs” from the ground and the season “spring” itself, the time when plants spring up — though the past form “sprang” stays with the physical leap.
“Spring” by the 16th–17th centuries also meant to surprise or set off suddenly — a trap springs, a plan is sprung on someone — and “to spring to one’s feet” became the idiom for explosive rising.
“Sprang” today is the vivid strong past of that sudden elastic motion — a company of dwarves springing up and knocking over the table in one burst.
