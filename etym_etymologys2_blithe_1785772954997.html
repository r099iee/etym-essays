Proto-Indo-European *bʰleh₁- "to shine, gleam, be bright" (reconstructed root family *bʰel-/*bʰleh₁-, reflected in English bleach, blank, blaze and German bleich; the "shine" reading is the standard one offered for blithe) → Proto-Germanic *blīþiz / *blīþijaz "gentle, mild, kind, glad" (semantic bridge: brightness of appearance → mildness and gladness of disposition; reconstructed stage of the 1st millennium BCE) → Old English blīþe "joyful, glad, cheerful, kind, gentle, friendly" (attested in glosses and poetry from the Anglo-Saxon period, 8th–9th centuries) → Middle English blithe, blythe "glad, merry, kindly disposed" (12th–15th centuries) → Early Modern English blithe "happy, cheerful; (increasingly) heedlessly gay" (16th–18th centuries) → Modern English BLITHE "cheerfully indifferent; lightheartedly unconcerned; (literary) joyous" (19th–21st centuries). Note on the debated stage: The exact derivation of the Proto-Germanic adjective is debated. Many scholars connect *blīþiz to the PIE "shine" root *bʰleh₁-, treating brightness as the source of the "mild, kind" meaning; others are cautious, noting the semantic leap and the possibility of a separate formation. The Germanic family itself is solid (Old Norse blíðr, Old High German blīdi, Old Saxon blīthi, Dutch blij, all "glad, mild, kind"), but the ultimate PIE attachment remains a matter of scholarly judgement rather than proof.
