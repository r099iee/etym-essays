PIE *steh₂- "to stand, be firm, set up, place" (reconstructed root; zero-grade *sth₂-, o-grade *stoh₂-; one of the most productive roots of Indo-European) -> Proto-Germanic *stō- "to stand, to place" (o-grade of the root) -> Proto-Germanic *stōwō- "a place, a placing" (o-grade + suffix *-w-; the "place" word of the western dialects) -> Old English stōw "a place" (attested from the 8th–9th c.; survives fossilised in English place-names such as Felixstowe "Felix's place" and Padstow "St Petroc's place") -> Old English stōwian (verb) "to place, to stow, to lay up" (attested 10th–11th c.) -> Old English be-stōwian (intensive be- + stōwian) "to place, to dispose, to confer" (attested Old English) -> Middle English bistowen / bestowen "to put, place, store; to give, confer" (13th–14th c.) -> English bestow (15th c., the modern form) -> English BESTOWED (past participle) Note on the root: *steh₂- is vast and securely attested across the family — Sanskrit sthā- "to stand" (tiṣṭhati "he stands", sthita "fixed", sthāna "a station"), Greek histēmi "I set up", stasis, stoa, Latin stare "to stand", status, statuere, Slavic Russian stat' "to stand, become". The chain to bestow runs through the Germanic o-grade *stōwō-, "a place," and the semantics are transparent: to bestow is literally to "be-place," to put a thing (or a gift) in its place. The prefix be- is the intensive/transitive-forming Germanic prefix also seen in bequeath, bedeck, befall; the modern sense "to confer as a gift" grew out of the physical sense "to put, place, stow" during the 14th–16th centuries.
