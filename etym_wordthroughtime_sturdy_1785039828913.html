- 13th century - Enters English from Old French 'estourdi' with nearly the opposite of its modern meaning: it describes one who is stunned, dazed, reckless - a headstrong, violent person who acts without thought.
- 14th-17th centuries - The sense pivots completely: from wild and impetuous the word shifts toward physical toughness, and a sturdy fellow becomes one who is bold and firmly built, dependable in a fight or a storm.
- Contemporary sense - Sturdy now means strongly and solidly made, firm and resistant to wear - a sturdy table, a sturdy little pony; the old recklessness is gone, replaced by quiet, compact reliability.
