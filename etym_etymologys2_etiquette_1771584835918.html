ROOT: PIE *steig- "to prick, stick, be pointed" (reconstructed) — a root of sharp, piercing contact: the pointed thing pierces, the stuck thing adheres, and the "prick" of a goad drives action. Every branch below remembers the same double image of piercing and sticking.

CHAIN (the route into English etiquette, via Germanic and Old French):
1. PIE *steig- "to prick, stick, be pointed" (reconstructed; no direct attestation).
2. Proto-Germanic *stikkaną / *stikōną "to stab, stick, prick" (reconstructed; reflected in Old English stician "to pierce, stab, fix" → English stick; Old English stiċċe "a piece, patch, stitch" → English stitch; German stechen "to sting, pierce"; Dutch steken "to stab, stick").
3. Frankish *stikkan "to stick, fasten, pin up" (the Frankish dialect of the Low Franconian/Germanic continuum, c. 5th–9th centuries) — carried into Gaul by the Frankish settlers.
4. Old French estiquer / estichier "to stick, fasten, affix" (c. 12th century), a Romance borrowing of the Frankish word.
5. Old French estiquet (postverbal noun) "a label, a ticket" (c. 13th–14th centuries) — literally "the thing stuck on"; this word was also borrowed directly into English as ticket (c. 1520s) "a small card or note."
6. Old French estiqueter "to label, attach a ticket" → French étiqueter "to label" → French étiquette (noun) "a small label, a placard; a ticket of rules" (17th century).
7. French étiquette — court sense: at the French court the étiquette was the card prescribing where each rank might stand; the word then shifted from "label" to "code of ceremony" to "rules of polite behaviour."
8. English etiquette (borrowed c. 1740) "the customary code of polite behaviour" — "as etiquette prescribed." ◄ OUR WORD.

NOTE (debated points): the exact route of the Old French -quet/-quette diminutive formation is nuanced, and some handbooks present estiquet and étiquette as variants of the same postverbal noun; the chain above follows the standard OED/etymological account. The sister borrowing English ticket (1520s) shares the same French stem and is NOT an ancestor of etiquette but a parallel loan. The PIE root *steig- is also the ancestor, via other branches, of Sanskrit tij- "to be sharp" (téjas "sharpness, brilliance" → Hindi tej तेज; tiktá "sharp-tasting" → Hindi tīkhā तीखा), Greek stízein "to prick, tattoo" → stígma (English stigma), and Latin instīgāre "to goad, incite" (English instigate) and distinguere "to mark off" (English distinguish).
