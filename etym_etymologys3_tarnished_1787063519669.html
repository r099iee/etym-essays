Tarnish arrives in English in the late 16th century, and its early history is a textbook case of a word crossing the Channel from French into English during the great Renaissance wave of borrowing. The first recorded English uses, around 1590, are transitive and technical: to tarnish a metal is to dim its brightness, most often said of silver, gold plate, and armour. The word is a borrowing of Middle French ternir, "to render dull, dim, or gloomy", itself from Old French ternir, attested from the 12th century in exactly the same sense. French ternir was not, however, a native Latin word: it is generally held to come from the Frankish verb *tarnjan, "to hide, conceal", a loan from the Germanic dialects carried into Gaul by the Franks. The semantic logic is clear: tarnishing was conceived as the metal’s bright surface being hidden away beneath a dark film. The Frankish verb belongs to the broader Proto-Germanic family represented by *darnijaną, "to hide", cognate with Old Norse darna and with Old English diernan, "to keep secret, conceal" — Old English dierne, "secret, hidden", is a member of the same root.

The ultimate pre-Germanic origin is debated. Many authorities take the Germanic root back to a PIE base *dʰer- meaning "to hold, support" in one branch and "dark, gloomy, soiled" in another — though the connection between these branches is not universally accepted. An alternative, older view tried to derive ternir from Latin *ternere, "to crush" (compare terere, "to rub, grind"), on the theory that rubbing dulls a surface; but no Latin ancestor is actually attested, and the Frankish hypothesis is today the preferred account. The case illustrates a recurring pattern in English etymology: a "Romance" word that turns out, on inspection, to be Germanic in disguise — loaned from Frankish into French and only centuries later re-exported to England.

Semantic development. From the beginning, tarnishing was associated with the noble metals and their care. In the 17th and 18th centuries, as polite English society grew rich on silver tableware, the verb settled into household discourse: plate "tarnishes" in damp air; housekeepers are told to keep it polished. Because the damage was visible, gradual, and reversible, tarnish never acquired the catastrophic force of rust or corrode. That very mildness is what fitted it for figurative service. The figurative extension — from the metal to the man — is first attested in the early 17th century, barely a generation after the literal sense, and grows steadily through the moralising literature of the 18th and 19th centuries. The theatre and the novel of sensibility loved the image: a "tarnished reputation", a "tarnished name", a "tarnished honour". For Victorian moralists the word carried a quasi-religious weight, shading toward "lost innocence" and "fallen virtue".

Modern career. In the 20th century the word’s centre of gravity moves decisively to the figurative register. Journalism discovers "tarnish" as the perfect, measured verb for scandal: it damages but does not destroy, and it leaves the possibility of polishing again. Politicians, celebrities, and corporations acquire "tarnished images"; sportsmen retire "with a tarnished legacy". Meanwhile the literal sense remains alive but is increasingly the preserve of materials science, numismatics, jewellery care, and electronics, where "tarnish" is a precise term for the surface oxidation or sulphidation of non-ferrous metals — silver sulphide on silver, copper oxides on copper. The two lives of the word exist in easy balance today: a jeweller’s website sells "anti-tarnish" cloths, while a newspaper announces that a minister’s career is "tarnished by scandal".

In Tolkien. The Hobbit’s use of tarnished belongs to a deliberately archaic, Romance-derived register that Tolkien, a philologist, handled with complete precision. The tarnished silver tassel on Thorin’s ragged hood is a miniature emblem of the whole story: royal dignity, dulled and hidden beneath hardship, yet — as with metal that can be polished — still potentially bright. The word’s mildness, its association with lost shine rather than destroyed substance, is exactly what the narrative requires: Thorin is diminished, not annihilated.
