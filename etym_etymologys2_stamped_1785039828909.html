Earliest stage to present STAMPED: - Proto-Indo-European *stebh- (variant *stembh-) "to support, place firmly on, steady" (c. 5th–4th millennium BCE). Cognates: Greek stembein "to trample, tread down"; Sanskrit stabhnāti "he supports", stambha "post, pillar". These are parallel descendants, not ancestors of English. - Proto-Germanic *stamp- / *stemp- "to pound, crush" — a nasalized form of *stebh-. - Old Norse stappa "to pound, stamp"; Middle Dutch stampen "to stamp, pound"; Old High German stampfon "to stamp with the foot, beat" (German stampfen "to stamp, pound"). - Old English stæmpan / stempan "to pound, crush, bray in a mortar" (recorded chiefly in compounds, 8th–11th c.). - Middle English stampen "to pound, crush" (c. 1200); the vowel was reshaped by Scandinavian influence. - English stamp (intransitive) "to strike the foot forcibly downwards" (mid-14th c.); "to tread heavily" (late 14th c.); transitive "to strike by a forcible downward thrust of the foot" (c. 1500). - English stamp (noun) stampe "pounding/crushing tool" (mid-15th c.); "impressed mark" (1540s); "postage stamp" (1840). - Modern English STAMPED (past tense/participle formed with -ed) "brought the feet down heavily and forcibly; also, imprinted, marked, endorsed, or crushed." Note: the direct ancestor chain runs through Germanic only; the Greek and Indo-Iranian forms are cognates of the same PIE root, illustrating the root's original pairing of "support" and "tread down."
