- c.1300 “hedgehog” - Middle English urchon, the ordinary word for the spiny land animal; neutral, countrified.
- 1500s “sea-hedgehog” - sea urchin coined for the spiny echinoderm; still transparent metaphor, zoological-factual.
- Late 1500s “prickly boy” - urchin extended to ragged mischievous children (spiky, hard to handle); faintly contemptuous, then affectionate.
- 1600s–1700s “goblin-hedgehog” - folklore/Shakespearean fringe: urchins as sprites and witches’ beasts; eerie, superstitious colouring.
- 1800s “Dickens’s urchins” - the street sense peaks: Oliver, Jo, Tiny Tim’s neighbours; pity and charm fused, the hedgehog forgotten.
- Present split inheritance - scientists and chefs own “sea urchin” (uni, sushi luxury); novelists and fond parents own “urchin” (cheeky child); the hedgehog sense survives only inside the compound.
