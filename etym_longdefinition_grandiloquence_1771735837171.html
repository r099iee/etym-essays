In this sentence “grandiloquence” names the distinctive public manner of Gibbon’s prose: “a man of Gibbon’s grandiloquence of phrase.” The word captures his famously stately, balanced, Latinate, periodic style — sentences of majestic symmetry, slow cadences, ironic parentheses, and a vocabulary drawn from the full height of the Augustan and Ciceronian tradition. Here the word is used appreciatively: it is the “grandiose” exterior, the formal and oratorical mask, beneath which the author will discover the surprising “intimacy” of the work. The tone therefore admits the majesty while priming the paradox — grandiloquence is the public costume of the Decline and Fall, and the essay’s whole point is that the man behind the costume can still speak to us privately. As a dictionary word, “grandiloquence” is a noun meaning pompous or extravagant language, style, or manner, especially in a way intended to impress; the use of lofty, inflated, or rhetorical speech that aims at grandeur. It is a quality of discourse — a way of speaking or writing — rather than of persons, and it can be used with three shades of feeling: (1) appreciatively, of a genuinely lofty, dignified oratorical style; (2) neutrally, of a grand manner judged merely by its style; and (3) critically, of grand language whose matter may not match its manner, shading into bombast. The related adjective “grandiloquent” means “speaking in a grand style”; the agent noun is rarer (“grandiloquence” is the manner itself). The word is emphatically literary and bookish, at home in essays, reviews, and rhetorical commentary rather than in ordinary conversation. Polysemy — the full range of uses:
  • The dignified sense: a genuinely elevated, balanced, stately manner of speech or writing — Gibbon’s “grandiloquence of phrase,” a Ciceronian period, the pulpit rhetoric of the seventeenth century — where the grandeur is real and the word is honorific.
  • The ironic or critical sense: grand language whose substance does not match its style — inflated, pretentious, theatrical speech — where the word slides toward bombast and pomposity and becomes a reproach.
  • The neutral descriptive sense: any notably lofty, oratorical register, judged for its style rather than its truth or emptiness.
  • Extended uses: “grandiloquence of gesture” or “grandiloquence of manner” — a sweeping, theatrical, self-important way of moving or behaving, transferring the verbal manner to bodily display.
  • Technical and rhetorical jargon: in rhetoric and literary criticism, a label for the grand or high style (the genus grande of classical rhetoric), opposed to the plain and the middle styles.
The boundary words sharpen the profile:
  • “Bombast”: Similar because both name showy grand language; differs because bombast is inflated, empty, showy speech — sound without substance — and is always a reproach, whereas grandiloquence is grand in scale and dignified in manner, risking but not necessarily committing the sin of pretension.
  • “Pomposity”: Similar because both involve self-important display; differs because pomposity points to the manner of a person who overestimates his own gravity — the solemnity of the speaker — while grandiloquence concerns the grand style of the language itself.
  • “Eloquence”: Similar because both name impressive fluent speech; differs because eloquence is unreservedly positive — persuasive and moving, with no hint of excess — whereas grandiloquence carries the risk of overstatement.
  • “Magniloquence”: Similar because both name elevated speech; differs because magniloquence is the nearest twin and nearly interchangeable, though it can carry a slightly more neutral, purely stylistic sense of speaking grandly (magnus “great” + loquī “to speak”).
  • “Loquacity”: Similar because both derive from loquī “to speak” and concern speech; differs because loquacity is sheer talkativeness — quantity of talk, often trivial — with no grandeur at all, while grandiloquence is grand in manner.
  • “Fustian”: Similar because both name inflated, pretentious language; differs because fustian is coarse and laboriously pompous — the word is itself heavy and condemnatory — while grandiloquence can be light, admired, or ironic depending on context.
  • “Rhetoric”: Similar because both concern elevated persuasive speech; differs because rhetoric is the neutral art or study of effective speaking, while grandiloquence is a specific grand manner of speech.
The word is built, transparently, from two Latin ingredients: grandis, “great,” and loquī, “to speak.” A grandiloquent speaker is, etymologically, one who speaks great — that is, speaks in a great manner, on a great scale, with great words. This transparency is part of the word’s character: even when it criticizes, it keeps the stateliness of its parts. In describing Gibbon, the sentence exploits both faces at once — the reverent recognition of a majestic, historic style, and the quiet admission that such a style is a costume, a public performance, beneath which the historian still speaks intimately.
