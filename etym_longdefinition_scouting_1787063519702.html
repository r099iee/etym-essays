Scouting is the gerund and present participle of the verb scout, meaning "to observe, explore, or reconnoiter in order to gather information." As a noun it names the activity itself: the systematic looking-out of a place, an enemy, a route, or an opportunity before committing to action. In the present card, Tolkien uses it in its pure military sense — Thorin sends out "a scouting expedition to spy out the land" — the classic reconnaissance operation in which a small, fast party goes ahead to gather intelligence on terrain, enemies, and gates.

Primary sense: military reconnaissance.

  • Reconnaissance: the organized observation of an area, force, or position to obtain information for planning. A scouting party or scouting expedition moves ahead of the main body, or into unknown territory, to report back.
  • The scout as a person: a soldier, spy, or frontiersman trained to move unseen, observe accurately, and report; in The Hobbit, the dwarves' scouting expedition is of this type.
  • Naval, aerial, and electronic scouting: in modern warfare, patrol aircraft, drones, and reconnaissance vehicles "scout" for targets, weather, and enemy movement; the word now includes the whole doctrine of reconnaissance.

The youth-movement sense.

  • The Scout Movement: from 1908, when Robert Baden-Powell founded the Boy Scouts, "scouting" became the proper name of the worldwide youth movement dedicated to character building, outdoor skills, self-reliance, and service.
  • Scouting skills: map-reading, compass work, tracking, camping, first aid, and woodcraft — the practical competencies the movement teaches.
  • Scouting as a verb for the movement's activities: "he went scouting with his troop," "scouting badges," "Scouting for Boys" (Baden-Powell's 1908 handbook).

Extended modern senses.

  • Talent scouting: in sports and entertainment, the professional search for promising athletes, actors, or musicians. "He does scouting for a Premier League club," "a scouting report" on an opposing team or a prospect.
  • Location scouting: in film and television, the pre-production search for suitable places to shoot scenes; location scouts photograph and log candidates for directors.
  • Market scouting / data scouting: in business and technology, the preliminary investigation of markets, competitors, or datasets to inform strategy.
  • General exploratory sense: "let's do some scouting" = let's look around a new neighborhood, store, or area before deciding. Informal but common.
  • Online scouting / web scouting: browsing or monitoring the internet for information, deals, or trends.

Semantic bridge. All these senses share one core idea: looking ahead, around, or beyond the present position to obtain information that will guide future action. The military sense is oldest; frontier and colonial exploration of the 18th–19th centuries broadened it; the youth movement (1908) gave the word its most famous non-military face, borrowing the adventure and self-reliance of the military scout; and the modern professional senses (talent, location, market) are all "scouting" in the original information-gathering sense, transplanted into civilian fields where finding the right person, place, or opportunity is the goal.

Register and nuance. The military and movement senses are the two main pillars of the word today. In British and Commonwealth English, "scouting" for the youth movement usually refers to the Scouts (boys) while girls are "Guides" or "Girl Guides" (in the US, "Girl Scouts"); the capitalized form Scouting commonly denotes the movement, the lower-case form the activity. In professional contexts (talent, location, market), scouting is a recognized, valued occupation with its own vocabulary of "scouts," "reports," and "shortlists." In The Hobbit the word is firmly military — a scouting expedition "to spy out the land" — and belongs to the ancient heroic pattern of sending out the swift and watchful ahead of the main host.
