- [Pre-Islamic Arabia / 6th c. CE] - coined as a traditional Meccan personal name (derived from *‘uthmān*, the chick of a bustard or strong mender of bones) - [perception: valued as an aristocratic, noble name symbolizing keen desert alertness, agility, and resilient strength]
- [610–644 CE / Early Prophetic & Companionship era] - borne by Uthman ibn Affan, the wealthy merchant convert who earned the title *Dhū al-Nūrayn* by marrying two of Muhammad's daughters - [perception: revered as the epitome of supreme modesty (*ḥayā'*), boundless financial generosity, and pious loyalty]
- [644–656 CE / Caliphate & Canonization era] - reigned as the 3rd Caliph, commissioning the definitive standardized written codex of the Qur'an (*al-Muṣḥaf al-‘Uthmānī*) - [perception: celebrated by the majority as the savior of scriptural unity; contested by political rebels over provincial appointments]
- [Late 7th–10th c. / Classical Islamic Historiography] - sanctified in Sunni orthodoxy as one of the Four Rightly Guided Caliphs whose assassination (*al-Fitnah*) was a catastrophic martyrdom; viewed critically in emerging Shi'a traditions - [perception: established as a foundational pillar of Sunni theological legitimacy and tragic historical memory]
- [13th–17th c. / Ottoman Imperial Rise] - adopted as the personal name of Osman I Ghazi, founder of the Ottoman dynasty, spreading the phonetic variant *Osman* across Europe and the Middle East - [perception: equated by European Christendom with the dreaded military power and imperial majesty of the 'Grand Turk' (the Ottoman Empire)]
- [19th–21st c. / Modern Global Onomastics & Scholarship] - ubiquitous worldwide as a prominent male given name (*Uthman*, *Osman*, *Usman*, *Ousmane*), while academic scholars reexamine his canonization of the Qur'an - [perception: recognized internationally by historians as the pivotal figure responsible for the enduring textual stability of the Qur'an across 1,400 years]
