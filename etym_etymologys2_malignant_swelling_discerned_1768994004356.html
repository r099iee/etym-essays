CHAIN 1 — MALIGNANT: Stage 1 — PIE root *mel- "bad, wrong; to deceive" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the basis of Latin malus "bad"; the reconstruction is standard but the root's wider range is debated.
Stage 2 — Latin malus "bad, evil, wicked" (attested from c. 3rd c. BCE).
Stage 3 — Latin male "badly" (adverb) + the suffix -gnus of gignere "to beget, to give birth" (from the PIE root *gen- "to give birth") → Latin malignus "wicked, ill-natured, evil-disposed" (lit. "ill-born"; attested from 1st c. BCE).
Stage 4 — Middle French malin (masc.), maligne (fem.) "ill-disposed, evil, harmful" (12th–15th c.).
Stage 5 — English malignant "ill-disposed, evil in nature" (16th c.); medical sense "tending to spread and destroy, resistant to treatment" (17th c.). Final node: malignant.
NOTE: The compound male + gignere is historically secure; the only debated point is whether the PIE "bad" sense belongs to *mel- or was an Italic innovation.
CHAIN 2 — SWELLING: Stage 1 — PIE root *swel- "to swell, to rise" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — DISTINCT from the homophone *sāwel-/*swel- "to burn, to shine" (Latin sōl, Sanskrit svàr "sun, light"): the two must not be merged.
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *swellan "to swell, to become larger" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English swellan "to swell, to inflate" (attested from c. 9th c., of rivers, wounds, sails).
Stage 4 — Middle English swellen → swell (12th–15th c.).
Stage 5 — Modern English swell; present participle swelling used as adjective and noun (the participle's adjectival use is attested from Middle English). Final node: swelling.
NOTE: No certain Indic or Romance cognates of the "swell" root are established.
CHAIN 3 — DISCERNED: Stage 1 — PIE root *krei- "to sieve, separate, discriminate" (c. 4000–3000 BCE), allied to the *sker- "cut" family; cf. Sanskrit kṛt- "to cut" (from the allied base).
Stage 2 — Latin cernere "to sift, separate, distinguish, decide" (attested from the 2nd c. BCE; present stem cern- built with an n-infix on *krei-, perfect crēvī, supine crētum).
Stage 3 — Latin discernere "to separate, distinguish, discern" (dis- "apart" + cernere; attested from the 1st c. BCE, Cicero and later).
Stage 4 — Old French discerner "to distinguish, discern" (12th c.).
Stage 5 — Middle English discernen, discern "to distinguish, perceive" (14th c.).
Stage 6 — Modern English discern; past/past participle discerned.
NOTE: The family is rich — the same Latin root cernere produced certain and certify (via the participle crētus, "decided"), secret (secernere, "set aside"), and excrement (excernere, "sift out"); Greek krī́nein "to separate, judge" gave crisis, critic, criterion.
