Single root word: satchel. The word is NOT of Indo-European origin: it is a cultural "wanderwort" transmitted through Semitic → Greek → Latin → Romance → English, so the chain begins with the earliest attested Near Eastern source and states explicitly that no PIE root is reconstructable. Derivation chain: 1. Akkadian šaqqu and Biblical Hebrew śaq (also Aramaic saqqa), "coarse cloth woven from goat hair; a sack or bag made of this cloth; sackcloth worn in mourning or penitence" — attested from the 2nd millennium BCE (Akkadian) and throughout the Hebrew Bible (e.g., Genesis 42:25). NO PIE source: the word family is of Semitic (or broader ancient-Mediterranean) origin and stands outside Indo-European etymology. 2. Ancient Greek σάκκος (sákkos), "bag of coarse cloth, sack, sackcloth; a strainer or sieve" — borrowed from a Semitic source (cf. Hebrew śaq), attested from c. 5th c. BCE (Herodotus uses it of a bag), and used in the Septuagint (3rd–2nd c. BCE) to translate Hebrew śaq. 3. Latin saccus, "sack, bag, purse; a garment of haircloth" — borrowed from Greek sákkos, attested from the 1st c. BCE (Cicero uses saccus of a purse or bag). 4. Latin sacculus, a diminutive of saccus (suffix -ulus), "a little sack; a small bag for money; a small purse or pouch" — attested from the 1st c. BCE onward (Cicero, Martial). 5. Old French sachel (also sakel, from Latin sacculum), "a small bag, a purse, a satchel" — attested from the 12th c. CE. 6. Middle English sachel / sakel, borrowed from Old French (via Anglo-French), "a small bag, esp. for money or books" — attested from c. 1340 CE (Chaucer's works use sacchel-type forms in the 14th c.). 7. Modern English satchel, "a small bag, often with a shoulder strap, for books or personal items" — the modern spelling and the shoulder-strap schoolbag sense consolidate from the 18th–19th c. onward — final node. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Two points deserve explicit flagging. First, the exact Semitic donor form is uncertain — Hebrew śaq, Akkadian šaqqu, and Aramaic saqqa are all candidates for the ultimate source, and some scholars suspect the word was already a "wanderwort" among ancient Near Eastern languages before reaching Greek. Second, whether English satchel descends straight from Old French sachel or was reinforced through Anglo-Norman is a minor route question; both pathways lead through the same French form. What is certain is that no Proto-Indo-European root is involved, and the Roman-to-English portion of the chain (stages 3–7) is securely attested at every step.
