Befallen is the past participle of befall, a verb meaning to happen to someone — especially suddenly, fatefully, and usually for the worse, as when night befalls the travellers, disaster befalls the expedition, or adventure befalls an unsuspecting hobbit. Where plain happen is neutral and accidental, befallen carries destiny inside it: what has befallen was, in some sense, waiting to fall.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses the word in its most classical register: adventure had suddenly befallen Bilbo at the age of fifty — not sought, not earned, but descended upon him out of the sky like weather. The adverb suddenly does the work of the lightning strike, and the participle does the work of fate: Bilbo did not find adventure so much as adventure found him, the way rain finds the unready walker. Frodo, approaching the same birthday, feels the symmetry gathering over him like a cloud.

Core meaning in detail: Befall joins the prefix be- (about, upon, thoroughly) to fall, so that events literally fall upon their victims and beneficiaries. The image is meteorological and biblical: night falls, fate falls, the lot falls, judgment falls — happening imagined as a descent from above onto heads below. The participle befallen freezes that descent into a completed state: the lot has been cast, the blow has landed, the adventure has arrived and changed everything. Modern prose reserves it for the grave and the grand — tragedies, honours, fates, adventures — and would sound comic ordering a pizza that befell the customer.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To happen to someone, especially something bad or fateful (primary sense, from Old English): what has befallen the house of Stuart, the fate that befell the climbers. The bridge is the falling image itself: events descend upon people as rain descends upon fields, unasked and indiscriminate, and the participle records the aftermath.
  • To come upon suddenly, to strike or overtake (adventures, night, silence): adventure befell Bilbo, darkness befell the city. The bridge narrows happening to ambush: the subject is active weather or fate, the object passive humanity, and the grammar makes victims of us all — things befall us, we never befall things.
  • To fall in place fittingly, to belong by lot (archaic, of shares and duties): as befell his rank, the lot befell Matthias in the Acts of the Apostles. The bridge runs through the casting of lots: what falls to a person by chance becomes what befell him by providence, and the participle once carried fairness as easily as calamity.
  • To occur in the course of narrative (literary and scriptural): it befell in those days, many strange things had befallen. The bridge is the storyteller's art: chroniclers and evangelists need a verb that lets events arrive with authority, neither proved nor questioned, simply fallen from the record of heaven into the text.
  • Passive misfortune in fixed phrases (modern residue): woe betide is kin, but befallen survives in phrases like befallen by tragedy and ill-befallen. The bridge is fossilisation: as everyday speech abandoned befall for happen, the participle retreated into news headlines, obituaries, and fantasy novels, where its very archaism guarantees seriousness.

Register and connotation: befallen is high, solemn, faintly archaic English — the Bible, the chronicle, the epic, the headline about disaster. Nobody uses it for errands or trivia; it dignifies whatever it touches with the weight of fate. Tolkien reaches for it precisely because the Shire is prosaic and adventure is not: the collision of the lofty participle with a respectable hobbit's birthday is the comedy and the gravity of the book in one verb. To say adventure befell Bilbo is to say the world is larger and stranger than the Shire admits — and that it may, at fifty, be waiting for Frodo too.
