- Late 16th century - "cavalcade" entered English from French, which took it from Italian "cavalcata" — a ride on horseback and, by extension, a troop of horsemen riding out together — the word descending all the way from Latin "caballus", the plain word for a work-horse.
- 17th-18th century - from the start it carried a hint of spectacle: a cavalcade is something to be watched, whether a royal entry, a noble arrival, or a military review — practical mounted travel dignified into a pageant.
- Modern English - the word still insists on riders (or loosely, vehicles) and a certain grandeur of occasion, and by metaphor it may apply to any continuous and impressive succession, as in a "cavalcade of events", though this figurative use remains rare and literary.
