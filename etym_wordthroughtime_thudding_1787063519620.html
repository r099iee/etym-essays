- c. 10th-11th c. - Rooted in Old English þyddan / þuddan, used primarily to mean "to thrust, stab, push violently, or strike with force" in martial and physical contexts.
- 14th-15th c. - Preserved in Northern Middle English and Early Scots (e.g. John Barbour's Brus) as thud, describing sudden blasts of tempestuous wind, furious gusts, or violent martial strokes.
- 16th-17th c. - Developed in Scots poetry (Gavin Douglas, Robert Sempill) into an explicit onomatopoeic noun and verb denoting the dull, reverberating acoustic sound of a heavy blow or falling body.
- 18th c. - Popularized in wider English literature through the Scottish Enlightenment and vernacular revival (Allan Ramsay, Robert Burns), where thudding began describing both physical impacts and somatic heartbeats.
- 19th c. - Adopted into standard Victorian and Romantic prose (Sir Walter Scott, Charles Dickens), becoming an established literary descriptor for heavy footsteps, muffled falls, and industrial machinery.
- 20th-21st c. - Ubiquitous in modern English across literature, cinema, and everyday speech, standard for describing cardiovascular panic ("thudding heart"), traumatic impacts, and visceral, non-resonant crashes.
