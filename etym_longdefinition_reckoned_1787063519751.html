Primary sense. Reckoned is the past tense and past participle of the verb to reckon: to count, calculate, or compute; to estimate; to consider, judge, or regard; and, in everyday idiom, to suppose or think. In the source sentence the word appears in the fixed expression "to be reckoned with" — meaning to be taken into account as a serious, dangerous, or unavoidable force: "Smaug was still to be reckoned with", that is, the dragon remained a threat too great to ignore. The form "reckoned" carries all of the verb's senses in its past tenses and participles.

Full range of senses.

  • To count or calculate (the root sense). The oldest and etymologically central meaning: "he reckoned the days of the journey", "reckon the cost before you begin". In this sense the word is close to "count", "total", "calculate", but it now sounds formal, archaic, or dialectal — except in settled phrases like "reckon up" and the noun "reckoning".
  • To estimate or judge. A natural extension: "I reckon it is ten miles to the ford", "she reckoned him honest" — forming an opinion by weighing the available signs, often with an informal, self-assured flavour.
  • To suppose, think, or be of the opinion. The colloquial, conversational sense: "I reckon it will rain", "I reckon he is right". This is the sense of the American South, the English West Country, Australia, and sailors the world over, and it is entirely neutral in tone — a confident, folksy "I think".
  • To include in a class or category. "Reckon among", "reckon with": "he was reckoned among the greatest of his age", "I did not reckon him among my friends" — the sense of counting someone or something as belonging to a group.
  • To take into account, to deal with — "reckon with". The phrasal verb "reckon with" means to consider as an important factor and, more forcefully, to deal with as an obstacle: "we must reckon with the weather". Its passive counterpart, "to be reckoned with", has become a fixed idiom meaning "formidable, not to be ignored" — "a force to be reckoned with", "still to be reckoned with", exactly as in the source sentence. This is now one of the two main living uses of the word in educated English.
  • To rely on — "reckon on". "Reckon on" means to count or depend upon: "we reckoned on your help" — the sense of calculating that something will be available.
  • Archaic senses. "To reckon" once meant to narrate, tell, or give an account of (its Old English meaning), and "to reckon" could mean to settle an account or a debt (surviving in "the day of reckoning", i.e., the day of settlement and judgement). These archaic senses explain the family's deep link between counting and moral accounting.

The idioms. Beyond the plain verb, "reckoned" lives in several fixed expressions: "to be reckoned with" (formidable), "a day of reckoning" (a time when past deeds are settled and judged), "dead reckoning" (navigation by calculated course and speed rather than observation — the noun form), and "pay one's reckoning" (settle a bill, now old-fashioned). The phrase "a force to be reckoned with" is the modern flagship: used of armies, athletes, companies, and hurricanes.

Register and tone. The word's register is strikingly split. In writing and formal speech, "reckon" outside its idioms now sounds old-fashioned or rural, a deliberate folksiness ("I reckon"). In its idioms it is entirely standard and even prestigious ("a force to be reckoned with"). The split mirrors the history: an ancient word of accounting, kept alive partly as everyday dialect speech and partly as high literary idiom.

Grammar note. Reckon is a regular verb: reckon / reckons / reckoned / reckoning. "Reckoned" is past tense ("they reckoned the distance") and past participle ("the cost was reckoned", "to be reckoned with"). The participle is frequently used in the passive construction "be reckoned with/among/as", and in the passive perfect "has been reckoned". The idiom "to be reckoned with" takes the participle after "be" and is fixed: "Smaug was still to be reckoned with."
