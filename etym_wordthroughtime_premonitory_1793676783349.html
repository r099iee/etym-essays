- Late 16th c. - Coined in late Middle and early Modern English from scholastic Latin praemonitorius, initially denoting formal legal, ecclesiastical, or moral notices delivering advance warning or authoritative admonition.
- 17th c. - Shifted into political and theological treatises to describe ominous omens, prodigies, and celestial portents warning a commonwealth or kingdom of divine displeasure or civil turbulence.
- 18th to 19th c. - Solidified into clinical medical terminology, where 'premonitory symptoms' became the standard diagnostic term for early physical sensations (auras, chills, malaise) preceding acute paroxysms of fever, epilepsy, or cholera.
- Late 19th to Early 20th c. - Broadened into psychological and aesthetic literature to describe eerie, uncanny premonitions, existential dread, or foreboding intuitions in Romantic, Gothic, and modernist fiction.
- Mid-20th c. to Present - Maintained dual usage: precise scientific and neurological discourse (premonitory urges in Tourette syndrome, premonitory seismic tremors) alongside sophisticated socio-political analysis denoting early crisis indicators that herald systemic collapse.
