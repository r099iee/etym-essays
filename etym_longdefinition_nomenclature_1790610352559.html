nomenclature denotes a system or set of names, terms, or designations used in a particular science, art, profession, or field, together with the rules and conventions governing their formation, assignment, and use. It is both inventory and code: the actual vocabulary, such as anatomical names or chemical names, and the principled procedure that authorizes them. To speak of a nomenclature problem is to point to difficulty not in things themselves but in what we call them, how consistently, and with what consequences for clarity, memory, and authority.

In everyday usage, nomenclature is a formal word for terminology, naming, or jargon, often with a suggestion of official or technical weight. A company may revise its product nomenclature; a wine writer may mock pretentious nomenclature; a critic may complain that traditional nomenclature obscures more than it reveals. The term implies deliberateness: names chosen, standardized, taught, and enforced, not merely evolved slang.

In science, nomenclature is highly regulated. Binomial nomenclature in biology, governed by codes for zoology, botany, and bacteriology, assigns each species a Latinized genus and epithet with priority, typification, and synonymy rules. Chemical nomenclature under IUPAC provides systematic names that encode structure, alongside retained trivial names. Medical, anatomical, and astronomical nomenclatures similarly balance descriptiveness, stability, uniqueness, and international intelligibility. A nomenclature problem here can stall research, confuse databases, endanger patients, or trigger priority disputes.

  • Core technical sense: system of names: the stock of authorized terms in a discipline, as in anatomical nomenclature or stratigraphic nomenclature.
  • Procedural sense: rules for naming: the code, principles, and committees that create and revise names, as in to reform chemical nomenclature.
  • General sense: terminology or labeling: any specialized vocabulary, as in legal nomenclature or fashion nomenclature, without formal code.
  • Rhetorical sense: act of naming as power: who gets to name patterns, diseases, places, peoples, and periods, and what ideological work those names do.
  • Extended literary sense: list or catalogue: occasionally the sheer inventory quality, the roll-call of names as texture, as in a poem luxuriating in nomenclature.

Polysemy is bridged by the idea of calling by rule. Latin nomen name plus calare to call or proclaim yields a calling of names, originally the function of a nomenclator, a slave who whispered names to a Roman canvassing for office. Modern sense abstracts from person to system: from one who calls names to the system by which names are called. Secondary senses retain that social dimension: nomenclature always implicates memory, recognition, politeness, ownership, and transparency. To defend traditional names for patterns as perfectly useful still is to defend a nomenclature not merely as convenience but as accumulated practical wisdom against an ideal that would dissolve names into pure showing.

Contemporary pressures intensify nomenclature problems: globalization demands multilingual equivalence; digitization demands unique identifiers and ontologies; decolonization demands renaming of eponyms, places, and specimens; commercialization breeds competing proprietary names. Nomenclature thus sits at the intersection of language, knowledge organization, and ethics, making it a perennial pedagogical and philosophical problem as well as an administrative one.
