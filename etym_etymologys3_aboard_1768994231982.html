aboard — The biography of aboard begins with an act of cutting, and it ends — three thousand years later — with a human-resources verb.

The Proto-Indo-European root *bherdh- meant "to cut," and in the Germanic languages it produced *burdą, "a board" — not a board in the abstract, but a plank cut from a log, a piece of wood shaped by the axe. From the beginning the Germanic board was multifunctional in a way that shaped the entire vocabulary around it: a board could be a plank underfoot, a table for meals, a shield held up against the edge of a sword. Old English bord carried all of these senses at once, and the modern English board still does — the blackboard on the wall, the board of directors seated at a table, the room and board of a lodging, the board of a ship.

The nautical turn. Because a ship's hull was built of boards, bord came to mean "the side of a ship," and from that came the spatial phrase: to go "on board" (Old English on bord, or a- "on" + bord) was to commit your weight to the wooden fabric of the vessel — to be aboard. Middle English contracted this to aboord and abord, partly reshaped under the influence of the Old French phrase à bord, and the word spread into the rest of the ship's grammar: overboard, "over the side" into the sea; starboard, the steering side where the rudder-oar was worked; larboard, the loading side. Every one of these is the same plank, seen from a different place on the hull. To be aboard a ship in the age of sail was to be inside a small wooden world that carried its own table, its own meals, its own command.

  • PIE *bherdh- "to cut": the reconstructed root; the board is "the thing cut" — a plank taken from a log.
  • Proto-Germanic *burdą: "board, plank, table, ship's side" — the cutting-root produces the multifunctional wooden slab.
  • Old English bord (8th c.): plank, table, shield, and ship's side all at once.
  • Old English on bord / a- + bord: "on board, at the ship's side" — the nautical phrase from which the word is contracted.
  • Middle English aboord/abord (14th c.): contracted form, converged with Old French à bord.
  • Modern English aboard: ships, trains, planes, and — figuratively — institutions.

Semantic drift in detail. (1) From "cut wood" to "a plank" — the root's action narrows to its product. (2) From "plank" to "ship's side" — the hull built of boards lends the word its nautical location. (3) From "ship's side" to "on the ship" — the spatial phrase "on board" hardens into the single word aboard. (4) From "on the ship" to "on any vehicle" — the railway and the aircraft extend the nautical frame to every public conveyance. (5) From "on a vehicle" to "within an institution" — the modern figurative leap, in which welcome aboard, take this on board, and to onboard transfer the vessel's grammar to the corporation, the team, and the project. No sense has died; the word stacks them, and a writer can reach any layer of the stack.

Sound and register. Aboard is a compact, strongly spatial word — the a- prefix (from on) plus the hard board gives it a sense of decisive placement, of weight committed to a surface. It is standard and slightly formal, and it is almost always dynamic: it accompanies verbs of motion or of state (go aboard, come aboard, climb aboard, be aboard) and it belongs to the vocabulary of embarkation in every register, from the sailor's logbook to the flight attendant's announcement. The card's sentence — "He is taken on for New Orleans aboard a flatboat" — uses the word in its oldest register, as the plain, documentary marker of a boy committing his weight to a drifting wooden raft for forty-two days of river travel.

The full circle. The deepest irony of the word is how far it has travelled from its own material. A board was once a rough plank cut from a log; a ship's side was a wall of such planks; to be aboard was to stand on them. The word then climbed from the hull to the railway carriage to the aircraft cabin to the corporate "onboarding" program — and every step of that climb is still the same cut plank, bearing the same weight. The Mississippi flatboat of the card, built of raw boards and destined to be broken up and sold as lumber, is the word's origin and its destiny in a single image: the whole frontier commerce of the river, compressed into a word that once meant nothing more than "on a cut plank."
