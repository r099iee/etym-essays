PIE *bʰuH- "to become, be, grow" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE)
→ Proto-Germanic *būaną "to dwell, cultivate, build, prepare" (1st millennium BCE–CE)
→ Old Norse búa "to dwell, prepare, arrange" and its reflexive búask "to get oneself ready" (attested 9th–13th c.)
→ Middle English busken "to hurry, make ready, prepare" (borrowed from Old Norse búask; attested from c. 1300)
→ Early Modern English bustle (c. 1610) "to be busily active, to move energetically with noise" — busk- + the frequentative suffix -le (as in sparkle, twinkle)
→ English bustle, noun "busy stir, commotion" (from the 1630s) ◄ TARGET (present day)

DEBATE / NUANCE: The chain is secure from Old Norse búask onward. The PIE root *bʰuH- is standard and massively attested across Indo-European (Sanskrit bhū, bhávati; Greek phúō, physis; Latin fuī; Slavic byti). Some scholars, however, propose Old Norse bustla "to splash about" as the direct source of bustle rather than busk- + -le; both hypotheses point to the same Old Norse verb family, but the immediate parent of the -le stem is debated. The fashion noun bustle (the 19th-century skirt frame) is possibly a separate word of unknown origin — some link it to French busc, a corset-stay — and is not a stage in this chain.
