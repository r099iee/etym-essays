1. The two ingredients: hap and mis-

Mishap is a compound of two old Germanic elements, each with a long and revealing history. The second, hap, is a borrowing into English from Old Norse happ, “chance, fortune, good luck” — the Vikings’ word for the luck that befalls a person. English took it up in the 13th century and built a small family from it: happen (“to come to pass, chance”), happy (“favoured by luck”, which then softened into “content, joyful”), hapless (“luckless, unfortunate”), and perhaps (“by chance, it may be”). The first element, mis-, is the common Germanic prefix for “bad, wrong, astray”, used in English since Old English times to build words of wrongness: mistake, mischief, misdeed, mishap. Both elements are native to the language — this is a word made entirely at home.

2. Coining and early weight (16th century)

Mishap is first attested in the early 16th century (about 1509), and its earliest sense is the literal one: bad luck, ill fortune, an unlucky accident. In Tudor and Stuart usage it is still a weighty word. Poets and chroniclers use it of shipwrecks, plagues, deaths, and strokes of fate; “mishap” in 1600 could be a tragedy. The compound had not yet been diminished by comedy; it simply meant “what happens badly”.

3. The gradual lightening (17th–18th c.)

Through the 17th and 18th centuries mishap begins its long slide down the scale of gravity. In plays and novels it comes to describe the household accidents and social embarrassments of daily life — the spilt wine, the torn sleeve, the missed appointment. Because the word never names a person’s fault (a mishap is a stroke of luck, not a blunder), it is the perfect vocabulary for the mildly comic misfortune that happens to everyone. By the 18th century the heavier sense has largely drained away, leaving “a small, annoying, unfortunate accident”.

4. The reassurances of the 19th century

The 19th century completes the transformation. The phrase “without mishap” — used of journeys, expeditions, and campaigns — becomes a staple of newspapers, adventure stories, and travel writing, always reassuring the reader that nothing went wrong. It is precisely in the adventurous prose of this period (including the source text here, a quest across dangerous cliffs) that “without mishap” sounds most natural and most reassuring: the risk was real, the outcome was fine. Mishap becomes the lightest member of the accident family, the one you can smile about afterwards.

5. The 20th–21st century: institutional understatement

In modern English mishap has two well-worn paths. The first is the comic-domestic one, still lively (“a string of kitchen mishaps”). The second is institutional understatement: military, aviation, medical, and corporate English says “training mishap”, “an unfortunate mishap”, “a minor mishap” to describe accidents without assigning blame or inviting alarm — a polite, non-legal register that no one mistakes for a disaster. The old heavy sense survives only in irony, euphemism, or the fossilised phrase “by some mishap”.

6. Today

  • Core meaning: a minor unlucky accident or brief piece of bad luck.
  • Register: standard, natural in speech and writing; “without mishap” is mildly formal or literary.
  • Boundaries: far lighter than misfortune or disaster; less about fault than blunder or mistake; more courteous than accident in institutional use.
  • The transparent formation mis- + hap still teaches the modern student the old Germanic vocabulary of luck.

Summary: Old Norse happ (“chance, luck”) met the Germanic prefix mis- (“bad, wrong”) in 16th-century English to mean literally “bad luck”; four centuries of daily, comic, and institutional use have lightened that bad luck from tragedy to triviality — the word that reassures us, in the end, that nothing serious went wrong.
