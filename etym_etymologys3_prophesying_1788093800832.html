Greek foundations. The chain begins with the PIE root *bʰeh₂- "to speak, say", which produced Ancient Greek phēmí "I say, declare" and its family (phanai "to speak", phōnē "voice"). From this root Greek built prophḗtēs (5th century BCE and later), a compound of pro- "before, in front of, on behalf of" and phētēs "speaker": literally "one who speaks before" or "one who speaks for (another)". In classical usage the prophētēs was the interpreter of an oracle — the priest who spoke for the god, as at Delphi, where the prophētēs rendered the Pythia's ravings into pronouncements. The two readings of pro- ("beforehand" → foreteller; "on behalf of" → spokesman) have been debated since antiquity; both are true to different uses, and together they explain why the word means both "one who announces" and "one who predicts".

Septuagint and New Testament. The decisive semantic turn came when the translators of the Septuagint (3rd–2nd centuries BCE) used prophētēs for the Hebrew nābî — the prophet who speaks God's word to Israel, announcing judgement and hope. The New Testament continues this: prophēteúō "to prophesy" includes both foretelling and inspired preaching (1 Corinthians 14). Greek prophēteia "prophecy" and prophēteúō passed into Christian Latin as prophetia and prophetāre (alongside prophetizāre), used throughout Jerome's Vulgate (4th century).

Through French into English. From Church Latin came Old French prophecie "prophecy" and the verb prophecier "to prophesy" (12th century). Note the sound change: Latin prophetia became Old French prophecie, where Latin -tia- became -cie- (as in gratia → grâce), so that French — and English, borrowing from it — spell the noun with -c-. Middle English borrowed both in the 14th century: prophecie (noun) and prophecien (verb), attested in religious and devotional texts and in the Wycliffite Bible (c. 1380s), where "prophecien" renders the biblical verb.

Spelling split: prophesy vs prophecy. In the 16th century, printers and grammarians regularised the forms: the noun became fixed as prophecy and the verb as prophesy (the -s- perhaps by association with the Greek verb stem, or simply as a visual distinguisher). Tyndale (1526) and the King James Version (1611) use prophesy throughout — "have we not prophesied in thy name?" (Matthew 7:22), "covet to prophesy" (1 Corinthians 14:39) — fixing both the spelling and the sacred register for centuries of English readers.

Secularisation.

  • 17th–18th centuries: political and apocalyptic prophecy flourishes; "prophesying" extends from scripture to statecraft and popular prophecy-mongering, often satirised.
  • 19th century: science and journalism secularise prediction; "prophesy" competes with "predict" and "forecast", keeping a loftier, more solemn tone.
  • 20th–21st centuries: the participle "prophesying" survives chiefly in religious contexts and in the ironic stock phrase "prophesying doom/disaster" — confident, unwelcome prediction with a mock-biblical flavour.

Summary. Prophesying has travelled from the Greek temple (speaking for the god) through the Septuagint and Vulgate (speaking God's word), through Old French and Middle English into the King James Bible, and then out into the secular world, where it still carries the echo of its sacred past — a word that makes every confident prediction sound, for a moment, like an oracle speaking.
