- Old English rendan: literal tearing of cloth and flesh, used in glosses to render Latin scindere 'to cut apart'; the verb is violent and physical from its first records, with no neutral or soft register.
- Middle English (c. 1200): enters devotional prose and romance; rending one's garments becomes the fixed gesture of mourning and penitence, and rending hearts the image of grief; the word acquires a strong emotional and religious load.
- 1500s–1600s: the King James Bible (1611) stamps its usage — 'Rend your heart, and not your garments' (Joel 2:13), 'the veil of the temple was rent in twain' (Matthew 27:51), 'I will rend the kingdom' (1 Kings 11:11) — and preserves the archaic strong past 'rent' in living literary form; concurrently, secular verse uses rending for storm, war, and catastrophe.
- 1700s–1800s: gothic and Romantic tastes make rending a favourite of melodrama — screams rend the night, storms rend sails and skies; the sentimental derivative 'heart-rending' (late 18th c.) crystallises the word's pathos.
- Modern: a literary and journalistic shell-word; ordinary speech prefers tear, rip, or split, so 'rending' now reads as archaic, dramatic, and final — its rarity is itself its power, and fantasy and biblical English keep the word alive.
