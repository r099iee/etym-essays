- The whirling toy — Coined in the 15th century from whirl + gig, the word is at first a plain, useful name for anything that spins round: the whipping top, the wind-spun toy, a rustic household word for spinning playthings.
- Fortune takes a spin — By Shakespeare's day the word has gone figurative: 'the whirligig of time brings in his revenges' makes it the emblem of fortune's giddy reversals, and the dictionaries extend it from toy to merry-go-round, to the spinning water-beetle, and even to a fickle, inconstant person — the spinning object becoming the image of human flightiness.
- The folksy century — Through the 18th and 19th centuries whirligigs whirl in gardens and at fairs as ornamental spinners of every sort, and the word begins to smell old-fashioned, whimsical, and faintly archaic.
- Contemporary sense — Modern English keeps 'whirligig' as an archaic-whimsical relic of children's literature, fairy-tale diction, and period dialogue — the perfect material for a dwarf's comic oath like 'Whistles and whirligigs!' — while its functional descendant in the hand is the fidget spinner.
