courtiers (plural noun; singular courtier): people who attend, reside at, or are attached to the court of a sovereign, prince, or other great lord — the companions, attendants, household officials, nobles, and hangers-on who surround a ruler, seeking political influence, personal favour, advancement, wealth, or royal patronage through their proximity to the throne.

1. Primary sense — the royal attendant

In its core, historically grounded meaning, a courtier is a member of a royal or princely court: one of the circle of people who live at or frequently visit a monarch's residence, take part in court life, and serve, advise, flatter, or entertain the sovereign. Courtiers were typically drawn from the nobility and gentry, but the circle could also include clergy, scholars, artists, diplomats, and professional officials. The plural form courtiers (the form on this card) refers to such people collectively — the whispering attendants of the Narnian Queen in the source context are exactly this: a court, closeted in a castle, watching and murmuring around an ailing sovereign. Key features of the primary sense:
  • Place: the physical court — the palace, castle, or great household of a ruler, and the institutional court of government and ceremony built around it.
  • Function: attendance, service, advice, ceremony, diplomacy, patronage-seeking, and the arts of pleasing — courtiers live by access to the ruler's ear.
  • Social role: courtiers occupy a middle ground between servants and statesmen: they need hold no fixed office, yet they exercise influence through proximity, etiquette, and intimacy with the monarch.
  • Register: today chiefly historical and literary; in everyday speech people usually say "advisers" or "aides" rather than "courtiers."

2. Secondary sense — the figurative insider and sycophant (modern)

Since the seventeenth century, and with growing frequency in modern prose, courtier is used metaphorically for any self-serving flatterer, sycophant, or insider who clusters around a powerful leader, executive, celebrity, or institution:
  • "Political courtiers" — the aides, spin-doctors, and influence-seekers around presidents, prime ministers, and party bosses.
  • "Media courtiers" / "corporate courtiers" — executives, advisors, and yes-men around CEOs, moguls, and media proprietors.
  • Any "court" of power — royal or not — produces "courtiers": people whose influence rests not on office but on access, gossip, and the art of pleasing.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the shift runs from physical residence inside a ruler's enclosed court (ancient and medieval fact) to social proximity inside a circle of power (Renaissance institution) to moral character — obsequiousness, calculation, flattery (the satirical verdict of the 1600s–1700s) — and finally to a generic metaphor for any insider feeding on power in any domain (modern journalism). The word's emotional temperature has cooled from neutral occupation to suspicious intimacy: a courtier is now someone whose place depends on currying favour rather than on merit.

3. Related meanings in the same family

  • Court itself: the institution — a royal court, a court of law, a tennis "court" — all ultimately from the same Latin root meaning an enclosed yard.
  • To court someone: to woo, solicit, or cultivate favour (from courting the monarch to courting a spouse, or courting disaster).
  • Courteous, courtesy, courtly: the etiquette of the court, extended to civil, refined behaviour in general.
  • Courtesan: historically a court-mistress (from French courtisane, "woman of the court") — a distinct but related word.
  • Do not confuse courtiers with curtsies (the bows of respect, from courtesy) or with a "court of law" official; the plural is pronounced with a final voiced /z/ like "courts."

4. Grammatical and usage notes

  • Count noun: "a courtier," "the courtiers," "a courtier of the king."
  • The plural courtiers is the form on this card; the singular courtier names one such person.
  • Style level: formal, literary, and now distinctly period-flavoured; C. S. Lewis uses it in the sick Queen's castle for deliberate fairy-tale archaism.
  • Compounds and derivatives: courtier-like, courtierly, courtly; the plural appears in fixed phrases like "courtiers of power."

5. Polysemy summary

  • (1) Historical royal attendant / palace official — primary sense.
  • (2) Modern figurative sycophant / insider around any powerful figure — secondary sense, dominant in journalism today.
  • (3) Agent-noun family member of "court" — includes courtly, courteous, courtesy, courtesan.
  • The semantic bridge in every case is proximity to power: whoever is inside the court lives by the ruler's ear — the exact image on this card.
