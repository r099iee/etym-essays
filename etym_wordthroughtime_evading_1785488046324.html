- [16th century] - Enters English from French meaning literally "to escape from" — a physical danger, a place, a blow; neutral and often heroic (one evades a pursuer).
- [17th–18th century] - Slides from physical escape to dodging a question, duty, or law; gains overtones of cleverness and a small moral shadow.
- [19th–20th century] - Becomes standard in legal and everyday prose ("evade the law", "tax evasion"); guilt-tinged when a duty is dodged, defensible when a bad question is sidestepped.
- [Modern register] - "Evading" keeps the side-step image: courteous, pragmatic, faintly rueful — a skilled dodge rather than open defiance or dishonest ignoring.
