- Mid-14th century - "insane, mad, raving" - in early Middle English (from Old French frenetique) the word describes outright mental derangement, the ranting lunatic, barely distinguishable from "mad."
- 15th-17th century - the madness sense retreats as "insane" and "crazy" claim literal mental illness; "frantic" keeps the raving energy but slides toward "wildly agitated by grief, fear, or passion" - a disorder of the emotions rather than of the mind.
- 18th-19th century - "frenzied activity" takes over: frantic describes desperate haste, feverish labor, breathless urgency, loss of composure without any hint of lunacy.
- 20th-21st century - everyday hyperbole: "a frantic rush," "frantic searching," "a frantic pace" - the word now names harried, out-of-control busyness, keeping its fever pitch while the raving, clinical sense fades to metaphor.
