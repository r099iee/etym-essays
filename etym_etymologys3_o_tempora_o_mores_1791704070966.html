Because O tempora, O mores is a classical multi-word rhetorical phrase combining three distinct linguistic elements—the exclamatory interjection O, the neuter plural noun tempora, and the masculine plural noun mores—an academically rigorous etymological account must trace the independent evolution of each constituent root in isolation before analyzing their historical synthesis in Roman oratory and their two-thousand-year legacy in Western literature.

PART I: THE ETYMOLOGICAL EVOLUTION OF “TEMPORA”

The noun tempora is the neuter nominative, accusative, and vocative plural of Latin tempus ("time, season, crisis"). Linguists trace tempus to the Proto-Indo-European root *temh₁-, signifying "to cut, divide, or carve out" (cognate with Greek temnein "to cut" and tomē "a cut"). To early Indo-European pastoralists, time was not an empty, abstract continuum, but a series of tangible, carved-out intervals: the natural segments of daylight, agricultural seasons, and lunar cycles.

In Proto-Italic, this root developed into the neuter s-stem noun *tempos-. During the early development of Latin (4th–3rd century BCE), two systematic sound changes reshaped this word:

  • Rhotacism: When case suffixes added a vowel to the root, the stem-final -s- found itself positioned between two vowels. Under universal Latin phonetic laws, single intervocalic -s- voiced and transformed into the liquid consonant -r- (rhotacism). Thus, archaic plural *tempos-a became tempora, and genitive singular *tempos-es became temporis.
  • Nominative Vowel Raising: In the bare nominative singular, where the final -s was not intervocalic, rhotacism did not occur, but the short vowel -o- raised to short -u- before final -s, yielding Classical Latin tempus.

Semantically, while the singular tempus often denoted abstract duration or an appointed hour, the plural tempora specifically came to signify "the times"—the broad political, social, and moral circumstances that characterize a particular era.

PART II: THE ETYMOLOGICAL EVOLUTION OF “MORES”

The noun mores is the masculine nominative, accusative, and vocative plural of Latin mōs ("custom, habit, character, will"). Its ultimate source is the Proto-Indo-European root *mō- or *meh₂-, carrying the core meaning of "to exert will, strive intensely, or be animated by strong purpose". In the Germanic branch, this identical root produced Proto-Germanic *mōda- ("courage, mind, anger"), providing Old English mōd and modern English mood.

In Proto-Italic, the root developed as *mōs-, originally designating an individual's personal disposition, inner resolve, or psychological attitude. Over centuries of Italic community life, this internal will shifted semantically toward the outward, recurring actions that it generated—hence "habit, custom, way of behaving".

Like tempus, mōs underwent 4th-century BCE Latin rhotacism: the nominative singular retained final -s (mōs), while all inflected cases shifted intervocalic -s- to -r-, producing genitive mōris and plural mōrēs. In Republican Rome, mores became the ideological heart of the state through the concept of the mos maiorum ("the custom of the ancestors"). When Romans evaluated the health of their civilization, they looked not merely to their statutes, but to whether the citizens' mōrēs remained uncorrupted by greed and luxury.

PART III: THE ETYMOLOGICAL EVOLUTION OF THE INTERJECTION “O”

The opening word O is an ancient Proto-Indo-European exclamatory particle (*ō / *oh), naturally emerging from the spontaneous phonetic phonation of human surprise, grief, terror, or awe. Present across Sanskrit (u), Greek (ō), Latin (ō), and Germanic (oh), it serves in Classical Latin as the definitive vocal marker of the vocative of address or the accusative of exclamation, instantly signaling to an audience that the speaker has moved beyond dispassionate forensic analysis into raw emotional truth.

IV. The Synthesis in Roman Oratory: Cicero and Catiline (63 BCE)

The historical convergence of these three words into an immortal phrase occurred on the morning of November 8, 63 BCE. Lucius Sergius Catilina, an impoverished patrician who had been repeatedly thwarted in his ambitions for the consulship, had organized a violent armed insurrection. He gathered an army of dispossessed veterans in Etruria, stockpiled weapons, and conspired to assassinate Consul Marcus Tullius Cicero, set fire to the city of Rome, and wipe out all senatorial debts in a bloody coup d'état.

Cicero, having uncovered the plot through secret informants, summoned the Senate to the heavily fortified Temple of Jupiter Stator. To the astonishment of all present, Catiline himself strode into the temple and took his seat, banking on his aristocratic pedigree and the legal immunity of a senator. The other senators recoiled, abandoning the benches near him.

Rising to speak without prepared notes, Cicero directed his opening barrage not at abstract legal statutes, but at the sheer moral outrage of the situation. He opened his First Catilinarian Oration with words that became the bedrock of Western rhetorical training:

"Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra? ... O tempora, O mores! Senatus haec intellegit, consul videt; hic tamen vivit. Vivit? Immo vero etiam in senatum venit, fit publici consilii particeps!"
("How long, Catiline, will you abuse our patience? ... Oh, the times! Oh, the customs! The Senate knows these things, the consul sees them; yet this man lives. Lives? Nay, he even enters the Senate, he participates in the public council!")

The rhetorical brilliance of pairing tempora with mores lay in Cicero's comprehensive diagnosis: tempora signified the external era of republican decay, paralyzed magistrates, and military terror, while mores pointed to the internal moral rot and cowardice of a ruling class that hesitated to execute a traitor caught in the act. The two nouns functioned as an inseparable diptych of social catastrophe.

V. The Two-Thousand-Year Literary Afterlife

Cicero's speech was studied, copied, and memorized by generations of Roman schoolchildren, transforming O tempora, O mores into an immediate cultural proverb. In the 1st century CE, the Roman epigrammatist Martial parodied the phrase in his satirical verses (Epigrams IX.70), mocking a pretentious contemporary named Caecilianus who constantly went around sighing "O tempora, O mores!" whenever anyone enjoyed a pleasant meal or wore a modern toga.

During the Middle Ages and Renaissance, the phrase became a central humanist trope. Desiderius Erasmus featured it prominently in his Adagia, and French essayist Michel de Montaigne deployed it in his Essais to dissect the vanity of political moralizing. In Elizabethan and Jacobean England, Cicero was affectionately known to scholars and authors as "Tully" (derived from his family name Tullius). English writers from Geoffrey Chaucer to William Shakespeare, John Milton, Alexander Pope, and Samuel Johnson frequently referenced "Tully's orations" and quoted his famous exclamation.

By the 19th and 20th centuries, O tempora, O mores had settled into its modern double life: a solemn touchstone of genuine civic indignation in high political commentary, and a wonderfully ironic, self-mocking catchphrase among cultivated literati—as illustrated in the source passage, where an old English professor playfully exclaims "O grammar! O tempora, O mores! as my friend Tully once remarked" to poke fun at his own lifelong obsession with linguistic standards.
