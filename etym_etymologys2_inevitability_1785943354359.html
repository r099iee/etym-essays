Latin vītāre (to shun, avoid; attested from early Latin; origin uncertain — traditionally connected with PIE *wei- "to drive, pursue, chase," via the notion of turning aside from pursuit, but no secure cognates exist and de Vaan records no convincing etymology)
→ Latin ēvītāre (to avoid completely, to escape; prefix ē-, from ex- "out," + vītāre; classical)
→ Latin ēvītābilis (avoidable; adjectival formation on ēvītāre)
→ Latin inēvītābilis (unavoidable, inevitable; negative in- "not" + ēvītābilis; classical, attested in Cicero and Seneca, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE)
→ Old French / Medieval French inévitable (unavoidable; learned)
→ Middle English inevitable (unavoidable, certain; first recorded 15th c.)
→ English INEVITABILITY (inevitable + -ity; first recorded 17th c.) ◄ TARGET

Note on debate: The root of vītāre is genuinely uncertain. The traditional equation with PIE *wei- "to drive, to pursue" (also seen in Sanskrit véti "drives, chases" and Greek híemai "I hasten") is plausible and often repeated, but no exact cognate is agreed upon, and de Vaan's etymological dictionary records vītāre as "of uncertain origin." This first stage should be treated as probable rather than proven. What is certain is the morphology of the later stages: ēvītāre, ēvītābilis, inēvītābilis, and the English borrowings inevitable (15th c.) and inevitability (17th c.).
