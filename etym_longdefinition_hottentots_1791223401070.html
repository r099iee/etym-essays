Hottentots is the historical, now offensive, English plural for the Khoikhoi (Khoekhoe-speaking) pastoralist peoples of South Africa and Namibia, used ubiquitously in colonial and early anthropological literature from the 1670s to the mid-20th century. The singular Hottentot was a Dutch settlers' mock-name imitating the click consonants of Khoekhoe as stuttering. The peoples so labelled call themselves Khoikhoi, Nama, Griqua and related names. In contemporary usage the term is regarded as a derogatory slur and should be replaced by Khoikhoi, Khoekhoe or specific group names except when quoting historical sources.

Senses and usage layers:

  • Historical ethnonym (plural): Refers to Khoikhoi pastoralists of the Cape, Namaqualand and Namibia as described by travellers and anthropologists. Example: "Among the Hottentots it was the custom to share surplus till all were equal." This is the sense in the source passage, where the author cites them alongside American Indians as evidence for primitive communism and hospitality.
  • Singular Hottentot as racial type: In 18th-19th-century racial science, a supposed Khoisan racial category distinguished from "Negro," "Caucasian" and "Bushman." Now wholly discredited pseudoscience.
  • Offensive slur (modern perception): In South Africa today Hotnot / Hottentot is hate speech targeting Coloured and Khoisan-descended people. Courts have penalized its use. Learners must recognize the shift from neutral-in-1800 to slur-today.
  • Extended pejorative in older English: By metaphor, 18th-19th-century English occasionally used hottentot to mean any "uncivilized, gibberish-speaking" person ("Don't talk Hottentot" = don't babble). The OED records this figurative sense as offensive slang.
  • Zoological / botanical relic: Survives in archaic common names like Hottentot fig (Carpobrotus edulis), Hottentot teal (Spatula hottentota), Hottentot buttonquail. Botanists and ornithologists are renaming many such eponyms.

The semantic bridge from speech-mockery to people-label to slur follows a common colonial pattern: outsiders name a group by unintelligibility (compare barbarian from bar-bar babble, Welsh from foreigner), the name hardens in law and science, then becomes stigmatized as power relations are exposed. The food-sharing custom noted in the passage reflects real Khoikhoi pastoral redistribution norms where wealthy cattle-owners were expected to lend, slaughter and share, maintaining egalitarian pressure despite wealth differences.

Grammar: capitalized Hottentots as ethnonym plural; lowercase hottentot in older generic slang. Pronounced /ˈhɒtənˌtɒts/ in RP. Modern guidance: do not use for living people; use Khoikhoi (people), Khoekhoe (language/culture), Nama (largest subgroup), Griqua/Khoekhoe mixed-descent communities, or San/Khoisan where appropriate. In Anki review, recognize passively but produce actively only with historical framing and trigger warning.
