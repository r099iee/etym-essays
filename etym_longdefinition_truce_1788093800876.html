Truce is a noun meaning a temporary agreement between opposing parties — usually armies, states or factions — to stop fighting for a limited period, without settling the underlying quarrel. It shares its historical root with true and troth: the oldest English form was a plural, trewes "pledges", i.e. the pledged terms of an agreement, and the modern singular truce results from treating that plural as an ordinary noun ending. In the source passage — "You shall not depart from it, until you call on your side for a truce and a parley" — Bard, having declared the Mountain besieged, states the condition under which the siege will lift: the dwarves must formally request a truce (a suspension of hostilities) and a parley (a conference between leaders), the two classic instruments of medieval negotiation, revived here with deliberate archaism in Tolkien's chronicle style.

Primary sense.

  • A cessation of fighting with a fixed term, agreed between commanders or governments: a three-day truce to bury the dead; a Christmas truce; to declare, observe, break or prolong a truce. The defining feature is temporariness: a truce suspends fighting; it does not end the war.
  • By extension, in private life: a suspension of open conflict between individuals, families or groups — an uneasy truce between the brothers; a truce in the argument. The word carries the implicit promise that hostilities will resume if the terms are not kept.

Secondary senses and related institutions.

  • Flag of truce: a white flag carried by an unarmed envoy to signal willingness to negotiate; "to send a flag of truce". The phrase is recorded from the early modern period and was later codified in the laws of war.
  • Truce of God (Treuga Dei): the famous Church institution of the tenth and eleventh centuries which forbade fighting on certain days and seasons — a truce imposed from above on the warrior aristocracy of Europe.
  • Legal and diplomatic nuance: in international law a truce is distinguished from an armistice (a general suspension of hostilities, often the prelude to peace negotiations) and from peace (the permanent settlement); the classical Latin term indutiae "truce" was revived in learned legal discourse from Grotius onward.
  • Figurative use: very common in journalism and everyday speech — a truce in the tariff war, a fragile truce between the parties, a truce in the family cold war. The word always implies mutual exhaustion or calculation: neither side can win outright, so both pause.

Boundaries. A truce differs from peace in duration (temporary vs. permanent), from a treaty in scope (a limited military arrangement vs. a comprehensive legal instrument), and from a lull in causation (agreed vs. accidental). Its connotation is of formal, sworn but brittle restraint — hope with a time limit.

Historical depth and modern idiom. In medieval custom the truce was a sacred institution: the “Truce of God” (Treuga Dei, tenth–eleventh centuries) suspended warfare on Sundays, holy days and Lent, and markets and fairs were held under a “king's truce” that protected buyers and sellers from the violence of local feuds. Later English preserved the word's legal solemnity in “flag of truce”, “to break a truce” and “truce terms”, and coined the rare adjective truceless — a truceless war, a truceless enmity — for conflict that admits no pause. Modern journalism exploits the same image in every arena of dispute — trade, politics, sport, family — so that “truce” has become the standard noun for any agreed, temporary halt in a quarrel that neither side has the strength or the will to finish.
