• pontiff: Similar because it is the direct English descendant of pontifex and can denote the same office; differs because in ordinary use it means the pope or any bishop, and it has lost the specific Roman reference to the head of the college.
  • pope: Similar because the bishop of Rome holds the title Pontifex Maximus and is the living referent of the phrase in modern English; differs because “pope” names the office in the Christian church, while Pontifex Maximus names a Roman priesthood that the papacy later adopted.
  • supreme pontiff: Similar because it is the exact English calque of pontifex maximus and is used in papal titulature; differs because it belongs to ecclesiastical diplomacy and never refers to the Roman priest or to a jocular modern “elder”.
  • high priest: Similar because it denotes the chief religious official of a cult; differs because it is a generic description that can be applied to Israel, Egypt or any hierarchy, whereas Pontifex Maximus is a specific Roman institution with a fixed title.
  • chief priest: Similar because it translates the literal sense of the Latin; differs because it is a plain descriptive phrase with no institutional or historical weight, and it does not imply life tenure or control of a calendar.
  • hierarch: Similar because it denotes a leading figure in a sacred hierarchy; differs because it is abstract and often used of Eastern Christian or metaphorical authorities, not of a named Roman priesthood.
  • prelate: Similar because it is a title of high ecclesiastical office; differs because a prelate is an administrative churchman, whereas the pontifex maximus was a priest of a civic religion and, after Augustus, the emperor himself.
  • flamen Dialis: Similar because it was one of the great priesthoods of the Roman state and was subject to elaborate ritual restrictions; differs because the flamen served a single deity (Jupiter) and had no authority over the college or the calendar.
  • rex sacrorum: Similar because it inherited the religious functions of the ancient kings and ranked within the same priestly system; differs because it was a purely ritual office, deliberately kept out of politics and subordinate to the pontifex maximus.
  • augur: Similar because augurs were Roman priests who read the will of the gods and could obstruct public business; differs because augury was a separate college concerned with signs, not with the law, the calendar and the Vestals.
