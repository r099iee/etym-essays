tripartite means consisting of three parts, divisions, or parties; divided into three; or involving three participants. It describes structures — agreements, souls, motives, governments — conceived as threefold yet unified. In the source sentence the full tripartite range of human motive is play, competition (or display), and purpose: three co-present springs of action that a reductive purposive psychology collapses into one. To allow the full tripartite range is to restore the other two-thirds of humanity to stylistic analysis.

The adjective is formal and chiefly attributive, preferred in law, diplomacy, theology, biology, and theory where triplicity must be exact. A tripartite agreement binds three parties; a tripartite soul (Plato) comprises reason, spirit, appetite; a tripartite brain (MacLean) comprises reptilian, limbic, neocortical layers; a tripartite leaf is cleft into three. Unlike casual three-part or threefold, tripartite implies principled, often symmetrical division, frequently with institutional or doctrinal weight.

  • Compositional sense: made up of three parts — tripartite structure, tripartite classification. Neutral and descriptive, emphasising completeness of the three.
  • Divisional sense: split or cleft into three — tripartite leaf, tripartite division of powers. Emphasises the act or result of three-way partition.
  • Contractual-diplomatic sense: involving three parties — tripartite pact, tripartite talks, tripartite commission. Here tripartite is quasi-legal, entailing mutual obligations among three signatories, as in the 1936 Tripartite Pact negotiations or ILO tripartism (governments, employers, workers).
  • Psychological-anthropological sense (source sense): positing three coexistent motives or faculties — tripartite soul, tripartite motive, tripartite self. Bridge from theology and philosophy (Augustine's memory-understanding-will; Freud's id-ego-superego as a later tripartite model) to behavioural stylistics: play, competition, purpose as irreducible trio.
  • Biological-botanical sense: technical descriptor for organisms or organs divided into three — tripartite placenta, tripartite thorax. Precise, non-metaphorical, often diagnostic.
  • Grammatical profile: non-gradable attributive adjective (one does not say *very tripartite); no adverbial *tripartitely in normal use; nominalisation tripartition for the act of dividing into three; collocates with agreement, treaty, division, soul, nature, scheme, system; register elevated and institutional, lending authority to threefold claims.

Its rhetorical power lies in legitimising threeness against both monism and dualism: to call motives tripartite is to insist that two is too few and four too many, that human action needs exactly play, contest, and purpose to be intelligible. The word thus does philosophical work by counting.

Diplomatic history illustrates the contractual force: a bilateral pact balances two interests, but a tripartite pact must triangulate three, creating mediating, chairing, and coalition dynamics absent from simpler bargains. The word therefore signals not mere counting but political geometry. To mislabel a three-part anthology as tripartite is harmless; to mislabel a bilateral deal as tripartite is a legal error, underscoring that the adjective carries enforceable weight about who is bound and how shares are parted.
