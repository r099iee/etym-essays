• foliage: Similar because foliage is the collective word for the leaves of plants, leaves en masse; differs because foliage is always a mass noun and never names a single sheet, a metal leaf or a page.
  • fronds: Similar because fronds are the leaf-like organs of ferns and palms, shaped like huge divided leaves; differs because fronds are botanically distinct from true leaves and the word never leaves the plant world.
  • needles: Similar because conifer needles are the leaves of pines, firs and spruces; differs because needles are a specialised botanical subset, and the word also means sewing and syringe implements with no flat-sheet extension.
  • blades: Similar because grass blades are long narrow leaves, and blade and leaf overlap on lawns; differs because blade chiefly names the cutting edge of a tool or sword, and its leaf-usage is restricted to grasses.
  • pages: Similar because a book leaf is a sheet and a page is one side of that sheet — near synonyms in the library; differs because page is only the reading-unit sense, while leaf also carries the botanical, metallic and table senses.
  • sheets: Similar because thin sheets of metal or paper are the modern everyday words for what gold leaves are; differs because sheets are generic flat layers of anything and lack the botanical root and the book-leaf two-sided sense.
  • foils: Similar because foil is another word for thin metal laminae (gold foil, tinfoil); differs because foil is a yet thinner film used for packaging and decoration, while leaf implies a stiffer craft-grade sheet.
  • laminae: Similar because laminae are thin plates or layers, the technical term for leaves of metal, rock or bone; differs because laminae is a scientific register word that never means a plant part.
  • departs / quits: Similar because for the verb leaves ("he leaves"), departs and quits are the closest synonyms; differs because departs is formal and intransitive, quits implies resignation or giving up, while leaves is the neutral all-purpose verb of English.
