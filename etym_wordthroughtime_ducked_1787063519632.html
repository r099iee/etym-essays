- Old English (c. 10th-11th c.) - The verbal base *dūcan ("to dive, plunge, dip") existed alongside the noun *dūce ("duck, the diving bird"), denoting rapid water immersion.
- Middle English (c. 12th-14th c.) - Recorded as douken or duken (past doukede / ducked), describing the physical plunging of people or objects into water, including baptismal and punitive dunking.
- Early Modern English (c. 16th-17th c.) - Expanded in Elizabethan fencing, military manuals, and theatre to describe quickly dropping the head or upper body to evade swords, arrows, and blows.
- 18th-19th c. - Widely recognized through the legal punishment of the "ducking stool" and adopted into sports terminology (boxing and cricket) for evading high balls or punches.
- 20th-21st c. - Pervasive in colloquial English for behavioral avoidance ("ducked the draft," "ducked the press"), aviation maneuvers, and casual social departures ("ducked out early").
