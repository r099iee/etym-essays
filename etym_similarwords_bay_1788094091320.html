• Gulf: Similar because both are indentations of the sea; differs because a gulf is everywhere larger and deeper — a bay can be modest, while a gulf swallows whole coasts (the Gulf of Mexico dwarfs any bay).
  • Cove: Similar because both are sheltered coastal indentations; differs because a cove is small, round, and often rock-fringed, while a bay is broad and open at the mouth.
  • Inlet: Similar because both are recesses of water into land; differs because an inlet is narrow and long, a mere opening of the sea, without the width or anchorage implied by a bay.
  • Bight: Similar because both are curves of coastline; differs because a bight is a wide, shallow, gentle curve with no strong shelter (e.g. the Great Australian Bight), while a bay is more sharply indented and protective.
  • Sound: Similar because both are coastal water bodies linked to the open sea; differs because a sound is a long, broad channel (often between island and mainland), a passage rather than a pocket of shelter.
  • Harbour / Harbor: Similar because both offer sheltered water for shipping; differs because a harbour is water shaped or improved for anchorage and refuge (often with works and docks), while a bay is a natural geographic shape that may or may not harbour anything.
  • Lagoon: Similar because both are sheltered bodies of coastal water; differs because a lagoon is shallow and enclosed behind a barrier (reef or spit), with little exchange with the sea; a bay stays open to it.
  • Estuary: Similar because both are coastal water bodies where river meets sea; differs because an estuary is defined by the mixing of river and tidal water, tapering inland, while a bay is a shape of the coastline itself and may have no river at all.
  • Fjord / Firth: Similar because both are long sea inlets; differs because a fjord is a steep-sided, glacier-carved inlet and a firth a Scottish estuary-like inlet; neither is the broad, rounded curve of a bay, and both have strong regional colour.
  • Ria: Similar because both are drowned river valleys of the coast; differs because ria is a technical term for a specific formation (a river valley flooded by the sea) used only in geomorphology, whereas bay is the general everyday word.
  • Arm (of the sea): Similar because both name a branch of the sea reaching into land; differs because "arm" stresses the narrow, elongated reach (like an outstretched limb), while bay stresses the rounded, sheltered pocket.
  • Creek: Similar because both can denote tidal water running inland; differs because in American English a creek is a narrow tidal inlet (or small stream), and in British English a creek is a small coastal inlet — in either case far smaller than a bay; the words never interchange for a large water body.
  • Roadstead / Roads: Similar because both are sheltered anchorages; differs because a roadstead is a place of anchorage offshore, not a shape of the coast at all, while a bay is the land-shape itself.
  • Embayment: Similar because both name an indentation; differs because embayment is the technical or process word (the bending of coastline into a bay, or the result of that bending), used in geology and hydrography, never in ordinary prose.
