PIE Roots (reconstructed, c. 3500 BCE)
*n̥- (not) + *neḱ- / *noḱ- (death, mortal injury)
‘un-’ + ‘to perish, inflict destruction’
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*nok-ē-
‘to cause injury, harm’
↓
Classical Latin Verb (c. 3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE)
nocēre
‘to harm, injure, inflict damage’
↓
Classical Latin Adjective (c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE)
innocuus
‘harmless, doing no injury; inoffensive’
↓
Renaissance / Early Modern English (late 16th c., c. 1590–1600)
innocuous
‘harmless, producing no ill effect’
↓
Modern English (current)
innocuous
‘not harmful or offensive; benign, toothless’

Note: The Latin base nocēre derives from the Indo-European root of violent death and destruction (*neḱ-*, yielding Greek nekros "corpse" and Latin nex "violent death"). Prefixed with the privative in- ("not"), Latin innocuus negated physical and moral injury. The English adjectival suffix -ous was appended to harmonize the Latin nominal stem ending in -uus.
