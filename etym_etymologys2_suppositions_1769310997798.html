1. PIE *upo- "under, beneath" (reconstructed; source of Latin sub-) + PIE *sei- "to put, let, cast" (reconstructed, provisional) — the root behind Latin sinere. 2. Latin sinere "to let, allow, put, leave" (attested). 3. Latin ponere "to place, put, set" — contracted from *posinere (po- "away" + sinere "to put") (attested from the 3rd c. BCE). 4. Latin sub- "under" + ponere → supponere "to place under, put beneath; to substitute" (attested 1st c. BCE; Cicero, Lucretius). 5. Latin suppositio "a placing under, substitution; hypothesis" (attested; used in Late Latin as the calque of Greek hypothesis). 6. Old French supposition "hypothesis, assumption, substitution" (13th c.). 7. Middle English supposition "assumption, hypothesis" (borrowed via Old French; 15th c.). 8. English supposition → plural noun SUPPOSITIONS. Debate / nuance notes: - The PIE root of sinere/ponere is reconstructed only provisionally; the Italic forms are securely attested, but the deeper equation (often given as *sei-) is uncertain. - Suppositio is the Latin calque of Greek hypothesis ("a placing under"). The "standing" idea sometimes attached to "hypothesis" belongs to the Greek side's thesis ("placing", from PIE *dʰeh₁- "to put"), NOT to a PIE *stā- "to stand" root; the Latin chain is built entirely on ponere "to place". - In medieval Scholastic logic, suppositio became a technical term for how terms "stand for" things (supposition theory); this technical sense preceded and shaped the ordinary modern sense.
