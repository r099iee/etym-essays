Derivation chain for perch (base of perched): 1. Latin pertica 'a long staff, pole, rod' (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus; a pole used for measuring and for supporting things, a rod or staff; the origin of pertica is uncertain - no secure Indo-European etymology has been established, and modern scholarship (de Vaan) treats the word as of unknown, possibly non-Italic origin). 2. Old French perche 'a pole, staff; a bird's perch' (9th-14th c.; a direct continuation of Latin pertica in Gallo-Romance, meaning both the measuring rod and the horizontal bar on which birds rest). 3. Middle English perche 'a pole; a bird's roost; a raised seat' (13th c.; borrowed from Old French after the Norman Conquest, recorded as a word of the farmyard and the falconer's mews). 4. Middle English perchen 'to set or rest on a perch' (verb, c. 1300; a denominative verb formed from the noun, first used of fowls and falcons settling on their bar). 5. Modern English perch 'to alight and rest on a high, narrow support; to sit on a raised seat; to be set high on anything' (16th c. onward), with its regular weak past tense and past participle perched (formed with the -ed suffix). Note on the debate: the Latin noun pertica has no securely reconstructed PIE ancestor; proposed connections to a root meaning 'pole, rod' or 'to stretch' are speculative, and the OED and de Vaan both classify the origin as uncertain. The chain through Old French is nonetheless firm, since perche is the regular Romance reflex of pertica. The homograph perch 'a freshwater fish' (Latin perca, from Greek perke 'spotted fish') is a separate word and has no connection with this chain.
