- Old English (c. 800–1100) - stemn / stefn denoted the sturdy wooden trunk of a tree and the massive upright timbers forming the prow of an Anglo-Saxon warship; viewed with respect as a symbol of structural strength and maritime endurance.
- Middle English (c. 1150–1400) - Expanded to botanical stalks, genealogical family lineages, and musical note uprights; absorbed the distinct Scandinavian loanword stemmen ("to dam, stop"), creating the twin homographic verb senses of "to stop/curb" and later "to branch from".
- Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700) - Idiomatic maritime expressions like "from stem to stern" entered common English literature; grammatical and linguistic scholars adopted stem to denote the invariant morphological base of inflected words.
- 18th to 19th Century - Industrial and glassware terminology adopted stem for wineglass necks, clockwork winding pins, and pipe mouthpieces; the figurative phrasal verb "to stem from" solidified in educated philosophical prose to denote causality.
- 20th to 21st Century - Advanced medicine established "brainstem" and revolutionized biology with "stem cells"; in sports, alpine skiing codified the "stem turn", maintaining the word's vast polysemic vitality in contemporary speech.
