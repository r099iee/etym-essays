Origin uncertain — the direct etymon is not securely attested; the chain below follows the leading scholarly hypothesis (OED, Etymonline) and marks the conjectural link.
PIE: *skeh₁i- / *skei- "to cut, split, separate" (attested: Greek skhízō "I split", Latin scindō "I split, cut", Sanskrit chinátti "he cuts off", Lithuanian skíesti "to separate").
Proto-Germanic: *skīd- "a split piece, billet" and *skībō "a slice, a splinter" — nominal formations on the cutting root.
Old Norse: skífa "a slice" — the Scandinavian reflex of *skībō; related Germanic forms include Old English scīd "a piece of split wood" (dialectal English shide), Middle Dutch schive/schiver "slice, splinter."
Conjectural stage — Middle Dutch schiver or a Scandinavian equivalent (perhaps an unattested *skefra-): a borrowing of a word meaning "splinter, slice" into English; the exact donor form and donor language are NOT attested and this stage is the disputed link in the chain.
Middle English (c. 1400): skevere / skewer "a skewer for roasting meat" — a wooden pin for fastening meat, first recorded c. 1400.
Modern English: skewer — spelling and pronunciation later remodelled by association with the unrelated verb skew "to turn aside" (from Old North French eschiuer "to shun, avoid"); the association is secondary and does not affect the meaning.
Modern English (noun and verb): SKEWER — the cooking pin; the verb "to skewer" (17th c.) "to pierce with a skewer," and the figurative sense (19th–20th c.) "to attack or demolish with a pointed criticism."
NOTE ON DEBATE: OED labels the origin of skewer "obscure/uncertain." The connection to the *skei- "cut, split" family via a Dutch or Scandinavian word for "splinter" is the most widely repeated hypothesis, but no direct donor form is attested, and an independent Germanic creation cannot be ruled out. The association with the verb skew (as in the mnemonic "skew the meat") is folk-etymological: skewer is not built on skew.
