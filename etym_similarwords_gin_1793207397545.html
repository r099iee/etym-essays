• Genever: Similar because genever is gin’s Dutch parent — malted, junipery, often aged; differs because genever drinks like whisky’s cousin (neat, cool), while modern gin is a clean distillate built for mixing.
  • Vodka: Similar because both are clear neutral white spirits for cocktails; differs because vodka is prized for flavourlessness, while gin is prized for the opposite — its piney botanical signature.
  • Whisky: Similar because both are grain spirits with national mythologies and cocktail roles; differs because whisky takes character from oak and age, while gin takes it from juniper and the still, unaged.
  • Rum: Similar because both were cheap colonial-era spirits of the poor and the navy; differs because rum is sugar-cane, dark or golden, and sweet, while gin is grain, clear, and resinously dry.
  • Brandy: Similar because both are distilled spirits with medicinal and morale-boosting histories; differs because brandy is distilled wine, aged and warming, while gin is flavoured grain spirit, unaged and bracing.
  • Schnapps: Similar because both are clear flavoured spirits knocked back across northern Europe; differs because schnapps is the German-Scandinavian fruit or herbal shot, while gin is specifically the juniper-botanical English-Dutch line.
  • Moonshine: Similar because both name cheap raw spirits of the desperate; differs because moonshine is illicitly distilled unaged whisky, while gin — however cheap — is a defined botanical recipe, legal since the Gin Acts.
  • Rotgut: Similar because Hogarth-era gin literally was rotgut, the gut-rotting cheapest bottle; differs because rotgut is pure slang for any vile liquor, while gin is the actual spirit, foul or fine.
  • Spirits: Similar because gin is the archetypal white spirit of the cocktail shelf; differs because spirits is the whole distilled family (whisky, rum, vodka), while gin is one juniper-flavoured member.
