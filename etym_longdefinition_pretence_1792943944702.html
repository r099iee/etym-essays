Pretence is the act or habit of making something appear what it is not: a false show, a feigned excuse, a claim advanced to hide the true motive. Conspirators keep up a pretence; lovers make no pretence of indifference; invalids abandon all pretence of working — in each case a face is presented to the world while the truth waits behind it.

In the source sentence the word does conspiratorial duty: one plotter must stay behind, manage inquisitive folk, and keep up as long as possible the pretence that nothing is amiss — the everyday camouflage of normality stretched over a secret enterprise, maintained smile by smile until it can be maintained no longer.

Core meaning in detail: Pretence covers the full theatre of deception, from the trivially polite (a pretence of interest at a dull dinner) to the criminally grave (a pretence of authority to extract money). Crucially it names the show rather than the lie: one can keep up a pretence by silence, by costume, by routine — by simply behaving as though all were ordinary. The law knows it as false pretences, the swindler's stock in trade; the novelist knows it as the air his characters breathe.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A false display to deceive (primary modern sense): the conspirator's pretence of normality in our sentence. The bridge is Latin praetendere, to stretch forth before — a screen held up in front of the truth.
  • A feigned excuse or pretext (from 16th c.): under pretence of friendship, on the pretence of illness. The bridge narrows the screen to a stated reason: the excuse stretched forth to cover the motive.
  • A claim to distinction, often dismissed (usually plural): to have no pretensions to learning; literary pretences. The bridge runs from putting forth a show to putting forth a claim — assertion as display.
  • False pretences in law (technical): obtaining money by false pretences — fraud by misrepresentation. The bridge carries the everyday show into the statute book, where the show becomes the crime.
  • Mere display without reality (literary): a life of empty pretence. The bridge generalises: the habit of showing forth hollows until nothing stands behind the screen.
  • An insincere profession of feeling (social): a pretence of grief, of surprise, of delight. The bridge is theatrical: emotions performed like lines, with the heart offstage.
  • A semblance kept for politeness (social): a pretence of listening, of agreement, of good cheer at dull gatherings. The bridge is courtesy as camouflage: society runs on small screens politely held, and no one calls them lies.

Register and connotation: pretence is the novelist's and the moralist's word — cool, observant, faintly weary. It condemns less fiercely than deceit yet sees further: where deceit names the lie, pretence names the whole staging. Note the spelling: British pretence keeps -ce for the noun beside verbal pretend, while American usage writes pretense.
