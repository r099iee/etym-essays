From “old” to “lordly”. The story of SURLY begins, improbably, with a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “old”. The root *sen- produced Latin senex, “old man”, and its comparative senior, “older; of greater age or rank”. It was one of the most widespread roots of the family, attested in Greek hénos “old”, Sanskrit sána- “old, ancient”, Celtic sen and Welsh hen “old”, and Lithuanian sėnas “old”. In Latin the root produced not only senex and senior but senātus, “the council of elders” — our senate — and senīlis, “old, aged”, our senile. The key step for our word is the comparative senior: from meaning “older” it came to mean “of higher rank”, since in traditional societies age and authority were the same thing. The title of the master. In the Romance world the contracted form of senior — Vulgar Latin *seior — became an ordinary title of respect: Old French sire (whence English sire, used of kings and later of fathers and stallions), Spanish señor, Italian signore, Portuguese senhor, and French sieur and monsieur (“my lord”). Anglo-Norman and Middle English adopted sir as a knightly and polite title of address — the word of lords, of knights, and finally of any man addressed with respect. From that noun, Middle English formed the adjective sirly (also spelled syrly), which originally meant “lordly”, “majestic”, or “befitting a gentleman of rank” — a descriptor of elevated bearing rather than of temper. Pejoration: how “lordly” became “rude”. As social attitudes shifted, “lordly” behaviour as seen from below was re-read as haughty, imperious, and ungracious. Between the later Middle Ages and the sixteenth century, sirly slid from grandeur toward arrogance and then toward rude, overbearing ill humour. This is one of English’s clearest cases of pejoration — a word of honour turned into a word of insult — and it happened because the perspective that mattered was not the lord’s but the servant’s:
  • 14th century — lordly, majestic: sirly is a status word, capable of positive grandeur or mild hauteur depending on context; it describes the dignity of a sir.
  • Late Middle English — early 16th century — haughty, imperious, overbearing: the lordly bearing is increasingly read as social offence; speakers begin to hear contempt rather than dignity in the elevated manner.
  • 16th–17th centuries — surly, churlish, uncivil: the spelling and pronunciation settle as surly, the positive “majestic” reading fades, and the word becomes a vice of manner; early modern drama and moral literature treat the surly man as churlish, uncivil, and forbiddingly bad-tempered.
Why the spelling changed. The shift from sirly to surly is a small but significant piece of the story. By the sixteenth century the pronunciation of the first syllable had relaxed, and the spelling surly stabilized, distancing the adjective from the everyday noun sir so completely that modern speakers no longer feel the etymological link. The separation was reinforced by meaning: a surly man is precisely someone who does not deserve the courtesy of “sir”, so the linguistic divorce between the title and the adjective mirrors the social judgement the word makes. Modern life of the word. In modern English surly is fully lexicalized as a temper-and-manners adjective. It applies to people, to speech, to looks, and, by a transferred sense, to weather and skies that feel unfriendly or threatening — a surly sea or a surly sky is heavy and forbidding, as though nature itself refused the courtesy of fine weather. It no longer means “noble” in any positive sense; any residual lordliness is ironic. The register remains slightly literary: journalists and novelists favour it for compact character judgement — as the card’s sentence does, where a single adjective condemns the guard who “remained surly and refused to return our greetings.” The history thus runs from age-based rank (*sen- → senior → sire/sir) through a status adjective (sirly) to a moralized demeanour (surly) — a miniature social history of how prestige can curdle into rudeness when seen from below.
