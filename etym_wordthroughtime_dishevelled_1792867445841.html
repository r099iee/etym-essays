- Old French — unbinding the hair: descheveler loosens the coif and lets the locks stream, the mark of grief or haste.
- 14th c. — mourning enters English: discheveled describes widows and the sorrowing bare-headed, disorder as ritual language.
- 16th–17th c. — poets generalise it: winds dishevel trees, passion dishevels dress, and heroes meet us noble in ruin.
- 18th–19th c. — the novelist's adjective: dishevelled heroines and cottages fill Gothic and Victorian pages, always pitied, never despised.
- 20th c. — spelling settles the Channel: British dishevelled keeps two l's while American disheveled keeps one, a tiny orthographic parting.
- Today — dignified ruin: from red carpets to foreclosed houses, dishevelled still mourns a better order lost rather than scolding the mess.
