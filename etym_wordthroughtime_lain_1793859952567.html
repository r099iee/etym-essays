- 8th–11th Century (Old English *ġeleġen*): Martial repose and solemn recumbency - integral to heroic Anglo-Saxon poetry (such as *Beowulf*), describing slain warriors lying motionless upon the blood-stained earth or gold lying buried in the dragon's barrow.
- 12th–15th Century (Middle English *i-lein / layne*): Broadened bodily rest, romance, and land management - commonly attested in Middle English verse (Chaucer, *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*) describing sleeping lovers, sickbeds, and agricultural land lying fallow.
- 16th–17th Century (Early Modern English *laine / lain*): Elevated biblical and literary currency - achieved canonical dignity in the King James Bible (*"Though ye have lien among the pots"*, Psalm 68:13) and Shakespeare's sonnets and tragedies.
- 18th–19th Century (The Prescriptive Era): The grammatical battleground - eighteenth-century prescriptivists (Robert Lowth, Lindley Murray) codified the strict grammatical distinction between intransitive *lie / lay / lain* and transitive *lay / laid / laid*, transforming the correct usage of *lain* into a supreme shibboleth of educated prose.
- 20th–21st Century: Literary preservation and high narrative register - famously preserved in classic fantasy and literary prose (notably J.R.R. Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings*), while colloquially receding in casual speech where it is frequently displaced by the transitive past participle *laid*.
