Tullia is a Latin feminine proper name, the feminine form of the Roman family name Tullius. Its most famous bearer is Tullia Ciceronis (c. 79–45 BCE), the only daughter of Marcus Tullius Cicero and his wife Terentia. In the source the name denotes her: Cicero’s “inordinate love of his daughter Tullia” is one of the three attachments by which the biographer characterizes him, and the following clause — that she “never seemed to have merited it particularly” — turns her into a silent test of her father’s emotional balance.

The woman

  • Family and birth. Tullia was born around 79 BCE, before her father’s consulship, and was the elder of his two children; her brother Marcus was born in 65 BCE. The household was that of a rising equestrian orator from Arpinum, and her mother Terentia was a woman of property and strong opinions who features prominently in Cicero’s correspondence.
  • Education and affection. Cicero addressed her as Tulliola, “little Tullia”, and called her his delight; the family letters show a father who treated her as a trusted member of the household’s correspondence. She grew up inside the most articulate household in Rome, and her marriages were negotiated with the same care as political alliances.
  • Three marriages. She was married first, about 63 BCE, to Gaius Calpurnius Piso Frugi, a young aristocrat of good family, who died around 57 BCE. She then married Furius Crassipes, a match that ended in divorce about 51 BCE. In 50 BCE she married Publius Cornelius Dolabella, a patrician of brilliant and unreliable character; Cicero mistrusted him, but the marriage went forward, and it ended in divorce in 46 BCE after Dolabella’s infidelities.
  • Death. In January 45 BCE Tullia gave birth to a son by Dolabella; the child died soon afterwards, and she herself died in February 45, aged about thirty-four. Cicero was in Campania at the time. He withdrew to his villa at Astura, wrote the Consolatio — a work addressed to himself and now lost — and planned a shrine (fanum) in her memory, a project that suggests how far grief had carried him.
  • Children. A son by Piso died in infancy; the son by Dolabella survived his mother only briefly. Tullia left no direct descendants, and her memory survives entirely through her father’s letters and the works written after her death.

Why she matters in the reading

The biographer’s aside about merit is a rhetorical judgement, not a documented fact. What the letters show is a real and often anxious affection, complicated by disagreements over her marriages and by her own strong will: Tullia pressed for the match with Dolabella, and she was not a passive instrument of her father’s plans. Her death devastated Cicero and became one of the great set pieces of his biography, retold by Plutarch and by every later Life of Cicero. The name in the source therefore carries a double charge — the daughter as an object of love, and the daughter as the occasion of a grief that bordered on the extravagant, which is exactly what “inordinate” is doing in the sentence.

The name in Roman society

  • Feminine of the nomen. Roman women normally bore the feminine form of their father’s clan name, with no praenomen: Tullius gave Tullia, as Julius gave Julia and Cornelius gave Cornelia.
  • Sisters and marriage. Sisters of the same name were distinguished as Maior and Minor; a woman kept her own nomen after marriage, so Tullia remains Tullia and never becomes Dolabella.
  • The gens. The Tullii were a plebeian clan, and Cicero’s branch, the Cicerones, had never held curule office before him; his consulship of 63 BCE made him a novus homo. The name Tullia therefore signalled respectable but not aristocratic birth.
  • Legendary namesakes. Livy’s Tullia Major and Tullia Minor, daughters of King Servius Tullius, gave the name a very different association: the younger Tullia is the archetypal unnatural daughter, who urged her husband to seize the throne and drove her chariot over her father’s corpse.

Register and use today

As a proper noun, Tullia has no plural and no common-noun sense. In English it is pronounced /ˈtʌliə/ and belongs to classical scholarship, to histories of the late Republic and to Italian naming practice, where it survives as a given name. Scholarly writing often uses the fuller form Tullia Ciceronis to distinguish Cicero’s daughter from the legendary Tullias.
