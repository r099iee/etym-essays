unimpressionable is an adjective meaning not easily affected, influenced, moved, or swayed by external impressions, sentiment, or emotional appeal — resistant to being shaped by feeling, opinion, fashion, or persuasion. It is the negative counterpart of impressionable, the word for a mind or character that takes a stamp from its environment like soft wax under a seal. The unimpressionable person, by contrast, is the hardened wax: nothing presses in, no mark is left, no current of feeling or opinion moves them. The word is built on the Latin-rooted metaphor, made famous by the empiricist philosophers, that the mind is a tablet or sheet of wax on which experience writes; an unimpressionable mind is one on which nothing is written, or whose surface has set too hard to receive a stamp.

In the literary context of this card the word does its work as a character portrait. It describes James Mill, the father, as a man of 'dry light' — the Baconian image of cool, unclouded reason — so emotionally austere, rigid, and impervious that his own son, John Stuart Mill, was raised without warmth and reacted, as the passage says, with the 'necessary and inevitable' exaggeration of sentiment. The irony deepens when we recall that James Mill was himself an associationist psychologist who taught that the mind is wholly built up from impressions; calling him unimpressionable is to say that his mind received no stamp from the world, that the wax was already hard. The word here points less at insensitivity to others than at a willed, stoic imperviousness to every emotional current — a character sealed against influence.

The full range of senses and uses may be catalogued:

  • Of persons and characters (the core sense): not swayed by emotion, persuasion, or example; firm, rigid, or stoic; as of James Mill, a nature sealed against influence.
  • Of minds in psychological and philosophical writing: a mind that receives no impressions, or on which experience leaves no lasting mark; in associationist psychology (Hartley, James Mill), the very antithesis of the tabula rasa doctrine.
  • Of audiences, readerships, and publics: an 'unimpressionable audience' — one unmoved by rhetoric, sentiment, or aesthetic effect; the word transfers from individual character to collective response.
  • Aesthetic and critical use: a spectator or critic proof against the emotional appeal of a work of art; not merely unemotional but genuinely unresponsive to its intended effect.
  • Negative-prefix register: like other un- + adjective formations, the word names an absence or privation — here the absence of the capacity to be impressed — rather than an active hardness; it is formal, literary, and comparatively rare in ordinary speech, where 'thick-skinned', 'unmoved', or 'hardened' are more usual.

unimpressionable must be distinguished from its neighbours. Unemotional states the bare absence of strong feeling but says nothing about influence. Impassive describes an outward blankness of expression that may conceal inner feeling, whereas the unimpressionable person is not merely concealed but genuinely unaffected. Stoical names a virtue — endurance of pain or misfortune without complaint — and presupposes suffering that is calmly borne; the unimpressionable person may feel no suffering at all. Callous adds a moral note of hardness toward others' distress, implying cruelty or neglect, which the context does not assert of James Mill. Impervious and insusceptible are near synonyms of mechanical resistance, usually followed by a preposition (impervious to criticism, insusceptible to suggestion), and they lack the specific mental-image of the mind that will not take a stamp. Uninfluenced is factual and passive — simply not having been affected on a given occasion — whereas unimpressionable names a permanent disposition of character.
