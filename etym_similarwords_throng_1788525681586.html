• crowd: Similar because both denote a large collection of people gathered in one place; differs because crowd is the generic, neutral term that does not necessarily imply the dense physical compression, eager dynamic movement, or poetic register inherent to throng.
  • mob: Similar because both describe a large, tightly massed group of human beings; differs because a mob specifically implies an unruly, chaotic, emotionally volatile, or aggressive crowd motivated by anger or lawlessness.
  • multitude: Similar because both indicate an immense quantity of individuals; differs because multitude is an elevated, often Biblical or philosophical noun emphasizing sheer vast numerical quantity rather than physical compression or active movement.
  • horde: Similar because both refer to massive gatherings; differs because horde carries historical connotations of nomadic, predatory, or barbarian armies sweeping across territory, often imparting a slightly menacing or chaotic tone.
  • swarm: Similar because both describe dense, shifting masses converging upon a space; differs because swarm evokes entomological imagery (insects, bees), implying erratic, buzzing, multi-directional motion.
  • crush: Similar because both emphasize physical contact and spatial restriction; differs because crush focuses almost entirely on the dangerous, suffocating pressure and physical constraint of an overcrowded enclosure.
  • flock: Similar because both describe groups congregating together; differs because flock derives from pastoral ovine and avian imagery, suggesting docile, collective following rather than energetic urban pressing.
  • congregate: Similar because both convey the act of assembling in large numbers; differs because congregate is a formal, Latinate verb describing a deliberate, orderly coming together, often for religious, civic, or institutional purposes.
  • surge: Similar because both describe dynamic collective human motion; differs because surge is a hydraulic metaphor emphasizing a powerful, wave-like forward rush rather than steady dense gathering.
  • press: Similar because both historically signify physical squeezing and crowding; differs because press emphasizes the mechanical force and tactile contact of bodies bearing down upon one another.
