An English word with no parents. Unlike most members of the vocabulary, throb cannot be traced back through a chain of ancestral languages. It appears in Middle English around 1400 as throbbe(n), "to beat, to palpitate", and every etymological dictionary, from the Oxford English Dictionary on, records its origin as obscure. The most widely accepted explanation is that the word is echoic - sound-symbolic - formed in English to imitate the thing it names: a dull, repeated, insistent beat. The echoic cluster. Throb belongs to a native English family of th- words for blows and impacts: thud, thump, thwack, thrash (from Old English therscan, "to thresh"), and the archaic thyddan, "to thrust, strike". In these words the initial th- cluster suggests a solid, percussive contact, and the short vowel followed by a voiced stop carries the impact home. Throb fits the pattern exactly: the vowels and consonants themselves suggest a pulse - a short, dull beat that is then voiced and released. The doubled consonant of the early spellings (throbbe, throbbing) is typical of such expressive coinages, which often double a consonant to suggest intensity or repetition. There is no attested Indo-European root beneath the word, and modern scholarship does not attempt to force one; throb is classed simply as of unknown, probably imitative, origin. This is worth emphasising because the automatic assumption that every English word has a Latin or Greek pedigree fails here: throb is one of those rare and precious words the language made for itself out of the sound of the world. From pulse to pain. Once coined, the word grew a complete English family of its own. The verb, meaning "to beat strongly and steadily", was used first of the heart and then of any body part that pulses with pain - a wounded limb throbs, a head throbs - because the inflamed tissue beats in time with the circulation. This is the oldest extension of the sense, and it gives English its stock phrase a throbbing pain, a phrase that unites the two halves of the word's experience: the pain is felt as rhythm. From the body the word extended outward to anything that pulses rhythmically: the throb of an engine, of a drum, of music, of the bass line of a dance, of the life of a city. In each extension the central image - a steady, insistent, living beat - is preserved. The noun. The noun throb is attested from the 1570s, and it quickly developed the two lives it still has: a concrete one - "a pulsation, especially of pain" - and a figurative one - "a pulse of emotion or vitality", as in a throb of joy or a throb of nostalgia. Seventeenth-century poetry made the noun a favourite word for the beating heart itself, and the compound heart-throb - literally "a beat of the heart" - was used in the 16th-17th centuries for the pulse of the heart itself. The same compound was later reborn in twentieth-century celebrity culture as heartthrob - first in the 1950s and fully in the 1960s - to name the film star or pop singer whose face makes the heart beat faster. The 1960s heartthrob is a small cultural event in itself: the word's leap from anatomy to celebrity fandom shows how a sound-symbolic word of the heart's pulse could become the language of popular desire. The word in literary and prose style. Because throb names a living, felt rhythm, writers of style and criticism have often reached for it to describe the vitality of language. In the present passage, the claim that the style and rhythm of English prose displayed "a throb of continuity over a millennium and more" turns a thousand years of literary history into the steady heartbeat of a living organism: the prose tradition is granted life, warmth and constant renewal. The word was well chosen for a passage about The Movement of English Prose - a book about rhythm - because a throb is itself a rhythm, and rhythm, in this metaphor, is the heartbeat of a language. Notice also that the passage deliberately prefers throb to the neutral "continuation": it is not merely that English prose continued for a thousand years, but that it went on living, beating, renewing itself, generation after generation. Conclusion. Throb is a rare thing among English words: a native coinage with no ancestors, formed onomatopoeically to imitate a beat, that went on to build a whole family of senses - the pulse of a heart, the ache of a wound, the rhythm of a machine, the vitality of a city, and the continuity of a literary tradition. Its origin is a reminder that not every English word comes from Latin, Greek or Germanic inheritance; some are simply the language shaping itself to the sound of the world, and then discovering that the sound can carry the deepest meanings: life, pain, joy, and the unbroken beating of a culture's heart.
