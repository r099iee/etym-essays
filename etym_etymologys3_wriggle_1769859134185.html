The evolution of wriggle is the story of a very old Germanic verb for "turning" that learned to squirm. The trail begins in the reconstructed Indo-European root *wreik- or *wreiǵ-, "to turn, twist, bend." This was a root of torsion — of turning things around, twisting fibres, bending what was straight. Its Germanic offspring were numerous and all retained the turning image: wring means to twist forcibly (to wring a cloth, to wring a neck); wrong originally meant "twisted, crooked," and only later "unjust, incorrect," because what is crooked is not right; and a whole family of wrig-, wrick-, wretch- words across the Germanic languages share the same twisted ancestry.

The immediate ancestor of our word is Old English wrigian, "to turn, move, go, twist, deviate." It appears in the earliest English glosses, where it translates Latin verbs of turning and going, and its meaning was simply to turn or move — without the special sinuous flavour the word has today. In Middle English the base verb appeared as wriggen, "to twist, move quickly," still a general verb of quick turning movement. Then, in the later Middle Ages, English did something characteristic: it added the frequentative suffix -len to make wrigglen. The frequentative (also seen in giggle, trample, crackle) turns a single action into a repeated, small, iterative one — and with that suffix, around 1490, the word acquired its modern identity as the specific motion of turning this way and that in quick, small, sinuous twists.

From the 16th century onward, wriggle grew in exactly the directions a turning verb would be expected to grow. It became the standard English verb for the movement of worms, eels, snakes, and other limbless creatures — creatures that can only make progress by turning their bodies sideways in waves — and from them it was applied, with a touch of comedy, to people: restless children wriggling on hard benches, dogs wriggling in pockets, prisoners of tight clothing wriggling to get out. The bodily sense was so strong that it carried the word into emotional register: a person wriggles with pleasure, embarrassment, or glee, the body itself squirming with feeling.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *wreiǵ- → Proto-Germanic *wrigjaną (the velar appears as Germanic -g-) → Old English wrigian (initial w retained; the -g- palatalized and eventually weakened) → Middle English wriggen → wrigglen (frequentative -l-) → Modern English wriggle /ɹˈɪɡəl/; the RP verb keeps the w and the short i, and the -le is the modern spelling of the frequentative suffix.
  • Semantic drift: "to turn, twist, move" (Old English wrigian) → "to twist to and fro quickly, especially of worms and eels" (Middle English wrigglen) → "to move or free oneself by twisting" → "to escape from a duty, obligation, or accusation by cunning twisting" (figurative, from the 17th century).
  • Socio-cultural factors: the figurative "wriggle out of" grew in a culture of duties and contracts — to slip out of an obligation is to twist like an eel escaping a hand, and the word still carries the mocking judgement of the person left holding the obligation; the 20th-century phrase "wriggle room" (freedom to manoeuvre) shows the same twisting image applied to negotiation.
  • Literary attestations: the OED records the verb from the late 15th century; from the 16th century it is standard in descriptions of worms, eels, and restless bodies, and from the 17th century in the figurative language of evasion; the word remains a favourite of comic and realistic prose — including the present passage, where Rover's wriggling in the pocket is both determined and comically futile.
  • Modern situation: the physical sense (worms, eels, tight clothes, confined bodies) and the figurative sense (wriggling out of trouble) both flourish, and the word carries a light, slightly amused tone that makes it a natural choice for whimsical storytelling.

Today wriggle still means what it has meant for five hundred years — to turn and twist in quick, small, sinuous movements — but it has acquired a second, purely mental life as the motion of evading responsibility. From the turning root that gave English wring and wrong, the little frequentative wriggle has become one of the language's liveliest verbs of movement and of sly escape, equally at home describing a worm on a hook and a dog determined to poke his nose out of a pocket.
