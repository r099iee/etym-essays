ONSLAUGHT descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *slak- "to strike", through the Germanic family into Dutch and thence into English. Stage 1 — PIE *slak- "to strike, smite, hit" (Pokorny; some scholars reconstruct *slā-): a western Indo-European root, fully attested only in the Germanic family. No descendant of *slak- is securely attested in Sanskrit, Hindi, Greek, or Latin. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *slahan- "to strike, smite, kill": the Germanic continuation of *slak-. Stage 3 — Germanic attestations (parallel branches of the family, not the direct line of descent): Old English slēan "to strike, to kill" (source of English slay, slain, slayer); Old Norse slá "to strike", with its deverbal noun slag "a blow, a stroke" (source of English sledge, the heavy smiting hammer, and of English slug "to strike hard", 19th century, probably from ON slá/slag), and Old Norse slátr "butchered meat, carcase", literally "what is struck down" (source of English slaughter, blended with slay in the 13th century, and of slaughterhouse, slaughterman); Old High German slahan (source of German schlagen "to strike, beat" and Schlag "a blow"). Stage 4 — Middle Dutch slāen "to strike", with the deverbal noun slag "a blow, a stroke, a smiting": the immediate stock from which the English word was taken. Stage 5 — Dutch aanslag (older aenslag) "a striking upon; an attack, onset, assault" (aan "on" + slag "blow"; in Dutch also "a levy, a tax assessment" — a "striking on" of the roll). Stage 6 — English onslaught (borrowed 17th century) "a violent onset or fierce attack, chiefly of troops or weapons": the spelling was later reshaped under the pull of slaughter, giving the -aught ending that is not Dutch. Stage 7 — English onslaught (the exact form of this card). DEBATED AND NUANCED POINTS: The root is reconstructed as *slak- by Pokorny (some scholars prefer *slā-); it is a western Indo-European root, and no Indo-Aryan descendant is attested, so the Sanskrit/Hindi branch is honestly omitted. The Dutch etymon aanslag is secure, but the modern spelling with -aught is an English reshaping under the influence of the unrelated-in-form but ultimately related-in-root word slaughter, not a historical form inherited from Dutch. The whole family — English slay, slaughter, sledge, slug, German schlagen, Dutch slaan — goes back to PIE *slak- "to strike".
