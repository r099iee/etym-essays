PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*deru- / *drew-
"to be firm, solid, steadfast; tree, oak"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*traustą
"confidence, trust, security, alliance"
↓
Old Norse (c. 9th c. CE)
traust
"confidence, help, protection, firm reliance"
↓
Middle English (c. 1200)
trust / trusten
"faith, confidence; to rely upon, believe"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1600)
intrust / entrust
"to commit to another with confidence"
↓
Early Modern English (17th–18th c.)
intrusted
"confided, committed to stewardship"
↓
Modern English (current)
intrusted
"committed to another's care under reliance of fidelity"

Note: The word is a hybrid formation: the base is the Scandinavian noun trust (derived from Proto-Germanic *traustą, from PIE *deru-, the root of tree and true, symbolizing steadfastness as firm as an oak), combined with the Latinate prefix in- (or French en-). In 17th- and 18th-century British and American English, the spelling intrust and intrusted was overwhelmingly favored in legal, philosophical, and constitutional documents before 20th-century lexicography consolidated around entrust.
