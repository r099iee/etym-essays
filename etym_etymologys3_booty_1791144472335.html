Booty in the sense spoils of the chase or war comes from Middle Low German bute / buite meaning distribution, share, plunder, itself from a Germanic verb meaning to divide and distribute. Its journey from Hanseatic docks to English battlefields to pirate chests to modern slang illustrates how war-words become play-words.

The Germanic base is reconstructed as *but- to divide, portion, distribute, seen in Old Norse byti exchange, barter, Old High German buten to distribute, and Middle Dutch buit booty. The semantic logic is that of the war-band: after a raid, loot is laid out and divided by lot or custom; the share is the booty. Middle Low German bute in Hanseatic trade and privateering contexts meant both the act of distribution and the goods distributed, especially seized cargo. This mercantile-maritime milieu explains borrowing into French and English during the Hundred Years' War era of free companies and sea-raiding.

Old French butin meaning plunder, profit, first attested 14th century, is itself a borrowing from Germanic, not native Romance. Its presence complicates the English story: Middle English botye c. 1425 in accounts of war may come directly from Low German sailors and mercenaries or via Anglo-French butin with English suffix -y. Most etymologists, following the OED, posit Low German origin with French reinforcement, noting phonetic shape booty matches Low German bute plus English nominal -y better than French butin. Early spellings botye, b precis stabilize to booty by the 16th century with lengthened vowel.

  • Sound development: Low German u > English oo /uː/ by regular adaptation; final -e reinterpreted as -y / -ie nominal ending; stress on first syllable; plural booties rare, usually uncountable booty.
  • Military institutionalization (15th–18th c.): Booty becomes a legal-military term: rightful spoils, division of booty, booty call? No, prize law distinguishes lawful prize adjudicated by courts from mere booty seized on land. Soldiers' expectation of booty drives early-modern warfare; to play booty meant to play for shares of winnings.
  • Pirate and colonial romanticization (17th–19th c.): Buccaneers, privateers and imperial troops bring home booty; literature from ballads to Treasure Island cements booty as chests of gold, jewels and exotic goods. The chase in the source context — yearning for the booty of the chase — preserves the older hunting sense: carcass, meat and trophy as hunter's share.
  • Figurative and slang extensions (19th–present): Electoral booty, meaning spoils system offices; booty as treasure in video games; booty call as late-night sexual invitation, from booty in buttocks/person-as-treasure sense converging with spoils metaphor; hip-hop booty for buttocks, technically a separate etymon from butt but now homophonous and punningly merged, as in booty-shaking.
  • Literary attestations: Paston Letters for early war-booty; Shakespeare's Henry V for soldiers' spoils; 18th-century prize acts; 19th-century anthropology for hunter's booty; 20th-century slang dictionaries for divergent buttocks and treasure senses.

The chief nuance is the homonymy. Modern booty buttocks, pronounced identically, derives from African-American Vernacular booty / boody from butt, not from Low German plunder, but speakers constantly blend them in jokes about treasure and body as prize. Etymologists stress keeping them distinct while acknowledging convergence in phrases like booty call and booty trap where both treasure and body readings resonate. The plunder etymology itself is secure; only the precise French versus Low German mediation remains mildly debated.
