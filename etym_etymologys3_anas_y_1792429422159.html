1. Indo-European Foundations: The Negation of Resentment. The name Anasūyā is an ancient Bahuvrīhi compound built from two foundational Indo-European components. The initial element is the Proto-Indo-European negative particle *n̥- (the zero-grade syllabic nasal), which functioned as the universal privative prefix across the entire language family, giving Germanic un- (English un-), Greek a- / an- (alpha privative), Latin in-, and Sanskrit a- / an-. Before a consonant, the nasal developed into Sanskrit a-, but before an initial vowel, it preserved its nasal articulation as an-. The second element descends from the PIE root complex associated with grumbling, murmuring hostility, or envious bitterness (comparable to Avestan ā-sūyā-, "envy, malice"), which crystallized in Indo-Aryan as the verbal base asūy-.

2. Vedic Semantics: Asūyā as the Poison of the Heart. In Vedic Sanskrit literature, asūyā was not merely a casual emotional irritation, but a grave ethical and spiritual defect. In the Ṛgveda, the Brāhmaṇas, and early legal codes (*Dharmaśāstras*), asūyā denotes the malicious disposition to find fault in the virtues of others, to begrudge another's prosperity, and to speak ill of righteous persons. Consequently, the negation an-asūyā signified the complete eradication of pettiness, envy, and carping hostility. To possess anasūyā was to behold the world with serene, generous, and unpolluted goodwill, celebrating merit wherever it arose without feeling diminished by another's excellence.

3. The Epic Transformation: The Anchorite Matriarch of Citrakūṭa. During the epic synthesis of the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (c. 5th–3rd century BCE), this abstract moral virtue was personified in the living character of Anasūyā, the wife of sage Atri. In the epic’s poetic economy:

  • Her name serves as a deliberate dramatic foil to Queen Kaikeyī. While Kaikeyī allowed the venom of jealousy (*asūyā*)—instigated by the hunchback Mantharā—to shatter the royal family of Ayodhyā and plunge the kingdom into mourning, Anasūyā represents the pure, untainted sanctuary of the forest where envy has no dominion.
  • When Sītā enters Anasūyā’s presence, she steps out of the fractured political sphere of envy and into the tranquil realm of unconditioned love. The miraculous gifts Anasūyā bestows—the unfading floral garland, celestial garments, and sacred unguent (*aṅgarāga*)—are concrete materializations of her own uncorruptible, non-envious moral power (*satya* and *tapas*).

4. Purāṇic Redaction: Maternal Supremacy Over the Cosmos. In the middle and late classical periods (c. 4th–10th centuries CE), as Purāṇic Hinduism consolidated around temple iconography and devotional sects (*bhakti*), Anasūyā’s status underwent an extraordinary elevation. In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa and Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa, her ascetic purity was elevated above that of the cosmic gods themselves. The narrative of turning Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Śiva into babies when they attempted to test her chastity marks an extraordinary moment in world mythology: the absolute supremacy of female ethical virtue (*tapas*) over male patriarchal divine power. Her subsequent birth of the *Trimūrti* avatar Dattātreya cemented her position as the spiritual fountainhead of the major medieval monastic traditions of western and southern India.

5. Linguistic Preservation and Contemporary Significance. Throughout millennia of literary and oral recitation, the phonology of Anasūyā remained remarkably stable across all Indo-Aryan languages (yielding modern Hindi/Marathi Anasūyā or Anusūyā). In contemporary Indological scholarship, particularly in feminist literary studies of the epics, Anasūyā has received intensive re-evaluation: far from representing passive, submissive domesticity, she is recognized as a formidable forest ascetic who commanded elemental nature, generated sacred rivers, humbled supreme deities, and granted political protection and divine sovereignty to Sītā through her profound spiritual wisdom.
