The history of indulgence is, in miniature, the history of European civilization: Roman law, Christian theology, the Reformation, and finally modern consumer capitalism all left their mark on this single word, which moved from imperial clemency to papal forgiveness to chocolate marketing without ever losing its core meaning of "yielding."

1. Roman Roots: Indulgentia as Imperial Clemency

The word begins with the Latin verb indulgēre, "to be kind to, to yield to, to grant, to give free play to." Its use is attested from the 2nd century BCE in the works of playwrights such as Plautus and Terence, and it became a favourite of Cicero. Roman etymology of the verb is uncertain — no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed, and the older association with dulcis "sweet" is a folk etymology — but its meaning is clear and wide: an emperor indulges his soldiers, a father indulges his children, a mind indulges a feeling. The abstract noun indulgentia named kindness and forbearance generally, but in the imperial period it acquired a precise administrative sense: the emperor's indulgentia was an act of clemency — a decree that remitted taxes, pardoned offenders, or granted special privileges to a province or city. This was the raw material out of which Christian theology would later build.

2. The Christian Transformation: From Imperial Pardon to Divine Remission

As the Roman Empire Christianized, the Church adopted the vocabulary of imperial law to structure its sacramental system. The doctrine of penance held that while confession and absolution forgave the guilt of sin, a "temporal punishment" still remained — a penalty to be paid either in this life by penance or after death in Purgatory. From this doctrine grew the medieval indulgence: a grant, made by the Pope or bishops, that remitted part or all of this temporal punishment on behalf of the Church, drawing on the "Treasury of Merit" — the infinite merits of Christ and the saints accumulated through the ages. In the High Middle Ages (11th–13th centuries) indulgences were granted for pious acts: crusades, pilgrimages, prayers, and alms. Chaucer's Pardoner, in The Canterbury Tales (c. 1380s), sells them with satirical bravado, and Chaucer's portrait already exposes the abuse that was to come.

3. Entry into English: A Purely Religious Word

English borrowed the word in the late 14th century, through Old French indulgence, and for the first centuries of its English life it meant almost exclusively the ecclesiastical remission of punishment. The Middle English period used it in sermons, confessional manuals, and legal documents about the Church. This narrow, technical meaning is the root from which everything else grew — a fact worth remembering when one calls a dessert an indulgence: the modern treat is a direct descendant of the medieval pardon.

4. The Reformation: The Word Destroyed and Reborn

The turning point of the word's history is 1517. The Papacy had begun financing grand projects — most famously the rebuilding of St. Peter's Basilica under Pope Leo X — by selling indulgences aggressively. The Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel hawked them with slogans like "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." This spectacle provoked Martin Luther to post his 95 Theses, whose fiercest articles attacked the theology and the sale of indulgences. Luther and the Protestant Reformers argued that no human grant could remit what God had not remitted, and that the sale of forgiveness corrupted the gospel. The Reformation permanently damaged the word's reputation in Protestant lands: indulgence became a byword for corruption, superstition, and spiritual greed. In England, which broke with Rome, the word lost its holy elevation in popular speech and picked up a taint of venality that survives in the Protestant memory of "selling indulgences."

5. Secularization: The 17th and 18th Centuries

As the theological scandal receded, writers secularized the word. The 17th century saw political acts called "indulgence" — most famously Charles II's Declaration of Indulgence of 1672, which suspended the penal laws against religious dissenters, using the word in its old imperial sense of clemency. Meanwhile, on the moral and interpersonal plane, indulgence came to describe the yielding of human character: parents who indulged their children, gentlemen who indulged their passions, souls who indulged their appetites. By the 18th century, essayists such as Joseph Addison and Samuel Johnson used it for parental over-permissiveness and for habits of comfort, and Jane Austen's novels use it to pin the moral characters of households — spoiling, self-gratification, and the quiet weakness of yielding to inclination. The word had completed the journey from canon law to the drawing-room.

6. The Modern Consumerist Inversion

The twentieth century performed the most remarkable transformation of all: the rehabilitation of indulgence as a virtue. With the rise of industrial advertising and consumer capitalism, marketers seized on the word for luxury foods, perfumes, chocolates, spa holidays, and resort vacations. "Indulge yourself" became a selling point. The moral stigma that the Reformation had attached to the word was deliberately inverted: an indulgence became the hard-won reward for discipline — a temporary, innocent escape from the rigours of work, not a capitulation to vice. Modern English now lives with both faces at once: the old moral one ("indulgence was my sin" — yielding to appetite as a moral failure) and the new commercial one ("a guilty indulgence" — a treat to be enjoyed). The coexistence of these opposites in a single word is a lexicographic jewel, and it is the direct result of the word's eight-hundred-year journey through imperial courts, medieval canon law, Reformation theology, Enlightenment morality, and Madison Avenue.

7. Conclusion

Indulgence shows how a word can carry an entire civilization's memory. It is still the emperor's clemency and the Church's pardon; it is still the moralist's warning and the chocolatier's promise. Its constant across all eras is the act of yielding — to a person, to a desire, to a request. Whether yielding is wisdom or weakness is a judgment the word itself has spent two thousand years negotiating, and it still has not settled the question.
