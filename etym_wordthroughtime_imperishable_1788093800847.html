- [c. 1814: "not liable to perish"] - Coined at the height of Romanticism; an idealistic word for fame, song, love and the soul that death cannot touch — born already glowing.
- [19th century: Victorian high diction] - "Imperishable memory", "imperishable glory": applied to heroes, monuments and national achievements; solemn, grand, public.
- [20th century: the descent to the pantry] - "Imperishable foods and goods" enter retail and emergency language; the word loses some of its poetic glow and gains a practical register.
- [Contemporary: deliberately grand] - Mostly literary or solemn now; "everlasting" and "undying" rule plain speech, so "imperishable" is chosen when weight and dignity are wanted — or in irony, when applied to things that will certainly perish.
