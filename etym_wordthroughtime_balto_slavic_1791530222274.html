- Early nineteenth century - the new comparative philology discovers deep correspondences between Lithuanian and the Slavic languages and treats them as one branch of the Indo-European tree
- Late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries - the label hardens into a standard classificatory node, printed in grammars and family-tree diagrams
- Mid twentieth century - accentological research makes "Balto-Slavic" a technical term with its own laws and debates rather than a mere bracket on a chart
- Late twentieth century - doubts grow: some linguists argue that "Baltic" is paraphyletic and that the node is partly a contact effect, so the word begins to signal a hypothesis rather than a fact
- Twenty-first century - the term is standard in historical linguistics but increasingly hedged; in popular and genetic writing it is used loosely, and specialists regularly warn against treating it as an ethnic or racial category
