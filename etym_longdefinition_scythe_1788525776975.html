A scythe (noun, pronounced /saɪð/) is an ancient agricultural hand tool consisting of a long, gently curved, razor-sharp steel blade attached at an angle to a long, ergonomically curved wooden or metal shaft (termed the snath or snead), fitted with two adjustable offset wooden handles (termed nibs). Operated with both hands in a fluid, pendulum-like rhythmic sweeping motion from right to left while the mower walks forward, the scythe is engineered specifically for mowing grass, harvesting hay, and reaping standing cereal crops close to ground level.

The scythe occupies a vital position across agricultural engineering, historical tool typology, military history, and universal artistic iconography:

  • Anatomy and Mechanics of the Tool: A traditional scythe comprises several precisely engineered components:
    • The Blade: Forged from high-carbon steel with a ribbed back (the chine) for structural rigidity, a sharp cutting edge, and an angled mounting bracket (the tang).
    • The Snath (Snead): The main pole, historically shaped from seasoned ash or willow with natural or steam-bent curves that allow the user to maintain an upright, ergonomic posture.
    • The Nibs: Two perpendicular wooden handgrips attached to the snath, positioned to match the mower's arm span for optimal leverage and balance.
    • Edge Maintenance (Peening vs. Honing): In Continental European traditions (Austrian scythes), the thin blade is periodically cold-hammered on a miniature anvil (peening) to draw out a paper-thin, razor edge, touched up in the field with a natural whetstone soaked in water from a belt-mounted horn (kumpf).
  • Agricultural Revolution and Harvest Efficiency: Originating in Iron Age Europe as an evolution of the smaller one-handed sickle, the two-handed scythe revolutionized medieval European farming. By enabling a single mower to cut up to an acre of meadow per day—tripling the speed of the sickle—the scythe made large-scale haymaking possible, providing the vital winter fodder that sustained expanded herds of cattle, horses, and sheep throughout temperate winters.
  • The War Scythe (Military Weapon): In times of social upheaval, European peasant revolts, and national rebellions (such as the 17th-century Monmouth Rebellion in England, the 1794 Polish Kościuszko Uprising, and the 19th-century January Uprising), agricultural scythe blades were unbolted and reforged vertically along the axis of long poles. These improvised polearms—known as war scythes—became lethal anti-cavalry weapons wielded by peasant scythemen (kosynierzy).
  • Iconography of Death, Time, and Mortality: In Western mythology, religious allegory, and visual art, the scythe is the universally recognized attribute of Chronos / Saturn (the Titan of devouring time and agriculture) and Death / The Grim Reaper. The sweeping blade symbolizes the relentless, egalitarian severance of mortal life, cutting down humanity like grass in a summer field.
  • Literary and Romantic Resonance: In 19th-century literature, the scythe became a romantic symbol of harmonious, pre-industrial human labor in rhythm with nature—famously depicted in Leo Tolstoy’s Anna Karenina (Levin’s transcendental mowing scene) and Andrew Marvell’s pastoral poetry. In Oscar Wilde’s fairy tale The Nightingale and the Rose, the yellow-rose tree evokes the scythe as a symbol of seasonal mortality: "...yellower than the daffodil that blooms in the meadow before the mower comes with his scythe."

Etymologically, scythe derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *sek- ("to cut"). The unpronounced initial letter c is a famous orthographic artifact inserted during the 15th to 17th centuries by Renaissance English scribes who erroneously assumed a false Latin derivation from scindere ("to cleave, split").
