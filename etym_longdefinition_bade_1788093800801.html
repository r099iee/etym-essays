Bade (pronounced /bæd/, often also /beɪd/) is the archaic past tense of the verb bid in its older senses — chiefly “to command, order, request”, “to invite or summon”, and “to utter a greeting or farewell”. When a speaker today says “he bade them stay” or “she bade me farewell”, the sentence is consciously literary, scriptural or old-fashioned: bade is the past tense of a world in which kings and hosts spoke with formal authority.

1. Sense inventory

  • To command / order. The core sense: “the king bade them enter”, “he bade the men be silent”. In this use bade is followed by a bare infinitive: “he bade them stay”.
  • To invite / summon. Middle English baden regularly meant “invited to a feast or gathering”; the phrase “the bidden guests” preserves this sense in modern English (those invited to a wedding).
  • To utter a greeting or farewell. “He bade farewell / good-night / welcome” — a fixed, still idiomatic pattern that has outlived every other use of the word.
  • To offer (literary). “He bade defiance to his enemies” = he offered, proclaimed defiance; “to bid defiance” still occurs in elevated prose.
  • Bade fair (idiom). “It bade fair to be a fine day” = it seemed likely to become one; an old idiom surviving in literary English, where “to bid fair” means to promise, hold promise.

2. The two “bid” verbs

Modern “bid” conflates two distinct Old English verbs with separate past tenses: bēodan “to command, proclaim, offer” (whose past is our bade) and biddan “to ask, entreat, pray” (whose past was bæd). By the Middle English period the forms merged into a single verb “bidden” carrying both sense-groups — command and request — and from the “offer” branch comes the commercial sense “to bid at an auction”. Curiously, the modern past tense of the auction sense is simply “bid” (“he bid five pounds”), while bade survives only in the command / invitation / farewell senses.

3. Register, pronunciation and boundaries

  • Register. Bade is archaic-literary: frequent in the King James Bible, Shakespeare, 19th-century novels and modern fantasy — the review line from J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit: “even though the king bade them stay a while in his halls”. In ordinary speech it is all but dead.
  • Pronunciation. The traditional British pronunciation is /bæd/ (rhyming with “glad”), reflecting the Middle English short-vowel form; /beɪd/ is common in American English and is often treated as a spelling-pronunciation from the final -e. Both are accepted.
  • Grammatical boundary. Only the command/invite/farewell senses take bade; the auction sense takes “bid”; the participial form of the command senses is bidden (or “bid”): “they were bidden to the feast”.

4. Semantic bridge

Old English bēodan meant “to announce, make known” — from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “to awaken, make aware”. From “announce” the verb slid into “proclaim a command” (whence modern bade) and “offer” (whence the auction bid); and because medieval hosts “announced” invitations, bade acquired the courteous sense “invited”. The modern word is a fossil of that ancient announcement-machine: it still commands, invites and takes leave, always with the ring of an older England.
