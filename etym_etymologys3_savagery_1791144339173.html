Savagery combines a Latin forest-word with a French collective suffix to name both a supposed state of wild life and the cruelty attributed to it. The base begins with Latin silva, forest or woods, and its derivative silvaticus, of the woods, wild, untamed, used for wild plants, animals, and men living outside cultivation. In Late Latin silvaticus undergoes regular sound change to salvaticus with a to al before l plus consonant, and semantic widening to fierce and strange. Old French inherits this as salvage, later sauvage with characteristic al to au, meaning wild, uninhabited, cruel, and by extension non-Christian peoples encountered in forest or distant lands. Middle English borrows savage around 1300 for fierce beasts, wild places, and heathen men, preserving the forest-to-fierceness equation.

The suffix -ery continues Latin -arius pertaining to through Old French -erie denoting state, practice, wares, or place: chevalerie chivalry, draperie drapery, sauvagerie wildness. Middle English takes -ery as a productive ending for condition and behaviour: bravery, slavery, mockery. Savage + -ery therefore regularly yields savagery, the state, quality, or act of being savage, first attested in the late 16th century when colonial encounters multiplied occasions for the word.

  • From woods to wildness: The core metaphor is Roman and universal: cultivated ager versus wild silva, citizen versus forest-dweller. To call men silvatici was to place them outside law, agriculture, and language. Medieval bestiaries and romances populate salvage lands with wild men covered in hair, bridging natural wildness and moral monstrosity.
  • Colonial intensification: After 1492 salvage / savage becomes a legal-anthropological category for Amerindians, Africans, and Pacific peoples. Savagery names their supposed stage: without letters, plough, or Christian polity. Elizabethan writers contrast civility and savagery as successive rungs. The source phrase improvident savagery belongs to this stadial theory, where savagery is millennial improvidence before storage and agriculture.
  • From stage to cruelty: By the 17th-18th centuries savagery splits: neutral-ethnographic state versus pejorative brutality. Montaigne's Of Cannibals already inverts it, arguing European cruelty exceeds cannibal savagery. Burke, Golding's Lord of the Flies, and modern journalism use savagery almost exclusively for atrocity: the savagery of war, of a murder, of a tackle. The forest sense survives only in savage wilderness.
  • Phonology: Latin silva /silwa/ to silvaticus /silwatikus/ to Late Latin salvaticus to Old French salvage /salvadʒə/ to Middle English savage /savadʒ/ to Modern /sævɪdʒ/ shows l-vocalization, palatalization of -tic- to -age, and English stress shift. Savagery adds -ery /əri/ with yod reduction, RP /ˈsævɪdʒri/ in three syllables.

Modern savagery thus holds three centres: the historical-anthropological state of wild life, now dated and offensive when applied to peoples; the moral sense of brutal cruelty, fully live; and the stylistic sense of rough vigour, as in the savagery of his satire. The movement from trees to tribes to atrocity shows how landscape vocabulary becomes social hierarchy and then moral condemnation, a trajectory now scrutinized by postcolonial critique that prefers to speak of violence rather than savagery when describing human acts, reserving the word as evidence of past perception rather than present description.
