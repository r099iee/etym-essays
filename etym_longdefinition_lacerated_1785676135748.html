LACERATED: Torn, mangled, or cut with rough, jagged, irregular edges — of flesh, skin, tissue, fabric, or any material torn by a coarse and violent force, as opposed to a clean incision. As a verb, to lacerate is to tear something roughly and jaggedly; the past tense and past participle lacerated names the completed act ("the rocks lacerated his hands") or the resulting state ("a lacerated leg"). The word's defining contrast is with the clean cut: a laceration is a ragged wound, made by tearing or crushing rather than by slicing, and the verb keeps a note of violence and pain that a word like cut entirely lacks. Nuance in this context. In the card's sentence — "The sharp jagged rocks __________ his hands as he scrambled up the cliffside" — the word does far more than report a cut: it converts a scrape into an assault. The past tense locks the damage into a completed, permanent past, and its echo of "jagged" — ragged edge against ragged edge — makes the wound the product of matched violence, the rock tearing the skin exactly as the stone's own teeth are torn. The tone is visceral and physical, the verb chosen not for medical neutrality but for its raw, tearing force: these are not clean incisions but splintered, uneven wounds torn by a coarse and hostile surface. In a climbing scene the word also does narrative work: it signals that the ascent is punishing, that the hands take the cost of every desperate grab, so that "lacerated" carries the reader into the pain of the scramble and marks the cliffside as an antagonist, a body of sharp rock actively hostile to the climber's flesh. Senses and uses.
  • Sense 1 — physical tearing of flesh (primary): "the broken glass lacerated his arm," "a lacerated hand," "lacerated wounds" — the jagged, irregular tearing of living tissue by rough force; the central medical and everyday sense.
  • Sense 2 — tearing of materials: "thorns lacerated his jacket," "the sails were lacerated by the gale" — the same jagged tearing applied to cloth, canvas, leather, and other materials; the word migrates freely between flesh and fabric because both share the ragged-tear image.
  • Sense 3 — figurative wounding (emotions and reputation): "a heart lacerated by grief," "lacerated feelings," "a lacerated reputation" — the emotional extension of the metaphor, developed in the 18th and 19th centuries; the pain of the physical tear is transferred to the psyche, and the word keeps its full violent force in the transfer.
  • Sense 4 — medical and forensic register: in surgery and law, "laceration" is the technical term for a jagged, irregular wound caused by tearing or crushing (distinguished from an incised wound made by a sharp edge); the noun laceration and the participle lacerated are the clinical vocabulary of emergency medicine.
The semantic bridge. The word descends from Latin lacer, "torn, mangled, rent to pieces," and its verb lacerāre, "to tear to pieces, mangle, rend." Latin used the family in vivid, physical contexts — lacerated bodies in battle, lacerated garments, the lacerated victim of a beast — and the whole family carries the idea of a violent tearing apart rather than a neat cut. English borrowed lacerate in the late sixteenth century, keeping the Latin violence intact. The figurative extension to the emotions (a heart lacerated by grief) is a straightforward metaphor: what tears the flesh in the physical sense tears the feelings in the psychological one, and the English word, like its Latin ancestor, applies with equal force to bodies, cloth, and souls. Collocations and register. The verb and participle collocate with "wound," "flesh," "hand," "skin," "leg," "arm," "face," "tissue," and with the adverbs "deeply," "badly," "severely," "horribly": "badly lacerated," "deeply lacerated flesh," "multiple lacerations." The noun laceration is standard in medicine ("the patient sustained multiple lacerations to the forearm"); the verb is literary and formal, at home in narrative prose and news reports of violent accidents, and rare in casual speech, where "tore," "ripped," or "cut up" usually takes its place. Derived forms: laceration (noun, "the act of lacerating; a jagged wound"), lacerative (adjective, "that lacerates," rare), lacerable (adjective, "capable of being torn"). Near-antonym family: incise, incised, incision — the clean cut with a sharp edge that is the exact opposite of a laceration.
