broods (verb; third person singular present of to brood). The primary literal sense of the verb is zoological and physical: a hen broods when she sits on her eggs to incubate them, keeping them warm by the pressure of her own body until they hatch; the noun brood names the young hatched together in one clutch. From that physical act of sitting-on and warming grew the word's dominant modern sense, which is mental and emotional: to brood (on, over, upon something) is to think about it deeply, continuously, and usually unhappily — to dwell on a grief, a grievance, or a fear with a sullen, compulsive attention. The third-person present form broods is the form used of a single subject: “the mountain broods over the valley,” “he broods on his past mistakes.”

The passage. In the sentence “He can neither read nor write and in him broods already a taste for mindless violence,” the verb is used with deliberate strangeness. The subject is not the boy but the taste, and the violence is what broods — and what it broods in is the child. This is the older, more physical sense of the word made newly active: to brood is to sit on a nest of eggs, to incubate, to keep something alive by the pressure of one's own heat. The use fuses both senses at once: the taste for violence is being hatched within the boy, warmed in the dark of his own body, and at the same time the child himself is a brooder, a silent watchful presence in the freezing kitchenhouse. The tone is cold and oracular — violence not chosen or learned but incubated, inherited as temperament, “mindless” because it needs no thought. The word describes an atmosphere rather than an emotion: a slow, heatless gestation of harm.

Polysemy and secondary senses. (1) Zoology: of a bird, to sit on eggs; to incubate; to keep young warm under the feathers. (2) Of landscape and weather: to hang over or threaten — a mountain broods over a valley, a storm broods on the horizon; the sense transfers the hen's silent sitting presence to looming weather or terrain. (3) Psychology: to dwell morbidly and sullenly on a subject; brooding is involuntary, unhappy, and self-feeding, unlike pondering or meditating. (4) Noun uses: a brood is the young produced at one hatching (a brood of chicks), and by extension a family's children — “a brood of five”; the plural noun broods is the word's other life. (5) Adjectival use: brooding describes a person, atmosphere, or face charged with melancholy or menace — “a brooding landscape.” Each secondary sense is one step along a single semantic bridge: the hen's silent, patient sitting-on is the image behind both the threatening mountain and the unhappy mind, because both are things that sit and press without moving, keeping something warm in the dark.

  • Primary sense: (of a bird) to sit on eggs to hatch them; to incubate.
  • Secondary sense: (of a person) to think gloomily and persistently about something distressing.
  • Tertiary sense: (of landscape, weather, atmosphere) to hang over, threaten, loom — a brooding sky, a brooding mountain.
  • Noun: the young hatched in one clutch; by extension a family of children.
  • Historical bridge: the verb grew directly out of the noun's physical scene of sitting-and-warming (Old English brēdan “to keep warm, cherish”), so the sullen thinker of today is etymologically a hen sitting on her egg.
