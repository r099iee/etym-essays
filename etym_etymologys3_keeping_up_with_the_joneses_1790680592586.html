Essay 1 — keeping (and keep up): from seizing to staying level. The verb behind keeping begins in the grasping hand. Old English cepan meant to seize, hold, and watch over — a cluster uniting clutching with guarding, the way keeping sheep means both holding and minding them. Its deeper ancestry is Germanic-only: scholars reconstruct Proto-Germanic *kōpijan and tentatively link it to a PIE grabbing root *gab-, though no firm Latin or Greek cognate survives, so the chain’s first link is honestly marked as debated. What is certain is the spectacular widening inside English itself. By Middle English kepen already spans guarding castles, keeping promises, keeping silence, keeping house, and keeping one’s place — the semantic thread being continued possession or observance against loss, lapse, or intrusion.

The phrasal combination keep up grows out of the locative half of that cluster: to keep something up is to prevent its sinking — keep up prices, keep up appearances, keep up one’s spirits. From there the construction with with is a short step: to keep up with someone is to prevent oneself from sinking below their pace, to stay level in a moving field. Sporting language of the nineteenth century (keeping up with the pack, the field, the leaders) supplied the competitive colouring, and the addition of the progressive -ing turns the act into a durative treadmill — not a single catch-up but a lifelong matching. By the time Arthur Momand titled his 1913 comic strip Keeping Up with the Joneses, the syntax was fully ripe: keep had travelled in a thousand years from clutching goods to matching neighbours, and the gerund named the race as a permanent condition rather than an event.

  • c. 800–1100: cepan — seize, hold, observe; guarding and retaining.
  • c. 1200–1500: kepen — vast extension to promises, houses, feasts, places, and company.
  • 1500s–1800s: keep up + keep up with — prevent sinking; stay level with a moving party.
  • 1913 onward: keeping up frozen into the idiom’s name for endless status matching.

Essay 2 — Joneses: how a Welsh patronymic became everybody’s neighbours. Jones is, by the numbers, one of the commonest surnames in the English-speaking world, and its ordinariness is the whole point. It is the Welsh patronymic son of John, formed with the English genitive -s on Jon/John during the late-medieval fixing of Welsh surnames under English administration. John itself descends through Latin Johannes and Greek Iōannes from Hebrew Yohanan, meaning Yahweh has been gracious — a theophoric name carried across three millennia by apostles, popes, and kings until it became, by the high Middle Ages, the single commonest male name in England. A name borne by every village supplied surnames to every parish: Johnson, Johns, and above all in Wales — where John saturated a small naming pool — Jones, which remains the quintessential Welsh surname and, through emigration, a quintessential American one.

That statistical banality made the Joneses ideal raw material for a generic-neighbour joke. Cartoonist Arthur R. Momand’s strip Keeping Up with the Joneses, launched in the New York Globe in 1913 and syndicated widely through the 1920s, never showed its title family; the humour lay entirely in the McGinis family’s frantic efforts to match neighbours the reader never sees. The unseen Joneses functioned like a variable in an equation — fill in whatever prosperous household sets your street’s standard. The strip ran until 1940, but the phrase escaped within a decade into general American speech, reinforced by 1920s consumer credit, automobiles, radios, and the new suburbia of visible goods. Thorstein Veblen had supplied the theory in 1899 (The Theory of the Leisure Class and its conspicuous consumption), and Momand supplied the proper name; together they gave the language its durable emblem of emulative spending.

Phonetically nothing changed — Joneses simply adds the regular plural /-ɪz/ to Jones — but semantically the capital letter does heavy lifting. Lowercase neighbours are people; the Joneses are a social function, the local benchmark of decency and display. Later decades generalised the slot (keeping up with the Smiths, the Patels, the Kardashians, the feed), yet no rival displaced the original, because its very blandness keeps it universal: anyone’s neighbours can be the Joneses. The source sentence trades on exactly this: the Joneses are other people, the strivers and show-offs, while the speaker claims exemption — the idiom’s eternal, self-flattering pragmatics, which satirists from Momand to Veblen exist to puncture.

  • Biblical–classical: Yohanan → Iōannes → Johannes — the name travels across Hebrew, Greek, and Latin.
  • c. 1500–1700: Welsh Jones fixed as son-of-John surname; becomes mass surname through popularity of John.
  • 1913–1940: Momand’s comic strip mints the Joneses as the unseen benchmark neighbours.
  • 1920s–present: idiom generalises to all status emulation; social media globalises the Joneses from one street to the whole world.
