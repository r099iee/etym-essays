Famished traces its line through French to the Latin word for hunger itself, famēs — the root that also gives English famine. It is a word with a severe history and a soft modern life: born to describe death by starvation, it survives today chiefly as a warm exaggeration for a healthy appetite, while keeping, just beneath the surface, its ancient grimness.

The Latin base: famēs. Latin famēs (genitive famis), 'hunger, starvation, famine', is attested from the earliest Latin literature, in which it is an exceptionally grim word — hunger as destroyer, as weapon, as public calamity. Its etymology beyond Latin is uncertain: most authorities regard the root as obscure, with no securely established Indo-European etymon (connections with Greek and Sanskrit have been suggested but never proven). The honest record is that famēs appears fully formed in Latin with no reconstructable ancestor of any certainty.

Vulgar Latin and the verb 'to starve'. From famēs, popular or Vulgar Latin created a verb of causation, *affamāre (literally 'to bring to hunger, to make hungry'), by prefixing ad- 'to, toward' to a stem meaning 'hunger'. The verb is not recorded in classical texts but is confidently reconstructed from its descendants: it is the source of the whole Romance family meaning 'to starve, to hunger'.

Old French: afamer. Vulgar Latin *affamāre passes into Old French as afamer, 'to starve, to kill or die of hunger, to reduce to hunger'. In Anglo-Norman French, the administrative and legal French spoken in England after the Conquest, the verb was borrowed with the same sense, giving English its direct line of contact with the word.

Middle English: famen. Middle English took up the verb in the fourteenth century as famen (and afamen), meaning 'to starve, to kill by hunger, to die of hunger'. Its participle, famished, described the result: the starving, the starved, the dead of hunger. The earliest English records use it without pity or hyperbole — famishing was dying.

The participle becomes an adjective. Over the late medieval and early modern periods the past participle famished hardened into a full adjective meaning 'starving, starved, wasted by hunger'. It appears throughout the literature of poverty and disaster: famished beggars, famished soldiers, famished prisoners, a famished land. The homilies and chronicles of the period use the word in earnest, and its emotional charge is of genuine, life-threatening want.

Softening in the modern age. As the food supply of Britain improved from the eighteenth century onward and literal famine receded from ordinary experience, famished began its characteristic softening. By the nineteenth century it was already used for mere strong hunger, and by the twentieth it had settled into its modern double life: a sincere word for genuine starvation (war reporting, animal rescue, historical fiction) and a warm, jokey exaggeration for the ordinary appetite ('I'm absolutely famished after that walk!'). The two registers coexist because the old seriousness is still audible in the word: calling yourself famished is only funny because the real thing is so terrible.

Literary attestation. In The Water-Babies (1863), Charles Kingsley gives the word its full old force: Tom looks at a man with 'a famished and a savage look like a dog that has been chained and forgotten in a kennel for a week' — the comparison to a starved chained dog is precise, and famished there means true, raging, desperate hunger, the hunger that drives a creature savage. The same strong sense appears in Dickens's descriptions of the urban poor and in countless accounts of sieges and shipwrecks.

Summary of the chain:

  • famēs (Latin, 'hunger, starvation, famine') — attested in the earliest Latin; Indo-European root obscure and debated
  • *affamāre (Vulgar Latin, 'to bring to hunger, to starve') — reconstructed
  • afamer (Old French, 'to starve, to kill or die of hunger')
  • famen / afamen (Middle English, 14th c., 'to starve, die of hunger')
  • famished (English past participle/adjective, 'starving, starved, wasted by hunger')
  • famished (Modern English, 'extremely hungry — sincere in real hunger, hyperbolic in everyday speech')

From the Latin noun for hunger as calamity, through a Vulgar Latin verb for starving people to death, into medieval English where it meant dying of want, famished has run the whole course from tragedy to appetite — a word whose history can still be felt in its weight.
