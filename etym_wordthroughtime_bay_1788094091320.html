- (c. 1400) - "Bay" enters English from Old French baie in nautical and travel prose: a sheltered curve of the sea-coast — a sailors' working word, borrowed from the Mediterranean French of the ports.
- (1500s–1600s) - Age of exploration: English charts fill with bays — Hudson Bay, Chesapeake Bay, Massachusetts Bay; each bay a potential harbour for fleets, the word a marker of empire on every new map.
- (1700s–1800s) - Civic identity: "the Bay State" (Massachusetts, from the Massachusetts Bay Colony) makes a bay a political nickname; naval-strategy texts fix "bay" as a topographic fact of war; physical geography codifies it (larger than a cove, smaller than a gulf).
- (1800s) - Romantic prose coats the word in calm and beauty — the "calm bay" of the present context is exactly the Victorian picturesque idiom, a sheltered water held quiet by a "promontory of rock".
- (1900s–today) - "The Bay" becomes metonymy for whole regions and industries — the San Francisco Bay Area and its tech economy, Tampa Bay and its sports teams, Green Bay; in ecology a bay is now a fragile habitat to conserve; six centuries from sailors' jargon to global geography.
