Chain for "anemone" — the ancestry of anemones: 1. Proto-Indo-European *h₂weh₁- (also *h₂wē-) — "to blow". A widely attested root of blowing and wind. Its zero-grade/nasal derivatives produce the "wind" words across the family: Hittite ḫuwant-, Latin ventus, Gothic winds, Sanskrit vāta. In Greek it gives the verbal stem behind the wind-noun. 2. Proto-Indo-European *h₂weh₁-n̥-mo- (nasal-infixed derivative) — "the blowing one, the wind". The -n- infix and -mo- suffix form the noun "wind" from the blow-verb. 3. Ancient Greek ἄνεμος (ánemos) (attested from the archaic period, 8th c. BCE onward) — "wind". The standard Greek word for wind, from the PIE nasal derivative above. 4. Ancient Greek ἀνεμώνη (anemōnē) (attested from the 4th century BCE, e.g. Theophrastus) — "the windflower". A derivative of ánemos meaning "daughter of the wind"; the flower was believed to open only when the wind blows (the explanation given by classical and later lapidary writers). Pliny repeats the story. 5. Latin anemōnē (1st century CE) — "the windflower, Anemone". Borrowed from Greek into Latin natural-historical vocabulary. 6. English anemone (late 16th century, first attested c. 1570s) — "the garden windflower, Anemone of the Ranunculaceae". Taken from Latin (and Greek); at first purely a plant-name. 7. English sea anemone (18th century, c. 1760s–1770s) — "the marine polyp of order Actiniaria, whose tentacle crown resembles flower petals". The transfer of the flower-name to the animal by British naturalists describing tide-pool life. 8. English plural anemones (with the modern sense of "sea anemones" alongside the flower sense) — anemones IS OUR WORD (in the Narnia passage it means the sea creatures). DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: The steps from PIE to Greek are standard and well attested. The one debated point is the reason for the flower's name: the classical "opens in the wind" story (Pliny, based on Theophrastus) is traditional, but some modern botanists note the flower also nods in the wind; the etymology of the derivative suffix -ōnē is itself not perfectly clear. The "sea anemone" transfer is securely dated to 18th-century naturalists and is not disputed.
