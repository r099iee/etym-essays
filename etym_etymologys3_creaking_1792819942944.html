The history of creaking is the history of an imitation that worked too well to die: a bundle of harsh kr- sounds by which Germanic speakers mimicked the raven, then the hinge, then the knee, carrying one grating noise across seven centuries of new things that happened to sound like it.

An expressive beginning: many of the oldest words for ugly sounds — croak, crack, crake, creak, crow — cluster around the same harsh opening consonants, and historical linguists treat the whole family as expressive or imitative formation rather than inheritance from a single neat proto-form. People in every century hear a grating noise and reach independently for the same throat-shape, so the word is constantly re-coined even as it is inherited. That is why handbooks give Middle English creken as of imitative origin and decline to attach it firmly to any reconstructed PIE root: to demand a laryngeal for a hinge-squeak is to misunderstand how such words are born.

  • c. 1320 — the raven first: Middle English creken is glossed as to croak or caw, the cry of ravens and crows. Sound and spelling sit close to croken, and the two verbs overlap for a century, throaty bird-noises not yet sorted into separate dictionary entries.
  • 15th–16th c. — from throat to timber: as houses fill with hinged doors, board floors, and wheeled carts, the verb migrates to wood. A door that creaks is heard as croaking mechanically, and writers begin to distinguish the bird's croak from the hinge's creak, the vowel narrowing as the senses divide.
  • Sound change: the Middle English long vowel in creken rises through the Great Vowel Shift toward the modern high front vowel, so that spelling ea comes to stand for the thin /iː/ heard in creak, squeak, and speak. The thin vowel suits the thin sound, an accident of history that feels like design.

Literary career: once attached to houses, creak becomes indispensable to atmosphere. Gothic novelists of the eighteenth century discover that a single creaking stair can do more work than a ghost; sailors report that hulls creak before gales, giving the word a weather-wise authority; Victorian realists use creaking boots, baskets, and signboards as shorthand for honest poverty, things too old to replace but too good to discard. The participle creaking does special duty here, naming not one noise but a habit: the creaking inn-sign complains all night, the creaking waggon complains all the way to market.

  • Medicine borrows it (19th c.): physicians describing arthritic joints reach for the carpenter's vocabulary, and creaking or crepitus enters the clinic. Folk practice agrees: joints, like hinges, are oiled, warmed, and rested, and the Gaffer's ointment is the hinge-oil of the body.
  • The state starts creaking (20th c.): political writers extend the participle to bureaucracies, empires, and economies — vast machines heard as old wood. The metaphor carries a prognosis: what creaks still moves, but the noise warns of coming seizure, and reformers present themselves as oil.
  • Form: the -ing ending is the ordinary Old English participial suffix carried through Middle English -inge to modern -ing, turning the sudden verb into a continuing condition, the very durativeness the Gaffer complains of each morning.

Today the word lives a triple life without confusion: children fear the creaking stair, grandparents laugh at their own creaking knees, and columnists denounce the creaking infrastructure, and each use refreshes the others, because all three name the same physics of stiffness complaining under movement and the same human answer of oil, warmth, and patience.
