Linear derivation chain for PURSUANCE (single root word): 1. PIE *sekʷ- "to follow, accompany" (reconstructed root; one of the best-attested roots of Indo-European, yielding the "follow" verbs of Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin and the "companion, society" words built on the image of walking together). 2. Latin prosequor, prosequī, prosecūtus "to follow forward, accompany, escort, pursue" (pro- "forward" + sequor "to follow"; Classical Latin verb). 3. Old French poursuivre / poursivre "to chase, pursue, carry on, continue" (from Vulgar Latin *prosequere, a reformed verb based on prosequī; the vernacular verb is attested from the 10th-12th c.). 4. Middle French poursuiance / Anglo-French pursiaunce, noun "the act of pursuing, a following up, a continuation" (formed in French on poursuivre + -ance; attested from the 13th-14th c.). (The parallel noun poursuite "pursuit" descends separately from the same verb.) 5. English pursuance "the act of pursuing; the carrying out or execution of a plan, course, or principle" (borrowed from the French noun, attested 1590s; almost immediately fixed in the formula "in pursuance of," used in legal and official prose) - PURSUANCE. Note on the derivation: pursuance could in principle have been formed in English on pursue + -ance, and it is morphologically equivalent to that formation; but the attested record shows it borrowed from the French noun poursuiance. It is a formal doublet of prosecution, which descends directly from the Latin participle prosecūtus by a different route (via Anglo-French), and a sibling of pursuit, which descends from Old French poursuite. The whole family - pursue, pursuit, pursuance, prosecution, pursuant - traces to the single PIE root *sekʷ-, "to follow".
