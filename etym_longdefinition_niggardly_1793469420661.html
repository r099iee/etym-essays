Niggardly is an adjective (and historically an adverb) meaning grudging, stingy, parsimonious, or extremely reluctant to part with possessions, money, honors, or praise. It characterizes an ungenerous disposition that measures out every coin, compliment, or resource with tightfisted hesitation and bitter reluctance. Beyond personal tightfistedness, it also describes conditions or provisions that are meager, scanty, or supplied in insufficient amounts, such as a niggardly harvest, a niggardly pension, or a niggardly allowance.

In the source text from Samuel Johnson’s The Rambler (No. 144, 1751), the word is deployed with psychological brilliance to dissect the malice of social envy. Johnson observes that people cheerfully lavish ornamental marble and glowing eulogies upon a dead man’s tomb (‘they grudge not the whiteness of the sepulchre’), because a corpse cannot enjoy honor or become an object of jealousy; but they are intensely niggardly of granting praise, reputation, or credit to living contemporaries whose success might advance their happiness or fortune. The word captures the precise malice of emotional parsimony: the tightfisted refusal to give what costs nothing to bestow, merely to prevent another living soul from prospering.

Morphology and grammatical function: The word is composed of the Middle English noun niggard (‘a miser, a stingy person’) plus the adjectival and adverbial suffix -ly. Although -ly frequently forms manner adverbs, niggardly has functioned primarily as an adjective since the Early Modern period. While historical authors occasionally used it adverbially (‘he gave niggardly’), modern stylistic convention strongly favors using it adjectivally (‘a niggardly benefactor’) or employing periphrastic adverbial phrases such as ‘in a niggardly manner’ or ‘niggardly fashioned’ to avoid awkwardness.

All senses, primary, secondary, and figurative:

  • Reluctant to spend or part with money; miserly (primary economic sense): Characterizing a person who hoards wealth and refuses to spend or donate even when resources are abundant. The semantic bridge is direct: the physical gesture of clutching or scraping one’s possessions without releasing them.
  • Grudging or stingy with non-material gifts (primary moral/psychological sense): Withholding compliments, recognition, admiration, affection, or mercy. As in Johnson’s essay, this is one of the word’s most prominent literary usages: a stinginess of spirit rather than merely of pocketbook.
  • Meager, scanty, or sparse in quantity (descriptive quantitative sense): Describing physical provisions, natural yields, or institutional funding that fall far short of need. A ‘niggardly diet’ or ‘niggardly budget’ implies not only that the amount is small, but that the entity providing it was stingy and ungenerous.
  • Archaic manner adverb (historical adverbial sense): In a stingy, closefisted, or begrudging manner. Common in Early Modern English translations and drama (e.g., spending niggardly, bestowing niggardly).
  • Modern sociolinguistic sensitivity and taboo avoidance: In contemporary English, niggardly has become extraordinarily rare in spoken and broadcast media due to phonetic collision: despite having a completely unrelated Scandinavian etymology, its phonetic resemblance to an extremely offensive racial slur has caused severe public misunderstandings and social controversy. Consequently, educated writers and speakers today frequently substitute Latinate synonyms such as parsimonious, frugal, miserly, or penurious to ensure clarity without unintended offence.

Register and stylistic tone: Niggardly belongs to an elevated, formal, and classical literary register. It conveys contemptuous moral condemnation: unlike thrifty or economical, which are virtues, niggardly denotes a petty, mean-spirited vice that diminishes human dignity and withholds joy from others.
