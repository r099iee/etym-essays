The biography of doltish begins deep in Proto-Indo-European with the root *dhel-, whose core sense is dimness - dimness of light, cloudiness of water, dullness of sound and of mind. The root's earliest Greek descendant, tholos, means "mud, murk, sediment", and the verb tholoo means "to stir up and make turbid", so the root's primal image is that of disturbed water losing its clarity. In the Germanic branch the same root, taken in its zero grade *dhul-, produced the adjective *dulaz, and there the meaning tilted from dimness of light to dimness of intellect: Old English dol "dull, foolish", Old Norse dulr "hidden, concealed", Middle Dutch dol "mad, frantic", and Old High German tol "foolish, mad", the ancestor of modern German toll "wild, mad" and of Tolpel, the "boor" or "clumsy oaf". English dull descends directly from the Old English adjective; it originally meant blunt (of a knife) and dim (of light or sound) and only by the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries came to mean slow of wit. A parallel Germanic extension, *dwel- "to go astray, be misled", produced Old English dwol "error, heresy, folly" and the verb that became English dwell, whose first sense was "to mislead, hinder" and later "to linger, remain" - a striking reminder that in this family, going astray and being dull were siblings.
  • Around 1540 the language created the noun dolt, a word for a slow-witted fellow, apparently built from the dull base with a -t formant and reinforced by the similar Middle Low German dolte "stub, plug, wedge" - the image of a blunt peg, a thing with no sharp point, is part of the word's very body. Within a generation the plain Germanic suffix -ish produced the adjective doltish in the 1560s.
  • The new word went to work in the rough-and-tumble of Elizabethan satire and jest, where blockheads, dolts, and dullards were favourite targets. The semantic niche is precise: doltish names not mere stupidity but a thick, unteachable, cloddish dullness, often carried with obstinacy - the very image of the "doltish mule" of the verse.
  • By the nineteenth century doltish had settled into fiction as the staple epithet for country simpletons and slow-witted servants, and it kept its barnyard, mocking flavour through the age of the novel. Today it is rare and literary, used with semi-humorous, archaically insulting force.
  • One caution keeps the tree honest: earlier conflations attached Sanskrit dhuli "dust" and Hindi dhul to this root, but those words descend from the separate PIE root *dhewh2- "to smoke, give off dust or clouds" - the family of Latin fumus "smoke" - and the two roots must not be merged. The dimness of *dhel- and the dustiness of *dhewh2- sound alike but are historically distinct.
In the passage, the word's etymological body does real work. The jeering world calls Bao-yu a "doltish mule": the mule supplies the obstinacy, and doltish supplies the dullness, and together they build the speaker's image of a boy too thick-brained to be worth teaching - an image that the novel's ironies immediately undercut, for the reader knows the dullness is in the jeerers' vision, not in Bao-yu. Etymologically, doltish is a word about a blunt peg and a turbid pool: it pictures intelligence as something sharp that has been worn dull, and mind as water that has been stirred to mud. That is a long way from a modern clinical word like intellectually disabled, and exactly the distance that the verse wants: a country-flavoured, archaic, contemptuous charge of slow wits, hurled with the assurance of a world that mistakes rebellion for stupidity.
