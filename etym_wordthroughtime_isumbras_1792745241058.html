- [14th c. Middle English] — Coined and popularized in the metrical romance Sir Isumbras (c. 1350), portraying a wealthy Christian knight humbled by God who undergoes extreme trials before redemption — [perception: exemplary figure of pious chivalry, Christian patience, and dramatic moral restoration]
- [16th–18th c. Post-Medieval & Antiquarian Eras] — Retained as a quaint, half-forgotten title in early print chapbooks (William Copland's 16th-century edition) and antiquarian ballad collections (Bishop Percy's Reliques) — [perception: regarded as an archaic curiosity of medieval minstrelsy and wandering minstrel tales]
- [1857 Victorian Pre-Raphaelite Revival] — Thrust into nationwide Victorian cultural prominence through John Everett Millais' painting Sir Isumbras at the Ford, depicting the aged knight carrying peasant children — [perception: revered as a touching Victorian emblem of chivalric humility, gentlemanly kindness, and Christian charity]
- [1954 J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings] — Adopted by Tolkien as the founding dynastic name of the Took Thains of the Shire (Isumbras I to IV), immortalized in Appendix C — [perception: transformed into an aristocratic, slightly eccentric hobbit dynastic name denoting venerable antiquity and hidden valor]
- [21st c. Contemporary Medievalism & Fantasy Studies] — Frequently analyzed by literary scholars (Shippey, Honegger) as a brilliant case study of Victorian Pre-Raphaelite and Middle English medievalism woven into modern epic fantasy — [perception: an iconic philological link connecting 14th-century romance, Victorian art, and Middle-earth world-building]
