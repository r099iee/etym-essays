The historical and linguistic pedigree of Celtæ takes us to the very dawn of European ethnography. In the sixth century BCE, as Greek navigators from Phocaea established the trading colony of Massalia (modern Marseille) near the mouth of the Rhône, they came into direct contact with the continental Iron Age societies of the interior. The earliest surviving written attestation of their name occurs in the fragmentary geographical writings of Hecataeus of Miletus (c. 517 BCE), who records that the city of Massalia was situated near the territory of the Κελτοί (Keltoi). A generation later, Herodotus of Halicarnassus (c. 450 BCE) described the Keltoi as living in the far west of Europe, beyond the Pillars of Hercules and dwelling near the headwaters of the Danube river (Histories 2.33). This Greek transcription, Κελτοί or Κέλται (*Keltai*), was not an external slur or fabricated label; it was the faithful Greek phonetic rendering of the native Celtic autonym *Kelto-.

Comparative Indo-European philology has long investigated the internal etymology of this ancient self-designation. Three primary linguistic hypotheses have been advanced by leading Celticists:

  • The ‘Hidden’ or ‘Sheltered’ Ones (PIE *ḱel-): The first hypothesis derives Proto-Celtic *kel-to- from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ḱel- (‘to cover, conceal, protect, shelter’). Under this derivation, *kelto- signified either ‘the hidden ones’ (perhaps an allusion to inhabitants of the dense, mysterious temperate forests of central Europe) or ‘the clothed/armored ones’ (referring to warriors clad in distinctive woolen cloaks or protective leather armor). This root is exceptionally productive across Indo-European, generating Latin celāre (‘to hide’), occulere (‘to conceal’), and Germanic cognates like Old English helan (‘to conceal’), heall (‘hall, covered dwelling’), and hell (‘the concealed underworld’).
  • The ‘Tall’ or ‘Lofty’ Ones (PIE *kelh₅-): The second hypothesis connects the name to the Proto-Indo-European root *kelh₅- (‘to project, rise, be high, prominent’). Here, Proto-Celtic *kelto- would signify ‘the tall ones’ or ‘the elevated/noble ones’. Classical Mediterranean writers consistently expressed astonishment at the great physical stature of Celtic warriors compared to the shorter Roman and Greek legions, making ‘the tall ones’ a compelling, natural autonym. Cognates include Latin celsus (‘elevated, lofty, noble’), collis (‘hill’), and Lithuanian kelti (‘to raise’).
  • The ‘Strikers’ or ‘Warriors’ (PIE *kelh₂-): A third hypothesis connects the name to PIE *kelh₂- (‘to strike, beat, slay’), yielding a Proto-Celtic noun meaning ‘the fighters’ or ‘those who strike down enemies’ (cognate with Latin percellere ‘to strike down, overturn’).

The Latin transmission of the word occurred during Rome's expanding diplomatic and military encounters with Gaul. Roman scribes rendered Greek Κέλται directly into Latin as Celta (singular) and Celtae (nominative plural), belonging to the Latin first declension. Julius Caesar crystallized the ethnographic distinction in the opening sentence of his Gallic War (58 BCE), recording that the central inhabitants called themselves Celtae in their own tongue, whereas the Romans called them Galli. Through Caesar’s widely copied manuscripts, Celtae became a cornerstone of classical literature.

During the Middle Ages, direct vernacular reference to the ancient Celts largely faded from Western European thought, as regional identities organized around feudal monarchies and newly arrived Germanic ruling classes (such as the Franks and Anglo-Saxons). The word was kept alive exclusively in classical Latin manuscripts. With the Renaissance of the sixteenth century, antiquarians rediscovered Caesar and Tacitus. In 1582, the Scottish humanist George Buchanan took the groundbreaking intellectual step of recognizing that the Scottish Gaels, the Irish, and the ancient Gauls shared a common ancestral language and heritage, designating them collectively through the Latin term Celtae. In 1707, the Welsh polymath Edward Lhuyd published his monumental Archaeologia Britannica, scientifically demonstrating the genealogical relationship of all Celtic tongues and establishing ‘Celtic’ as an enduring linguistic family.

When eighteenth-century Enlightenment historians like David Hume composed their sweeping national narratives, they adopted the unassimilated Latin form Celtæ, complete with the classical æ ligature, to maintain authentic scholarly alignment with Roman historiography. Over the course of the nineteenth century, the word was thoroughly anglicized into Celts. The vocalic development of Celtæ in English mirrors the history of classical loanwords: the Latin diphthong ae monophthongized to /eː/ in Medieval Latin, which underwent the Great Vowel Shift in Early Modern English to yield the modern terminal long vowel /iː/, producing the traditional British Received Pronunciation /ˈsɛl.tiː/ or reformed classical /ˈkɛl.tiː/.
