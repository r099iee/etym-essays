DERIVATION CHAIN FOR ROTA: 1. Proto-Indo-European *ret- / *Hreth₂- (verbal root, 'to run, roll, turn') [c. 4500-3000 BCE; reconstructed, no direct attestation] 2. Proto-Italic *rotā (noun, 'wheel, turning thing') [c. 1200-800 BCE; reconstructed, no direct attestation] 3. Latin rota (noun, 'a wheel': cart-wheel, potter's wheel, wheel of torture, wheel of fate; with the verb rotāre 'to turn round, revolve') [attested from the 3rd-2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus] 4. English rota (noun, borrowed directly from Latin: first 'the Rota' club and rotational membership; later a roster of duties) [borrowed c. 1650s-1660s] 5. Modern English ROTA (noun, 'a list of people's names and the jobs they have to do at a particular time; a roster') [contemporary British institutional English] DEBATED / NUANCED NOTES: The Proto-Indo-European root is debated. The traditional reconstruction *ret- 'to run, roll' is widely cited, but modern Indo-Europeanists often prefer a laryngeal-initial *Hreth₂-. The wheel sense is an Italic specialization: the same root elsewhere gave 'run' words and chariot terms (Sanskrit rátha- 'chariot, wagon', Old Irish roth 'wheel', Lithuanian rãtas 'wheel', Welsh rhod 'wheel'), so 'wheel' appears to be a semantic offshoot of 'run/roll' rather than the root's core meaning. English rota is a seventeenth-century learned loan of the Latin noun itself, not a descendant of the native Old English wheel-word hwēol; the roster sense is an English specialization of the Latin 'wheel.' The number of steps here (5) reflects the attested record; the missing Proto-Italic stage is reconstructed and is explicitly flagged as such rather than asserted as a documented form.
