- 8th to 11th Century (Old English) - Strong class VI Germanic verb *hebban* (past tense *hōf*, past participle *hafen*); originally meant to lift, raise up, elevate, or exalt into the air.
- 12th to 15th Century (Middle English) - Evolved into *heven*; alongside the traditional strong preterite *hof* / *hef*, weak analogical past forms *heved* / *hevede* began appearing in dialectal speech; expanded to manual hauling, throwing heavy objects, and the undulating surge of ocean waves.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - The weak regular past form *heaved* largely superseded the strong past in general prose, while the older strong form survived as *hove* in specialized maritime language (*hove to*, *hove in sight*); Shakespeare and his contemporaries cemented the respiratory metaphor *to heave a sigh*.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Achieved iconic status in Gothic and Romantic literature as the supreme physical expression of turbulent sentiment (*her bosom heaved*, *heaved a mournful sigh*); celebrated in nautical fiction (Herman Melville, Frederick Marryat) for shipboard rope-hauling (*heave-ho*).
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues with vigorous versatility across athletic throwing (*heaved a pass*), civil engineering (*frost heave*), gastroenterology (*dry heaves*), and everyday emotional relief (*heaved a sigh of relief*).
