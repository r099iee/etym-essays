Ominous is an adjective meaning giving the impression that something bad, threatening, or fateful is about to happen — full of ominous warning, like a dark cloud that has not yet burst, a silence that lasts a beat too long, or a number that feels charged with fate. An ominous sky threatens rain; an ominous silence threatens revelations; an ominous symptom threatens the diagnosis the doctor has not yet spoken aloud.

In the source sentence Tolkien sets the word inside a characteristically hobbitish piece of numerology: fifty is a number Frodo feels is somehow significant (or ominous) — the age at which adventure suddenly befell Bilbo, and therefore the age at which it might be waiting for him too. The parenthesis is doing double duty: significant is the polite word, ominous the midnight word, and the hesitation between them is the sound of a comfortable hobbit discovering that fate may have marked his calendar.

Core meaning in detail: Ominous describes appearances that seem to carry a message about the future — not proof, but presentiment. Its grammar is the grammar of signs: clouds, silences, pauses, numbers, symptoms, headlines, and trends can all be ominous, because all of them can look like nature or fate dropping a hint. The word belongs to the ancient human habit of reading the world as a text — augurs watching birds, sailors watching skies, patients watching doctors' faces — and it survives because the habit survives: modern people who laugh at Roman entrails still call a jobs report ominous.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Threatening evil or harm to come (primary modern sense, from the late 16th c.): ominous clouds, an ominous knock at midnight. The bridge is the Roman omen read as warning: what the augur declared unlucky becomes, by extension, anything whose look or sound promises trouble.
  • Of or pertaining to omens, portending fate good or bad (original learned sense, 1580s): ominous birds in Virgil, ominous dreams in chronicles. The bridge is direct derivation: Latin ominosus meant full of omens, and early English writers used it for fateful signs of either kind before the bad sense swallowed the good.
  • Fatefully significant, heavy with coming change (Tolkien's parenthetical sense): fifty felt significant or ominous. The bridge runs from threat to weight: the number promises not ruin but eventfulness — adventure befell Bilbo at fifty, so fifty carries a charge — and ominous becomes the word for significance felt in the stomach rather than the head.
  • Unpromising in practical forecasts (modern institutional use): ominous economic figures, an ominous scan, ominous polling. The bridge carries the augur into the office: analysts and physicians are today's readers of entrails, and their graphs and images give warnings exactly as birds once did — hence headlines declaring every downturn ominous.
  • Menacing in manner or sound (of people, voices, music): an ominous tone, ominous drums in a film score. The bridge is theatrical: composers and actors manufacture the signs of doom — low strings, slow footsteps, level stares — so audiences feel fate approaching on cue, and critics borrow the augur's vocabulary to praise the effect.
  • Humorously overblown foreboding (playful, everyday): the ominous silence of teenagers asked about homework, an ominously empty biscuit tin. The bridge is bathos: speakers borrow fate's vocabulary for domestic trivia, and the grandeur of Rome collapses delightedly into the kitchen.

Register and connotation: ominous is literary without being archaic — at home in novels, newsrooms, and weather forecasts alike. It flatters the thing it describes with gravity: to call clouds ominous is to grant the sky intentions, and to call a number ominous is to confess, however playfully, that numbers can feel alive. Against its near-synonyms it keeps a priestly distance: sinister is eviller, menacing is closer, foreboding is more inward — ominous alone stands on the temple steps, reading the sky for the city.
