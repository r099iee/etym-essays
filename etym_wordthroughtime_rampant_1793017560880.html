- Germanic roots — clawing and clutching: Frankish *rampōn grips and crawls, the barnyard verb of beasts pulling themselves upward.
- Old French — creeping and climbing: ramper means to creep on all fours or climb, and its present participle rampant names whatever rears and climbs.
- c. 1300 — the heraldic beast: English knights bring home rampaunt, the lion rearing on its shield, and the word stands for noble violence.
- 14th–17th c. — fierce men and passions: chronicles call soldiers and tempers rampant when they rear and strike like the lion.
- 18th–19th c. — the weeds take it: gardeners call invasive plants rampant, and moralists follow with rampant vice and rampant corruption.
- Today — the editorial's engine: inflation, disease, and rumour go rampant across headlines, while the lion still rears, unchanged, on a thousand shields.
