prophet: a person regarded as an inspired teacher or proclaimer of the will of God, especially one who reveals divine truth, warns of judgement, or foretells the future on behalf of a deity; more broadly, any person who makes inspired or confident predictions about what will happen.

Primary sense — religious proclaimer of divine will: In Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, a prophet (Hebrew nāḇī, Greek prophētēs, Arabic nabī) is someone chosen by God to receive and communicate revelation — calling people to repentance, announcing judgement, and foretelling events. The word is used for the Hebrew nevi'im (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel), for John the Baptist and the apostles in the New Testament, and for figures such as Moses, Elijah, and Muhammad. A "false prophet" is someone who falsely claims this inspiration.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings:
  • Secular forecaster (contemporary): "Prophet" now routinely names any confident predictor of future trends — "prophet of doom," "a prophet of the climate crisis," "tech prophet," "market prophet." Semantic bridge: Greek prophētēs originally meant "one who speaks before/on behalf of another"; the sense narrowed from "spokesman for a deity" to "one who foretells," and then loosened further to any bold predictor.
  • Visionary advocate: A "prophet" may be a moral or cultural visionary who announces truths others do not yet see — e.g., "a prophet of social justice," "a prophet of the machine age" — used of thinkers, artists, and reformers, not only of religious figures.
  • Prophet of doom / Cassandra figure: In journalism and everyday speech, "prophet of doom" labels a person (sometimes ironically) who constantly predicts disaster; the phrase echoes the classical figure of Cassandra and Biblical warning prophets.
  • Derived forms and compounds: prophecy (the message or prediction), prophesy (the verb), prophetic (adjective, "having the quality of foreseeing"), prophetess (female prophet), false prophet, prophet-martyr, weather prophet (one who predicts the weather), and the phrase "prophet's reward."
  • Sports, finance, and forecasting jargon: "Prophet" appears playfully in betting, stock-picking, and forecasting contexts for anyone whose predictions prove accurate.
  • Honorific and institutional titles: "The Prophet" as a fixed title refers to Muhammad in Islam, and "prophet" functions as a title for founders of movements — "the prophet of the movement."

Historical semantic bridge: In Ancient Greece a prophētēs (from pro- "before/for" + phētēs "speaker", from phēmi "I say") was the interpreter or spokesman who proclaimed the cryptic utterances of an oracle — he "spoke for" the god, not primarily "foretold." The Jewish translators of the Septuagint (3rd–2nd c. BCE) used prophētēs for the Hebrew nāḇī, shifting the word's centre of gravity to "one who foretells and proclaims the will of God," and it entered Latin, Old French, and English with that sacred, revelatory sense. From that religious core grew today's secular, ironic, and journalistic uses.
