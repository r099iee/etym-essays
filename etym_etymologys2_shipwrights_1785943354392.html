COMPOUND WORD: SHIPWRIGHTS = SHIP + WRIGHT. Complete derivation chain for each element in isolation, followed by the fused compound. CHAIN 1 — SHIP (first element): PIE *skei- "to cut, split, separate" (root of cutting; cf. Greek skhizein "to split", Latin scindere "to cut", Old Irish scían "knife") [c. 4000–2500 BCE] → Proto-Germanic *skipą "ship, boat" (reconstructed neuter noun; cf. Gothic skip, Old Norse skip, Old Saxon skip, Old High German skif) — the traditional semantic link is that the first ship was a dugout canoe made by "cutting out" a hollowed tree trunk [c. 500 BCE – 200 CE] → Old English scip "ship, vessel, boat" (attested) [attested 7th–11th c.] → Middle English ship / schip "ship" [attested 12th–15th c.] → Modern English ship "a large seagoing vessel" — the first element of shipwright [16th c.–] NOTE ON THE DEBATE: The derivation of *skipą from PIE *skei- "to cut" rests on the dugout-canoe theory. It is widely repeated but not universally accepted; *skipą may alternatively be a non-Indo-European borrowing (a Wanderwort) of unknown origin, since its cognates appear mainly in the Germanic and Celtic branches. The cut-out log theory is best labelled probable-but-debated. CHAIN 2 — WRIGHT (second element): PIE *werg- "to do, make, work" (cf. Greek érgon "work, deed" — the source of English erg, energy; Armenian gorc "work"; Avestan varəza "activity") [c. 4000–2500 BCE] → Proto-Germanic *wurkijaną "to work, make, do" and *werką "work, deed" [c. 500 BCE – 200 CE] → Old English wyrcan "to work, make, build" and weorc "work" (both attested) [attested 8th–11th c.] → Old English wyrhta "worker, maker, craftsman" (agent noun from wyrcan + agent suffix -ta; attested alone and in compounds such as scipwyrhta "shipwright") [attested 8th–11th c.] → Middle English wright(e) / wyrhte / wriht "maker, craftsman, builder" (the simplex survives mainly in compounds) [attested 12th–15th c.] → Modern English wright "craftsman, builder" (now archaic as a free word; chiefly in compounds) [16th c.–] FUSED COMPOUND — SHIPWRIGHT: Old English scipwyrhta "a builder of ships" (scip + wyrhta; attested in Old English glosses and texts) [attested 8th–11th c.] → Middle English shipwright(e) "a builder of ships" [attested 13th–15th c.] → Modern English shipwright "one who builds or repairs ships (traditionally of wood)" [16th c.–] → plural → SHIPWRIGHTS
