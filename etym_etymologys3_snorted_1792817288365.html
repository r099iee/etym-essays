The history of snorted is the history of a noise that learned to mean something. Almost every authority agrees on the starting point: snort is imitative — a word shaped by the sound it names, like buzz, hiss, or its twin snore. Somewhere in the Germanic-speaking world, speakers rendered the harsh nasal blast of horses and heavy sleepers as a syllable beginning with the cluster sn-, the sound-symbolic onset English still reserves for nose-words (sniff, sneeze, snout, snot, snore) — a whole snuffling family whose kinship is felt in the nostrils before it is proved in any grammar.

From stable to page:

  • Reconstructed Germanic beginnings: comparative evidence — Old Norse snorta (to snore, snort), Middle Dutch snorcken, German schnarchen (to snore) and schnauben (to snort, pant) — points to a shared imitative stock rendering forced noisy breathing. Because imitative words can be coined independently wherever noses blow, scholars treat the Germanic forms as a sound-symbolic cluster rather than the inheritance of one datable root, and no single PIE ancestor is securely assigned.
  • Middle English arrival (c. 1350): snorten enters written English describing loud breathing — snoring sleepers, blowing horses — with no social attitude attached. It is husbandry and bedroom vocabulary, the noise of beasts clearing their nostrils and men sawing logs in their sleep.
  • Early modern transfer (16th–17th c.): writers begin applying the beast's blast to human scorn. The mechanism is the era's humoral body: passions were physical events, and contempt, like disgust, was felt in the nose — wrinkled at foul smells, flared at insults. To snort at a proposal was to treat it as a bad odour, and the metaphor stuck because the physiology was visible.
  • The novel institutionalises it (18th–19th c.): the rise of dialogue-heavy fiction gave snorted its signature job as a speech tag. Where said is neutral, snorted smuggles in the narrator's verdict: this speaker despises what he answers. Dickensian colonels, Austenian dowagers, and adventure-story sergeants all snort, and generations of readers absorbed the convention until the bare verb could carry a paragraph of attitude.
  • Twentieth-century extensions: two slang senses branch off. Drug culture reverses the airflow — to snort cocaine is to inhale sharply through the nose, keeping the anatomy while flipping the direction. Pub slang makes a snort a quick nip of spirits, metonymy from one sharp nasal blast to one sharp fiery swallow. Meanwhile snorting with laughter becomes the standard confession of mirth that propriety failed to suppress.

Sound shifts and form: remarkably little has changed in six centuries: snorten to snort loses only its infinitive ending, and the past snorted follows the regular weak-verb pattern, pronounced with a syllabic ending. The stability is typical of imitative words — the sound anchors the form, so while great vowel shifts roll over the rest of the lexicon, the snort stays a snort. Its spelling twin snore drifted off toward sleep, leaving snort the waking, social, contemptuous member of the pair, the one that talks back.
