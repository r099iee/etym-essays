• Attackers: Similar because both name the offensive party against a place or army; differs because attackers is generic — covering raids, skirmishes and assaults of any duration — while besiegers specifically surround, blockade and hold fast for a prolonged investment.
  • Assailants: Similar as active combatants against a position; differs because it is primarily a legal or journalistic word for sudden onslaught or street-level violence, with no implication of encirclement or patience.
  • Aggressors: Similar as the initiating hostile party; differs because it is a diplomatic and moral label (the party at fault) rather than a description of method — besiegers may be the defenders’ moral equals, aggressors never.
  • The besieging force / besieging army: Similar because the exact technical paraphrase of the same referent; differs because it is a phrase of formal military prose, while besiegers is its single-word compression used in narrative.
  • Beleaguerers / beleaguering forces: Similar because they surround with hostile intent; differs because the noun is rarer, and the adjective beleaguered has drifted toward “overwhelmed by difficulties or requests” rather than literal siege.
  • Blockaders: Similar as forces that cut a place off; differs because a blockade may be naval or economic and may never involve an attack or the intention to capture, while besiegers press the place itself.
  • Investors (archaic, military): Similar because those who formally “invest” (encircle) a fortress are beginning a siege; differs by register — an obsolete technicality of 17th–19th-century tactics, and confusing today for the financial meaning.
  • Leaguers: Similar because a 17th-century English word for besieging armies (from French/Dutch leger “camp”); differs because archaic and historical only.
  • Encirclers / surrounding forces: Similar in the encircling geometry; differs because non-technical, and encirclement applies to armies in the field as well as to places, without the prolonged siege-operation meaning.
  • The enemy / hostiles: Similar as the opposing armed parties; differs because broader and perspective-laden — not all enemies besiege, and not all besiegers are the whole enemy force.
  • Tormentors / harassers (figurative): Similar as the metaphorical “besiegers of privacy or peace”; differs because fully de-militarised, describing petty or persistent annoyance rather than encircling pressure.
