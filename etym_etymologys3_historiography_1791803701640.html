The witness. The Greek noun ἱστορία descends from ἵστωρ, a word already present in Homer, where it denotes a witness or an arbitrator—someone who knows because he has seen. The root is *weyd-, “to see, know,” which also yields Latin videre “to see,” Sanskrit veda “knowledge,” and English wit. The earliest meaning of the family is therefore forensic and ocular: knowledge grounded in having witnessed.

Inquiry. By the fifth century BCE ἱστορία meant “inquiry, investigation.” Herodotus opens his work by calling it the display of his historia—his researches—and the word still refers to the process of finding out rather than to the past. Hecataeus had used the same vocabulary for his geographical and genealogical enquiries. Thucydides, by contrast, wrote a ktēma es aei, a possession for all time, and the difference between the two marks the beginning of the tension between history as research and history as literature.

Philosophy and genre. Aristotle drove a wedge between history and poetry in the Poetics: poetry speaks of what might be, history of what has been, and is therefore more particular and less philosophical. That judgement shaped the ancient hierarchy of genres, in which history was a branch of rhetoric, judged by its usefulness and its style. Greek writers of history were eventually called ἱστοριογράφοι, “history-writers,” a term used for the official and courtly historians of the Hellenistic kingdoms. The abstract noun ἱστοριογραφία, “the writing of history,” belongs to the later, Roman-era vocabulary of scholarship; its exact first attestation is uncertain, and it should not be imagined as a classical Athenian word.

Rome and the Middle Ages. Latin took historia both as “narrative of events” and as “knowledge obtained by inquiry,” and Roman historians from Livy to Tacitus worked within a rhetorical tradition in which invention of speeches and moral judgement were legitimate. Medieval writers folded history into providence: universal chronicles from the creation to the Last Judgement, saints' lives, dynastic annals. The critical examination of documents was not absent—medieval forgery detection is a real discipline—but the governing assumption was that history displayed divine purpose.

Renaissance to Enlightenment. Humanist scholars recovered classical texts, developed textual criticism and antiquarianism, and produced the ars historica, a body of writing on how history should be composed. The word historiography entered English in the 1560s, at first simply naming the writing of history. Enlightenment historians—Voltaire, Gibbon, Robertson—widened the subject from politics and war to manners, commerce and religion, and began to claim a philosophical authority for history.

Professionalisation. The nineteenth century transformed the word. Ranke's seminar, the Monumenta Germaniae Historica, the archival profession and the modern research university made history a discipline with methods; the study of how history had been written became a subject in its own right. Historiography acquired its modern academic sense: the critical, comparative study of historians and their schools. National traditions were codified, and Butterfield's The Whig Interpretation of History (1931) named the habit of writing history as the inevitable triumph of the present—the source of the later label “Whig historiography.”

Theory and after. In the twentieth century historiography became a field of theory. Marxist historians analysed class and mode of production; the Annales school turned to long durations and mentalities; Hayden White's Metahistory (1973) argued that historical writing is organised by pre-critical narrative tropes. Postcolonial and decolonial scholarship exposed the imperial assumptions of the archive, and memory studies asked how societies remember and suppress. The word now covers both the literature on a topic and the reflexive study of that literature.

  • Semantic chain: witness → inquiry → written account → the writing of history → the study of historical writing → a body of scholarship.
  • Morphology: a Greek compound, historio- + -graphia, mediated through Latin and French.
  • Register: academic and reflexive; it names a meta-level of historical work.
  • Modern extension: “the historiography of X” now means the interpretative literature on X.
