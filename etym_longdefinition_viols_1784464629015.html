Viols (the plural of viol) are fretted, bowed string instruments of the Renaissance and Baroque consort family — typically built with sloping shoulders, a flat back, C-shaped (or flame-shaped) sound-holes, gut frets tied on the fingerboard, and six (or sometimes seven) strings tuned in fourths with a major third in the middle — played underhand with the bow and usually held between the legs (the viola da gamba type) or, for the smallest, on the lap or at the shoulder. They are emphatically not modern violins under another name: the frets, flat back, sloping shoulders, tuning, bow hold, and the whole consort culture set the viol family apart from the later violin family. In The Hobbit, Dwalin and Balin return from their errand “with viols as big as themselves,” i.e. bass- or violone-range instruments whose comic bulk and archaic name deepen the Unexpected Party's orchestra from little fiddles toward something older, grander, and more solemn — a sound-world preparation for the dwarves' mountain-song. The tone is archaic-musical, slightly ceremonial; the instruments feel like heirlooms from a medieval court rather than modern concert-hall gear.

Precision matters in organology. A violin (and the dwarves' fiddles) is the later, unfretted instrument of the violin family, with arched back and shoulders, tuned in fifths, played overhand; a viola is the alto member of the violin family (related name, different instrument); a cello or double bass may share the bass range of large viols but lacks frets and consort history; a viola da gamba is the leg-held member of the viol family proper; a violone is the largest bass viol; a lyre, harp, or lute is plucked rather than bowed. Viols keeps the fretted, flat-backed, bowed consort family — here plural and oversized, in Tolkien's joke of scale: the instruments themselves are as big as the dwarves who carry them, like comic bass fiddles from an old chronicle.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings

  • Consort instrument family (primary musical sense): any of the fretted, flat-backed bowed instruments of the Renaissance/Baroque viol family — treble, alto, tenor, bass, and great bass (violone) — played in a consort (a matched set).
  • Viola da gamba specifically: in early-music practice “viol” often names the leg-held gamba type, distinguishing it from the arm-held (braccio) violin family.
  • Historical organological term: in musicology and organology, “viol” is the precise technical name distinguishing this family from the violin family and from the medieval fiddle (vielle, rebec).
  • The name-family confusion (viola, violin, violoncello): the modern words viola, violin, and violoncello (cello) derive from the same Italian diminutive and suffix family but name the later, unfretted instruments — a classic case of shared name, different instrument.
  • Bowed-string general sense (archaic): in older English (15th–17th c.) “viol” could be used loosely for any bowed stringed instrument, including what we would now call a violin or fiddle; Elizabethan usage is not always anatomically precise.
  • Modern early-music revival sense: since the 20th-century early-music movement, “viol” again denotes a living instrument played by consorts worldwide — instruments newly made to historical patterns.
  • Figurative / cultural use: “viol” carries a strong connotation of courtly, ceremonial, old-music prestige; it is often used deliberately to evoke the Tudor and Stuart court, consort music, and the sound-world before the violin's ascendancy.

Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: The word's history is a history of instrument culture. Medieval Latin vitula named a bowed fiddle-type; Provençal and Old French forms (viola, viole) carried the name through the 12th century; and by the 16th century English viol was fixed as the name of the fretted consort family that flourished under Elizabeth I and the Stuarts. When the violin family triumphed in the 18th century, the word's living musical sense retreated into history books — until the 20th-century revival brought viols back onto the stage. The plural viols is simply the ordinary inventory plural for a matched set of such instruments, the form Tolkien uses.
