The past of bleary is short, honest, and refreshingly free of antiquity: a medieval word for rheumy eyes that softened, over four centuries, into the friendliest adjective of exhaustion in English.

Middle English — pity, not comedy: Around 1300 blere means dim-sighted or watery-eyed, and its company is bleak: aged beggars, crones, the infirm whom sermons bid the congregation pity. The verb bleren, to dim or to make the eyes water, works both ways, describing failing sight and the bleared deceived eye of the cheated, for a blear eye is easily imposed upon. Medicine of the time files it under rheums and fluxes, humours running where they should not, and no laughter attaches.

Tudor and Stuart — drink joins age: By the sixteenth century the tavern has adopted the adjective. Ale blears the eye as surely as age, and rogues' pamphlets, plays, and jest-books show blear-eyed goodfellows squinting through smoke. Shakespeare's contemporaries use blear for eyes thickened with weeping, watching, or wassail alike, and the longer form bleary begins appearing, first as a variant, then as the kinder-sounding heir, its trailing -y softening the infirmary into the morning room.

The debate — where did blere come from: Here honesty requires a pause, for blere has no certain parentage. Etymologists have proposed kinship with blur, itself obscure; with Middle Dutch and Low German words for swelling; with an imitative complex around ble- for blinking and babbling. None commands consensus, and the dictionaries mark the origin unknown. This is the correct scholarly posture and the essay keeps it: some words are orphans, and bleary is loved no less for having no pedigree.

Victorians — the urban morning: Dickens, Thackeray, and the illustrated papers fix modern bleary: clerks at dawn, watchmen off duty, travellers in railway waiting rooms, eyes gummed by gaslight and early shifts. The empire's timetables manufactured millions of bleary mornings, and the adjective, short and sympathetic, rode every omnibus. Hospitals, newsrooms, and barracks each added their own bleary archetype, and the word crossed into American papers without changing a blink.

Twentieth century — airports and headlines: Flight, shift work, and the all-night sitting spread bleary across the globe until bleary-eyed travellers became journalistic wallpaper. Photographers lent a transferred sense, the bleary snapshot, and meteorologists a bleary dawn, the world itself described as the tired eye sees it. Its milestones read:

  • Medieval infirmity: blere pities the dim-sighted old, with bleren verbing the dimming of sight and judgement alike.
  • Tavern adoption: drink joins age as the great blearer, and the adjective turns comic without losing its water.
  • Softening suffix: bleary supplants blear in affectionate use, the -y turning a diagnosis into a description.
  • Victorian ubiquity: timetables and gaslight make bleary mornings a mass urban experience and a literary stock-in-trade.
  • Modern transfer: from faces to photographs and dawns, the adjective migrates from the viewer to the viewed.
  • Honest obscurity: no secure Germanic or PIE ancestor, a debated origin openly declared rather than invented.

So Sam, jumping up rather bleary, stands at the kind end of seven centuries: no longer pitied as infirm, only recognised, with love, as every sleeper hauled too soon from dreams.
