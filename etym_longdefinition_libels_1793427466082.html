Libels is the plural form of the noun libel, designating published defamatory statements, writings, prints, signs, or permanent representations that tend to expose an individual, group, or institution to public hatred, contempt, ridicule, or financial injury, or to injure them in their trade, office, or professional standing. In modern civil and tort jurisprudence, libel constitutes the permanent, visible branch of defamation, standing in stark contrast to slander, which encompasses oral, fleeting, or unrecorded defamatory speech. Because written and printed communications possess permanence, deliberateness, and the potential for widespread, enduring dissemination, the common law has historically treated libel with far greater severity than oral slander, often presuming general damages without requiring the plaintiff to prove specific, out-of-pocket pecuniary losses.

In the source text from the introductory letter of Jonathan Swift’s Gulliver’s Travels (‘A Letter from Capt. Gulliver to his Cousin Sympson’), Gulliver angrily protests the abusive reception of his book: ‘Yet, so far have you been from answering my expectation in any of your letters; that on the contrary you are loading our carrier every week with libels, and keys, and reflections, and memoirs, and second parts; wherein I see myself accused of reflecting upon great state folk; of degrading human nature (for so they have still the confidence to style it), and of abusing the female sex.’ In Swift’s early eighteenth-century Augustan milieu, libels referred directly to the torrential flood of hostile pamphlets, scurrilous lampoons, pirated keys, and anonymous personal attacks that Grub Street hack writers churned out to attack popular authors, mock ministers, and profit from literary controversies. Swift uses the word to dramatize the spiteful pettiness of the London literary marketplace, where honest philosophical satire is instantly met by a barrage of libelous polemics.

Primary and Secondary Modern Senses:

  • Actionable Published Defamation (Primary Legal Sense): In contemporary civil law, any false and unprivileged published statement conveyed in permanent form (print, digital media, broadcast, film, or posted images) that degrades reputation. In many jurisdictions, public officials or public figures must satisfy the rigorous standard of proving ‘actual malice’ (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth) to establish actionable libel.
  • Seditious and Criminal Libel (Historical Public Law Sense): The historic common law offense of publishing writings that provoked a breach of the peace, vilified the monarch, incited hatred against the government or church, or attacked the administration of justice. Under the authoritarian jurisprudence of the Tudor and Stuart Court of Star Chamber, truth was famously declared no defense (‘the greater the truth, the greater the libel’), because a truthful exposure was deemed even more provocative of public disorder and rebellion.
  • Admiralty, Ecclesiastical, and Civil Law Pleadings (Specialized Procedural Sense): In Roman, canon, and historical admiralty law, the libel was the formal initial written pleading, bill of complaint, or declaration submitted by a plaintiff (the ‘libellant’) setting forth the grounds of the claim and requesting judicial relief. While largely replaced by the term ‘complaint’ in modern civil procedure, this technical sense survived in maritime law well into the twentieth century.
  • Scurrilous Pamphlets, Lampoons, and Polemical Broadsides (Literary / Augustan Sense): Throughout the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, libels referred directly to short, published polemical booklets, satires, and character assassinations printed cheaply for wide circulation on the street, precisely as depicted in Swift’s letter.
  • Colloquial and Metaphorical Misrepresentation (Extended Sense): Loosely used in everyday conversation to describe any unfair criticism, damaging caricature, or gross mischaracterization that insults someone’s true character or achievement (e.g., ‘calling that gourmet meal bland was an absolute libel on the chef’).

The Semantic Bridge from ‘Little Book’ to ‘Defamation’: The extraordinary semantic journey of libels traces from Latin liber (‘tree bark’, hence ‘book’) through its diminutive libellus (‘little book, booklet, petition’). Because judicial complaints and petitions in Roman courts were drawn up as little written booklets, libellus became the legal term for a written charge. Concurrently, clandestine writers circulated defamatory anonymous pamphlets, which the Romans formally classified as libellus famosus (‘a defamatory booklet’). Over centuries of Anglo-Norman and English common law development, the qualifying adjective famosus was dropped, leaving libel to embody the defamatory character of the writing itself.
