MAGGOT names, in its literal core sense, the soft-bodied, legless larva of a fly — the white, wriggling grub that hatches from fly eggs in decaying flesh, rotting fruit, and other organic waste, feeding on the decomposing matter and eventually pupating into an adult fly. It is the standard everyday English word for this stage of the fly's life cycle. In the context sentence the word is used of a person — "the maggot who took my son confessed to an accomplice" — showing the word's second, insulting sense: a despicable, contemptible, or parasitical individual. The semantic bridge is transparent: a creature that breeds in rot, feeds on refuse, and squirms in corruption becomes an image of a morally rotten person.

THE MODERN SENSES IN FULL

  • Entomology, literal (core): the legless larva of a fly (order Diptera), found in decaying organic matter, carrion, dung, and wounds; plural "maggots" — "the rotten fruit was infested with maggots".
  • Figurative insult: a contemptible, despicable person — "he called the informer a maggot"; stronger and more visceral than "worm", implying moral rottenness and parasitism. Very common in military and coarse slang.
  • Fishing: "maggots" are a standard freshwater bait (also called "gentles"); in British angling "maggot" is a routine word.
  • Medical: "maggot debridement therapy" (MDT) — the deliberate, sterile use of blowfly larvae to clean necrotic tissue from non-healing wounds; a recognised modern clinical technique.
  • Obsolete idiom (17th–19th c.): "a maggot in the brain/head" — a strange whim, caprice, or obsessive fancy; hence the adjective "maggoty" (whimsical, capricious) and "maggot-headed". The wriggling motion of the larva was transferred to a "wriggling notion" in the mind.
  • Historical music/dance (17th c.): a "maggot" was a whimsical country dance or its tune (e.g., "My Lady Cullen's Maggot"); the whimsy sense lies behind this naming.
  • Adjective "maggoty": literally, infested with maggots ("a maggoty carcass"); figuratively, whimsical or (in dialect) ill-tempered.
  • Dialect and related uses: "maggot" is used loosely of other soft larvae (of moths, beetles), and in Scots of a whimsical or eccentric person.

THE HISTORICAL BRIDGE

Middle English magot (14th c.) is recorded for fly larvae, alongside the older maddok (13th c.); both belong to the Germanic stock of "worm, maggot" represented by Old English maþa and by Norse maðkr. The literal meaning was never lost. What changed over time was the figurative extension: in the 16th–17th centuries the word began to be used of fanciful whims (the strange "wriggling" of a fancy) and as a contemptuous term for a person, senses that hardened into the modern insult. The larvae's habitat of rot and waste supplies the moral image, while its restless wriggling supplies the image of a caprice that will not settle in the mind.

REGISTER AND MODERN USE

In literal use "maggot" is plain, everyday, and slightly unpleasant — a kitchen, farm, or fishing word. As an insult it is coarse and contemptuous, common in colloquial speech ("you maggot!"), often with military overtones (a sergeant addressing a recruit). The whimsy senses are now obsolete or archaic, surviving mainly in older literature and in the names of historical dances. The word has no positive connotations; it is used either neutrally of biology and angling or harshly of people, and its clinical return in "maggot debridement therapy" gives it an unexpected modern dignity in medicine.
