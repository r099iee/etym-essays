Indo-European base. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *men- "to remain, stay, abide, wait" — the root of Greek ménō "I endure, abide, stay", attested since Homer, and conventionally distinguished from the homophonous root meaning "to think". From *men- Latin built manēre "to remain, stay, hold out, await", a staple verb of Classical Latin from Plautus and Cicero onward.

Latin and the prefix re-. On manēre Latin prefixed re- ("back, again") to form remanēre, "to stay behind, to remain over, to be left" — used of soldiers who remain at their post, of possessions left behind, of whatever survives a subtraction. Its present participle remanēns (genitive remanentis) means "remaining", and a late abstract form remanentia, "the state of remaining; a remainder", passes into medieval Latin documents. It is this participle-and-abstract family, not the finite verb, that fed the Romance languages. Two Romance strands matter for English: Old French remaindre/remeindre gives English remain and the legal-brief remainder, while the participle stem remanant- gives Old French remanant, "the rest, the remainder, that which is left over" — the direct ancestor of our word. Anglo-French legal usage of remanentia in land law (the part of an estate that "remains" to be conveyed) is the ancestor of the modern property-law "remainder".

Middle English. English borrowed remanant in the 13th–14th centuries as remenaunt (also remanant, remeinaunt), meaning simply "the rest, the remainder" — of a period of time, of a sum of money, of a story, of a group of people. It is a workaday word in Middle English: chroniclers, romancers and petitioners use it exactly as we use "the rest". The spellings in -aunt reflect the nasal vowel of the French suffix and the same graphic instability seen in chant – chaunt or branch – braunch; the modern form remnant lost the weak vowel of the middle syllable in pronunciation (/ˈrɛm.nənt/), and the spelling followed.

The dressmaking centuries. By the 16th century the word had begun specializing toward the cloth trade: a "remnant" is a piece of cloth left on a roll after the rest has been cut and sold, sold by the draper at a reduced price. The cloth sense turns a large, abstract "remainder" into a small, concrete, buyable thing. Two or three centuries later the "remnant sale" and the "remnant shop" were fixtures of every town, and the word carried its commercial flatness through the industrial era.

The biblical remnant. The Reformation gave the word its deepest resonance. The sixteenth-century English Bibles — Tyndale's New Testament (1526), Coverdale's Bible (1535), and finally the Authorized Version (1611) — rendered the Hebrew and Greek terms for the surviving residue of God's people as "remnant": Isaiah's doctrine that a saving remnant of Jacob shall return (Isaiah 10:21, "The remnant shall return"), and Paul's assurance that "there is a remnant according to the election of grace" (Romans 11:5). From scripture the word passed into sermons, hymns and political-religious rhetoric: English Protestants described the "godly remnant" surviving persecution, and the term has been reused ever since for any faithful minority within a collapsing institution.

The nineteenth century. The Victorians industrialized the fabric sense while the romantic-historical prose of the period (Scott, Carlyle, Ruskin) favored "remnant" for what survives of empires, peoples and ages — giving the word its archaeological, melancholy colour. A novel could speak of "the remnant of a great house" and its readers would hear at once the Bible, the battlefield and the auction room.

The scientific twentieth century. Twentieth-century science annexed the word. As stellar astronomy matured, the expanding shell of debris left after a supernova explosion was named a "supernova remnant" (the Crab Nebula and its kind); cosmology speaks of relic or remnant radiation; conservation biology of "remnant populations" isolated in habitat fragments; cardiology of "remnant cholesterol". These are exact technical extensions of the old sense: what remains after the main event.

Register change overall. Remnant thus travelled from estate ledgers to drapery counters, pulpits, telescopes and textbooks, always keeping the same deep image: the part that remains after subtraction, loss or judgment. Its modern flavour — formal, faintly biblical, mildly mournful — is the accumulated residue of all those centuries of use, and it is exactly that gravity which makes Roäc's "small remnant of the great folk of Durin" in The Hobbit land with the weight of epic.
