- 15th Century (Middle English) - Originally adopted from Anglo-Norman French in military and chivalric chronicles to describe warriors storming a besieged castle or frontline combatants leaping into melee battle; perceived as a high-register martial term.
- 16th–17th Century (Early Modern English) - Expanded into legal terminology and criminal indictments to identify individuals guilty of felony assault; simultaneously gained rhetorical currency in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama for verbal tormentors.
- 18th–19th Century (Enlightenment & Victorian Era) - Broadened extensively in forensic journalism, police court gazettes, and detective fiction (such as Conan Doyle) to describe street ruffians, thugs, and violent felons threatening urban order.
- 20th–21st Century (Modern English) - Firmly entrenched as the standard formal and journalistic descriptor in criminal proceedings, forensic reports, and figurative medical/political discourse.
