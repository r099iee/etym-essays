“Culled” is the past participle of the verb “cull”, used here adjectivally: “selected, gathered, or harvested with care and discrimination from a range of sources.” In the passage, the old prosodic handbooks’ lists of recherché metres were culled from the poetry of Algernon Charles Swinburne and Robert Bridges — that is, combed out of those two poets’ work and collected for the classroom. The nuance is crucial. Culling implies deliberate, discriminating selection: the picking of the best or most serviceable pieces out of a larger body, with the quiet implication that whatever was rejected has been set aside. In this context the word carries a precisely balanced double edge. It grants the compilers a measure of scholarly respect — they did not invent their examples or stumble upon them; they searched Swinburne and Bridges and gathered what served them, and there is genuine care in the choosing. Yet the verb also carries a faint undertone of gleaning, of living off another’s orchard rather than growing one’s own, which fits the passage’s gently amused verdict on the second-hand character of these handbooks’ learning. The tone is that of a connoisseur who acknowledges good taste in borrowing while smiling at the borrowedness itself. The modern word is richly polysemous, and the sense chain explains how a single verb came to cover both picking out the choicest and removing the surplus:
  • Core sense — selective gathering: to choose and collect the best or choicest items from a larger group, with positive valuation (“he culled the finest examples from the anthology”). This is the sense in the passage.
  • Herd and flock management: to select and remove surplus, weak, sick, or unproductive animals from a population to keep the stock healthy, productive, or manageable (“the farmer culled the flock”, “culling the deer herd”).
  • Wildlife and conservation: controlled, deliberate removal of animals from the wild, usually for population control, disease prevention, or species management (deer culls, badger culls, seal culls); a scientifically routine but ethically controversial term in modern conservation discourse.
  • Figurative pruning: to cut out the unwanted elements of a collection, text, dataset, or body of work (“the editor culled dead passages from the draft”, “cull the evidence for what truly matters”).
  • Computer-graphics jargon: “culling” (backface culling, frustum culling, occlusion culling) means discarding invisible or out-of-view polygons, objects, or surfaces before rendering, to save processing power — a precise, technical extension of “removing the surplus”.
  • Data and programming jargon: culling records, rows, or outliers from a dataset; code-culling (removing dead code); the sense is always “discard what is not needed and keep what is”.
The historical bridge is the root itself: Latin colligere meant simply “to gather together”, and the Old French cuillir kept the neutral “gather, pluck, select”. English, however, fused two later developments: the positive “gather the best” (the gardener culling ripe fruit) and the negative “remove the worst” (the stockman culling the weak). Both remain alive, and which edge the word cuts depends entirely on context — here the positive, admiring edge, with the faintest shadow of the second. In register, “culled” is formal and literary, with agricultural and animal-stock roots that make it heavier, more deliberate, and more discriminating than “gathered”, “collected”, or “selected”. The boundaries against near-synonyms are precise. “Gathered” is neutral and casual; it names the action without judging it. “Harvested” implies agricultural or large-scale collection and is too mechanical for the fine discrimination at issue. “Compiled” is the dry, bookish word for assembling material from sources — closest in meaning, but it describes the act of building a list rather than the quality of the choices, and it cannot hint at pruning or rejection. “Plagiarized” and “lifted” would inject dishonesty and theft, which the passage emphatically does not intend: the handbooks are borrowing in the open, with acknowledged taste. “Gleaned” shares the farming metaphor and is very close, but it stresses picking up scattered remnants left behind after a harvest, whereas culled stresses positively picking out the choicest specimens from a standing crop. “Winnowed” images the removal of chaff — closer to rejection than to acquisition. “Curated” is the modern museum- and media-flavoured word for expert choice, but it lacks the agricultural ancestry and the possible edge of removal that culled still carries.
