The story of county is the story of a travelling companion who ended up owning the road. It begins with two of the oldest fragments of Indo-European speech: *kom, beside or with, and *h1ey-, to go. Latin fused them into comes, literally one who goes with you — a fellow-traveller, then a member of the emperor’s retinue, then a great officer of state. When the Western Empire crumbled, the Frankish kings kept the title and paid it in land: a comes, now a count, held a comitatus, a warlord’s territory to rule, tax, and defend. The companion had become a landlord, and the word for fellowship had become a word for turf.

The Norman Conquest carried that turf-word across the Channel. William’s clerks looked at the Anglo-Saxon shire — Wessex’s old administrative division, run by its shire-reeve, the sheriff — and called it a counte, the equivalent of a French count’s domain, even where no count ruled it. The name stuck with the tenacity of all conquerors’ paperwork: Domesday Book, the county courts, the knights of the shire summoned to Parliament — all filed under the French label. Phonetically the Old French conté softened in English mouths, the nasal vowel flattening and the final stress retreating, until county sounded as native as the shires it named. By Chaucer’s day the two words lived side by side, shire for poetry and the north, county for the court and the clerk.

From companion to court-house:

  • Rome — the retinue: comites ride with the emperor; the word means company, not country, and owns not an acre.
  • Francia — the fief: counts hold great territories as the king’s deputies; comitatus now means both the lord’s following and the land that feeds it.
  • 1066 — the relabelling: Norman scribes christen every English shire a county, and sheriff, county court, and county town grow up under the new sign.
  • The Atlantic crossing — the template: Virginia planters and New England townships lay out counties on the English pattern; the Revolution keeps the frame and elects the officers.
  • Industrial England — the institution: county councils, county constabularies, and county cricket (the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries) turn the old shire into a modern service — and a team to shout for.
  • Today — the two fates: in England the county softens toward heritage and sport while unitary councils do the work; in America 3,000-odd counties still tax, try, jail, and register, the Frankish warlord’s riding become the address on every deed.

What makes the word remarkable is how little of the count survives in it. No English county has a count — the nearest equivalents, the earls, lent their name to nobody’s map — yet the title rides on invisibly in every county council and county fair. And the deeper history hides a quiet irony that Cobbett, hater of Norman placemen, would have relished: the sturdy Saxon shire he loved to ride was wearing, and has worn for nearly a thousand years, the name-tag of the Conquest. The traveller’s contrast he admired in Surrey — meadow against heath, loam against gravel — unfolds entirely inside a word that crossed with William, was filed by his clerks, and stayed to become the most English of measurements.
