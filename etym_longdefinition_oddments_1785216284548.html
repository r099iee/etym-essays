Oddments (plural noun) means miscellaneous leftover articles — odds and ends; stray, disconnected objects of little individual value that remain after the main supply has been used, sorted, or distributed. The word is the plural of oddment, a formation of odd (surplus, extra, unmatched) plus the noun suffix -ment. In the context 'He found oddments in his pockets,' the tone is casual and mildly whimsical: the pockets yield no coherent collection and no treasure, but a jumble of trifling remnants — a stray button, a pencil stub, an old coin, a pebble — each too trivial to count as a possession, yet together suggesting the gentle clutter of ordinary living. The word carries a faintly old-fashioned, bookish flavour and stresses that the items are survivals or bits left over, not deliberate acquisitions; there is no suggestion of value, only of accumulated residue, and the plural form insists that they form a scatter rather than a single curiosity. Grammatically it is an ordinary count noun, and the plural is the form that normally appears when the scattered collection is meant; a single oddment would be one odd article.

Similar words differ subtly. Odds and ends is the plain conversational synonym for the same idea of miscellaneous leftovers, but it is looser in register and structurally a fixed, ancient pair. Remnants emphasizes pieces left behind after a larger whole has been consumed, especially cloth cut from a bolt. Scraps stresses smallness and inferiority, often of food or paper, with a slightly dismissive tone. Miscellany names a deliberate, curated collection of diverse items — a book of miscellany is a purposeful anthology, not an accidental pile. Bits and pieces is colloquial and even more informal, and odd lot (in trade) denotes a mixed batch sold as it stands. Unlike all of these, oddments preserves the etymological idea of the 'odd' — the extra, unpaired, surplus thing that does not fit the set — so the word implies objects whose oddity lies precisely in being unmatched and superfluous, each one the third glove of some vanished pair.


Polysemy and the full modern range of oddments:
  • Primary sense: miscellaneous odd articles; leftover bits; odds and ends — 'the drawer was full of oddments of string and buttons.' This is the sense in the passage.
  • Single-article sense (rarer, countable): an oddment is a single odd article, an extra, a leftover; the earliest recorded uses are accounting and commercial, where oddments are the surplus pieces of a stock, what remains after the main supply is gone.
  • Technical trade sense (printing and bookbinding, 19th–20th c.): in printing and bookbinding, oddments are the leftover sheets, waste leaves, and end-matter of a book — the surplus material remaining after the main work is printed. Bridge: the 'leftover bits' idea is hardened into a professional term for the physical residue of the printing process.
  • Figurative sense: oddments can name stray scraps of information, memory, or knowledge — 'the oddments of a misspent education' — miscellaneous unassimilated bits. Bridge: the leftover-object idea transfers to leftover ideas and recollections that fit no tidy system.
  • Register: old-fashioned, slightly bookish, and whimsical; everyday speech prefers 'odds and ends,' 'bits and pieces,' or 'bits and bobs.' Oddments is chosen in narrative and reflective prose for a deliberate, gentle quaintness, and its plural insists on a scatter rather than a single curiosity.
  • Related formations: the word family of odd — oddity (1713, a strange thing or person), oddball (twentieth century), odd duck, odds (sixteenth century, imbalance, then chance and likelihood), at odds (seventeenth century, in disagreement), over the odds, and odds and ends (eighteenth century) — shows the same root passing from arithmetic through the strange and the leftover; oddment is the nominalised 'odd thing' par excellence, the oddity stripped of wonder and reduced to a leftover.

In sum, oddments today names a scatter of unvalued leftovers, keeps a technical life in the printing trade, and reaches figuratively into stray bits of information and memory — always with the etymological ghost of the 'odd,' the unmatched surplus thing, behind it: a pocketful of oddments is a pocketful of thirds of vanished pairs, and the word's quiet humour lies in taking such surplus seriously enough to give it a name of its own.
