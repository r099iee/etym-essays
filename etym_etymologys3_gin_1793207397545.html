The drinking gin begins with a tree the Romans never distilled. Latin juniperus named the juniper whose berries flavoured wines and medicines around the Mediterranean, and Old French genievre kept both the tree and the flavour. The Dutch, Europe’s great early-modern distillers, steeped juniper in malt spirit to make genever — a cordial sold by apothecaries and drunk by soldiers — and English troops campaigning in the Low Countries through the Thirty Years’ War and William’s wars acquired the taste along with the catchphrase Dutch courage. After the Glorious Revolution, Parliament walled out French brandy with tariffs and let home grain be distilled almost free: geneva flooded London, and English tongues clipped it to gin by the 1720s.

What followed was the Gin Craze, the sharpest drink epidemic in English history. At its height London’s stills poured six gallons a year for every adult; drunk for a penny, dead drunk for twopence, clean straw for nothing advertised cellars where the poor drank themselves insensible. Mortality spiked, the birth-rate sagged, and magistrates warned of a city pickled. Parliament answered with a stutter of Gin Acts — licensing, duties, informers’ bounties — until the 1751 Act finally bit, aided by Hogarth’s Gin Lane, good harvests, and a Methodist revival. Gin sobered into respectability through the nineteenth century: the continuous Coffey still (1830) made a cleaner spirit, the Empire carried it to the tropics where quinine tonic made the sundowner medicinal, and the American bar crowned it in the martini.

The twentieth century gave gin two opposite careers. Prohibition made it the bootlegger’s easiest fraud — raw spirit with juniper oil, the bathtub gin that blinded and delighted jazz-age America — while vodka’s rise after 1950 nearly buried it as old-fashioned. Then came the craft resurrection: from the 2000s, small copper stills from London to Tasmania revived jewel-like gins with foraged botanicals, and the cocktail renaissance restored the martini and the negroni to fashion. Meanwhile the other gin — the trap from engine, cousin of ingenious and ingenue through Latin ingenium — kept its quiet country life in rabbit-warrens, and lent its name in 1793 to Whitney’s cotton engine, the machine that re-founded plantation slavery.

  • The tree: juniperus, of obscure Latin origin, flavours medicine and wine for two millennia before meeting the still.
  • The Dutch still: genever as cordial and courage — malt spirit, juniper, and the English wars in Flanders.
  • The Craze: penny gin, the 1736 and 1751 Acts, Hogarth’s propaganda-by-engraving, and a city’s slow recovery.
  • Empire and cocktail: tonic water, the officers’ būti, the Coffey still, and the American bar carry gin round the world.
  • The homonym: engine to gin — traps, snares, and Whitney’s fateful cotton machine travelling a wholly separate road.
  • Resurrection: craft stills and cocktail culture turn the demon drink respectable again.

Cobbett met gin at its moral lowest ebb among the rural poor — the bottle as proof of taxes, misery, and decline, the twin of St Giles’s in his demonology. That a century later the same three letters would promise juniper groves and copper stills on a cocktail menu is the word’s strangest distillation: from mother’s ruin to mother’s ruin bars, the spirit kept its juniper heart while England changed its mind around it.
