Scoundrels, the plural of scoundrel, is a hearty term of moral condemnation for men — and by extension people — deemed thoroughly dishonest, unprincipled, or villainous: cheats, liars, swindlers, and betrayers whom decent society scorns.

In the source sentence the word rings out as a direct accusation: You are a set of deceitful scoundrels, he said, turning to the others — the speaker unmasks his companions as knaves, men whose fair show hides fraud, and the plural sweeps the whole gang into one verdict.

Core meaning in detail: A scoundrel is not a grand tragic villain but a low one: the card-sharp, the defaulting debtor, the seducer, the informer, the political turncoat. The word judges character rather than any single crime — a scoundrel is someone in whom meanness seems settled and habitual. The plural sharpens the sting, since scoundrels hunt in packs: gangs, cliques, and conspiracies of rogues backing one another's deceit.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A dishonest unprincipled rogue (primary modern sense, from 1580s): a cheat or villain of low character; the accusation the source sentence hurls at the deceitful set.
  • A likeable rogue (affectionate, 18th–19th c.): the charming scoundrel of comedy and adventure — rakes, smugglers, and rogues with twinkling eyes whom audiences forgive. The bridge is familiarity: rogues long known become rogues half loved, and the scolding word softens into a wink.
  • A political or public villain (journalistic): corrupt officials and betrayers denounced as scoundrels in editorials and hustings speeches. The bridge is the word's public-oratorical register — it was born for denunciation from a platform.
  • A cowardly mean-spirited fellow (colloquial): one who bullies the weak, deserts friends, or kicks a man when he is down. The bridge runs from dishonesty to baseness: the scoundrel's fraud shades into general contemptibility.
  • An unfaithful lover (literary and melodramatic): the scoundrel who trifled with her affections haunts Victorian novels and stage melodrama. The bridge is betrayal of intimate trust — the domestic form of the cheat's fraud.
  • A cheat at cards, dice, or wagers (gaming): the card-table scoundrel with aces up his sleeve and dice that obey his thumb. The bridge is fraud made dexterous: cheating ceases to be mere lying and becomes a manual trade, and the word follows the money across the green baize.
  • A heartless seducer or fortune-hunter (romantic): the scoundrel who trifles, promises, and vanishes, leaving debts and broken engagements. The bridge runs from public fraud to private faithlessness: the same man who forges a bill will forge a vow.

Register and connotation: scoundrels is theatrical and old-fashioned in the best way — the vocabulary of Dickens, of stage melodrama, of parliamentary insult. It condemns without clinical coldness and without obscenity, which is why orators, novelists, and indignant parents still reach for it: strong enough to wound, clean enough for print, and always carrying a faint swashbuckling echo of cloaks, daggers, and exposed conspiracies.
