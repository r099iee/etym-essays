The compound rightabouts welds two profoundly old English materials — right, the straight and lawful hand, and -abouts, the vagabond suffix of neighbourhood — into a word that marched from the parade square to the prairie.

Right: the straight hand that rules. Behind right stands PIE *h₃reǵ-, to move in a straight line, to direct and rule, the root that also yields Latin regere and rex, king. Germanic shaped it into *rehtaz, straight and just, which arrived in Old English as riht: straight in geometry, just in morals, and the right hand in the body — the hand that throws the spear and swears the oath. By Middle English right already governed direction, correctness, and entitlement together, so that to turn right was to turn toward the strong, skilful, lawful side, opposed to the left, long burdened with sinister associations. Drill language seized on this clarity: Right! could never be mistaken on a noisy field.

Abouts: the grammar of neighbourhood. The second half begins in Old English abutan, literally on-be-utan, on the outside of, built from utan, out. It meant around, round about, on every side. Middle English aboute widened it to near to and concerning, and early modern English added the adverbial genitive -s, the same -s heard in always, besides, and towards, producing abouts as an independent adverb and noun: the parts round about. From it grew a whole family — thereabouts, hereabouts, whereabouts — each naming a vague but serviceable district. Rightabouts is the directional cousin of that family, the district defined not by here or there but by right.

  • Drill codification (17th–18th c.): English, Prussian, and later American manuals teach facings — right face, left face, right about face — as the alphabet of discipline. The command falls in two beats so boots strike together; fusion in memoir spelling follows naturally.
  • Compound fixing (18th–19th c.): Right-about, then rightabout, enters dictionaries of military art beside left-about. The plural rightabouts generalises the exercise, exactly as horsemen speak of practising voltes rather than one volta.
  • Frontier migration (mid-19th c.): volunteer regiments and waggon-trains carry parade language west. A captain who once wheeled recruits now wheels families and stock; to the rightabouts slides from pivot to place, the quarter a rider must cover.
  • Literary colour (late 19th–20th c.): war memoirs, western stories, and boys’ adventure papers keep the shout alive because it stages authority in six syllables: hide, cover, despatch, obey.

Sound and spelling: the stress falls hard on RIGHT-, with -abouts hurrying after, mirroring the movement itself — decision, then swing. Spellings wobble between hyphen, space, and fusion for a century, settling in reprints as the single word when the command hardens and as two words when the sense is purely local. No sound shift is at work beyond ordinary compounding rhythm; the history is semantic, not phonetic.

Why the two senses belong together: both pivot on the body’s right. The drill turn rotates the body toward the right rear; the frontier noun rotates attention toward the right-hand country. The suffix -abouts is the hinge, converting a direction of motion into a region of action. To understand rightabouts is therefore to watch English do what it does best: take a barked order, wear it smooth on the march, and spend it as geography.
