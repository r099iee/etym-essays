- Late 14th to 15th Century (Middle English Theological Inception) - Borrowed via Anglo-Norman and Late Latin; used primarily in ecclesiastical law and devotional treatises to designate moral repentance, self-correction, and the ethical reformation of one's conduct (emendatio vitae).
- 16th to 17th Century (The Humanist Philological Revolution) - Rapidly specializes during the Renaissance Revival of Letters under humanists like Erasmus, Scaliger, and Casaubon; becomes the definitive scholarly term for restoring classical Latin, Greek, and biblical texts corrupted by medieval scribes.
- 18th Century (Neoclassical and Shakespearean Battles) - Explodes into mainstream English literary consciousness through the monumental rival editions of Shakespeare (Lewis Theobald, Alexander Pope, Samuel Johnson); the validity of editorial "emendations" becomes the subject of fierce public satire and intellectual polemics.
- 19th Century (Lachmannian Scientific Philology) - Codified into nineteenth-century German stemmatics and scientific textual criticism under Karl Lachmann; strictly demarcated into emendatio ope codicum (by manuscripts) and emendatio ope ingenii (by editorial conjecture).
- 20th–21st Century (Modern Genetic Criticism and Editorial Ethics) - Operates as the central ethical touchstone in modern literary scholarship (especially in Proust, Joyce, and Woolf studies), strictly contrasting principled, evidence-based emendations against unauthorized editorial tampering and censorship.
