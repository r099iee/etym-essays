Ascent is the English noun for the act of going up, and its history is a textbook example of how Latin verbs, once borrowed, continue to generate new native English words. The root is Indo-European *skand-, 'to leap, spring, dart', a root that in its oldest attested forms describes sudden, springing motion: Sanskrit skándati means 'he leaps, springs, darts', and the same root, with a notion of springing up or springing off, lies behind Greek skandalon, 'a stumbling-block, a trap' — the thing one springs on or trips over, source of our scandal and scandalize. In Latin the root developed from 'leap' to 'climb': scandere meant 'to climb, mount', of scaling a wall, boarding a ship, or mounting a horse, and Latin built a whole family on it — descendere 'to climb down', ascendere 'to climb up', transcendere 'to climb across or beyond' (our transcend).

The compound ascendere is made of ad-, 'to, toward', with scandere, the -d assimilating to -s- before the following sc-, a regular Latin sound-sandhi; the verb means literally 'to climb to, climb up'. From its past participle stem ascēns- Latin formed the fourth-declension noun ascēnsus, 'an ascending, a climbing up', and from the same stem came the ecclesiastical ascēnsio, 'ascension', which entered English early through the Christian calendar — the Ascension, Christ's rising into heaven — and remains a separate, solemn word. The everyday secular noun, however, was created in English rather than borrowed. The verb ascend itself had entered Middle English in the fourteenth century from Old French ascendre, and by the early seventeenth century English speakers, seeing the established pair descend : descent, formed the matching ascent on the same model: ascend : ascent. The first recorded uses cluster around 1610. This is a small but significant fact of word-history: one of English's most dignified nouns of rising was made in the workshop of the language itself, not imported whole, by the same analogical habit that gives us pairs like accuse and accusal.

Once formed, ascent expanded rapidly along two tracks. Literally, it named the act of climbing — the ascent of a mountain, the first ascent of a peak, the ascent of a balloon or of a bird — and the upward path itself, a steep ascent in the road. Figuratively, it named every kind of rise in rank, fortune, or reputation: the ascent to power, the ascent of man from savagery to civilization, the soul's ascent to God. The twentieth century gave the word its modern technical careers: in aviation and rocketry, "ascent" is the climb phase of flight and the "rate of ascent" its vertical speed; the Apollo Lunar Module's ascent stage is the part that lifts off the Moon; in music, an ascent is a rising passage of melody; in astronomy the ascending node is where a body's path crosses the plane of reference. The spelling trap with assent — the noun meaning 'agreement', from Latin ad- + sentīre 'to feel' — is a reminder that identical pronunciation is no guarantee of common ancestry: the two words rhyme perfectly but share nothing but the initial syllable. The sound changes in the descent of ascent itself are few — the classical -sc- remained /sk/ in English, the prefix settled as /ə/ in normal speech, and the final consonant cluster simplified — so that the modern pronunciation /əˈsent/ is nearly a letter-for-letter continuation of the spelling settled in the seventeenth century.

What makes ascent an interesting word historically is not sound change but semantic geometry. Every sense — mountaineering, political rise, flight dynamics, melodic rise, spiritual elevation — is the same upward vector applied to a different domain, and the vector's direction was fixed by the ancient image of leaping and climbing. When the card's context says the whole of King's Landing "must witness his __________", the word does in one noun what the Roman verb ascendere did in one verb: it plots a course upward through power, and the climb is witnessed, chronicled, and remembered. Four thousand years separate the springing skand- of the Sanskrit texts from a political ascent in Westeros, but the direction of travel has never changed.
