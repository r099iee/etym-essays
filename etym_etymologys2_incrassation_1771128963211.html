INCRASSATION is a single-root word; its chain is linear: 1. Proto-Indo-European *ker- 'to grow, nourish, increase' (c. 4000–3500 BCE) — the source of Latin creare 'to create' and crescere 'to grow'. Note: the link between this root and Latin crassus is the traditional etymology but is DEBATED; some scholars prefer *ker- 'to make firm, strengthen', and no definitive PIE etymon for crassus is universally agreed. 2. Latin crassus 'thick, fat, dense, solid' (attested from the 2nd c. BCE, Plautus), understood on the traditional view as 'grown thick, accumulated'. 3. Latin incrassare 'to make thick, to thicken' (in- + crassus), attested in classical and post-classical Latin (e.g. Pliny the Elder, 1st c. CE, of thickening fluids and plant parts). 4. Latin incrassatio, incrassatiōnis 'a thickening, a making thick' — post-classical / Late Latin noun of action, attested from c. the 4th–6th c. CE in medical and natural-historical writing. 5. English incrassation — borrowed from Latin incrassatiōnem in the 16th–17th centuries, first recorded in medical and alchemical contexts; modern English INCRASSATION.
