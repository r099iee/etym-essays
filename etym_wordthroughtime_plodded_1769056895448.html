- Late 16th century - 'plod' enters English as an echoic verb of the heavy footstep; its plosive 'pl-' and dull '-od' imitate weighted, reluctant walking on land.
- 17th century - the word extends metaphorically from the road to the desk: to 'plod' at work or studies is to toil doggedly and without brilliance, and a 'plodder' becomes the steady, unglamorous drudge of proverbs and satire.
- 18th-19th century - the figurative sense dominates public perception: 'plod' connotes dogged, unstoppable application, admired as perseverance even while mocked as dullness, while the literal walking sense stays current.
- Contemporary - 'plod' straddles foot and mind: heavy, flat, monotonous progress in either sphere; the preterite 'plodded' suspends the action in one long, weary, unheroic walk, quietly sympathetic rather than tragic.
