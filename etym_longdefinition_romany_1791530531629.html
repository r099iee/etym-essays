Romany, also spelled Romani or Rommany, is an English noun and adjective referring to the language and people of the Roma. In careful contemporary usage Romani is the preferred spelling for the language and Roma for the people; Romany belongs to an older British tradition, in which it named both the language and an individual Roma person. The word is an endonym borrowed into English, not an external label like Gypsy. Its core is Romani rom "man, husband, a Rom", plural roma, with the adjective-forming ending -ni: romani means "of the Roma" and, in the phrase romani chib, "the Romani tongue".

Primary sense: the language. Romany is an Indo-Aryan language of the Indo-Iranian branch of Indo-European, spoken by Roma communities across Europe, the Americas, and parts of Asia. Estimates of speakers vary widely, from roughly three to four million, because many communities are bilingual and census categories are inconsistent. It is not a single standardized language but a family of dialects: Balkan Romani, Vlax Romani, Central Romani, Northern Romani, and the distinctive Sinti and Kale varieties, among others. Mutual intelligibility varies, and speakers often use the language mainly in the home, alongside a national language. Some countries recognize Romani as a minority or official minority language; it has an ISO 639-3 code (rom) and a growing written literature, though orthographic conventions differ.

Secondary sense: a person. In nineteenth- and early twentieth-century English, a Romany could mean a Roma man or woman, and the Romany could mean the Roma collectively. This usage is now dated and is often replaced by a Rom (plural Roma) or a Romani person. The noun can also mean the language itself, without an article: He speaks Romany. The adjective Romany describes Roma culture, music, history, or speech: Romany songs, Romany traditions, a Romany family. In older literary English the adjective also carried a romantic, wandering, free-spirited colour, a legacy of the Gypsy stereotype rather than of Romani self-description.

Tertiary and technical senses. In linguistics, Romani is the standard term for the language; Romany is a variant spelling, and Romanes is an adverb or adjective used by some British Roma ("to speak Romanes"). Para-Romani denotes mixed varieties in which Romani lexicon is combined with the grammar of another language, such as Anglo-Romani or Scandoromani. In anthropology and Romani studies, Romany survives in older titles and in the names of societies and journals. The word is also used in the titles of literary works, most famously George Borrow's Romany Rye (1857), where Romany Rye means a Roma gentleman or a non-Roma who has adopted Roma ways. Such uses belong to a romantic tradition that modern scholarship treats with caution.

Modern usage and semantic bridge. The semantic bridge from rom "man, husband" to a whole people and language is a common pattern in ethnonyms: the ordinary word for "person" or "man" becomes the name of the group, and the adjective formed from it becomes the name of the language. English borrowed the form in 1812, at a time when European philologists were discovering the Indian origin of Romani and when the older exonym Gypsy was beginning to be seen as inaccurate. Romany therefore entered English as a corrective, self-derived term. In the later twentieth century, Roma activism and the first World Romani Congress (1971) favoured Roma and Romani; Romany became a British archaism, still used in names and in literary English but less common in institutional writing. It is not inherently pejorative, though it can sound old-fashioned or romanticized.

  • Language - the Indo-Aryan language of the Roma, also called Romani.
  • Person - a Roma man or woman (dated; now usually Rom or Roma).
  • Adjective - of or relating to Roma people, culture, or speech.
  • Linguistic - a dialect cluster, not a single standardized tongue; ISO rom.
  • Literary - a romanticized term in older English writing, as in Romany Rye.
