Single root word: prospectors (plural of prospector). Stage 1 — PIE *spek- "to observe, to look, to watch": reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root (Pokorny *spek-), the family's great seeing-root, attested across Indo-European in words of sight, watching, and spying. Stage 2 — Latin specere "to look, to see": from *spek-, attested from early Latin; the base of a large family of compounds. Stage 3 — Latin prospicere "to look forward, to foresee": compound of pro- ("before, forward") + specere; attested in classical Latin (Cicero, Caesar). The semantics: to look out ahead, to see what is coming. Stage 4 — Latin prospectus "a looking forward; a distant view; expectation": the verbal noun of prospicere (past participle prospectus, "looked forward to"); attested in classical Latin. Stage 5 — English prospect "a view; a distant landscape; an expectation": borrowed from Latin prospectus in the 15th century (a prospect of the countryside from a height; a prospect of success). Stage 6 — English prospect "a place showing signs of minerals" and to prospect "to search for minerals": the mining sense recorded from the 18th century (a gold prospect; to prospect a district). Stage 7 — English prospector "one who searches for mineral deposits": formed from the verb to prospect with the agentive suffix -or; first recorded in the 19th century, in the era of the California gold rush; the plural prospectors is regular. Note on nuance: the chain is wholly secure — there is no debated stage. The only point of interest is that English built prospector by derivation on its own verb (prospect + -or) rather than borrowing a Latin agent-noun, so the modern word is an English coinage sitting on a Latin root. Hindi's everyday dekhnā ("to see") is NOT a reflex of *spek- (it descends from PIE *derḳ-, cf. Sanskrit dṛś-), a fact worth stating to avoid a false cognate.
