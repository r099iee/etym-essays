- 17th c. — an exotic, Gallic term for French castles and noble houses, used by travellers and translators.
- 18th c. — the Grand Tour and French travel writing make “chateau” the standard English term for a French country seat, especially the Renaissance houses of the Loire.
- 19th c. — the Bordeaux wine trade turns the word into a commercial designation for a wine estate; “château-bottled” and “château” labels give it a second, mercantile life.
- 20th c. — tourism and heritage consolidate the romantic sense: a château is a place to visit, rent or restore, and “château life” becomes a lifestyle cliché.
- 21st c. — in English the word is mostly wine, travel and property vocabulary; the English spelling dominates outside France, and the older sense “French castle” survives mainly in historical writing such as Carlyle’s.
