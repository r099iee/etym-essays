"Trampled" is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb "trample": to tread heavily and repeatedly on something or someone, stamping, pressing, and crushing underfoot. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2 ("Roast Mutton"), Bilbo, hiding from the goblin rush, fears being "bumped into, ________ to death, or caught by feel" — the word names one of the three ways the blind horde could kill him: not by a deliberate blow, but by the sheer mass of many feet pressing down on him as the goblins pour through the tunnel, crushing the life out of him beneath the stampede. The tone is grim and bodily: "trampled" makes the reader feel the weight of feet, the dark floor of the cave, the helpless smallness of a body underfoot; it is a death without agency — no enemy chooses it, it is simply the accident of being beneath a crowd. Core semantics. The verb implies repetition and weight: not one step but many; not a light touch but a grinding press; often the tread of many feet at once — a herd, a mob, a stampede, cavalry. It is used of:
  • vegetation and crops (grass trampled down by a crowd, flowers trampled underfoot);
  • bodies and persons (trampled to death in a stampede or a charge);
  • objects and places (a document trampled underfoot in contempt);
  • and, by clear figurative extension, abstractions: rights, laws, feelings, and dignity "trampled on" or "trampled underfoot".
The figurative sense is old and vigorous: to "trample on" something is to treat it with brutal contempt and careless disregard, as if it were grass beneath boots. The heavy foot has become the emblem of tyranny, bigotry, and callousness — "he trampled on her feelings", "the regime trampled the constitution". Near-synonyms, carefully separated:
  • trod / trodden — plain walking or stepping on something, without the sense of crushing force.
  • stamped — a single deliberate violent footfall, usually of anger.
  • stomped — heavy, angry walking (informal, chiefly American).
  • crushed — a general word for squeezing or breaking under pressure, not necessarily by feet.
  • squashed — flattened by pressure, often softer or informal.
  • flattened — pressed level with the ground, stressing the result rather than the repeated treading.
  • ground (into) — crushed by a twisting or abrasive pressure, or destroyed metaphorically.
  • run over / trampled down — the sense of being passed over by a mass or vehicle.
  • overwhelmed — a mental or emotional analogue, "crushed" by forces.
  • rode roughshod over — the idiomatic cousin meaning to disregard brutally, originally of a horse shod with studded shoes.
Polysemy & register. In the literal sphere "trampled" covers (1) the repeated treading of a single walker, (2) the mass-crushing of a stampede or charge, (3) the flattening of vegetation or ground, and (4) the trampling of a battlefield, where bodies and grass alike are pressed into the earth. In the figurative sphere it means (5) to treat a person, right, or value with contempt and disregard ("trample on", "trample underfoot"), and (6) in modern reporting, a "trampling" is the crowd-crush disaster itself. The historical bridge runs from the neutral gait-word of the 14th century (to "tramp" = to walk heavily) to the crushing verb of the mid-15th century (to "trample" = to beat down by continuous treading) to the moral metaphor of the 19th century. The word gained a victim, then a conscience, without ever losing its original heavy, repetitive foot.
