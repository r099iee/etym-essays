Annals is a plural noun denoting a concise historical record or chronicle of events arranged strictly year by year in chronological sequence. In its broader and more figurative modern application, it refers to historical records, archives, or public memory in general, often carrying connotations of monumental permanence, epochal significance, or institutional prestige (as in the phrase ‘to go down in the annals of history’). In academic and scientific publishing, it also designates periodic scholarly journals issued by learned academies and medical societies that document annual advancements in research.

In the source text from David Hume’s The History of England, the author outlines his historiographical strategy regarding the early periods of the British Isles. Hume dismisses prehistoric mythologies, moves swiftly through the Roman occupation, and declares his intention to ‘hasten through the obscure and uninteresting period of Saxon annals’. Here, Hume uses annals with acute philosophical intent: he refers specifically to early medieval monastic chronicles (such as the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle), which recorded dry, isolated occurrences—famines, eclipses, raids, and the accessions or deaths of petty chieftains—year by year without analytical commentary, political synthesis, or thematic cohesion. To an Enlightenment philosopher seeking moral instruction and civic wisdom from history, these barren yearly tabulations seemed ‘obscure and uninteresting’ compared to the fully articulated, verified historical narrative he reserves for later centuries.

Grammatical Profile and Morphological Status: Grammatically, annals is a pluralia tantum (a noun that appears almost exclusively in the plural form, derived from the Latin neuter plural annāles). While syntactically constructed with plural verbs (‘the annals demonstrate that...’), it is occasionally treated as a singular collective concept when referring to an entire body of historical records or an individual serial publication. Unlike a narrative history, which organizes events by causal connections and psychological motives, the structural hallmark of the annalistic format is its absolute subservience to the calendar year.

Exhaustive Polysemy, Modern Senses, and Domain Usages:

  • Chronological Year-by-Year Historical Records (Primary Strict Sense): Historical compilations organized strictly by the calendar year, in which the events of each twelve-month period are entered sequentially under that year's heading without an overarching narrative plot (e.g., Tacitus's Annales, the Annals of Ulster, the Annals of the Four Masters). The historical bridge connects this format directly to the ancient Roman practice of posting annual public tablets.
  • Comprehensive Historical Memory and Public Archives (Figurative / Monumental Sense): A broad, elevated synonym for historical records, chronicles, or the collective memory of human achievement (e.g., ‘her courage has earned an enduring place in the annals of exploration’, ‘a tragedy unmatched in the annals of modern aviation’). The semantic bridge transfers the idea of an official ledger of noteworthy events to the metaphorical ledger of human memory.
  • Learned Journals and Scientific Periodicals: A formal title adopted by prestigious scholarly, mathematical, and medical publications that publish peer-reviewed papers at regular annual or periodic intervals (e.g., Annals of Mathematics, Annals of Internal Medicine, Annals of Botany). This usage developed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when learned academies adopted the prestigious Latinate term to denote their yearly published transactions.
  • Historiographical Methodology (The Annales School): In the philosophy and methodology of history, the term specifically invokes the influential twentieth-century French historiographical movement known as the Annales school (founded in 1929 by Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre around the journal Annales d'histoire économique et sociale). This school revolutionized historical scholarship by rejecting traditional political event-history (*histoire événementielle*) in favor of long-term socio-economic structures (*la longue durée*) and collective mentalities.
  • Rhetorical Antithesis to Narrative History: Used by literary theorists and philosophers of history (such as Hayden White) to distinguish between three escalating modes of historical representation: the annals (a simple list of events arranged by year without narrative closure), the chronicle (a continuous account with narrative flow but lacking a centralized moralizing plot), and history proper (a fully plotted narrative explaining causal mechanisms).

Register, Connotation, and Stylistic Value: Annals belongs to an elevated, formal, and dignified register. It confers gravitas upon any subject it modifies, transforming mundane human actions into permanent entries in the grand ledger of time. Writers employ the word to evoke permanence, solemnity, and archival authority, warning readers that the events described will be scrutinized by generations yet unborn.
