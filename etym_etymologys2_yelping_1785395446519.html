Yelp is a native English verb; the participle adds the ordinary present-participle suffix. The chain runs from the reconstructed Indo-European root through Germanic into Old English, with a documented semantic shift along the way. Chain — "yelp" (present participle "yelping"): - PIE *ǵʰel- (also reconstructed *gʰel-) "to shout, to cry out, to resound" — a root of disputed exact reconstruction, traditionally used for Germanic and Balto-Slavic words of calling and resounding; the laryngeal and palatal details are debated → Proto-Germanic *gelpaną "to boast, to exult, to cry aloud" (reconstructed; attested through Old English gielpan, Old Norse gjálpa "to yelp, to brag," Middle Dutch ghelpen "to shout") → Old English gielpan "to boast, to exult, to glory in oneself; to cry aloud" (strong verb class III; attested from the earliest Old English, including the poetic record of Beowulf) → Middle English yelpen "to cry out loudly, to shout; to boast" (12th–15th centuries; the initial g- had by now palatalised to y-) → Early Modern English yelp "to utter a loud sharp cry; specifically, to cry out in the short, sharp, high-pitched manner of a dog in pain or alarm" (16th–17th centuries; the boastful sense dies out) → Modern English yelp "to utter a short, sharp, high-pitched cry of pain, surprise, or distress" → yelp + -ing (the present-participle suffix) = YELPING "uttering short, sharp cries." Chain — the present-participle suffix -ing: - PIE *-ont- / *-ent- (the active-participle suffix) → Proto-Germanic *-and- (the present-participle suffix) → Old English -ende (the present-participle ending) → Middle English -ende / -inge / -ing (the ending merges with the verbal-noun suffix -ung/-ing, 12th–15th centuries) → Modern English -ing, the suffix of the present participle and verbal noun, giving yelping. Note on debate: the exact PIE root behind the Germanic *gelp- set is not securely attested; *ǵʰel- is the traditional reconstruction, but the root has a thin Indo-European distribution and some scholars treat the Germanic set as an independent onomatopoeic formation. The semantic shift from "boast" (Old English gielpan) to "utter a dog's cry" (modern yelp) is fully documented across Middle and early Modern English and is not in dispute; the palatalisation of initial g- to y- before front vowels is the regular Old-to-Middle English sound change (compare gieldan → yield, gear → year).
