Purport is both noun and verb centred on meaning and claim. As a noun (pronounced /ˈpɜːpɔːt/), it means the essential meaning, gist, drift, or substance of something said or written, especially its intended point. As a verb (pronounced /pəˈpɔːt/), it means to claim, profess, or appear to be something, often without proof, and to have as its ostensible meaning. In the sentence "I did not get the purport," it is the noun: I did not grasp the point or gist.

The core image is of carrying: a message carries a meaning as a vessel carries cargo. The purport is what is carried forth to the recipient. If you get the purport, the cargo arrived; if you do not, it miscarried. This is why purport collocates with documents, remarks, memos, and letters rather than with physical objects.

Two tones must be distinguished. The noun is neutral and formal: the purport of the speech was reconciliation. The verb is often suspicious: he purports to be a surgeon. The same carrying metaphor yields both: neutral conveying versus potentially false labelling.

  • Noun - the gist, substance, or main point (of speech or writing): the primary modern nominal sense. Example: the purport of the email was an apology. The semantic bridge is containment: as a jar contains preserves, a text contains a point; purport names that contained point.
  • Noun - intention, purpose, or tenor (formal, legal): to the purport and effect that..., the purport of the statute. Here purport shades from meaning toward legal intent, what the drafters meant to achieve.
  • Verb - to have as its meaning, to signify (neutral): the clause purports that all members are equal. The subject is usually a text or utterance; the sense is simply means.
  • Verb - to claim or profess falsely or ostensibly to be something (wary): a man purporting to be a policeman, documents purporting to show fraud. This is the dominant journalistic and legal use: it reports a claim while withholding endorsement, protecting the reporter from libel.
  • Verb - to intend or purpose (archaic): they purported a journey to London. Once common in the 15th-17th centuries, now obsolete except in conscious archaism; it preserves the Latin pro- (forth) as purpose.
  • Adjectival participle - purported (alleged, reputed): the purported author, a purported confession. Though technically a participle, it functions as a wary adjective signalling unverified status, indispensable to news style.

Register is formal, official, and slightly legalistic. You ask a friend for the gist, a colleague for the purport. Purport promises precision: not every detail, but the exact point that matters. Unlike meaning (broad), import (weighty consequence), or tenor (general drift), purport stresses intended carriage: what the sender meant you to receive.

In short, as a noun purport means the intended gist carried by words, and as a verb it means to carry (or claim to carry) that gist or identity, often under suspicion.
