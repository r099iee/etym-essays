Hemmed is the past tense and past participle of the verb hem, used here in its enclosing sense: "hemmed in" or "hemmed about" = surrounded, confined, shut in on every side so that escape is hard or impossible. Tolkien's sentence — "they were forced into a great ring, facing every way, hemmed all about with goblins and wolves returning to the assault" — is a textbook instance: the survivors are fenced round by enemies on every side, exactly as cattle are hemmed in a fold. The participle is used because the enclosing is complete: the perimeter is already drawn, and the ring is closed.

  • Sense 1 — to enclose or confine. From Old English hemman, "to enclose, check, restrain", the verb has always pictured a physical perimeter: hemming sheep into a pen, hemming a fleeing army against a river. The participle constructions that dominate — "hemmed in", "hemmed about", "hemmed round" — spell the perimeter out explicitly; the bare past "hemmed" carries the same meaning when the context supplies the ring, as in our sentence, where "hemmed all about with goblins and wolves" names both the ring and its makers.
  • Sense 2 — to edge or border a garment. The same verb, in sewing: to hem is to fold and stitch the raw edge of cloth into a neat, finished border — "she hemmed the skirt". The two senses converge in the noun hem, "the border or edge of cloth": a garment's hem encloses the fabric's cut edge just as a ring of enemies encloses its victims. English speakers feel the two as one word, and the knot of senses — border, enclose, confine — is exactly what the Germanic ancestor carried.
  • Figurative and modern use. Today "hemmed in" is overwhelmingly metaphorical: hemmed in by debts, by regulations, by mountains, by circumstance. The bridge is the fold: from the physical enclosure of animals and garments, the word moved to social and psychological pressure — a person "hemmed in" is confined as if by a wall, though no wall is visible. The construction "hemmed in by X" is the standard modern frame, and the participle is far commoner than the plain past tense.
  • Form, grammar and false friends. "Hemmed" is regular (hem–hemmed–hemmed), with the consonant doubled before the past ending; the noun hem, the compound hemline and the fashion verb all belong to this word. But "to hem and haw" (to hesitate, dither, clear one's throat) is a wholly different, imitative word from the 17th century — identical sound, unrelated origin — a classic learner's trap; "hem" as an interjection (a throat-clearing "hm!") is the same imitative family.

Semantic bridge. The thread is enclosure. A Germanic verb meaning "to restrain, halt, enclose" (cf. Old Norse hemja "to check, curb", Old High German hemmen "to hinder") gives English its enclosing hem; the garment-hem arises because sewing a border literally encloses the cut edge; and every modern "hemmed in" — by goblins, by debt, by rules — redraws the same perimeter around a person. The word has never left its first picture: a ring drawn tight, whether of cloth, fence or enemy.
