- c. 1300s — the barnyard blast: Middle English snorten describes horses, boars, and snoring sleepers — pure animal and bodily noise, no attitude yet.
- 16th–17th c. — humans borrow it: writers let angry and scornful men snort, transferring the stable blast to human contempt; the nose becomes an organ of opinion.
- 18th–19th c. — the novelist's dialogue tag: Fielding, Dickens, and their heirs plant he snorted after speeches to stamp them as derision, fixing the sneering sense in fiction.
- Late 19th c. — laughter joins in: snorting with laughter spreads as Victorian propriety demands suppressed mirth, and the nose becomes laughter's escape valve.
- 20th c. — the street takes it sideways: to snort a drug reverses the airflow, and a snort of whisky names a quick sharp drink.
- Today — the default signal of fictional contempt: from newspaper cartoons to fantasy dialogue, snorted instantly tells the reader that the words it carries are meant as their opposite.
