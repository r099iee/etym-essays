MISLIKE (verb; also archaic noun) — to dislike, to feel aversion toward, or to disapprove of; in its oldest English uses, "to displease" (impersonal, "it mislikes me" = "it displeases me"); as a noun, a feeling of displeasure, aversion, or suspicion. The word is today archaic and literary, a consciously old-fashioned synonym of dislike that survives in poetry, historical fiction, and fantasy prose — and it is a favourite of writers from Shakespeare to George R. R. Martin who reach for it precisely because it is antique. Core senses and usages
  • Transitive verb sense (the main modern sense): to dislike, to view with aversion or disapproval. "I mislike the tone of his letter." The object may be a person, a thing, a course of action, or a tone — the feeling is intuitive, instinctive, and often a little mysterious.
  • Intransitive / impersonal historical sense: in Old and early Middle English the verb was often impersonal: me mislīcað = "it displeases me", "I am displeased with it". The word's earliest meaning was "to be displeasing, to offend", and the polarity — whether the grammatical subject is the thing disliked or the person disliking — was fluid. Traces of this old impersonal construction survived into early modern English.
  • Noun sense (now rare or archaic): a mislike, a feeling of dislike, aversion, or suspicion. "He conceived a mislike of the whole enterprise." The noun was more common in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries than it is now.
  • Literary-register sense: in modern prose, mislike is chosen over dislike to convey a deep, subtle, intuitive aversion — a foreboding, a distaste that the speaker cannot quite justify. It is a favourite of fantasy and historical dialogue (as in the works of George R. R. Martin, where characters "bear a mislike" for one another), and of literary criticism describing archaic effect.
  • Derived formations: misliking (noun and participle), misliker (one who mislikes). The adjective mislikeful is attested in early modern English but did not survive.
Why the word means what it does — the semantic bridge Mislike is a native English compound built in Old English: the prefix mis- (from Proto-Germanic *missa-, "badly, wrongly, amiss") reversed the polarity of the verb līcian, "to please, to be agreeable". Old English mislīcian therefore meant "to displease, to offend, to be disagreeable" — the exact reverse of līcian. The verb is attested from about the year 1000, and it remained the ordinary English word for "to displease" and "to dislike" throughout the Old and Middle English periods: in Middle English misliken is standard, in both the impersonal ("it misliketh me") and the transitive ("I mislike him") constructions. The turning point came in the sixteenth century, when English borrowed and naturalized the Norman-French compound dislike (from Old French des- + the Germanic like). Within a century or two dislike had displaced mislike in ordinary speech: the two words meant the same thing, and the marketplace of synonyms kept the more current-looking one. Mislike did not die, but it receded into the literary and archaic registers, where it was kept alive by poets and by writers of historical or fantastical prose. Its archaic glamour is now its entire point: to use mislike is to signal an older, more formal, more instinctive world. Register and connotation Today mislike is marked as archaic, dialectal, or literary. It collocates with to bear a mislike for, to mislike the look of, and plain I mislike; it sounds faintly Elizabethan and faintly foreboding. It is the word of the historian, the poet, and the fantasy novelist, and its smallness — a five-hundred-year-old native verb surviving beside its Norman rival — makes it a treasure of English synonymy.
