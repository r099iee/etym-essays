The name Antoninus is a problem for the etymologist in the best sense: it refuses to be Indo-European, and so it forces the whole Indo-European apparatus aside and asks to be read instead as social history. It is a Roman family name that became a dynastic title that became an age-label — and along the way it accumulated false etymologies, legendary ancestors, and one very strange spelling accident in English. Its true root lies in the opaque onomastic stock of Etruria, and every other meaning attached to it is cultural rather than linguistic. The Etruscan root. The Latin nomen Antonius is widely judged to be Etruscan in origin. Etruscan itself is a non-Indo-European language isolate, and its personal-name material is only partially understood; no "meaning" for the base of Antonius can be responsibly reconstructed. This is the honest stopping-point: we know the name-form, we know it is ancient, and we do not know what it originally meant. The gens Antonia enters Roman history as a plebeian family; it produced magistrates, and its most famous Republican member is Marcus Antonius, the triumvir of 83-30 BCE, whose rivalry with Octavian and alliance with Cleopatra ended at Actium (31 BCE). Mark Antony's own family encouraged a heroic legend: the Antonii, it was said, descended from "Anton", a son of Hercules. That claim is political mythmaking in the service of a new dynasty, not an etymology — no Hercules son named Anton is attested anywhere. False etymologies. The name attracted folk etymologies as soon as it travelled beyond Latin. The most persistent links it to Greek anthos, "flower" — hence the idea that Antonius means "flowery" or "inestimable". The association is ancient but baseless, and it had a real-world consequence: in the Middle Ages Latin forms such as Anthonius appeared, and the English given name "Anthony" preserves an unhistorical h inserted on this false floral analogy. A rival popular etymology connected the name to anti-, "against", again without foundation. Scholarship (beginning with Schulze's great study of Latin onomastics) classifies Antonius among Etruscan-derived gentilicia and sets all these meanings aside. From nomen to cognomen. The step from Antonius to Antoninus is ordinary Latin derivation: the suffix -inus forms adjectives of belonging, so Antoninus means "of the Antonii, belonging to the Antonian family". As a cognomen it marked a branch or an adopted heir's claim on the name. In the second century AD this ordinary adjective was lifted into the imperial name. Antoninus Pius — born Titus Aurelius Fulvus Boionius Arrius Antoninus — took the cognomen as emperor (AD 138-161), and his adopted heir Marcus Aurelius ruled as Marcus Aurelius Antoninus (AD 161-180). The name thereby ceased to be a private family label and became a dynasty. The dynasty and the age. Because the Antonine succession ran by adoption, "Antoninus" became synonymous in Roman and later European thought with orderly, virtuous, adoptive rule — the reverse of the hereditary chaos that followed. Gibbon's grand thesis turns on this: the "happy period of more than fourscore years" is the age of the Antonines, and its end is marked not by a catastrophe but by the death of the last good Antonine, Marcus Aurelius, whose biological son Commodus then undid a century of adoptive wisdom. When Gibbon writes "from the death of Marcus Antoninus, to deduce the most important circumstances of its decline and fall", he is using the name as a chronological device whose dynastic resonance supplies the meaning of the boundary. The name's later career. The title outlived the dynasty. Third-century emperors exploited it: Caracalla styled himself Marcus Aurelius Antoninus; Elagabalus and Severus Alexander bore the name too. The word also passed into archaeology (the Antonine Wall in Scotland, built under Antoninus Pius in the 140s), into the history of disease (the Antonine Plague of 165-180), and into periodization ("the Age of the Antonines", praised by Gibbon as the period in which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous). In the Christian world, Antoninus is a saint's name — Saint Antoninus of Florence (1389-1459) — and the cognomen lives on in given names such as Italian Antonino. The English spelling accident. One more thread deserves note. The English given name "Anthony" descends from Latin Antonius by way of French Antoine; the famous "h" in Anthony appears in Middle English under the influence of the false Greek etymology (anthos, "flower"), exactly as if the name were spelled with an aspiration it never had in Latin. The result is that the same ancient Etruscan stem surfaces in English in two very different costumes: the vernacular Anthony (with the unetymological h) and the learned historiographical Antoninus (used by Gibbon). Conclusion. Etymologically, Antoninus is a short chain: an opaque Etruscan name-stock, the Latin nomen Antonius, the derivative cognomen Antoninus, and the English learned form. Its real depth is historical — a plebeian gens, a triumvir's myth, an adoptive dynasty, a golden-age boundary, and an empire's favorite brand of good government. No flower, no "against", no Hercules: just a name that worked so well it became an era.
