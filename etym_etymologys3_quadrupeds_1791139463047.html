Quadrupeds is the regular English plural of quadruped, itself a learned 17th-century borrowing of Latin quadrupes, from quadru- four plus pes foot. Its deep history therefore belongs to two of the most stable PIE roots: four and foot, both attested across nearly every Indo-European language.

PIE *kʷetwer- four yields Sanskrit catvaras, Greek tessares, Latin quattuor, Old Irish cethair, Old English feower (modern four). The qu- form in Latin quadru- is the combining stem used in compounds: quadrupes, quadruplex, quadriennium. PIE *ped- foot yields Sanskrit pad, Greek pous / podos, Latin pes / pedis, Old English fot (modern foot). Latin pes, genitive pedis, nominative plural pedes, provides the -ped in biped, quadruped, centipede, millipede.

  • Latin quadrupes: adjective and noun meaning going on four feet, a four-footed beast. Cicero, Pliny, and Varro contrast quadrupedes with bipedes (men, birds), pisces, and serpentes. Genitive quadrupedis, plural quadrupedes.
  • Late Latin / Medieval Latin: quadrupeda as noun for beast of burden, opposed to homo erectus. Bestiaries classify Creation by feet: quadrupedia, volatilia, reptilia.
  • Middle French quadrupede (15th c.): natural-history term revived with Aristotle translations. English antiquaries first use quadrupede, then Latinized to quadruped under Renaissance classicism.

English quadruped enters in the 1640s in the Pavilion of natural philosophy and Bible commentary. Sir Thomas Browne's Pseudodoxia Epidemica debates whether elephants are quadrupeds; translators of Buffon and Linnaeus cement quadruped as a taxonomic convenience, not a true clade. Linnaeus's Quadrupedia (1758) grouped mammals roughly by four feet, including whales awkwardly, later replaced by Mammalia. The plural quadrupeds with -s follows normal English morphology, replacing Latin quadrupedes.

Phonetically, Latin /kʷadrupes/ becomes French /kadʁyped/ then English /ˈkwɒdrʊped/ with stress on first syllable and anglicized vowels. Spelling stabilizes as quadruped by 1700. The quadru- prefix remains productive: quadrilateral, quadruple, quadruplet, quadbike.

  • Scientific use: quadruped locomotion, quadruped robot, quadruped gait. Biomechanics distinguishes lateral-sequence vs diagonal-sequence walking, trotting, galloping.
  • Literary use: as in the prompt, chattering quadrupeds is deliberately deflating: monkeys, dogs, or farm beasts imagined chattering like humans, then rising against man. The author groups them with centipedes and bacilli to suggest all non-human life resenting the marauding biped (man).
  • Figurative / insult: to call humans quadrupeds implies brutishness, going on all fours, loss of erect dignity.

Socio-culturally, quadruped marks the early-modern shift from theological beast to zoological mammal. To name something a quadruped was to place it in a comparative anatomy table, measuring its feet against human feet. Victorian evolution then revalued the term: quadruped versus biped became evidence of common descent, Huxley and Darwin arguing that man's erect bipedalism evolved from quadrupedal ancestors. Modern robotics revived the word positively: Boston Dynamics Spot is proudly a quadruped.
