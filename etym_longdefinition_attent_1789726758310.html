ATTENT is a rare, archaic and poetic adjective meaning watchfully attentive, keenly alert and mentally bent toward an object — the clipped, monosyllabic form of attentive preserved in verse for its concentrated force. In Ted Hughes's line "Terrifying are the attent sleek thrushes," it does not mean merely "awake": it means every nerve is stretched toward prey, eye, ear and muscle gathered into a single listening stillness that is already half an attack. The word carries an old sense of stretching toward (its Latin root), so that attention is felt as a physical tension, a bow-string of the senses drawn tight.

Primary sense today — vigilant, heedful alertness. Where it survives, attent denotes sharp, undivided observation: an attent eye, an attent ear, an attent thrush. It implies active, willed focus rather than passive wakefulness. A sleepless sentry is awake; an attent thrush is locked onto the faintest stir beneath the grass, body motionless but inwardly sprung. The connotation is predatory and electric rather than calm or contemplative. Hughes chooses attent over attentive precisely because the shorter form sounds harder, more darting and beak-like — two clipped syllables that strike rather than flow.

Secondary senses — intent, expectant, purposeful. Historically attent also meant intent upon, fixed in purpose, awaiting an outcome. Middle English writers use it for a mind "attent to" prayer, study, or a lover's command: diligent, zealous, not easily distracted. From this grows the sense of expectation: to be attent is to wait braced for what comes next, as the thrush waits for the worm's movement. The semantic bridge is natural: sustained looking hardens into readiness, and readiness hardens into intention. To attend to a thing long enough is to become aimed at it, and to be aimed is to be about to move.

Tertiary and archaic senses — courteous regard, obedient heed, devotional care. In older courtly and religious use, attent could describe respectful, dutiful listening: an attent servant, an attent congregation, attent ears turned to a lord or to God. Here the tension is social and moral rather than predatory — one stretches toward a superior in deference. That courteous sense has vanished from living speech, surviving only in the fossil attentive ("an attentive host," "attentive service"), but it explains why the word once felt warm as well as sharp. What Hughes does is strip away the courtesy and keep only the steel: attent without kindness, attention as hunger.

Domain, grammar and literary nuance. Grammatically attent is almost always attributive and predicative adjective ("the attent thrushes," "they are attent"), rarely adverbial, never verbal in modern use. Stylistically it is a deliberate archaism: no present-day speaker says "be attent" in conversation; poets use it to summon an older, leaner English and to tighten metre. In nature writing it has become a signal word for the anti-sentimental gaze — seeing the bird not as songster but as killer, "more coiled steel than living." The bridge from ordinary attentive to Hughesian attent is thus a bridge from benevolent noticing to lethal focus: the same stretched nerve, redirected from care to capture, from listening to lunging.

  • Primary — vigilant alertness: keenly watchful with all senses engaged; e.g. the attent eye that misses no stir. Bridge: Latin stretch toward becomes mental stretching toward a stimulus.
  • Secondary — intent expectancy: fixed, purposeful waiting for an imminent event; e.g. attent before the pounce. Bridge: prolonged attention converts to anticipation and aim.
  • Tertiary — diligent application: assiduous devotion to a task, study or duty; e.g. attent to one's craft. Bridge: focus generalizes from momentary look to habitual industry.
  • Archaic — courteous heed: respectful, obedient listening to a superior or to God. Bridge: attention as social deference; later bleached into polite attentive service.
  • Literary-predatory — coiled readiness: Hughes's specialisation: attention as stored violence, stillness charged with killing. Bridge: the devotional bow-string is restrung for the hunt; awe replaces worship with terror.
  • Usage note: clipped form of attentive; chosen in verse for brevity, hardness and archaic weight. Modern prose prefers attentive, alert, watchful; attent marks a consciously fierce, old-stone diction.

In short, ATTENT compresses into six letters a whole physiology of attention: to stretch, to fix, to wait, to spring. It reminds the reader that looking, at full intensity, is already a kind of touching — and, in the thrush's world, a kind of killing.
