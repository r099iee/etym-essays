Proto-Indo-European root *tag- (also *tāg-, "to touch, handle, grasp") — reconstructed, no attested date. The root underlies Latin tangere "to touch" and its whole family (tact, tactile, contact, integer). This root is not directly attested; its forms are reconstructed by comparative method from the Italic and other branches. Latin *tangō, tangere, tetigī, tāctum "to touch, handle" — attested 3rd–1st c. BCE. The present stem shows a nasal infix (*ta-n-g-), a characteristic PIE present formation. Latin tāctum, past-participial stem "touched" (base of tāctus) — the source of English tact, tactile, contact. Vulgar Latin *tastāre "to touch, to try by touching, to test" — reconstructed (not directly attested); formed as a frequentative from the tāct- stem, with the assimilation of *ct to *st producing *tast-. The existence of this stage is inferred from the Romance outcomes (Old French taster, Italian tastare, Spanish/Provençal tastar) and is accepted by most etymologists, though the exact intermediate forms are not attested in writing. Old French taster "to touch, feel, handle, sample; to taste" — attested from the 9th century onward. In Old French the tactile sense dominated; the gustatory sense ("to try by the mouth") developed alongside it, and the two coexist in the medieval verb. Middle English tasten "to touch; to taste, to try the flavour of" — borrowed into English c. 1300 (verb), with the noun taste following c. 1330. Both the tactile and the gustatory senses entered English together; the tactile sense faded during the Middle English period. English taste (noun and verb) — modern standard form after the Great Vowel Shift. Early Modern English TAST — Milton's spelling of taste in Paradise Lost (1667), Book I, "whose mortal tast / Brought Death into the World". This is the target form of this note. ◄ TARGET Note on debated/nuanced stages: the intermediate Vulgar Latin stage *tastāre is not directly attested in surviving texts; its reconstruction rests on comparison of the Romance verbs and is generally accepted but technically inferred. An alternative, less favoured view connects the word directly to a reshaping of Latin *tax(t)āre, a frequentative of tangere meaning "to appraise, to value", which would be a parallel rather than the same line; the etymological consensus, however, treats *tastāre as the direct Romance ancestor of taster, tastare, and taste.
