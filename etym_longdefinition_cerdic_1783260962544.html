Cerdic (prop. n.) — the traditional founder of the Anglo-Saxon Kingdom of Wessex and of its royal house, named in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (entry for the year 495) as the ancestor from whom later English monarchs claimed descent (reigned c. 519–534 CE). On this card the word appears in the sentence "The followers of Ida and Cerdic, on the other hand, brought to their settlements in Britain all the superstitions of the Elbe": the name here stands as a synecdoche for the pagan Anglo-Saxon conquest of southern Britain, a heathen warlord whose people carried the old religion of the continental homeland across the sea.

Sense 1 — The historical-legendary figure (the primary sense). According to the Chronicle, Cerdic and his son Cynric landed in southern Britain in 495, fought the Britons in a series of battles, and by about 519 established the kingdom that became Wessex ("land of the West Saxons"). The royal house of Wessex — and through it the later kings of England and the British monarchy — traced its descent from Cerdic; he is therefore the legendary ancestor of the English royal line. Modern historians treat him as at best semi-legendary: he may be a real warlord, a back-projection of West Saxon royal ideology, or a British chieftain absorbed into the dynasty's pedigree.

Sense 2 — The philological and onomastic sense. In historical etymology and the study of Anglo-Saxon culture-contact, Cerdic is famous as a Germanic king bearing a name that looks Brittonic (Celtic): most scholars derive it from the Celtic name *Caratācos ("beloved," Romanized as Caratacus), via a Welsh intermediate such as Ceredig. The un-palatalized hard initial C- in the Old English spelling is a phonological fossil that supports this — a native Old English name would have palatalized c to ch- before e.

Sense 3 — The historiographical and literary sense. In 19th-century historiography (as on this card, in the manner of Macaulay) Cerdic became a symbol of the pagan Anglo-Saxon conquest — the bringer of "all the superstitions of the Elbe" — set in deliberate contrast to the Christianized Germanic dynasties of the continent (Alaric, Theodoric, Clovis, Alboin). The name carries a quiet verdict of cultural backwardness in that older scholarship.

  • Register: a historical proper name used in chronicles, historiography, genealogy, and onomastics; it has no everyday modern sense.
  • Collocations: King Cerdic, Cerdic and Cynric, the house/lineage of Cerdic, the Cerdicings (his descendants), the followers of Cerdic.
  • Historical bridge for the name's meaning: from the Celtic word for "beloved," the name travelled into Old English as the founding name of the Wessex dynasty; its deeper resonance is the culture-contact between Britons and Saxons preserved in a single hard consonant.
  • Debated historicity: scholars dispute whether Cerdic was a historical figure or a legendary founder; the name's linguistic form, however, is independent of the question of whether its bearer was real.
  • Genealogical note: the West Saxon pedigree, as it survives in the Chronicle, extends from Cerdic down through Cynric, Ceawlin, and later kings to Alfred and the eventual kings of all England, making Cerdic the root of the English royal claim to antiquity and legitimacy.
