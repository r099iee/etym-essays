Single linear derivation chain for jack (name-root), with the money sense traced at the end: 1. Biblical Hebrew Yəhōchānān (יְהוֹחָנָן) — "Yahweh is gracious" (attested in the Hebrew Bible, 1st millennium BCE). The Semitic personal name that ultimately underlies John and Jack. 2. Koine Greek Iōánnēs (Ἰωάννης) — "John" (attested from the Septuagint period, 3rd–1st c. BCE, and in the New Testament, 1st c. CE). The Hebrew name is Hellenised as the Gospels spread through the Greek-speaking world. 3. Latin Iōannēs / Iōhannēs — "John" (attested from the 1st c. CE). The Greek name enters Latin with early Christianity. 4. Old French Jehan / Jan — "John" (attested 9th–12th c.). The Latin name evolves in Gallo-Romance; French also possessed Jacques ("James/Jacob"), which influence some accounts of the form "Jack." DEBATE: whether "Jack" descends purely from John (via the pet-form Jankin) or partly from Jacques (James/Jacob) is disputed; OED derives it from the Middle English pet-form of John, with the influence of Old French Jacques a secondary factor. 5. Middle English Jakke / Jacke, and the diminutive Jankin / Jakin — "little John; a young man, a common fellow, a servant" (attested 13th–14th c.). The pet-name becomes a generic noun for any ordinary man or labourer. 6. Modern English jack — "an ordinary man, a labourer; a labour-saving device; money, cash (slang, American)" (attested from the 14th c. onward; the money sense from the late 19th c.) — the final node, the note's exact word form, highlighted above. NOTE ON THE MONEY SENSE: the "money" meaning is a late, slangy offshoot of the generic "common man / fellow" sense — the working man's wages and stakes — rather than a direct descendant of any single stage. OED marks its precise origin as uncertain; the connection to the generic name is the standard explanation, and the chain above shows how the name could generate it.
