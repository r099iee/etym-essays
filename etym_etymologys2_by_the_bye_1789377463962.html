[Root 1: BY (Preposition/Adverb: Proto-Germanic *bi / PIE *bʰi "near, around, by")]
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*bʰi / *h₁epi
"near, at, around, against"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 1000–500 BCE)
*bi / *be
"near, around, concerning, by"
↓
Old English (c. 5th–11th c.)
bī / be
"near, along, through, concerning, according to"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.)
by / bi
"near, along, at the side of"

[Root 2: BYE (Substantive Noun: "side-track, secondary course, incidental matter")]
Old English (c. 9th–11th c.)
bī- (in compounds like bī-genga, bī-leofa)
"subsidiary, side, secondary"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th–15th c.)
by / bye (used substantively)
"side-way, secondary track, subsidiary course"
Note: The preposition by was converted into a noun to denote that which lies off the main path; hence a 'by-path', 'by-way', or in sports a 'bye'.
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
the bye / the by
"a side matter, a subordinate issue, an incidental affair"

[Phrasal Synthesis: BY THE BY / BY-THE-BYE]
Early Modern English (c. 1600–1615)
by the by → by-the-bye
"along the side track; incidentally, parenthetically, in passing"
↓
Modern English (present)
BY-THE-BYE
