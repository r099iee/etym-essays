incessant is an adjective meaning continuing without pause, ceasing, or interruption — unbroken, unremitting, relentless in time. An incessant noise never quite stops; incessant rain falls all afternoon; incessant practice, as in the source sentence, is practice repeated so steadily that its cumulative effect builds something durable, here the self itself. The word describes objective continuity (no gaps) but almost always adds a subjective coloring of weariness, urgency, or impressiveness: what is incessant is felt as too much to ignore, whether admirable (incessant devotion) or oppressive (incessant demands).

Grammatically it is a grading adjective used attributively (“incessant chatter”) and predicatively (“the chatter was incessant”), frequently modified by almost, nearly, seemingly, or quite when the speaker hedges literal endlessness. It collocates with sound and motion nouns above all — noise, chatter, babble, ringing, barking, dripping, humming, rain, traffic, demands, questions, complaints, work, efforts, practice — and with agents of persistence: incessant campaigning, incessant surveillance, incessant messaging. Note the distinction from its near-neighbors: ceaseless is loftier and more poetic; constant can mean merely faithful or regular; continuous is neutrally technical; incessant almost always implies felt pressure on a perceiver, the inability to get relief.

Polysemy is narrow but instructive:

  • Temporally uninterrupted (core): “incessant rain” — no perceptible break. Bridge: direct inheritance from Late Latin incessans “not ceasing.”
  • Recurrent with annoying frequency: “incessant phone calls” — strictly there are gaps, but they feel like none. Bridge: hyperbole, the felt continuity of the harassed recipient generalized to the event series.
  • Relentless in pursuit (of persons/efforts): “an incessant campaigner,” “incessant practice.” Bridge: transfer from the action’s continuity to the agent’s character; persistence read as identity.
  • Rhetorical intensifier (borderline): “incessant optimism.” Bridge: modern loosening where temporal meaning bleeds into mere emphasis, akin to “endless.”

In the Mead sentence, incessant practice carries the third sense with the first echoing behind it: the self is not given but rehearsed — role after role, greeting after greeting, apology after apology — until repetition compacts into identity. The word choice dignifies the mundane: what looks like trivial daily repetition is, precisely through its incessancy, the engine of personhood. Stylistically, incessant belongs to educated expository prose; it is more formal than nonstop or endless and less archaic than unremitting or unceasing, making it the default epithet when a social scientist wants to stress cumulative process without lapsing into poetry or slang. Its faintly negative prosody — incessant things tire us — is here neutralized by the argument: the tirelessness is productive, not merely tiresome.

Pedagogically the word is a favorite precisely because it makes mere repetition morally interesting. Teachers warn against incessant error and praise incessant drill in the same breath; the difference lies entirely in what is being repeated and to what end. In examination prose, choosing incessant over constant or repeated signals that you perceive the temporal pressure — the subject cannot escape the recurrence — and that you judge cumulative force to matter more than any single instance. That is exactly Mead’s point about the self: no single role-taking makes a person, but incessant role-taking does.
