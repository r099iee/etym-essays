- [10th c.–11th c.] - Originates in Anglo-Saxon England as Old English Stæfford, naming the fortified Mercian burh and strategic river crossing on the River Sow founded in 913 by Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians - [connotation: royal military stronghold, safe river passage, Mercian frontier defence]
- [11th c.–15th c.] - Recorded in the Domesday Book (1086) and adopted as a feudal habitational surname by the Anglo-Norman dynasty of Robert de Stafford, which rose to immense power as Earls of Stafford and Dukes of Buckingham - [connotation: high Anglo-Norman aristocracy, baronial wealth, tragic Tudor political intrigue]
- [16th c.–19th c.] - Expands across Great Britain, the Midlands ceramic and footwear industries (Staffordshire), and British colonial settlements as a prominent surname and regional place name - [connotation: English industrial craftsmanship, Midlands civic identity, colonial geographic heritage]
- [20th c.–Present] - Enters the pantheon of modern American letters through author Jean Stafford (1915–1979), whose Pulitzer Prize-winning stories and profound affinity for Marcel Proust established the surname as a symbol of stylistic brilliance and psychological complexity - [connotation: supreme literary craftsmanship, psychological realism, Pulitzer prestige, tragic brilliance]
