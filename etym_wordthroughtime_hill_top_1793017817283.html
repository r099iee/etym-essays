- Old English — two everyday words: hyll names raised ground and top names the summit or tuft; the compound is not yet fixed, but speakers freely join them.
- Middle English — the compound settles: hil-top and hill top appear for the crown of an ascent, alongside poetic synonyms like hill-crest and dun.
- Early modern — beacons and ballads: hill-tops carry Armada beacons, border warnings, and gibbets; ballads set trysts and tragedies on them, fixing the summit as the landscape's stage.
- 18th–19th c. — the picturesque and the hymn: landscape painters compose around hill-top clumps of trees, while hymn-writers climb hill-tops toward the heavenly city; estate villages advertise their healthy hill-top air.
- 20th c. — suburbs and masts: new estates and schools take hill-top names for prestige, while radio masts and memorials crown the summits, joining beauty to utility.
- Today — the walker's goal and the agent's prize: footpaths aim at hill-top views, and hilltop sells houses — one spelling now solid, the meaning unchanged for a thousand years.
