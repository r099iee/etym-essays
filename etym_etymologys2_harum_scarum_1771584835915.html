ROOT: harum-scarum (English, coined 1670s) — a rhyming reduplication, NO single PIE ancestor. The word was coined in English; its two components have separate histories. The final -um is a jocular mock-Latin ending (Latin hārum, genitive plural feminine of hic/haec "of these"), giving the whole a comic pseudo-scholarly air. The whole = "harrying-and-scaring."

CHAIN 1 — the first component, hare (n./v.):
1. PIE *ḱas- "gray" (a colour-based animal name, "the gray one") — reconstructed, no direct attestation.
2. Proto-Germanic *hasan- "the gray one, the hare" (2nd–1st millennium BCE, reconstructed).
3. Old English hara "hare" (attested from the 8th century, e.g. in glosses and in the Old English plant-name hara-mægð "hare-mayweed").
4. English hare (n.) "hare"; then hare (v.) "to run wildly like a hare; to chase, harry" (verb from the 17th century) — likely first element of harum-scarum.

CHAIN 1b — the alternative first component, harry (v.):
1. PIE *koro- "war-band, host" (reconstructed; cf. Lithuanian kãrias "army", possibly Greek koíranos "commander").
2. Proto-Germanic *harjaz "army" (reconstructed).
3. Old English here "army"; hergian "to make war, ravage, plunder" (attested in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, 9th–11th centuries).
4. English harry (v.) "to ravage, harass, worry" — proposed as an alternative first element (the OED regards the exact first element as uncertain; both hare and harry are candidates).

CHAIN 2 — the second component, scare (v.):
1. Old Norse skjarr "shy, timid, skittish" (adjective, attested in Old Icelandic texts) — with its causative verb skirra "to frighten away, to make shy; to shun, avoid".
2. Middle English skerren "to frighten, scare" (borrowed from Old Norse skirra; attested from c. 1300).
3. English scare (v.) "to frighten, to alarm" (late 14th century); scarecrow (16th century). Deeper PIE ancestry is NOT securely established; the often-cited link to PIE *sker- "to cut, to spring aside" remains speculative and unproven.

COMBINATION — harum-scarum (1670s):
1. Adjective (1670s) "reckless, flighty, headlong" ("harum-scarum ways").
2. Noun (1803) "a reckless, impetuous person" — our card sense, from the verse-portrait of Bao-yu.
3. Adverb "to run harum-scarum about" (18th–19th centuries).

NOTE (structural siblings, NOT relatives): helter-skelter (1590s), pell-mell, hurly-burly, higgledy-piggledy, topsy-turvy are English rhyming reduplications of the SAME pattern but share no root with hare, harry, or scare; they illustrate how English recycles its rhymes rather than inheriting its words.
