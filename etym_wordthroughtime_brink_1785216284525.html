- [early 13th century: English adoption] - Borrowed from the continental Germanic dialects of the Low Countries (Middle Dutch brinc, Middle Low German brink, "edge of a field, grassy slope"), but English from its first attestations attaches the word to the edge of a steep place - a riverbank, the rim of a pit, the lip of a precipice; the rural field-boundary sense never takes root in English.
- [14th-18th century: literal edge of the abyss] - The word fixes as the marker of the point where the ground falls away - the unseen edge one would discover only by stepping over it; its tone is grave and suggestive, implying danger and the nearness of falling in a way that neutral edge never does.
- [19th century: figurative threshold] - Brink migrates from landscape to crisis: "the brink of the grave," "on the brink of war," "the brink of ruin"; the physical precipice becomes a metaphor for imminent, irreversible change, and this idiom belongs to English alone among the Germanic languages.
- [20th century: brinkmanship] - The Cold War coins brinkmanship (modeled on gamesmanship) for the diplomacy of pushing a crisis to the edge; the word's register tilts from literary dread toward political strategy.
- [Contemporary sense] - The literal precipice-edge and the figurative threshold of disaster coexist; brink in both now signals the moment just before a fall, a change, or a decision.
