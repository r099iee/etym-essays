- [Old English & Proto-Germanic Era (c. 8th–11th c.)] — Existed as the simplex verb dwīnan, meaning to waste away, languish, fade, pine, or disappear through disease, sorrow, or drought — [perception: somber, tragic physical wasting away, often associated with mortal sickness and famine]
- [Middle English Period (c. 12th–15th c.)] — Continued as dwinen ("to pine away, vanish, shrivel"), often paired in alliterative poetry with verbs of death and decay — [perception: melancholic bodily or spiritual decay]
- [Late 16th-Century Frequentative Innovation (c. 1590s–1600s)] — Augmented with the frequentative/diminutive suffix -le (yielding dwindle), popularized famously by Shakespeare in Macbeth ("Weary sev'nnights nine times nine / Shall he dwindle, peak and pine") — [perception: evocative of gradual, steady, inexorable reduction in physical substance or fortune]
- [18th–19th Century Broadening (c. 1700–1900)] — Extended from physical emaciation and supernatural curses to financial resources, populations, daylight, armies, and abstract political power — [perception: neutral to elegiac description of diminishing quantities, assets, or numbers]
- [Modern & Contemporary Usage (20th–21st c.)] — Pervasive across ecology (dwindling species), economics (dwindling profits), and literature (dwindling hope) to express gradual, irreversible depletion — [perception: evocative of poignant decline, ecological crisis, or inevitable sunsetting]
