- Old English "weder": the noun "weather", with the verb "wederian" "to expose to the weather, to blow (of wind)"
- c. 1300: "widren" splits off from the weather-verb by a vowel change (e → i) — "to dry up, shrivel" — applied to plants, grass, and flesh left too long to wind and sun
- 14th–16th c.: literal drying becomes the core sense; biblical usage (the "withered hand" of Matthew, Mark and Luke, KJV 1611; "the grass withereth") fixes the image of lifeless, shrunken flesh and fleeting life
- 17th–19th c.: figurative extension — hopes, affections, reputations, love, and empires "wither" in Milton, the Romantics, and Victorian prose; the word joins the vocabulary of mortality and decay
- Today: both literal (botany, horticulture, medicine) and figurative (decline, decay); always carries the undertone of irreversible loss of vitality.
