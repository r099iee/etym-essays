From a tribe to a language. The name of the English language is a fossil of migration. Tacitus, writing in 98 CE, lists the Angli among the Germanic peoples of the north, and Bede later places them in the district of Angeln, on the neck of the Jutland peninsula. When the Angles settled Britain, their name travelled with them and, because the Angles were the largest or most visible group, their name rather than the Saxons' became the name of the whole speech community: Old English Englisc, the language of the Engle.

The morphology. Englisc is a compound of the tribal stem Engl- and the suffix -isc, the ancestor of modern -ish and a relative of Latin -iscus and Greek -iskos. The suffix originally formed adjectives of origin and affiliation, so Englisc means "pertaining to the Angles" before it means "the English language." The same pattern gives Scottisc, Frencisc and Denisc.

  • The initial vowel of Englisc is short /e/, and the medial g was pronounced /ɡ/ or /ɣ/ depending on dialect and date.
  • The loss of the final -isc to -ish is a regular Middle English reduction of unstressed syllables.
  • The spelling Anglish restores the a of Latin Angli and the tribal name; it is a spelling variant, not a separate derivation.
  • The modern standard English reflects the East Midland and London dialect, which displaced the older Englisc and its many regional spellings.

Middle English spellings and the survival of "Anglish." Medieval English had no fixed orthography. Scribes wrote Engelish, Englisch, Englyssh, Inglish and Anglish, and the choice often reflected dialect or the writer's model. The Ang- forms keep the tribal vowel and make the connection with the Angles obvious; they appear in chronicles and in some devotional and legal texts. With the arrival of printing and the standardisation of London English in the sixteenth century, English became the norm and Anglish was pushed to the margins, where it survived as an archaism and a dialect form.

Nationalism and the nineteenth century. The Victorian passion for Anglo-Saxon origins revived the old spelling. Writers who wanted to emphasise the Germanic roots of English, or who distrusted the Norman-French element, found Anglish a useful token of authenticity. The same period saw the rise of linguistic purism in many European countries, and in England it produced proposals for replacing Latin and French vocabulary with native words. These proposals were marginal but they prepared the ground for the modern movement.

The twentieth-century revival. In the mid-twentieth century, journalists and humorists began writing "Anglish" versions of English prose as a game and as a critique of jargon. The practice was popularised by Poul Anderson's essay "Uncleftish Beholding" (1989), a short account of atomic theory written almost entirely in native Germanic vocabulary, which became a model for the community. The internet then gave the movement a home: wikis, word-lists, translation projects and style guides now define what "Anglish" means for its users.

  • Principles: prefer native words, revive obsolete ones, calque classical compounds, and keep the grammar recognisably English.
  • Limits: the movement is a stylistic exercise, not a claim that English can be fully de-Latinised; everyday technical and scientific language is deeply borrowed.
  • Register: Anglish prose often sounds archaic, alliterative and biblical, which is part of its appeal and part of its limitation.
  • Critique: linguists point out that borrowing is normal and that the "native" layer itself contains loanwords from Celtic, Latin and Norse.

Literary and cultural attestations. The word appears in medieval chronicles as a spelling of English, in antiquarian writing of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, in twentieth-century language reform pamphlets, and in twenty-first-century online culture. It has also been used in alternate-history fiction to name a deliberately Germanic England. The modern sense is thus a conscious revival, layered on top of a genuine historical variant, which is why the word can feel both ancient and invented at the same time.

Why the word matters. Anglish makes visible a fact that the standard name conceals: English is named after the Angles, not the Saxons, and its identity has always been contested between a Germanic inheritance and a classical, French and global overlay. The word is therefore a small laboratory for arguments about purity, authenticity and change in language — arguments that are as old as the English themselves.
