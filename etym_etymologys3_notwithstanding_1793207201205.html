The notwithstanding story starts in Rome, or at least in Rome's paperwork. Latin chancery style loved the ablative absolute non obstante — literally nothing standing in the way — as the formula that lets a grant, privilege, or command override every obstacle in advance: this shall hold, non obstante any law to the contrary. When England's medieval clerks, translating statutes, chronicles, and papal bulls into English, needed the same bulldozer, they Englished it piece by piece: not for non, withstonding (the present participle of withstanden, to stand against) for obstante. The result, not withstonding, first appears in late-fourteenth-century official prose as a loose absolute — the rain not withstanding, any statute not withstanding — a whole little clause dangling beside the main sentence and brushing the obstacle away.

What makes the word remarkable is what happened next: the dangling clause fused into a single governing word and then learned three grammars. First it became a preposition, swallowing its noun — notwithstanding the rain — and joining the small company of English prepositions (alongside during, concerning, considering) that began life as participles. Then, almost uniquely, it kept the old word order alive as a postpositive adverb — the rain notwithstanding — preserving in amber the syntax of the medieval absolute centuries after every similar construction had died. And with that it learned to govern whole clauses as a conjunction — notwithstanding that the evidence was thin — completing a grammatical range no rival possesses: despite cannot follow its noun, although cannot take one, nevertheless cannot govern at all. The fossil did not merely survive; it diversified.

Sound, spelling, and the lawyers:

  • From verb to particle: the engine of the word is Germanic stand — PIE *steh₂-, to stand, through Proto-Germanic *standaną and Old English standan — joined to the old adversarial with (which in Old English meant against, as in withstand, withdraw in its hostile sense). The compound withstand supplies the participle; not negates it; use fuses all three into one unanalysed block. By 1600 no speaker hears stand inside it without thinking, which is exactly why the word feels so solid: its metaphor died into its mass.
  • Why one word, sometimes two: early texts space it (not withstanding), later ones join it; the hyphenated and two-word spellings linger into the eighteenth century. The fusion mirrors the grammar: while speakers still felt the participle working, they kept the space; once it became a mere function word, the space closed like a healed wound.
  • The lawyers' adoption: conveyancers discovered that no other word could open a paramount clause — notwithstanding anything herein to the contrary, the landlord may… — with quite the same irresistible gravity. Despite sounds arguable; notwithstanding sounds enacted. Generations of deeds, leases, statutes, and treaties have therefore preserved the word in its full plumage long after conversation moulted it, and law students still learn its postpositive trick as a rite of passage.
  • Cobbett and the polemicists: political writers of Cobbett's stamp adore the word because it performs concession and verdict in one gesture — yes, the millions; no, not beautiful. His Middlesex sentence is the pattern: name the enemy's best fact (the wealth sucked from the kingdom), override it with one participle, and pass sentence (ugly). The long Latinate stride of notwithstanding amid short Saxon hammer-blows is pure Cobbett rhythm — the seal pressed upon the paragraph.
  • The plain-English siege and survival: from Fowler's Modern English Usage (1926) onward, style guides have listed notwithstanding among the stiff officialisms to be replaced by despite, although, or however — and in ordinary prose they have largely won. Yet the word refuses to die, because its triple grammar is irreplaceable, because the law cannot rewrite ten centuries of deeds, and because writers still sometimes need exactly its judicial weight. Corpus counts show it holding steady in legal, academic, and formal journalistic prose while vanishing from speech — the profile of a word that has retired from labour to ceremony, and performs the ceremonies perfectly.

So notwithstanding is a Roman formula in Saxon dress: born non obstante, raised not withstonding, hardened into preposition, adverb, and conjunction, captured by lawyers, wielded by radicals, and besieged by reformers — and still standing, nothing standing in its way, in every statute book and solemn verdict in the language. Cobbett stamped Middlesex ugly with it; judges still stamp judgments final with it; and as long as English needs to name an obstacle and override it in a single breath, the word will keep its place at the head of the sentence — or, Roman-like, at its tail.
