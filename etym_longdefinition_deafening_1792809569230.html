Deafening is the present-participial adjective of deafen: so loud, sudden, or all-enveloping in sound that it seems to steal the hearing itself — the roar that drowns thought, pumps the chest, and leaves the ears ringing in the silence after. Cannon fire, jet engines at full thrust, stadium crowds at the winning goal, and Tolkien’s firework dragon bursting over Bywater all earn the word honestly.

In the source sentence the dragon’s finale is a deafening explosion — the climax of a sky-show built in rising noise: the roar, the whizzing circuits, the somersault, and then the burst that caps them all. Tolkien chooses the strongest noise-word in English deliberately: after such marvels mere loudness would be an anticlimax, so the dragon detonates at a volume that momentarily unmakes the ears that hear it. Every reader who has stood too near the bonfire on the Fifth of November knows the exact ringing aftermath the hobbits must have felt.

Core meaning in detail: Strictly, deafening describes sound at or beyond the threshold of pain — roughly 120 decibels and up, where the ear’s defences surrender and hearing compresses, distorts, and then blanks. Loosely, and far more often, it describes any noise big enough to dominate its moment: deafening applause, a deafening welcome, deafening cheers. The grammar is worth noting: the word is almost always attributive (a deafening roar) or predicative (the roar was deafening), and it intensifies everything it touches — even silence, in the famous paradox of deafening silence, where an absence of expected sound presses on the ears as heavily as a cannonade.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Overwhelmingly loud, seemingly robbing hearing (primary sense, from the 1590s): a deafening explosion, deafening engines, deafening cheers. The bridge is literal physiology pushed to hyperbole: the noise approaches what would truly deafen, and the body answers as if it had — wincing, covering ears, feeling the blast in the ribs.
  • Stunning or overwhelming the senses generally (extended): a deafening blow to the head, deafening shock. The bridge runs through the ear’s old kinship with stupor — in ancient roots deaf and dizzy were cousins — so that any stunning impact, sonic or not, can deafen.
  • Drowning out all competition or reply (figurative): a deafening majority, deafening propaganda, the deafening clamour of the market. The bridge is the acoustic metaphor of democracy and debate: one voice so loud the rest might as well be dumb, the opposition deafened though its ears work perfectly.
  • Deafening silence (paradoxical idiom, 19th c.): the reply was a deafening silence; the empty house was deafening. The bridge inverts the physics: utter quiet after expected noise — the unanswered question, the missing applause — presses on attention exactly as a roar presses on the eardrum, and the same adjective serves both pressures.
  • Emphatic intensifier for acclaim (modern journalistic): deafening applause greeted the encore, a deafening reception for the returning team. The bridge generalises volume into enthusiasm: the crowd’s love is measured in decibels, and deafening certifies that the meter burst.
  • Making literally hard of hearing over time (medical/occupational): deafening machinery, deafening gunfire without protection. The bridge returns the hyperbole to fact: sustained exposure to what we casually call deafening genuinely deafens — the shipyard, the battlefield, and the front row of the rock concert all collect their tribute from the inner ear.

Register and connotation: Deafening is dramatic but thoroughly respectable — at home in ballistics reports, concert reviews, match reports, and epic fantasy alike. It flatters its subject even as it complains: a deafening welcome is the loudest compliment a crowd can pay, and a deafening explosion is the firework-maker’s triumph. Only the medical sense carries warning, reminding the reveller that the ringing after the show is the ear asking, politely at first, to be spared next time.
