THE EVOLUTION OF EPITHETS: FROM "PUT UPON" TO THE INSULT **A compound of two ancient roots** The biography of *epithets* begins with two separate Proto-Indo-European roots that were fused together only inside Greek. The first is *epi, "on, upon, at," a preposition and prefix that surfaces across the family — Greek epí "upon," Latin ob "towards, against," Sanskrit ápi "beside, upon" — a tiny word whose whole meaning is one of placing something on top of something else. The second is *dheh₁-, "to put, place, set," one of the most productive roots in Indo-European: Sanskrit dadhāti "he places," Avestan dadāiti "he puts," Greek tithénai "to put," Latin facere "to make, do," and, through the Germanic line, the very English verbs do and the root of deed and deem. What is striking is how much conceptual weight rides on so simple a pair: the whole notion of an epithet is built from the physical act of laying one thing upon another. **The Greek fusion and the birth of the word** In Ancient Greek the prefix epi- was joined to the verb tithénai "to put," producing epitithénai, "to put upon, place on." From this verb Greek formed the verbal adjective epithetós, "put on, added, attributed," and then substantivized the neuter to tò epítheton, literally "that which is added." The step that turned a word for "an add-on" into our modern noun was a grammatical one: Greek grammarians spoke of the epítheton ónoma, "the added name," as their technical term for the adjective — the word placed alongside a noun to qualify it, exactly as an adjective is "added to" its noun. By the fourth century BCE the rhetoricians, above all Aristotle, used the term for the ornamental and characterizing tags of poetry and oratory, and the poet of poets, Homer, had already made the fixed epithet the engine of epic diction. **The fixed epithet and the epic tradition** Homer's practice deserves special attention because it shaped the word's destiny:
  • "Swift-footed Achilles," "rosy-fingered Dawn," "wine-dark sea" are stock epithets — formulaic, metrically fixed phrases repeated throughout the Iliad and Odyssey.
  • These tags were not decoration in the modern sense but compositional machinery: they supplied ready-made metrical units that let the oral poet build hexameters on the fly while identifying a character or object by its essential trait.
  • The habit spread through all classical poetry and rhetoric: the "epitheton ornans," the ornamental epithet, became a recognized device, praised as dignity and splendor or mocked as padding, from the Roman rhetorical schools to Johnson's criticism of "the frippery of epithets."
**The road through Latin and French into English** - Latin borrowed the finished noun directly as epitheton, and the Roman grammarians and rhetoricians naturalized it, passing it on to the medieval schools; the same idea was independently rendered in Latin as adjectivus, "that which is added," which English later took as adjective — a second expression of the identical grammatical notion. - French took the word as épithète through the learned channels of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, and English received it in the sixteenth century, first in the learned form epitheton and then clipped to epithet. - For two centuries in English the word remained purely literary and grammatical: the epithet was the characterizing tag of poetry and panegyric, the "added name" of the old grammarians. **The semantic darkening: from ornament to insult** The modern history of the word is the history of its slide from praise toward abuse: - In the nineteenth century the plural began to be used for any characterizing label, especially a hostile one, and Carlyle's "ambiguous epithets" — words that simultaneously flatter and wound — are an elegant example of the transitional stage, where the ornamental tag still coexists with the pointed barb. - By the twentieth century dictionaries had settled "an abusive or contemptuous word or phrase" as a standard sense of the noun, and the plural became a decorous synonym for "insults." - The great modern collocation is "racial epithets," the hostile labels fastened on ethnic and racial groups. The historical bridge is unbroken: an epithet is by definition a label that imposes an interpretation; when the label targets a stigmatized group it hardens into a slur, and sociolinguists now study "epithets" precisely as instruments of social hierarchy and dehumanization. - Meanwhile the old senses survive untouched: the Homeric stock epithet remains a technical term in literary criticism, the grammatical sense survives in textbooks, and botanists still speak of the "specific epithet," the second, distinguishing part of a scientific name. **Why the word is polysemous today** The full modern range of *epithets* — from the metrical tag of epic, to the psychological characterization of biography, to the slur of the schoolyard and the newsroom — is a single continuous development. At every stage the core idea holds: an epithet is something added to a name that makes the hearer see the bearer in a particular way. That is precisely why Carlyle's epithets for Harriet Taylor could be "ambiguous": the word itself was built to carry praise, judgment, and wound all at once, and the history of the noun is the history of that double-edged power being discovered, exploited, and finally named.
