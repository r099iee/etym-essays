Derivation chain for QUILTS (the target root word is bold at the final stage): 1. Latin culcita "mattress, cushion, stuffed sack or pad" (attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE, e.g. in Pliny the Elder's Natural History and in household inventories). Its own ultimate origin is uncertain and debated. 2. (Debated intermediate) Latin culcāre "to tread down, stamp, stuff" — a by-form of calcare "to tread" (from calx "heel"); if culcita derives from culcare, the root image is a mass trodden or stuffed into shape. This derivation is often repeated but not universally accepted, and calx itself has no secure PIE etymology; the chain before Latin culcita is therefore unconfirmed. 3. Vulgar Latin / Gallo-Romance *colcita → Old French coilte / cuilte / coute "padded bed-covering, mattress" (attested from the 12th century). 4. Anglo-Norman transmission → Middle English quilte / qwilt "padded bed-covering; mattress" (first attested c. 1290–1300; the verb "to quilt" appears in the 14th century). 5. Early Modern English quilt (noun) "padded coverlet with the filling held in place by lines of stitching" (sense stabilized 16th–17th c.); quilt (verb) "to stitch through layers so the filling cannot shift" (15th–16th c.). 6. Modern English quilts (plural) "warm padded bed coverings made with a stitched layer of filling." Debated / nuanced stages: The Latin stage culcita is certain, but everything before it is uncertain. The derivation from culcare / calcare is plausible and often cited (via the notion of a "stuffed / trodden" pad) yet is not securely proved, and no PIE root for the family is established in the standard handbooks; treat the pre-Latin origin as unknown rather than attested.
