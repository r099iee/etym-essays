Abrege is the unaccented form of French abrégé (/a.bʁe.ʒe/), the past participle of abréger "to shorten", used substantively as a noun: "an abridgement, a summary, an epitome, a condensed account". In the source it is the first word of a book title, Abrege Chronologique de l'Histoire de France — literally "Chronological Abridgement of the History of France".

The missing accent is purely typographic. French prints Abrégé chronologique de l'histoire de France, with an acute accent on the final e; English and older compositors often dropped it, and OCR routinely loses it. Without the accent the spelling is not standard French, and a French reader would take final -e as mute, but the meaning of the title is unaffected. The capital A is simply the capitalisation of a title's first word, and English readers often anglicise the word as /ˌæb.reɪˈʒeɪ/.

What the word denotes

  • A shortened version of a longer work, made by omission rather than by rewriting: an abridgement, a digest, a compendium.
  • By extension, any compact statement of a subject: un abrégé de la philosophie, un abrégé de l'histoire romaine.
  • The set phrase en abrégé means "in short, in brief" and survives in ordinary modern French.
  • Unlike résumé, which can be a summary of one's own argument, abrégé usually denotes a separate, shorter work derived from a larger one, and it carries a scholarly, bibliographic flavour.

The book behind the citation

The title belongs to Charles-Jean-François Hénault (1685–1770), président of the Parlement de Paris and a fixture of the Parisian literary world. His Abrégé chronologique de l'histoire de France appeared in 1744 and became one of the most successful historical books of the century, reprinted repeatedly and continued by other hands into the 1770s and 1780s; the citation in the source is to a Paris edition of 1775. Its method was exactly what the title advertises: the history of France laid out year by year, reign by reign, with dates in the margin and short anecdotes, portraits and particularités attached to them. It was an abridgement in the eighteenth-century sense — not a bare skeleton of facts, but a portable, agreeable digest for readers who wanted the shape of French history without the folios of the learned Benedictines.

Morphology

  • Abrégé is a past participle turned noun, like résumé, extrait, précis and digest in English. Its grammatical gender is masculine: un abrégé.
  • It is built on the verb abréger, from Old French abregier, from Late Latin abbreviāre "to shorten", itself ad- "to" (assimilated to ab- before b) plus breviāre "to shorten", from brevis "short".
  • The Latin -vi- was lost in French, giving breger and then abréger; the modern spelling marks the stressed final vowel with an acute accent, and there is no circumflex in this word.
  • The word takes de for its subject: un abrégé de l'histoire de France. In the title, chronologique stands between noun and complement, and de l'Histoire de France completes the phrase.

English neighbours and false friends

  • English abridge is the same word at one remove: Middle English abregen from Old French abregier. Abridge and abbreviate are therefore doublets, one borrowed through French and one straight from Latin.
  • Abridgement (or abridgment) is the native English counterpart of abrégé, and English book titles of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries use it exactly as the French use theirs.
  • Brief, brevity, abbreviate and abbreviation all descend from the same brevis family, so the whole cluster shares the metaphor of shortness.
  • The French word is not used in English outside quotations of titles; an English writer would say "abridgement" or, in a bibliographic note, keep the French form and its accents.

Register and use

  • In French, abrégé is learned and slightly old-fashioned; modern publishers prefer résumé, synthèse or précis for student aids.
  • In English the word survives only as a foreign term in titles and in the phrase en abrégé, which is printed in italics.
  • Because the title names a genre as well as a book, the word also tells the reader how to use the citation: the anecdote of the king's illness is being taken from a chronological digest, not from an archive.
