- [Old French 'meal'] - In its current form since Middle English (from Old French repast, from repaistre 'to feed'), 'repast' has always meant a meal - but a formal, dignified one, a literary word for eating.
- [Dignified register] - Through the centuries it kept its elevated flavour: a 'repast' is a meal taken with ceremony and reflection, never the plain modern 'meal' of everyday speech.
- [19th-century historical use] - In 19th-century prose (as in Macaulay) the plural 'repasts' dignified the very stuff of daily life - dress, furniture, repasts - elevating the ordinary into a fit subject for serious history.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'repast' is consciously formal or old-fashioned, used for effect or elegance - a word that refuses to be ordinary about the humble act of eating.
