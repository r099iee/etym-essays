- 17th c. - English borrows aeon from Greek aiōn (age, lifetime, eternity) through ecclesiastical Latin; it first serves theology — the Gnostic "aeons", the eternal ages or emanations of the divine — a grand, cosmic, religious word.
- 17th–18th c. - it passes into general learned use for "an age or indefinite period", still carrying a religious or cosmic halo.
- 19th c. - geology takes it over: eon becomes the formal name of the largest division of the geologic time scale, lending the word scientific precision; in ordinary prose the plural aeons becomes a rhetorical intensifier for "an unimaginably long time".
- 19th c. - the American spelling eons drops the diphthong; aeons keeps a British, classical, period-flavoured ring, and writers use the spelling itself to sound older and graver.
- 20th–21st c. - the plural remains the standard literary form (aeons ago, for aeons, aeons of erosion); the word now coexists easily with cosmic and geological contexts, the largest loose unit in the vocabulary of time.
