Fostered is the past tense and past participle of the transitive verb foster, meaning to nourish, encourage, promote the development of, or sustain something over time through active care and protection. It also denotes the act of rearing, parenting, or providing shelter and upbringing to a child who is not one's biological offspring, typically without formal legal adoption. The verb carries an intrinsic sense of patient, sustained dedication: whether one is fostering a vulnerable infant, a budding artistic talent, an economic enterprise, or a shared cultural sentiment, the action involves providing a supportive environment in which the object of care can flourish and reach maturity.

In the source passage from John Ruskin’s Modern Painters, the word is deployed in an elevated, abstract psychological and cultural sense: Ruskin observes that the truly great thinkers and artists of later eras have, almost without exception, ‘fostered in others the veneration of departed power which they felt themselves’. Here, fostered does not refer to the physical feeding or rearing of children; rather, it describes the generous, deliberate cultivation of an attitude of reverence. Great creators do not seek to burnish their own reputations by denigrating their predecessors; instead, they actively nurture, encourage, and cultivate within their disciples and the public a deep veneration for the masterworks of the past. The word underscores the gentle, continuous, and selfless effort required to keep an inherited cultural tradition alive in human hearts.

Core grammatical and morphological profile: Morphologically, fostered is formed by appending the regular weak dental preterite suffix -ed to the base verb foster. The base itself is a native Germanic nominal derivative tracing back through Old English fōstrian to the root of food and feed. Syntactically, fostered functions primarily as a transitive verb taking a direct object (‘the mentor fostered independence in her students’) or within passive constructions (‘a culture fostered by mutual trust’). It also serves as a participial adjective modifying nouns (‘a fostered child’, ‘a fostered ambition’).

All senses, primary, secondary, and technical:

  • To encourage, cultivate, or promote growth and development (primary modern sense): To stimulate the active expansion, improvement, or success of an institution, skill, relationship, or idea (e.g., ‘the new economic policy fostered technological innovation across the manufacturing sector’). Semantic bridge: Metaphorical extension from physical feeding: just as physical nourishment allows a seedling or infant to grow, institutional or emotional support allows abstract enterprises to thrive.
  • To rear, nourish, or provide parental care to a non-biological child (domestic / legal sense): To act as a foster parent, providing a temporary or long-term home, food, education, and emotional support to a child placed by social welfare agencies without formal adoption. Semantic bridge: Direct continuation of the earliest native Germanic meaning of foster, which meant to provide sustenance and household rearing.
  • To harbor, cherish, or nurse a feeling or thought internally (psychological / emotional sense): To hold onto, indulge, or quietly sustain a belief, grievance, hope, or suspicion over a long period (e.g., ‘for years he fostered a secret resentment against his former business partner’). Semantic bridge: Turning the nourishing action inward; the mind ‘feeds’ and sustains an internal emotion, whether virtuous or destructive.
  • To shelter, protect, or cherish (poetic and literary sense): To provide a haven or hospitable refuge for someone or something vulnerable (e.g., ‘the secluded mountain abbey fostered ancient learning throughout the Dark Ages’). Semantic bridge: Broadening from physical feeding to encompass physical sanctuary and guardian care.
  • Agricultural and botanical husbandry (technical / historical sense): To nurse frail saplings, livestock, or crops through adverse conditions by shielding them from harsh elements and supplying concentrated nutrients. Semantic bridge: Applied biological application of the maternal/parental feeding impulse to domesticated nature.

Register, connotations, and distinctions: Fostered is overwhelmingly positive in modern standard English when applied to public, institutional, or creative realms (fostering peace, fostering cooperation, fostering creativity). However, when paired with negative psychological nouns such as resentment, illusions, hatred, or grievances, it implies culpable indulgence—nurturing a toxic flame that ought to have been extinguished. It differs markedly from caused or created: fostered implies that the growth was organic and required time, care, and the right conditions, rather than being instantaneously manufactured by external force.
