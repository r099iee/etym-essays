In Gibbon's sentence INHABITANTS names the civilian population of the Roman empire at its height: "Their peaceful inhabitants enjoyed and abused the advantages of wealth and luxury." The phrase is a compact moral portrait of imperial maturity. The inhabitants are the people who live within the empire — in this context, the whole civilian population of the provinces and Italy — and Gibbon describes them in a double light: they "enjoyed" the advantages of wealth and luxury (a legitimate happiness, the fruit of the "happy period" he is celebrating), and they "abused" those same advantages (a hint of softness, decadence, and moral decline that foreshadows the empire's fall). The word is deliberately neutral and demographic — "inhabitants" simply means the dwellers of a place — which lets Gibbon's adjectives and verbs supply all the moral colour: peace has made them consumers rather than defenders of prosperity. The core sense of INHABITANTS (plural of inhabitant) is: "persons who live in or occupy a particular place; the residents or dwellers of a region, city, country, or planet." An inhabitant is anyone who dwells in a place, regardless of citizenship, nationality, or legal status — the mere fact of residence or habitation is sufficient. The singular inhabitant means "one who inhabits," and the word is formed from the verb inhabit (to dwell in, to occupy) plus the agent suffix -ant. In Gibbon's sentence the plural is the default and the collective sense dominates: the inhabitants of the empire are its whole human content.
  • Residents/dwellers of a place: the core sense — persons who live in a country, city, region, island, or any defined territory (e.g., "the inhabitants of the island," "the peaceful inhabitants of the empire").
  • Human vs. non-human occupants: by extension, inhabitant is used of animals, plants, and even objects or concepts that occupy or are found in a place ("the inhabitants of the deep sea," "the inhabitants of the forest," "the inhabitants of the database" — the last metaphorical).
  • Census/demographic sense: in statistics and administration, inhabitants are the counted residents of a place, as in "the city has one million inhabitants," "per inhabitant" in economic measures.
  • Ecological/zoological sense: in biology and ecology, the species or organisms living in a habitat are its inhabitants — "the inhabitants of the coral reef."
  • Planetary/extraterrestrial sense: by natural extension, "the inhabitants of Earth," "the inhabitants of Mars" — the beings living on a world (current or hypothetical).
  • Register: neutral and formal: unlike "citizens" (legal status) or "natives" (birthplace), "inhabitants" is the neutral ethnographic and demographic term for those who merely dwell in a place.
The historical bridge from the verb to the noun is entirely transparent. Latin habitāre means "to dwell, to inhabit, to reside," a frequentative form built on habēre, "to have, to hold": to inhabit is literally to "hold" or "occupy" a place continuously. The prefixed Latin verb inhabitāre means "to dwell in." Its present participle, inhabitāns (genitive inhabitantis), means "dwelling in" and, nominalized, "one who dwells in." English borrowed the noun from the French form of this participle: the Latin inhabitans → Old French inhabitant → Middle English inhabitaunt / inhabitant, recorded in English from the 15th century. The word's deep etymology, therefore, roots the "inhabitant" in the idea of holding: an inhabitant is one who holds or occupies a place. That is exactly the sense Gibbon exploits — the peaceful inhabitants hold the land and its goods, enjoying and abusing wealth, without the martial strain that once secured that holding. Register: INHABITANTS is standard formal English, at home in history, geography, law, census prose, ecology, and journalism. It is neither slang nor archaic. Collocations include "peaceful/local/native inhabitants," "inhabitants of the city/province/country," "among the inhabitants," "the inhabitants of the island," "per inhabitant." The singular is used of a single dweller; the plural is the default in survey statements about whole communities. Pronunciation is /ɪnhˈabɪtənts/ (in-HAB-i-tuhnts), with the stress on the second syllable.
