In the passage from the Chinese classic, a chime is not decorative music but an official acoustic signal: a warning sound from the inner gate that, together with a shout outside the door, announces the arrival of a Mr Wang on an official call. It interrupts Yu-cun's private reading of the satirical rhyme about the Nanking families and reasserts the ritual timetable of the yamen. The tone is formal, procedural, and slightly abrupt — the "warning chime" marks a transition from leisurely intelligence-gathering to public duty. Sound here is bureaucracy made audible: rank, arrival, and interruption are all encoded in a single ring or strike. The full sense inventory is as follows:
  • Primary sense — "the sound of a bell or a set of bells": the ringing or striking of a bell, especially one tuned as part of a series, as in a clock or carillon; "the chime of the church clock."
  • Instrument sense — "a set of bells struck to produce a tune; a bell or series of bells": the material instrument itself, e.g. the chimes of a church tower, a doorbell chime, or a musical instrument made of metal tubes or bowls that ring when struck — the sense given in the card's shortDefinition.
  • Deep-bowl instrument sense — "a musical instrument, typically of metal and in the shape of a deep bowl, that rings when struck": a bell-like vessel (closely related to the cymbal and gong families) that produces a ringing tone; the historical link to Greek kymbē "bowl" and kymbalon "cymbal" is built into the word's etymology.
  • Single-bell sense: one bell of a set, or a single small bell used to give a time signal; "the chime of the gate bell" in the passage is such a single, functional sound.
  • Wind chime: the modern decorative instrument of hanging tubes or rods that tinkle in the breeze; a domestic and garden object.
  • Verb, intransitive — "to ring or sound": "the clock chimed the hour"; "the bells chimed."
  • Verb, figurative — "to chime in": to join a conversation, to interject, especially in agreement or with a comment; the image is of a voice joining the sound of others.
  • Verb, figurative — "to chime with": to be in harmony with, to fit or agree with: "the plan chimes with our aims"; derived from the idea of sounds sounding together harmoniously.
  • Rare abstract noun — "harmonious agreement": a "chime" of agreement or concord, from the musical idea of notes fitting together.
The semantic thread running through all senses is the production of a clear, musical, usually single or patterned ringing sound. A chime sits between the raw noise of a clang and the full sonority of a toll: it is distinct enough to warn, pleasant enough to be civilised. That is why the word migrates so easily between the clock, the church, the doorbell, the wind, and the law court — every setting that needs an audible signal that is at once musical and functional. The boundary against near-synonyms sharpens the sense:
  • Bell: the instrument or its sound in the most general sense; a chime is a bell's patterned or musical sound, or a bell of a set, not the generic toll of any bell.
  • Gong: a broad flat metal disc struck for a deep, resonant sound, often ceremonial or East Asian; a chime is higher, lighter, and more musical.
  • Toll: a slow, solemn, usually single repeated striking, often funerary or marking the hour; a chime is quicker and often patterned.
  • Peal: a loud ringing of a full set of bells, as in change-ringing; a chime can be a single bell or a short pattern, without the grandeur of a peal.
  • Knell: the sound of a bell rung for a death or funeral; a chime is not mournful and carries no deathly association.
  • Clang / clash: harsh metal noise without musical polish; a chime is tuned, clear, and ringingly musical.
  • Jingle / tinkle: light, high, discontinuous little sounds; a chime, even when small, rings with a sustained musical tone rather than a mere tinkle.
Collocations and phrases:
  • Time and clock: "the clock chimes the hour," "the chimes of midnight."
  • Signal: "a warning chime," "the door chime," "the chime of the gate bell" — the pattern in the passage.
  • Musical and domestic: "wind chimes," "carillon chimes," "chime bars."
  • Figurative verb: "chime in" (interject), "chime with" (harmonize with, agree with).
In the passage, the word's double identity is exactly right: the chime is a functional warning — an audible notification of rank and arrival — and at the same time a civilised sound, the aural furniture of a great household. It is music and bureaucracy in one ring.
