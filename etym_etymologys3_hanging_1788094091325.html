From suspension to scaffold: the double descent of hanging. Hanging is a word with two founding images that slowly fused: the homely picture of things held up from above — lamps, weapons, textiles — and the terrible picture of a body dropped from a gallows. Both descend from the same Germanic verb, and the history of the participle hanging traces how an everyday physical word came, over a thousand years, to carry one of society’s most feared penalties, then quietly receded into technical and decorative uses.

I. The Indo-European base. The Germanic family — Gothic hahan, Old Norse hanga, Old High German hāhan, Old English hōn and hangian — points back to Proto-Germanic *hanhaną “to hang,” on the evidence of a PIE root *ḱenk- “to hang, be suspended.” Whether Latin cūnctārī “to delay” and Sanskrit śaṅkate “to doubt” belong here (“to hang back” → “to hesitate”) is a genuine scholarly question; some accept the mental-hanging metaphor, others reject the sound correspondence. Nothing Germanic is in doubt: English, German (hängen), Dutch (hangen), and the Norse tongues share the verb across the centuries.

II. Old English: two verbs, one shape. Old English possessed a strong verb hōn, hēng, hangen — transitive, “to hang up, suspend, crucify” — and a weak verb hangian, hangode — intransitive, “to be suspended, to remain hanging.” The early senses are concrete: shields on walls, ropes from beams, the Crucifixion itself — early English piety made hōn the very verb of Golgotha. The weak/strong split mattered: for centuries after, English inherited two past forms and two domains that modern speakers still juggle — hanged for people, hung for things.

III. Middle English: hangen and the birth of the judicial word (13th–15th c.). Middle English merged the conjugations into hangen; the present participle hangynge/honginge began to act as adjective and noun. Two developments mark the period. First, hangings became the lordly word for wall textiles — tapestries and embroidered cloths that insulated and adorned stone halls (the Bayeux Tapestry, c. 1070s, is precisely such a hanging). Second, hanging became the standard noun for execution by the gallows: “they were hanged”; a “hanging” was what happened at Tyburn and on the town gibbet; and the supreme treason formula “hanging, drawing and quartering” (fixed in the statute of 1351) froze the word into legal terror. From the fourteenth century, the participle lived two lives at once: decoration indoors, death outside the walls.

IV. Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: overhang and suspense. The adjective now describes landforms that jut above what lies beneath: hanging rocks, hanging cliffs, and, via Renaissance accounts of Babylon, the “hanging gardens” — terraced gardens that seemed to float in the air. Figurative uses multiply: a “hanging matter” moves from “a matter punishable by death” to simply “an unsettled matter”; a “hanging question” dangles unresolved. The related senses “to hang about, loiter” and “to be dependent” (his life hangs on it) deepen the suspension metaphor into the language of fate and dependence.

V. Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: grammarians, firearms, and geology. The prescriptive eighteenth century split the past tenses: hanged for persons judicially executed, hung for everything else — a rule observed in law and obituaries ever since (though “hung jury” and “hung parliament” survive in the shadow of the noun). “Hanging fire,” from slow-burning muskets whose priming hesitated, supplied the language of hesitation and delay. The nineteenth-century sciences annexed the word: geology named the hanging valley, a tributary stream pouring over a cliff into the main valley, and the hanging glacier; mining named the hanging wall, the rock above a seam or fault. Each term preserves the old image: something held up with nothing beneath it.

VI. The twentieth–twenty-first centuries: technical niches and cultural retreat. Typography produced the hanging indent and hanging punctuation; grammar textbooks teach the “hanging participle,” the dangling modifier; butchery speaks of the hanging carcass and the hanging weight; horticulture of the hanging basket. In 2000, the American presidential election gave the world the “hanging chad” — a ballot punch-out left dangling, whose disputed counting carried the word into front-page history around the globe. Meanwhile the judicial sense has receded as Britain and other states abolished or marginalized capital punishment: modern hanging in that sphere is clinical, historical, or literary.

VII. Summary. Hanging began as a verb of everyday physics; its participle split into a decorative noun (tapestries), a terror noun (the gallows), an overhang adjective (cliffs and banks), and a suspense adjective (unsettled matters); and modern technology has re-farmed each branch. The “hanging banks” of the source text — banks whose brows overhang a shingly shore — reach back across a millennium to the oldest sense of all: the land holds its green edge up over the water with nothing beneath, and hangs there, as the word always has, between support and fall.
