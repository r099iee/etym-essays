The English plural clarinets rests on a French instrument-name built from Latin ‘clear’. Latin clārus means clear, bright, loud, or famous—the quality of something that rings out distinctly. That adjective (from a PIE ‘call / shout / clear sound’ complex, often reconstructed around *kel-) feeds a whole Romance family: English clear, clarity, declare, and the musical clarion, a high, clear trumpet call from Old French.

  • Sound and form shifts. The chain is conservative on the Romance side: Latin clārus → Old French clair ‘clear, bright’, with the -ā- vowel of classical Latin developing along the usual French path. French then forms clarine, a little bell or high clear-sounding signal instrument, and from it the diminutive clarinette—literally ‘a little clarine’—for the new single-reed woodwind whose selling point was a clear, flexible voice. English borrows clarinet in the eighteenth century, dropping the French diminutive spelling to a simpler form, and the plural clarinets is a routine English inflection.
  • The instrument's history. The clarinet is a relative latecomer: it developed around 1690–1700, most famously in the workshops of Johann Christoph Denner of Nuremberg, out of the earlier single-reed chalumeau by adding a register key that extended the range upward. The French-style name ‘little clarine’ stuck even though the instrument matured in German-speaking lands. From the mid-18th century it entered the concert orchestra, where Mozart wrote for it with special affection, and by the 19th century it was a standard of orchestras, wind bands, and chamber music, prized for its range from dark chalumeau tones to brilliant high notes.
  • Semantic drift. The semantic history stays close to the instrument. The name always points at the clear, bright, ringing quality of the tone: from the high clear signals of bells and trumpets to the smooth, vocal, flexible woodwind voice. Figurative uses are rare compared with fiddle or drum; the word has not bred a large idiom set. The main modern development is family-internal: ‘clarinet’ can now name the whole family, so that bass clarinet, alto clarinet, and contrabass clarinet are all ‘clarinets’, and in acoustics the word labels the cylindrical-bore, single-reed design and its overblowing at the twelfth.
  • Socio-cultural context. The clarinet's social career spans high and low. It was a virtuoso solo and orchestral instrument in the 18th–19th centuries; in the 20th it moved into jazz and popular music, where its flexible, vocal quality made it one of the most expressive of woodwinds—think of New Orleans jazz and the swing-era ‘licorice stick’. That double life—concert hall and dance band—mirrors the fiddle's but with a different register: the clarinet's tone is smooth and singing where the fiddle's is reedy and driving.
  • Literary attestations. In The Hobbit, Bifur and Bofur's clarinets among the walking-sticks complete the comic orchestra inventory. Tolkien writes modern English instrument-words into a dwarven company so that Bag End fills with a full band's texture—reeds beside strings and drum—before the song of the Mountain deepens the mood. The word itself is a transparent ‘clear-sounding little instrument’, so the noun quietly reinforces the scene's homely brightness. The card form is the plural: two (or more) clear-voiced woodwinds fetched from the hall like any other travelling gear.
