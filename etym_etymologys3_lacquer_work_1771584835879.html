The biography of lacquer-work is the biography of a substance, not merely of a word. It begins in India with the Sanskrit noun lakṣā (लक्षा), which named the red resinous secretion of the lac insect (Kerria lacca). For thousands of years Indian dyers and varnishers gathered this resin, dissolved it, and used it both to colour cloth with a deep crimson dye and to seal and ornament wooden surfaces. From the very beginning, the word was tied to a luxury good and a craft tradition: the lac tree itself (the palas or dhak) was cultivated in groves for the resin it yielded, and the finished wares travelled far beyond the subcontinent. The word then migrated westward along the very trade routes that carried the resin. It entered Persian as lāk, Arabic as lakk, and then, with the Portuguese maritime expansion of the sixteenth century, into the Romance languages as Portuguese laca and lacre (whence “sealing-wax”) and Spanish laca. French took it as laque, with an older form lacre, and from French and Portuguese English drew its own terms: lac (recorded around 1660 for the resinous substance), lake (about 1610, the crimson pigment, via French laque), and lacquer itself (1570s, “a kind of varnish”), through the obsolete French lacre. Meanwhile the numeral sense of the related Sanskrit word lakh (Hindi लाख, “one hundred thousand”) entered Indian English separately, kept alive ever since in journalism and administration in the subcontinent—a sense tied to the swarm-like abundance of the insects, though modern scholars treat the connection with caution. In the seventeenth century the term lacquer was applied by extension to a wholly distinct and still more celebrated craft of East Asia: the layered, polished tree-sap lacquer of China and Japan, made not from insect resin but from the sap of the lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum). English visitors and merchants, already using lacquer for the Indian varnish, transferred the word to the Chinese and Japanese wares that were the wonder of the East India trade—red and black cabinets, screens, and boxes built up from dozens of coats of urushiol sap, cured in damp cellars and polished between coats to a depth impossible in European varnish. It is this East Asian craft that most modern uses of “lacquer-work” actually describe, and it is the craft at work in the passage from The Story of the Stone, where a “low rose-shaped table of coloured lacquer-work” signals the wealth and refinement of a great household. The compound lacquer-work was coined in English on the standard pattern of craft nouns: lacquer + work, like needlework, woodwork, or stonework. It names both the activity (the making of lacquered objects) and its products (the objects themselves), which is exactly why the term can describe a table, a cabinet, or the technique by which both were finished. From the seventeenth century onward the compound appears in inventories, travel accounts, and the records of the East India trade; in the eighteenth century it faced competition from japanning, the European imitation, but retained its association with genuine Oriental provenance and with slow, patient, hand-made labour. Sound, spelling, and form: the word shows no native sound change in English, since it is a late loan (lacquer is recorded only from the 1570s, well after the Great Vowel Shift). Its form reveals its route of transmission: the -er ending reflects the Portuguese/French forms, and the spelling settles into the modern lacquer alongside the older lac and lake. The initial l- cluster and the -quer spelling (with the -u- of French laque) preserve the medieval Western reading of the Arabic/Persian form. Semantic drift and modern extensions:
  • Substance → coating: from the raw resin (lac) to the varnish made from it (lacquer).
  • Coating → craft: from the substance to the discipline of applying it (lacquer-work).
  • Craft → objects: from the technique to the finished wares (lacquer-work = the objects).
  • Finish → aesthetic metaphor: “lacquered” comes to mean polished, glossy, superficially perfect, concealing its base—a metaphor extended in modern English to hair lacquer, nail lacquer, and automotive lacquer.
  • Colonial trade colouring: the word’s exotic, connoisseurial register is a product of the European encounter with Asian luxury goods in the 17th–19th centuries.
Literary and documentary attestations: inventories and wills of the 17th century listing “lacquer-work cabinets”; the records of the East India Company; the novels of the Chinese tradition in translation, where lacquer-work marks the grandeur of great households (as in The Story of the Stone); and, in modern craft and museum discourse, the standard term for the decorated lacquer objects of China, Japan, and India. Across all these uses the word keeps its oldest meaning intact: a finish that is the product of time, labour, and accumulated coats.
