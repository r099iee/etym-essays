Nominal continues one of the great stable roots of Indo-European, PIE *h₁nomn- "name", reflected in Sanskrit nāman, Greek onoma, Latin nomen, Gothic namo, Old English nama/name. Latin nomen meant both personal name and grammatical noun — the name of a thing — and the derived adjective nominalis meant "pertaining to a name or noun", used by Varro and later grammarians for noun inflection and by jurists for debts reckoned by name. The form is transparent: nomen + -alis "relating to", parallel to realis, verbalis.

Old French took it as nominal for noun-questions in scholastic logic (nominal definitions vs real definitions), and Middle English borrowed it in the fifteenth century in the same schoolroom sense: nominal questions, nominal differences. The crucial early-modern extension is from grammar to reality: if a distinction is merely nominal, it exists in name only. Seventeenth-century theology and law are full of nominal Christians, nominal payments, nominal distinctions — labels without substance. Eighteenth-century political economy sharpens this into the nominal/real opposition for prices, wages, and values that persists in every economics textbook: nominal is what the coin says, real is what it buys.

  • Sound change: PIE *h₁nomn- loses its laryngeal to give Latin nomen; French and English preserve spelling nominal with stress shift to /ˈnɒmɪnəl/, the -al anglicised and unstressed.
  • Morphology: root nomin- + adjectival -al; productive family includes nominate, nominee, nominalise, nominalism, nominalist, nominally, each exploiting a different facet of naming.
  • Semantic layering: grammatical (of nouns), ontological (in name only), economic (face-value), technical (designated), stylistic (noun-heavy) — all co-present in modern dictionaries without confusion because domain disambiguates.
  • Philosophical fame: medieval nominalism (Roscelin, Ockham) — universals are nomina, mere names — makes nominal a battle-flag in metaphysics, revived in modern nominalist philosophies of mathematics.
  • Style criticism: twentieth-century plain-English movements (Orwell, Gowers, Strunk & White) stigmatise nominal style — nominalisations, zombie nouns — as the bureaucratic disease; the source sentence parodies critics who love to name it while practising it.

From sacred names to grammar drills to inflation charts to prose workshops, nominal has travelled by holding fast to one contrast: what is called versus what is there. Its longevity proves how often civilisation needs a word for the label that outruns the thing.

Stylistic moralising gives the story its sting: Orwell on verbs, Gowers on abstract nouns, and contemporary plain-language audits all count nominalisations as fog indexes, urging writers to restore agents and actions. Yet linguists reply that nominal density has virtues - packing information, building technical taxonomies, and achieving cohesion - so the hypotactic nominal style mocked in the source persists because in research, law, and administration it efficiently names complex processes. Nominal thus remains double-edged: the name that clarifies and the name that conceals.
