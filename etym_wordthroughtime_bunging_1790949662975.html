- 15th to 16th Century - Physical plugging of barrels: Attested as *bunge* from Middle Dutch; strictly meant plugging, corking, or stopping up the bunghole of beer and wine casks.
- 16th to 17th Century - Elizabethan Underworld Cant: The noun *bung* became thieves' cant for a pocket or purse, and a pickpocket was a 'bung-nipper'.
- 18th to 19th Century - Slang emergence of throwing & bribery: Evolved in British vernacular to mean thrusting money into a pocket ('bunging a bribe') and violently or casually tossing/chucking an object into a place.
- Early 20th Century - P. G. Wodehouse & British Public School Slang: Became a beloved colloquialism for tossing objects casually ('bunging stones', 'bung it over here') or casually putting things somewhere ('bung it in the oven').
- Contemporary Usage - Thrives in British, Australian, and New Zealand informal English for casual tossing, stuffing things into bags, plugging holes, or Australian slang for breaking down ('bung it on', 'go bung').
