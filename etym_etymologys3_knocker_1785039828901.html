**The sound of a blow: KNOCKER and its imitative ancestry** The biography of KNOCKER begins not with a reconstructed root but with a sound. The word belongs to the expressive, imitative stratum of Germanic vocabulary in which a word is shaped by the noise it names. Its earliest English ancestor, the Old English verb *cnocian* (Anglian *cnucian*), recorded from the early medieval period, meant "to pound, beat" and, from the start, "to knock at a door" — the sharp, repeated tap that announces a visitor. The initial cluster *cn-* (later simplified to *kn-*) is itself sound-symbolic: it belongs to a whole family of Germanic words built on the expressive base *kn-* — *knob*, *knoll*, *knuckle*, *knurl* — all describing small, hard, protruding things that strike, tap, or catch. No Proto-Indo-European reconstruction is reliably attested for this group; these are imitative and expressive formations rather than inherited inflected forms. In Old English the cluster *cn-* was pronounced as a genuine stop cluster /kn-/, and it survived untouched through the Anglo-Saxon period. **From verb to agent noun in the Middle Ages** From *cnocian* came Middle English *knokken*, the verb of striking and tapping, and from the verb, in the fourteenth century, the noun *knokke*, "a blow or the sound of a blow." From there it was a small step to form an agent noun with the suffix *-er*: a *knocker* is simply "one who or that which knocks." The suffix *-er* is the same ubiquitous Germanic agentive that produced *reader*, *speaker*, *hunter*; in the case of *knocker* it gave a word that could straddle the human and the mechanical — a person who deals blows or a device that delivers them — and this very ambiguity is what Tolkien exploits in the goblin chant "Knocker and gongs!" where the noun hovers between a goblin thug and a brutal instrument of iron. **The door-knocker and the house** In the early modern period the word flourished in two registers. It named the great brass door-knocker of English houses — the ring or clapper lifted and struck against a metal plate to announce a visitor — and it named persons: the *knocker-up* who walked the streets before dawn rapping on the windows of sleeping laborers. The door-knocker became a small object of social display: its size, material, and ornament silently declared the wealth and standing of the household behind the door, and the idiom "on the knocker" (going door to door, as a salesman or canvasser does) grew directly out of the custom of announcing each call by the knocker. **The industrial century and the figurative turn** In the nineteenth century the verb went mechanical, naming the "knock" of an engine (1869) — the metallic tapping of a detonating cylinder — and figurative, meaning "to disparage, to criticize" (1892), so that a *knocker* also became a fault-finder, a carping detractor. The verb proved extraordinarily productive: *knock-down* (1760, auctioneering), *knockout* (1887, boxing), *knock-off* (mid-20th century: to stop work, to steal, to kill, to copy cheaply), *knockabout*, *knockback*. In twentieth-century British and Australian slang the plural *knockers* drifted into crude anatomical territory (1930s onward), a transfer from the idea of things that knock together. **Literary and cultural life** Culturally, *knock* and *knocker* have a long literary record. The Old English *cnocian* appears in glosses and homilies; in the Middle English *Ancrene Wisse* and in Langland's *Piers Plowman* the verb describes the knocking of the soul at the door of mercy. The Authorized Version's "knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Matthew 7:7) fixed the knock as a metaphor of spiritual seeking. Dickens's London is full of door-knockers, and the *knocker-up* became a stock figure of industrial memoir, remembered in the social history of the mill towns. In the twentieth century the "gong-show" and the "knockout" carried the vocabulary into entertainment and sport, while the plain, oldest sense — a thing that strikes hard and loudly — survives unchanged in Tolkien's underground chant, where the goblins' knocker beats time with their cruel march. The word has thus kept, for more than a thousand years, the imitative force of its first sound: it still sounds like the blow it names.
