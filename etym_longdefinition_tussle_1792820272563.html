Tussle names a short, vigorous, disorderly struggle between two parties — bodies grappling, hands pulling, each side striving to prevail without quite reaching the dignity of a battle or the violence of a brawl. It is the word for what happens when disagreement turns physical but stays small: wrestlers on a rug, puppies over a slipper, toddlers over a toy.

In the source sentence Frodo has a tussle with young Sancho Proudfoot, who has begun an excavation in the larger pantry where he thinks there is an echo. The pantry setting tells us everything about the register: this is no duel and no assault, but a scrambling, laughing, elbow-and-knee affair among hobbits, half earnest and half play, ending presumably with both parties dusty and the echo untested. Tolkien chooses tussle precisely because it refuses to take itself seriously.

Core meaning in detail: A tussle is brief, close-quarters, and comparatively good-natured. Unlike a fight, it implies no hatred; unlike a brawl, no crowd and no chairs thrown; unlike a duel, no rules and no honour. The verb to tussle (they tussled for the microphone) and the noun (a tussle for the leadership) shade into each other freely. Duration matters: prolong a tussle and it becomes a struggle; arm it and it becomes a fight; multiply it and it becomes a melee.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A physical scuffle or grapple (primary sense, from 1620s): the children had a tussle on the lawn, puppies in a tussle over a bone. The bridge from its parent tousle (to rumple, to handle roughly) is direct: a tussle is what two people do when each is tousling the other.
  • A sporting contest, close and hard-fought (extended sense): a midfield tussle, a title tussle, a relegation tussle. The bridge is bodily competition without enmity: footballers shoulder to shoulder are doing with a ball what wrestlers do with each other.
  • A verbal wrangle or dispute (figurative sense): a courtroom tussle, a cabinet tussle over budgets. The bridge carries grappling from arms to arguments: positions lock, twist, and seek a fall exactly as bodies do.
  • A political or legal power struggle (journalistic sense): the tussle for the nomination, a diplomatic tussle over fishing rights. The bridge is the headline writer's need for a word lighter than conflict but livelier than dispute — combat scaled to committee rooms.
  • A playful or affectionate rough-and-tumble (domestic sense): father and son in their nightly tussle on the carpet. The bridge is the word's built-in good humour: because tussle never quite means real violence, it is the natural name for mock combat that ends in laughter.
  • An inner or moral struggle (literary fringe): his tussle with conscience, her tussle with temptation. The bridge internalises the grapple: the self splits into two wrestlers, and the soul is the mat they roll upon.

Register and connotation: tussle is warm, brisk, and faintly comic — the sports pages and the nursery rather than the police report. It miniaturises conflict: to call something a tussle is to promise the reader that nobody was badly hurt and something may even have been fun. That is why politicians love it for their rivalries and dread it nowhere near as much as feud or showdown. Say it aloud and the double s seems to enact the scuffle — a small masterpiece of sound suggesting sense.
