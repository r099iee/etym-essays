Confounded is the past participle and adjective of confound: in its dominant modern sense, thrown into bewilderment, perplexed beyond easy sorting-out — the mind's contents poured together until nothing can be told apart. In older and exclamatory use it is a softened curse: confound it, you confounded nuisance — literally wishing confusion and ruin upon someone, bleached by centuries of use into mere grumbling.

In the source sentence — a string of confounded visitors hanging on the bell — the word is pure irritable adjective: unwelcome callers pestering a household that craves peace, damned only mildly, in the way of a respectable narrator who will not swear outright. The visitors are not bewildered; they are bewildering — a nuisance, a plague, confounded in the damn-them sense that descends directly from its Latin root: to pour together, to ruin, to destroy.

Core meaning in detail: At the centre of every sense sits the Latin image of liquids poured into one vessel: con- (together) + fundere (to pour, melt, cast). What is poured together loses its boundaries — streams merge, faces blur, plans collapse into each other. From that one physical picture English has drawn three great uses: to confuse mentally (thoughts poured together), to mistake one thing for another (two faces poured into one), and to curse (may your affairs be poured into chaos — ruined, damned).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Bewildered, perplexed (primary modern sense): a confounded expression, confounded by the instructions — the mind unable to separate the streams. The bridge is direct from the root: ideas poured together cannot be poured apart again, so confusion is mixture that will not unmix.
  • Mistaken one for another, mixed up (classical sense): to confound right and wrong, to confound the twins. The bridge is the same vessel: two distinct things treated as one liquid, distinctions dissolved — the theologian's and the logician's confound.
  • Cursed, damned, blasted — mild imprecation (colloquial, from 17th c.): this confounded rain, you confounded fool. The bridge runs through ruin: Latin confundere also meant to destroy and bring to ruin, so confound him wished annihilation — then centuries of drawing-room use sanded it down to a grumble no stronger than wretched or blasted.
  • Refuted, put to shame, overthrown (formal, now literary): to confound one's enemies, confound their arguments — to pour their plans into chaos. The bridge is military-theological: Babel's tongues were confounded, armies are confounded, pride is confounded — confusion as defeat, still alive in hymns and liturgy.
  • Abashed, struck dumb with shame (archaic): he stood confounded before the accusation. The bridge is inward: the same mixture happens inside the face and tongue — blushes, stammers, silence — confusion worn on the body.
  • Softened oath — the minced-curse family: confound it all stands where stronger damnation was felt to be profane. The bridge is social: respectable speakers needed a curse with the damnation washed out, and since confound already meant ruin, it cursed politely — damnation with its boots wiped.

Register and connotation: confounded lives a double life. As bewildered it is neutral, bookish, slightly formal — examination papers and manuals confound their readers without any ill temper. As cursed it is cosy period grumbling: Wodehouse uncles, Tolkien narrators, railway-porter English — annoyance with its hat tipped, never the hard profanity it replaced. The two senses rarely collide because syntax parts them: confounded by bewilders, while confounded before a noun damns — confounded by the map versus that confounded map. Tragedy takes the first; comedy takes the second.
