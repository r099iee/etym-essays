- Late 14th to 15th Century - [Diplomatic envoy & legal petitioner] - Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman and Latin; primarily designated an ambassador, advocate, or envoy sent to plead a sovereign's cause, as well as a humble complainant in courts of equity.
- 16th to 17th Century - [Classical humanist speaker] - Under the influence of Renaissance humanism and the widespread rediscovery of Cicero and Quintilian, the term shifted decisively to designate a master of public eloquence, rhetoric, and moral philosophy.
- 18th to 19th Century - [Parliamentary & pulpit virtuoso] - Reached its cultural zenith during the golden age of parliamentary debates and religious revivals; viewed as the ultimate mark of statesmanship, moral authority, and gentlemanly education.
- 20th Century to Present - [Grand statesman or ironic windbag] - Retains supreme prestige for monumental historical voices (such as Churchill or King), but in everyday contemporary prose is often applied with humorous irony to pompous, long-winded speakers as modern communication favors casual soundbites over classical grandiloquence.
