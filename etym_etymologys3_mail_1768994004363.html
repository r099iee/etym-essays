MAIL — evolution essay

The story of "mail" the armor is the story of a single Latin word with two faces — one stained, one netted — and of the second face's long medieval career. The Latin ancestor is macula, attested in Classical Latin (1st century BCE) with two senses that may or may not be the same word: first, "a spot, a stain, a blemish," and second, "the mesh, the open hole of a net." Etymologists are not agreed on the word's deeper past: no secure Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed, and de Vaan's dictionary marks macula as of uncertain, possibly substrate origin — it is, in effect, a native Italic word without known relatives outside Latin. What matters for our word is the second sense: the "mesh." A net, seen up close, is a lattice of open holes; the Latin word for that holey fabric came, in the Romance languages, to mean any mesh — a stitch, a lattice, a link.

In Old French the word took its decisive technical form: maille, "a mesh, a link of chain armor, a stitch." The step from mesh to armor is easy to picture: the chain-armorer took the open mesh of a net and forged it into links of metal, producing a fabric of interlocked rings that moved and yielded like cloth while still turning a blade. From the 12th century onward the French maille is standard for the link and for the armor built of links, and from French the word entered Middle English in the 14th century (c. 1300–1400) as mail(e) or mayle: armor of interlocked rings. For the next four hundred years "mail" was the plain English word for ring-armor — the mail-shirt, the mail-coif, the hauberk of mail worn by knight and man-at-arms from the Crusades to the Hundred Years' War, the flexible metal fabric that made the medieval knight a fortress that could still run and ride. The sound shifts and spellings were many — maille, mayle, male, mayl — but the word itself was never in doubt: it named the rings.

Two later developments completed the word's modern shape. The first is the broadening of "mail" from rings to armor in general. By the 18th and 19th centuries "mail" could name any armor of small pieces — overlapping plates, scales — and, by a natural metaphor, any natural protective covering: the mail of a turtle, of an armadillo, of a crocodile, of a beast with a scaly hide. This is the sense the passage uses when it calls the vortlup's skin "scaly mail": the armorer's craft is borrowed into natural history, and the creatures become walking fortresses, their hides turning the abrasive storm as a wall turns a blade. The second development is linguistic: in the 18th century, when "mail" alone had grown opaque to everyday speakers (who now thought of "mail" as letters), the pleonasm "chain mail" was coined to name the ring-and-link fabric specifically — a redundancy that has proved so useful and so evocative that it has entirely displaced the bare word in popular speech, even though "mail" alone always meant exactly the rings.

Throughout its history the armor-word has had to share its spelling with an unrelated homograph: "mail" the postal system, from Old French male, "a bag, a wallet," of Frankish and Germanic origin (Proto-Germanic *malhō, "a satchel" — letters traveled in a bag). The two words are completely unrelated — the armor-mail descends from Latin macula, the post-mail from a Germanic bag-word — yet the collision has colored modern perception: because of the postal homograph, "mail" the armor sounds faintly antique, a relic kept alive in period prose, fantasy, and metaphor. The same collision has also preserved the armor-word in the compound "chain mail," whose survival owes as much to the need to distinguish armor from post as to any technical precision. The passage's "scaly mail" draws on exactly this relic-glow: an ancient word of the armorer, made new for the hide of a desert beast, standing beside the armored scales of a creature wholly insensible to the blowing sand.
