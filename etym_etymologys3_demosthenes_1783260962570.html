The name Demosthenes is a compound Greek personal name, Δημοσθένης (Dēmosthenēs), "strength of the people," built from two independent Indo-European roots, each of which deserves its own biography before the two fuse in the orator's name. Element one: the people (PIE *deh₂-, "to divide, share out"):
  • The first element, δῆμος (dēmos), "the people," is the standard Greek word for the citizen body — but its root meaning is "a division, a portion, a district." It descends from the PIE root *deh₂-/*dā-, "to divide, share out, allot," the same root that produced Sanskrit dáti "cuts, divides, allots," and the Greek "portion, feast" δαίς (dāis), the shared-out meal. Etymologically, the people were those who had received their allotted portion of the land: the dēmos was the body of share-holders in the community.
  • The Greek form is attested already in Mycenaean, where Linear B da-mo means "the district, the community," and the Doric dialect kept the form dâmos. The development from "allotted district" to "the people of the district" to "the citizen body, the commons" is a classic example of a political term rising out of a land-distribution term — the same path by which Latin populus and English people took on their modern senses.
  • From dēmos came the whole family of Greek political vocabulary, above all δημοκρατία (dēmokratia), "people-power," which travelled through Latin democratia and Old French democratie into English democracy. The "people" who were first divided into shares of land became, in the end, the people who rule.
Element two: the strength (PIE *segh-, "to hold, prevail"):
  • The second element, σθένος (sthenos), "strength, might, bodily force," is etymologically harder. The traditional and still standard account connects it with the PIE root *segh-, "to hold, prevail, conquer," the source of Greek ἔχω (ékhō), "I hold, have," of Sanskrit sah-, "to overcome, endure," with its noun sahas, "might, power," of Avestan hazah, "power," and of the Germanic victory-word *seg-, which produced Old Norse sigr, German Sieg, and the Old English name-element sige, "victory," as in Sigebert and Siegfried.
  • It must be recorded honestly, however, that Robert Beekes treats σθένος with marked caution and allows the possibility of a Pre-Greek (substrate) element; the *segh- connection is the standard scholarly account, but the word's deeper history is not beyond doubt. In the compound the sense is the simple, physical "might" of the people, borne proudly as a political and family claim.
The fusion and the name:
  • Dēmosthénēs, "strength of the people," is a transparent compound personal name of the classical Greek aristocratic type, formed exactly like Sophokles, "glory of wisdom," or Timotheos, "honouring god." The name was already in use in the family — the orator's father was also Demosthenes, of the deme Paeania — and it proclaimed the family's claim to serve and strengthen the dēmos. This is a "speaking name," and the coincidence that its greatest bearer was Athens' greatest popular orator made the etymology prophetic.
  • The name passed into Latin unchanged as Dēmosthenēs, and from Latin into English in the form Demosthenes, a learned proper noun with no subsequent sound shift. Its English stress, on the second syllable (de-MOS-the-nes), reflects the Greek accentuation Dēmosthénēs, preserved through the Latin-Greek school tradition.
Sound, culture, and literary afterlife:
  • Ancient criticism from Cicero and Quintilian onward made Demosthenes the canon of eloquence: Cicero modelled his own Philippics on the orator's, and "Demosthenic" became a technical term of rhetoric for the intense, concentrated, climactic style, as opposed to the more periodic manner of Isocrates or Cicero himself.
  • The legendary training story — pebbles in the mouth to overcome a stammer, speaking over the roar of the sea, reciting verse while running uphill — made the name a byword for self-conquest, repeated in every handbook of public speaking from antiquity to the modern self-help tradition.
  • In political history the name carries the tragic glamour of a lost cause: Demosthenes opposed Philip and Alexander of Macedon, demanded the surrender that would have destroyed Athenian democracy, and preferred suicide to capture in 322 BC. His Philippics gave English "philippic," a bitter invective or denunciation, and his career gave the type of the orator-statesman who stakes everything on the spoken word.
  • Macaulay's placing of "the masterpieces of Sophocles, of Demosthenes, and of Plato" in the mouth of the Byzantine pedants shows the name's canonising power: by the nineteenth century Demosthenes stood, with the tragedian and the philosopher, as one of the three prose-and-poetry summits of Greek civilisation, still preserved in the school-texts of an empire that had lost every cause he died for.
Socio-cultural factors: The name's history condenses three centuries of Greek political development: the rise of the dēmos from land-share to sovereign people, the fourth-century crisis of the free polis under the Macedonian monarchy, and the eternal myth of the orator who is the people's voice. "Strength of the people" was a noble family's boast; history made it the epitaph of Athenian democracy itself. The word today, in English, is at once a historical proper noun, a term of rhetorical art (Demosthenic eloquence), a symbol of resistance to tyranny, and the name of the most famous overcoming of a speech impediment in recorded history — all four senses flowing from the single compound of "people" and "might."
