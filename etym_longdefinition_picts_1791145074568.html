Picts (plural proper noun, singular Pict) denotes the ancient Celtic-speaking confederation of peoples who inhabited northern and eastern Scotland (north of the Forth-Clyde line) during Late Antiquity and the early Middle Ages (roughly 3rd to 9th centuries CE), known to Romans as Picti, "the painted ones," famed for body-paint or tattoos, carved symbol stones, and resistance to Rome. In Durant's sentence they appear as one of the "later peoples" among whom cannibalism was allegedly found — Irish, Iberians, Picts, Danes — illustrating his thesis of once-universal savagery.

Historically the Picts were Iron-Age farmers, fishermen, and warriors organised in small kingdoms (notably Fortriu), who repelled Roman invasions, raided Roman Britain alongside Scots and Saxons, accepted Christianity through missions of Columba and Ninian (6th–7th c.), produced the great cross-slabs (Aberlemno, Sueno's Stone) and illuminated manuscripts (Book of Kells milieu), and finally merged with Gaelic Scots under Kenneth MacAlpin to form the Kingdom of Alba, ancestor of Scotland.

  • Strict historical sense (core): The 3rd–9th c. northern British peoples: Pictish kingdoms, Pictish language (Brittonic Celtic, now extinct), Pictish stones, Pictish Christianity. This is the scholarly use — precise, dated, archaeological and textual. Example: Pictish symbol stones, Pictish kings list.
  • Roman-ethnographic sense: Picti in Latin sources as barbarian raiders: painted, fierce, beyond the Wall. Bridge: Roman external labelling by appearance. Carries the savage-freedom stereotype (Tacitean noble savage vs. Jerome's cannibal slander).
  • Medieval-legendary sense: Picts as a vanished, mysterious race in chronicles (Bede, Irish annals). Bridge: after absorption c. 843 they survive only in king-lists and origin myths (Picts as Scythians, as matrilineal). Connotation: lost, enigmatic founders.
  • Modern romantic-popular sense: Tattooed warriors of Braveheart-era imagination, woad-painted freedom fighters, New-Age symbols. Bridge: 19th-century antiquarianism + modern Scottish nationalism + fantasy fiction. Often exaggerated: blue-painted hordes, matriarchy, druidic secrets.
  • Durant-era anthropological sense: Picts asIDR exemplars of primitive cannibalism. Bridge: early-20th-century evolutionist anthropology credulously repeating classical cannibal tales (Jerome on Attacotti). Modern scholarship: no reliable evidence; funerals (contrary to Durant's "funerals were unknown") are in fact well attested — long-cist cemeteries, Christian burials.
  • Grammatical note: Always capitalised, almost always plural (the Picts). Singular Pict = one member; adjective Pictish = language, art, period. "Eleventh-century Danes" is dated; Picts by the 11th century had long ceased to exist as such (absorbed 9th c.) — Durant groups diachronic peoples synchronically for rhetorical effect.

In sum, Picts names both a real early-medieval civilisation — farmers, monks, stone-carvers who became Scots — and a screen onto which outsiders projected savagery or nobility. Durant uses the savage screen; modern history restores the carvers and Christians alongside the warriors.
