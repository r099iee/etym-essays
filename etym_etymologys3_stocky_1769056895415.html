stocky — The biography of stocky opens with an action, not a body. Its oldest reconstructible ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *steu-, "to push, strike, knock; to thrust, stick", a root whose violence can still be heard in Vedic Sanskrit tudáti "he strikes, thrusts" (root tud) and in Ancient Greek týptō "I strike, beat" (from the extended *steup-). In the Germanic branch the root was rebuilt with a doubled consonant as *stukkaz, and the meaning narrowed from striking to the thing struck: a stump, a log, a tree-trunk — the block cut or knocked from a tree. This is the classic Indo-European route from verbal root to concrete noun, and Old English stocc "stump, trunk, post" preserves it exactly, attested from the early Anglo-Saxon period. A stocc was at once a felled trunk, a post, a block of wood, and — by extension of the same image — the wooden pillory in which offenders were locked: the "stocks" are simply the plural of the block, the criminal locked into the trunk. In Middle English stok became one of the great semantic radiators of the language. Because a trunk is the standing base of a tree, the word came to mean base, foundation, and source: the stock of a family (the trunk from which descendants branch), goods in stock (the stored base of trade), livestock (the breeding base of a farm), stock as the capital or shares of a company (the financial base), and stock as broth (the base of a soup made from bones and vegetables). The gun-stock keeps the trunk-idea in firearms, and the verb to stock ("to supply, to fill up") and stock-still preserve the same solid base. Each of these senses is a metaphor standing on the same trunk: the word stock is itself a tree from which the meanings branch. The adjective stocky arrived late and with two distinguishable lives. In the sixteenth century stocky first meant "stock-like": stiff, stock-still, immovable, even dull and stupid, in line with stockish — the sense of a person as wooden as a block. It was only in the late nineteenth century, in the 1890s, that the modern sense crystallised: stock + the adjective-forming suffix -y, "having the nature of a stock", applied to the human body to mean short, broad, and solidly built — dense, firmly planted, immovable as a log. The word was born into an industrial and agricultural world that already understood physical type through the shared image of a low, solid tree-stump, and it passed quickly into the vocabulary of physical description in sport, labour, and the armed forces: sailors, pitmen, farmers, and soldiers described as stocky are compact, dense, and built to bear weight. The cultural career of the word is closely tied to genre fiction. In the twentieth century stocky became the default epithet of Dwarves in the wake of Tolkien and Lewis — a physical typing that borrows the stump's dignity, stability, and stubborn immovability. In the "Prince Caspian" passage, Lewis's use of the word is therefore not merely descriptive but genre-defining: the Dwarf is presented as a body built for weight and endurance, rooted, hard to move, the very opposite of the slender, graceful frame of the romantic hero. The word's neutrality is its strength — it can describe a dwarf, a terrier, a rugby forward, or a chair without changing its tone.
  • Sound change and form: *steu- → *stukkaz shows the extension *-k- and the expressive geminate *-kk- common in Germanic; Old English stocc reflects the geminate, and later English simplified /kk/ to /k/.
  • Morphology: stocky is a dead-simple derivation, stock + -y, where -y (Old English -ig) means "having the nature or quality of", the same suffix as in stony, woody, rocky.
  • Chronology: the physical sense is late (1890s), two and a half millennia after the root, because English first used the trunk-word for the trunk itself and only very late transferred the image to the human body.
  • Literary attestation: the modern sense first becomes common in the sporting and working-class prose of the late Victorian era and then floods fantasy fiction in the twentieth century, where "the stocky dwarf" hardens into a stock character.
  • Social register: unlike its contemptuous neighbours squat and dumpy, stocky carries a worker's respect — the dignity of the stump, the body built to bear weight and to stand firm.
