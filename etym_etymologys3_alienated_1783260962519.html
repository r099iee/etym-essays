The biography of alienated begins, unpromisingly enough, with one of the smallest and most fundamental words in Proto-Indo-European: the stem *h₂el-, meaning “beyond, other, foreign.” From it was built the adjective *h₂élyos, “other,” which split the Indo-European family into a brilliant fan of near-identical reflexes: Greek állos, Latin alius, Germanic *aljaz, Armenian ayl, and Tocharian ālak. There is no reliably reconstructed Indic cognate, and no scholar has credibly forced one; the honest tree simply leaves the Indo-Aryan branch empty rather than inventing a Sanskrit phantom. The Germanic reflex is the one every English speaker already knows: Old English elles, which survives in modern English as else — so that the little adverb “elsewhere” is, in the deepest sense, the same word as “alienated.” The root's meaning is pure relation: it names whatever stands across a boundary, beyond the group, outside the house. Everything that follows is the slow career of that idea of otherness as it was built into Roman law, Christian theology, and modern social science.

The Roman legal career. In Latin the stem produced alius, “other,” and from it a cluster of derivatives that fixed the concept of otherness in the legal vocabulary of the Roman world. Aliēnus meant “belonging to another,” hence “foreign, strange,” and the verb aliēnāre meant literally “to make another's” — a technical verb of Roman property law for transferring ownership of an estate from one person to another. This is the origin of the double sense that the English word still carries: an alienation in law is a conveyance of property, while an alienation of affection is the transfer of a heart or a loyalty away from its proper object. Both meanings travelled together into the language of the late empire and the Church; in Christian Latin, aliēnātus could describe the soul made foreign to God by sin — a transfer of the soul itself. The English noun alienation was borrowed in the fourteenth century, the verb alienate (from Old French aliéner) in the fifteenth, both as learned words of law and theology before they were ever used of feelings or politics.

Into English: from law to politics. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word moved from the courts into the wider political vocabulary. To alienate a kingdom was to transfer it; to alienate a class or a church was to drive it out of its allegiance — and in the constitutional struggles of seventeenth-century England, especially in the crises of the 1680s, the political sense grew strong. This is the register Macaulay uses: his sentence is a lawyer's word applied to a nation, so that “the errors alienated a loyal gentry and priesthood” reads like the transfer of the realm's two greatest estates out of the king's hands. The old legal gravity is exactly what makes the metaphor work; the writer is describing a conveyance of loyalty, and the language of the property law gives the political rupture its dry, conclusive force.

The psychological and sociological turn. The nineteenth century pushed the word inward. German philosophy and social theory, above all Marx, used Entfremdung and Entäußerung for the estrangement of the worker from his labour and its product; when Marx's early writings were translated into English, alienation was the chosen rendering, and the word became a term of art in social science. In the twentieth century the sociological and psychiatric literature generalized it: alienation as powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, and social isolation, and “the alienated individual” as a stock figure of modern criticism — the estrangement of the self from society, work, and even itself. At the same time the adjective alien, borrowed from French centuries before, was reborn in 1953 science fiction as the extraterrestrial — the being who is other by nature — a popular revival of the very oldest sense of the root.

Macaulay at the hinge. Written in the 1840s, Macaulay's usage sits exactly at the boundary between the old legal meaning and the new psychological one. The word is still political and institutional; but its emotional force — the pathos of loyal men made strangers to their own king — anticipates the personal sense that would soon dominate. The sentence thus contains the whole career of the word in one stroke: otherness as law, otherness as politics, otherness as feeling. The etymology underwrites the politics: the gentry and priesthood were made other, transferred across a boundary, exactly as a Roman estate was made another's by conveyance — and the reader is meant to feel the loss.

  • PIE: *h₂el- “beyond, other” → *h₂élyos “other” (Greek állos, Latin alius, Germanic *aljaz, English else).
  • Latin: alius “other” → alienus “belonging to another, foreign” → alienāre “to make another's, transfer, estrange” (law of property) → alienātus “made other.”
  • English: alienation (14th c.), alienate (15th c.) — first legal and theological, then political (17th c.), then psychological and sociological (19th–20th c.).
  • Sister loans: English alien “foreign” (from Latin/French), reborn as “extraterrestrial” in 1953.
  • Marx: Entfremdung rendered as alienation — the worker estranged from product, labour, humanity, and self.
