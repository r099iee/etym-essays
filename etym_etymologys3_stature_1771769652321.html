The history of STATURE is, in miniature, the history of how a physical word learned to describe the moral life. The word is built on one of the oldest and most securely attested roots in the Indo-European family: PIE *steh₂-, "to stand, to make stand." This root appears with remarkable stability across nearly every branch of the family. In Sanskrit it yields sthā-, the verb "to stand," with an enormous derived vocabulary (sthāna, "place"; sthiti, "stability"); in Greek it yields histēmi, "I set up," and stasis, "a standing, a faction, a stoppage"; in Germanic it gives Old English standan and Modern English stand; and in Latin it gives stāre, "to stand," with its own vast family — status, station, statute, stable, constant, instant, and, at the end of one branch, statura. The root is thus a true paneurasian word of standing, a root whose descendants have been spoken for roughly six and a half thousand years. The specifically Latin development begins with the simple verb stāre, "to stand," attested from the earliest Latin literature, in the comedies of Plautus and Terence around 200 BCE. From stāre Latin formed the past participle status, "standing," "the manner of standing," and from there a noun status meaning "position, condition, state" — a word of enormous later importance in English via words like state and statute. The step that produces our word is the addition of the feminine suffix -ūra to the stem, giving statūra, "the manner of standing, stature, bodily height." The suffix -ūra was the ordinary Latin device for forming abstract and result nouns from verb stems — compare scriptūra "a writing" from scribere, pictūra "a painting" from pingere, cūra "care" (the formation is ancient and productive). By adding -ūra to the "standing" stem, Latin created a word that meant, quite literally, "the standingness" of a body: its vertical measure, its height as a standing thing. The word is attested in classical Latin of the first century CE — in Pliny the Elder's Natural History, describing the heights of peoples and animals, and in Ovid, using statura of bodily form — so the physical sense is fixed from the start. The transmission to English runs through French. Latin statūra passed into Old French as estature, and from there, or from the Anglo-French dialect that supplied so much vocabulary to medieval England, it entered Middle English around 1300 as stature, with the physical sense "bodily height" recorded by c. 1320. For roughly three centuries the English word meant only what the Latin meant: a person's height and build, a measurable fact of the body. Chaucer and his contemporaries use it plainly of physical form — a description of a knight's or a lady's stature is a description of their height and bearing, with no more than a hint of dignity carried by the very idea of standing upright. The decisive semantic shift came in the seventeenth century, in the intellectual climate of the Restoration. It is a shift of metaphor, and its mechanism is easy to state: the idea of standing tall — of rising above the physical crowd — was transferred to the moral and intellectual world, where a person of great achievement "stands tall" in reputation. The physical image had always carried a latent evaluative charge: a person of commanding stature was, by that very height, marked out. The seventeenth century made that charge explicit and general. In the prose of the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, one begins to find stature used of importance, dignity, and moral weight — a minister "of stature in the state," a man whose "stature of mind" matched his eminence. The eighteenth-century encyclopedists and essayists, including Gibbon himself, whose sentence this card quotes, could rely on the word's double register: the physical height of a person and the moral height of a reputation were, by now, the same metaphor in one word. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries consolidated the dual life. The physical sense remained the primary dictionary sense — the tape-measure sense of medicine, anthropology, and the census — while the figurative sense extended far beyond persons. Institutions gained stature, nations gained stature, works of art and literature gained stature; one could speak of the "stature" of an empire or an idea as naturally as of the stature of a man. The word also hardened in certain technical registers: in physical education and the medical sciences, stature became the standard term for measured standing height, and the anthropometric literature speaks of "stature" exactly as the old naturalists did. In the twentieth century, the figurative use came to dominate journalism and biography — "a leader of international stature," "a writer of considerable stature" — while the literal sense retired into medical and everyday descriptive use. The sociological note is worth adding. Stature is a word that flatters: to say a person "has stature" is to attribute not merely fame but weight, solidity, earned importance — a height of reputation that cannot be conferred by wealth or office alone but must be gained "by ability or achievement," as the card's short definition has it. This is why the word, unlike prestige or celebrity, suggests something durable: prestige can be borrowed, celebrity can be manufactured, but stature, in the image the word itself preserves, is the height at which a person genuinely stands. The history of the word — from a PIE root about standing, through a Latin suffix that measured the body, into an English word that measures the soul — is thus a history of metaphor doing the quiet work of moral description, and Gibbon's "beauty or deformity of stature" is a perfect example of the word still doing both jobs at once in a single sentence.
