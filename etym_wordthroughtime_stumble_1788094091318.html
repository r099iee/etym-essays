- (c. 1300) - "To trip, miss one's footing" — enters Middle English (stumblen) from Old Norse stumra; a plain word of everyday bodily mishap, used of men, beasts and horses.
- (1400s–1500s) - Extended to moral and spiritual failure — "stumble into sin", "stumble in the way" — the physical near-fall becomes the standing figure for a lapse of faith or conduct; the Bible translators give English the "stumbling-block" (rendering Greek skandalon), still standard idiom.
- (1500s–1600s) - Figurative "to falter, hesitate" in speech and action — "stumble at a word", "stumble over an argument"; the word gains a mental range beside the bodily one.
- (1700s–1800s) - "Stumble upon / on / across": to find by chance — the clumsy step is reinterpreted as serendipity; accident becomes discovery, and "stumbling-block" passes into secular idiom for any obstacle.
- (2000s) - The internet age: StumbleUpon (2001–2018) makes the word a brand for pleasant accidental finds; news English uses "stumble" of markets, campaigns and governments losing their footing — seven centuries after the Vikings, the word still means the fall that is not quite a fall.
