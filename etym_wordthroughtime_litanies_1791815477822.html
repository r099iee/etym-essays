- 13th c. — Middle English letanie names the Latin-rite petitionary prayer and the processions in which it was chanted; a technical, clerical word.
- 14th–15th c. — The plural litanies covers the set forms appointed for Rogationtide, ordinations and the dying; the word is familiar to every parishioner but remains liturgical.
- 1544–1549 — Cranmer’s English Litany and the Book of Common Prayer give the word a confessional edge: to have or omit the litany becomes a marker of reformed worship.
- 17th–18th c. — “Litany” begins to be applied outside the church to any long recitation, especially of woes; the figurative use is still occasional and literary.
- 19th c. — The figurative “litany of complaints/woes” establishes itself in prose and journalism, helped by the Victorian taste for enumeration and social reportage.
- 20th–21st c. — The figurative sense becomes dominant in general English, while the liturgical sense survives chiefly among churchgoers; the word now often signals weariness, accusation or bureaucratic repetition.
