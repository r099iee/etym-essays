1. Origins: clothing as plunder (Proto-Indo-European to early medieval). The word family that produced robe begins not with garments but with violence. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *reup- meant “to break, tear, or snatch,” and from it descended the Proto-Germanic verb and noun for seizing the goods of the defeated: *reufaną “to tear, break” and *raubō “booty, spoils.” In early medieval warfare — where textiles were scarce, costly, and highly portable — the most desirable plunder was clothing: a dead or captured enemy’s fine mantle was worth more than almost anything else a warrior could carry. The same root gives English rob, robber, and robbery, and it is this semantic world — the world of Germanic raiding — that lies behind the innocent modern word for a judge’s gown or a bathrobe.

2. From booty to garment: the Frankish and Old French stage (5th–12th c.). The shift by which “spoils” became “garment” happened inside continental Germanic, in the Frankish speech of the Merovingian and Carolingian worlds. Frankish *rauba evidently narrowed to mean specifically the desirable outer garments stripped from the defeated, and then, by a further natural step, any fine garment worth wearing. When French developed from the Romance speech of Gaul under Frankish influence, it borrowed the word: Old French robe is attested with the plain meaning “garment, clothing” from the ninth and tenth centuries onward. Crucially, the French descendant kept the “fine outer garment” sense — the robe of a lord, prelate, or lady — and French courtly fashion soon made robe the leading term for the long, richly cut garments that marked noble status in the later Middle Ages. The whole of European wardrobe vocabulary was reshaped by this French dominance: where English had used native hakele, rugg, or weeds for loose garments, the imported robe took over the most prestigious register.

3. English adoption and the semantic rise of the official robe (13th–16th c.). English borrowed the word in the thirteenth century, in the wake of the Norman Conquest, at first as a general but glamorous term for a fine outer garment. Its rise to ceremonial prominence was institutional. Medieval universities (Bologna, Paris, then Oxford and Cambridge) made long gowns the uniform of scholars and masters; the Church clothed its clergy in the alb, stole, and cope; and law courts — above all the English Inns of Court — developed the black gown and wig tradition that still hedges the bench. By the sixteenth century the robe was the visual signature of every office that claimed dignity: king, bishop, judge, professor, alderman. The metonymic step followed naturally: in French, gens de robe (“men of the gown”) named the legal-administrative nobility; in English, the long robe named the legal profession itself. A garment had become an institution.

4. The literary robe: Reformation, Renaissance, and Romanticism (16th–19th c.). English literature took up the word with enthusiasm, because its double life — concrete garment and metaphor of envelopment — suited poetry. Elizabethan and Jacobean drama dressed kings and ghosts in robes; Milton’s verse used robed divinity and robed night as figures of majesty and darkness; and the eighteenth-century oratorical style made “robed in” a standard formula for anything that clothed an abstraction in dignity. The Romantic poets inherited this richness. Wordsworth’s line in The Prelude — the source context of this card — speaks of clothing a “renovated spirit” in a priestly robe: poetic numbers themselves are imagined as a vestment that invests the renewed soul for “holy services.” Here the ancient path of the word is complete: what began as the spoils torn from the slain has become the sacred garment in which inspiration wraps the spirit.

5. The modern bifurcation (20th–21st c.). The twentieth century drove the word in two opposite directions at once. On the ceremonial side, robes retained their full institutional weight — judicial, academic, ecclesiastical, and civic — and the verb robed thrives in journalism (“robed in secrecy,” “robed in state”). On the domestic side, North American English quietly reassigned the unqualified noun to the soft garment worn after bathing, so that a sentence like “he put on his robe” is, to an American ear, entirely homely. British English preserves more of the old gravity (“dressing gown” for the bathrobe; robe still ceremonial), a dialectal division that itself illustrates how a word’s social register can fork within a single language. Contemporary usage therefore spans the widest possible spectrum: coronation robes of ermine, the black gowns of the Supreme Court, the white alb of a priest, and the terrycloth bathrobe of a hotel bathroom all wear the same etymological garment, whose underlying meaning has remained constant since the seventh century: a loose garment that wraps the whole body.
