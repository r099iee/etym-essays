- 14th–15th c. — Middle English pounce was an artisan's craft noun denoting a pointed stamp or punch used by metalworkers, tailors, and engravers to indent, perforate, or prick patterns onto metal, leather, and vellum.
- 15th–16th c. — In the elite language of falconry and hawking, pounce became the technical anatomical term for the talon or claw of a bird of prey (because a hawk's talons puncture quarry like punches).
- Late 16th c. (Elizabethan era) — Shifted dynamically from the hawk's claw into a violent verb: to pounce meant to swoop down and strike prey with the talons (Spenser, Shakespeare).
- 17th–18th c. — Broadened beyond raptors to describe predatory cats (leopards, lions, domestic felines) springing upon quarry, and extended into dramatic, predatory human ambushes.
- 19th c. — Developed pervasive psychological, rhetorical, and economic figurative senses: eagerly leaping upon an opponent's argumentative error, pouncing on an economic bargain, or capitalizing on an unexpected opening.
- 20th–21st c. — Fixed in modern vernacular as an expressive action verb of sudden physical ambush or rapid opportunistic reaction in debate, corporate competition, journalism, and everyday conversation.
