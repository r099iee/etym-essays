The word epoch travels from a Greek verb meaning to hold to a modern noun meaning an age — a journey from stopping to spanning. Its root is Proto-Indo-European *segh- meaning to hold, hold in, or have power, which yields Greek ekhein (to have, hold), Sanskrit sah- (to overpower), Germanic *sigiz- (victory, that which holds), and English scheme distantly. Prefixed with epi- (upon), Greek formed epokhe: a holding-back, check, cessation — the moment when motion is held.

Hellenistic astronomers used epokhe for the fixed reference point from which planetary positions are reckoned — the instant at which reckoning is held still. This technical sense passed into Medieval Latin as epocha (starting date of an era, e.g., Diocletian epoch, Christian epoch) and into Scaligerian chronology, the great Renaissance science of dating. French epoque (17th century) broadened it from the datum to the whole age that follows the datum, and English borrowed both senses together around 1615–1620.

  • Chronological datum (17th c.): Early English epoch or epocha meant the zero-point: the epoch of the Olympiads, the epoch of Nabonassar. Treatises list epochs as tables of starting dates. Pronunciation kept Greek ch as /k/.
  • Historical age (18th c.): Enlightenment historians — Voltaire, Gibbon, Scottish stadial theorists — use epoch for the great stages of civilization: epoch of barbarism, epoch of commerce. The source sentence belongs to this tradition: an epoch of oscillation as a stadial transition.
  • Geological formalization (19th c.): Lyell and followers divide Tertiary time into Eocene, Miocene, Pliocene epochs (1833). Here epoch becomes a precise rank in the stratigraphic hierarchy (eon > era > period > epoch > age), defined by fossils and later by absolute dates. The -cene names (recent, less recent) remain textbook vocabulary.
  • Astronomical retention: While historians shifted to span, astronomers kept the point sense: epoch J2000.0, epoch of osculation. Catalogues specify equinox and epoch separately — a living fossil of the Greek original.
  • Computing reinvention (20th c.): The Unix epoch (1970-01-01T00:00:00Z) and similar zero-dates revive the datum sense for digital time-counting, exposing programmers to a word otherwise literary. Epoch time, epoch milliseconds are everyday backend terms.

Spelling settled as epoch after competing epocha, epo que, epock; stress is initial in RP /ˈiːpɒk/. Semantically the drift from held instant to held age is complete and stable: to fix a beginning is to create the period it begins. That is why the word feels both precise and grand — a surveyor's peg that became a monument.

Its endurance lies in this double utility: historians need a word grander than period for ages that feel fateful, while scientists need a fixed rank and a fixed datum for measurement. Epoch uniquely supplies both, which is why a newspaper can call an election epoch-making in the morning and a programmer can store epoch milliseconds in the afternoon without either sensing a clash.
