TITHING names the act, institution, or system of paying one tenth (10%) of one's produce, income, or property as a religious obligation, charitable contribution, or fiscal levy, and — in the history of English law — a specific Anglo-Saxon administrative unit of ten householders bound together by mutual legal responsibility. CORE SENSE: At its heart, tithing is the practice of giving a tenth. The fraction is the whole point: the word is the noun built on the ordinal numeral "tenth," and it refers both to the tenth part itself and to the act of setting that part aside. In the medieval Western Church the tithe was a compulsory payment of one tenth of the annual agricultural produce — grain, wool, livestock, and the increase of all flocks and herds — owed to the parish. It was levied as a tax in kind, stored in the great tithe barn, and distributed between the incumbent clergy and the relief of the poor, and its legal status was guaranteed by a web of canon law, royal writ, and manorial custom. RELIGIOUS SENSE: In Christian usage, tithing descends from the biblical requirement (Leviticus 27:30, Deuteronomy 14:22, Malachi 3:10) that the faithful offer a tenth of harvest and herd to God. The practice, inherited from ancient Israel and given legal force by church councils and Carolingian legislation from the eighth century onward, made the tithe the fiscal backbone of the medieval parish. In England the tenth of all produce remained a legal obligation until the Tithe Commutation Act of 1836 converted payment in kind into money rents charged to the landowners. In modern religious and charitable contexts "tithing" denotes voluntary proportional giving, conventionally ten percent of income, and it remains a fixed discipline in some denominations — above all in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, where the word names the required contribution of a tenth of one's annual income. MUNICIPAL / LEGAL-HISTORICAL SENSE: Alongside the tax, tithing designated, in Anglo-Saxon and early Norman law, a unit of ten households (traditionally the smallest subdivision of the hundred) whose members stood surety for one another. Each tithing elected its tithingman (headborough or borsholder), and under the frankpledge system every free man over twelve was enrolled in a tithing, making the group answerable for producing its members before justice. The same word therefore carried two distinct tenth-based meanings in medieval England — a tenth of produce and a group of ten men — both anchored in the numeral itself. MODERN AND TRANSFERRED SENSES:
  • Voluntary charity: "tithing" one's time or money to a church, charity, or cause, with the strict 10% rule loosened into any generous proportion.
  • Secular quota: a fixed tenth portion of anything, and by extension any mandatory proportional contribution to an organization.
  • Historical administration: the ten-household unit, its court (the smallest English local court), and the office of the tithingman, studied in constitutional and legal history.
  • Mormon usage: Latter-day Saint tithing as a literal 10% of income, the best-known living use of the term.
  • Figurative: any exacting "tax" imposed by circumstances, e.g. "the tithing of his time," a rare but attested extension.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE: Every sense flows from the ordinal numeral "tenth." Old English tēoþa "tenth" produced tēoþung / tēoþing, used both for the tenth-part payment and the ten-man grouping; the mathematical fraction 1/10 anchored the religious duty of surrendering a tenth of produce and the administrative habit of counting householders in tens. Tithing thus unites arithmetic with institutional history, and its modern survival is chiefly in religion, charity, and legal historiography.
