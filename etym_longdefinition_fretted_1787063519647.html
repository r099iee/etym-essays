fretted is the past tense and past participle of the verb fret, a word of remarkable depth: in modern English its dominant sense is psychological — to worry, to be anxious — but its older senses are fiercely physical — to gnaw, to corrode, to chafe — and it has an entire second life as a technical and decorative term in woodwork, music, and heraldry. In the source text (Tolkien's The Hobbit), the barrels "creaked and fretted" — wood under strain groaning and complaining — an animate metaphor that draws on the word's oldest physical energy.

PRIMARY MODERN SENSE — WORRY AND ANXIETY

  • To be constantly or needlessly anxious: "don't fret, everything will be fine", "she fretted about the children all night".
  • To worry in a fussy, restless, repetitive way — fretting is smaller and more nibbling than agonising; it is the worry that gnaws.
  • Noun and adjective: fret = a state of anxiety ("in a fret"); fretful = peevish, restless, anxious (especially of babies and invalids).
  • The bridge from the physical senses: what "gnaws" at the body comes to "gnaw at" the mind — worry is internal corrosion.

SECONDARY SENSE — GNAWING, CORROSION, CHAFING (older, literary, technical)

  • To eat into, corrode, or wear away gradually: "rust frets iron", "water frets the stone", "the rope fretted against the rock".
  • To chafe or rub sore: "the collar fretted his neck"; a "fretted" surface is one worn by rubbing.
  • Of cloth or metal: to fray, unravel, or erode at the edges.
  • This is the oldest English sense (from Old English fretan "to devour"), preserved in literary and technical registers long after the worry-sense took over.
  • Bridge: corrosion is a kind of eating; the same verb that describes a rat eating a sack also describes rust eating iron; from external eating to internal anxiety is a short metaphorical step.

TERTIARY SENSE — CREAKING AND COMPLAINING (Tolkien's usage)

  • Of inanimate things — timbers, barrels, cart-wheels, ships — to creak, groan, strain, and complain under pressure: "the barrels creaked and fretted".
  • This is fretful behaviour transferred to objects: the wood is imagined as fretful, restless, anxious under the strain of the river.
  • Tolkien's sentence makes the barrels almost alive: they are lashed together, and they creak and fret like fretful captives of the current.
  • The bridge: "fret" as "to be restless and uneasy" applied to groaning wood — sound and emotion fused.

THE SEPARATE HOMONYM — DECORATIVE FRETWORK AND GUITAR FRETS

  • Fret (noun/verb, from Old French frete "interlaced lattice, trellis"): a decorative pattern of interlaced lines — "Greek key fret", "fretwork", "fretted ceiling" (a ceiling decorated with interlaced carving).
  • Fret (noun, from the same Old French family via the metal band or ferrule): the raised ridges on the neck of a guitar or other stringed instrument that divide the fingerboard into semitone intervals; to "fret" a note is to press the string against a fret; "fretted instruments" (guitar, mandolin) are contrasted with "unfretted" ones (violin, cello).
  • Fret (heraldry and architecture): interlaced borders and the "fret" charge of heraldic blazon — a lattice of interlaced pieces.
  • These senses are etymologically unrelated to the worry/gnaw verb (see etymology) but share its form; speakers routinely pun on the two, and "fretted" in a craftsman's mouth means "decorated with interlacing", while in a mother's mouth it means "anxious".

FURTHER TECHNICAL SENSES

  • Of tides and waterways: a "fret" (chiefly Scottish/nautical) is a mist, a current, or a narrow strait (e.g. the Fret of Dover) — from Old Norse, yet another homonym.
  • In dyeing and metallurgy, "fretted" can describe a surface eaten or pitted by acid — the corrosion sense made industrial.
  • In music technology, "fretted" describes the buzzing or rattling of a string against a fret when the set-up is wrong — "the low E frets out on the fifth fret" — a technical usage in which the instrument "worries" audibly.

REGISTER AND STYLE

  • Modern spoken English: fret is mild-to-moderate worry, slightly old-fashioned, warm and homely ("don't fret, Mum").
  • Literary English: fret retains its physical edge — "fretted by the wind", "fretted with cares" — and poets love its double nature (gnawing and worrying in one word).
  • Technical English: fretwork, fretted instruments, fret-saw, fretted ceiling — precise and decorative.
  • Tolkien's usage is a deliberate archaism-with-life: the barrels do not merely creak, they fret — they worry audibly, like fretful prisoners of the stream.
