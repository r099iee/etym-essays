Woden is the Old English name of the chief god of the Germanic pantheon, and its biography is really the biography of a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to blow, to inspire, to excite." The reconstructed root *weh₂t- describes the action of a wind or breath stirring someone up — and from that image of being blown upon, both prophecy and madness were born. The root's oldest certain reflexes split the Indo-European world into two great branches. In the Italic and Celtic worlds it produced Latin vātēs and Gaulish *uātis, words for a "seer, prophet, soothsayer, or inspired poet"; Latin vātēs even supplied English with the learned words vates, vatic, and vaticinate, "to prophesy." In the Germanic world the same root produced the adjective *wōdaz, "mad, furious, possessed," a word for the frenzy of the battle-mad or the divinely inspired. From *wōdaz each early Germanic language shaped its own word. Gothic, the oldest attested Germanic language, had wōþs, "mad, possessed," in Wulfila's fourth-century Bible translation. Old Norse had óðr, "furious, raging," and — crucially — also Óðr, the name of an independent god of inspiration and passion; the Norse themselves formed the god-name Óðinn, "the frenzied or exalted one," from the same stem, the word that modern English borrows as Odin. Old High German had wuot, "madness, rage, frenzy," with the god appearing as Wuotan, revived in modern German as Wotan largely through Richard Wagner's nineteenth-century operas. Old English had the adjective wōd, "mad," which survived into Middle and early Modern English as wod or wood, "insane, raging" — a sense now obsolete — and from this same adjective the god's name Wōden was formed: literally "the frenzied one." As the Anglo-Saxon chief deity, Woden presided over war, poetry, wisdom, magic, runes, and the dead. Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies traced descent from him, and the kings of Wessex claimed him as their ancestor — an important detail for the passage under study, since it is precisely a Wessex king Macaulay imagines performing rites in Woden's temple. The most durable trace of his worship is the weekday. The Latin week named the fourth day diēs Mercuriī, "Mercury's day," because Roman observers, operating on the principle called the interpretatio romana, equated the Germanic god of speed, cleverness, and communication with the messenger-god Mercury. The Germanic peoples simply substituted their own god of the same profile: *Wōdanas dagaz, "Woden's day," which in Old English was Wōdnesdæg, in Middle English Wednesdai, and in modern English Wednesday. Thus the English name Woden survived the conversion to Christianity itself — hidden, almost unrecognized, in the middle of the week. When Christianization reached Anglo-Saxon England, the god was not eliminated so much as demoted; the fuller mythology was preserved in the Norse texts, and the form Odin (from Old Norse Óðinn) was reintroduced into English only in the nineteenth century, so that modern readers meet "Odin" in the Eddas and popular culture while "Woden" remains the Anglo-Saxon name. In Macaulay's sentence the word is deliberately archaic and native: the kings of Wessex and Mercia "performing savage rites in the temples of Thor and Woden" stand for the last holdout of pagan Germanic religion against the literate Christianity of the converted continental courts. The name carries in one word the entire story of the root *weh₂t-: divine breath, frenzy, prophecy, and the haunting of our calendar. KEY STAGES IN ONE LINE
  • Sound shift: PIE *weh₂t- -> Germanic *wōdaz shows laryngeal loss and lengthened grade; the cluster -dn- in Wōdnesdæg preserved the god's stem in the weekday compound.
  • Semantic drift: 'blown upon, inspired' -> 'mad, furious, possessed' -> 'the frenzied god' -> the god of wisdom, war, poetry, and the dead; the same root gives Latin vātēs 'seer'.
  • Socio-cultural factors: interpretatio romana equated the god with Mercury, which is why Wednesday replaced Mercury's day; Christianization demoted him to a heathen idol while preserving his name in the weekday.
  • Literary attestations: Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies (8th-9th c.) trace Wessex kings to Woden; Bede and the Old English martyrologies mention him; Wagner's Wotan (19th c.) and Macaulay's 'temples of Thor and Woden' fix the modern resonance.
