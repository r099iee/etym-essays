Sayest is the archaic second-person singular present of the verb say, meaning you say or thou sayest: the form that Early Modern English required whenever the subject was the singular familiar pronoun thou. Where modern English says you say for every subject under the sun, Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and Bunyan carefully distinguished I say, thou sayest, he sayeth, and sayest is the middle member of that lost trio, instantly recognisable by its -est ending, the grammatical fingerprint of thou.

In the source sentence — How sayest thou, my sister — the word carries its full period flavour: a wonder-tale dialogue rendered in consciously antique diction, where a hero addresses his sister with the intimate singular pronoun and its matching verb. The speaker is not asking for information in a modern way but performing olden-time speech, and sayest does half the work of that performance, turning a plain question into a line from a ballad or a Bible story.

Core meaning in detail: Sayest adds nothing to the meaning of say beyond person and number: it asserts, utters, declares, or opines, exactly as say does, but marks the sayer as thou. Its pronunciation in period was two syllables, roughly say-est, though poets freely compressed it to one when metre demanded. The form belongs to the southern and Midland dialects that became standard; northern England preferred says or sayes even with thou, a reminder that -est was a choice, not a law of nature.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • You say, thou sayest (primary grammatical sense, Old English to 17th c.): the ordinary second-person singular of say in all its meanings — utter, tell, recite, allege, suppose. The bridge is pure inflection: stem say- plus the thou-marker -est, doing the work modern English leaves to the pronoun.
  • Biblical solemnity (stylistic sense, 1611 onward): after thou died in speech, sayest survived frozen in the King James Bible — what sayest thou of thyself — so that the form itself now sounds divine, oracular, and authoritative. The bridge is liturgical preservation: millions heard it weekly for three centuries after it left the street.
  • Poetic archaism (literary sense, 18th–19th c.): poets from Milton's heirs to Keats deploy sayest to dignify apostrophe and dialogue, as in hymns and ballads. The bridge is deliberate time-travel: the dead ending lends the verse the gravity of scripture.
  • Mock-solemn irony (modern humorous sense): as in what sayest thou, pizza or curry, where friends play at prophets over trivial choices. The bridge is bathos: the most solemn verb-form in the language applied to the least solemn decisions.
  • Grammatical specimen (metalinguistic sense): in classrooms sayest exemplifies the lost thou-conjugation alongside hast, doth, wilt, the paradigm every student of Early Modern English must master. The bridge is pedagogy: the form teaches a whole dead system in six letters.

Register and connotation: sayest is never neutral. In scripture it is awesome, in poetry elevated, in ordinary modern mouths jocular or pretentious — there is no context left where it is simply the unmarked way to speak. To use it sincerely is to preach or to act; to use it lightly is to wink. Its connotation is therefore entirely borrowed glory: three hundred years of Bibles, stage Hamlets, wedding vows, and Christmas carols condensed into a single verb ending.
