SANCTITY denotes the state or quality of being holy, sacred, or inviolable: the condition of a person, thing, or principle that is set apart as sacrosanct, too pure, too precious, or too fundamental to be violated, defiled, or compromised. The word rests on the Latin adjective sanctus, 'consecrated, holy', but in modern English it has travelled far from the altar. In the sentence behind this card - 'Instead of safeguarding the sanctity of my family!' - sanctity names the moral claim that the family is an inviolable institution: the speaker invokes its sacred character as the very ground of outrage, so that 'sanctity' is doing ethical rather than narrowly religious work. The word is never merely descriptive; it is an appeal to absolute value, a demand that something be treated as untouchable.

CORE SENSES
  • Religious holiness: the state of being consecrated, hallowed, or dedicated to God - the sanctity of a temple, a shrine, a relic, the sabbath, or the divine name. This is the primary and historically earliest sense, and it underlies all the others.
  • Moral inviolability: the quality of being so pure, so right, or so fundamental that it must never be attacked or defiled - the sanctity of human life, of marriage, of the person, of the conscience.
  • Legal and political inviolability: the principle that certain obligations, institutions, or boundaries are absolute - the sanctity of contracts, of treaties, of property, of borders, of diplomatic immunity.
  • Figurative and ironic extension: the exaggerated, quasi-religious seriousness with which a mundane practice is treated - the sanctity of a deadline, of a coffee ritual, of office hours - frequently used with gentle satire when the 'sacredness' is trivial or self-serving.
POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY MODERN MEANINGS IN DETAIL
  • Bioethics - 'the sanctity of life': the principle that human life possesses inherent, unconditional value and must not be deliberately ended. Central to debates on abortion, euthanasia, and capital punishment, the phrase imports a religious absolute into secular law and medicine.
  • Contract and commercial law - 'the sanctity of contract': the doctrine that agreements freely made must be honoured as made and not lightly rewritten by courts. Here the word is wholly secular yet retains its inviolability: the contract is 'holy' in being untouchable.
  • Diplomacy - 'the sanctity of borders' and 'of sovereignty': the principle that a state's territory and self-rule are inviolable, a modern transposition of the older sanctity of boundaries.
  • Marriage and domestic life - 'the sanctity of marriage' and 'of the home': the claim that the marital bond and the household are sacred, protected from intrusion, and above utilitarian bargaining.
  • Tradition and ritual - the sanctity of custom, burial, oaths, and blood: the residue of the word's religious origin in every context where something is held to be 'not to be touched'.
HISTORICAL AND SEMANTIC BRIDGE
The bridge from altar to contract is ancient and structural, not a modern invention. Roman religion used sacer 'sacred' and sancire 'to consecrate, make binding' of altars, temples, and the gods, and the participle sanctus described what ritual had made untouchable. At the same time, Roman law used sancire for the solemn ratification of statutes and treaties, so that the word family fused the religious and the legal from the very beginning: what is sanctified is both holy and legally unassailable. Latin sanctitas, 'holiness, sacredness, inviolability', distilled both threads, and early Christian Latin (the Vulgate and the Fathers) took the word over for the saints and for the purity of the redeemed soul. When the noun entered Middle English in the fourteenth century it arrived with the whole double inheritance, religious and legal, and every modern use - sanctity of life, of contract, of marriage - is a fresh deployment of that Roman fusion.

REGISTER AND USAGE
  • Sanctity is formal and elevated, and it attaches chiefly to abstractions rather than to persons; one does not normally call a person 'sanctity' (that personal sense lives in holiness, saintliness, or sainthood), but one speaks of the sanctity of whatever is to be protected.
  • The family shows a dark mirror in sanctimony and sanctimonious, from Latin sanctimonia 'sanctity, purity': when the claim to holiness is false or self-righteous, the same root turns mocking.
  • Sacrosanct ('consecrated and inviolable') is the emphatic twin of sanctity, and sanctify is the verb from which the whole family is governed.
  • Register-wise, sanctity outranks holiness in formality and abstraction; it is the word of law, bioethics, and high moral rhetoric, while its meaning as 'the state of being sacred' remains available at any time to be aimed back at the divine.
