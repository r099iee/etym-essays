- 1650s - the frequentative verb draggle (from drag) first appears: 'to trail and soil in water and mud' - a homely, physical word for the wet outdoors
- 17th-18th centuries - the participle draggled settles as an adjective: wet, dirty, and bedraggled from trailing - used of wet clothes, fowls, and travellers caught in the rain
- 19th century - Victorian novelists (Kingsley, Dickens, the Brontes) make draggled a stock word of pity: poverty, exposure, and hardship leave figures 'draggled' and 'bedraggled'; the wet straw in a sweep's draggled beard in The Water-Babies (1863) is a classic use
- 20th century onward - the prefixless draggled retreats before bedraggled, surviving in literary English; its senses widen from purely physical filth to any limp, spiritless, or defeated state - a draggled plant, a draggled mood, a draggled reputation
