CHAIN FOR THE FIRST CONSTITUENT, HAND (2 attested stages; PIE stage unknown)

  1. Proto-Germanic *handuz "hand" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE; attested in Gothic handus, Old Norse hǫnd). No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon has been established for this word; it is a Germanic isolate whose deeper origin is unknown. Various proposals (a connection to a base meaning "to seize or grasp," or to a root meaning "to point") have been offered, but none commands scholarly consensus.
  2. Old English hand "hand" (attested from the 8th century; identical form in Modern English). The extended sense "at hand, near, available" is ancient and lies at the heart of the compound.

CHAIN FOR THE SECOND CONSTITUENT, MAIDEN (2 attested/reconstructed stages; PIE base debated)

  1. Proto-Germanic *magad- "maiden, unmarried girl, virgin" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE; attested in Gothic magaþs "girl, virgin," Old Norse mǫgr "son" from the same family, Old High German magad). The deeper PIE base is debated: the traditional proposal connects it with the Germanic base *mag- "power, ability, to be able" (cf. English may, might; Gothic magan "to be able"), on the theory that a maiden is a girl grown to full power, nubile and capable; but this is speculative and not universally accepted.
  2. Proto-West-Germanic *magadīn "little girl, young maiden," a diminutive formation in *-īn. From this comes Old English mægden "young girl, virgin, maiden" (attested from the 8th century), Middle English mayden "maiden," and Modern English maiden.

COMPOUND FORMATION

  1. Middle English hand + mayde / mayden combined in the 14th century as handmayde, handmayden, and handmaid, "a female servant who waits at hand, a maidservant," first appearing in English biblical and devotional texts. The form handmaiden is the standard modern spelling.

→ Modern English HANDMAIDEN (the exact form of this card).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The origin of Proto-Germanic *handuz is genuinely unknown; no PIE root commands scholarly consensus, so the chain begins honestly at the Germanic stage.
  • The etymology of *magad- is uncertain; the connection to a root meaning "power / be able" (via the idea of nubility or capability) is a traditional but debated proposal, and some scholars treat the word as an isolated Germanic formation.
