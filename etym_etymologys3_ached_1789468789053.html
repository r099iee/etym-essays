The verb ache and its past inflected form ached possess an ancient Germanic heritage rooted in Proto-Indo-European somatic vocabulary, while exhibiting one of the most celebrated orthographic and phonological histories in the English language.

The evolutionary trajectory unfolds across several major epochs:

  • Proto-Indo-European & Germanic Origins: Historical linguists trace the word to the Proto-Germanic strong verb *akanan ("to ache, suffer pain"), which likely derives from an expressive, onomatopoeic Proto-Indo-European root *ag- or *ak- denoting vocalized cries of bodily torment or piercing discomfort. In Germanic, this formed a Class VI strong verb that expressed sustained physical distress.
  • Old English Strong Conjugation: In Old English, the verb appeared as acan, inflecting strongly with preterite ōc and past participle acen (cognate with Middle Dutch aken). It was employed in Anglo-Saxon medical texts (such as the Bald's Leechbook) to denote continuous, deep-seated internal ailments—headaches, toothaches, and visceral afflictions—standing in clear contrast to acute lacerations or puncture wounds.
  • Middle English Phonological Split (The Noun/Verb Divergence): During the Middle English period, regular phonological sound changes created a distinct split between the noun and the verb:
    • The verb aken maintained the velar stop /k/ (spelled ake), retaining its ancestral consonant.
    • The derived noun developed palatalization before front vowels, yielding ache, pronounced /eɪtʃ/ (rhyming with batch or speech). This parallel pair mirrored other Old English phonological alternates such as speak / speech, bake / batch, and break / breach.
  • The Renaissance Hellenizing Re-spelling: In the late 16th and early 17th centuries, Renaissance humanists and printers mistakenly assumed that English ake / ache was derived from the Ancient Greek noun ἄχος (achos, "grief, pain, distress"). Driven by this false etymological assumption, printers began applying the Greek-style ch digraph to both the noun and the verb. During Shakespeare's era, the noun was still pronounced /eɪtʃ/ (as in the famous pun in Much Ado About Nothing: "an you be not turned Turk, there's no more sailing by the star... Beatrice: By my troth, I am exceeding ill. Hey-ho! Margaret: For a hawk, a horse, or a husband? Beatrice: For the letter that begins them all, H [ache]"), while the verb was pronounced /eɪk/.
  • Johnson's Standardization & Modern Synthesis: In the mid-18th century, Dr. Samuel Johnson in his landmark Dictionary of the English Language (1755) codified the spelling ache for both parts of speech based on the erroneous Greek association, but the pronunciation of the verb /eɪk/ eventually swallowed the noun's /eɪtʃ/ sound entirely. Concurrently, the verb completed its transition to weak inflection (ached rather than oke). John Keats famously immortalized this synthesis in his 1819 Ode to a Nightingale ("My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains / My sense"), firmly wedding the somatic weight of Germanic pain with sublime emotional longing.
