The history of naught is the history of English negation doubling down for emphasis. Old English already had plain negatives, but speakers loved to reinforce them, and nāwiht — literally not plus anything — was the strongest way to say nothing whatsoever. It combined the negative particle ne, heir of Proto-Indo-European *ne, with āwiht, itself a compound of ā meaning ever and wiht meaning thing or creature, so the whole word unpacks as not ever a thing.

Through Middle English the vowels levelled and the spellings naught and nought circulated interchangeably for the pronoun nothing, the noun cipher, and the adjective worthless. Chaucer uses it naturally, devotional writers moralise with it, and by the fifteenth century account-keepers were writing naughts for zero figures, since an empty counting place and an empty world took the same name. The arrival of Hindu-Arabic numerals made the cipher sense newly practical: the little O that held a place in 101 was a visible naught.

The word reached its cultural peak in the age of Shakespeare and the King James Bible. Shakespeare puns and moralises on it across the plays — worthless fellows are naughty, scorned warnings are set at naught — and the translators of 1611 made come to naught and set at naught carry the cadence of prophecy. From this pulpit and stage authority the idioms passed into the permanent stock of English proverb, outliving the everyday pronoun that bore them.

Sound and spelling shifts:

  • Contraction and weakening: unstressed nāwiht lost its middle glide to become naught, while the everyday adverbial use wore further down to nat and finally not, leaving modern English with a grand doublet — solemn naught beside workhorse not.
  • Spelling split: printers and schoolmasters gradually assigned naught to the literary nothing and nought to the mathematical zero, though the boundary was porous for centuries and dictionaries still cross-refer them.
  • Semantic narrowing: as nothing (no plus thing, a parallel but separate compound) conquered neutral prose in the 17th–18th centuries, naught retreated uphill into solemn, playful, and formulaic uses rather than dying outright.
  • Nursery survival: Victorian game books fixed noughts and crosses as the British name for tic-tac-toe, giving the cipher sense a second life wherever children chant the title.

Literary attestations keep the word audible: Miltonic nothingness, Dickensian scolds calling children naughty in the softened descendant sense, and twentieth-century fantasy — Tolkien foremost — deploying naught to age dialogue by centuries in a single syllable. The Hawk of the source sentence belongs to that tradition: a talking bird that says naught instead of nothing signals fable at once. What began as reinforced grammar thus ends as stylistic colour, a zero that signifies antiquity itself.
