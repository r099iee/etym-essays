- 9th-10th c. - cuppe is borrowed into Old English as a plain vessel word, associated with monastic refectories, markets and the hall; it feels useful, borrowed and slightly learned.
- 11th-13th c. - Middle English cuppe becomes the ordinary word for a drinking vessel in every household; it is neutral, domestic and unremarkable.
- 14th-15th c. - Religious and poetic uses deepen: the cup of sorrow, the cup of salvation, "in one's cups" for drunkenness; the word acquires a moral and sacramental charge.
- 16th-17th c. - The Reformation and the Authorised Version fix the cup in Protestant devotion; Shakespeare and the poets exploit its symbolic range, while silver cups become marks of status.
- 18th-19th c. - The trophy cup and the loving-cup flourish; "a cup of tea" becomes a national ritual, and the word grows cosy, domestic and slightly sentimental.
- 20th c. - Sport takes over the public sense (World Cup, FA Cup), while bra cups and protective cups extend the word to anatomy and kit; the measuring cup standardises home baking.
- 21st c. - "Cup" is split between stadium spectacle, specialty coffee, reusable-cup ethics and the idiom "not my cup of tea"; it feels at once global, domestic and environmentally charged.
