prejudices carries a Roman courtroom into modern morals. Latin praeiudicium joined prae- (before) to iudicium (judgment, from ius, right, + dicere, to say: a saying of what is right). In procedure it meant a preliminary inquiry or sentence that pre-judged the main case, hence a precedent, and by natural extension the harm such anticipation could do a party. Old French prejudice (13th century) kept both threads — opinion beforehand and damage — and Middle English borrowed both together: Chaucer and Gower know prejudice as harm (to the prejudice of) and as prior judgment.

Each half has deep roots. Prae- continues PIE *per- (before, through, forward), seen in Greek pro, Sanskrit pra, English for, fore, first. Ius (right, law, broth? No — oath, justice) continues PIE *yewes- (customary law, what is right), seen in Sanskrit yos (welfare), Old Irish huisse? The details are debated but the law-word is secure (de Vaan). Dicere continues PIE *deyk- (show, point out, say solemnly), seen in Greek deiknumi (show), Sanskrit disati (points), English token, teach, dictate. Latin fused law-speaking into iudex (judge, he who says the right) and iudicium (his saying). Phonologically praeiudicium /praejuːˈdiki.um/ loses its diphthong in Late Latin to /prejuditʃum/, Old French palatalises -ci- to /s/ then /z/? Actually to /dʒ/: prejudice /predʒyˈdis/, Middle English anglicises to /ˈprɛdʒʊdɪs/ with stress retracted and final -ce /s/.

  • Legal to moral transfer (14th-17th c.): lawyers’ without prejudice, to the prejudice of preserves harm-sense intact. Moralists transfer pre-judging from court to mind: Bacon’s Idols (1620) are prejudices of tribe, cave, market and theatre; Locke’s Essay warns against received prejudices. The plural prejudices becomes catalogue of mental furniture, countable and confessable.
  • Enlightenment attack and Romantic defence: Voltaire, Hume and Kant make préjugés / prejudices the enemy of reason; Burke’s Reflections (1790) retorts that prejudice is latent wisdom, habit made virtue, affection that reasons faster than logic. Scott, Coleridge and then Ruskin inherit Burke: Tory prejudices are loves before proofs — for kings, church, home. Ruskin’s sentence performs Burke’s move while conceding the Liberal word: call them prejudices if you must; I cannot unlove them, though I cannot pedigree them.
  • Modern condemnation and science (19th c.-present): abolition, feminism and anti-racism fix prejudice as adverse group bias and make unprejudiced the liberal ideal. Psychology operationalises it: Allport’s Nature of Prejudice (1954), implicit-association tests, contact theory. Law keeps prejudicial for publicity that biases juries. The spelling never wavers; the moral charge reverses twice — neutral to vicious to affectionate to vicious again — leaving Ruskin’s tender-ironic plural intelligible only if both histories are heard.
