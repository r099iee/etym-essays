curtness — compound noun: curt + -ness; the single etymological chain runs through curt. Stage 1 — PIE: *sker- "to cut, shear, separate" (one of the most productive cutting-roots in the family; also the source of English shear, share, short, shirt, skirt). Stage 2 — Pre-Latin: *kṛ-tó- "cut off" (the "cut off" participle of the cutting root; zero-grade form). Stage 3 — Latin: curtus "shortened, cut short, docked, mutilated" (attested from the 2nd century BCE; used of lopped trees, docked tails, crops harvested too early). Stage 4 — Old French: court / curt "short" (attested from the 9th–10th centuries). Stage 5 — Anglo-Norman / Middle English: curt "short, brief" (borrowed into Middle English in the 14th century). Stage 6 — English: curt "rudely brief, abrupt in speech" (attested c. 1825; the spatial sense "short" transferred from bodies and objects to words). Stage 7 — English: curt + suffix -ness → curtness "rudely brief or abrupt behaviour" (attested from the 19th century). NOTE ON THE SHORT/SHIRT/SKIRT COUSINS: the same PIE *sker- produced, through the Germanic branch, Proto-Germanic *skurtaz "short," the ancestor of English short, shirt, and skirt (garments "cut" to length); Latin curtus and Germanic short are thus parallel realisations of the same prehistoric cutting-metaphor — to be short is to have been cut. This is a sibling relationship, not part of the direct chain of curtness.
