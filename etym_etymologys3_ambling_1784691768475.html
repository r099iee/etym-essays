The biography of ambling begins in the very architecture of walking. Latin ambulāre, "to walk about", was built on the preposition ambi-, "around, on both sides" — the element behind English ambient, ambidextrous and ambiguous, and the cousin of the Greek amphí of amphitheatre and amphibious — combined with a verbal base that most modern etymologists take from the Indo-European root *h₂el-, "to wander, to roam". The underlying picture is of movement around and through the world: not the headlong dash of a driven beast but motion with room to spare, going about one's business. The older derivation of the base from *h₂eǵ-, "to drive" (the root of Latin agere, Greek ágein and Sanskrit ájati, "he drives"), was long repeated in popular reference works and is still the version found in older dictionaries; it has, however, been abandoned by modern handbooks on phonological and morphological grounds. Neither analysis changes the practical history of the English word, which begins not in classical Latin but in the stables of medieval Europe. The word entered medieval speech as a term of horsemanship. Old French ambler took the general Latin sense "to walk" and narrowed it to a special riding gait: the amble, in which both legs on the same side of the horse move together, producing a smooth four-beat roll rather than the jolting diagonals of the trot. For medieval travellers this was the gait of comfort; an ambling horse carried its rider over long roads without shaking him, and was correspondingly a prized possession. Across the Romance world the same family travelled far — French aller, "to go", Spanish andar and Italian andare all descend, by paths of their own, from the Latin family of ambulāre — while the specifically equestrian descendant came into English. Middle English adopted the verb around 1300 as amblen, and by the age of Chaucer the ambler was a familiar figure on the roads of England, so much a part of the landscape that the noun ambler could stand for a horse trained to the gait. It is worth stressing that the medieval amble was not necessarily slow. Lateral gaits can be surprisingly fast; the medieval ambling horse was often a quick and comfortable traveller. The modern sense "to walk leisurely" is a softening of the word, in which the comfort of the ride came to overshadow its speed. That semantic drift, from smooth-and-comfortable to slow-and-relaxed, is the central event of the word's later history. From the sixteenth century onward, amble and ambling broadened from the stable to the street: a man could amble through a market or a garden, and the verb slipped its technical harness to become a general word for easy, relaxed progress on foot. Key stages and shifts:
  • c. 1300: amblen enters Middle English from Old French as an equestrian term; an ambler is a horse (or rider) moving in the smooth lateral gait.
  • 16th century: the pedestrian sense appears; to amble becomes to walk at leisure, and the comfort of the gait comes to outweigh its speed in the word's meaning.
  • Modern English: both lives of the word survive — the equestrian amble (still used in horsemanship and racing) and the pedestrian amble (everyday, relaxed walking) — while the medical and institutional Latin family (ambulate, ambulatory, ambulance) continues the root in formal registers.
Literary and cultural resonance: the word's equestrian colouring has always given it a touch of gentility. In Shakespeare and his contemporaries, ambling horses and ambling palfreys are figures of comfortable, unadventurous travel; in the context sentence Tolkien draws on exactly this association, letting the farmer amble by on business in a countryside whose gait is relaxed, rhythmic and unhurried. The modern word remains what the medieval one was, a name for movement without strain, and its drift from the stable to the street is a small illustration of how a technical term becomes a domestic one, and how the comfort of the ride survives in the calm of the walk.
