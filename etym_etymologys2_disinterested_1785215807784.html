Three morphemes compose the word; each is traced separately, followed by the full linear chain. Chain 1 — the full word “interested” → “disinterested”: - PIE *h₁es- “to be” (attested verbal root, c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Latin esse “to be” (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) → Latin interesse “to be between, to be among, to differ, to be of importance” (inter + esse; Classical Latin, attested in Cicero) → Latin interest (3rd person singular of interesse) “it matters, it concerns, it is of importance” (Classical Latin; also the source of the Italianate commercial form interesse) → English interest (noun, 15th–16th c., via Old French interest / Medieval Latin interest) “a legal claim or share; concern; advantage; the yield of money on capital” → interested (adjective, 17th c.) “having a stake or concern; biased by personal interest” → French désintéressé (past participle of désintéresser, “to divest of interest”; 17th c.), formed as dis- + intéresser “to interest” → English disinterested (adjective, first recorded 1659) “impartial, free from self-interest” → Modern English DISINTERESTED. Chain 2 — the prefix dis-: - PIE *dwís “twice, in two, asunder” → Latin dis- “apart, asunder, in different directions; not” (prefix, Classical Latin) → English dis- “not, apart, away” (via Old French des- / Latin dis-), as in disinterested. Chain 3 — the element inter-: - PIE *h₁énter “between, inside” (derived from *h₁en “in”) → Latin inter “between, among” (preposition, Classical Latin) → present in Latin interesse and hence in interest, interested, and disinterested. Note on debate: the financial sense of interest (“money paid for the use of money”) developed in medieval Italian commercial circles (interesse) and reached English in the 16th century; the semantic bridge is the notion of “a share or concern,” a sum of money in which one has a stake. The adjective disinterested entered English from French and initially meant “impartial”; the modern “bored” sense is a 20th-century development condemned by prescriptive grammarians and is not part of the historical derivation chain.
