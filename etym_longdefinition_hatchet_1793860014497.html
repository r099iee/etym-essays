A hatchet is a compact, short-handled cutting and chopping tool belonging to the axe family, characterized by a single-handed handle (typically measuring between 10 and 16 inches in length) and a balanced forged steel head featuring a sharp, beveled cutting blade on one side and a flat, weighted hammer face (known as the poll or peen) on the reverse. Unlike full-sized felling axes that require a wide, two-handed swing and substantial kinetic momentum to fell large timber, a hatchet is optimized for nimble, controlled, single-handed manipulation, making it an indispensable implement for splitting kindling, limbing felled branches, carving wooden implements, butchering game, and bushcraft shelter construction.

Lexicographically, the word exhibits extensive technical variations and vivid metaphorical idioms deeply woven into social, political, and military history:

  • Craft & Functional Typology:
    • Carpenter's Hatchet: Features a straight, chisel-ground cutting edge for precise flattening and hewing of joists, frequently equipped with a notch in the blade or poll for extracting nails.
    • Shingling Hatchet: A specialized roofer's tool fitted with an adjustable gauge pin or threaded peg along the blade to measure uniform shingle reveal, alongside an integral hammer head for driving cedar shake nails.
    • Camp / Bushcraft Hatchet: A multi-purpose outdoor tool with a gently curved cutting belly and a sturdy hammer poll, balanced for splitting hearth kindling, driving tent stakes, and dressing field game.
  • Idiomatic & Cultural Expressions:
    • Bury the hatchet: A famous idiom meaning to settle an acrimonious dispute, end hostilities, declare peace, or re-establish harmonious relations. The phrase directly reflects an authentic diplomatic ceremony practiced by Native American nations, particularly within the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy. During solemn inter-tribal peace treaties, leaders literally buried their tomahawks, war clubs, and bladed weapons beneath the roots of an eastern white pine (the "Tree of Peace"), symbolizing that weaponized violence was cast into oblivion and that eternal peace was established. Documented in Anglo-American colonial treaties since the late 17th century.
    • Hatchet job: A figurative phrase originating in early 20th-century journalism, denoting a ruthlessly biased, vicious, and systematic public attack designed to annihilate someone's reputation, artistic work, or political standing.
    • Hatchet man: Originally applied in late 19th-century San Francisco to paid Chinese tong assassins who carried concealed hatchets, the term migrated into modern corporate and political jargon to describe a ruthless operative or subordinate deployed by leadership to execute harsh, unpopular, or destructive duties (such as mass layoffs, corporate liquidations, or hostile political character assassination).
    • Hatchet-faced: Having sharp, gaunt, narrow, and prominently angular facial features (sharp jawbone, prominent bridge, hollow cheeks), evocative of the thin, wedge-like profile of an iron chopping bit.
  • Nautical & Historical Combat: In the Age of Sail, naval fighting forces deployed boarding hatchets—compact iron axes equipped with rear spikes—used in naval boarding assaults to sever enemy rigging, grapple gunwales, smash cabin bulkheads, and suppress shipboard fires by chopping away flaming canvas and cordage.
