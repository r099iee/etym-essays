Ingratiating is a textbook Renaissance coinage: English scholars needed a verb for deliberately winning favour, so they stitched Latin in- into plus gratia favour and gave it an English tail. Its history runs from Vedic hymns of praise to modern performance reviews.

The deep root is PIE *gʷerH- to raise the voice in praise, to welcome. It yields Sanskrit gṛnati he praises, sings, Avestan gar- to praise, and Latin gratus pleasing, literally praised, welcomed. From gratus comes gratia, one of Latin's richest nouns: favour freely given, grace, gratitude, influence, pardon. Gratia underlies gratis, grateful, congratulate, disgrace, and grace itself via French. Roman society ran on gratia: networks of favours, patronage, and thanks that bound client to patron.

  • Latin gratia: Cicero uses in gratiam redire to return into favour, se in gratiam insinuare to worm oneself into favour - the exact ancestor idiom of ingratiate. No Classical verb ingratiare exists; the concept was phrasal.
  • Italian ingraziarsi (Renaissance): to ingratiate oneself, to curry favour, from in- + grazia. Court manuals of Castiglione's age taught sprezzatura, the art of pleasing princes without seeming to try. English travellers brought the word home.
  • English ingratiate (1620s): first in the reflexive to ingratiate oneself with, meaning to bring oneself into favour. Thomas Shelton, Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy, and later Johnson use it. The -ate makes it sound learned and deliberate.

Ingratiating, the present participle / adjective, follows by the late 17th century. Two senses diverge early and persist. The active-ingratiating sense means deliberately flattering to win favour: an ingratiating smile, letter, manner. The passive-ingratiating sense means pleasing, endearing, winning favour whether intended or not: an ingratiating voice, simplicity, novel. Dictionaries record both, but usage guides warn the first dominates and taints the second; even innocent charm sounds calculated when called ingratiating.

Phonetically the word keeps Latin g hard: in-GRAY-shee-ate-ing. Stress on grat. The -ing is double-duty: progressive verb (he is ingratiating himself) and pure adjective (an ingratiating child). Spelling stable since 1650 except occasional ingraciate.

  • 1700s: Chesterfield's Letters advise against ingratiating arts as servile; Johnson defines ingratiate as to get in favour. Moralists condemn it as fawning.
  • 1800s: novels use ingratiating for servants, suitors, politicians, and centipedes in fables - creatures that crawl pleasingly. The prompt's ingratiating centipedes plays on this: many-legged flatterers wriggling for favour.
  • 1900s-present: psychology distinguishes ingratiation as impression-management tactic: flattery, opinion conformity, self-presentation. American social psychology (Jones, 1964) makes ingratiation a technical term. Everyday use remains pejorative: nobody thanks you for being ingratiating.

Socio-culturally, ingratiating tracks changing attitudes to hierarchy. In courts and patronage systems, pleasing the powerful was a survival skill, half-admired. In democratic, egalitarian rhetoric, it becomes sycophancy, condemned as insincere. To call centipedes ingratiating is thus doubly contemptuous: they are low creeping things and dishonest flatterers, the perfect image for a misanthropic author imagining nature's revenge on man.
