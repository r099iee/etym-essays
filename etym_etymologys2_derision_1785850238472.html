CHAIN: FROM PIE TO DERISION (7 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *wreid- "to turn, twist" (traditional reconstruction, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; DEBATED — see note below; the bridge to laughter is the idea of contorting or twisting the face into a grin).
  2. Latin rīdēre "to laugh" (attested from Plautus, early 2nd century BCE).
  3. Latin derīdēre "to laugh at, mock, deride" (classical, attested 1st century BCE; de- "down, completely" + rīdēre).
  4. Latin derīsiō, derīsiōnis "contemptuous laughter, mockery, derision" (imperial Latin, 1st–4th centuries CE; built on the perfect participle derīsus of derīdēre).
  5. Old French derision "mockery, derision" (attested from the 12th century).
  6. Middle English derisioun "derision, mockery" (late 14th century, borrowed from Old French; used in the Wycliffite Bible, c. 1382).
  7. English DERISION — modern form, spelling stable since the 16th century; the pronunciation /dɪˈrɪʒ.ən/ shows French-influenced palatalization of the -s- to /ʒ/.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The pre-Latin history of rīdēre is uncertain: de Vaan records no secure PIE etymology, and the traditional link to *wreid- "to turn" is an inference from the notion of twisting the face; a connection to Sanskrit vrīḍ- "to be ashamed, blush" is sometimes suggested but insecure. The PIE stage is therefore probabilistic, not certain.
  • The derivative family is secure from Latin onward: rīdiculus "laughable" gave ridiculous and ridicule, risus "laughter" gave risible, and derīdēre gave both deride and, via derīsiō, derision.
