The word is a compound of Greek en- “in” and kōmos “revel, festive procession.” Each element is traced separately, then the compound is completed. Chain 1 — the prefix en- “in”: - PIE *h₁en “in, inside” (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Ancient Greek en- “in, within” (prepositional prefix, attested) → Greek enkōmion “in the revel, i.e. the song of the festive procession” (5th c. BCE) → Latin encomium “praise, tribute” (learned borrowing, classical and post-classical Latin) → English ENCOMIUM (late 16th c., learned borrowing via Latin). Chain 2 — the base kōmos “revel, festive procession”: - Origin debated: either (a) Pre-Greek substrate, with no secure PIE ancestry, or (b) PIE *keim- “to lie, to rest, to settle” (if accepted) → Greek keīmai “to lie, be situated” → Greek kōmē “village, settlement” (possibly from *keim-) → Greek kōmazein “to revel in a village festival, to hold a kōmos” → Greek kōmos “a band of revellers; a festive, singing, dancing procession” (attested in classical Greek, 5th c. BCE). - Greek en- + kōmos → Greek enkōmios “belonging to the kōmos, of the revel” → Greek enkōmion “a song of praise sung in the kōmos; a formal ode in praise of a victor” (5th c. BCE; Pindar and the lyric tradition) → Latin encomium “an encomium, a formal tribute of praise” (rhetorical Latin) → English ENCOMIUM “a formal expression of high praise” (recorded from the 1590s). Note on debate: The derivation of kōmos is genuinely disputed. The PIE link through *keim- is uncertain because it would require an irregular development, and many authorities (including Beekes) treat kōmos as Pre-Greek — a word of the Aegean substrate with no reliable Indo-European etymology. If kōmos is Pre-Greek, then enkōmion has no reliable Indo-European origin beyond its prefix en-. The same kōmos underlies comedy (Greek kōmōidia, “song of the revel”).
