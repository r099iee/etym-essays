Alder-trees names alders considered expressly as trees — the water-loving riverside trees of the genus Alnus that line streams and meres with leaning trunks, dark sticky leaves, and dangling catkins.

In the source sentence the stream runs like a black ribbon bordered with leaning alder-trees: Tolkien chooses the compound to make the trees stand up out of the bankside scrub as individuals — stooping, leaning silhouettes reflected in dark water, the classic alder posture, roots in the wet and crowns tipped over the current.

Core meaning in detail: The common alder (Alnus glutinosa) is Britain's riverside tree par excellence: a medium tree to twenty metres, with rounded, faintly sticky leaves, purple winter buds, male catkins that lengthen in spring, and small woody cones that persist like miniature fir-cones. Its roots partner with nitrogen-fixing bacteria, enriching poor wet soils, and its freshly cut wood blazes orange before fading to pale pink — then, paradoxically, lasts for centuries underwater, underpinning Venice, Amsterdam, and countless mills.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The alder as a standing tree (primary sense): the riverside tree itself, distinguished from alder scrub, alder carr, or alder timber. The bridge from the simplex alder is specification: alder-tree insists on the full-grown woody individual.
  • Riverside and waterside vegetation collectively: alder-trees bordering a stream evokes the whole wet woodland fringe — willows, osiers, sedges — of which alder is the type. The bridge is ecological association: where alders lean, water runs.
  • Alder timber and its underwater endurance (material sense): piles, sluices, clogs, and charcoal. The bridge is product-for-tree: the tree is named for what builders take from it, famous for hardening rather than rotting below the waterline.
  • Markers of wet, gloomy ground (literary sense): alders signal mires, flood-plains, and haunted mere-edges from Theocritus to Tolkien. The bridge is habitat symbolism: the tree that loves the wet becomes the emblem of the wet place itself.
  • Folkloric and mythic tree (cultural sense): the alder bleeds red when cut, and Irish and Welsh legend makes it the tree of resurrection and oracle — Bran's tree, the fairy's wood for shields and whistles. The bridge is the orange-red sap: a tree that seems to bleed invites stories of wounding and return.
  • Compound transparency (grammatical note): unlike opaque compounds, alder-tree wears its parts openly — kind plus category — a pattern English loves for emphasis and metre (oak-tree, willow-tree, apple-tree). The bridge is rhythmic insistence: two stresses where one would do, so the line leans with the trees.

Register and connotation: alder-trees is damp, northern, and faintly mournful — the vocabulary of riverbanks in late autumn, catkins hanging like lambs' tails over black water. It carries none of the oak's majesty or the birch's gaiety; its beauty is crepuscular and musical, all whisper and reflection. Yet countrymen valued it warmly: the tree that fed no pigs and made no beams still shod the poor with clogs, powdered their gunpowder with charcoal, and held the riverbank together root by root.
