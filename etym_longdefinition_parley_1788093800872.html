PARLEY is a noun and verb of negotiation, borrowed into English in the mid-sixteenth century from Middle French parlée, "a talk" — a noun made from the feminine past participle of parler, "to speak". In its full sense it is a conference — a formal conversation — held between hostile or opposing parties, typically at the boundary of their conflict, under an arrangement that suspends fighting, in order to discuss terms: surrender, ransom, exchange of prisoners, division of territory, or a truce. In the source passage, Thorin's besiegers come expecting battle and instead announce that "there is matter for a parley and a council" — the word marks the moment when enemies agree, however briefly, to talk.

Primary senses.

  • Military conference under truce: historically a parley was held under a flag of truce or after a signal — "to sound or beat a parley" (drum or trumpet) — and was protected by custom, and by early-modern military law, with rules of safe conduct. Enemy commanders meet, usually at the very edge of their lines, to arrange an armistice or the details of a surrender.
  • The verb, "to parley with": to hold such a conference — "to parley with the enemy" — and, more loosely in older English, simply "to speak, talk, discuss". The modern verb retains the sense of talking to someone you are otherwise fighting or opposing.
  • General conference and discussion (dated): 16th–19th-century English also used parley for any formal talk or deliberation between parties — "a parley of kings", "in parley with his councillors" — with the military sense gradually dominant.

Secondary and extended senses.

  • Figurative — talking instead of fighting: modern use is often jocular or literary — "the warring factions parleyed all night", "no parley with temptation" — where the word's value is its antique, swashbuckling flavour.
  • Pop-culture revival: since the film Pirates of the Caribbean (2003) — where a pirate may invoke "parley" as a right to safe-conduct negotiation under the pirate code — the word is widely known as cinematic slang for a negotiated truce, and "parley" circulates in gaming and meme vocabulary for peace talks.
  • Related borrowings from the same French root: parliament (English, c. 1300, from Old French parlement, a speaking-assembly), parleyvoo (pidgin for French parlez-vous?, "do you speak?"), and parole (a word, a promise — from the same Latin parabola "speech, word").

Semantic bridge. The word's whole career is a narrowing: Latin parabolāre "to speak" → Old French parler "to speak" → French parlée "a talk" → English parley, which within a century of its borrowing narrowed from "any talk" to "a talk between enemies". What makes parley special is that it is the one English negotiation-word built on the plain verb "to speak": its history says that at the boundary of war, speaking is already a concession.
