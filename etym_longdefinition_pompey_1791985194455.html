Pompey is the traditional English form of the Roman name Pompeius, borne most famously by Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (106–48 BCE), the general who cleared the Mediterranean of pirates, reorganised the Roman East, and in 60 BCE joined Crassus and Caesar in the alliance historians call the First Triumvirate. The source context names him in exactly that role: “Pompey, the great military hero of the time, joined with Crassus and Caesar to form the first triumvirate.”

English uses the name in three ways: as the historical individual; as a shorthand for the type of the great general whose prestige makes and unmakes civilian regimes; and as a place nickname. The form Pompey rather than Pompeius belongs to the old English habit of anglicising classical names — Tully for Tullius, Virgil for Vergilius — and gives the name a familiar, almost affectionate sound that the formal Latin does not have.

The historical Pompey

  • Origins: born on 29 September 106 BCE in Picenum, son of Gnaeus Pompeius Strabo, consul in 89 BCE; the family was wealthy and locally dominant but not of the old Roman aristocracy.
  • Early commands: he raised troops for Sulla in the civil war, won victories in Sicily and Africa, and celebrated his first triumph in 79 BCE, extraordinarily young and without having held a magistracy.
  • The pirate war: under the lex Gabinia of 67 BCE he was given command over the whole Mediterranean; he cleared the pirates in about three months, a feat that made him the most popular man in Rome.
  • The East: under the lex Manilia of 66 BCE he took over the war against Mithridates VI, defeated him, annexed Syria, intervened in Judaea and took Jerusalem in 63 BCE, and reorganised the eastern provinces and client kingdoms.
  • Politics: he returned to Italy in 62 BCE, disbanded his army, and found the Senate unwilling to ratify his eastern settlement or to grant land to his veterans; hence the alliance with Caesar and Crassus in 60 BCE.
  • Downfall: after the deaths of Julia (54 BCE) and Crassus (53 BCE) the alliance collapsed; Pompey was defeated by Caesar at Pharsalus on 9 August 48 BCE and murdered on the Egyptian shore near Pelusium on 28 September 48 BCE.

Why the name matters

Pompey is the paradigm of the general who is indispensable to a civilian government and therefore dangerous to it. His career shows how the Republic's military commands had outgrown its political institutions: proconsular power over the whole Mediterranean, extraordinary commands voted by the people, and armies loyal to a man rather than to the state. His decision to ally with Caesar and Crassus in 60 BCE was an admission that the Senate could not give him what he wanted; the alliance held only as long as the three men's interests coincided.

The name as a byword

  • The great general: “a Pompey” is rare, but “Pompey the Great” is a standard English phrase, and the name stands for military glory on the grand scale.
  • The political partner: in narratives of the late Republic, Pompey is the prestige that legitimises the triumvirate — the soldier whom the politicians need and cannot control.
  • The loser: in later literature, above all Lucan's Pharsalia, Pompey becomes the doomed champion of the Republic against Caesar's genius, a figure of dignified defeat.
  • The place nickname: “Pompey” is the affectionate nickname of Portsmouth and its football club, of uncertain origin and unrelated to the Roman.
  • The monument: “Pompey's Pillar” at Alexandria is a column erected for Diocletian, misnamed by later travellers who thought it marked Pompey's tomb.

Register and connotation

The name is neutral in historical writing and heroic in popular narrative. “Pompey the Great” carries a hint of irony, since the epithet was taken seriously by Pompey and by his soldiers but has often seemed to historians a little too large for his achievements. The familiar English form makes him seem more approachable than Caesar; the nickname “Pompey” for Portsmouth has given the word a cheerful, regional flavour quite absent from the Latin.

The name itself

Pompeius is a gentilicium, the second element of the Roman name, belonging to the plebeian gens Pompeia, which came from Picenum and produced its first consul in the second century BCE. Its etymology is uncertain. The most quoted suggestion links it with the Oscan numeral pompe “five”, and hence with the city of Pompeii, whose Oscan name is púmpaiians; on that view the name would mean something like “of the five”, perhaps a fifth child or a member of a five-part division. The link is plausible but unproven, and other scholars treat the name as simply an Italic formation of unknown sense. The agnomen Magnus, “the Great”, was adopted by Pompey in the late 80s or 70s BCE, according to Plutarch after Sulla greeted him with the title.
