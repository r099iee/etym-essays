- 16th century – dialectal 'slump' appears, meaning to sink into mud or slush, to fall heavily — a sound-symbolic word whose very shape mimics a soft mass meeting something wet and heavy.
- 17th–18th centuries – the sense extends from bogs to bodies and objects: to fall or sink suddenly and heavily, losing all muscular tension and support; the slump is graceless, heavy, and final.
- 19th century – 'slumped' becomes the word for exhausted or defeated posture — slumping into a chair, a body collapsing — and extends to buildings slumping into ruin.
- 20th century – the great figurative leap: an economic 'slump' (a sudden heavy fall in prices, a depression) and a batting 'slump' (a loss of performance) — every sudden loss of vitality, from markets to athletes, is now described by the same heavy fall.
- 21st century – both senses coexist: the physical 'slumped' (limp, defeated, surrendered to gravity) and the figurative 'slump' (a decline or drop), the body's last word in an argument with gravity.
