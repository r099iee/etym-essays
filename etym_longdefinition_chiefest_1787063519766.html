Chiefest is the superlative of the adjective chief, meaning “foremost, most important, most eminent, highest in rank or authority”. It is formed by adding the regular superlative suffix -est to chief, and it therefore means “of the first importance; greatest; most prominent”. In Tolkien's line — “O Smaug the Chiefest and Greatest of Calamities” — Bilbo flatters the dragon with a doubled superlative: Smaug is not merely a great calamity but the chiefest, the very foremost and most magnificent of all possible calamities, a phrase engineered to appeal to a dragon's vanity.

Morphology and gradability. Chief is one of a class of adjectives that are semantically absolute or non-gradable — like unique, perfect, or supreme — in that they already denote the topmost degree. Yet historically English speakers have repeatedly applied comparative and superlative forms to such words (compare perfectest, chiefest, supremest). Chiefest is thus an intensifying, emphatic form: it doubles down on “chief”, insisting on absolute pre-eminence. This is a “double superlative” in spirit, though structurally it is simply chief + -est, exactly parallel to hardest or darkest.

Core sense. “Most chief; first and foremost; holding the highest rank, position or importance.” It is used of people, things, causes, virtues, and dangers: the chiefest treasure, the chiefest concern, the chiefest among friends, the chiefest of calamities.

Polysemy and usage shades.

  • Rank and authority: the highest in position — the chiefest lord, the chiefest of the clan, the chiefest officer.
  • Importance and value: the most important or valuable — “the chiefest of all virtues”, “the chiefest ornament of the city”.
  • Emphasis and rhetoric: used to intensify praise or blame in high style — as in Tolkien's grandiloquent flattery of Smaug, or in Shakespearean and Biblical cadences.
  • Archaic-literary register: in modern English the form survives chiefly in literary, rhetorical, Biblical or archaic-sounding contexts; in ordinary speech it has been largely replaced by “foremost”, “most important”, or simply “chief”.

Not a true double superlative — but perceived as one. Grammatically, chief + -est is a plain inflected superlative, analogous to hardest. But because chief already implies “first”, many writers and critics have felt that chiefest is redundant, and prescriptive grammarians since the 18th century have often discouraged it. The perception of redundancy, however, is precisely what gives the word its rhetorical force: it signals emphatic, unqualified primacy. Writers use it deliberately to elevate tone, and it would be wrong to call it an error in literary usage — it is a stylistic intensifier with a long and respectable pedigree.

Related forms. The noun chief (“leader, head of a group”) and the adjective chief (“main, principal”) share the same history; the adverb is chiefly (“mainly, primarily”); the abstract nouns of state are chiefship and chieftaincy. The comparative chiefer exists but is very rare; chiefest is by far the better-known inflected form. The word's neighbours include chieftain (the leader of a clan or tribe), which entered English separately through French.

Note on the source text. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) is set in a world where speech is deliberately heightened, and Smaug is addressed with mock-archaic courtliness. “Chiefest and Greatest” is a doubled superlative of flattery — a classic dragon-taming rhetorical gambit that the narrator explicitly praises. The word was chosen for its sonorous, old-fashioned gravity; in contemporary English it reads as a deliberate archaism, and that is exactly the effect Tolkien wanted: a hobbit using the speech of ancient, heroic courtesy to soften a dragon's heart.
