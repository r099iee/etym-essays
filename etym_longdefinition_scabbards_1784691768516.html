SCABBARDS is the plural of scabbard, a noun denoting the protective case or sheath in which the blade of a sword, dagger, or bayonet is carried. A scabbard is distinguished from a mere wrapping or a soft bag by its firm construction: traditionally it is built around two wooden slats or a rigid wooden or metal core, covered in leather, cloth, or metal sheathing, and it is fitted with a mouth or throat (the locket) at the top, a tip-guard (the chape) at the bottom, and often rings or suspension mounts by which it hangs from a belt, baldric, or saddle. The scabbard has a double function that has been constant since the earliest civilisations: it protects the blade from weather, rust, and accidental damage, and it protects the bearer from the weapon's own sharp edge. From the earliest attested periods, however, the scabbard has also been an object of display, ornamented with precious metals, gems, enamel, or tooled leather as a mark of the owner's rank and of the weapon's worth. A sword's scabbard is thus at once a piece of military equipment and a piece of jewellery.

Polysemy and modern usage:

  • Military and martial sense (the core sense): the standard rigid case for a sword, dagger, or bayonet, worn by soldiers, officers, cavalrymen, and ceremonial guards from antiquity to the present day; the working gear of the soldier who draws and re-sheathes the blade in combat drill.
  • Curatorial and archaeological sense: in museums and archaeology, scabbards are studied as artefacts in their own right; their fittings (locket, chape, suspension rings), their ornament, and their materials are primary evidence for dating, for the technology of metallurgy and leatherwork, and for the social status and regional style of the original owner. Surviving scabbards from Bronze Age, Roman, Viking, and medieval contexts are prized museum objects.
  • Heraldic and ceremonial sense: the scabbard appears in heraldry and state ceremonial as an attribute of authority; ceremonial swords are carried scabbarded in coronations and parades, and 'to draw' or 'to bare' a sword from its scabbard is the classic gesture of declaring war or beginning combat, giving the scabbard a symbolic role as the container of peace until the moment of drawing.
  • Figurative and idiomatic sense: the phrase 'to throw away the scabbard' (a translation of the French jeter le fourreau) means to commit oneself unconditionally to war, to abandon all hopes of peace; the scabbard thus symbolises restraint, peace, and the boundary of civilised conduct, while the bared blade symbolises unleashed violence. The word is also used figuratively for any protective covering that 'sheathes' a dangerous or precious thing.
  • Extended technical and colloquial sense: by analogy, any rigid protective housing shaped closely around an elongated object may be called a scabbard, though this use is rare and mostly restricted to specialised writing about weapons, costume, and stagecraft.

Historical and semantic bridge: the word's senses all flow from the physical object and its role in the history of warfare. Because swords were, for millennia, the most expensive and symbolically charged objects a warrior owned, the scabbard that protected and adorned the blade inevitably acquired ceremonial, heraldic, and figurative meanings. The semantic bridge from 'protective case' to 'symbol of restraint' is the action of drawing: a scabbarded sword is a promise of peace; a bared sword is a threat. Every secondary sense — archaeological, heraldic, figurative — extends this single image of the sword held in its decorated case.

In the literary context of this card (from Chapter 2, 'Roast Mutton', of The Hobbit), the plural appears when the company examines the trolls' hoard: 'Two caught their eyes particularly, because of their beautiful scabbards and jewelled hilts.' These are the great Elvish swords Glamdring and Orcrist, forged long ago in the hidden city of Gondolin for the wars against the goblins. The beauty of their scabbards is not mere decoration: it is evidence. Nothing in the crude troll-hoard could have produced work of such fineness, and the jewelled fittings immediately signal to Gandalf and Thorin that these are no common blades but heirlooms of ancient craftsmanship, weapons of noble lineage — which is why Thorin later speaks of the swords as dearer to him than a whole shipload of gold. The scabbards, in other words, are the first clue by which the company recognises the true nature of its spoils; the ornamental scabbard is here a narrative device that lets the characters (and the reader) identify the swords as legendary before their names are even spoken.

Related terms and register: sheath is the older, more general English word for any close-fitting blade-cover, from a knife-case to a plant's leaf-base; scabbard is the more specific term for the firm, usually ornate case of a sword, dagger, or bayonet; holster belongs to firearms; quiver to arrows. In register, scabbard is formal and slightly archaic, at home in military history, fantasy, and ceremonial writing, and it carries an antique, dignified flavour that the plain sheath does not.
