Curtness has a short biography in time but a deep one in imagery, because it begins not with a word about rudeness but with a word about blades. The ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *sker-, whose verbal core means "to cut, to separate, to shear off." At this earliest level the meaning was purely physical: hands that cut stalks, tools that shear wool, edges that split one thing from another. From this single root two great rivers descend, and each carries a different flavour of the cutting metaphor. In the Italic stream the root hardened into the Latin adjective curtus, "shortened, cut short, docked, mutilated" — a word used of lopped trees, of animals whose tails had been docked, of crops harvested too early. In the Germanic stream the same root produced *skeran- "to shear" (hence English shear and share, the portion sheared off) and *skurtaz "short" (hence English short, shirt, and skirt, garments that were "cut" to length). Curtus and *skurtaz are thus cousins — two parallel realisations of the same prehistoric metaphor: to be short is to have been cut.

The word we actually use arrived through the Norman conduit. Latin curtus passed into Old French as court or curt, "short," and from Old French it entered Middle English in the fourteenth century with the same blunt, spatial meaning — a "curt" staff was simply a short staff, a "curt" man a short man. The semantic shift that produced the modern sense is one of the most elegant in the English vocabulary: at some point around the early nineteenth century (the first attestation of curt meaning "rudely brief in speech" is dated c. 1825), the spatial idea of being "cut short" was transferred from bodies to words. A curt reply is a reply that has been cut short — docked like a horse's tail, lopped like a branch — the sentence stripped of its courteous appendages, its "pleases," its hedging, its softening. What had been a purely physical description became a judgement of character, and the abstract noun curtness was formed in the nineteenth century to name that quality: the disposition that clips speech, the habit of the man who treats conversation as a transaction to be minimized.

The socio-cultural context of the shift is worth noting. The early nineteenth century, the age of the telegraph and the railway timetable, of reform bills and a rising commercial middle class, was precisely the period in which verbal economy began to be prized as a virtue and its excess — brevity without courtesy — began to be diagnosed as a vice of character. Curtness entered this vocabulary of the efficient modern temperament: the man who is curt is the man who has internalized the blade, who applies to conversation the economy that the age applied to business. The word has kept that blade ever since. In literature it is a stock instrument of character portraiture — the officer, the manager, the head of household who disposes of questions "with his usual curtness," as the passage puts it — and the "usual" is the tell: curtness, unlike a single snub, is a fixed behavioural signature, a habit of mind made visible in the clipped rhythm of speech.

Set beside its synonyms, the word shows its exact niche. Brevity, terseness, succinctness are neutral or admired; curtness is their negative shadow. Abruptness names the sudden break, brusqueness the gruff tone, bluntness the frank content; curtness alone names the refusal — the choice to say as little as possible, shearing away every courtesy, and by that shear to assert dominance, distance, or contempt. When the passage gives Bellman "his usual curtness," it uses the full history of the word in one gesture: the PIE blade that cut stalks, the Latin adjective for docked tails, the French court of the medieval court, the nineteenth-century diagnosis of the efficient man, and the clipped rhythm of a dismissal that ends with the strange punctuation "Not likely?." — a question cut short, an answer refused, the whole conversation lopped like a branch. In a single word, the passage converts three thousand years of cutting into a portrait of a man.
