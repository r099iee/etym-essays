jog-trot names at once a horse's gait and a way of life: a slow, short, bouncy, monotonous trot — and by extension any slow, jogged, humdrum, steady-going routine. In the source phrase — at a jog-trot pace, and through the panoramic opening of the four windows — the father deliberately does not gallop or post at full speed but ambles from customer to customer through England and Wales, the postchaise rocking gently enough for a child to gape, read, and remember. The pace is commercial prudence (sparing hired horses), domestic comfort (a small boy on a bracket), and narrative method: at jog-trot you see high-roads, crosses, villages, and bookish confusions like Glendearg for Glen Farg.

As a gait, the jog-trot sits between walk and trot proper: a two-beat diagonal gait, low, collected, jolting rather than extended, the Western rider's jog and the English hack's dog-trot. Horsemen distinguish it from the floating extended trot (covering ground), the collected trot (school work), and the rack or pace. A jog-trot is easy on horse and rider, sustainable for hours, but monotonous — bump-bump, bump-bump — hence its figurative gift to language. As a noun it is the gait itself (to go at a jog-trot); as a verb (to jog-trot along) it is to move so; as an adjective (a jog-trot existence) it is to live so.

  • Literal equestrian sense (source sense): e.g. to ride at a jog-trot, a jog-trot pace. Slow, regular, jolting, economical. The bridge from jog (a push, shake) plus trot (a gait) is onomatopoeic mechanics: the rider is jogged as the horse trots.
  • Human locomotion sense: to jog-trot on foot: e.g. to jog-trot round the park, a jog-trotter. A shuffling run between running and walking, ancestor of modern jogging. The bridge is analogy: the human imitates the horse's easy two-beat shuffle.
  • Routine-existence sense (dominant figurative): e.g. a jog-trot life, jog-trot business, the jog-trot of parish duties. Humdrum, unvarying, respectable, a little dull — the daily round done at the same pace. The bridge is pace-to-habit: as the gait never quickens, so the life never surprises.
  • Mental-tempo sense: e.g. a jog-trot mind, jog-trot prose, jog-trot thinking. Steady, plodding, unimaginative but reliable; e.g. critics dismiss a novel as jog-trot. The bridge is gait-to-style: ideas trot when they should gallop.
  • Jog as push-shake (first-element background): e.g. to jog the elbow, to jog the memory, to jog along. A short abrupt push or reminder. In compounds it supplies the jolt: jog-trot is a trot that jogs you.
  • Trot as gait and bustle (second-element background): e.g. to break into a trot, on the trot (in succession), a globe-trotter. Brisk two-beat movement, also busy coming-and-going. Jog-trot selects only the slowest, least showy end of trotting.

Orthography records meaning. The hyphen marks a true compound gait (jog + trot), not a casual phrase; 19th-century printers hyphenate it (jog-trot) where modern sportswriters often close it (jogtrot) or open it (jog trot). The note preserves Ruskin's hyphen exactly, and with it the period's ear for horse-rhythm as moral rhythm: to go at jog-trot is to choose observation over haste, customers over conquest, childhood memory over dispatch.
