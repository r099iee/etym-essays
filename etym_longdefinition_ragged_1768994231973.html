ragged (adjective): Ragged describes cloth, clothes, or similar materials that are old and torn — worn until their edges have broken into irregular points, fringes, and holes, their surface rough with loose threads and tatters. The core sense is a garment in the terminal stage of its life: a fabric so badly worn that it no longer has clean edges but hangs in shreds, its weave broken, its parts ragged points. In the source sentence — "He is pale and thin, he wears a thin and ragged linen shirt" — the word is diagnostic and almost clinical: together with "pale" and "thin" it completes a picture of extreme poverty, a figure whose clothes have come apart in exact proportion to his body's failure. The tone of the sentence is flat and pitiless — the observer reports the shirt as one reports a weather condition — and that flatness is precisely what makes the poverty so moving; there is no exclamation and no pity in the grammar, only the fact of the ragged linen, and the reader must supply the horror. The word thus carries, in its most important modern use, a social meaning: ragged is the adjective of the poor, the unmended, the outcast — "ragged children," "a ragged beggar," "the ragged edge of society."

Polysemy and extensions: (i) the primary sense — of cloth, clothes, or their wearer, old and torn, worn to tatters ("a ragged coat," "a ragged shirt"); (ii) of a person, dressed in torn clothes; shabby, poverty-stricken in appearance ("ragged children"); (iii) of surfaces and natural things, rough, jagged, uneven, irregular — "ragged rocks," "ragged cliffs," a "ragged coastline" of broken, indented shore; the transfer from torn cloth to broken outline is old and natural, since both are uneven, irregular edges; (iv) of sound and voice, rough, harsh, broken, uneven — "ragged breath," "a ragged cough," "ragged breathing," the irregular, broken rhythm of illness or exhaustion; (v) of lines, formations, and groups, uneven, straggling, irregular — "a ragged line of battle," "a ragged crowd," a column that has lost its formation and trails unevenly; (vi) of abstract processes, uneven, jerky, lacking smoothness — "ragged performance," "ragged timing," a musical phrase played with uneven notes; (vii) the figurative use for anything unfinished, broken, and left at odds — "a ragged plot," "ragged edges of a plan" (as in "the ragged edges of the problem"), suggesting work left rough and untidy; (viii) botanical and zoological technical uses — a "ragged robin" (the pink-flowered Lychnis flos-cuculi, whose petals are deeply cleft into ragged lobes), "ragged-leaf" plants with deeply cut foliage; (ix) in sewing and textile terms, deliberately "ragged" edges — a cut edge left unfinished and fringed as a design feature. The historical and semantic bridge: the word entered English from Old Norse with the sense "shaggy, rough, tufted" (like coarse hair or rough wool), moved onto cloth as "worn into tufts and points," and then — because a broken, uneven edge is the common image — spread to rocks, voices, lines, crowds, and plans, so that ragged today is the general adjective for anything that has lost its smooth, clean, regular outline, from a torn shirt to a broken breath.
