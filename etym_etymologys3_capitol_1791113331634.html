The evolution of capitol is inseparable from the topography and mythology of ancient Rome, and it is also a story of orthographic politics in modern American English, where a single vowel distinguishes a building from a city and an idea.

The deepest recoverable ancestor is PIE *kap-ut-, meaning head. This root is extraordinarily productive: Latin caput, English head by Germanic path, German Haupt, French chef and tête by different routes, and words like captain, chief, capital, cattle, chattel and decapitate. The semantic logic is transparent: the head is the top, the chief part, the counting unit of persons and beasts, and the seat of authority. From head comes height, prominence, leadership and principal status.

In Latin this became caput, capitis, one of the most frequent nouns in the language, with senses spanning anatomy, summit, mouth of a river, person counted in a census, capital punishment (loss of head / civic head), and chief city. Upon one of Rome's seven hills, the Mons Capitolinus, stood the great Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, called the Capitolium. Roman legend explained the name by a portent: workmen digging foundations found a human head, caput humanum, interpreted as a sign that Rome would become the head of the world. Whether folk etymology or genuine derivation, the association stuck: Capitolium meant the head-place, the citadel sanctuary, the symbolic head of the Roman state.

  • Ancient Rome: citadel and sanctuary. Capitolium denoted both the hill and the temple complex. It housed Jupiter, Juno and Minerva, received triumphal processions, and served as the religious anchor of the Republic and Empire. To go to the Capitol was to go to the centre of power.
  • Medieval and Renaissance transmission. Through Late Latin and Middle French capitole, the word survived as a learned reference to the Roman Capitol. English antiquaries in the 16th-17th centuries used Capitol for the Roman temple and hill, borrowing directly from Latin and French literary usage.
  • American neoclassical appropriation. When the United States built its national legislature on Jenkins Hill in Washington, Thomas Jefferson and others self-consciously named it the Capitol, invoking Roman republican virtue. The cornerstone was laid in 1793. By extension each state called its own legislature building a capitol with a lowercase c, reserving uppercase Capitol for the Washington edifice.
  • Phonetic stability, orthographic split. Phonetically the word barely changed: Latin /ka.piˈtoː.li.um/ simplified to French /ka.pi.tɔl/ to English /ˈkæp.ɪ.təl/. The crucial modern change was semantic and orthographic: capital with an a for the city, wealth, uppercase letter and chief importance; capitol with an o only for the building. The -ol spelling preserves the Latin diminutive/neuter ending -olium, while -al reflects the adjectival capitalis, from head.
  • Literary attestations. Livy describes the prodigy of the head on the Capitol; Gibbon uses Capitol as shorthand for Roman power; American newspapers after 1800 constantly contrast the Capitol dome with the cathedral, museum and university as emblems of civic façade versus squalor behind, exactly as in the present context.

Socio-culturally, capitol thus shifted from sacred hill to secular legislature, from unique proper noun to generic common noun. It carries neoclassical aspiration: democracy housed in columns and domes. The lower-case plural capitols now dots American English, while British English rarely uses it except for Rome or Washington. Confusion with capital remains one of the most tested spelling distinctions in schools, precisely because the two words share a head.

Today capitol means almost exclusively a legislative building. Its evolution illustrates metonymy (hill for temple for legislature), borrowing for prestige, and orthographic disambiguation to protect two meanings that grew from the same head.
