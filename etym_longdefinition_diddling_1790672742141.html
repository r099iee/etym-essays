diddling is the present participle and gerund of diddle, and it carries two intertwined families of meaning that meet in the idea of trifling: to waste time on trifles, and to cheat on a small scale. In the context sentence — harmless antiquarian diddling — the first family dominates: fiddling, dawdling, pottering about with words as curiosities rather than instruments of thought. To diddle here is to piddle, to tinker lovingly but unseriously, to treat language as a collection of antiques to be dusted rather than tools to be used.

The verb diddle in standard modern use has three principal senses. First, to waste time, to dally, to fiddle idly: to diddle about, to diddle away the afternoon, to diddle with a gadget. This is informal, mildly affectionate, and close to dawdle, doodle, and fiddle. Second, to cheat or swindle in a petty way: to diddle someone out of change, to diddle the taxman, to feel diddled. Here the tone is still small-scale — one diddles widows out of shillings, one does not diddle nations out of billions; for that we have defraud. Third, in older and dialectal use, to jiggle, to totter, to move unsteadily, as a child diddled on a knee or a drunkard diddling down the lane. This physical sense survives in nursery diddle-diddle-dumpling and in the image of diddling fingers.

  • Trifling / time-wasting sense: the antiquarian sense on this card. Looking at words rather than through them to ideas is diddling because it substitutes curiosity for understanding. The author concedes it is harmless — affectionate contempt rather than condemnation.
  • Petty-fraud sense: to trick by small cunning. He diddled me out of five pounds. British English especially favours this sense; American English prefers gyp, short-change, or rip off for the same act.
  • Fiddling / fidgeting sense: to manipulate aimlessly with the hands. Stop diddling with that dial. Close to twiddle, and often paired with it.
  • Sexual slang sense (vulgar, avoid): in coarse modern slang diddle can mean to fondle or molest. This sense is entirely absent from the antiquarian context and should not be imported into literary reading; register and era disambiguate completely.

Grammar and register are essential. Diddling as a gerund — antiquarian diddling — packages the activity as a hobby, parallel to dabbling, dilettantism. The -ing form stresses ongoing, habitual, slightly contemptible indulgence. The word is informal and faintly comic; its very sound — the repeated light di-di — enacts triviality. No one diddles gravely. Even the cheating sense retains a music-hall roguishness: Jeremy Diddler, the stage swindler who gave the word literary currency, is a charming rogue, not a felon.

Collocations include harmless diddling, mere diddling, diddle about, diddle away, diddle with, diddle out of, feel diddled. The preposition selects the sense: diddle with is fiddling, diddle out of is cheating, diddle about is wasting time. In academic polemic, as here, diddling functions as a dismissive understatement: to call scholarship diddling is to accuse it of unseriousness while pretending the charge is gentle.
