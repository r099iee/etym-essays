- Late 16th c. — Classical borrowing: Entered English during the Elizabethan Renaissance directly from Latin annāles, used to describe Roman historical works like those of Tacitus and Ennius.
- 17th c. — Monastic and national chronicle application: Extended to medieval and domestic historical records, specifically referring to yearly compilations of national events maintained by monks and state heralds.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment critique and rhetorical grandiosity: Used by historians like Hume and Gibbon both critically (to contrast dry yearly listings with philosophical history) and rhetorically (as the eternal repository of fame, as in Thomas Gray’s ‘short and simple annals of the poor’).
- 19th c. — Institutional and academic branding: Adopted widely as the prestigious title of scientific, mathematical, and medical periodicals published by learned societies to record annual discoveries.
- 20th–21st c. — Historiographical revolution and popular idiom: Enshrined in academic history by the French Annales School, while flourishing in general English in dramatic idioms of monumental achievement (‘in the annals of sport/crime’).
