Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*bʰel-
"to blow, swell, inflate"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*blēstrą / *blas-
"a swelling, puff, blast, eruption"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
blestre
"swelling, cutaneous tumor, pimple"
↓
Middle English (c. 1300)
blister / blester
"vesicle of the skin; watery swelling"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 15th–16th c.)
blister (verb)
"to raise skin vesicles; to scald or plague"
↓
Modern English (late 16th c.–present)
blistered
"covered with vesicles; scorched; looking miserable"
Note: The etymological descent of blister involves an intricate convergence between Germanic roots and Gallo-Romance transmission. The ultimate root is Proto-Indo-European *bʰel- ("to swell, blow"), which yielded Germanic *blēstrą ("blowing, swelling"). In Frankish or continental Germanic, this gave rise to Old French blestre ("swelling, bump"), which was introduced into Middle English following the Norman Conquest. Simultaneously, native Scandinavian/Norse cognates like Old Norse blástr ("a blast, swelling, furnace draft") and Middle Dutch bluyster reinforced the form in regional English dialects. The participial adjective blistered emerged naturally as the verbal conjugation stabilized in Early Modern English.
