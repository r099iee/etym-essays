The English form Nanking is a historical romanization of the Chinese toponym 南京 Nánjīng, a transparent Sinitic compound: 南 nán 'south' + 京 jīng 'capital'. Neither morpheme has any Proto-Indo-European ancestry; both belong to the Sinitic branch of Sino-Tibetan, so the honest derivation must be told as Chinese political naming followed by Western spelling practice, not as an Indo-European root tree. The compound is old: 南 'south' is attested in oracle-bone inscriptions of the late Shang period (c. 1200–1046 BCE) as a directional character, and 京, which appears to have meant 'hill' or 'high place' before it meant 'capital', is likewise attested in early script. The semantic path of 京 runs from a high mound (the elevated seat) to the great city and ultimately to 'capital', a narrowing that reflects how early Chinese capitals occupied elevated, fortified sites.

The political naming. Nánjīng, literally 'Southern Capital', pairs structurally with 北京 Běijīng 'Northern Capital'. The binary crystallized in imperial geography whenever north and south needed clear labels for seats of power. The city now called Nanjing has borne many names — Jinling, Jiankang, Yingtian among them — and its identity as the 'southern capital' was consolidated in the Ming dynasty and again in Republican-era usage, even as the actual seat of government shifted across dynasties. In Dream of the Red Chamber, whose action unfolds in the capital, Nanking is the ancestral southern sphere of the powerful houses, the source of the 'Nanking Four' and of letters that reorder household priorities; the name is therefore a social magnet, not a pin on a map.

Reception in Europe and the -king spelling. Westerners received the name through Jesuit, trade, and diplomatic channels from the sixteenth century. Missionary geographies of the seventeenth century (for example Martino Martini's 1655 Novus Atlas Sinensis) used forms like 'Nanquin' and 'Nankin'; English trade and diplomatic English settled on 'Nanking'. The Postal Map romanization, standardized around 1906 and widely used in English before pinyin, rendered 南京 as Nanking and 北京 as Peking. The '-king' spelling is an English approximation of an older southern/Jianghuai reading of 京 (something like kīng), close to the pronunciation that European visitors of the era encountered in the lower Yangtze region. It must not be confused with the English noun king: that word is Germanic (Proto-Germanic *kuningaz) and has no genetic link to the Chinese morpheme — the resemblance is a pure accident of romanization.

Trade offshoots and historical memory. Because the city was famous for a yellowish or buff cotton cloth, English developed nankeen (also nankin) as the name of the fabric, extending the place-name into the vocabulary of commerce; 'Nanking' likewise appears attributively with other goods (Nanking silk). The name entered diplomatic history permanently through the Treaty of Nanking (1842), which ended the First Opium War and whose English text fixed the spelling for historians. In the twentieth century, pinyin made Nanjing the standard form in modern writing, while Nanking survived in older translations, historical prose, and treaty references, acquiring a period flavour of classic sinology.

Why the novel keeps Nanking. Translators of Hong lou meng often retain Nanking (and Peking) for internal consistency with the period style of the translation and for the slightly archaic, dignified tone it lends the family geography. In the source passage, a letter 'from Nanking' arrives and immediately draws Wang Xi-feng into urgent conference: the etymology and the romanization together explain why that single name can carry so much weight — a compound meaning 'Southern Capital', delivered into English by centuries of trade, mission, and treaty, and loaded in the novel with ancestral authority.
