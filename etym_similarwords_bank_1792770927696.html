• embankment: Similar because both refer to raised artificial or natural mounds of earth or stone constructed alongside a route or watercourse; differs because an embankment is explicitly engineered, large-scale, and artificial (built of compacted fill to carry a railway, canal, or motorway across low ground), whereas a bank can be entirely natural, pastoral, small-scale, or ruggedly irregular.
  • slope: Similar because both designate an inclined surface of terrain departing from the horizontal; differs because slope is a general geometric and geological term for any angled ground regardless of boundary function, whereas a bank specifically acts as a lateral retaining wall, border ridge, or water margin.
  • ridge: Similar because both denote an elevated linear spine or crest of land; differs because a ridge is typically a prominent, elongated upland chain or mountain crest standing alone in the landscape, while a bank borders and flanks an adjacent hollow, road, ditch, or stream bed.
  • mound: Similar because both describe raised earthworks or heaps of soil; differs because a mound is generally isolated, rounded, or hillock-shaped (such as a burial barrow or defensive knoll), whereas a bank is elongated and continuous along a line of travel or water flow.
  • berm: Similar because both refer to a raised ledge or shelf of earth bordering an excavation or road; differs because berm is a technical civil and military engineering term denoting a narrow, flat horizontal bench or barrier designed specifically to deflect erosion or military fire, lacking the pastoral and geographic breadth of bank.
  • verge: Similar because both denote the land flanking the edge of a road; differs because a verge is specifically the flat grass strip immediately adjoining a paved carriageway, whereas an earthen bank rises upward or drops steeply away from the road surface.
  • levee: Similar because both designate earthen ridges preventing floodwaters from inundating surrounding plains; differs because a levee is specifically a broad, engineered river dike built along major floodplains (especially in French and American hydrologic terminology), whereas a bank can be the natural shore of any river, road cutting, or hedge boundary.
  • bluff: Similar because both describe high, steep earth formations overlooking water or roads; differs because a bluff is a high, broad, precipitous cliff face offering a panoramic view, whereas a bank is typically lower, gentler, and more intimate in scale.
