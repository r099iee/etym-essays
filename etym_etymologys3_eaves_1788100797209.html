Introduction. Eaves is a word whose entire recorded history is about edges and thresholds: the edge of the roof, the edge of the ground beneath it, and finally the moral edge of privacy — for no common English word has produced a more famous family than the eavesdroppers who once stood beneath it. Its written record runs from the oldest English glosses and charters to the modern roofing manual, and at every stage the -s that makes it look plural is doing something else entirely.

1. The Indo-European and Germanic background

The prehistory of eaves is less secure than one would like, and honest scholarship says so. The Germanic family is clear: Old English efes (also yfes, ēfes), “edge of a roof”; Old Frisian ose; Middle Dutch oese; Middle Low German ōvese; and Old High German obasa/obisa — the Old High German word meaning “porch, gallery, gallery under the roof”, which preserves the same overhang concept from the inside. These point to a Proto-Germanic feminine *ubisō, and the traditional etymological account — followed by the standard dictionaries with due caution — builds that noun on the Germanic preposition for “over”, *uber (Proto-Indo-European *upér, from the base *up-, “up, over, above”, the family of over, up, above, and Latin super): the eaves are, on this view, “the over-part”, the piece of the roof that extends over the wall. The Oxford English Dictionary remains hesitant, marking the origin “uncertain” while recording the Germanic cognates and the plausible over-connection; some scholars have wondered whether a lost root meaning “to overflow, drip” might lie behind it. The parallel with German Traufe, “eaves”, literally “the dripper” (from triefen, “to drip”), shows how different languages chose to name the same strip — by its drips rather than its overhang. The honest summary is that the Germanic forms are certain and the Indo-European step is a well-argued hypothesis, not a fact.

2. Old English: efes and yfesdrype

Old English efes is a singular feminine noun meaning “the edge of a roof”, attested in the vocabulary material and, most interestingly, in the legal-charter world through the compound yfesdrype, “eaves-drip, the fall of water from the eaves”. In Anglo-Saxon land charters and the later medieval law of houses, the eaves-drip was a boundary and a right: the strip where the rain fell belonged to the roof’s owner for that fall, and one neighbour could not build so close to another’s land that his drip crossed the line. The word thus entered English law as a measure-word — an edged, drip-defined strip of the world — centuries before it entered the building trade in its modern narrow sense.

3. Middle English: eves, brink, and the eavesdropper

Middle English eves/ewes (13th–15th centuries) broadened the meaning to “any edge or brink” — the eaves of a roof, but also the eaves of a wood or a cliff — while the building sense remained primary. Its most famous Middle English achievement is the eavesdropper family: the person who lurked under the eaves, where light and sound leaked out around the overhang, in order to hear what was said inside. The noun evesdropper / eves-dropper is recorded from the mid-15th century, and it carries an almost legal sharpness: the medieval borough courts punished eavesdroppers as a public nuisance, and the word appears in town records as a recognised category of offender — the man or woman who stood at the wall’s edge, under the drip, to steal speech itself.

4. The re-analysis of the plural (16th–18th centuries)

The 16th and 17th centuries deliver the word’s great grammatical drama. Because the final -s of efes had become indistinguishable from the plural ending, speakers stopped feeling eaves as a singular: “the eaves is” gave way to “the eaves are”, the plural form became the only normal form, and — the surest sign of complete re-analysis — a fresh singular eave was back-formed in the 18th century for the individual overhang. Modern builders use this newborn singular freely in compounds: eave height, eave overhang, and the Canadian eavestrough. Meanwhile the verb eavesdrop appeared around 1606 — Ben Jonson’s Volpone uses “eaves-dropper” in the same years — and the sneak-watchers of the late medieval town slid smoothly into Jacobean London, where eavesdropping was pursued under the statute of 1562 against eavesdroppers as disturbers of the peace.

5. The utilitarian centuries and the modern word

In the 18th and 19th centuries, as architectural publishing and the building trade standardised terminology, eaves narrowed back to its old core: the projecting edge of a roof, now measured, drawn, and fitted with gutters and fascias. Dictionaries from Johnson onward registered the plural-only habit as settled fact, and the old “brink of a wood” sense faded into dialect. The 20th-century roofing industry made the word exact — eave depth, raised eave, flared eave, and the hanging gutter the Canadians call the eavestrough — while ordinary speech kept the plural “the eaves” with its airborne image of nests, icicles, and drips. The word has thus completed a full circle: from the drip-line of Anglo-Saxon land law, through the eavesdroppers of the medieval town, to the humble, exact, and still faintly poetic name of the roof’s edge — the place where, in Wilde’s story, the crystal daggers hang.
