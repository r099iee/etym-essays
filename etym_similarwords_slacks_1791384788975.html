• Trousers: Similar because both refer to two-legged outer garments extending from waist to ankles; differs because trousers is the overarching, formal British term that encompasses tailored suit pants, formal wear, and uniform garments, whereas slacks specifically denotes relaxed, casual or semi-formal garments not sold as part of a suit.
  • Pants: Similar because both denote bifurcated lower-body clothing; differs because pants is the universal general American term for all trousers, while in British English pants denotes underwear, and slacks specifically implies a slightly loose, tailored drape suited for leisure or business-casual settings.
  • Chinos: Similar because both are popular choices for business-casual and smart-casual attire; differs because chinos are made strictly from a specific lightweight cotton twill fabric (originally military khaki), whereas slacks are frequently fashioned from wool, flannel, gabardine, polyester, or linen.
  • Jeans: Similar because both serve as everyday informal legwear; differs because jeans are constructed from heavy, riveted indigo denim with visible topstitching and patch pockets, designed originally for rugged manual labor, whereas slacks feature softer tailoring, slash pockets, and a more refined, dressy profile.
  • Khakis: Similar because both provide comfortable, non-suit alternatives for workplace and weekend wear; differs because khakis typically emphasize tan or olive cotton twill with a casual, utilitarian finish, while slacks span a wider spectrum of fabrics, colors, and dressiness.
  • Breeches: Similar because both represent bifurcated historical garments worn on the lower body; differs because breeches fasten snugly at or just below the knee and were the rigid standard of formal menswear before the nineteenth century, whereas slacks extend down to the shoe tops with a loose, flowing drape.
  • Pantaloons: Similar because both emerged historically as looser alternatives to tight knee-breeches; differs because pantaloons were a transitional late-eighteenth and early-nineteenth-century fashion item, often tightly fitted to the calves with ankle buttons, whereas slacks originated from loose naval trousers and emphasize unstructured comfort.
