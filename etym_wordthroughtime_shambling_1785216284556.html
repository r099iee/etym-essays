- [16th century: crooked limbs] - the adjective 'shambling' describes legs that are crooked and ill-set, splayed and badly jointed, likened to the ramshackle timber of a butcher's bench.
- [17th-18th century: slovenly gait] - the word becomes a verb of motion: shambling is walking with loose, dragging, slovenly steps, feet shuffling and body slouched — clumsy, lazy, faintly degenerate.
- [19th-20th century: drift toward the uncanny] - as 'shambles' comes to mean a scene of ruin and disorder, shambling darkens: it starts to describe the wrecked, broken gait of the feeble, the criminal, the monstrous.
- [Contemporary: horror register] - in modern fiction and film the shambling walk is the signature of zombies, ghouls, and broken bodies; the old awkwardness remains, but the word has gained a distinct undertone of the uncanny and the menacing.
