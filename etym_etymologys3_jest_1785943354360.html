FROM DOING TO DEEDS: THE LATIN BASE

The story of jest begins with a Latin verb about carrying and doing. Gerō, gerere meant "to carry, bear, conduct, do, perform": a Roman official gerit bellum, "wages war"; gerit magistratum, "holds office"; gerit rem publicam, "runs the state". The verb's past participle was gestus, "carried, performed", and its neuter plural, gesta, meant literally "things performed" — deeds, exploits, achievements. Latin writers used gesta as a title for collections of notable deeds: the late-medieval Gesta Romanorum, "Deeds of the Romans", a compilation of moral tales, and similar titles such as Gesta Caroli. In Latin the word never meant "a joke"; it meant the record of great action.

The deeper ancestry of gerere is a genuine scholarly problem. Older popular etymologies confidently set it under a PIE root *ger- "to carry"; modern scholarship does not. The verb has no securely reconstructed Indo-European etymon: de Vaan's etymological dictionary of Latin records gerere as of uncertain origin, and a proposed link to PIE *h₂eħ- "to drive, lead" (the root of Greek ágein, Latin agere) is not generally accepted. We must therefore say plainly that the chain we can attest runs from Latin onward, and that the Latin verb itself stands as an etymological isolate whose prehistory is unknown.

FROM DEEDS TO TALES: OLD FRENCH

In Old French the noun geste took two connected senses: "deeds, exploits" (what was done) and "the telling of deeds" (the story). The famous literary genre is the chanson de geste — the "song of deeds", the Old French epic celebrating the heroic exploits of Charlemagne and his paladins (the Song of Roland, c. 1100, is the most famous example). A geste could thus be both an act and an account of an act, and this double sense is the hinge on which the whole semantic history turns: once geste could mean "a story", it was free to drift from "story of heroic deeds" to "story in general" to "story told for laughter".

FROM TALE TO JOKE: MIDDLE AND EARLY MODERN ENGLISH

Middle English took the word in the early fourteenth century (attested c. 1300) as geste, jeste, "a tale, a story, a narrative". The earliest English examples are often romances and tales of adventure, exactly matching the French sense. But the register was already sliding: alongside the heroic tale, a geste came to mean any story, and story-tellers — minstrels, jesters, and the compilers of jest-books — increasingly supplied comic material. Two developments fixed the modern word:

  • The jest-book genre (16th–17th centuries): printed collections of merry tales, practical jokes, and witty sayings — such as A C. Mery Talys (1526) and Tarlton's Jests (c. 1600) — institutionalised "jest" as comic narrative.
  • The verb "to jest" and the noun sense "a joke" (16th century): "jest" moved from "tale" to "joke, witty remark", and "in jest" ("not in earnest") became a fixed phrase. By Shakespeare's time the comic sense was dominant (Mercutio in Romeo and Juliet: "A jest's prosperity lies in the ear / Of him that hears it").

WHY THE WORD FALLS FROM EPIC TO COMEDY

The descent is not accidental. The heroic epic was a genre of oral performance; as it declined, the same performers and audiences who had enjoyed tales of Roland turned to lighter fare. Because geste meant "story" with no necessary heroic content, it could survive the death of the epic by changing its referent from deeds to jokes. The word carried its past as a halo of formality: a "jest" even today sounds like a courtly, rehearsed joke rather than a spontaneous gag — a memory of the jester's profession.

SOCIETY, REGISTER, AND THE MODERN WORD

In modern English "jest" is a consciously literary word. It survives in set phrases ("only in jest", "jesting apart", "a sorry jest"), in the profession of the jester (court fools from the medieval to the Tudor period, made famous again by Shakespeare's fools), and in the archaic heroism of "geste" within literary history. Its near-synonym "joke" now does the everyday work, and "jest" has retreated to elevated or ironic registers — which is precisely why the word's long fall from deeds to wit remains one of the most instructive semantic descents in English vocabulary, a casebook example of a word that outlived its genre by changing its meaning.
