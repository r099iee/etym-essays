A culture-word of the bogs. PEAT is a northwestern European culture-word, rooted in the material life of bogs rather than in the Classical Mediterranean agriculture that supplied most English vocabulary. Etymologists connect it to a Celtic base meaning roughly “piece” or “portion” — the cut sod of a bog as a countable bit of wet earth — a family well preserved in the Brittonic languages: Welsh peth and Cornish peth mean “thing, piece”, and Breton pez means “piece”. The underlying sense is concrete and practical: among Celtic-speakers of the damp west, the stuff cut from the bog for burning was naturally called “a piece”, and that everyday notion travelled into English through the documentary Latin of the British Isles. Through Medieval Latin and Middle English. Medieval Latin peta, “a piece of turf, peat”, appears in charters and estate accounts of the British Isles — the word is a Latinization of the local Celtic or northern vernacular term for the cut bog sod, not a Classical Latin inheritance. From it, and from the living vernacular, Middle English took pete / peat, recorded from about the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, especially in northern and Scottish spheres, where the bog economy was an everyday reality. The path is regional: in the wood-poor districts of Scotland, Ireland, and northern England, blocks sliced from waterlogged ground and dried by the hearth were an essential household fuel, and the word that named them travelled from the bog-cutter’s speech into the language at large. The stages of its career can be set out:
  • c. 1200s–1400s — the fuel sod: Anglo-Latin peta and Middle English pete name a cut piece of bog turf for burning; the perception is practical and local, tied to northern British and Irish fuel economies, and the word is concrete and material.
  • 1500s–1700s — the domestic staple: peat becomes a household heating fuel of Scots and northern English life; peat smoke defines a home’s smell; the word appears in regional speech and travel accounts, and landscape law develops around it — turbary rights, the right to cut turf and peat on another’s land.
  • 18th–19th centuries — science redraws the word: natural history and geology re-describe peat as an organic deposit and an early stage of coal formation; educated readers meet the word in science alongside its folk fuel sense, and horticultural use begins to rise.
  • 20th century — the dual life: rural fuel use declines in many areas as coal and oil spread, while garden and greenhouse demand for peat moss soars; “peat bog” becomes fixed in school geography, and the word has two public faces — the dying hearth and the growing industry.
  • Late 20th–21st centuries — environmental reframing: peatlands are recognized as major carbon sinks and endangered habitats; “peat-free” products signal ethical consumerism, and “peat extraction” becomes a policy and climate-news phrase while traditional hearth associations survive in heritage and whisky contexts (peat-smoked malt is a defining flavour of Scotch).
Semantic bridges and cultural freight. The word’s polysemy is a record of its users. The metonymy from substance to landscape (“peat land”, the card’s phrase, where the hikers’ boots sink deep into the spongy waterlogged ground) is ancient and natural — the material names the terrain it forms. The metonymy from substance to fuel (“a peat fire”) reflects centuries of household economy. The chemical identity of peat as a carbon sink and its historical role as a fossil fuel place the word at the centre of the modern climate debate, where “peat” names both an endangered habitat and an emitter of stored carbon when drained. Throughout, the core denotation — partially decayed, waterlogged plant matter of the bog — has never wavered; only the uses and the moral weight have changed.
