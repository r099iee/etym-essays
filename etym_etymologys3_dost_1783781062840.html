Dost is a living fossil of one of the deepest and most productive roots of the Indo-European family, *dʰeh₁-, "to put, place, set." The root's reflexes cover nearly the whole history of European civilization: Sanskrit dádhāti "he places," Greek títhēmi "I put" (whence thema, thesis, library via thēkē), Latin faciō "I make" (via the labialized variant *dʰeh₁-), and the English word deed and the suffix -dom. Into Germanic the root produced the verb *dōną "to do," and it is a measure of how far the meaning traveled that the original sense "to put, place, set" became the general English verb of acting, performing, making, and, ultimately, the purely grammatical auxiliary of questions and negation.

The second-person singular present of Old English dōn was dēst (West Saxon) or dōst (Anglian): the stem dō- plus the old second-person ending, whose -i caused the fronting (i-mutation) of the root vowel in the standard dialect. This -st ending is itself a direct inheritance from the Indo-European second-person singular ending *-si, which Germanic hardened to *-s(i) and then *-st; it is not a Middle English innovation but the ordinary present-tense marker of the oldest layer of the language. The same ending gives modern hast, shalt, wilt, and the archaic art, sitst, sayst. By Middle English the vowel distinction leveled and the form stabilized as dost (with the fuller doest available when verse metre needed an extra syllable).

In Early Modern English dost did a double job, and understanding it requires understanding the history of do-support. In Old and Middle English, questions and negatives were formed simply by inversion or by ne + verb. In the 15th and 16th centuries the verb do began to be used as an auxiliary that carried the tense and the person while the lexical verb appeared in the infinitive, and at first this do was semantically charged: it added emphasis, habituality, or formality. When Milton writes "Thou that dost prefer" at the very opening of Paradise Lost, dost is not an empty filler — it is the ordinary present of the full verb "to prefer," but its auxiliary-like placement already points toward the modern system. In the next century, do-support became fully grammaticalized: modern English requires do for questions ("do you prefer?"), negatives ("you do not prefer"), and emphasis ("you DO prefer"). The archaic dost therefore preserves a snapshot of the moment before that grammaticalization was complete, when the auxiliary still felt like a real word meaning "act, perform."

The distribution of dost in the early modern canon is a matter of register as much as grammar. The Authorized Version of 1611 uses dost and doth hundreds of times, both as the simple present of do and as emphatic auxiliaries, always in the intimate, reverential thou-register. Shakespeare uses dost freely across comedies, tragedies, and sonnets. And Milton — the most deliberate of early modern stylists — opens his great poem with "that dost prefer," choosing the archaic form to place the reader in direct, solemn address to the Holy Spirit, on whom the entire invocation depends. The -st endings and the thou-pronouns were the natural vehicles of that solemnity, and dost is one of their most frequent markers.

The disappearance of dost is once again the disappearance of thou. Between the late 17th and 18th centuries, standard English collapsed the singular and plural into you; with you the verb took one form for all persons, and the whole -st paradigm — dost, doest, didst, hast, wast, wert, satst, knowst, saist — left the living language together, surviving only in the Bible, in liturgy, in Quaker speech, and in archaizing poetry. Modern English retains exactly one fossil of the emphatic auxiliary, the stressed "do" of "do be careful," and it retains the memory of the second person singular only in the archaic canon that every educated reader knows. Dost today is a deliberate archaism: it signals scriptural or early modern diction, the reverential address to a single "thou," and the older syntax in which do was a word with meaning rather than a mere grammatical scaffolding.

Summary of the evolutionary chain:

  • Root: PIE *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, set" → PGmc *dōną "to do" → OE dōn; the semantic drift from "put" to the general "do, act" is complete within Germanic.
  • Inflection: the inherited second-person ending *-si → *-st, with i-mutation in West Saxon (dēst); leveled to dost/doest in Middle English.
  • Syntax: the rise of do-support in the 15th–16th centuries; dost as the emphatic/habitual auxiliary before do was fully grammaticalized.
  • Register: the thou-paradigm carries reverence and intimacy; dost is the standard thou-form of do in the Authorized Version (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton.
  • Loss: the collapse of thou into you in the 17th–18th centuries removes dost from speech; it survives as a fossilized archaism.
  • Key attestations: Old English dēst; Wycliffe and Chaucer dost; Authorized Version 1611 ("Why dost thou..."); Shakespeare; Milton, Paradise Lost I.17 (1667).
