CHAIN 1 — the adjective stern (the card's sense):
PIE *ster- "stiff, rigid" (reconstructed)
→ Proto-Germanic *sternjaz / *starnjaz / *sturnijaz "severe, strict" (proposed reconstruction; the exact form is unrecorded and debated)
→ Old English styrne "severe, hard, strict, cruel, inflexible" (attested 9th c.)
→ Middle English sterne "severe, strict, harsh" (attested 12th–15th c.)
→ English stern "severe, strict, uncompromising, grim" ◄ TARGET (present day)

CHAIN 2 — the homograph noun stern "rear of a ship" (a distinct word, given in full for completeness):
Proto-Germanic *steurjan "to steer, guide" (reconstructed)
→ Old Norse stýri "rudder, steering; the steering apparatus at the back of a vessel" (attested 9th–13th c.)
→ Middle English sterne "rudder; the rear part of a ship" (attested from the 13th c.)
→ English stern "the rearmost part of a ship or boat" (homograph, unrelated to the adjective)

DEBATE / NUANCE: The connection of the adjective to PIE *ster- "stiff" is presented as probable, not certain, by the OED and standard handbooks; the exact Proto-Germanic form (sternjaz vs starnjaz vs sturnijaz) is unrecorded and therefore debated. What is certain is the semantic metaphor: a stern person or law is "stiff," unbending. The nautical noun is a separate word from the steering root (cf. steer, starboard) and must not be merged with the adjective. A third English homograph, sternum "breastbone," comes from Greek stérnon and is also unrelated.
