CHAIN 1 OF 2 — DISCONTENTED
Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch, extend, draw taut," c. 4500–3000 BCE. Reflexes: Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches," Greek teínein "to stretch," Latin tendere and tenēre.
Stage 2 — Latin tenēre, "to hold, keep, grasp, possess," attested 2nd–1st c. BCE (Plautus, Cicero); from PIE *ten- "to stretch."
Stage 3 — Latin continēre (contineō, continēre, continuī, contentum), "to hold together, contain, restrain," attested 2nd c. BCE; con- "together" + tenēre.
Stage 4 — Latin contentus, -a, -um, past participle of continēre, "held together, contained; hence satisfied, content," attested 1st c. BCE.
Stage 5 — Old French descontent, "displeased, dissatisfied," 12th c.; des- "un-" + content.
Stage 6 — Middle English discontent (adjective, noun, verb), "dissatisfied, displeased," 15th c., from Anglo-Norman.
Stage 7 — English discontented (discontent + -ed), "dissatisfied, restlessly ill at ease," late 16th c. — FINAL NODE discontented.
CHAIN 2 OF 2 — CAPRICE
Stage 1a — Proto-Indo-European root *kaput-, "head," c. 4500–3000 BCE. Reflected in Latin caput (attested 3rd c. BCE) and, via Proto-Germanic *hafud-, in English head; Greek kephalē "head" is of debated origin and is probably NOT from this root.
Stage 2a — Latin caput, capitis (neuter), "head," attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE (Plautus); the source of capital, captain, chief, and of the Romance words for "head."
Stage 3a — Italian capo, "head," from Latin caput, attested from the 13th c.; Old Italian capo.
Stage 1b — Proto-Indo-European root *ghers-, "to bristle, stand on end," c. 4500–3000 BCE; reflected in Latin ēricius "hedgehog" (attested 1st c. CE, Pliny), the small bristling animal.
Stage 2b — Latin ēricius, "hedgehog" (also the sea-urchin), attested 1st c. CE; from PIE *ghers- "to bristle."
Stage 3b — Italian riccio, "hedgehog; curly, bristling," from Latin ēricius, attested from the 14th c.
Stage 4 — Old Italian caporiccio, literally "head-hedgehog" — a head whose hair bristles like a hedgehog's quills in sudden terror — attested 15th–16th c.; shortened to capriccio, "a shudder, a sudden fright; a sudden impulse, a whim," 16th c.
Stage 5 — French caprice, "caprice, whim," borrowed from Italian capriccio, mid-16th c.
Stage 6 — English caprice, "a sudden, unaccountable change of mood or behaviour; a whim," attested from the mid-17th c. (1650s), from French — FINAL NODE caprice.
Debate note: The origin of Italian capriccio is genuinely contested. The traditional derivation is capo "head" + riccio "hedgehog," i.e. "head with bristling hair" (a shiver of terror transferred to a sudden whim), and this is the account favoured by the dictionaries. A serious rival connects the word to capra, "she-goat": a capriccio would be a "goat-like leap," the skittish jump of a goat, extended to a sudden leap of the mind. OED presents both hypotheses and prefers the head-and-hedgehog derivation without excluding the goat. The connection of Latin tenēre to PIE *ten- in CHAIN 1 is standard and undisputed.
