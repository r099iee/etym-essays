coastwise — In the image "Flights of pelicans coastwise above the gray swells," the word is an adverb describing the direction and manner of the birds' flight: they are moving along the shore, following the line of the land rather than cutting across it or turning inland. The suffix -wise gives the word its peculiar flavor — it is the suffix of manner and direction (otherwise, clockwise, lengthwise) — so that coastwise reads almost like a mariner's chart notation, a course-description from a coast-pilot's log. The tone is precise, observational, and aerial: the narrator watches the pelicans the way a surveyor watches a coastline, and the word performs the same tracing motion it describes, the birds serving as living marks along the land's edge, as though the flock were part of the shore's own geometry.

Primary sense (adverb of direction): Coastwise means "in the direction of the coast, along the coastline," with motion and bearing, not mere position, built into it. It is at home with verbs of movement: to steam coastwise, to fly coastwise, to sail coastwise. It answers the question "which way?" rather than "where?"

Secondary sense (adjective / technical maritime usage): Coastwise also functions as an adjective in the fixed vocabulary of shipping and admiralty law: coastwise trade, coastwise commerce, coastwise shipping, and the coastwise laws, the American legislation (the Jones Act of 1920 being its modern pillar) that reserves domestic water transport between U.S. ports for U.S.-built, U.S.-flagged, U.S.-crewed vessels — the American counterpart of European cabotage. In this sense it means "carried on along the coast, not crossing oceans."

The suffix -wise and its own polysemy: The combining suffix -wise has three live values in modern English: (1) manner — likewise, otherwise, clockwise; (2) direction — coastwise, lengthwise, crosswise; (3) respect or sphere — the productive modern colloquial use (weather-wise, money-wise, career-wise). This third, "in regard to" sense is a twentieth-century extension of the old manner sense, and it is why sentences like "we were doing well publicity-wise" are perfectly standard today.

Synonym boundaries:

  • Alongshore: nearest in meaning ("along the shore"), but it lacks the technical, legal weight that coastwise carries in maritime law and course-recording.
  • Coastal: a pure adjective of location — something belonging to or standing on the coast — with no sense of motion along it.
  • Inshore / offshore: fix position relative to the sea (toward or away from the land), not a course traced along the coastline.
  • Littoral: the scientific term for the zone between high and low tide, positional and ecological rather than directional.

Only coastwise combines the noun coast with the adverbial -wise of manner and direction, which is why it collocates so naturally with verbs of motion and appears in the fixed phrase "coastwise shipping."

Register: navigation, admiralty, and American maritime law; in general prose it is rare and formal, carrying a charted, logbook gravity — precisely why the pelicans' flight, rendered "coastwise," feels like a line drawn on a chart.

swells — In the same sentence, the swells are the broad, rolling undulations of the sea: long, unbroken waves moving in slow succession beneath the birds, gray in the fading light. The tone is elemental and oceanic; a swell is not a breaking wave but the deep, patient heave of open water.

Primary sense (noun, oceanography): A swell is a long, unbreaking wave, or a set of such waves, that has traveled beyond the storm that generated it; unlike wind-driven chop or a breaker, a swell is regular, rounded, and deep-bodied. Surfers read "a good swell"; mariners speak of "a heavy swell."

Verb senses: To swell (from Old English swellan) means to grow larger, to expand, to rise: a river swells after rain; a bruise swells; a sail swells with wind; an audience swells. It is used transitively (to cause to grow) and intransitively (to grow by itself). The noun and verb are the same word in English, a feature the language kept from its Germanic core.

Secondary modern senses:

  • Groundswell (19th–20th c.): a deep, slow gathering movement of opinion rising from beneath the visible surface of politics — a "groundswell of support." The physical heave of the sea becomes the figure for mass feeling.
  • Swelling (medical): an abnormal enlargement of a body part, a tumor or edema.
  • A swell (19th-c. colloquial noun): a fashionable, dashing, elegantly dressed person — a dandy — with the adjective swell ("that's swell!") meaning excellent, fine. The roundness of the rising wave turned into social polish and approval.

Synonym boundaries:

  • Wave: the general term for any moving ridge of water; a swell is specifically the long, regular, unbreaking wave of open water.
  • Billow: a literary word for any great swelling mass, of water, smoke, or sail; less technical than swell.
  • Breaker: a wave that breaks on a shore or reef — the opposite of a swell, which does not break until it shallows.
  • Surge: a sudden, powerful forward rush of water, abrupt rather than rhythmic.
  • Roller: a long heavy wave, close kin to swell but with a sense of mass and weight.

Semantic bridge: the core idea of swell is "to expand by pressure from within" — water rises into a rounded body; a fashionable man "swells" with prosperity and pride. All the modern senses — ocean wave, political groundswell, medical swelling, social dandy — are the same rounded, rising motion applied to water, opinion, tissue, and style.
