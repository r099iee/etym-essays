- Classical Roman Antiquity (1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE) — Revered by senatorial aristocrats and Stoic philosophers as an uncompromising champion of constitutional libertas and lawful tyrannicide, but officially branded by the Augustan and Julio-Claudian imperial dynasties as a parricidal rebel who plunged Rome into bloody civil war.
- High & Late Middle Ages (12th c. – 14th c.) — Condemned through the theological and feudal lens of universal Christian monarchy; immortalized in Dante's Inferno (c. 1320) where he is placed in the lowest pit of Hell, chewed eternally by Lucifer alongside Judas Iscariot as the ultimate embodiment of treacherous disloyalty.
- Renaissance & Early Modern Drama (16th c. – 17th c.) — Reconceptualized with rich psychological and tragic nuance; crystallized by William Shakespeare's Julius Caesar (1599) as the conflicted, high-minded "noblest Roman of them all," whose fatal flaw was rigid philosophical idealism exploited by clever manipulators.
- Enlightenment & Age of Revolutions (18th c. – early 19th c.) — Resurrected as a secular hero and supreme patron of civic virtue striking down despots; fervently celebrated by French Jacobins and American patriots, directly influencing the Virginia state motto Sic Semper Tyrannis.
- Modern & Contemporary Era (20th c. – 21st c.) — Functioning primarily in popular rhetoric, political journalism, and literature as an eponymous metaphor for an intimate traitor who delivers a devastating backstab under the guise of friendship or high moral principle.
