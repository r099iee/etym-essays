- Old English (c. 700s–1100): *wanian* 'to lessen, diminish, decay, dry up' - Applied literally to physical objects, waters receding, the moon shrinking in its monthly cycle, and the depletion of earthly goods.
- Middle English (c. 1100–1500): *wanen* 'to decrease in size, light, or vigor' - The astronomical pairing with *waxen* ('wax and wane') became deeply rooted in philosophical and allegorical poetry reflecting mutability and the wheel of fortune.
- Early Modern English (1500s–1700s): 'to lose power, beauty, health, or fortune' - Shakespeare and Renaissance dramatists widely employed *wane* for declining kingdoms, aging beauty, fading daylight, and dying love.
- 19th c.: 'to draw toward an end' - Victorian literature solidified idiomatic constructions like 'the wane of the year' and 'a waning empire,' linking the word to romantic melancholy and imperial twilight.
- 20th c.–Present: 'to decrease in popularity, market share, or enthusiasm' - Expanded into modern economics, journalism, and cultural critique ('waning interest,' 'waning influence,' 'waning support'), while retaining its core astronomical reference.
