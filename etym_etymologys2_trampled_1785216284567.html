Expressive Germanic stem *tramp- (imitative of heavy footfall; no securely reconstructable PIE etymon — the word is classed as imitative/expressive rather than inherited, and its ultimate origin is unknown) → Middle Low German trampen / Middle Dutch trampen "to stamp, tread heavily, trample" (attested in continental Germanic from the medieval period; cognate with Old High German trampen and with modern German trampeln "to trample", Dutch trappelen "to stamp the feet") → Middle English trampen "to walk heavily, stamp the feet," first attested late 14th century (c. 1386, in Chaucer’s usage of heavy walking) → Middle English trampelen / trampelyn "to tread or crush repeatedly underfoot," an iterative/frequentative formation in -elen (-le), first attested c. 1440 (mid-15th c.); the -le suffix marks repeated or continuous action, matching the "many small treads" of the word’s meaning → Modern English trample (verb) → TRAMPLED, the weak past tense and past participle, formed with the past suffix -ed, regular from the earliest attestations of the verb. NOTE ON THE DEBATE: some etymologists have proposed a PIE link to *drem- "to run" (the family of Greek drómos "course" and Sanskrit dramáti "he runs"), arguing that trampling implies moving feet; this connection is considered uncertain, and the mainstream view treats the stem as imitative/expressive with no recoverable Indo-European ancestor.
