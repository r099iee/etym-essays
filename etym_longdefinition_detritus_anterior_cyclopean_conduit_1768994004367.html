Four root words — DETRITUS, ANTERIOR, CYCLOPEAN, CONDUIT — in one passage. The source sentence — "It had been swept clean of all detritus, and was like the anterior of some Cyclopean conduit that might give upon a sub-Martian Erebus" — packs the four words into a single deliberate contrast: the tunnel is bare where it should be littered, and it leads, like the front porch of a giant's water-pipe, toward a darkness worthy of the Greek underworld. Each word earns its place.

1. DETRITUS — waste, debris, the loose fragments of rock and organic matter worn away by erosion, decay, or use. In this passage the word names what is notable by its total absence: a natural cave, especially one that once carried water, would be choked with rubble, pebbles, silt, and organic fragments; that the passage has been "swept clean of all detritus" is the first sign that the place is not natural — something has deliberately scoured it. The word carries a geological flavour (detritus is the ground-down fragments of rock produced by erosion), and that flavour is essential to the effect: the absence of geological detritus in a geological setting is uncanny, a cleanliness that implies intent. The tone is quietly ominous: the pristine tunnel is too clean to be innocent.

2. ANTERIOR — "located at the front," a spatial term of anatomy, zoology, and geometry, the opposite of posterior. In the passage the word does something sly and precise: it applies a technical, scientific, body-orientation word to a tunnel, so that the cave is implicitly given a front — a face, a leading end — as if it were an organism or an engineered structure rather than a random geological void. "The anterior of some conduit" is the opening, the mouth-end, the threshold of the pipe; the word converts the tunnel into a directed, purposive thing, pointing forward into the darkness. The clinical register of anterior (the language of dissection and laboratory reports) reinforces the strangeness: the explorers are not wandering a cave but reading the anatomy of an artifact.

3. CYCLOPEAN — "enormous or massive," literally "of the Cyclopes," the one-eyed giants of Greek legend; specifically, in archaeology, the adjective names the vast, irregular stone masonry of Mycenaean citadels (Mycenae, Tiryns), which the ancient Greeks believed to have been built by the Cyclopes because no ordinary hands could move such blocks. In the passage "some Cyclopean conduit" thus carries three charges at once: colossal scale (a pipe too big for human engineering), alien engineering (built by beings like giants), and immense antiquity (the masonry of the heroic age). The word is a compressed way of saying "built on a scale that shames and exceeds anything human."

4. CONDUIT — a pipe, channel, or tube through which water, gas, electricity, or other fluids pass; more generally, any channel through which something flows or is conveyed. In the passage the word completes the industrial-anatomical conceit: the tunnel is not a cave but the pipe of a vast, ancient water system, "a conduit that might give upon a sub-Martian Erebus" — a channel opening onto the underworld. The modern technical senses (electrical conduit — the protective tubing for wiring; conduit in finance and politics, "a channel through which funds or influence pass") all descend from the same core: something that conducts, that leads a substance or a current from one place to another.

5. The four words together. Detritus (the worn-down matter that should be there and is not), anterior (the front, the threshold), Cyclopean (the scale and alienness of the engineering), conduit (the purpose and direction of the channel): the sentence moves from the geological to the anatomical to the mythological to the engineering-technical, and the four registers combine into one picture — a tunnel so clean, so fronted, so colossal, so purposeful that it can only be the product of some gigantic, ancient intention, opening at last onto the Erebus of a dead world.

6. Synonym fields.
  • For detritus: debris (closest and most general — broken or scattered remains, but without the fine-grained erosional sense); rubbish and refuse (human waste, colloquial, domestic); litter (small scattered objects, lighter, often pastoral); sediment (matter settled from water, a subset of detritus without the "worn-away" sense); scree, rubble, tailings (geological and industrial debris).
  • For anterior: frontal, foremost, front, leading, forward, ventral (anatomical), earlier/prior (the temporal sense); the opposites are posterior, dorsal, rear, hind, later.
  • For Cyclopean: colossal, gigantic, monumental, massive, titanic, megalithic, brobdingnagian — all sharing the sense of superhuman scale; only Cyclopean carries the specific archaeological charge of ancient, alien, giant-built masonry.
  • For conduit: pipe, tube, channel, duct, flue, trough, main, pipeline — the physical carriers; and the figurative medium, vehicle, channel for anything that conveys.
