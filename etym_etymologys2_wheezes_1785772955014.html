Imitative / onomatopoeic Germanic base for air forced through a narrow aperture — no secure Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed; the word is sound-symbolic (see note) → Proto-Germanic *hwēs- / *hwais- type "to hiss, pant, blow" (reconstructions vary among scholars; the exact form is uncertain) (date uncertain, prehistoric Germanic) → Old Norse hvæsa "to hiss" (snakes, steam, angry breath) — the closest attested parallel (recorded in Old Norse literature) → Middle English wheesen / whesen / weosen "to breathe hard, breathe with noise, pant" (15th century) → Early Modern English wheeze "to breathe with a hoarse whistling sound" (spelling stabilized in the 16th–17th centuries) → English wheeze (verb); wheezes (third-person singular present — the exact form on this card); also noun "the whistling sound; a clever scheme, gag, or stock comic routine" (slang sense from the 19th century) → WHEEZES Note on debate: because the word is onomatopoeic, small phonetic variation across dialects is historically expected, and scholarly reconstructions of a Proto-Germanic *hwēs- type base differ in detail; some scholars treat the English word as native, others stress the influence of North Germanic hvæsa. No PIE etymon is secure.
