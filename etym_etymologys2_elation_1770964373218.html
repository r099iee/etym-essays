Single root word: elation (English noun from Latin ēlātiō). Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *bher- "to carry, bear" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives: Vedic Sanskrit bharati "he carries", Ancient Greek phérein "to carry", Latin ferre "to carry", Proto-Germanic *beraną (English bear). 2. Latin ferre "to carry, bear, bring, endure" — the Italic reflex of *bher-, attested from the earliest Latin records. 3. Latin efferre "to carry out, carry away, carry up; to lift up, exalt, extol" — ex- "out, up" + ferre — attested in classical Latin (Cicero, Vergil). 4. Latin ēlātus (suppletive past participle of efferre) "carried up, raised, exalted; puffed up, elated" — ex- + lātus, where lātus is a separate stem from PIE *telh- "to lift, bear, endure" (cf. Latin tollō "I raise", tolerāre "to endure") — classical. NOTE: this is the famous suppletive irregularity of Latin fero / tuli / latum. 5. Latin ēlātiō (gen. ēlātiōnis) "a carrying up, elevation; exaltation of mind, elation, pride" — noun of action from ēlāt- — attested in classical Latin (Cicero, Quintilian). 6. Old French elacion "elation, exaltation" — attested from the 12th century. 7. English elation "a lifting up, elevation; buoyant, exalted joy" — attested from the 14th century (reinvigorated from French and Latin in the 16th century); the purely positive "exhilarating joy" sense has dominated since the 18th–19th centuries. Final node: elation. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The chain is secure at every step, but it contains a notable morphological irregularity worth flagging: the participle ēlātus does not derive from *bher-/ferre but from the separate "bear, lift" root *telh- — the suppletive gap ferō-tulī-lātum inherited in Latin. English elation quietly preserves the result of that ancient stem-suppletion. The narrowing of the sense from "exaltation (praise or pride)" to "pure joy" is an attested semantic drift of the 18th–19th centuries, not a contested origin.
