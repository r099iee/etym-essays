• Magistrates: Similar because both denote elected or appointed officials with governing authority; differs because "magistrate" is the broad class of Roman (or modern) officers, while "consul" names the specific apex pair of annual chief magistrates in the Republic.
  • Praetors: Similar because both were senior Roman magistrates holding imperium; differs because praetors ranked below consuls and chiefly administered justice and led lesser commands, while consuls held the supreme executive power.
  • Proconsuls: Similar because both derive from the consulship and governed with consular authority; differs because a "proconsul" was a former consul (or ex-praetor) appointed to govern a province, not one of the two city magistrates of the year.
  • Tribunes: Similar because both were elected officials of the Roman state who could veto acts; differs because tribunes were plebeian officers protecting the people and could not command armies, while consuls held supreme military command.
  • Censors: Similar because both were pairs of annually (or quinquennially) elected senior magistrates; differs because censors took the census and controlled public morals and the senate list, while consuls held executive and military command.
  • Dictators: Similar because both were supreme magistrates with full power; differs because the dictator was a single temporary emergency office with unchecked authority, while the consuls were two, annual, and mutually checkable - the constitutional opposite.
  • Senators: Similar because both belonged to the governing elite of the Roman Republic; differs because senators were members of the deliberative council and did not hold executive office, while consuls were the elected executives who presided over the Senate.
  • Governors: Similar because both are officials exercising authority over a territory; differs because "governor" is a general term for provincial or modern administrators, while a consul was the city-based chief magistrate (and, in the modern sense, a commercial representative abroad).
  • Ambassadors: Similar because both are official representatives of a state in relation to others; differs because an "ambassador" represents a state diplomatically at a foreign capital, while a "consul" (modern sense) manages commercial affairs and citizens' welfare in a foreign city, and historically named the Roman chief magistrate.
  • Envoys: Similar because both denote official representatives sent by a state; differs because "envoy" is a generic term for any diplomatic messenger, while "consul" names a specific rank with specific consular duties and a long separate history.
  • Legates: Similar because both were Roman officers with delegated authority; differs because legates were deputies or ambassadors sent by a commander or the Senate, subordinate and temporary, while consuls were the supreme annual magistrates themselves.
  • Executive / chief executive: Similar because both denote the holder of supreme executive power; differs because "chief executive" is a modern generic title, while "consul" is the specific Roman office of two men sharing power for a year.
