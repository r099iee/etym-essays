resound is a verb, chiefly intransitive, whose core meaning is acoustic: a sound resounds when it rings out with force and is carried or thrown back through a space, and a place resounds when it is filled with such sound. English normally frames the second use with with: a hall resounds with applause, a valley with gunfire, a church with music. That is the frame Carlyle uses in the reading — “Churches resound not with excessive groanings” — where the negative is pointed: the churches are not ringing with lamentation, because the only prayers being said are the paid Litanies of the priests.

The primary senses

  • Of a sound: to ring out loudly, to reverberate, to be heard again and again as an echo. The subject is typically a noise, a voice, an instrument or a cry, and the verb implies both volume and a containing space that returns the sound.
  • Of a place: to be filled with a loud, echoing sound, usually expressed with with. The place is grammatically active but semantically passive: the church does not make the sound, it becomes a resonator for it.
  • Of an object or instrument: to give out a full, resonant note, especially of a bell, organ, trumpet or vaulted room.

Transitive and causative uses

  • To cause a place to ring with sound: “the choir resounded the vault with praise”. This is literary and now rare.
  • To send back a sound, to re-echo: “the cliffs resounded the shout”. Also archaic.
  • To utter or proclaim loudly; in older verse, to resound someone’s praises is to extol them noisily and publicly, and to resound a deed is to spread its report abroad.

The sense of fame and report

A distinct branch treats the verb as a matter of reputation rather than acoustics: a name, a deed or a person resounds through a country when it is spoken of everywhere. The bridge is the image of a space filled with a name — fame as the sound of many voices. This sense is old (it exists in Latin and in medieval French) and was common in English from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, but it is now archaic or deliberately poetic: “his name resounded throughout Europe” reads as a period flourish rather than ordinary prose.

Figurative and inward uses

  • In the mind or memory: words, a warning or a phrase can resound in someone’s head, meaning they continue to be heard inwardly, with emotional force. This extends the echo image from architecture to consciousness.
  • The participial adjective resounding: by the nineteenth century this had detached itself into a modifier meaning “emphatic, unmistakable, on a grand scale” — a resounding victory, a resounding defeat, a resounding success, a resounding failure. The metaphor is that the event makes a noise heard everywhere.
  • Ironic extension: a “resounding silence” is a silence so complete that it seems to echo — the same acoustic logic inverted.
  • Public opinion: a “resounding endorsement” or “resounding rejection” treats an election or vote as a noise produced by a whole population, the modern descendant of the fame sense.

Register, connotation and collocation

The verb is elevated and literary. It is at home in narrative prose, hymns, rhetoric, oratory and journalism, and it is uncommon in casual speech, where echo, ring or be filled with would be used instead. Its connotations are fullness, volume, publicity and ceremonial weight: things resound in cathedrals, caves, stadiums and halls of state, not in small rooms. The dominant patterns are resound with (a place) and resound through/across (a space, a country, a period). Because the verb implies a large, public acoustic event, it can dignify a subject or, used of complaints and criticism, lend it a monumental, wearisome quality; “the corridors resounded with complaints” makes the complaining sound institutional.

Two words behind one spelling

Modern English also has a separate, hyphenated verb re-sound (/ˌriːˈsaʊnd/), formed afresh from the prefix re- and sound, meaning “to sound again” (to re-sound a bell, to re-sound a note). It is unrelated in formation to resound (/rɪˈzaʊnd/), the inherited Latin verb, although both go back to the same Latin sonāre at a distance. The homography is a trap for readers, and the stress is the surest guide: the echo verb has a weak re-, the “sound again” verb a full long e and a secondary stress.

In this reading

Carlyle chooses resound rather than echo or ring because it fills the building: the church is treated as a stone vessel whose function is to amplify public prayer. Set against “sobs” and “groanings”, the verb measures the distance between spontaneous human grief and institutional worship; and the negative construction makes the emptiness of the churches audible. The word returns later in the paragraph’s “immeasurable tide of French Speech”, where the whole city, rather than a building, is the resonator.
