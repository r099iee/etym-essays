- 5th c. BCE to 1st c. CE (Classical Rome) — Coined in Roman constitutional law (sacrosanctus) to designate the legal and religious inviolability (sacrosanctitas) of the plebeian tribunes, enforced by deadly oaths against transgressors.
- 4th–15th c. (Late Antiquity & Middle Ages) — Adopted into ecclesiastical Latin to characterize the holy sacraments, episcopal offices, and sacred scriptures of the Church, signifying ultimate protection from secular interference.
- Late 16th to 17th c. (Renaissance & Early Modern English) — Borrowed into English directly from Latin to describe classical Roman republican liberties and divine religious ordinances.
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Era) — Secularized across political philosophy, diplomacy, and constitutional law, applied to sovereignty, international treaties, and human rights.
- 20th–21st c. — Ubiquitous in cultural criticism, academia, and journalism; frequently used with mild irony to describe dogmatic assumptions or institutional sacred cows that resist empirical scrutiny.
