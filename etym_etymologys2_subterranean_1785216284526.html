Root word SUBTERRANEAN is a compound of two Latin elements, so it has two derivation chains, each given in isolation.

CHAIN 1 — the prefix sub- "under":
1. PIE *upo "under, below, up from under" (prehistoric reconstruction, attested by many descendants).
2. Latin sub "under, below" (Classical, by regular *p → b voicing between vowels; attested 3rd c. BCE onward).
3. Latin subter "underneath, below" (formed from sub + old comparative suffix -ter-; attested 1st c. BCE).
4. Latin subterraneus "situated beneath the earth" (subter + terra "earth" + adjectival -aneus; attested 1st c. CE, e.g. in Pliny).
5. English SUBTERRANEAN "existing under the earth's surface" (early 17th c., direct learned Latin loan; first recorded c. 1611).

CHAIN 2 — the element terra "earth":
1. PIE *ters- "to dry" (prehistoric reconstruction; the root of Latin torrere "to parch", Greek tersomai "to become dry", OE þurst "thirst").
2. Latin terra "earth, dry land" (Classical; literally "the dry [land]", the firm ground as opposed to the sea; attested 3rd c. BCE).
3. Latin subterraneus "situated beneath the earth" (subter + terra + -aneus; the two chains join here; attested 1st c. CE).
4. English SUBTERRANEAN (early 17th c., direct learned loan).

Note on the debate: the branching forms are secure for Latin. The Old French souterrain (from Latin subterranus) was separately re-borrowed into English in the 18th c. as an archaeological term for an underground chamber; the two lines are historically distinct though related. The PIE reconstructions *upo and *ters- are standard but are reconstructions, not attested texts.
