• Lift: Similar because both mean moving something upward. Differs because lift is neutral and effortless-sounding, while heave implies great weight and straining effort, often with a sudden jerk.
  • Raise: Similar because both mean moving something up. Differs because raise is orderly and broad (raise a flag, raise a hand), with no sense of weight or strain; heave is always heavy and effortful.
  • Hoist: Similar because both mean hauling something heavy upward, often with ropes or machinery. Differs because hoist usually involves tackle, winches, or hoists; heave is the raw muscular pull of the body.
  • Haul: Similar because both involve pulling a heavy load, and “heave ho” is the hauling cry. Differs because haul stresses dragging or conveying over a distance, while heave stresses the upward, effortful motion itself.
  • Tug: Similar because both mean pulling with effort. Differs because tug implies short, repeated, jerky pulls; heave implies one long coordinated surge, and additionally carries all the breath, sea, and stomach senses.
  • Pull: Similar because both mean drawing something toward oneself. Differs because pull is the colourless general verb for any traction; heave is heavy, sudden, and upward in orientation.
  • Drag: Similar because both move heavy things with effort. Differs because drag means pulling along a surface with friction; heave lifts against gravity.
  • Wrench: Similar because both involve violent muscular effort. Differs because wrench stresses twisting or tearing free, while heave is a straight upward push or throw.
  • Yank: Similar because both are sudden forceful pulls. Differs because yank is quick, sharp, and usually one-handed; heave is slower, collective, and weight-driven.
  • Throw: Similar because both mean propelling something through the air. Differs because throw is the neutral everyday verb for any projection; heave is reserved for heavy objects and effortful, arcing throws.
  • Hurl: Similar because both mean throwing with force. Differs because hurl stresses violence and speed of the throw itself; heave stresses the weight of the object and the effort of the thrower.
  • Fling: Similar because both are energetic throws. Differs because fling suggests careless, sweeping abandon, while heave suggests heavy, deliberate effort.
  • Toss: Similar because both can mean throwing. Differs because toss is casual and light (toss a coin); heave is heavy and laboured.
  • Pitch: Similar because both can mean throwing or the rising-falling motion of a ship. Differs because pitch of a ship is fore-and-aft motion, while heave is the vertical rise and fall; as a throw, pitch suggests aiming, which heave lacks.
  • Cast: Similar because both are literary-formal words for throwing, and both are used of ships and eyes. Differs because cast is the wider word (moulds, actors, computing) and is no longer strictly heavy; heave keeps the weight.
  • Sling: Similar because both mean throwing with force. Differs because sling suggests a looping, swinging action; heave is a straight muscular thrust.
  • Chuck: Similar because both are informal words for throwing. Differs because chuck is casual and light-hearted; heave is effortful and could equally mean vomiting.
  • Lob: Similar because both are throws with an arcing path. Differs because lob is gentle, high, and deliberate; heave is strained and forceful.
  • Heft: Similar because both concern lifting something heavy (to heft a sack). Differs because heft means to test or carry weight, not to throw, and has no wave, breath, or vomit senses.
  • Retch: Similar because both name the spasm before vomiting. Differs because retch is exclusively the medical-bodily spasm, while heave is far wider and the vomiting sense is informal.
  • Vomit: Similar because both can mean throwing up. Differs because vomit is the clinical-crude term for the act, while heave is indirect, euphemistic, and still carries the lifting image.
  • Surge: Similar because both describe a powerful swelling or rising movement of water or feeling. Differs because surge is smooth, streaming, and directionally forward; heave is slower, massier, and vertical.
  • Swell: Similar because both name the rising of the sea or of bodily parts (a swelling chest). Differs because swell implies enlargement through internal pressure; heave implies rhythmic lifting motion.
  • Billow: Similar because both describe a large rising mass (billowing sails, heaving sea). Differs because billow is used of cloth, smoke, and clouds; heave is used of water, earth, and bodies.
  • Upheave: Similar because it is the same family — to heave up, especially of earth. Differs because upheave is archaic-literary and nearly always literal geology; heave is the ordinary word with the full range.
