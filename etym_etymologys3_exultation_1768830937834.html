The biography of exultation begins in a physical act. Its deep ancestor is the PIE root *sel-, which designated a quick, elastic spring of movement — a root whose reality is inferred from a spread of ancient cognates: Sanskrit sárati, “to run, to flow,” still showing the root as smooth gliding motion; Greek hállomai, “to leap,” preserving the bounding sense; and, most importantly for our word, Latin saliō, salīre, “to leap, to spring, to jump.” The Latin verb was a workhorse of classical poetry, used of athletes, dancers, horses, and the quick motion of anything alive. From it the language built the frequentative saltāre, “to leap about, to dance” — the -t- suffix marking repeated, habitual action — and the verb at the centre of our word, exsultāre, made by compounding ex- (“out, up from”) with saltāre: literally “to leap out,” then “to leap up again and again,” to bound and prance. The decisive semantic shift happened here, and it is the most interesting part of the story: a verb of purely physical motion became a verb of emotion, but of an emotion that remains physically manifested. Exultāre came to mean “to rejoice triumphantly” precisely because the joy it named could not stay inside the skin — the rejoicer leapt, bounded, and pranced. The body was the proof of the feeling.

Christian Latin then carried the verb into its most influential territory. The Vulgate — Jerome’s Latin Bible of the late 4th century — uses exsultāre for the rejoicing of the soul: exsultabo et laetabor, “I will exult and be glad” (Psalm 9:2); et exsultabit cor meum, “and my heart shall exult.” Through the liturgy, the psalmody, and centuries of devotional prose, exsultāre and its noun exsultātiō became the standard Christian vocabulary for religious joy — the joy of salvation, of deliverance, of the redeemed soul springing up toward God. It is important to see that this religious colouring was never a mystification of the physical sense; the Psalms themselves are full of leaping (the hills skip, the lame leap like a hart), so the biblical exultation kept its body.

Into Old French the Latin noun passed as exultation, and into Middle English as exultacioun (15th century) — the era of the word’s first recorded English life. The verb exult followed in the 16th century, and for the next two centuries exultation was the lofty, ceremonial member of the joy-family: the word of orators, poets, and scripture. Yet even in this period a medieval confusion haunted it. Latin had two near-homophone verbs — exsultāre, “to leap for joy,” and exaltāre, “to raise high,” from altus, “high” — and manuscript copyists and early English writers repeatedly ran them together, so that exultation was sometimes treated as a raising-up rather than a springing-up. The two are etymologically unrelated, and the confusion is now recognized as an error, but it left a trace in the way English speakers feel the word: exultation seems to lift and exalt the rejoicer even as he leaps.

By the 18th and 19th centuries the register had hardened. Exultation settled as the most Latinate, formal noun for triumphant joy, and dictionaries and stylists fixed its boundary against its neighbours: elation is the calm interior lifting of the spirit; jubilation is communal, carnivalesque celebrating; triumph is the public narrative of victory; exultation is the leaping, shouting cry of the individual victor. In the 20th and 21st centuries the word has become, in ordinary use, consciously archaic and literary — chosen precisely because it sounds classical, the loud upper register of the soul. It is rarely applied to everyday happiness; it is the word for victory cries, for sports commentators at the moment of triumph, for biblical rejoicing, and for prose writers who want the acoustics of the antique. That is exactly the register Jack Vance exploits in Tales of the Dying Earth, where the air of the world is an archive of sound and exultation is one of the distinct species of human utterance it has held — the shout of triumph, the acoustic signature of victory, the word that still carries, after two millennia, the bound and the prance of a body leaping for joy.
