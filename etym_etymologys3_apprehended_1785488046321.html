The story of APPREHENDED opens with the Proto-Indo-European root *gʰed-, "to take, to seize", whose most primitive application was the physical taking of something into the hand. The root is ancient and wide. In the Germanic branch it became *getaną "to obtain, to get", and it reached English twice: through Old Norse geta, giving modern get, and through Old English begetan, giving beget and the revealing sense-pair forget — literally "to lose hold of". In Greek the root yielded khandánō, "to hold, to contain". In Latin it gave the reduced stem *hendō, "to seize", a verb that survives only inside compounds, but that combined with prefixes to form one of the most productive verb families of the language.

The central Latin verb is prehendō, "to lay hold of", formed from prae-, "before", and *hendō. From prehendō Latin built a whole family by prefixation:

  • comprehendere, "to grasp together" — the mind takes hold of a thing wholly; English comprehend, comprehensive.
  • reprehendere, "to hold back, to blame, to reprove"; English reprehend and, via Latin reprehēnsio, reprimand.
  • apprehendere, "to take hold of, to seize, to arrest"; the direct ancestor of this card.
  • pre(he)nsio, "a seizing"; English prehension and, in zoology, prehensile — the monkey's tail that grasps.

Apprehendere itself is ad- "towards" plus prehendere, literally "to seize hold of". English borrowed apprehend in the late fourteenth century with two immediate senses: physically, "to arrest or take into custody", and mentally, "to lay hold of with the intellect, to perceive or understand". The abstract noun apprehension followed, carrying the same double meaning — "perception" and "arrest". The word family then widened through Old French: apprendre, "to learn" — literally "to take hold of knowledge" — gave English apprentice, the learner who is bound to a master; and the Romance verb apprendre is exactly the same hand-image, applied to the acquisition of skill.

The most interesting development is the noun's shift toward fear. From the medieval habit of "seizing upon" future outcomes in the mind, apprehension acquired the specialised sense "fearful anticipation, dread": to apprehend a danger is to catch hold of it in advance, and to catch hold of a future evil is to fear it. By the eighteenth century this sense was fully established, and it is today the everyday meaning of the noun — the queasy foreboding of something bad that may come. Modern English has thus kept the double life of the family: the police apprehend a fugitive, the mind apprehends a pattern, and the heart feels apprehension.

In the present sentence the author uses the mental, perceptual sense: patterns are apprehended "through ordinary habits of perception", that is, seized directly by natural cognition rather than by the trained, tradition-bound habits that govern metre. The muscular imagery of the original root remains alive: to apprehend a rhythm is to catch it, as a hand catches a falling object — a perfect contrast with the word's darker sibling, apprehension as anxiety, the mind's frightened grasp upon an imagined future. The persistence of this ancient hand-image across four thousand years, from a shepherd's grasping hand to a poet's perception of rhythm, is the whole biography of the word.
