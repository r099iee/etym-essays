mended — past tense and past participle of "mend": repaired, restored to sound condition, put back into working order; of a person or animal, restored to health, recovered from illness or injury. In the literary passage this card draws on — a wounded child cared for by a tavern keeper's wife, who is discharged from her room only once his body is functional — the word is brutally economical. The sentence runs "By the time he is mended he has no money to pay her and he leaves in the night," and the participle is chosen over its grander rivals with obvious deliberation. He is not "healed," a word that would invoke medicine, time, and narrative restoration; he is "mended," the verb one would use of a torn sleeve, a broken fence, a cracked pot. The effect is to treat the child's body as an object patched to working order — whole enough to leave, whole enough to be discharged from care — and then, in the same breath, to reveal that the restoration was economically worthless: mended, but penniless; restored, but only to the road and the riverbank. The tone is one of dry, stoic accounting: it domesticates a gunshot wound into a household repair, and the domestication is precisely what makes the passage so bleak. The word is richly polysemous, and its senses radiate outward from a single core image of restoring soundness:
  • To repair an object (primary concrete sense): to mend a torn sleeve, a broken fence, a cracked pot, a road, a net, a shoe. The object is made whole enough to serve again; the repair is usually homely, manual, and provisional rather than engineered.
  • To heal a living body (intransitive and passive): "his leg is mending," "he is on the mend," "the wound mended slowly." The body is treated as a thing that knits itself back together; the register is modest and non-technical, deliberately smaller than "heal."
  • To improve conduct or condition (figurative): "to mend one's ways" — to reform one's behaviour, amend one's life — and "to mend matters," to improve a bad situation. Here the object is moral rather than physical.
  • To repair relations (modern figurative): "to mend fences" — to restore goodwill after a quarrel, a phrase drawn from the rural repair of boundary walls and now standard in politics and family life.
  • To recover, generally (intransitive, of conditions): "the situation is mending," a course toward improvement; "the market is mending," a slow return to health in economic or social prose.
  • To add to, augment (archaic and dialect): in older usage "mend" could also mean to improve, enlarge, or advance ("mend one's pace," to quicken one's step) — a now-archaic sense that shows the verb's root breadth.
In register, "mend" occupies a deliberately modest middle. It is more colloquial and more affectionate than repair (the formal, mechanical term for machines and infrastructure), less casual than fix (the modern catch-all), and far smaller than restore (which implies returning something to its original, often pristine condition) or heal (which implies biological or spiritual renewal). Where patching is a temporary, visible, and partial repair, mending is a quieter, more thorough restoration of working order — the needle going in and out until the thing is whole enough to be used again. Its homeliness is not a weakness but its point: mending is the kind of repair that love, poverty, and care perform in silence, which is exactly why the passage uses it of a wounded child who has no money to pay for his own recovery.
