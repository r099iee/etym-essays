Racket (noun, also racquet in the sporting sense) has two distinct families of meaning, plus a homograph, and an exhaustive account must keep them separate. 1. The noise sense (the core of this note). A racket is a loud, confused, and disagreeable noise — a clatter or din of many sounds striking together — and, by extension, any boisterous disturbance, uproar, or noisy fuss that disturbs the peace. The word carries a distinctly reproachful colouring from its first recorded uses in the 1560s, where it meant riotous merrymaking and noisy frolic: to call something a racket is to complain of it as needlessly, irritatingly, and even contemptibly loud. The sense extends to any sustained objectionable clamour — a party next door, a clattering workshop, a squad of heavily booted travellers — and it is always a judgment on the sound rather than a neutral description of it. 2. The criminal sense. In twentieth-century American English, racket acquired the meaning of a dishonest scheme or fraudulent business, most notoriously the "protection racket", in which money is extorted in exchange for "protection". The semantic bridge runs through the older colloquial sense of racket as "a busy line of work or way of life" — a word for the whole noisy, money-making business of something — which American slang narrowed and criminalised in the early twentieth century. From it English formed the agent noun racketeer (1920s, with the suffix -eer on the pattern of buccaneer and pioneer), the verb to racketeer, and the noun racketeering, which passed into international legal and journalistic usage. In this register "racket" can still be used neutrally for an occupation ("what's your racket?") and, in the phrase "the whole racket", for the whole affair of anything. 3. The verbal sense. To racket (verb) means to make a racket, or to live a noisy, gay, dissipated social life; the -ing form racketing appears in the same colloquial register, and the verb is recorded from the late 17th century. The phrase "to kick up a racket" (make a noisy disturbance) is 18th-century, and "to stand the racket" (to pay the cost, take the consequences) is also traditional English idiom. 4. The homograph — the sporting implement. The racket or racquet of tennis, squash, badminton, and racquetball is a completely separate word with no etymological connection to the noise sense: it descends from Arabic rāḥat "palm of the hand" (the earliest tennis implement was literally the gloved hand), through Old French rachete and Middle English raket. It shares only the spelling and pronunciation. Modern usage favours racquet in formal sporting contexts and racket in general use. In the passage from The Hobbit, chapter 2, the word appears in Bilbo's private grumbling as the company tramps along the road: he "had sniffed more than once at what he called 'all this dwarvish racket,'" even though the narrator assures us that no ordinary passer-by "would have noticed anything at all on a windy night, not if the whole cavalcade had passed two feet off." The word is doubly significant. First, it voices Bilbo's comic exasperation: the respectable, quiet-loving hobbit regards the dwarves' singing, jingling gear, and heavy boots as an affront of noisy indiscretion, a breach of his own beloved hush. Second, the narrator's aside reveals that the "racket" exists largely in Bilbo's hyper-sensitive hobbit hearing; what is to a human ear nothing at all is to him a clatter worthy of repeated complaint. The word thus performs both a character sketch and a quiet joke about relative perception, establishing the hobbit's sheltered, hearth-bound sensibilities against the dwarves' blithe and public noisiness. Key distinctions from near-synonyms:
  • Noise: similar because both are audible sound; differs because "noise" is the neutral, all-purpose term for any sound, while "racket" is noise judged objectionable, carrying impatience and distaste.
  • Din: similar because both are loud, unpleasant sounds; differs because "din" stresses prolonged, deafening, overwhelming volume, while a racket can be a smaller but still irritating clatter.
  • Clamour: similar because both are loud confused outcries; differs because "clamour" suggests shouting or urgent outcry, while "racket" is the clash of many inanimate sounds.
  • Hubbub: similar because both are noisy disturbances; differs because "hubbub" is a confused murmur of many voices, while "racket" is boisterous, clattering, and reproachfully loud.
  • Commotion: similar because both are noisy disturbances; differs because "commotion" emphasises the general agitation and movement, while "racket" emphasises the sound itself.
  • Uproar: similar because both are violent noisy disturbances; differs because "uproar" is larger in scale and often a crowd's roar, while a racket may be a smaller but still offensive clatter.
  • Clatter: similar because both are sounds of things striking together; differs because "clatter" is a looser, hard, knocking sound, while "racket" is a judgment on overall objectionable noise.
  • Rumpus: similar because both are noisy disturbances; differs because "rumpus" is comic and domestic in register, while "racket" is more general and reproachful.
  • Pandemonium: similar because both are wild disorder and noise; differs because "pandemonium" is total, chaotic uproar, far larger in scale than a mere racket.
