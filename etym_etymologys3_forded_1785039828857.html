The preterite forded is the past tense of the denominal verb "to ford," built on the Old English noun ford, "a shallow place where water can be crossed" — and that noun is one of the oldest and most wide-branching inheritances of the Indo-European family, the root *per-, "to lead over, to pass through, to cross." Its suffixed form *prtu-, literally "a going, a passage," lies beneath an astonishing set of words for passages of every kind. Latin turned it into portus "harbour" — the place where ships pass in — whence English port, airport, and seaport; into porta "gate," the passage through a wall, source of portal and porch; and into portare "to carry," the verb of transporting things across, behind porter, portable, report, import, and export. The root per "through" itself gives English per and persist. Greek contributed poros "passage, way," which via Latin porus yields our pore and porous, and peran "beyond." In the Indo-Iranian branch the root names exactly the thing our verb does: Sanskrit para- "the far bank, the opposite shore" and pára- "beyond" give Hindi pa:r (पार) "across, the other side of a river" and Odia para "the other side," while Avestan peretu- "ford" survives in the very name of the Euphrates, "good to cross over."

Phonological development.

  • PIE *per- to Proto-Germanic *furdu-: under Grimm's Law the initial *p- becomes Germanic *f-; the *-tu- suffix (a nominaliser of the root) yields *-du- through the Verner's Law voicing of the medial consonant in certain positions, giving *furdu-.
  • *furdu- to Old English ford: Germanic *u- vocalism and *-rd- cluster; the West Germanic forms level to ford, furt, forda.
  • Old English ford to Modern English ford: the noun is essentially unchanged in form for over a thousand years; only the vowel is modernised to /ɔː/.
  • Old Norse fjörðr: the Norse cognate developed the meaning "inlet, firth" — for the seafaring Vikings the sea itself was the highway — and was borrowed into English as fjord in the 19th century.

Semantic and cultural development.

  • Old English: ford is an ancient Germanic noun for the shallow place where a stream could be crossed on foot; in a land without bridges, the ford was a daily feature of roads and travel, fixed into place-names like Oxford, Stratford, and Hereford, and remembered in the old road system that linked the fords.
  • Middle English: the verb "to ford" is formed from the noun and stays a workaday verb — armies, traders, and pilgrims ford rivers; the ford is a recognized point of passage on every route; fordian is attested in Old English as the denominal verb.
  • 16th–17th centuries: the modern verb to ford is re-formed and recorded (1610s), along with fordable, "able to be forded," as the word passes into the vocabulary of travel and geography.
  • 18th–19th centuries: as turnpikes, bridges, and ferries multiply, fording recedes from ordinary travel, and the verb begins to sound rustic, historical, or colonial — the term of pioneers and wilderness narrative rather than of the modern road.
  • Contemporary: forded is archaic and consciously evocative — it belongs to adventure, saga, and historical prose; its lingering weight is carried by the old names in the landscape that keep the memory of the shallow crossing alive.

Literary life. Fords are landmarks of epic and romance: from the biblical fords of the Jordan to the Welsh fords of Arthurian legend, the shallow crossing has been the meeting place of armies and of fates. In the travel-narrative line in which the company "forded a river at a wide shallow place full of the noise of stones and foam," the verb preserves the ancient geography of the journey: the businesslike, physical act of taking the route the river permits, the gravel underfoot and the foam at the ankles, recorded with the settled finality of a completed crossing. The word, like the ford itself, is a passage — from the PIE root for leading over, through the Germanic noun and its denominal verb, to the old names in the English landscape that keep the memory alive.
