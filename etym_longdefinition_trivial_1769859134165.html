trivial is an adjective meaning "of little value or importance; minor, insignificant, commonplace, or lightweight; not worth serious attention." In the specific literary context of the passage provided — a long-lived narrator describing the first motorcar he ever saw — the word carries a tone of dry, whimsical understatement: "a new thing is always interesting, in its trivial fashion." The narrator, an ancient being who has lived for over a century and imagines he knows every species of local fauna, treats the astonishing new machine not as a miracle of technology but as a minor, amusing novelty. "Trivial" here means modest, unassuming, and charmingly unimportant — interesting despite (or because of) being a small matter. The register is ironic, light-hearted, and faintly condescending, in keeping with the speaker's vast age and experience.

Primary sense. The core meaning is lack of significance: a trivial matter is one that does not merit serious attention, effort, or concern. The word shades from "small and unimportant" toward "superficial" and "commonplace," and is frequently used dismissively ("a trivial objection," "trivial details").

Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Of little value or importance (dominant sense). "A trivial matter," "trivial details," "a trivial complaint." The dominant modern use, dismissive or gently mocking.
  • Commonplace, ordinary, everyday (archaic). In the 16th–17th centuries trivial meant "of the common sort," "belonging to the public road or marketplace" — the opposite of distinguished. This sense survives in the etymology and in older literature.
  • Technical (mathematics, logic, computer science). A "trivial solution," "trivial proof," or "trivial case" is one that is self-evident, elementary, or degenerate — so obvious it needs no effort (e.g. x = 0 as a trivial solution of an equation). A precise, value-neutral technical sense.
  • Trivia (back-formation noun). Bits of miscellaneous, unimportant information, and the genre of quiz game that tests them. The noun was revived in the early 20th century from Latin trivia "crossroads" (plural of trivium), then popularized globally by the game "Trivial Pursuit" (1981).
  • Trivial name (chemistry, taxonomy). A non-systematic, everyday name for a substance or organism, as opposed to its systematic technical name (e.g. "salt" versus "sodium chloride").
  • Historical and academic: of the trivium. In medieval universities the trivium was the elementary division of the seven liberal arts (grammar, rhetoric, logic); "trivial" thus once meant "of the elementary curriculum" — foundational but humble, a sense that fed the modern meaning of "minor."

Register, collocation, and grammar. Trivial is a gradable adjective. Common collocations: "trivial matter," "trivial detail," "trivial objection," "trivial pursuit," "seemingly trivial," "trivial complaint," "trivial in the extreme." It is frequent in dismissive and self-deprecating contexts ("It's only trivial — don't worry") and in academic English, where "trivial" has a precise technical antonym ("non-trivial"). The register runs from casual speech through literary irony to scientific and mathematical prose.
