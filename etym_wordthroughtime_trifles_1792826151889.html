- c. 1300 — idle tales and mockery: trifle first names false stories and foolish talk, words that signify nothing.
- 14th–15th c. — worthless things in general: from empty talk to empty things, any matter of no value becomes a trifle, and to occupy oneself with such is to trifle.
- 16th c. — the dessert appears: a light frothy dish of cream, cake, and wine takes the name, either as a small easy thing or as cream whipped into a froth out of nothing.
- 17th c. — Shakespeare fixes the moral sense: trifles light as air become confirmations strong as proofs of holy writ, and to trifle with love or honour becomes dangerous play.
- 18th–19th c. — polite dismissal: a trifle softens into a quantifier, a trifle tired, a trifle long, the drawing-room way of minimising without offence.
- Today — the double edge survives: trifles still dismisses with superiority, yet literature from Shakespeare to Tolkien keeps warning that trifles dismissed by the great prove fatal to the small.
