endowed is the past participle of the verb to endow, which English borrowed in the fourteenth century from Old French and Anglo-Norman endouer, formed from the prefix en- ("in, into") and douer, "to provide with a dowry or marriage portion". Behind douer stands Latin dōtāre, "to portion, to give a dowry", derived from dōs (genitive dōtis), "dowry, marriage gift", which itself descends from the verb dare, "to give", and ultimately from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *deh₃-, "to give".

The deep root and its wide family. The PIE root *deh₃- is one of the most widely distributed "give" roots in the Indo-European world: it appears in Sanskrit dā- and dadāti "he gives" and Hindi dān "gift, alms, charity"; in Greek dídōmi "I give" (whence dōron "gift" and Dositheus, "gift of God"); in Old Irish dán "gift, art, craft"; in Lithuanian dúoti "to give"; in Old Church Slavonic dati; and in the Latin family of dare, dōnum (whence donation, donor), datum (whence data), and dōs/dōtis, "dowry". A common etymological misconception must be noted: English give does NOT belong to this family — it descends from a different PIE root, *gʰeh₁bʰ- (compare Latin habeō "to have"), so the story of endowed is entirely a Latin-Romance story, not a Germanic one.

From dowry to endowment. The decisive concept is the dowry. Latin dōs was the portion a bride carried into marriage — a settled gift of property intended to support the new household and, in the Roman view, to give it a proper foundation. Dōtāre, "to endow", was to furnish a bride with this portion. When the word passed into Old French as douer and then, with en-, into endouer, it carried exactly this legal and financial content: to endow was to settle property upon someone or something — a woman on her marriage, a church with lands, a monastery with an income — in permanence.

Life in English.

  • 14th–15th centuries. The first English uses of endowen are legal and financial: to endow a bride with her portion, to endow a college, chantry, or hospital with lands and rents. The word belongs to the language of property and settlement, and endowed means "provided with a settled income for permanent support".
  • 16th–17th centuries — the figurative extension. The great semantic step occurs when the word extends from money to qualities. To be "endowed with" intelligence, courage, patience, or grace is to receive from nature or fortune a portion as lasting and as valuable as any dowry. The image of the settled gift, given to last, is carried over entire. This is the sense in which Shakespeare and his contemporaries speak of men "endowed with" talents, and in which the word enters moral and theological prose — endowment as the gift of God or of nature.
  • 18th–19th centuries — the dignified formal use. The word flourishes in biography, moral philosophy, and institutional discourse. Mill writes in his Autobiography that he "was endowed with that intense mental energy which is so often combined with physical weakness" — a compensating gift of nature, permanent in his character, set against the bodily frailty of a lifetime. In the same era, the financial sense consolidates: an endowed school, an endowed hospital, an endowed chair are institutions whose capital supports them in perpetuity. The two senses are now firmly parallel.
  • 20th–21st centuries. The double life persists unchanged: institutional and financial ("an endowed chair", "a richly endowed library", "the university's endowment") and personal and figurative ("endowed with patience", "endowed with a remarkable voice"). The word remains formal and dignified, and its root idea — what is endowed is given to last — is still alive in both halves.

Sound and form. Latin dōtāre (doh-TAH-reh) became Old French douer by regular loss of the medial syllable and diphthongisation of the long ō; the prefixed endouer entered Middle English as endowen and settled to endow, with the participle endowed attested from the fifteenth century. The RP pronunciation /ɛndˈaʊd/ retains the French -ou- (now -ow-) of the medial vowel. The family includes endowment, endower, and the related dowry, dower, and dowager — all from the same Latin dōs, "dowry".

Socio-cultural meaning. The history of the word traces the shift from a world of marriage settlements and church endowments to a world of universities and foundations: the dowry that founded a household became the endowment that founds a chair. Through every stage the word has kept its defining connotation of permanence and generosity — a gift that is meant to outlast the giver, whether it is a fortune settled on a college or a talent conferred on a mind.
