- c. 1400 — "stocky" is formed from "stock" (a tree trunk or stump cut close to the ground) plus the adjectival -y; its first recorded senses describe things — short and thick, stump-like, set solid like a cut trunk.
- 16th–17th c. — the word transfers from wood to bodies: a person is "stocky" who is short and solidly built, thick in the trunk and broad across the chest; it stays plain and descriptive, carrying none of the contempt of "squat" or "dumpy."
- 19th c. — in an age of physical labour "stocky" becomes a working-man's word — sailors, pitmen, farmers and soldiers described as stocky are compact, dense and built to bear weight, rooted like a thick tree-stock; the tone is neutral-to-favourable.
- Contemporary — "stocky" holds its sturdy, well-proportioned register in sport and everyday speech: a "stocky build" (rugby forwards, boxers, wrestlers) is powerful rather than graceful and still suggests a body made for carrying weight, the dignity of the stump intact.
