• Fool: Similar because both name a witless or silly person; differs because fool is the broad timeless word (court fool to April fool), while loon is warmer slang with a whiff of the wild bird's cry in it.
  • Lunatic: Similar because modern loon often means a crazy person; differs because lunatic is clinical and originally lunar-medical, while loon grew from Scots rogue-words and only later leaned on lunatic by sound.
  • Loony: Similar because both mean madcap or eccentric; differs because loony is the outright clipped adjective from lunatic (loony tunes, loony bin), while loon is the older independent noun the bird and the rogue share.
  • Madman: Similar because both can describe a crazed fellow; differs because madman is stark and sometimes fearful, while loon is usually comic and harmless.
  • Simpleton: Similar because both mock weak wits; differs because simpleton stresses born silliness and pity, while loon stresses odd behaviour and high spirits.
  • Clown: Similar because both play the laughing-stock or make laughter; differs because a clown performs folly deliberately (or professionally), while a loon simply cannot help it.
  • Rascal: Similar because Scots loun began as exactly this, an idle rogue; differs because rascal kept its roguish charm, while loon drifted toward daftness and the bird.
  • Diver: Similar because the British diver is the American loon, the same genus Gavia; differs because diver describes the bird's habit neutrally, while loon carries the Norse lameness-name and, in America, all the wilderness romance.
  • Grebe: Similar because both are fish-diving waterbirds of northern lakes; differs because grebes are smaller, with lobed toes and ornate ruffs, while loons are heavy dagger-billed divers with the famous night wail.
