MERCIA — single chain. PIE *merǵ- 'boundary, border, edge' (reconstructed; attested as Latin margō 'edge, border', 1st c. BCE-1st c. CE; Sanskrit maryā 'boundary, limit', Vedic; Old Irish mruig 'borderland') -> Proto-Germanic *markō 'boundary, borderland, frontier district' (reconstructed; cf. Old Norse mǫrk 'forest, borderland' as in Danmǫrk 'Denmark') -> Old English mearc 'boundary, limit, frontier, mark' (attested, 8th-11th c.) and Old English Mierce / Myrce 'the border-people, the Mercians' (plural ethnonym, attested in Anglo-Saxon sources; the i-mutation of mearc shows the derived adjective/tribal formation) -> Latinized Mercia 'the land/kingdom of the border-people' (medieval scribal Latinization, 9th-12th c.; used in charters and by later historians) -> English Mercia (adopted by English historians from the 16th c. onwards; as in Macaulay, 19th c.). NOTE ON THE CHAIN: The first stage is a standard PIE reconstruction; the reflex family (margin, march, mark, marquis) is one of the best-attested border-word families in Indo-European. Exactly which frontier the Mercians named themselves from (Welsh frontier vs. a boundary within the English settlement) is debated by historians, but the sense 'border-people' is secure.
