- Middle English blot (noun, c. 1300): "a stain, spot" — above all an ink stain on parchment in the scriptorium; origin obscure, possibly Old Norse. Connotation: physical blemish, scribal error.
- Early 15th c.: verb blotten "to stain, spot, defile"; to blot writing = to cancel it by covering it with ink. Connotation: correction, deletion.
- 16th–17th c.: "blot out" becomes the great biblical phrase — "blot out my transgressions" (Psalm 51:1, KJV 1611), names blotted from the book of life (Revelation 3:5); figuratively "a blot on his name" = disgrace. Connotation: divine erasure, honour and shame.
- 16th–19th c.: blotting paper (16th c.) makes "blot" an everyday schoolroom and office word; "blotted" = smudged with ink, spoiled. Connotation: mundane, domestic.
- Modern: "blotted out" = obliterated, swallowed by darkness (Tolkien: "Now it was lost and gone, blotted in the dark"); in biology the blot is a laboratory technique (Southern blot, 1975). Connotation: total disappearance; technical jargon.
