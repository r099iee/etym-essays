PINED (verb, past tense and past participle of pine; RP /p'a'nd/). The word means suffered from intense longing, grief, or confinement — to have wasted away under the pressure of desire, sorrow, or captivity; to have grown thin and faint with yearning or homesickness. In the opening of Wordsworth's Prelude the poet, escaped from "the vast city," says he "long had PINED" there, and the following line identifies him as "a discontented sojourner": the city years were a slow wasting, a longing for freedom and nature that ate at him like a low fire. Pined holds two senses in a single word — the active ache of wanting and the passive decline of the body that the wanting causes — and the Romantic line depends on both at once: the prisoner pines for what he lacks, and the pining itself consumes him until he is "now free." The primary sense is to suffer intense longing, usually for an absent person, place, or thing, with the implication that the longing takes a physical toll. One pines for a homeland in exile, pines for a lost love, pines for freedom when confined. The word almost always carries the collocation pine for (the object of longing) or pine away (the physical decline): "she pined for her homeland," "he pined away after her death." An older, transitive sense — to afflict with torment — survives only in archaic use; its descendant is the modern intransitive verb. The full semantic range of pined / pine radiates around the image of consuming longing:
  • Intense longing: to feel a deep, painful desire for an absent person, home, or thing — "the caged bird pined for the open skies," "he pined for his homeland during years of exile." This is the core modern sense; the object is always something one does not have.
  • Wasting away with grief or confinement: to decline physically and spiritually under sorrow, longing, or imprisonment — "the plant withered and pined in the dark corner," "she pined away." The body itself is described as shrinking and losing vigor, as if consumed.
  • Lovesickness: in literary use, the specific pining of the lover separated from the beloved — the medieval and Renaissance "malady" of lovers who sicken and fade with desire. This is one of the word's oldest literary roles.
  • Homesickness and exile: pining for one's native place — the emotion of the displaced, the refugee, the prisoner, the sailor. The Wordsworthian "long had pined" in the city is homesickness for nature and liberty.
  • Archaic transitive "to torment": the oldest recorded sense (from Old English pinian "to torture, afflict") survives in archaisms such as "to pine a criminal" or the passive "pined by grief." The modern word has almost entirely shed this sense, but it explains the verb's violent ancestry.
  • Withering of living things: applied to plants and creatures, to waste and droop for want of nourishment, light, or freedom — "the seedling pined in the shade." A natural transfer of the human wasting to the vegetable and animal worlds.
The semantic bridge that explains the range is etymological: the verb descends from Old English pinian "to torment, torture, afflict," which itself comes from the noun pin "pain, torment," a borrowing of Latin poena "punishment, penalty." To pine is, at the root, to be tormented — and the modern sense transfers that torment from external torture to the internal torture of desire and grief. The longing does to the sufferer what a jailer's cruelty does to a prisoner: it wastes the flesh, dims the spirit, and consumes the self. That is why pined can mean both "longed intensely" and "wasted away": the two are one action seen from the side of the emotion and the side of the body. There is a persistent and instructive confusion with the pine tree, which must be set aside: the tree's name (from Latin pinus) is etymologically unrelated to this verb. The vivid image of a tree "pining away" is a folk etymology — the verb's wasting sense has nothing to do with the evergreen, and the two words merely converge on the spelling pine. In register, pined is a literary, archaic, and elegiac word. Ordinary speech prefers longed, yearned, or missed; but pined carries a physical weight and an old-fashioned music that no plain synonym matches, which is why poets reach for it in passages of exile, loss, and yearning. Its past form, pined, keeps that softness especially — a grief-verb whose very etymology (punishment, blood-price) connects longing to the deepest registers of human loss, and whose use in Wordsworth's Prelude turns a sentence about a city into a sentence about the whole ache of the spirit in captivity.
