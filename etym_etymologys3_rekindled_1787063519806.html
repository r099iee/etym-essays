A fire word with two parents. Rekindled is a composite: the English verb kindle, "to light, set fire to", plus the Latin prefix re-, "again, back, anew". To tell its story is to tell the history of fire-and-passion metaphor in English, from the cold hearths of the Viking North to the rhetorical hearths of Romantic and modern prose.

The deep roots of kindle. The verb kindle entered English around the year 1200 as cundlen, borrowed from Old Norse kynda, "to set fire to, to kindle". Old Norse itself continues a Proto-Germanic verb kundijaną, built on a Germanic base kind- that many scholars connect — through the standard PIE reconstructions of Pokorny and Watkins — with the root *kand-, "to glow, shine, give light". That same root underlies Latin candēre "to shine, be white-hot" (source of candle, candid, incandescent) and Sanskrit cand- "to shine, appear bright". The Germanic connection is, however, debated: some handbooks treat kind- as isolated within Germanic and resist the link to *kand-. What is certain is the sense: from the very first attestations the word is about fire — about the striking of sparks and the coaxing of flame. The frequentative suffix -le (Old Norse kindla) reinforces the iterative, effortful image of repeatedly striking and blowing a fire into life.

Kindle in English before the prefix. In Middle English, cundlen / kindelen meant "to set on fire, cause to burn" and, very early, also "to be set on fire, to blaze up". Already in medieval usage the figurative leap was made: Wycliffe's Bible and Middle English sermons use kindle of wrath, love, and zeal — a burning heart was a passionate heart long before the Romantics. By the 16th century kindle was fully at home in both worlds: a wood fire kindles, and a quarrel kindles.

The birth of rekindle. English began prefixing re- to kindle in the mid-16th century, producing rekindle, "to kindle again, to relight". At first the word was entirely literal: a fire that had gone out could be rekindled. The prefix re- (from Latin, via French, and already native in English formations) contributed its basic value of "again" — with the crucial presupposition that the fire, or the passion, had existed before and had died down. That presupposition of a prior state is the semantic engine of the whole word family: rekindled love is love that once was; rekindled hope is hope that had faded.

  • 16th c.: literal relighting; earliest figurative stirrings in religious writing — "rekindle thy zeal".
  • 17th-18th c.: the fire = passion equation becomes standard rhetoric; poets and preachers rekindle love, hatred, war, and piety.
  • 19th c.: Romanticism makes rekindled a staple of emotional description — memory, romance, and religious fervour are rekindled in novels and verse.
  • 20th-21st c.: journalism generalises it to public life — rekindled debates, controversies, and diplomatic tensions; the digital age rekindles old friendships and old quarrels alike, and the word retains its warm flame imagery throughout.

Phonetic and orthographic notes. The verb is stressed on the second syllable in RP: /ˌriːˈkɪndəl/. The prefix is pronounced long (ree-) in formal speech, though in rapid everyday speech it may reduce. The participle rekindled is /ˌriːˈkɪndəld/ (the -le syllabic and the final past-tense -d pronounced together). Spelling has been stable since the 17th century; the old alternative re-kindled with hyphen survives only in careful archaic typography.

Semantic mechanics of the revival sense. Why did a fire word become the leading term for reviving emotions? The bridge is the metaphorical identification of passion with heat and flame, already present in ancient poetry (the "flame" of love, the "fires" of anger) and reinforced in English by a dense network: inflame, burning desire, ardour (from Latin ardēre "to burn"), smoulder, fire up. Rekindle occupies a precise niche within this network: unlike ignite or inflame, which begin something new, rekindle insists on a prior flame. That semantic presupposition — something valuable existed, cooled, and can be restored — makes it the natural word for hope, romance, friendship, and curiosity, all of which are assumed to have burned before.

Socio-cultural trajectory. The word's rise tracks the emotional vocabulary of public life. In the 16th and 17th centuries it served sermons and political pamphlets. The 19th-century novel adopted it for the psychology of attachment and regret. The 20th century turned it into a headline word — wars and peace-processes, strikes and reconciliations are endlessly "rekindled" or rekindled afresh. In the 21st century the word has found a new domestic home in discussions of relationships and personal growth, while retaining enough warmth to remain distinctly literary. Tolkien's usage in The Hobbit — "had rekindled all the fire of their dwarvish hearts" — is a perfect specimen of the modern figurative sense applied to an ancient passion.

Conclusion. Rekindled began as a plain literal verb for relighting fires and has become one of English's most expressive words for revival. Its meaning has not drifted far — the flame is still at the centre — but its referent has moved from the hearth to the heart, and from the hearthside of the 16th century to the headlines and friendships of the 21st.
