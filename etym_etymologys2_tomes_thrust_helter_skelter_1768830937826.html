CHAIN 1 FOR TOMES: PIE *temh₁- — "to cut" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) ↓ Greek τέμνω temnō — "to cut" (attested from Homer, c. 8th c. BCE) ↓ Greek τόμος tómos — "a slice, a cut-off piece; a section of a written work; a volume" (attested 5th c. BCE; Aristotle uses tomos for parts of treatises) ↓ Latin tomus — "a volume, a cut section of a work" (borrowed from Greek; attested Imperial Latin, c. 1st–4th c. CE) ↓ Middle French tome — "a volume" (attested 13th–16th c.) ↓ English tome — "a volume, a large scholarly book" (attested 16th c., c. 1570s) ↓ Modern English tomes — plural of tome, "large, heavy books" (present day) CHAIN 2 FOR THRUST: PIE *treud- — "to press, push, thrust" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE; cf. Latin trūdō "to push, thrust," attested Classical Latin) ↓ Proto-Germanic *þrustijaną — "to press, thrust, force" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE–200 CE) ↓ Old Norse þrýsta — "to press, thrust, force" (attested; Viking Age, c. 9th–13th c.) ↓ Middle English thristen / thrusten — "to press, push, thrust, stab" (attested c. 1200, 13th c.) ↓ Modern English thrust — "to push suddenly and forcibly; (noun) a propulsive force or the central point of an argument" (present day) CHAIN 3 FOR HELTER-SKELTER: Dialectal English skelter — "to scurry, dash, hurry, scamper" (the earliest recorded element; origin uncertain, probably echoic; attested in dialect records) ↓ Rhyming reduplication helter-skelter — "in headlong, confused, disorderly haste" (first recorded 1593) ↓ Modern English helter-skelter — adverb and adjective of chaotic haste; (noun, 20th c.) a spiral fairground slide (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE: For THRUST, the Old Norse verb's internal derivation is not fully certain, and the connection to PIE *treud- (supported by Latin trūdō and Germanic cognates such as Old English þrēotan "to weary") is accepted by most handbooks but involves a reconstructed Germanic intermediate whose precise form is debated; the Norse-to-English steps are securely attested. For HELTER-SKELTER, no PIE or even Germanic reconstruction applies — the word is a native English rhyming formation of the late 16th century built on a dialectal base of obscure, probably imitative origin, so the chain is deliberately short and no pre-Germanic stage exists or is claimed.
