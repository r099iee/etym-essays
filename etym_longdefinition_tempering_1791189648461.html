Tempering is the present participle of the verb temper, meaning in metallurgy to harden and toughen metal (especially steel) by controlled heating and cooling, and more generally to moderate, soften, or bring into due proportion by mixing or restraint.

The technical metallurgical sense is primary in your context: after softening metal by heating, the smith tempers it — reheats quenched steel and cools it again — to reduce brittleness while preserving hardness, producing a blade or tool that is both strong and supple rather than shatteringly hard or uselessly soft. Tempered steel, tempered glass, and tempered chocolate all share this logic of controlled thermal treatment producing resilient finish.

  • Metallurgical hardening (core technical sense): Heating quenched steel to a specific temperature and cooling to relieve stresses — e.g., tempering blades, tempering springs, oil-tempering, temper colours. Antonym chain: annealing softens, quenching hardens abruptly, tempering toughens by moderated reheat.
  • Moderating or mitigating (general sense): Softening severity with mildness — e.g., tempering justice with mercy, tempering criticism with praise, tempering expectations. Implies wisdom that avoids extremes without abandoning firmness.
  • Mixing to right consistency (older craft sense): Bringing clay, mortar, or paint to workable state by mixing, moistening, kneading — e.g., tempering clay, tempering mortar. Still used in pottery and masonry.
  • Tuning and attuning (musical and psychological sense): Adjusting pitch or mood to proper level — e.g., tempered scale in music (equal temperament), well-tempered character, tempering enthusiasm. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier is the famous monument to this sense.

Polysemy is unified by Latin temperare: to mix in due proportion. Whether mixing hot and cold, hard and soft, severe and merciful, or high and low pitch, tempering finds the usable middle. Paradoxically it can mean both hardening (tempering steel) and softening (tempering anger) because both move an extreme toward balanced fitness.

  • Grammar note: As participle-adjective, tempering qualifies process (tempering furnace, tempering colours); as gerund-noun, tempering names the process itself (tempering takes an hour). Your blank — softening them, tempering them — is finite verb parallel to softening: he was tempering them.
  • Domain usages: In materials science, tempering has precise temperature-colour charts; in cooking, tempering chocolate or spices means controlled heating for texture; in law and ethics, tempering means mitigating rigour with equity; in music, temperament means tuning system.
  • Collocations: tempering steel, tempering glass, tempering chocolate, tempering justice with mercy, tempering expectations, tempered optimism, well-tempered, ill-tempered, bad-tempered.

In short, tempering is the art of the due middle — using fire, mixture, or mercy to make hardness usable and strength supple.
