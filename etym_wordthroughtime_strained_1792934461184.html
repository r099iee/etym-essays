- Latin roots — drawn tight: stringere, to bind and press, gives Old French the verb that will cross the Channel.
- c. 1300 — pulling and pressing: Middle English streinen means to draw tight, to press, to force — ropes, wine-presses, and arguments alike.
- 16th c. — the injured body: athletes and labourers give medicine strained muscles and backs, tension turning into diagnosis.
- 17th–18th c. — filtering and forcing: cooks strain broth through cloth while divines strain at gnats and courtiers strain compliments — the sieve and the effort side by side.
- 19th c. — society stretches: novels discover strained relations and strained silences, carrying the taut rope into drawing rooms and diplomacy.
- Today — the universal participle: from physiotherapy to foreign ministries, strained names anything pulled to its limit — with the hobbits' listening ears as its most beloved literary instance.
