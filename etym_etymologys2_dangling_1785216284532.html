The etymology of DANGLING is short, obscure, and thoroughly northern; no Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for it. Danish dangle "to hang loosely, swing about" / Norwegian dangla "to hang loosely, swing about" (Scandinavian; ultimate origin unknown — the word is attested in the Scandinavian languages of the medieval and early modern period, but no older Germanic or Indo-European ancestor is securely reconstructable) → Early Modern English dangle "to hang loosely and sway with one's own weight" (intransitive, borrowed c. 1590; the OED's first citations date from the 1590s, in the period of intensified English maritime and trading contact with the North) → Early Modern English dangle, transitive sense "to let (something) hang loosely" (early 17th century) → English dangling (present participle, formed with -ing, attested from c. 1600; also the adjective "hanging loosely," and later the figurative "held out as a temptation," 19th century) → English dangling (present participle used in the Hobbit passage: "and he dangled his large feet over the side"). NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: the ultimate origin of the Scandinavian verb is uncertain. A connection to Old Norse dengja "to strike, hammer" has been proposed — on the theory that a hammer swings and strikes — but the dictionaries record it as unproven, and many scholars suspect the word is ultimately imitative or expressive, the repeated -ng- echoing the pendular sway of the thing it names. No Proto-Indo-European root is forced, and none should be invented. The English word is a loan from the North Germanic languages; it is not descended from Old English.
