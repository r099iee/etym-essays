Cuneiform is, in its primary sense, the writing system of ancient Mesopotamia in which scribes pressed a cut reed stylus into soft clay to leave wedge-shaped marks, used for some three thousand years to record Sumerian, Akkadian, Elamite, Hittite, Hurrian, and Old Persian, and preserved on hundreds of thousands of tablets that carry laws, letters, accounts, omens, epics, and star-tables.

In the source sentence the word appears inside a catalogue of non-alphabetic scripts: work on orality has contrasted speech with alphabetic writing rather than with other systems such as cuneiform, Chinese characters, the Japanese syllabary, and Maya script. Here it stands for the great alternative literacies, a reminder that writing is not one thing and that the alphabet is only one late member of a large family.

Core meaning in detail: Early Sumerian pictographs of about 3400 BCE were drawn with a pointed tool; around 2600 BCE scribes began rotating the stylus and pressing its rectangular edge, so curved pictures collapsed into arrangements of wedges — verticals, horizontals, diagonals, and the characteristic nail-heads. A single sign could be logographic, syllabic, or determinative depending on context, and the mature Akkadian repertoire ran to some six hundred signs. Tablets were sun-dried or baked, filed in temple and palace archives, and when libraries burned the fire baked the clay harder, saving the record for archaeologists.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The Mesopotamian wedge script (primary noun sense, from 17th c. in learned use): the Sumerian-Akkadian system and its adaptations, including Old Persian cuneiform, which is a simpler semi-alphabetic cousin. The bridge is the object itself: wedge-marks on clay.
  • Cuneiform inscription or tablet (metonymic noun): as in a museum case of cuneiforms. The bridge runs from system to instance, the way alphabet can mean a single alphabet-book.
  • Wedge-shaped in anatomy (adjectival, 18th–19th c.): the three cuneiform bones of the foot, also called tarsal wedges, plus cuneiform cartilages of the larynx. The bridge is pure shape analogy: anatomists borrowed the Latin wedge-word for small bones that taper like clay wedges.
  • Wedge-shaped in botany and zoology (adjectival): cuneiform leaves that narrow toward the stalk. The bridge repeats the same visual metaphor in the field guide.
  • Figurative sense of dense, difficult, indecipherable marks (journalistic): handwriting or code described as cuneiform. The bridge is cultural memory: to modern eyes the wedges look impressively unreadable, so the word lends itself to any forbidding scribble.

Register and connotation: cuneiform is learned but concrete, at home in museums, textbooks, and documentaries, carrying dust, clay, and lamplight with it. As an adjective for bones and leaves it is quietly technical; as a metaphor for bad handwriting it is affectionately teasing. The plural of the script-sense is rare, since the system is usually a mass noun, while anatomists freely write cuneiforms for the foot bones. Everywhere it keeps a faint aura of firstness: the oldest writing most schoolchildren ever see pictured.
