In the source context — only one bay, shaggy-maned mare dropped an ear — bay is the equine coat-colour term: a horse whose body is reddish-brown to tan with sharply contrasting black points — mane, tail, lower legs and ear rims. Tolstoy (in Strider / Kholstomer, from which all five Batch 15 passages come) uses it as an attributive adjective identifying one mare in a crowded paddock. A bay mare is not merely brown: genetically she carries at least one dominant Agouti (A) allele acting on a black base (E_, aa suppressed), which restricts black pigment to the extremities and leaves the trunk red-brown. Horsemen distinguish shades — light bay, blood bay, dark bay / brown-bay, mahogany bay, sandy bay — but all share black mane and tail, which separates bay from chestnut/sorrel (reddish body with red or flaxen mane, no black points) and from black-brown / seal brown. The word functions both as adjective (a bay mare, bay gelding) and as noun (a bright bay, three bays in the yard), with plural bays.

As a stable and racing term bay is precise and institutional. Stud books, sale catalogues and cavalry remount rolls record every animal as bay, brown, black, chestnut, grey, roan, dun, piebald, because colour was identity before microchips. To call the mare bay, shaggy-maned therefore tells a 19th-century reader her exact silhouette: warm russet trunk, coarse dark mane standing unclipped, black legs. Behaviourally the passage makes her the one sensitive animal who reacts to the herdsman’s frivolity while the herd walks away — colour as individualising tag. Pronunciation is /beɪ/, rhyming with day.

  • Core equine sense: reddish-brown horse with black points (context sense). A bay colt, a dark bay hunter, bay with three white socks. Semantic anchor: continuous from Latin badius chestnut-brown (of horses) via Old French bai. Implies hardiness and ordinariness in Russian realism — bays are working horses, not flashy greys or costly blacks. Bridge to genetics: what grooms saw as one colour, modern science reads as black + agouti restriction.
  • Extended animal-colour sense: bay in hounds, cattle, birds. A bay hound, bay coat in dogs, bay plumage tinge. Bridge: generalisation of horse term to any reddish-brown animal coat with darker extremities, common in 18th–19th-century sporting literature.
  • Homograph 2 — coastal bay: broad inlet of sea or lake. The Bay of Biscay, Hudson Bay, a sheltered bay. Entirely different etymology (Old French baie, Late Latin baia) but identical spelling. Bridge: none to horse sense except accident of convergence; the semantic link is water hollowed into land, from a root meaning an opening. Learners must treat this as a separate dictionary headword that happens to share form.
  • Homograph 3 — architectural and spatial recess: bay window, bay of a nave, engine bay, sick bay, loading bay. A compartment or marked-off hollow in a larger structure: a bay window projects from a wall, a choir bay is one vaulted division, an engine bay houses machinery, a sick bay is a ship’s infirmary, a parking bay is one stall. Origin is Old French baee opening, from baer to gape. Bridge within this family: an opening left in a wall becomes any regularly divided compartment; metaphor then carries it to ships, aircraft, hospitals and car parks.
  • Homograph 4 — laurel and bay leaf: aromatic leaf for cooking and crowns. Bay leaves in stew, a bay wreath, poet bay-crowned. From Latin baca berry, via Old French baie berry. Bridge: the sweet bay tree (Laurus nobilis) bears berries, so berry-name transferred to leaf; classical victors’ crowns make bay a symbol of honour, hence to win the bays.
  • Homograph 5 — barking and pursuit: to bay, at bay, baying hounds. Hounds bay (bark deeply) when the quarry turns; the stag stands at bay (faces pursuers, cannot flee). From Old French abaier to bark. Bridge: the sound of cornered prey and pursuing pack gives the idiom to keep / hold at bay, now generalised to holding any threat — infection, creditors, doubts — at a distance. No link to horse colour except spelling.
  • Modern technical extensions. In aviation and computing a bay is a modular slot (cargo bay, bomb bay, drive bay); in botany bay compounds name trees (bay willow, rose bay). Bridge: the compartment sense (3) supplies the template — any hollow that receives a unit is a bay.

Tolstoy’s choice is deliberately plain: among a hundred horses only colour plus mane distinguishes this mare, and her bay coat marks her as a common working animal whose sensibility contrasts with the herd’s indifference. Translators sometimes render Russian гнедая (gnedaya) as bay precisely because both languages lexicalise the same agouti phenotype as a basic colour word.
