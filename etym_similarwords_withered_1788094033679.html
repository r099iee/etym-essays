• Shrivelled / shrivelled up: Similar because both denote dried, contracted, wrinkled material; differs because "shrivelled" stresses the surface wrinkling and contraction, while "withered" stresses the loss of moisture and life (and extends more naturally to people and abstractions).
  • Dried / dried up: Similar because both mean "deprived of moisture"; differs because "dried" is purely literal and neutral, without the connotations of death, decay, and frailty that "withered" carries.
  • Parched: Similar because both mean extremely dry; differs because "parched" emphasises extreme thirsty dryness (parched lips, parched earth) and is less about living tissue shrivelling and dying.
  • Desiccated: Similar because both mean dried out; differs because "desiccated" is a clinical, technical word for thorough dehydration, lacking the human frailty of "withered".
  • Sere / sear: Similar because both mean withered or dried ("sere leaves"); differs because "sere" is archaic and poetic, almost confined to literary and botanical idiom, while "withered" is everyday.
  • Wizened: Similar because both describe a face or body shrivelled and wrinkled with age; differs because "wizened" is used almost only of aged people and faces, rarely of plants or reputations.
  • Wrinkled: Similar because both describe creased, shrunken skin; differs because "wrinkled" is neutral and can be perfectly healthy (a wrinkled brow of worry), while "withered" implies an unhealthy drying-up.
  • Wilted: Similar because both describe a plant that has lost firmness and drooped; differs because "wilted" is about loss of turgor (often reversible with water), while "withered" implies drying to the point of death.
  • Faded: Similar because both mean "lost colour or vitality"; differs because "faded" concerns loss of colour or brilliance rather than shrivelling and drying.
  • Decayed / decomposed: Similar because both mean "broken down, rotted"; differs because these stress organic decomposition by rot and putrefaction, while "withered" stresses dry contraction without necessarily rotting.
  • Blighted: Similar because both describe something struck down and ruined; differs because "blighted" names the agent (blight, a disease) and is strongly figurative for ruin, while "withered" describes the resulting dried state.
  • Frail / decrepit: Similar because both describe the weak shrunken state of aged people; differs because these describe general weakness and broken-downness, while "withered" keeps the specific image of drying and shrinkage.
  • Wasted / emaciated: Similar because both describe a body or limb shrunken from disease or starvation; differs because these stress the loss of flesh and weight, whereas "withered" stresses drying and loss of vigour.
