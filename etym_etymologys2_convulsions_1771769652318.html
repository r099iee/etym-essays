Linear derivation chain for the single root word CONVULSIONS: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *uelh₁- 'to turn, wind, roll'. Reconstructed, not directly attested. NOTE: the deeper PIE ancestry of the Latin verb vellere is DEBATED — the immediate Latin base is reconstructed as *uels-, and the further link to *uelh₁- is probable rather than proven (see de Vaan, Etymological Dictionary of Latin); the chain therefore presents the traditional reconstruction with this caution stated at the outset. Stage 2 — Latin (attested from the 2nd c. BCE): vellō, vellere 'to pluck, pull, tear, tug at' — an earthy, physical verb used of plucking out hairs, pulling wool, stripping feathers, tearing leaves from a branch; built on the Latin base *uels-. Stage 3 — Latin (classical): convellere 'to wrench, tear to pieces, shake violently' — formed with the intensive/assimilated prefix con-/com-, 'together, thoroughly, with force,' + vellere; attested in classical Latin prose (e.g., in Livy and the historians) with both literal and figurative force. Stage 4 — Latin: convulsus 'violently wrenched, torn, shaken' — past participle of convellere; the participle stem becomes the base for the noun of action. Stage 5 — Latin (classical, 1st c. BCE–1st c. AD): convulsiō (genitive convulsiōnis) 'a cramp, violent spasm, shaking' — noun of action in -iō formed on the participle stem; used by Roman medical writers (e.g., Celsus) for involuntary muscular spasms and seizures; later extended to any violent upheaval. Stage 6 — Late Latin: convulsiōnem 'a seizure, a shaking' — the accusative singular, the form that passes into the Romance vernaculars and Old French. Stage 7 — Old French (13th–14th c.): convulsion 'a fit, a cramp' — medical borrowing from Latin. Stage 8 — Middle English (14th c.): convulcioun 'a fit, spasm, convulsion' — borrowed from Old French; the modern spelling convulsion settles in Early Modern English. Stage 9 — English (modern): convulsion 'a violent, involuntary spasm' — extended figuratively in the 17th–18th centuries to social and natural upheavals; the plural form convulsions (as in Gibbon's 'convulsions of the elements') becomes the dominant shape of the word in both the medical and the figurative registers. Note on debated stages: the single most uncertain point is Stage 1 — the PIE reconstruction of vellere's ancestry. Romanists reconstruct the Latin base as *uels-, and the connection of that base with *uelh₁- 'to turn, wind, roll' (also claimed for Greek heliks 'spiral' and Germanic *waltaną 'to roll') rests on probability rather than proof; some scholars treat vellere as of obscure or isolated origin. All stages from Latin vellere (Stage 2) onward are securely attested.
