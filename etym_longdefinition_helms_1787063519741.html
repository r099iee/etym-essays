Helms is the plural of helm, meaning (1) a helmet — a protective covering for the head worn in battle or ceremony; and, by extension, (2) protection, defense, or a protective thing generally. In the source sentence, "coats of mail, helms and axes, swords and spears hanging," helms are plainly the helmets of an armory — the plated head-guards of warriors hanging on the wall.

Primary senses of helm:

  • A helmet (head armor). The oldest sense in English: a piece of defensive armor covering the head, from the conical Anglo-Saxon helm to the great helm, bassinet, armet, and sallet of medieval chivalry. Now archaic or literary in this literal sense, but standard in fantasy, historical, and medieval prose.
  • Protection or covering (archaic/figurative). In older English, anything that guards or covers could be a helm — a roof as the helm of a house, a king as the helm of his people. This transferred sense follows naturally from the basic idea of "covering."

A distinct but entangled word:

  • Helm as steering gear. A second, unrelated Old English word, helma, meant "the tiller or steering apparatus of a ship." By extension, helm came to mean the position of the person steering, and figuratively leadership or command: "to take the helm," "at the helm," "a change of helm." This sense is alive and common today — indeed it is now the most frequent use of the word in everyday English.
  • To helm (verb). To steer, to guide, to be in command of: a ship helmed through a storm; an organization helmed by its director. A modern verbal use of the steering sense, now fully standard.

Register and connotation. As a head-armor word, helm survives today chiefly in archaic, literary, fantasy, and gaming registers, helmets being the everyday term; the word carries chivalric weight and age. As a steering word, helm is modern, common, and figurative — found on every business page ("a new CEO takes the helm"). The coexistence of the two words in a single spelling is a historical accident that modern English has turned to its advantage: a word that can simultaneously conjure a knight's helmet and a captain's command.

Etymology in brief. The helmet sense descends from Proto-Indo-European *ḱel- "to cover, conceal, protect," via Proto-Germanic *helmaz "helmet, covering" and Old English helm; the steering sense is a separate Old English helma of uncertain Germanic ancestry, perhaps connected to a root for "handle" or "to turn."

Historical and cultural depth. The plural helms belongs to the language of the armory and the battlefield: it evokes ranks of warriors, the chivalric romances of the Middle Ages, and the great collections of weapons hung on castle walls — exactly the scene in the source passage, where helms hang beside coats of mail, axes, swords, and spears in a dragon-guarded hall. Its literary flavor makes it a favorite of fantasy and historical fiction, where it lends scenes an old, heroic, and slightly archaic dignity that the everyday word helmet cannot match.
