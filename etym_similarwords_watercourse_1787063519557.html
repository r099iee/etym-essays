• Stream: Similar because both involve bodies of flowing freshwater; differs because 'stream' specifically describes the moving body of water itself, whereas 'watercourse' emphasizes the physical channel, bed, and geographical corridor (which may be active, seasonal, or bone-dry).
  • Channel: Similar because both denote a linear topographic groove guiding liquid flow; differs because 'channel' is a broader geometric and nautical term (e.g., deep navigation channel in a harbor, communication channel), whereas 'watercourse' is inherently hydrological and riparian.
  • Riverbed: Similar because both refer to the ground beneath a river; differs because 'riverbed' refers strictly to the bottom ground surface of a large river, whereas 'watercourse' encompasses the entire hydraulic system including bed, banks, and flowing water.
  • Gully / Ravine: Similar because both are erosional landforms created by water; differs because 'gully' and 'ravine' emphasize the deep, steep-sided chasm or scar cut into earth, whereas 'watercourse' focuses on the functional conduit of fluid flow.
  • Arroyo / Wadi: Similar because both describe dry, rocky watercourses in arid regions; differs because 'arroyo' (Spanish) and 'wadi' (Arabic) are regional ecological terms for desert wash channels, while 'watercourse' is the universal English hypernym.
  • Canal: Similar because both can carry flowing water; differs because 'canal' is almost always artificial, excavated by human engineering for navigation or mass irrigation, while 'watercourse' encompasses both natural rivers and constructed channels.
  • Brook: Similar because both describe small flowing water features; differs because 'brook' is a gentle, poetic, and lively natural small stream, whereas 'watercourse' is formal, technical, and structural.
  • Ditch: Similar because both refer to linear excavations for water; differs because 'ditch' implies a crude, narrow, man-made trench for simple agricultural drainage, lacking the scale or natural origin of a watercourse.
