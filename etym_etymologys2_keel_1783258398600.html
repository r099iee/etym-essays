Stage 1 — Proto-Germanic *keluz / *keulaz "a ship; the keel-timber" (the deep structural timber that gave the whole vessel its name). DEEPER ORIGIN UNCERTAIN: no securely attested PIE etymon exists. The proposed link to PIE *gʷel(h)- "to swallow" (the ship as the hollow "throat" that gulps the waves; cf. Latin gula "gullet", German Kehle "throat") is SPECULATIVE and must not be presented as fact. Stage 2 — Old Norse kjǫlr "keel of a ship; a long ship" (North Germanic; attested in Old Norse). Stage 3 — Middle English kele, keel "the keel-timber; a ship" (14th century; the Norse word reinforced the native Old English cēol "ship", which had meant the ship itself rather than the timber). Stage 4 — English keel "the lowermost longitudinal timber of a ship's hull; the ship (poetic); the base or foundation (figurative)". FINAL NODE: KEEL. SEPARATE BORROWING (same spelling, different source): Middle Dutch kiel "ship, barge" → English keel "a flat-bottomed barge on the River Tyne" (16th–19th centuries; the "keelmen" of Newcastle). SEPARATE BORROWING: Dutch kielhalen → English keelhaul (17th century). SIBLING FORMS: Old English cēol "ship" (poetic word, Beowulf); Old High German kiel / German Kiel "keel"; Modern Scandinavian kjøl, köl "keel; backbone; mountain ridge".
