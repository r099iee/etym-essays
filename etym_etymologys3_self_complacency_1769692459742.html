The compound self-complacency is a hybrid of two very different linguistic ancestries that meet in one of the most characteristically modern of human attitudes — the quiet, unexamined pleasure we take in ourselves. Half the word is native Anglo-Saxon, the reflexive pronoun self that has stood in English for over a millennium; half is an imported Latin philosophical term for "thorough pleasure," freighted with centuries of theological meaning. Their union, first recorded in the 17th century, charts the slow moral decline of the idea of being pleased with oneself — from a state of innocent, even saintly, contentment to the target of novelists' satire and moralists' censure.

Part 1 — The self lineage: The first element descends from the Proto-Indo-European reflexive root *se-/*swe-, "self, one's own," one of the most ancient layers of the language — it named the individual as opposed to the group, one's own kin and one's own possessions. In Sanskrit it gave sva "one's own" and svayam "oneself" (seen in modern swaraj, "self-rule"); in Latin, the reflexive se; in Greek, hé. In the Germanic branch it grew into Proto-Germanic *selbaz, which yielded Gothic silba, Old Norse sjálfr (source of self in Icelandic), Old High German selb, and Old English self or seolf. From Anglo-Saxon times to the present, self has been the central English word for personal identity, and its combination with nearly any noun or adjective — self-confidence, self-respect, self-deception — shows how productive this ancient root remains.

Part 2 — The complacency lineage: The second element comes from the Proto-Indo-European root *plāk-, "flat, smooth," whose physical sense (Greek plax, "flat surface"; Germanic *flakaz, source of English flat) shifted, as such words often do, toward the psychological: to "smooth over" is to soothe, and to soothe is to please. Latin built placēre, "to please, be agreeable to," a verb at the heart of Roman social life (as in the famous judgment formula placet, "it pleases"). With the intensive prefix com- ("thoroughly"), Latin formed complacēre, "to please greatly," and from it the abstract noun complacentia, "thorough pleasure, delight." Church Latin used complacentia for the pleasure God takes in his creation and the satisfaction of the soul at peace with God — a deeply positive, even mystical concept. English borrowed complacency in the 17th century (c. 1640s–1650s) with this broad sense of "quiet satisfaction," and the word carried a theological halo for a while.

Part 3 — The compound: English joined self and complacency in the same period — self-complacency is first recorded in the mid-17th century in theological and moral writing, where it still often meant a legitimate, calm satisfaction with one's own conduct, the "ease of conscience" of a well-ordered life. The literal force — "being thoroughly pleased with oneself" — was at first not a crime.

Part 4 — Semantic decline and modern use:
  • 17th–18th centuries: "self-complacency" is morally neutral or mildly positive — a serene self-approbation close to contentment.
  • 18th–19th centuries: Satirists and novelists of manners seize on it. The age of Jane Austen, whose works (as in the card's context) dissect precisely this attitude, transformed "self-complacency" into a term of quiet moral censure: the smug, uncritical self-satisfaction that blinds a person to their own defects. The word becomes almost always pejorative.
  • 19th–20th centuries: The idea expands into social criticism — the "smug self-complacency" of nations, classes, and institutions before crises, and the psychological vocabulary of overconfidence and self-congratulation. "Complacency" (without "self-") becomes the more common word, leaving "self-complacency" a formal, precise, slightly old-fashioned term.
  • Contemporary: "Self-complacency" is used in formal and literary English (essays, criticism, self-help rhetoric) to name the passive, unexamined self-regard that blocks improvement — the very opposite of justified self-confidence.

Part 5 — Literary and cultural attestations: The concept is everywhere in English moral writing: in the novels of Jane Austen (whose Pride and Prejudice context the card preserves, warning that human nature "particularly prone" to a "feeling of self-complacency" is rarely resisted), in the essays of Addison and Steele and of 19th-century sermon-writers, in George Eliot's moral psychology, and in modern management and self-help critiques of complacency. The word crystallizes a whole ethical tradition about the danger of being "thoroughly pleased with oneself."

Summary of the drift: from PIE "one's own" and "flat/smooth/please" → Anglo-Saxon self and Latin complacentia → a 17th-century English compound for "being pleased with oneself" → a term that travelled from theological delight to moral neutral to sharp censure, and now names the quiet, uncritical smugness that precedes a fall. Its two halves — the ancient reflexive pronoun and the Roman verb of pleasing — together describe an attitude that is entirely modern, and entirely human.
