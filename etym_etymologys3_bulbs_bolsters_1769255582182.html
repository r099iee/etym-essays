bulbs. The story of bulb begins in the Mediterranean vegetable plot. Ancient Greek called the onion-like, swollen root bolbós (βολβός), a word of solid everyday use for bulbs, squills, and edible root-plants. Its ultimate pedigree is genuinely uncertain: the Dutch comparativist Robert Beekes classes bolbós as Pre-Greek — a loan from the substrate that preceded Indo-European in the Aegean — for which no PIE ancestry can be demonstrated; Julius Pokorny, more optimistically, attached it to the Indo-European root *bʰel-, "to swell, to blow up," on the theory that a bulb is simply "the swollen thing." The honest position is that the word is a loan or substrate item whose deep history is unresolved. In the Roman period Latin took the word wholesale as bulbus, and by the sixteenth century English had imported it (through French bulbe and directly from Latin) as bulb, used at first of onions and other bulbous roots. From there the meaning swelled like the thing itself: any rounded swelling — the bulb of a thermometer, the bulb of the eye, and finally the glass light bulb of the nineteenth century, the word's most famous modern home, the onion given a job. The adjective bulbous, "swollen, rounded," the noun bulbil (a small offset bulb), the neurological bulbar (of the medulla oblongata), and the marine bulbous bow all descend from the same Latin-Greek stock, as do the Romance sisters French bulbe, Italian and Spanish bulbo. In the garden the word also developed strict companions — corm, tuber, and rhizome — that botanists now keep distinct, even though laypeople call everything a bulb. The word's whole history is a series of swellings: the swollen root of the onion, the swollen eyeball, the swollen thermometer, the swollen globe of light that ended up lighting every modern room. bolsters. The biography of bolster is shorter in time but rests on a firmly reconstructed Indo-European base. Its root is *bʰel-, "to swell, to blow up, to bloom," the great swelling-family of the northern languages: from it descend Old English belg, "bag, belly," giving belly and bellows; blōwan, "to bloom," giving blossom; and the blow of the wind. A bolster, in this ancient view, is simply "the swollen, stuffed thing" — a bag or cushion made to swell with feathers, wool, or straw — and the Germanic noun *bulstrą (o-grade of *bʰel- with a noun-suffix) carried exactly that meaning into Old English as bolster, "a pillow, a cushion, a pad," attested from the ninth century. The word then did something the pillow itself never does: it grew a verb. By the sixteenth century "to bolster" meant "to prop up, to support," and from the physical propping of a sleeping head the verb moved steadily into the abstract — supporting morale, confidence, an argument, an economy. Modern English keeps both lives in harness: the noun is an old-fashioned bed-furnishing (the long, firm, full-width pillow on which a tired head rests), and the verb is a vigorous, everyday word for supporting anything that might sag or fall. Together. The two words are perfect companions for a Narnian oath precisely because they are so homely. "Bulbs" brings the round, earth-swollen life of the vegetable plot; "bolsters" brings the soft, firm support of the bedroom; and both keep, under their plain surfaces, histories of remarkable depth — one a Mediterranean substrate word that has become the name of the light in every modern house, the other an Indo-European swelling-word that has become the verb of moral support.
