AVIGNON has no Indo-European etymology; it belongs to the pre-Roman substrate of western Europe and the Celtic hydronymic vocabulary. A single chain, with the earliest stage reconstructed: - Substrate/Celtic hydronymic base *ab- / *abonā- "river, water, running water" (a word of the pre-Indo-European or Celtic substrate stratum of western Europe; attested in the Celtic branch: Welsh afon "river", Cornish avon, Breton aven, Old Irish ab / abann, Scottish Gaelic abhainn; the same element underlies the English river-name Avon and the Celtic aber "river-mouth" in Aberdeen, Aberystwyth) — the stage before Avennio is not attested, and whether the base is inherited in Celtic from Indo-European or absorbed from an older substrate is debated → Gaulish/Latinized *Avenniō "place at the river; town by the waters" (name of the oppidum of the Cavares in Gallia Narbonensis, on the Rhône; a derivative with the Celtic/Italic suffix -iō; the exact intermediate Gaulish form is not attested) → Latin Avenniō (genitive Avenniōnis) "Avignon" (attested as Avennio in Pliny the Elder's Natural History, 1st c. CE, and in later imperial and Merovingian records) → Late Latin accusative Avenniōnem (the oblique form through which the name entered Romance) → Gallo-Romance / Old Occitan *Avenniōne → Old Occitan / Provençal Avinhon (Occitan form; Avinhoun in Mistralian orthography) → Middle French Avignon → Modern French Avignon → English AVIGNON (the English name is adopted from the French form, from the 14th-c. papal period onward). Note on debate: two explanations compete for the base. The traditional view derives Avennio from the hydronymic element *abonā- "river" (hence "town on the river"), supported by the town's position at the confluence of the Rhône and the Durance and by the widespread Celtic river-word. A minority view derives it from a Gaulish personal name *Avennius plus the possessive suffix -iō, i.e. "estate of Avennius." Both analyses are reconstructions, and the pre-Roman forms are unrecorded.
