- Old English (8th–11th c.) — fellan/fællan: “cause to fall, strike down” — the transitive causative of fall, used of men, beasts and trees alike — a plain verb of violence and woodcraft
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) — fellen: cutting down trees, felling enemies in battle — everyday in chronicles, romances and Bible translations; the tree-sense and the combat-sense sit side by side
- 16th–18th c. — “felling” enters the language of forestry and estate management; Evelyn's Sylva (1664) treats the felling of timber as a science; in duels and executions felling strokes are recorded — the word covers woodcraft and combat equally
- 19th c. — industrial logging makes “felling” the standard technical term of the timber trade; “clear felling” and “selective felling” become professional vocabulary
- 20th c. — the combat sense narrows to boxing and literary prose (“a felling blow”); the environmental era reverses the word's emotional charge — felling, once praised as industry, is now what conservationists protest
- Today — technical-neutral in forestry, negative in environmental discourse, vivid and archaic-flavoured in figurative use (“felled by grief”); the homograph “fell” (cruel) survives only in “one fell swoop”
