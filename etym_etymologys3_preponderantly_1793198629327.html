The evolution of preponderantly begins at the hanging balance of the ancient world, where goods, coins, and arguments were all literally weighed, and the heavier pan decided price, debt, and truth.

The reconstructed PIE root *pend-, to hang, gives Latin its great weighing family: pendere, to hang and hence to weigh and to pay out by weight, from which English still draws depend, suspend, pendant, and expend; and the noun pondus, weight, source of pound itself. From pondus came the verb ponderare, to weigh, which crossed early into English as ponder, to weigh thoughts in the mind. Prefix prae-, before or beyond, made praeponderare, to outweigh — to throw more weight into the pan than the other side holds — and the adjective praeponderans named whatever tipped the scales.

Seventeenth-century English, hungry for Latinate precision in science and statecraft, borrowed the family wholesale: preponderate as a verb, preponderant and preponderance as the state of outweighing, and finally preponderantly as the adverb of measured majority. Natural philosophers weighed airs and humors, divines weighed evidences for Providence, and parliamentarians weighed interests — all preponderating, all tipping pans of brass and rhetoric together.

Landmarks in the weighing of the word:

  • Antiquity — the hanging balance: Roman merchants and judges weigh metal, grain, and testimony, and praeponderare names the heavier pan in market and court alike.
  • Middle Ages — reasons weighed: scholastic disputation treats arguments as weights, and the preponderating opinion is the one with more authority in the pan.
  • 17th c. — English borrows the scales: Baconian science and parliamentary debate naturalize preponderate and kin for evidence, armies, and humors that outweigh.
  • 18th–19th c. — counting peoples: censuses, elections, and geologies describe populations and strata as preponderantly this or that, hedging with arithmetic.
  • 20th c. — the scholar's hedge: folklorists and sociologists facing uncountable material, like the Slavic songs of the source sentence, weigh corpora by impression and report the tilt honestly.

Sound change was minimal because borrowing was late and learned: the Latin syllables survive almost intact, merely softened in English stress toward the -pon- center. What changed was scope: from bronze pans to ballot boxes to song corpora, the scales grew ever more abstract while the verb stayed put. The adverb's stiffness is thus a feature, not a flaw — its very formality certifies that weighing was done.

In the source sentence the word performs its highest office. The scholar cannot count every song of three peoples across centuries, yet must characterize the tradition truthfully; preponderantly epic concedes the lyric margins while reporting the weighed center. That is the whole ethics of the word in one use: never wholly where the record forbids it, never partly where the weight is clear — but preponderantly, with the scales held level and read honestly.
