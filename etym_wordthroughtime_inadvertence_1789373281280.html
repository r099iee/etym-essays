- 14th to 15th Century - Enters late Middle English from Old French *inadvertance* and Medieval Latin; initially deployed in religious, theological, and scholastic treatises to distinguish venial sins committed through unwatchfulness from deliberate, mortal sins committed through calculated malice.
- 16th to 17th Century - Broadens into legal commentary, parliamentary debate, and early modern philosophy (Bacon, Browne, Milton); established as a standard legal term to describe procedural oversights and statutory omissions occurring without fraudulent intent.
- 18th to 19th Century - Highly favored in Augustan essays, Johnsonian prose, and Victorian literary criticism; becomes the quintessential polite, aristocratic euphemism used in gentlemanly society and diplomatic exchanges to pardon someone's blunder without accusing them of stupidity or dishonesty.
- 20th Century - Extensively codified in modern textual criticism (e.g., editing Shakespeare, Joyce, Proust) to label scribal slips and typographical errors, and in corporate jurisprudence to categorize accidental clerical defaults.
- 21st Century - Continues as an elevated, formal descriptor in administrative law, academic discourse, and diplomatic statecraft, emphasizing unintentional human cognitive error amidst increasingly automated, high-speed information systems.
