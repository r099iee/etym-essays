Single linear derivation chain for gallon: 1. ORIGIN DEBATED / UNCERTAIN — no secure PIE root is attested for this word. The most widely accepted proposal is a Gaulish (Celtic) substrate noun *galla, "vessel, container, wooden bowl," of the kind bequeathed to Gallo-Roman speech without literary record; an alternative connects it to a general vulgar Latin word for a liquid jar. No attested earlier form survives, so this stage is recorded as a hypothesis, not a fact. 2. Medieval Latin galo, galonem (also galeta, galletum) — "a liquid measure, a jar, a bowl for wine or other liquids" (recorded in medieval documents from the 9th–11th c.). The container-word enters the administrative Latin of Carolingian Europe, where it begins to name a standard quantity. 3. Old Northern French galon (Central French jalon) — "a liquid measure" (attested 12th c.). The Latin measure-word develops in the northern French dialects that supplied much of England's commercial vocabulary. 4. Anglo-Norman galun / Middle English galoun, gallon — "a gallon, a measure of liquid capacity" (attested from the late 13th c., c. 1290, after the Norman Conquest). The word enters English with the wine trade. 5. Modern English gallon — "a unit of liquid capacity; US 231 cubic inches (3.785 L) or Imperial 4.546 L" (attested from the 14th c. onward; the modern split between the US and Imperial standards dates from the 19th c.) — the final node, the note's exact word form, highlighted above. NOTE ON DEBATE: the old habit of deriving the gallon from Latin galleta "bucket" or from a claimed PIE root is not secure; OED records the origin as uncertain, with the Gaulish *galla proposal the standard hypothesis. The chain from Medieval Latin onward is the attested record; the earliest stage is explicitly a hypothesis.
