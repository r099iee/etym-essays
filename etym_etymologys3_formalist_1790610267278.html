Formalist joins a very old noun to a very productive suffix. The base is Latin forma, shape, figure, mould, beauty, recorded from Plautus onward. Its deeper origin is disputed: de Vaan and others note possible connection to PIE *dher- to hold or support via Etruscan mediation, or to Greek morphe by taboo deformation; what is certain is that by Classical Latin forma meant both outer shape and ideal pattern, the mould that gives matter intelligibility. From it Late Latin regularly formed formalis, pertaining to form, and formalitas, formality, used by scholastic philosophers like Duns Scotus to discuss haecceitas and formal distinction.

Old French took formel as ceremonial, orderly, explicit, and Middle English borrowed formal by the late 14th century in both senses: shapely and ceremonious. Early uses include formal cause in Aristotelian philosophy, formal attire, formal invitation. The pejorative overtone — caring too much for externals — is already present in Reformation polemics where Protestants accuse Catholics of formal religion, mere form without faith.

The suffix -ist comes from Greek -istes, agent who practises, via Latin -ista and French -iste. English from the 16th century onward creates artist, Calvinist, deist, realist on this model. Formalist first appears in the 1600s for one who stands upon forms, especially in religion and manners. Nineteenth-century aesthetics then sharpens it: German Formalismus, French formalisme, English formalism name the doctrine that beauty resides in form, and formalist names its adherent. Art historians contrast formalists who discuss line and composition with colourists or realists who discuss subject and truth.

  • Mould to manners: Latin shape becomes medieval ceremony, linking physical pattern to social protocol.
  • Ceremony to pedantry: Reformation and Enlightenment turn love of rite into insult for hollow observance.
  • Pedantry to doctrine: 19th-century -ism creates self-conscious aesthetic and legal philosophies of form.
  • Doctrine to school: Russian Formalism 1915-1930 institutionalizes formalist as proud scientific identity with journals, manifestos, and defamiliarization theory.
  • School to method: Anglo-American New Criticism imports formalist close analysis into classrooms as combination of formalist principle and rhetoric.

Russian Formalism deserves emphasis because it fixed modern literary meaning. In wartime Petrograd, Viktor Shklovsky’s 1917 essay Art as Technique argued that art defamiliarizes perception by device; Boris Eichenbaum, Yuri Tynianov, and Roman Jakobson founded OPOYAZ and the Moscow Linguistic Circle to study plot versus story, verse language, and narrative functions. Soviet authorities condemned them as bourgeois formalists by 1930, making formalist a dangerous political insult. Exiled Jakobson carried Formalism to Prague and America, where Cleanth Brooks, Wimsatt, and Wellek adapted it as New Criticism’s close reading.

Today formalist thus oscillates between slur and badge. Administrators dismiss formalist compliance; theorists defend formalist attention against impressionistic, boozy generalities and historicist reduction. The passage’s nostalgic “formalist critical principle and traditional rhetorical analysis” captures that mid-century confidence that counting stresses and naming tropes could reveal how prose styles were put together — before catastrophe, fashion shifted and context devoured text. From Roman mould to Muscovite manifesto, the formalist remains the guardian of shape.
