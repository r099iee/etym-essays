- Late 17th Century (c. 1690s) - Coined in London during the Financial Revolution as joint-stock enterprises (such as the East India Company, the South Sea Company, and the newly chartered Bank of England) emerged; early stockbrokers operated informally in the Royal Exchange and neighboring coffeehouses along Change Alley (notably Jonathan's and Garraway's), frequently castigated in popular pamphlets as unprincipled, parasitic "stockjobbers."
- 18th Century - Gradual formalization and institutional regulation; spurred by parliamentary acts to curb fraudulent stockjobbing (such as Sir John Barnard's Act of 1734) and culminating in the founding of the London Stock Exchange in 1773 and New York's Buttonwood Agreement of 1792, establishing stockbroking as a regulated, self-governing mercantile profession.
- 19th Century (The Industrial Revolution & Bourgeoisie) - Reaches unprecedented social prestige, political influence, and immense personal wealth as railroad capitalization, government bonds, and industrial joint-stock companies transform global economies; immortalized in 19th-century literature (Balzac, Trollope, Zola) as central figures of upper-middle-class European power, embodied in France by the aristocratic monopoly of the Paris *agents de change*.
- Mid-20th Century - The classic era of the retail commission stockbroker and the Wall Street wirehouse (e.g. Merrill Lynch), democratizing equity ownership across middle-class households via telephone order-routing, stock ticker tapes, and personalized portfolio recommendations.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Completely revolutionized and disintermediated by financial deregulation (the abolition of fixed commissions on "May Day" 1975 in the US and the 1986 "Big Bang" in London), followed by electronic communication networks, zero-fee online discount brokerages, and algorithmic high-frequency trading; the traditional human commission broker has largely transitioned into a comprehensive, fee-based financial planner or private wealth manager.
