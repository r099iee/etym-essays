Braggart is a French import with a Germanic suffix and an English-looking root, which is why its history is so tangled. The English noun is first recorded in the 1570s, borrowed from French bragard, "a boaster, a swaggerer". French had formed that word from the verb braguer, "to brag, to swagger", which is attested in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Beyond that point the record becomes uncertain: braguer may have been borrowed from Middle English braggen, "to boast", or the English and French verbs may be independent formations on a common Germanic stock. The English verb brag itself appears in the late fourteenth century, and its own origin is unknown; imitative explanations (boasting as a kind of loud noise) are plausible but unproven.

The suffix that does the work. What makes braggart a term of abuse rather than a neutral description is the element -ard. It descends, through Old French, from a Frankish or Germanic formative *-hard meaning "hard, bold" — the same element that appears in German names such as Leonhard and in the English -ard of drunkard, coward, dullard, sluggard, niggard, wizard and bastard. In Romance and then in English, this suffix specialised in forming nouns of agent with a distinctly unflattering colouring: the word names a person by the vice that defines him. French bragard therefore already meant something like "one of the bragging sort", and English took over both the form and the sneer.

Englishing the form. The French bragard appears in English as braggart, with a final -t and a doubled g. The doubling reflects the English verb brag, whose participle and gerund are bragging; the final -t is an English alteration of the French ending, the kind of phonetic tidying that borrowings often undergo when they are assimilated to familiar patterns. The result is a word that looks as though it were native — brag plus -art — and that has consequently always felt more English than its French origin would suggest.

  • Late 14th c.: English brag (verb) is recorded in the sense "to boast"; origin unknown.
  • 15th–16th c.: French braguer, "to brag", and bragard, "boaster".
  • 1570s: English braggart is recorded as a noun.
  • 1590: Spenser's Faerie Queene introduces Braggadocchio, the type-figure of the braggart, whose name becomes the common noun braggadocio.
  • 17th c.: attributive use ("braggart soldier", "braggart tongue") becomes common.

The stage ancestry. Long before the word existed in English, the character existed on stage. Plautus's Miles Gloriosus ("the boastful soldier") gave Roman comedy a figure who claims superhuman feats in war and is revealed as a fraud; the type passed into the Renaissance via Terence and Plautus, and into the commedia dell'arte as Il Capitano, the Spanish braggart officer. When English dramatists of the 1590s needed a name for such a man, braggart was waiting. Ben Jonson's Bobadill in Every Man in His Humour is the classic English version: a penniless soldier who boasts of duels he never fought.

From the stage to politics. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word remained close to its dramatic origin, applied to duellists, footmen, pretenders and military men. In the nineteenth century it widened again, and "braggart" began to modify nations and parties — braggart patriotism, braggart diplomacy, the braggart tone of a press campaign. This extension follows the same logic as the personal sense: a collective that advertises imaginary triumphs is a collective braggart.

Modern standing. Braggart is now a relatively rare word, felt as slightly old-fashioned and distinctly literary. It survives in historical writing, in translations of classical comedy and in political invective that wants a period flavour. Its modern rivals — show-off, blowhard, boaster — are more colloquial and less judgemental; braggart retains the completeness of the old moral verdict, naming a man who is, rather than merely a man who does.
