The English toponym Algiers exemplifies a remarkable linguistic and historical journey across Afroasiatic Semitic morphology, medieval Maghrebi tribal geography, Mediterranean nautical Romance, and Early Modern English cartography. Etymologically, Algiers is the Anglicized derivation of the Arabic broken plural noun al-Jazā'ir (الجزائر), signifying "the islands," which originally described a cluster of four small, rocky coastal islets that sheltered the ancient harbor before being incorporated into the modern breakwater.

1. Proto-Semitic and Classical Arabic Morphological Roots:

The linguistic lineage of Algiers originates in the Proto-Semitic triconsonantal root *g-z-r, which conveyed the core verbal action of "to cut off, sever, separate, or divide" (which produced Hebrew gazar "to cut, divide" and Ugaritic gzr). In Classical Arabic, this root generated the verbal stem jazara (جزر, "to cut off, slaughter, or recede as the tide").

From this verbal concept arose the feminine substantive jazīra (جَزِيرَة), formed on the standard fa'īlah nominal pattern, denoting an "island" or "peninsula"—literally a landmass that has been "cut off" or severed from the continental mainland by the surrounding sea (the identical root that generated Al Jazeera, "The Peninsula", referring to the Arabian Peninsula). When pluralized according to Arabic non-concatenative broken plural morphology, jazīra assumes the fa'ā'il (فَعَائِل) pattern, yielding jazā'ir (جَزَائِر, "islands"). Compounded with the Arabic definite article al- (الـ), it forms al-Jazā'ir (الجزائر, "the islands").

2. Berber Settlement and the Islands of Mazghanna:

Before the Arab conquests, the site was occupied by the Phoenician trading outpost of Ikosim and the Roman colony of Icosium. Following centuries of decline during the Vandal and Byzantine periods, the town was refounded around 944 CE by Bologhine ibn Ziri (founder of the Zirid dynasty, belonging to the Sanhaja Berber confederation). Medieval Arab geographers (such as al-Bakri in the 11th century and al-Idrisi in the 12th century) recorded the newly thriving port under its full medieval name: Jazā'ir Banī Mazghanna (جزائر بني مزغنة, "The Islands of the Sons of Mazghanna").

The "islands" referenced in this name were four prominent rocky reefs situated roughly three hundred meters off the shoreline. These islets formed a crucial natural breakwater that protected medieval merchant galleys from the treacherous north-westerly gales of the Mediterranean. Over time, vernacular speech shortened the cumbersome tribal compound simply to al-Jazā'ir.

3. Mediterranean Maritime Transmission into Romance Languages:

During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the kingdom of Majorca, the Crown of Aragon, and the Republic of Genoa established extensive maritime trade networks with the Maghreb. Catalan, Majorcan, and Italian cartographers transcribed the Arabic name onto their navigational portolan charts (such as the famed Catalan Atlas of 1375):

  • Because the voiced postalveolar affricate of Arabic jīm (ج, [dʒ]) was frequently heard by Iberian Romance speakers as a voiced velar stop, Catalan navigators transcribed al-Jazā'ir as Alguer or Alguere.
  • In Castilian Spanish, the name evolved into Argel, exhibiting regular liquid dissimilation where the lateral -l- shifted to rhotic -r- before a voiced consonant.
  • In French, the Catalan form was Gallicized as Alger (pronounced [al.ʒe]), appearing in diplomatic correspondence with the Ottoman Regency from the sixteenth century onward.

4. Adoption into Early Modern English:

English travelers, privateers, and dramatists encountered the city during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I. In Elizabethan and Jacobean drama, the city was initially transcribed as Argier (following the Spanish variant, as famously preserved in William Shakespeare's The Tempest, Act I, Scene 2, where the sorceress Sycorax is said to have been born "in Argier").

By the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, the French form Alger was adopted into English but received an excrescent terminal -s, yielding modern Algiers. This terminal sibilant was a standard English habit for Anglicizing foreign Romance city names (compare English Marseilles from French Marseille, Lyons from Lyon, and Tangiers from Tangier).

5. Barbarossa's Mole, Delacroix's Masterpiece, and Modern Sovereignty:

The physical islands that gave Algiers its name no longer exist in their natural state. In 1529, the Ottoman privateer Khair ad-Din Barbarossa captured the Spanish fortress on the central islet and utilized thousands of Christian slaves to construct an enormous stone mole connecting all four islets directly to the city walls, creating the modern harbor basin.

In June 1832, following the French invasion, Eugène Delacroix visited Algiers. Granted access to a private domestic apartment, he executed studies that culminated in his 1834 masterpiece The Women of Algiers in their Apartment, which entered the permanent collection of the Louvre. Delacroix's painting transformed Algiers from a fearsome symbol of Barbary privateering into a timeless touchstone of modernist art, celebrated for its revolutionary harmony of light, color, and domestic intimacy.
