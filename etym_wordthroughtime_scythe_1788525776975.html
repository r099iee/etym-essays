- Antiquity to Old English (c. 1st Millennium BCE – 11th Century CE) - Evolved from short iron sickles into the two-handed curved mowing blade (Old English sīðe / sigðe), transforming European agriculture by tripling haymaking efficiency - Essential farming implement.
- Middle Ages (12th–15th Century) - Solidified across medieval Europe as the premier harvest tool (Middle English sithe); fitted with wooden cradles for reaping grain and codified in iconography as the weapon of the Grim Reaper - Agrarian mastery and macabre allegory.
- Early Modern Period (16th–18th Century) - Underwent an unetymological spelling shift from 'sithe' to 'scythe' due to Renaissance scribal confusion with Latin scindere (to cut); modified in peasant uprisings into the feared 'war scythe' polearm - Classical literature, peasant revolt, and orthographic change.
- 19th Century - Celebrated in romantic and aesthetic poetry (e.g. Oscar Wilde, Alfred Tennyson, Leo Tolstoy's Anna Karenina) as an emblem of authentic, rhythmic rural labor before industrial mechanization - Romantic pastoral nostalgia.
- 20th–21st Century - Replaced in industrial farming by motorized machinery, yet experiencing a modern renaissance in permaculture, sustainable eco-mowing, and competitive scything competitions for its zero-carbon, silent efficiency - Ecological renaissance and artisanal heritage.
