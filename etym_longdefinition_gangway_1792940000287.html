Gangway is in its most familiar modern sense a narrow passage, opening, or movable bridge that lets people get past one another or get aboard: the plank or covered ramp lowered from a ship to the quay, the accordion-sided jet bridge that joins an airliner to the terminal, or the tight aisle between rows of seats down which an usher squeezes sideways. In the source sentence it carries exactly the old embarkation sense: Merry leads his pony over a gangway — a boarding plank or short bridge — on to the ferry, with the others following hoof by hoof, the little party crossing the gap between bank and boat on a strip of timber barely wider than a pony.

Core meaning in detail: The word is a transparent compound of gang, an old northern word for a going, journey, or passage, and way, a road or path: literally a going-way, a place made for going. Every sense keeps that pinch-point idea. A ship's gangway is a narrow going-way between shore and deck, shipped or lowered as needed, with rails or ropes at the sides; a ferry gangway of Tolkien's Shire is humbler still, a stout plank thrown across the water-gap, strong enough for ponies. An aircraft gangway is the same idea industrialised into steel, rubber seals, and telescoping tunnels. An auditorium or parliamentary gangway is the same idea turned indoors: the slot of floor left open between blocks of seats or benches so that people, papers, and latecomers can move without climbing over everyone else.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Boarding bridge to a ship or ferry (primary historical sense, from the 17th c.; the source sense): a plank, ladder, or railed platform giving access between quay and vessel, or between vessel and vessel. The semantic bridge is direct composition: the gang, the going, made into a way across the water-gap, laid down to embark and taken up before sailing.
  • Aircraft boarding ramp or jet bridge (modern extension, 20th c.): the mobile stairway or enclosed telescopic tunnel through which passengers board. The bridge runs by functional analogy: what the plank did for the ferry, the steel tunnel does for the airliner, and the name travelled intact from dock to airfield.
  • Narrow passage between rows of seats (theatre, cinema, church, aircraft cabin): the aisle down which latecomers sidle. The bridge is the pinch-point idea moved indoors: a going-way left open through a seated crowd, exactly as a ship's gangway is a going-way left open through rails, cargo, and rigging.
  • Clear-the-way shout — Gangway! (interjection, 19th c.): stand aside, let me through, coming through with something bulky. The bridge runs from noun to command by clipping the implied sentence clear the gangway down to the bare noun barked at full volume by stretcher-bearers, stagehands, dockers, and hurrying commuters.
  • Shipboard passageway and naval station (nautical, 18th–19th c.): the narrow deck passages fore and aft, the openings in the bulwarks for boarding, and in warships the muster positions where the crew fall in. The bridge is generalisation: every narrow going-way aboard, whether for feet, boats, or guns, took the same name.
  • Mining and industrial passage (technical): a level underground roadway or main haulage passage in a mine, especially in older coalfield usage. The bridge is the going-way idea carried underground: the main road along which coal, tubs, and men travel, as distinct from side workings.
  • Parliamentary and formal-assembly gangways (British): the aisles dividing bench blocks in the Commons, where hovering members buttonhole ministers. The bridge is again the indoor aisle, but with added political colour: to be buttonholed in the gangway is to be caught in transit, neither seated nor gone.

Register and connotation: gangway is plain, workmanlike, faintly salty — the vocabulary of docks, decks, and practical movement rather than architecture. Said flatly it is a thing of timber and steel; shouted with weight on the first syllable it becomes one of the bluntest imperatives in English, somewhere between excuse me and out of the way. The ferry-plank of the source sentence keeps the word at its gentlest and most pastoral: no steamship railings or airport hydraulics, just a pony clopping over boards above the water while the Brandywine slides beneath.
