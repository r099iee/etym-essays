Etymology of FLURRY — an English expressive formation of unknown, probably imitative, origin (no PIE ancestry can be established): Stage 1 (origin unknown, c. 17th c.): English dialectal expressive base flurr — "to throw with the hand, scatter, sprinkle" — an onomatopoeic/expressive formation of obscure origin; no PIE or other clear ancestor can be attested, and the OED marks the ultimate source unknown. (Debated: older dictionaries proposed a link to Latin furere "to rage, rave", but this is phonologically unsupported and now rejected; a connection with "flutter" is often floated but the forms do not match.) Stage 2 (formation, 17th c.): flurr + -y (English nominal/adjectival suffix) → English flurry (1620) — "commotion, great hurry" — attested as a noun meaning sudden excitement or confusion. Stage 3 (semantic extension, 18th–19th c.): flurry (1828, American English) — "a sudden squall or gust of wind, often carrying snow"; then generally "a brief, sudden fall of snow" (a snow flurry). Stage 4 (modern, present day): FLURRY — "a sudden brief period of commotion or excitement; a brief gust of wind or fall of snow."
