puffed (adjective & verb participle): The past tense and past participle of the verb to puff, describing an entity that has expelled or been acted upon by short, sudden blasts of wind, breath, smoke, or vapor; or characterized by being physically distended, swollen with air or fluid, visibly rounded, out of breath from exertion, or metaphorically inflated with vanity and exaggerated praise.

The term is fundamentally echoic (onomatopoeic) in origin, but through centuries of linguistic development it has radiated into a wide constellation of concrete, physiological, culinary, sartorial, and psychological domains:

  • Respiratory Exertion & Breathlessness: In colloquial and Commonwealth English, puffed (often paired as puffed out) describes an individual who is temporarily breathless, panting, and winded following vigorous physical exertion (e.g., after sprinting up the four flights of stairs, she was completely puffed). The semantic bridge arises directly from the audible sound of the lungs expelling short, labored blasts of air through pursed lips as the body attempts to remediate an oxygen deficit.
  • Localized Physical Swelling & Edema: Used descriptively to denote tissues, facial features, or body parts that are softly distended or swollen as a consequence of crying, exhaustion, allergy, lack of sleep, or fluid retention (e.g., her eyes were red and puffed after the ordeal; puffed-up ankles). Unlike bruised or inflamed, which suggest acute trauma or heat, puffed conveys a cushion-like, tender, and aerated or serous distension.
  • Discontinuous Emission of Vapor, Smoke, or Wind: Denotes the intermittent, billowing discharge of gaseous matter in small, distinct clouds (e.g., the steam locomotive puffed thick plumes into the winter sky; he puffed thoughtfully on his briar pipe). In the natural world, it describes erratic, gentle gusts of wind that rise and subside quickly (the breeze puffed against the sails before dying away entirely).
  • Culinary & Food Science Expansion: In culinary arts and commercial food manufacturing, puffed designates foodstuffs that have expanded into light, porous, cellular structures via the rapid vaporization of internal moisture under heat and pressure. Key examples include:
    • Puff pastry (French pâte feuilletée): A laminated dough layered with butter that separates into hundreds of crisp, delicate leaves as steam expands during baking.
    • Puffed grains (e.g., puffed rice, puffed wheat): Grains subjected to high pressure in an enclosed chamber and suddenly released into atmospheric pressure (a process known as 'gun puffing'), causing the moisture inside the starch granules to flash into steam and explode the grain into a light, airy foam.
  • Sartorial & Fashion Styling: In historical and modern dressmaking, puffed describes sleeves, bodices, or skirts constructed with gathered fullness that billows outward from the body (e.g., the puffed sleeves fashionable in Renaissance doublets, Romantic-era gowns of the 1830s, and 1980s couture). Historically, fabric was slashed and pulled through outer slits in decorative puffs (called panes).
  • Figurative Pride & Moral Conceit ('Puffed Up'): A deeply entrenched idiomatic and theological sense meaning arrogant, conceited, or self-aggrandizing. Famously crystallized in early English translations of the New Testament (such as William Tyndale and the King James Version of 1 Corinthians 8:1: 'Knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth'), this usage exploits the metaphor of an empty bladder or bullfrog inflating itself with air to appear formidable, mistaking hollow volume for genuine moral substance.
  • Commercial Hyperbole & Promotional 'Puffery': In media, law, and commerce, puffed refers to products, books, theatrical productions, or personalities that have been artificially inflated with extravagant, uncritical praise. In advertising law, puffery denotes subjective promotional exaggeration that no reasonable consumer would interpret as literal factual warranty.

The lexicographical charm of puffed lies in its sensory unity: whether describing a wind dying down in a series of tiny gasps, a runner gasping for breath, an eyelid swollen with tears, a grain of rice exploding into foam, or an arrogant politician swollen with pride, the root image remains that of air gathering, swelling outward, and softly bursting.
