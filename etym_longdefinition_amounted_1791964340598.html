amounted is the past tense and past participle of the intransitive verb amount (amount — amounted — amounted — amounting). It is a regular weak verb: the ending -ed carries no irregularity, and the whole interest of the form lies in the idiom it almost always belongs to, amount to.

In the source sentence the verb is used in its evaluative, not numerical, sense: “he has had what at times amounted to an almost unbelievable impact upon the language of western culture.” The subject is the fused relative “what”; amounted to asserts an equivalence — Cicero’s influence was, at certain moments, tantamount to an almost unbelievable impact. Nothing is being counted. The construction is a measured, slightly formal way of saying “was effectively” or “constituted”, and it carries a faint suggestion of surprise at the scale of the thing being described.

Grammar and valency

  • The verb is intransitive and cannot take a direct object: one says the bill amounted to £400, never the bill amounted £400.
  • In the equivalence sense the preposition to is obligatory before a noun phrase or gerund: amounted to a breach of contract, amounted to saying nothing. It is not followed by an infinitive.
  • It has no passive. A past participle (amounted) is not used attributively — there is no “an amounted sum”.
  • The subject may be a sum of money, a quantity, a mass noun, a collection of items, an action, a statement, or a fused relative such as what.
  • Because the verb is stative in the equivalence sense, it usually appears in the simple past or present rather than in progressive forms: it amounted to, not normally it was amounting to.

Sense 1 — to come to a total (numerical)

The oldest surviving modern sense: to add up to, to reach (a figure or quantity). The subject is typically a set of figures, costs, losses, evidence or receipts, and the complement is a sum or measure.

  • The repairs amounted to more than the value of the car.
  • Combined losses amounted to several million pounds.
  • His published output amounted to fourteen books and some two hundred essays.

This is the sense in which accountants and statisticians use the word, and it is the bridge to all the others: once a column of figures “amounts to” a total, the total is what the column is worth, and “worth” slides easily into “significance”.

Sense 2 — to be equivalent in effect (evaluative)

The dominant sense in educated prose and the one used in the source: to be tantamount to, to constitute effectively. The complement is usually an abstract noun — consent, admission, revolution, a confession, a declaration of war — and the implication is “in effect, if not in name”.

  • His silence amounted to consent.
  • The changes amounted to a quiet revolution in the department.
  • What at times amounted to an almost unbelievable impact — the source’s own construction, where the equivalence is deliberately hedged by at times.

Sense 3 — to result in, to develop into

A slight extension of sense 2, with an outcome rather than a status in view: the quarrel amounted to a full-blown crisis. Here the verb looks forward along a timeline; it says what the thing turned out to be, not merely what it equalled at a moment.

Sense 4 — the negative idioms

English is fond of using the verb to deflate a claim: not amount to much, amount to little, amount to nothing. The construction is a compact way of saying that something promised more than it delivered, and it is a staple of reviews, biographies and political commentary. It amounts to the same thing is the neutral counterpart, asserting equivalence between two courses of action.

Sense 5 — the obsolete literal sense

From the fifteenth to the seventeenth century amount could still mean “to go up, ascend, mount”, preserving the physical image of its French original. That sense is dead in standard English, surviving only as a fossil inside the financial metaphor of sums mounting up.

The noun, for comparison

Amount is also a noun meaning a total or quantity — a large amount of work, any amount of time, the amount due. It is used with mass nouns, whereas number is used with count nouns; an amount of people is widely criticised for that reason. Fixed phrases include amount due, principal amount, amount in controversy (law) and amount realized (tax).

Register

The numerical sense is neutral and everyday. The evaluative amount to is slightly formal, at home in journalism, biography, legal drafting and academic prose, where it allows a writer to make a strong claim while appearing to measure rather than assert. That is exactly its function in the source, where at times amounted to converts a sweeping tribute into a calibrated one.
