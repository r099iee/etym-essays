The story of spits in The Hobbit — "the very mutton off the spits" — is the story of a very old piece of kitchen ironware and of the word that named it for two thousand years and more. But the English noun spit is entangled with an older, unrelated verb that happens to sound exactly like it, so this essay must follow two lineages at once: the pointed rod that pierced the meat, and the mouth that ejected the spittle.

The rod. The roasting spit goes back to the invention of fire-cooked meat itself: thread the meat on a pointed stick, prop it over the coals, and turn it — that is the whole technology, and the word that names it is nearly as old as the gesture. The English noun descends from Old English spitu, "a spit, a pointed rod", from the reconstructed Proto-Germanic *spituz, "spit, skewer", whose continental cousins include Old High German spiz, "point, spit", and Middle Dutch spit. Beyond Germanic, the standard handbooks connect the group to the Proto-Indo-European root *spey-, "sharp point, pointed stick, spike", the same root that gives Latin spīca, "ear of grain, point", and spīna, "thorn, spine" — and English spine, spica, and (through French) spire are its distant relatives. The semantic core is a sharp, tapering point: the rod that pierces, the thorn that pricks, the grain-ear that ends in a point. That one idea — a long sharp point — is what holds the whole family together, and it is exactly what a spit is.

The medieval kitchen. For most of English history the spit was the centre of serious cooking. A great household roasted whole oxen, sheep, and pigs on spits as long as a man is tall, set in firedogs over the hearth, with a dripping-pan beneath to catch the fat; the turnspit, whether the boy who turned the handle or the turnspit dog bred to run inside the wheel, kept the joint rotating so it cooked evenly on every side. The word appears throughout medieval and early modern English in this concrete kitchen sense — "on the spit", "roast on the spit", "spit-roast" — and the mechanical roasting-jack of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a clockwork and later a steam-powered machine, replaced the boy and the dog without replacing the word: the meat was still on the spit.

  • Sound shifts. Within English the changes are small: Old English spitu → Middle English spite / spete (the final vowel reduced and the spelling jostled) → modern spit, with the long "i" of the modern form settled by the sixteenth century; the plural spits is a regular English inflection. The Germanic consonant frame — sp- plus vowel plus -t — is remarkably stable across two thousand years, because the word descended into English from its own Germanic ancestor rather than being borrowed through another language.
  • Semantic drift 1 — from rod to land. By the seventeenth century the pointed shape of the spit had been extended to the landscape: a "sand-spit" is a long narrow tongue of sand or shingle running out from a coast into the sea, exactly the shape of the rod laid flat. Today this geographical sense is one of the word's two great survivors, in coastal science ("spit", "cuspate foreland", "baymouth bar") and in everyday phrases like "a spit of land".
  • Semantic drift 2 — the spade's depth. A second, now-obsolete extension made "a spit" a measure: the depth to which a spade-blade can be driven, roughly a foot — "dig to a spit's depth". Here the pointed blade of the spade, itself a spit-like implement, gives its name to the hole it digs; gardeners and labourers used the measure for centuries.
  • The separate verb — saliva. The other spit, the verb of ejecting saliva, has no connection with the rod. Its Old English ancestors spīwan and spǣtan continue the Proto-Germanic *spīwaną, "to spit, vomit", from the Proto-Indo-European imitative root *spyēu-, the same sound-symbolic root that produced Latin spuere, Greek ptýō, and Sanskrit ṣṭhīv — a root that mimics the sound of spitting and is therefore one of the most conservative words in the language, recognisably the same "spit" for six thousand years. The verb bred idioms: "spit and polish" (the frantic cleaning before inspection), "the spitting image" and "the very spit of" (exact likeness — from the old belief that a child's spittle carried the parent's nature), "to spit at" (contempt), "to spit it out" (to speak at last). In modern British English "it's spitting" means light rain is falling. All of these belong to the saliva verb, not the rod.
  • The verb from the noun. A third, smaller word completes the tangle: the verb "to spit" in the sense "to thread on a spit, to impale" — "spitted meat", "a spitted bird" — is a simple conversion of the rod noun, and it belongs with the rod, not the mouth. "Spitted" in early modern English most often means "impaled on a spit" (and, by metaphor, "pierced, transfixed"), while the past tense of the saliva verb is "spat". The two verbs, one from the rod and one from the mouth, have coexisted in the same spelling for centuries without ever merging.
  • Register and the decline of the open hearth. As closed kitchen ranges replaced the open hearth in the nineteenth century, the roasting spit withdrew from everyday domestic use and grew archaic and rustic, surviving in period writing, in the phrase "on a spit", and in the grand cookery of spit-roasting. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the barbecue revival and the rotisserie gave the spit a second life: the "rotisserie" is literally the "turning spit" (French rôtir "to roast" plus the same rod idea), and spit-roasting whole animals over fire is once again fashionable. The word is therefore both ancient and newly useful: a troll camp in The Hobbit roasts mutton on spits in exactly the way the word describes, and a modern weekend barbecue does the same.
  • Tolkien's spits. In the sentence "pinched the very mutton off the spits", the spits are doing quiet period work: they locate the trolls' cooking in a pre-industrial world of open fires and whole roasted animals, and they make the proposed theft vivid and physical — the meat must be lifted, still hot, off the very rods over the fire. It is the small, concrete, archaic word doing its small, concrete, archaic job.
