ENTICED — the biography of fire becoming flattery. The trail begins with the Latin noun titio — a firebrand, a brand plucked from the hearth, glowing and smoking — a word whose own prehistory is obscure. Latin lexicographers have no securely attested Indo-European cognates for titio, and its doubled tit- shape looks like one of those expressive, half-onomatopoeic formations Latin used for crackling, flickering things (compare Latin titillare, to tickle, and the reduplicated baby-talk of many such nouns). Whatever its origin, the noun was enormously productive in the late spoken Latin that became the Romance languages, spawning a whole family of "firebrand" words that survive to this day: Old French tison, Italian tizzone, Spanish tizón, Portuguese tição, Catalan tió — and the derived Spanish verb tiznar, "to blacken with soot". No PIE root is asserted for titio, and the uncertainty of its deeper ancestry is part of the word's honest record. Alongside these nouns, spoken Latin built two prefixed verbs by setting the firebrand at the center of an action. Adding in- ("in, on") produced Vulgar Latin *intitiāre, literally "to set fire to, to kindle"; adding ad- ("toward") produced *attitiāre, "to poke or stir up a fire". Old French inherited enticier from the first of these, and at first the French verb meant simply to stir up — to provoke, to excite, to incite, in the way that a firebrand provokes a blaze. This is the sense with which enticier crossed the Channel: Middle English enticen (c. 1300) meant "to incite, instigate", and early English uses it in contexts of provoking anger, stirring up quarrels, and instigating wrongdoing. The word was a verb of agitation before it was a verb of attraction. Then a remarkable slippage occurred — one of the most instructive in English. To "stir up" a person's passions came to mean to awaken his desires, and to awaken desire is to attract. By late Middle English the aggressive, instigating sense faded, and the modern meaning took over: to entice is to allure or attract by holding out pleasure or advantage, no longer to goad by provocation. The noun enticement and the adjective enticing registered the same softening, and the word settled into its modern position on the moral scale, below the heaviness of tempt and above the superficiality of allure.
  • The sister verb survived in French: attiser still means "to stoke, to stir up a fire, to inflame" (anger, hatred), preserving the fiery sense that English entice has left behind.
  • The fire is still faintly visible in English: an enticing prospect is one that kindles desire; to be enticed is to be drawn toward a warmth.
  • Literary record: entice appears in the Authorized Version ("entice thee, saying, Let us go serve other gods", Deuteronomy 13:6), in Shakespeare, and throughout the moral and cautionary prose of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, always with a hint of craft and wiles.
By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word had become the natural verb for intellectual and fashionable lures as well as bodily pleasures. In the Gibbon passage the historian's past participle is used with exact moral discrimination: the essayist pairs enticed with driven to oppose the two dishonorable ways of taking up a subject — pulled from ahead by flattering causes, pushed from behind by the pressures of the age. The enticed historian surrenders his independence to something pleasurable, and the resulting scholarship is subtly compromised. The word here carries its oldest residue of blame: to be enticed is to be quietly seduced from the truth by an attractive offer. The twentieth century wore the word's edge smooth. Advertising adopted it wholesale — enticing offers, enticing deals, an enticingly low price — and in commercial use it is now mildly positive and neutral, meaning little more than "attractive". The moral weight that once clung to enticement (enticement to crime, enticement to desertion) has largely drained away, though the older gravity survives in literary prose and in the memory of the word's fiery ancestry.
  • Semantic drift: firebrand → to kindle → to stir up passions → to awaken desire → to attract by pleasure or advantage → (modern, commercial) merely attractive.
  • Register: from legal and moral censure toward neutral advertising language, while keeping a literary reserve.
  • Modern state: enticed is the passive participle of an ordinary verb, at home in narrative prose and marketing alike, with the fire of its Latin ancestor reduced to a warm glow.
The evolution of enticed is thus a study in how a word about fire and provocation cooled into a word about charm — and how the cool modern use still carries, beneath the surface, the image of a brand that sets a blaze.
