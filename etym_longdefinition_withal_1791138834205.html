withal is an archaic or literary adverb (formerly also preposition and conjunction) meaning in addition, besides, moreover, as well, along with all that, and in older use with it, thereby, therewith, notwithstanding. In the Dickens sentence — “cold, bleak, biting weather: foggy withal” — it simply adds one more item to the catalogue: foggy as well as cold, bleak and biting. The word is no longer productive in ordinary speech and survives mainly as a conscious archaism, in the Bible, Shakespeare and legal formulae.

Grammatically it fused the preposition with + the quantifier all (“with all that, with everything taken together”). From that transparent origin it developed a family of related connective senses that modern English now expresses with different words. All share the idea of “adding to the pile” or “carrying along with.”

  • Additive adverb = moreover, besides, also, too: the dominant modern dictionary sense. “He was learned, and wise withal” = learned and wise in addition. “Foggy withal” = foggy too. This is the only sense still occasionally met in essays, hymns and pastiche.
  • Comitative / instrumental adverb = with it, thereby, therewith: very common in Shakespeare and the King James Bible. “To fight withal” = to fight with (that weapon). “Content withal” = satisfied with it. Modern English would say with it, with that, thereby.
  • Concessive adverb = nevertheless, notwithstanding, yet: “Rich withal unhappy” = rich and yet unhappy. The addition carries a hint of contrast: despite all that, this too.
  • Obsolete preposition = with: “came withal a host” = came with a host. This use died by about 1700, leaving only the fused adverb.
  • Obsolete noun (rare): in Scots and dialect, a withal could mean something furnished or wherewithal, but this is normally spelled wherewithal today.

Register and usage notes:

  • Register: formal, bookish, biblical, legal. Using it in conversation today sounds deliberately old-fashioned, humorous or pulpit-like. Dickens uses it to give the opening weather report a chronicle-like gravity.
  • Position: almost always postposed — after the adjective or clause it augments: “foggy withal,” “wise withal,” “poor withal honest.” It is rarely sentence-initial like moreover.
  • Punctuation: often set off by commas or, as in Dickens, by a colon that introduces the whole weather list, with withal closing the list.
  • Distinction from with all: two words means “accompanied by everyone / everything” (“he came with all his friends”); one word means “in addition” (“he came, tired withal”). Stress also differs: with ALL vs withAL /wɪˈðɔːl/.
  • Modern replacements: also, too, as well, besides, moreover, furthermore, in addition, together with that, at the same time for the additive sense; with it, thereby for the instrumental sense; yet, nevertheless for the concessive sense.

Why the secondary senses arose is transparent: if you have “all that” with you, you can be said to do something “with it” (instrumental), to be something “in addition to it” (additive), or to be something “despite it” (concessive). The semantic bridge is always co-presence: having X along with Y. Over time English preferred more specialised connectives, so withal narrowed to a stylistic marker of age and solemnity rather than a neutral connector. Learners should recognise it passively for reading the Bible, Shakespeare, Milton, Bunyan, Austen and Dickens, but should not use it productively except for deliberate archaic effect.
