- 1640s–1700s: Emerged during the turmoil of the English Civil War and early modern political tractates, denoting anything tending to subvert, overthrow, or destroy established laws, monarchies, and religious constitutions.
- 1750s–1850s: Broadened during the Enlightenment and Age of Revolutions to encompass radical philosophical and scientific theories that challenged ancient dogmas, church authority, and aristocratic privileges.
- 1890s–1950s: Heavily politicized during the Cold War and era of total warfare, becoming a standard intelligence and legal classification for secret communist, anarchist, or anti-state agents working to undermine democratic or authoritarian states from within.
- 1960s–1980s: Adopted enthusiastically by cultural theory, avant-garde art movements, feminist theory, and poststructuralism to celebrate transgressive literature, satirical deconstruction, and non-conformist lifestyle choices.
- 1990s–Present: Retains its serious political and philosophical depth in academic and geopolitical contexts, while experiencing slight commercial bleaching in fashion, advertising, and pop culture to mean trendy, cheeky, or unconventional.
