A MESSENGER'S WORD: THE BIRTH OF NUNTIUS

The biography of renounce begins with a messenger. At its root stands Latin nuntius, 'messenger, bearer of news', the man sent to bring word - and the verb nuntiare, 'to announce, to report, to bring word', which was formed from it. The origin of nuntius itself is lost: the traditional etymology traces it to a Proto-Indo-European root *neu-, 'to shout, to call out', but modern scholarship is cautious, and de Vaan records the word as of obscure, probably Italic origin, with no secure Indo-European ancestry. The honest account is that Latin nuntius simply appears in the record as a messenger - a word of the streets and the forum whose deeper roots are unknowable. What is certain is the verb built on it: nuntiare, the workhorse of Roman announcement, used for everything from reporting a victory to announcing a death.

RENUNTIARE: THE REPORT THAT BECOMES A REPUDIATION

The prefix re- gave the verb a double life. Its first meaning was spatial and neutral: renuntiare meant 'to bring back word, to report back' - the return of the messenger with news. But Roman political and legal practice gave the compound its decisive force in two directions. In the civic sphere, the officials who presided over elections renuntiabant the results - they officially reported them back to the people - and, with exquisite Roman economy, the verb also served for the opposite movement: a magistrate who laid down his office was said renuntiari, to be 'reported as having relinquished', so that the word carried the formal act of resignation. In the legal sphere the prefix turned hostile: renuntiare came to mean 'to declare against', to send back word repudiating a claim, a connection, or an obligation. From 'bringing word back' the verb had come to 'bringing word back against' - announcement and repudiation in a single stem, which is exactly the tension the modern word keeps.

THE PASSAGE THROUGH MEDIEVAL LATIN AND OLD FRENCH

  • Late Latin and the early medieval Church took renuntiare into their own vocabulary: renuntiare saeculo, 'to renounce the world', became the standing phrase for the monk's or convert's formal rejection of worldly life, and the liturgical tradition of renouncing the devil and his works descends from this usage.
  • Old French inherited the verb as renoncer, and the Romance form kept both the legal and the religious values: to renoncer a claim or inheritance, and to renoncer le diable.
  • Sound changes along the way: Latin renuntiare, with its unaccented medial syllables, was compressed in the vernaculars; the -nti- cluster was simplified and the verb's ending reshaped, giving Old French renoncer.
  • Middle English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century as renouncen, recorded around 1375, and it entered the language already carrying the formal, declarative sense: to declare against a claim, a right, or a belief, or to disown a person.
SEMANTIC DRIFT: FROM REPORT TO RENUNCIATION

  • 14th-15th centuries: Middle English renouncen means 'to give up formally, to relinquish by declaration', used of rights, offices, and allegiances; Chaucer uses the family in legal and moral contexts.
  • 16th century: the Reformation and the wars of religion fix the religious sense - to renounce the papacy, the devil, one's former faith - and the word becomes a verb of confession and conversion, used on both sides of the divide.
  • 17th-18th centuries: the legal and diplomatic sense matures: renouncing the throne, renouncing citizenship, renouncing an inheritance become fixed formulaic uses in constitutional and international law.
  • 19th century: the word spreads to the moral and personal sphere - renouncing pleasure, renouncing revenge, renouncing the world - and Victorian moral writing makes renunciation (the noun, from Latin renuntiatio) a central ethical ideal.
  • 20th-21st centuries: 'renounce violence' and 'renounce terrorism' enter the vocabulary of diplomacy and counterterrorism, where a public renunciation is a formal, often verified, act; the word retains its performative, formal character in law, religion, and politics alike.
SOCIO-CULTURAL FACTORS AND LITERARY ATTESTATIONS

  • The baptismal liturgy preserves the oldest performative use: 'I renounce the devil and all his works' - a public declaration by which the speaker formally severs allegiance.
  • Shakespeare uses renounce for solemn public abandonment (a king renouncing his crown, a lover renouncing the world), cementing the word's dramatic weight in English.
  • In nineteenth-century fiction and ethical writing, renunciation becomes a virtue in itself - the deliberate sacrifice of personal desire - and 'self-renunciation' enters the moral vocabulary.
  • The legal phrase 'renunciation of citizenship' is a modern cornerstone of nationality law; the word is still the formal verb by which one declares the surrender of a status.
  • The question on this card - 'will they rather not renounce her?' - draws on the word's harshest face: the formal, public act of disowning a person, the ancient 'declaring against' made final.
DIRECTION OF CHANGE

Renounce has drifted from neutral reporting to hostile repudiation and then widened again into every kind of formal giving up. It has never lost the idea of the messenger: to renounce is still, deep down, to send back word - the word that one no longer holds. The verb remains formal, performative, and public, at home in the courtroom, the liturgy, the diplomatic protocol, and the moment when someone declares, before witnesses, that the bond is broken.
