corporeal is a formal adjective meaning "of, relating to, or having the nature of a body; physical, material, tangible". It is the adjectival counterpart of the noun body, drawn from Latin corpus ("body"), and it is most characteristically used in explicit or implicit contrast with what is spiritual, mental, incorporeal, or immaterial. The word carries a distinct philosophical and theological resonance: it belongs to the vocabulary of the mind–body, matter–spirit, and material–immaterial distinctions that have structured Western thought since antiquity. In the card's context, "a corporeal lodgement" is a physical place or body in which ethereal, immaterial forces seek to reside and manifest—the word marks the shift from the abstract and spiritual to the concrete and material.

Sense 1: The core sense—"of the body, bodily". In its primary, neutral meaning, corporeal describes anything pertaining to the body of a living creature (especially a human being): corporeal needs (food, shelter), corporeal existence (life in the flesh), corporeal form (a physical shape). Unlike the more clinical somatic, corporeal is not limited to physiology: it refers to the body as a whole, with all its material and sensory reality. This sense is used across law (see Sense 3), religion, philosophy, and ordinary formal prose.

Sense 2: The oppositional sense—"material, as opposed to spiritual or immaterial". The word's defining feature is its use as the term of contrast to the immaterial. In theology and metaphysics, corporeal means "having a body, material, belonging to the physical world", as opposed to incorporeal ("without a body"), spiritual, ethereal, or mental. Ghosts are said to lack corporeal substance; angels, in many theologies, are incorporeal beings. In the card's sentence, the "shifting ethereal forces" are by definition non-corporeal; their search for "a corporeal lodgement" is a search for a material anchor, a body or place in which they can take physical form and become manifest. This sense gives the word its weight: corporeal names the heavy, the bounded, the mortal, the visible—everything the spirit is not.

Sense 3: The legal sense—"corporeal property / hereditaments". In law, the adjective survives in fixed technical phrases. Corporeal property (or corporeal hereditaments) means tangible physical property—land, buildings, goods—as opposed to incorporeal property such as rights, easements, and intellectual property. This legal usage directly continues the philosophical distinction: corporeal things can be seen, touched, and physically possessed; incorporeal things are abstract claims and rights. The phrase corporeal hereditament is standard in the older English law of real property (Blackstone, 18th century).

Sense 4: Register, derivatives, and related forms. Corporeal is a formal, literary, and technical word. Its noun is corporeality (the state of being corporeal; also corporealness), and its antonym is incorporeal ("without a body"). It belongs to the same Latin family as corpse, corps, corporation, corpus, and corpuscle, all from corpus "body". In everyday speech one is far more likely to say physical or bodily; corporeal is chosen precisely when the speaker wants the philosophical or theological contrast with the immaterial to be felt.

Synonym spectrum and boundaries.

  • Somatic: Similar because both mean "of the body"; differs because somatic is strictly clinical and physiological (often opposed to psychological), lacking the metaphysical soul-versus-body contrast of corporeal.
  • Physical: Similar because both mean "material, tangible"; differs because physical is the generic term covering all of nature and matter (laws of physics, physical objects), while corporeal is anchored to the idea of a living body and its contrast with spirit.
  • Bodily: Similar because both mean "pertaining to the body"; differs because bodily is plain, everyday English, while corporeal is formal and carries philosophical weight.
  • Carnal: Similar because both relate to the flesh; differs because carnal is laden with moral and sensual judgment (carnal desires, sins of the flesh), while corporeal is neutral and descriptive.
  • Material: Similar because both mean "of matter"; differs because material can refer to raw stuff in general (building materials), while corporeal retains the sense of a formed, living, or organized body.
  • Incarnate: Similar because both involve taking on a body; differs because incarnate is specifically theological (flesh-taking) and implies embodiment of a pre-existing spirit, while corporeal simply describes the bodily state.

Conclusion. Corporeal is the precise English word for everything that has a body, exists in space, and can be touched—set over against everything ghostly, spiritual, and immaterial. Its use in the card transforms a spatial description into a metaphysical one: the ethereal forces, by seeking a corporeal lodgement, are seeking to cross from the realm of pure spirit into the realm of flesh, matter, and tangible presence.
