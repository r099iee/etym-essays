Germanic and Merovingian Roots: The Name of Kings

The toponym Louisiana is an eponymous territorial creation derived from the French royal personal name Louis. Etymologically, Louis does not have a Latin or Gallo-Roman origin; rather, it represents the Romance phonetic evolution of a dithematic Proto-Germanic warrior name reconstructed as *Hlūdawīgaz. This compound was formed from two distinct Germanic roots:

  • The first element, Proto-Germanic *hlūdaz ("loud, heard, famous, renowned"), traces to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ḱlew- ("to hear"), the source of Greek kleos ("glory, fame") and Latin inclutus ("renowned").
  • The second element, Proto-Germanic *wīgą ("fight, battle, conflict"), derives from Proto-Indo-European *weyk- ("to conquer, overcome"), source of Latin vincere ("to defeat").

Together, the name signified "one who is famous in battle" or a "renowned warrior". In the fifth century CE, as the Western Roman Empire collapsed, the Salian Frankish chieftain who unified the Frankish tribes and founded the Merovingian dynasty bore this name in Old Frankish as *Hlōdawīg. In contemporary Latin chronicles, scribes Latinized his name as Clodovicus or Chlodovechus (giving rise to Modern French Clovis). Over subsequent centuries, through regular Romance sound shifts in Gallo-Romance (the loss of the initial aspirate /h/ and lenition of the intervocalic consonants), the name was also Latinized as Ludovicus, which in Old French regularly evolved into Looïs, and eventually Modern French Louis.

The Sun King and the Naming Ritual of April 9, 1682

Between the accession of Louis I (the Pious) in the ninth century and the French Revolution in the eighteenth century, nineteen monarchs of France bore the name Louis. None held greater imperial power or presided over a more formidable military apparatus than Louis XIV (reigned 1643–1715), known throughout Europe as Louis le Grand or the "Sun King". Under his rule, France pursued vigorous colonial mercantilism across the globe.

In early 1682, the intrepid French explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, accompanied by Henri de Tonti, French coureurs des bois, and Native American allies, canoed down the entire length of the Mississippi River from the Illinois country. On April 9, 1682, having reached the river’s mouth at the Gulf of Mexico (near modern Venice, Louisiana), La Salle held a formal legal ceremony of possession.

He erected a wooden cross and a column bearing the royal coat of arms of France, buried a lead plate inscribed with the king's name, and solemnly proclaimed that he took possession, in the name of the most high, mighty, invincible, and victorious Prince, Louis the Great, King of France and Navarre, of the entire Mississippi River basin and all waters flowing into it. He formally christened this colossal territory La Louisiane, appending the Latinate geographical suffix -ane / -iane (cognate with English -ana, derived from the Latin adjectival suffix -iānus, signifying "pertaining to, belonging to the land of").

From Continental Empire to American Statehood

In early French documents and maps (such as those by cartographers Jean-Baptiste Franquelin and Guillaume Delisle), the territory was labeled La Louisiane. When English writers, merchants, and diplomats discussed the colony in the eighteenth century, they Latinized and anglicized the French feminine form into Louisiana, adhering to the standard English practice for naming American colonies (compare Carolina from Charles, Georgia from George, and Virginia from the Virgin Queen).

Following the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau in 1762, the territory west of the Mississippi River passed to Spain, becoming the Spanish colony of La Luisiana. When Napoleon Bonaparte coerced Spain into retroceding the territory to France under the Treaty of San Ildefonso in 1800, his dreams of rebuilding a French Western Hemisphere empire were thwarted by the Haitian Revolution and impending war with Great Britain. In 1803, Napoleon sold the entire imperial territory to the United States in the Louisiana Purchase.

Upon taking control, the United States Congress initially divided the vast purchase into the northern District of Louisiana and the southern Territory of Orleans. When the Territory of Orleans was admitted to the Union on April 30, 1812, its residents successfully petitioned Congress to adopt the ancient historic name for their new state: Louisiana. Today, the name remains a permanent geographic testament to the seventeenth-century monarch whose explorers first claimed the heart of the continent.
