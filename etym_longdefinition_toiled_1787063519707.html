toiled is the past tense and past participle of the verb toil, and in the source text — Tolkien's The Hobbit — it describes days of exhausting search: "day by day they toiled in parties searching for paths up the mountain-side." To toil is to work hard, continuously, and often laboriously — physical or mental effort that drains the worker; toiled names that sustained effort in the past. The word belongs to the oldest stratum of English vocabulary for heavy labour, and it still carries the strain of its French ancestor, which meant "to stir, entangle, and drag about".

PRIMARY SENSE — HARD, SUSTAINED WORK

  • To work with great and continuous effort: "they toiled in the fields from dawn till dusk", "generations toiled in the mine", "he toiled over the manuscript for years".
  • The effort is prolonged and wearing: toiling is not a burst of energy but an endurance — day after day, hour after hour, in service of a task.
  • The work may be physical or mental: farmers toil, miners toil, students toil over examinations, scholars toil over texts; the word stresses the cost of the effort.
  • The past form toiled is used both simply of the worker (they toiled) and with a goal or path (they toiled to reach the summit; they toiled at the task; they toiled up the slope).
  • In the source text the toiling is desperate and purposeful: the party searches day by day, in parties, for a path up the mountain-side — an image of stubborn, exhausting, collective labour against a hostile landscape.

SECONDARY SENSE — TOIL IN PHRASE AND IDIOM

  • "Toil and trouble" — the famous alliterative pair from Shakespeare's Macbeth ("Double, double toil and trouble"), where toil means hard work compounded by trouble, and by extension dire labour and mischief.
  • "Toil up / toil over / toil at" — to move or work with great difficulty: toil up a steep hill, toil over a difficult book, toil at one's trade.
  • The noun toil = hard, tiring work: "the sweet rewards of toil", "a life of toil"; the word is used with a respect for honest effort that plainer "work" lacks.
  • "Toils of..." (plural noun) is a DIFFERENT word — see below — meaning nets, snares, or entanglements.

THE HOMONYM — TOILS AS NETS AND SNARES

  • A wholly separate word toil (usually plural toils) means "a net, snare, or trap", and by extension "entanglement, snare": "caught in the toils of the law", "in the toils of passion".
  • This toil comes from French toile "cloth, web, net" (the source of English toile and toilet), from Latin tela "web, woven fabric"; it is unrelated to the labour verb.
  • The two words have been homographs since the Middle Ages, and in literary English the shared spelling lets writers play on them: the labourer is "in the toils" (ensnared) of his toil (labour).
  • On this card, however, the word is the past verb toiled — the labour sense only; the homonym is noted here to prevent confusion when reading older literature.

REGISTER, TONE, AND RELATED FORMS

  • Register: toil is more formal, dignified, and literary than "work" or "labour" in everyday use; it implies struggle and weariness, and it usually carries sympathy for the worker.
  • Because of its biblical and moral history, toil has an ethical colour: toil is the honest effort that deserves reward, the burden inherited from Adam's curse ("In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread").
  • Related forms: toiler = one who toils; toilsome = requiring hard work; toilful (rare) = laborious; the participle toiled is the form on this card.
  • The modern flavour is slightly old-fashioned: a contemporary writer reaches for "toiled" for dignity or strain, where everyday speech would say "worked hard" — but the word is fully current in literary, narrative, and journalistic prose.

SEMANTIC BRIDGE — FROM TUGGING TO LABOURING

  • Old French toillier meant "to stir, mix, entangle" and, in a natural extension, "to pull or drag about" — the image of struggling with something heavy, tangled, or resistant.
  • Middle English toilen kept this sense of struggling, contending, and dragging; from "to pull about" the verb slid to "to labour hard", the struggling becoming steady effort.
  • Modern toil has lost the tugging and kept the labour: no one thinks of ropes or tangles when saying "toiled", yet the strain of the ancestor still glows in the word's muscular, weary tone.

SUMMARY

  • toiled = worked hard, continuously, and with great effort; struggled or laboured, physically or mentally.
  • In the source text it conveys the exhausting, day-by-day, purposeful labour of a party searching the mountain-side for a way up — the word's strain and dignity both in play.
