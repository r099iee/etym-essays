litter (noun and verb) — the dominant modern sense of litter is trash or waste material — paper, cans, bottles, wrappers — carelessly left lying in an open or public place. Unlike garbage awaiting collection, litter is refuse that has been strewn: it is waste that has escaped the bin and lies scattered on streets, pavements, parks, and countryside. In the example context of this card, "The yard was covered with litter after the party," the word names exactly that — the dispersed debris of celebration, dropped where people stood and left where it fell.

Primary senses
  • Scattered refuse (modern dominant sense) — "litter on the beach", "an anti-litter campaign": waste paper, cans, bottles, and packaging left in open public places; the verb "to litter" means to strew such waste carelessly.
  • A group of young animals born at one birth — "a litter of puppies", "a litter of kittens": the offspring produced in a single birth by a mammal, typically dogs, cats, pigs, and other multiparous animals.
  • Cat litter — the absorbent granular material (clay, silica, or plant fibre) used in trays for pet cats to urinate and defecate in, sold commercially since the mid-twentieth century.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings
  • A stretcher or carrying-couch — a "litter" is a portable bed, stretcher, or covered couch carried on poles, used to transport the sick, wounded, or (historically) the wealthy and royal; the "sedan" was one form, and the medical litter still carries casualties today.
  • Straw bedding for animals (archaic / dialectal) — straw, rushes, or hay spread on a stable floor as bedding for livestock; the historical core sense from which most others developed.
  • Forest litter — in ecology and forestry, the layer of dead leaves, twigs, and organic debris lying on the forest floor; a technical sense that preserves the oldest "strewn material" idea.
  • Verb "to litter" — to scatter waste carelessly ("please do not litter"); to make untidy ("the room was littered with papers"); and, transitively of animals, to give birth to a litter.
  • Compound and derived words — "litterbug" (a person who litters, coined in the late 1940s), "anti-litter law", "cat litter", "litter bin".
  • Figurative uses — "a litter of papers" (a scattered disorder of documents), "to be littered with" (to be full of scattered examples: "the history is littered with failures").

Historical semantic bridges
  • The whole family of meanings grows out of one Latin idea: the bed and the act of lying down. Latin lectus (bed) produced a Late Latin word for "bedding," and that bedding — straw spread on a floor — is the pivot of the entire history.
  • Bedding → refuse: straw and rushes scattered on stable floors were, from a town's point of view, the first "litter" — strewn material underfoot; as urban life grew, any strewn refuse, and finally waste left in public places, inherited the name. The ecological "forest litter" preserves the older "strewn layer" sense directly.
  • Bedding → offspring: because sows, bitches, and she-cats give birth lying on the straw of their bedding, the word was transferred by the late 15th century to the young animals themselves, born "on the litter."
  • Bedding → couch: the same Latin base also produced the portable bed carried on poles — the aristocratic "litter" of queens and nobles — and from there the modern medical stretcher.
  • Bedding → cat box: the twentieth-century commercial product simply revived the ancient idea of "spread bedding material" for a house-pet's tray.

Modern technical and professional usage
  • Medicine — a "litter" is standard equipment for carrying the injured in the field (a stretcher or casualty-carrying frame).
  • Ecology and forestry — "forest litter" and "leaf litter" are technical terms for the decomposing organic layer of a forest floor.
  • Zoology — "litter size" is the standard term in animal breeding and genetics for the number of offspring in a single birth.
  • Waste management and law — "litter" is a legal category of minor waste offence, distinct from "fly-tipping" or "dumping" which are larger-scale.
  • Pet industry — "cat litter" and its products are a major commercial vocabulary.

Boundaries against near-synonyms
  • Unlike garbage or rubbish, which name collected household waste, litter specifically names waste scattered in open public space.
  • Unlike brood, which applies to birds and fish (and human families of many children), litter is restricted to the young of mammals born at one birth.
  • Unlike debris, which implies the broken remnants of destruction, litter implies casually discarded everyday items.
  • Unlike stretcher (the modern hospital device), litter in its carrying sense retains the idea of a bed-like platform, while a stretcher is a plain frame.
