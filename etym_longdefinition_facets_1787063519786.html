facets (noun, plural) — the flat, polished surfaces cut onto a gemstone, or, more broadly, any one of the many distinct sides, aspects, or phases of a complex thing. In the Hobbit passage the Arkenstone 'was like a globe with a thousand facets': the word fuses the gem-cutting sense with the dazzling multiplicity of the stone's faces, each catching the light in its own way.

Core meaning: the singular facet means 'one side or face of a many-sided object'. The plural facets emphasises multiplicity — the pattern of many faces together forming a single whole.

  • 1. Gemmology (primary, concrete sense): a small, flat, polished face cut into a precious stone so that light enters, is reflected internally off the pavilion, and returns through the crown to produce brilliance and fire. Diamonds and rubies are 'brilliant-cut' with dozens of facets; the plural facets names the whole cut surface of a stone.
  • 2. Transferred concrete senses: by analogy, a flat side of any cut crystal, a segment of a beaded surface, or a face of any multifaceted object (e.g. a honeycomb, a machined part, or a screen on a display).
  • 3. Figurative sense — the most productive modern use: an aspect, dimension, or side of a complex matter, situation, or personality, e.g. the many facets of her character, one facet of the problem. A thing described as 'multi-faceted' is rich, complex, and to be seen from several viewpoints.
  • 4. Scientific and technical uses: in crystallography, a natural flat face of a crystal; in computer graphics, a polygonal face of a 3D mesh or model; in geometry, a 'facet' of a polytope is a face of maximal dimension.
  • 5. The word's ghost meaning: facet literally means 'a little face' (French facette), and that etymological sense still glimmers through: each facet is literally one 'face' of the object turned toward the observer.

Register and collocation: 'facets' is formal to neutral. Common collocations: the many facets of, a facet of the problem, every facet of, multi-faceted. In gemmology it is specialist trade vocabulary, but the figurative plural is widely understood and lightly elegant.

How the senses connect: all the meanings rest on the same mental image — a whole made of many small flat faces. A diamond's beauty is not one surface but the play of light across dozens of planes, each cut at a precise angle; a personality or a policy question is beautiful or interesting in the same way, revealing a different pattern as it turns. The figurative sense is therefore not a weakening of the concrete one but a transfer of its geometry to the abstract. When someone says a book 'has many facets', they are asking you to view it, as it were, rotating it in the light.

Degree and scope: 'facets' is almost always plural in the figurative use, because its whole point is multiplicity — a single 'facet' is only meaningful as one of several. Where a synonym like aspect can comfortably name just one side ('one aspect of the matter'), saying 'one facet of the matter' already implies that other faces remain to be examined. The plural therefore does more than count: it hints at hidden richness yet to be seen.

Practical distinctions: in everyday writing, prefer facet when you want the glint of gem-cutting imagery or a sense of polished, countable planes; prefer aspect when you want plain, neutral enumeration. In technical contexts the word is precise: crystallographers count the natural facets of a crystal, and computer modellers describe the number of facets (polygons) in a 3D mesh, where more facets mean smoother surfaces but heavier computation.
