Adulation is uncritical, excessive admiration or flattery, praise that goes beyond deserved compliment into servility, sycophancy, and worship. Where admiration respects and compliment encourages, adulation fawns: it inflates, idealises, and abases the praiser before the praised. In the source sentence "The adulation of the multitude means very little to one," it denotes the noisy, indiscriminate cheering of crowds, implying that mass worship is fickle, uninformed, and morally weightless.

The core semantic components are excess, insincerity or uncriticality, and asymmetry. Excess distinguishes adulation from praise: praise can be measured and true, adulation by definition overshoots. Uncriticality distinguishes it from respect: respect sees faults, adulation blinds itself. Asymmetry distinguishes it from mutual appreciation: the adulator grovels, the adulated is elevated, creating a hierarchy of worshipper and idol. The word is almost always pejorative in modern use; to call something adulation is to condemn both the flatterer and the vanity that accepts it.

Typical contexts are celebrity, politics, and hero-worship: media adulation, public adulation, uncritical adulation of a leader. It collocates with verbs of receiving and pouring — to bask in adulation, to lap up adulation, to shower adulation upon — metaphors of bathing and drinking that betray its intoxicating, sensuous quality. The register is formal and literary; in everyday speech people more often say sucking up, bootlicking, or hero-worship, while adulation belongs to essays, reviews, and historical judgment.

  • Servile flattery sense (core, interpersonal): Fawning praise to gain favour — e.g., "the courtiers' adulation of the king." The bridge from Latin adulari "to fawn (as a dog wags its tail)" preserves the animal image of abasement. This sense stresses insincerity and self-interest.
  • Mass hero-worship sense (collective): Uncritical public idolatry — e.g., "the adulation of the multitude," "pop-star adulation." The bridge is extension from individual sycophant to crowd psychology: crowds, like dogs, wag, cheer, and follow. It stresses irrationality and transience rather than calculation.
  • Intoxicated admiration sense (psychological): Rapturous, almost erotic absorption — e.g., "sheer adulation in his eyes." Here the emphasis is on emotional excess rather than motive. The bridge is metaphorical intoxication: adulation as wine that befuddles judgment.
  • Religious / hyperbolic sense (extended): Worship-like veneration — e.g., "the adulation of saints," "critical adulation." In theology and art criticism it can be less pejorative, meaning intense devotion. The bridge is the old overlap between flattery and worship; Latin adulatio was already used for divine honours paid to emperors.

Understanding adulation requires keeping praise, admiration, and adulation distinct on a scale of measure and motive. Praise says "well done" for an act; admiration says "I respect you" for qualities; adulation says "you can do no wrong" from need, infatuation, or calculation. Its moral charge — contempt for the flatterer, suspicion of the flattered — makes it a key word for political and cultural criticism, warning that what looks like love may be dependence, fear, or show.
