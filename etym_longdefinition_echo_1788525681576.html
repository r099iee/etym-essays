Echo (noun; also used as a capitalized proper noun and as a transitive/intransitive verb) — An acoustic phenomenon whereby a sound wave is reflected off a distant, acoustically reflective surface (such as a cliff face, cave wall, mountain ridge, or building interior) and returns to the listener with sufficient delay and clarity to be perceived as a distinct, repeated auditory event; in classical mythology, the proper name of the Oread (mountain nymph) cursed by Hera to repeat only the last words spoken to her, who subsequently pined away for the love of Narcissus until nothing survived of her except her disembodied voice.

Primary sense — physical acoustics and reflection. In physical science, an echo is an acoustic reflection that arrives at the ear after a time delay greater than approximately 50 to 100 milliseconds following the original sound. If the delay is shorter, the human auditory system integrates the reflected sound with the direct wave, producing reverberation rather than a discrete echo. The phenomenon requires a large, hard boundary whose acoustic impedance differs substantially from that of air, enabling specular reflection of sound pressure waves.

Sense 2 — mythological personification (The context in Wilde). In classical Greco-Roman mythology, as immortalized in Book III of Ovid's Metamorphoses, Echo (Greek Ἠχώ) was a mountain nymph renowned for her captivating conversation. When Hera discovered that Echo had detained her with chatter while Zeus dallied with other nymphs, Hera deprived Echo of the power to initiate speech. When Echo later fell tragically in love with the vain youth Narcissus, she could only repeat his own utterances. Spurned by him, she retreated to solitary grottos and valleys, her flesh wasting away into stone until only her voice persisted. In Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose, Wilde personifies Echo directly as this mythical inhabitant of the hills: "Echo bore it to her purple cavern in the hills, and woke the sleeping shepherds from their dreams."

Sense 3 — figurative resonance, imitation, and political discourse. Figuratively, an echo is any close imitation, residual trace, secondary reflection, or unconscious reproduction of an idea, style, ideology, or previous event. In modern sociopolitical discourse, an echo chamber describes an insular social, intellectual, or media environment where individuals encounter only beliefs and opinions that coincide with their own, reinforcing preexisting biases without exposure to dissenting views.

  • Telecommunications and computing — in computer networking, the ICMP Echo Request (popularly executed via the ping command) transmits packets to a network host to test reachability, awaiting an ICMP Echo Reply. In telephony and audio engineering, acoustic echo cancellation (AEC) removes feedback loops where speaker output re-enters a microphone.
  • Radar, sonar, and ultrasound — in remote sensing and medical diagnostics, an echo refers to the reflected electromagnetic or ultrasonic pulse used to map underwater terrain (sonar), track aircraft (radar), or image fetal and cardiac structures (echocardiogram).
  • Psychiatry and neurology — echolalia is the involuntary, pathological repetition of vocalizations made by another person, while echopraxia is the involuntary imitation of another's movements.
  • Literary and poetic registers — describes intertextual allusions, rhythmic refrains, or thematic resonances where a later writer deliberately or subtly evokes the diction and cadence of a predecessor.

Register and expressive tone. The word possesses a rare dual life: it operates with rigorous quantitative precision in physics and telecommunications, while remaining one of the most hauntingly lyrical, melancholic motifs in world literature and mythology.
