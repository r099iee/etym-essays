- (c. 1300) “Dead body of a beast,” borrowed from Anglo-French carcois - a plain, neutral field-and-slaughter word for a dead animal.
- (14th c.) Extended to the human corpse - now loaded with disgust, decay, and moral weight; the body as rotting matter, the worm's inheritance.
- (15th–17th c.) Reaches the LIVING body, self-deprecating or insulting (“my poor carcase”) - the human reduced to meat while still breathing.
- (16th–17th c.) Structural sense “carcase of a ship/building” - the dried, mechanical meaning of frame, hull, and shell, emptied of flesh entirely.
- (17th c.) Military “carcass,” an incendiary shell - a body that exists only to burn, the word itself weaponised.
- (18th–19th c.) The standard corpse-word of prose: legal, criminal, satirical, and natural-historical writing treat “carcase” with clinical detachment or gallows humour.
- (Modern) Spelling split hardens: “carcass” is the world's default; “carcase” survives in British trade and reference style. In the abattoir it is neutral commodity; on the street it is still insult, self-mockery, or epitaph.
