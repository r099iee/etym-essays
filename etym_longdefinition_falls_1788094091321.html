Falls (plural noun) is the standard English name for a waterfall — a place where a river or stream drops steeply or vertically over rock, so that the water "falls". The word is the plural of "fall" ("a fall of water", attested from the 16th century), and the plural is the default form in geography and common speech: "the falls", "Niagara Falls", "the falls of the river". In the source context — "the noise of the falls could be heard like a distant roar" — the plural names the whole cataract heard from afar as a single sound, exactly its normal narrative use.

Primary senses

  • A waterfall, a cataract: a steep or vertical descent of a river's water over a ledge of rock; the plural "falls" is used whether the descent is one sheet of water or a series of drops — "the falls" as a whole stairway of water.
  • Geographic place-name: "the Falls" and "…Falls" name actual waterfalls, and the towns and regions around them — Great Falls, Niagara Falls, Victoria Falls, Reichenbach Falls, the Falls of the Ohio; in North America the plural travelled west with settlement as a naming convention, and it remains the most common English geographic term for a waterfall.

Secondary senses of the plural "falls"

  • Multiple acts of falling: the ordinary plural of "fall" — several falls, of bodies, objects, prices ("sharp falls on the stock market", a staple of financial journalism), or of snow and rain (rainfalls, snowfalls, falls of rain).
  • Wrestling and sport: "a fall" is a throw or pin that decides a bout; "best of three falls" (or "two out of three falls") is the classic match formula, the plural naming the rounds of a contest.
  • Season: dialectally (chiefly 19th-century American), "the falls" could mean autumn, from "fall of the leaf"; standard American English keeps the singular "fall" for the season.
  • Verb agreement: because the plural is so often a place-name, "the falls is" and "the falls are" both occur; the word hovers between plural noun and singular proper name, like "the Netherlands" and "the Straits".

Kinds of falling water

  • Cataract: a large, powerful waterfall — and, independently, a medical word for blindness of the eye, a striking homonym pair with two separate histories.
  • Cascade: a smaller waterfall, often broken into steps or stages over a series of ledges.
  • Rapids: fast, turbulent water over a steep or rocky bed — not a free drop, but the same family of descending motion.
  • Chute: a steep channel or shoot down which water pours.
  • Plunge / plunge pool: the falling itself, and the basin scoured at the foot of a fall.

The semantic bridges

The word is built on the oldest verb of descent in the language — "to fall" — and the bridge from verb to noun to geography is short and physical. Water is the most famous thing that falls, so "a fall of water" names the phenomenon; a river's descent is rarely a single drop but a sequence of drops, rapids, and ledges, so the plural "the falls" names the whole stairway of water; and because such places were landmarks on trade routes, the plural hardened into proper names. The modern figurative uses (falls in prices, falls from grace) are simply the ancient plural of "fall" doing the work the noun has always done since Old English.

Register and usage

"Falls" is plain, everyday, and universal — the word of travel guides, hydrology reports, and adventure fiction alike; in geographic naming it outnumbers "waterfall" itself. Its grammar is a curiosity: always plural in form, it routinely takes a singular verb when the reference is one place ("the falls is magnificent at flood").
