Linear derivation chain (real attested forms with dates; 7 stages): 1. PIE root *dhe- (also reconstructed *dheh1-) "to put, place, set, make, do" (reconstructed, c. 4000-2500 BCE; Greek tithemi "I set", Sanskrit dadhati "he places"). 2. Latin facere "to make, do, construct" (attested from earliest Latin, c. 7th-3rd c. BCE; from PIE *dhh1- with the well-known correspondence of PIE *dhh1- to Latin f-, cf. Latin feci "I did"). 3. Latin afficere (ad- "to, toward" + facere) "to do something to, act upon, influence, affect" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE). 4. Latin affectare - frequentative of afficere - "to strive after, aim at, pursue eagerly, pretend to" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE-1st c. CE; Cicero, Quintilian). 5. Latin affectatio "a striving after; an assumed or studied manner" (attested Imperial Latin, 1st-2nd c. CE). 6. Old French / Middle French affectation "studied manner, striving after effect" (attested c. 14th c.). 7. Middle English / Modern English affectation "artificial behavior designed to impress" (attested from the 1540s; in Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, 1813, "Affectation of candour") - AFFECTATION. Note: the chain is securely attested. The only reconstructed node is the PIE root *dhe-, whose descent into Latin facere is standard (PIE *dhh1- > Latin f-). The verb affect "to pretend" comes from the same Latin affectare and is distinct from affect "to influence" (from afficere).
