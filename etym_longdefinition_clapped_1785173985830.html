clapped is the simple past (and past participle) of the verb clap: struck, placed, or brought suddenly and forcefully into contact, originally with a sharp sound, as of flat surfaces meeting. The verb is native and imitative — the word itself sounds like the impact it names, and its family (clap, clack, click, clang, clatter) is the sound-symbolic cl- group of Germanic strike-words. In the fixed idiom clapped eyes on, the meaning shifts from sound to sight: the eyes are treated as if slapped onto the object of vision, an abrupt visual seizure. "As soon as I clapped eyes on the little fellow" is recognition at first glance, lively and slightly rough in register — colloquial rather than courtly, the past form reporting the completed moment of seeing. In this use the verb keeps all the suddenness of the original blow while losing the literal sound: nothing need actually make a noise; the eye "slaps" onto the target like a hand onto a table.

The senses of clap/clapped branch widely from that single tactile-auditory core:

  • Strike with sound (core sense): to strike the palms together, to cause flat surfaces to meet sharply — "the thunder clapped overhead," "he clapped his hands," "the door clapped shut."
  • Applause (most common modern sense): to clap the hands in approval — the crowd clapped; the applause sense is a specialization of the striking sense and is now the dominant meaning of the bare verb.
  • Sudden placement (extended sense): to put, place, or fix something quickly and forcefully — "he clapped a hand on my shoulder," "clap a hat on your head," "clapped in irons" (imprisoned), "clapped into prison."
  • Idiom — clap eyes on (the present card's sense): to catch sight of suddenly, to set eyes on for the first time — "as soon as I clapped eyes on him, I knew."
  • Clap of thunder (noun, same stock): a sharp, sudden peal — the noun form of the sound.
  • Clapped out (British/Commonwealth informal, 20th century): worn out, dilapidated, broken down — "a clapped-out car." Semantic bridge: repeated hard striking and banging leaves a thing battered and exhausted.
  • Clap (venereal, historical sense, 16th century onward): gonorrhoea, from Old French clapoir, "a swelling, bubo"; the past participle "clapped" could mean infected with the disease. Now largely archaic.
  • Throb/palpitate (obsolete sense): Old English clappian could mean "to throb" — a heart "claps" — a bodily echo of the same sudden beat.

Morphologically clapped is perfectly regular: the base verb clap adds the weak ending -ed, with the final consonant doubled (clap → clapped) because the vowel is short and stressed — the ordinary English spelling rule seen in stop/stopped, trap/trapped. The register of the idiom is informal and oral; it is at home in narrative prose and direct speech, and the lowercase form on the card should be kept exactly as given. The full meaning in context, then, is: the speaker caught sudden sight of the little fellow — recognition delivered with the same instantaneous, slightly violent immediacy that the verb's root meaning of impact always carries.
