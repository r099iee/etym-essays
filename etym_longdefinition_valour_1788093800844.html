Valour (British spelling; American valor) is great courage in the face of danger, especially in battle: the quality of a person who acts bravely and fearlessly when life or safety is at risk. It is a formal, elevated word — one associates it with soldiers, knights, epic poetry and ceremonial commendation rather than with everyday speech, where courage and bravery are the unmarked terms. Pronounced /ˈvælə/, it is a mass noun: one speaks of “an act of valour”, “deeds of valour”, “valour in the field”.

  • Primary sense — martial courage: valour is courage shown in war or in any grave danger; the word's core is the warrior's willingness to face death. In honours systems and military citations it coexists with gallantry: Canada's Cross of Valour (1972) and the United States' Public Safety Officer Medal of Valor (2001) institutionalise the word itself.
  • Sense 2 — archaic “worth, value”: in Middle English and early modern usage, valour could simply mean “worth, value, merit” in general — the sense it inherited from Old French. This sense died out in the 17th century, displaced by value (a doublet from the same Latin root), leaving valour to specialise in courage.
  • Sense 3 — chivalric and courtly valour: in medieval romance and Renaissance epic, valour is the defining virtue of the knight — courage fused with honour, loyalty and prowess. The word's register is permanently coloured by this chivalric past: “valour” sounds heroic and slightly archaic, the diction of Arthurian prose, epic verse and historical romance from Malory to Walter Scott.
  • Sense 4 — the proverb: Shakespeare's Falstaff (1 Henry IV, 1597) made “the better part of valour is discretion” one of English's best-known proverbs, ironically proposing prudence over recklessness. The proverb still governs the word's moral texture: true valour is courage guided by judgement, not foolhardiness.
  • Sense 5 — metaphorical and moral valour: by extension, valour can be attributed to non-military courage — endurance of suffering, moral steadfastness, intellectual daring (“moral valour”, “the valour of the patient”). These uses are literary and rhetorical rather than colloquial, and they show the word broadening back out from the battlefield to any principled risk.
  • Sense 6 — spelling split: valor (from the Latinising 17th-century spelling) is standard in American English; valour is standard in British and Commonwealth English, alongside honour, labour, colour. The word family — valiant, valiantly, valid, value, evaluate, avail, prevail, equivalent — all descend from the same Latin root valēre “to be strong”.
  • Sense 7 — literary register: from Shakespeare's histories to Milton's epic and Tennyson's Arthurian poems, valour is the word chosen when courage must sound dignified. It appears in eulogy, in regimental mottos and in the citation language of honours — always with the weight of tradition behind it.

Semantic bridge: the entire semantic history is a narrowing. Latin valor meant “worth, value” (abstract worth in general); in the feudal world of Old French, a man's worth (valor) was measured above all by his courage in arms, so the word narrowed from “worth of any kind” to “worth shown in battle”, i.e. bravery. English inherited both senses in the 14th century and then let value take over “worth” while valour kept “courage”. The modern word thus preserves, inside a word for bravery, the fossil of an entire social system in which a warrior's value was his valour.
