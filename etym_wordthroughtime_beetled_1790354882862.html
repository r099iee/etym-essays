- 14th c. [Middle English bitel-brouwed] - Initially emerged in compounds describing individuals with prominent, shaggy, overhanging eyebrows resembling an insect or mallet, carrying rough, sullen, or sinister connotations.
- Late 16th c. [Shakespearean innovation] - Back-formed into a dramatic literary verb meaning to overhang or loom threateningly over a precipice (e.g., Hamlet's cliff that 'beetles o'er his base'), elevating the word to sublime, dizzying heights.
- 18th–19th c. [Industrial & Textile era] - Maintained a rigorous technical definition in Irish linen mills for fabric pounded with heavy wooden beetles to produce a glossy finish.
- Early 20th c. [British Colloquial & RAF Slang] - Shifted decisively into jaunty, humorous spoken English for scurrying away or driving briskly ('beetled off'), popularized by P. G. Wodehouse with affectionate, comic overtones.
