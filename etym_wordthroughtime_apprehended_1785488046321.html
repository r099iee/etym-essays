- [Latin root: to lay hold of (14th c. entry)] - From Latin apprehendere, "to lay hold of, seize" (ad + prehendere, "to grasp"), apprehend entered English meaning literally to take hold of — to seize something physically.
- [Arrest and custody (16th–17th c.)] - The physical sense specialized into the legal one: to apprehend a criminal is to seize and take into custody. This remains the dominant everyday sense of the verb.
- [Mental grasp (17th c. onwards)] - In philosophy and learning, apprehend came to mean to grasp with the mind — to perceive and take hold of a pattern or idea in the very act of perception, before full systematic understanding; the mind "seizes" its object as the hand seizes a thing.
- [Offshoot: fearful anticipation (18th c. onwards)] - Because to "catch hold of" a future event in the mind was felt as dread, apprehension spun off the specialized sense of fearful anticipation — an anxiety-laden offshoot that colours the whole word family to this day.
