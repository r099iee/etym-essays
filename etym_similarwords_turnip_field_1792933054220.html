• Cornfield: Similar because both are sown acreages of a single staple crop; differs because cornfield waves tall golden grain in summer, while a turnip-field lies low and green-purple with roots in autumn mud.
  • Potato-field: Similar because both grow starchy underground staples lifted in autumn; differs because potatoes hide wholly blind in ridges and need clamps, while turnip shoulders show above ground and sheep graze them standing.
  • Beet-field: Similar because both are drilled root breaks of the rotation, mangolds and sugar beet included; differs because beet runs sweeter and redder toward sugar factories, while turnip runs to table and sheep-fold.
  • Pasture: Similar because both feed livestock within hedges or fences; differs because pasture is permanent grass grazed year-round, while a turnip-field is arable land under a sown winter root break.
  • Paddock: Similar because both are enclosed agricultural parcels; differs because a paddock is a small grass enclosure for horses, while a turnip-field is a large tillage acreage under crop.
  • Allotment: Similar because both grow vegetables including turnips in rows; differs because an allotment is a small rented town garden worked by hand, while a turnip-field is broad commercial or farm acreage.
  • Market garden: Similar because both raise table vegetables intensively; differs because a market garden mixes many high-value crops near towns, while a turnip-field is one bulk root crop in open country.
  • Fallow: Similar because both belong to rotation vocabulary around the root break; differs because fallow is land rested bare to recover, the very waste the turnip-field was invented to abolish.
  • Stubble-field: Similar because both mark autumn farmland after or amid harvest; differs because stubble is the shaven aftermath of corn already cut, while a turnip-field is a living standing crop still to lift.
