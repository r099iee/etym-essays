Chain for the first element “down” (adverb/prepositional element of the compound): - PIE *dhu-n- / *dūno- (reconstruction debated) — the Proto-Celtic word for a fortified hill; the ultimate Indo-European ancestry is uncertain and disputed, so the chain is commonly treated as beginning at the Celtic level rather than at a secure PIE root. - Proto-Celtic *dūno- “fort, fortified hill, stronghold” (c. 1st millennium BCE). - Gaulish *dūnon / Old Irish dún “fortress, fortified height” (attested in place-names from c. 1st century BCE–CE, e.g. Lugdūnum, Camulodūnum). - Old English dūn “hill, mountain, elevated open ground” (c. 8th–10th century; almost universally regarded as a loanword from Celtic/Brittonic *dūn, cf. Welsh din; the “hill” sense entered English as the fortified-height word was transferred to high ground in general). - Old English phrase of dūne / adūne “off the hill, downward” (c. 10th century) — the direct source of the modern adverb. - Middle English doun / down “from higher to lower; toward the ground” (12th–15th century). - Modern English down (adverb/preposition/adjective; 16th century onward). - In the compound downcast, “down” is the first element (14th century onward). Note on the debate: some scholars instead derive English dūn, Dutch duin, and German Düne “dune” from a shared north-west European substrate or a native Germanic formation, and the Germanic dune-words are themselves often explained as borrowed from or blended with Celtic. The English semantic path (hill → from the hill → downward) is secure whatever the ultimate source is judged to be. Chain for the second element “cast” (verbal root of the compound): - Old Norse kasta “to throw, fling, hurl” (attested c. 9th–11th century; ultimate origin outside Germanic is unknown—no secure PIE etymon is reconstructed, and the verb may be an expressive or substrate formation). - Middle English casten “to throw, fling; to direct (the eyes); to mould; to calculate” (attested c. 1200 onward; borrowed from Old Norse via the Danelaw). - English cast (verb and noun; 14th century onward) — “to throw,” and by extension “to direct (a look),” “to mould (metal),” “to compute (accounts),” “to assign a part (in a play).” - English phrasal verb cast down “to throw down, overthrow; to lower (the eyes); to dispirit” (14th century onward). - English participial adjective downcast “thrown down; overthrown” (14th century), then “dejected; with lowered eyes” (16th–17th century) — the compound down + cast. Sisters: Icelandic kasta, Swedish kasta, Danish kaste, Norwegian kaste “to throw.” The Old Norse origin of English cast is undisputed, but the deeper pre-Germanic ancestry of kasta is unknown; do not invent a PIE root for it. Composite gloss: down “from high to low” + cast “thrown” → downcast “thrown down; lowered; dejected.”
