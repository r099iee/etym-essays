Origins in falconry. The story of ROUSE begins not with sleep but with feathers. In Middle English the verb rousen (recorded from around the fifteenth century, though likely older in spoken use) meant, in the technical vocabulary of hawking and falconry, to shake the feathers or to cause a hawk to ruffle and rustle its plumage. A falconer watching his bird 'rouse' saw the bird's disturbance and preparation — the physical stirring that precedes flight. The word entered English from Old French or Middle French reuser / reüsser, meaning to drive back, to spring up again, or to set in motion, itself most plausibly traceable to the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *kreus-, 'to begin to move, push, shake'. This root also underlies Germanic forms related to 'rush' and 'rowdy'; the exact linking chain beyond Old French is a matter of scholarly debate and must be treated as a probable rather than certain reconstruction.

From feathers to bodies. The decisive semantic shift occurred as the hawking term crossed into general use. Because a roused hawk suddenly becomes alert, agitated, and ready, the verb came to describe the act of bringing any sleeping or passive creature into wakefulness and alertness. By the sixteenth century ROUSE meant to wake someone from sleep and, by extension, to stir soldiers, crowds, or nations into vigorous action. Shakespeare's works are rich in this usage — characters rouse one another from grief, coma, and slumber — and the verb carried the same sudden, energetic force that it had in the falconry yard.

Broadening to the emotions. From waking bodies, the word reached inward to waking feelings. From about the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, ROUSE could mean to provoke or kindle an emotion: anger, fear, courage, hope, or suspicion. The semantic bridge is transparent: to rouse an emotion is to 'lift it from dormancy' and set it in motion, exactly as a hawk or a sleeper is roused. This emotional sense shaded gradually into the modern literary use in which a speech, a sight, or a piece of news 'rouses' a response in a person or a public.

The drinking sideline. Alongside these, an older, now archaic noun sense developed: a 'rouse' as a copious draught or a carousal — a boisterous drinking bout. This sense arose from the verb 'to carouse', a word of German and ultimately Italian ancestry (carouso, 'right out'), and from the shared association of raucous, rowdy energy. The two words blurred in popular use, so that for a time 'rouse' also meant a spree or a heavy drink. This usage is now rare or obsolete and survives chiefly in historical and dialectal writing.

Relationship with 'arouse'. A crucial development was the rise of the prefixed form 'arouse' (a- + rouse). Over time 'arouse' became the preferred verb for awakening emotions and internal states — 'to arouse suspicion', 'to arouse desire' — while 'rouse' retained a somewhat more physical, energetic, and person-directed identity. The two coexist, with 'arouse' often carrying the stronger implication of a deliberate stimulus to a feeling.

Modern register. In contemporary English ROUSE remains an active, literary-flavored verb. It is less neutral than 'wake' and more energetic, and it survives strongly in the adjective 'rousing' ('a rousing ovation', 'a rousing chorus'), meaning exhilaratingly stirring. Its history is a textbook example of metaphorical extension — from a hawk ruffling its feathers, to waking a sleeper, to galvanizing a crowd and kindling an emotion — illustrating how a narrowly physical, technical action became one of the most vivid verbs in English for the transition from passivity to vigorous life.
