Balmy is an adjective meaning, chiefly, pleasantly warm — so mild, soft, and fragrant that the air itself feels soothing — and, in older and extended use, having the qualities of balm: soothing, healing, fragrant. It is the adjective derived from balm, and its warm-weather sense is a metaphor: air that is like balm, that soothes and heals as an ointment does. In the card's context sentence — "The balmy winds are effused during the spring, bringing a sense of renewal to the valley" — the word means the warm, gentle, fragrant spring winds whose softness and scent refresh and restore the landscape; the winds are literally balm-like, bringing renewal.

Sense 1 — Pleasantly warm and mild (weather, the dominant modern sense). A "balmy breeze", "balmy summer evening", "balmy climate" means warm in a gentle, agreeable way — not hot, not humid to excess, but soft and caressing. This is the sense in the card's context: balmy winds are mild, spring-warm winds that soothe. The word never means merely "hot"; its core is the pleasantness and softness of the warmth.

Sense 2 — Fragrant, sweet, like balm. Because balm is a fragrant resinous ointment, "balmy" also carries the sense of sweet-smelling, fragrant, or perfumed — a "balmy" air may literally be filled with the scent of spring flowers. The card's context hints at this with "effused ... bringing a sense of renewal", the fragrance of spring winds.

Sense 3 — Soothing, healing, restorative (the root figurative sense). In older and literary use, balmy means having the healing, soothing qualities of balm: "balmy sleep", "balmy influence", "balmy air". This is the bridge between the ointment and the weather: the same word that names a healing salve names air that heals and comforts. This sense is historically primary and still available in elevated prose.

Sense 4 — Archaic: bearing or yielding balm. The earliest sense (16th century) was literal — a "balmy" plant or tree is one that produces balm. This sense survives in herbal and historical texts.

Sense 5 — The card's contextual sense. In "The balmy winds are effused during the spring", balmy combines senses 1 and 3: the winds are mild and warm, and they soothe and renew the valley — the spring breeze as a healing presence, the restorative gentleness of the season.

Polysemy, jargon, and metaphoric shifts.

  • Weather sense: pleasantly warm, mild, soft — "a balmy evening", "balmy breezes"; the dominant modern meaning.
  • Fragrance sense: sweet-scented, perfumed, balm-scented air or vegetation.
  • Soothing/healing sense: restorative like balm — "balmy sleep", "balmy influence", "balmy air"; the figurative bridge from the ointment.
  • Archaic literal sense: bearing or producing balm, of plants and trees (16th–17th century).
  • Slang/pun sense (British) — "barmy": "Balmy" is a frequent homophone-misspelling of barmy ("frothy, hence foolish, eccentric"), so in British slang "a bit balmy" can punningly mean slightly mad; the two words are distinct in origin (barmy from barm, the froth of fermenting ale) but often confused in spelling and pronunciation.
  • Register: literary, romantic, and pastoral — a word of gentle, fragrant warmth, now chiefly of weather in moderately elevated prose.

Semantic boundaries. Against mild, balmy adds fragrance and soothingness; against warm, balmy adds softness and pleasantness; against clement, balmy is sensory and poetic rather than formal and meteorological. The word never describes harsh or extreme warmth, and it carries an undertone of comfort and healing from its balm ancestry.
