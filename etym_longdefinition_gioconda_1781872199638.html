In Walter Pater's aesthetic criticism, La Gioconda is not merely a famous painting title but a concentrated emblem of specialized aesthetic virtue. The Italian name designates Leonardo da Vinci's portrait of Lisa Gherardini, wife of the Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo—so the painting is "the Giocondo woman," La Gioconda, the feminine of the family name. Pater places it beside landscape, personality, Carrara's hills, and Pico of Mirandola as objects valuable for their "virtues": each yields a unique, irreplaceable impression of pleasure to the sensitive observer. The tone is rapt, almost liturgical; the name itself carries the Latin-Italian sense of jocund merriment, yet the smile Pater immortalized is enigmatic rather than simply cheerful—a paradoxical fusion of sweetness and strangeness that Victorian prose made the archetype of the modern artistic soul. The painting and its lady. The portrait was executed by Leonardo around 1503–1506, probably in Florence, and is now in the Louvre, Paris. The sitter, Lisa Gherardini, married the merchant Francesco del Giocondo in 1495, and the title La Gioconda registers precisely that marital identity. Vasari's Lives identifies the sitter and popularized the work; the famous "enigmatic smile" became the subject of endless commentary. In Italian the painting is called La Gioconda; in French La Joconde; in English the pet-name form Mona Lisa (from Italian monna Lisa, "Madam Lisa") long dominated, though art history and aesthetic criticism freely use La Gioconda when they want the Italian resonance and the cheerful etymological halo that the surname Giocondo carries. Title semantics and near-forms. Compared with Mona Lisa, La Gioconda stresses marital identity and the surname's semantic charge of cheerfulness rather than the given name alone. Portrait is a generic genre label that erases the specific cultural-Italian resonance; masterpiece names rank of achievement without the person-linked, smile-centered myth; icon or masterwork abstracts the object into cultural status. Only La Gioconda fuses proper name, Italian register, and the jocund root, which contrasts so fruitfully with the portrait's unreadable expression—the happiest name, the most enigmatic face. Polysemy and modern usage:
  • Painting title: Leonardo's portrait of Lisa Gherardini del Giocondo, in Italian La Gioconda, known in English primarily as the Mona Lisa.
  • Surname and person: Gioconda, feminine of the Italian surname Giocondo, from the adjective giocondo "cheerful, merry, jovial"; hence "the Giocondo woman."
  • Art-historical and literary name: La Gioconda as the emblem of the enigma of beauty and of the modern artistic soul, fixed by Pater's celebrated 1869 passage (published in The Renaissance, 1873).
  • Opera and culture: the name recurs in the arts—Amilcare Ponchielli's opera La Gioconda (1876) and its aria, as well as countless allusions—as a floating sign of Italian beauty and mystery.
  • Metonymic use: "a Gioconda" for any mysterious or inscrutable smile, or for a subject with an enigmatic expression.
Collocations and register. Typical collocations include La Gioconda's smile, the enigma of La Gioconda, Pater on La Gioconda, Leonardo's La Gioconda, and museum or catalogue phrases such as the Gioconda Room. Register is literary-critical and art-historical rather than casual: the Italian form signals cultivated discourse (essays, catalogues, comparative aesthetics), whereas everyday English prefers Mona Lisa. In Pater's list the name operates as the supreme artistic instance of the principle that a picture, like a landscape or a personality, affects the observer with a unique, irreplaceable impression of pleasure.
