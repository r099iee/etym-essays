• Merchant: Similar because both engage directly in the buying, selling, and commercial distribution of goods and commodities for profit; differs because merchant is a secular occupational term found across all human cultures, whereas vaiśya is a hereditary, ritual social class (varṇa) in Hindu society endowed with specific religious duties, Vedic initiation (dvija status), and pastoral/agrarian obligations.
  • Trader: Similar because both are fundamentally involved in trade, commerce, and marketplace transactions; differs because a trader is an individual functional role regardless of religious, ritual, or familial heritage, whereas vaiśya encompasses an entire social order encompassing cultivators, cattle-breeders, and financiers alongside traders.
  • Tradesman: Similar because both denote individuals engaged in commercial or productive trade; differs because a tradesman in English often connotes a skilled manual craftsman or small shopkeeper, whereas a vaiśya can range from an agricultural landholder to a colossal merchant-banker (śreṣṭhin) financing international maritime ventures.
  • Brāhmaṇa: Similar because both belong to the three "twice-born" (dvija) classes entitled to Vedic study and sacred thread investiture; differs because the brāhmaṇa is the priestly, spiritual, and intellectual authority prohibited from commercial usury and trade, whereas the vaiśya is the economic producer responsible for material wealth and commerce.
  • Kṣatriya: Similar because both share upper-varṇa dvija status and participate in state council assemblies; differs because the kṣatriya wields physical force, political sovereignty, military command, and administrative governance, whereas the vaiśya focuses on trade, wealth generation, and cattle protection.
  • Śūdra: Similar because both represent the productive working populace sustaining the civil community; differs because the śūdra was classified as once-born (advija), traditionally excluded from Vedic initiation and sacred rituals, and assigned to service roles, whereas the vaiśya is twice-born and holds independent property rights.
  • Bourgeoisie: Similar because both describe the urban merchant, commercial, and property-owning middle-and-upper economic class that rose to prominence through enterprise and capital; differs because the bourgeoisie is a modern Marxist and European socio-economic class born of capitalist industrialization, whereas vaiśya is an ancient sacred scriptural status combining religious duties with hereditary economic roles.
  • Śreṣṭhin (Setthi): Similar because both identify the commercial and financial leadership of ancient Indian society; differs because śreṣṭhin is an elite title or office for the guild-master and banker-magnate within urban centers, whereas vaiśya is the overarching social varṇa embracing all agrarian, pastoral, and mercantile members of the community.
  • Guildsman: Similar because classical vaiśyas organized their economic enterprises into powerful hereditary mercantile and craft guilds (śreṇis); differs because a guildsman is a member of a trade association in medieval Europe, whereas a vaiśya is a member of a sacred cosmic varṇa.
  • Bania: Similar because Banias represent the prominent contemporary regional Indian trading communities identifying within the classical vaiśya varṇa; differs because Bania is a specific colloquial and sociological caste cluster (jāti), whereas vaiśya is the overarching classical Sanskrit scriptural category.
