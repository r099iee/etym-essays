• hailstones: Similar because both refer to the actual frozen ice precipitation; differs because hailstones refers specifically to individual discrete balls or pellets of ice, whereas hail refers to the weather phenomenon as a collective mass or process.
  • sleet: Similar because both are forms of frozen or semi-frozen winter precipitation; differs because sleet consists of small, clear ice pellets formed when falling raindrops freeze while passing through a shallow cold layer near the ground, lacking the violent convective updraft formation and layered size of hail.
  • graupel: Similar because both are forms of solid precipitation generated in clouds; differs because graupel (soft hail / snow pellets) consists of fragile, crushable, opaque rime-coated snowflakes, whereas hail consists of hard, dense, layered ice.
  • barrage: Similar because both can describe a dense, overwhelming shower of incoming projectiles; differs because barrage is primarily a military/tactical term referring to continuous deliberate artillery or gunfire, whereas hail is a natural meteorological metaphor.
  • volley: Similar because both denote a simultaneous or rapid succession of missiles; differs because a volley is a coordinated, simultaneous discharge of weapons at a single moment, whereas hail implies an uncoordinated, chaotic downpour.
  • shower: Similar because both describe precipitation or figurative falling items; differs because a shower is typically gentler, liquid, and of brief duration, lacking the violent kinetic impact and hardness of hail.
  • bombardment: Similar because both evoke physical destruction and pounding blows; differs because bombardment emphasizes explosive attack or sustained hostile assault, whereas hail emphasizes rapid physical pelting.
  • torrent: Similar because both denote a heavy, forceful downpour; differs because a torrent refers specifically to rushing liquid water or rapid streaming flow rather than frozen ice pellets.
