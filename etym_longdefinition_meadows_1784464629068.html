Meadows is the regular plural of meadow: areas of low-lying grassland, especially those kept for hay or pasture rather than for intensive tillage — open, green, often moist ground by streams or below arable slopes. In the description of Bag End in The Hobbit, the best rooms "were the only ones to have windows, deep-set round windows looking over his garden, and meadows beyond, sloping down to the river." The plural draws the middle distance of the Shire view: not wild forest, not ploughed strips alone, but the soft pastoral belt between garden and water. The tone is gentle, settled, English-rural; the word does quiet world-building for hobbit comfort and the fertility of the Water's valley. The meadows "slope down to the river," so they are landscape in motion toward drainage and green — the classic English water-meadow idea carried without technical jargon. Preserve the plural: a stretch of such grounds in the view from the Hill, not a single named enclosure. The word's core is very stable. Etymologically the meadow is "the mown place," land whose defining feature is that it is cut for hay: Old English maed descends from a root meaning "to mow," and the word has meant grass-land managed by the scythe for as long as it has been recorded in English. That sets it apart from the field, which may be any enclosed agricultural space including crops; from the pasture, which stresses grazing use; from the lawn, which is ornamental and mown short; from the moor, which is high, open, often heather and peat; from the prairie, which is a different ecological and American register; and from the steppe or the savanna, which belong to other climates entirely. The meadow is low, lush, watered, and harvested — the green middle band of a farmed landscape between tillage and waste.
  • Primary sense: an area of low-lying grassland, especially one kept for hay or pasture, often beside a stream or below arable slopes.
  • Agricultural sense: a mowing-field, the parcel of grass cut for winter fodder; the hay-meadow is a fixed feature of traditional mixed farming.
  • Ecological sense (modern): species-rich unploughed grassland, often managed by a single late cut, prized for wildflowers and invertebrates; "meadow" now carries conservation value as well as farming meaning.
  • Landscape-architecture sense: a deliberately planted or maintained grass area in a garden or park, sometimes a wildflower meadow replacing the formal lawn.
  • Compound and derivative uses: water-meadow (seasonally flooded pasture by a river), flood-meadow, meadowlark, meadowsweet, meadow-grass, the poetic short form mead ("the mead's green").
  • Geographical/place-name sense: Meadow, Mead, and Mede recur as English place-name elements, preserving the word's centuries of countryside use.
Polysemy has grown mostly along one axis: from the agricultural hay-field to the valued semi-natural habitat to the designed garden feature. The historical bridge is continuous. The old mowing-ground was simply a working field; when traditional hay-cutting declined in the twentieth century, the same grassland became rare, and the word carried that rarity into a new ecological and aesthetic meaning. The water-meadow specialization is old and purely agricultural: low river meadows that were deliberately flooded in winter to improve the grass, a practice documented in England since medieval times and still visible in surviving systems. The poetic sense is equally ancient and continuous — Chaucer, Spenser, Milton, and the Romantics all use the meadow as the emblem of green peace — and it is this sense that Tolkien inherits when he places meadows "beyond" the garden of Bag End, sloping down to the river. Register: meadow is unmarked, pastoral, and warmly positive; it carries no negative associations at all. In scientific English the word became a precise habitat category in the twentieth century; in gardening English it names a fashionable alternative to the lawn; in ordinary speech it remains the gentle word for soft grass. The plural meadows simply multiplies the green belt into a landscape, exactly as the text requires: the view from the round windows runs garden, meadows, river — the three-stage prospect of hobbit comfort, from the worked ground of the kitchen garden to the harvested grass of the valley to the water that makes it all fertile.
