Introduction. Shoulder is one of the oldest words of the English body — present in the earliest Old English poetry, continuous in the language for over thirteen centuries, and today both a plain anatomical noun and a prolific source of metaphor, idiom and technical jargon. Its history divides into two stories: the sound history of the Germanic family (which is well attested), and the semantic career of the word in English (which is remarkable chiefly for its restless productivity — a body part that became a mountain, a road, a cut of meat, a verb of pushing, a verb of responsibility, and a whole shelf of idioms).

Phase 1 — the Germanic inheritance (before c. 700 CE).

  • The word is Common Germanic: Old English sculdor matches Old Frisian skoldere, Middle Dutch scouder, Dutch schouder, Old High German scultra/scultarra, and German Schulter — all pointing back to a reconstructed Proto-Germanic *skuldraz "shoulder".
  • Outside Germanic no certain cognates exist. The OED states plainly that the word has no established Indo-European etymology; proposals have been made (a connection with PIE *skel- "to bend, curve", making the shoulder "the bending joint"; a link to the Germanic shield-word, Old High German scilt) but none commands general assent, and responsible dictionaries record the origin as uncertain.
  • The Germanic form is morphologically interesting: the -dr-/-tr- cluster (sculdor, scultra) suggests an old r-stem or agent-noun formation, and the base may have meant something like "the (load-)bearing part" — but this is speculation.

Phase 2 — Old English (c. 700–1100).

  • Old English sculdor (also sculdra) is attested in the earliest poetry: in Beowulf the hero's shoulder is the site of blows and of wounds, and the word is used of both men and beasts. It was a completely ordinary word of the body, with no recorded figurative uses.
  • Phonology: the sc- spelling was pronounced /ʃ/ (the Old English palatalisation of sk before front vowels — the same change that gives ship, shall, sheep and shirt from their Germanic kin); the d in sculdor was a voiced dental stop.
  • Old English also formed the derivatives one expects of a body word — e.g. the adjective sculdorhēah "high-shouldered" — showing the word was fully woven into the vocabulary.

Phase 3 — Middle English (1100–1500).

  • Middle English schulder (with many spellings: shulder, schuldre, shuldir, sholder) continued the word unchanged in sense; the sc- > sh- spelling simply reflects the earlier /ʃ/ pronunciation.
  • The first figurative extension is recorded in this period: by the 15th century "shoulder" is used of the projecting part of a hill or mountain — the landscape begins to wear the human body's shape, exactly the use in the Tolkien sentence that frames this card.
  • The butchery sense (shoulder of a beast as meat) is also medieval, reflecting the word's everyday place in kitchen and market.

Phase 4 — early modern English (1500–1700).

  • In the 16th century the modern spelling shoulder appears, with -ou- inserted on the analogy of could, would, should (which acquired their own -ou- spellings in the same period); Shakespeare and the King James Bible use both the noun and the new verb freely.
  • The verb "to shoulder" is recorded from the 16th century: first "to push with the shoulder" ("shouldering through a crowd"), then the military "shoulder arms" (placing a musket on the shoulder). The verb "to shoulder" a burden — taking weight onto the shoulders — is the direct ancestor of the modern moral sense.
  • By 1600 the word had already generated compounds such as "shoulder-blade" and "shoulder-knot"; the idiomatic machine was running.

Phase 5 — the great idiom century (1700–1900).

  • The 18th and 19th centuries built the modern idiom-shelf. "Shoulder to shoulder" (troops in close order, then solidarity) is 16th-century military in origin but became the common figure of united effort in the 19th century.
  • "To give (someone) the cold shoulder" is first recorded in Walter Scott's The Antiquary (1816): a snub — from the old custom of serving an unwelcome guest a cold shoulder of mutton, or, more plainly, of turning a cold back on a visitor.
  • "A chip on one's shoulder" (a grievance, a belligerent attitude) is American and 19th-century, from the frontier custom in which a boy daring a fight would place a wood chip on his own shoulder and dare another to knock it off.
  • Road engineering adopted "shoulder of the road" for the verge beside the carriageway; "hard shoulder" followed in the 20th century with the motorways.

Phase 6 — the twentieth century to today.

  • Modern extensions show the word still generating new senses: "shoulder season" in tourism (the sloping flank of the demand curve), "shoulder surfing" in computer security (peeking over a shoulder at a screen), "shoulder check" in hockey and driving, "shoulder-fired" in military technology, "frozen shoulder" in clinical medicine.
  • In literature the topographic sense remains a living poetic transfer — Tolkien's "great shoulder of the south-pointing mountain-spur" is the direct descendant of the 15th-century hill-shoulder metaphor.

Summary. A Common Germanic body-word of uncertain deeper ancestry, shoulder has spent thirteen centuries doing one thing brilliantly: radiating outward from the human body. From the Old English battlefield to the modern motorway verge, every sense — mountain flank, road edge, cut of meat, act of pushing, weight of responsibility — is the same metaphor: the rounded, load-bearing projection of the body, projected onto the world.
