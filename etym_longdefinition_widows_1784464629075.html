Widows (plural of widow) are women whose husbands have died and who have not remarried. The word marks a legal and social status of loss — not merely "unmarried woman" or "older woman". In Bilbo's cascade of Gandalf-memories — "the unexpected luck of widows' sons" — the genitive plural is formulaic fairy-tale diction: the widow's son is the classic underdog hero of northern and continental märchen, the boy without a father's protection who wins fortune by luck, courage, or hidden birth. The register is storybook, slightly archaic, affectionate; Bilbo is listing the wizard's repertoire of wonders, and "widows' sons" stands for a whole plot-type. The card form is the plural noun as it appears in that phrase.

Core senses:

  • Bereaved spouse (female) — a woman whose husband has died and who has not remarried (the sense in this card). Male counterpart: widower, a later, analogical formation.
  • Typographical widow — a single short line (the last line of a paragraph) stranded alone at the top of a page or column, set apart from its paragraph; the paired opposite is the orphan (a first line stranded at the bottom).
  • Computing "widow and orphan" control — typesetting rules that prevent such stranded lines.
  • Grass widow / grass-widow — historically a woman whose husband is temporarily away; later, a married woman separated from her husband, or a divorced woman.
  • Idioms — widow's cruse (a jar of oil that never runs dry, from the Hebrew Bible); widow's mite (the poor woman's two small coins, Mark 12); widow's peak (a V-shaped hairline, so called from peaked mourning-caps).
  • Abstract nouns — widowhood (the state of being a widow); widowed (adjective: rendered a widow or widower).

Historical bridge & semantic logic: the word descends directly from an ancient Indo-European term for a bereaved wife (root *h₁widʰ- "be empty, separate"), attested across the whole family — Sanskrit vidhávā, Latin vidua, Gothic widuwō. The typographical "widow" is a metaphor of abandonment: a line separated and left alone at the top of a page, like a woman left solitary by death. The status itself — the protected, mourning, remarriable widow — kept the word stable in meaning for over three thousand years.

Usage notes & idioms: the plural widows appears chiefly in institutional and narrative contexts (widows and orphans funds, the widows of war, "widows' sons" in tales), while the genitive constructions widow's and widows' are pronounced identically and distinguished only in writing. The word also yields the derived adjective widowed and the noun widowhood, and in typography a "widow" is a stranded line. Because the status is legally and socially consequential — inheritance, mourning customs, remarriage rights — the word has always carried a strong social register, from Old English law-codes through medieval guild charities to modern bereavement support.

Differentiate: an orphan has lost parents (either sex, usually young); a spinster has never married (or left marriage without the husband's death); bereaved is the general word for anyone who has lost a loved one; a divorcée left marriage by legal decree, not by death. Context is the stock phrase of wonder-tales, not a census category. Preserve WIDOWS as the plural base of widows' sons.
