- c. 1300 — behind and backward: Middle English takes Old French arere for lateness in place and time, and applies it to money not paid when due.
- 14th–15th c. — the accountant’s word: in arrear becomes the standard formula of manor rolls and Exchequer tallies for rents, fines, and taxes outstanding.
- 16th–17th c. — law fixes it: statutes and leases write arrears of rent into the legal vocabulary, and Shakespeare’s England knows the debtor in arrear as a stock figure.
- 18th c. — the plural takes over: arrears crowds out the singular in daily business, while in arrear survives in formal and Scottish official prose.
- Cobbett’s 1820s — the antique singular as weapon: a large arrear to the public turns an old-fashioned noun into a charge of corruption against a tax collector.
- Today — the plural owns the word: in arrears belongs to banks, landlords, and courts, and the lone singular survives in statutes, ledgers, and history books.
