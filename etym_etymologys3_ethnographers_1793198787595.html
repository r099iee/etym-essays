The word ethnographers is young — barely two centuries old — yet it carries three thousand years of Greek inside it. Its first half, ethno-, comes from ethnos, which in Homer means simply a band or company: a band of warriors, a swarm of bees, a throng of the dead. Only gradually did it settle into nation, people, tribe — the human group bound by blood, speech, and gods. Its second half, -grapher, comes from graphein, to scratch, then to draw, then to write: the hand that cuts marks in wax or stone becomes the hand that records the world. Put them together and the ethnographer is, with disarming literalness, one who writes peoples down.

The compound waited for the nineteenth century to be born. Geography had long described lands and biography had long described lives; as empires delivered whole new peoples to European curiosity, learned French coined ethnographie in the 1830s for the missing discipline, English imported ethnography by 1834, and the practitioner — the ethnographer — followed in the 1840s. The timing matters: this is the age of railways, museums, and colonial offices, when tales from the steppe or the South Seas could fill a London lecture hall and when young nations, Russia among them, were hunting their own origins in peasant song.

How the craft matured:

  • Armchair and questionnaire (mid-19th c.): the first ethnographers seldom travelled; missionaries and officers sent home vocabularies and marvels, and theorists in Paris or Berlin arranged them into ladders from savagery to civilisation.
  • Russian mythological school (1860s–1870s): scholars such as Afanasyev adapt folk material into vast mythological systems, reading the fairy tale as weather-myth and the house-spirit as a fallen god — the very adaptation named in the source sentence, half collection and half creation.
  • Boasian revolution (1890s–1910s): Franz Boas insists on texts in the native language, history over hierarchy, and years of fieldwork, demolishing the ladder and enthroning the monograph.
  • Malinowski's tent (1915–1922): stranded in the Trobriands by war, Malinowski pitches his tent among the villagers, learns Kiriwinan, and writes Argonauts of the Western Pacific — participant observation becomes the sacrament of the craft.
  • Professional century (1930s–1960s): doctoral fieldwork, kinship diagrams, and village restudies standardise the ethnographer as a university scientist, complete with grants, ethics, and return visits.
  • Writing culture (1970s–1990s): critics expose the colonial frame and the absent author; native ethnographers answer back; the reflexive preface, the dialogue, and the co-authored text remake the monograph from within.
  • Everywhere fieldwork (2000s–today): the method outgrows its village: intensive-care units, trading floors, fan forums, and refugee camps all receive residents with notebooks, and the ethnographer is anyone who learns a world by dwelling in it.

Phonetically the word is pure learned English: Greek elements filtered through French spelling and English stress, the th kept voiceless as in ethnic, the plural adding a plain -s. Semantically it has travelled from imperial curiosity to humble witness — from the scholar who classified the peasant to the scholar who asks the peasant's leave to quote. The Russian ethnographers of the source sentence stand at the hinge of that journey: they preserved what the Church-dominated press had ignored, the fairy tale and the rite, yet they dressed it in systems of their own devising. Every ethnographer since inherits both their debt and their warning.
