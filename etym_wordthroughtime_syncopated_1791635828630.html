- Late 16th c.: Grammatical and Rhetorical Abbreviation - Entered Early Modern English strictly as an esoteric philological term describing the syncope or internal contraction of letters and syllables in Latin and Greek verse.
- Early 17th c.: Pathological and Medical Symptom - Applied in medical treatises to describe pulse irregularities, palpitations, and swooning fits associated with cardiac syncope and sudden loss of consciousness.
- 17th–18th c.: Formal Baroque and Classical Counterpoint - Adopted into European musical theory as an Italianate harmonic device (*sincope*) denoting notes tied across barlines or suspensions resolving on weak beats.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c.: Ragtime, Jazz, and Cultural Controversy - Exploded into popular culture to describe African American ragtime rhythms, initially greeted with conservative moral panic and elitist disdain before becoming the celebrated foundation of modern popular music.
- Mid-20th c. to Present: Pervasive Rhythmic and Metaphorical Standard - Universalized across global genres (funk, hip-hop, salsa, rock) and extended metaphorically to describe fractured, fast-paced, or discontinuous rhythms in literature, urban architecture, and modern cinematic editing.
