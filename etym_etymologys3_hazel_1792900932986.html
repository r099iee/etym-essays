Of all English tree-names, hazel is among the oldest and the most widely travelled. Its reconstructed ancestor, PIE *koselo-, is that rare thing: a botanical word shared across the whole Indo-European family. Latin has corylus, Old Irish coll, Welsh collen, and the Baltic and Slavic languages keep their own cognate hazel-words — proof that the tree mattered to the steppe pastoralists before they ever became Romans, Celts, or Germans. When a word for one species survives five thousand years of migration, war, and language shift, it tells us the thing itself was indispensable: food, timber, and boundary-marker in a single shrub.

The Germanic chapter follows the great consonant shift like clockwork. PIE initial k became Proto-Germanic h under Grimm’s Law, turning *koselo- into *haslaz — compare Old High German hasal, Old Norse hasl, and Old English hæsel. The Anglo-Saxons coppiced it, wove it, ate its nuts, and marked holy boundaries with its rods; hazel already meant livelihood. Middle English smoothed the spelling to hasel and the Great Vowel Shift lifted the vowel to modern hazel, pronounced with the voiced z that still surprises spellers.

Stages of the grove:

  • PIE steppe (c. 4000 BCE): *koselo- names the hazel wherever Indo-European speakers gather its nuts; daughter languages will carry the word from Ireland to India’s frontiers.
  • Germanic shift (c. 500 BCE): Grimm’s Law remakes the initial as h-, giving *haslaz; the form is stable across Old High German, Old Norse, and Old English, a pan-Germanic agreement rare for plant-words.
  • Old English (9th–11th c.): hæsel is hedge, food-store, and building material; boundary charters name hazel-marks, and place-names like Hazelwood and Haslemere root the tree in the map.
  • Medieval (12th–15th c.): coppice rotations under forest law make hazel a cash crop of poles and spars; Irish manuscripts preserve the rival magic tradition of the nine hazels of wisdom over Connla’s well.
  • Early modern (16th–17th c.): the colour sense appears — hazel eyes in lyric and portraiture — while herbalists praise hazel rods for divining water and physicians prescribe the nuts against coughs and colds.
  • Modern (18th c. onward): Kentish cobnut orchards commercialise the nut; ecologists recognise hazel coppice as England’s richest small-mammal habitat; Tolkien plants deep brakes of it along the Shire’s lanes, sealing its place in fantasy’s flora.

Phonetically the word’s journey is written in its spelling: the Old English ash-vowel æ raised and rounded through Middle English into the modern a, while the medial s voiced to z between vowels — the same voicing that gives houses its buzz. Semantically it only ever widened: tree, then nut, then wood, then colour, then wisdom. Few words have served stomach, eye, hand, and imagination so evenly; the hazel fed the Mesolithic child, roofed the Tudor cottage, coloured the lover’s gaze, and taught the salmon wisdom — and every sense still answers to the green brakes on Tolkien’s rising slopes.
