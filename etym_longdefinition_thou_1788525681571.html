thou (pronoun, second-person singular nominative) — The subjective case form of the archaic, dialectal, and liturgical second-person singular personal pronoun in English, equivalent to modern standard "you" when addressing a single individual as the subject of a sentence or clause. In Oscar Wilde’s fairy tale The Selfish Giant, when the Giant sees the stigmatic prints of nails on the little boy's hands and feet, he asks with reverent wonder, "Who art thou?", illustrating the word’s transition from an everyday intimate address to a vehicle of sacred solemnity and sublime revelation.

Grammatical characteristics and verbal agreement. In classical Early Modern English, thou strictly governed specific inflectional suffixes on present and past tense finite verbs. In the present indicative, verbs taking thou as their subject added the ending -(e)st (e.g., "thou knowest", "thou givest", "thou seest"), with irregular auxiliary forms including art ("thou art"), hast ("thou hast"), dost/doest ("thou dost"), and wilt ("thou wilt"). In the preterite (past tense), weak verbs took -edst ("thou lovedst"), while modals took specialized archaic stems such as couldst, wouldst, shouldst, didst, and hadst. In subjunctive or imperative moods, the bare verb stem was used ("see thou to that").

Sociolinguistic evolution — power, solidarity, and the T/V system. In the sociolinguistic framework of Brown and Gilman’s pronoun dynamics, thou represented the English "T-pronoun" (cognate to French tu, German du, Latin tu). From the fourteenth to seventeenth centuries, thou operated on two distinct axes:

  • The Solidarity Axis (Intimacy and Equality) — Spoken among family members, parents to children, lovers, close companions, and social equals within informal settings, expressing deep affection, warmth, familiarity, and emotional closeness.
  • The Power Axis (Hierarchy and Condescension) — Spoken downward by social superiors to inferiors (lords to peasants, masters to servants, magistrates to prisoners). Conversely, inferiors were strictly required to address superiors using the honorific plural "V-pronoun" ye/you.

Polysemy, extended usages, and modern registers:

  • Liturgical and theological address — Through the King James Bible (1611) and the Book of Common Prayer, thou became the universal English pronoun used when addressing God ("Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name..."). Because modern speakers only encountered thou in church, a complete psychological inversion occurred: a pronoun that historically signalled intimate informality came to be perceived as maximally formal, grand, majestic, and holy.
  • The transitive verb 'to thou' — In Early Modern English, thou was nominalized and verbalized into a transitive verb meaning to insult, address insolently, or demean someone by calling them thou instead of respectful you ("Do not thou me, sir!").
  • Quaker religious egalitarianism — Early Quakers used thou systematically to every human being regardless of aristocratic title or royal status, witnessing against vanity and worldly class distinctions.
  • Dialectal survival in Northern England — In Yorkshire, Lancashire, Derbyshire, and Cumbria dialects, thou survived in the reduced form tha or ta (as in "What's tha doin'?", "Tha knows"), serving as a cherished badge of regional identity and community warmth.
  • Literary and poetic invocations — In poetry (such as Keats’s "Ode on a Grecian Urn": "Thou still unravish'd bride of quietness") and apostrophes to abstract concepts or nature, thou elevates personification to mythic proportions.

Modern syntactic status. In contemporary standard English, thou has been entirely displaced by the generalized pronoun you. It remains active as a stylistic device in period drama, historical fiction, religious prayer, and regional vernaculars, representing one of the most historically rich grammatical markers in the English language.
