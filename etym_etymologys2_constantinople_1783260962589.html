Derivation chain for CONSTANTINOPLE (a Greek compound of two root words — the imperial name Constantinus and Greek polis; chains given for each element in isolation, then the compound): ROOT 1 — Constantinus (the emperor's name): 1. Proto-Indo-European *steh2- "to stand" (reconstructed root; reflexes include Sanskrit stha- "to stand", Greek histemi "I stand, I set", Latin stare "to stand", English stand), c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE. 2. Latin sto, stare "to stand" (inherited from *steh2-; attested from the earliest Latin records, 6th-3rd c. BCE). 3. Latin constare "to stand together, hold firm, be composed of" (com- "together" + stare; attested 2nd c. BCE, e.g., Plautus). 4. Latin constans, gen. constantis "standing firm, steadfast, constant" (present participle of constare; attested 1st c. BCE, e.g., Cicero). 5. Latin Constantinus "Constantine" (cognomen formed from constans with the suffix -inus; borne notably by the Emperor Constantine I, ruled 306-337 CE; attested 3rd c. CE onward). 6. Greek Konstantinos (Greek form of the imperial name; used in the Greek East), 4th c. CE. ROOT 2 — polis "city": 1. Proto-Indo-European *plh1- "to fill" (per Beekes: the city as the "full settlement"; an older school, following Pokorny, reconstructed *pel- "fortified height, citadel" instead; the ultimate root is therefore debated), c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE. 2. Greek polis "city, city-state, citadel, the polis community" (attested from Mycenaean Greek, where it appears in Linear B tablets, and in Homer, 8th c. BCE). 3. Greek Konstantinoupolis "the City of Constantine" (compound of Konstantinos + polis; the popular name of the new capital from the 4th c. CE, alongside the official Nova Roma), 4th c. CE. 4. Latin Constantinopolis (Latinized form of the Greek compound; attested 4th c. CE in Latin writers). 5. English CONSTANTINOPLE (via Latin and French; attested in English chronicles from the 12th-13th c. CE, e.g., in Middle English histories of the Crusades; the standard modern form), 12th-21st c. CE. Note on the debate: the root of polis is not fully settled — Beekes favours a derivation from *plh1- "to fill" (the filled, settled community), while Pokorny's older *pel- "fortified height" remains in circulation; a Pre-Greek or substrate element has also been suggested by some scholars. The imperial name Constantinus, by contrast, has a fully secure chain from *steh2- "to stand" via constare and constans. The compound Konstantinoupolis was formed in Greek; the official dedication was Nova Roma, but the Greek name "City of Constantine" quickly became the universal form and is the direct ancestor of English Constantinople.
