Derivation chain for PROMONTORY (single root word): 1. Proto-Indo-European *men- "to project, stand out, tower above" (reconstructed root; kept distinct from the homophone *men- "to think," ancestor of Latin mēns and English mind — the two roots are unrelated). 2. Latin minēre "to project, overhang" (a base verb attested chiefly inside compounds such as ēminēre "to stand out," imminēre "to overhang," prōminēre "to jut out"). 3. Latin prōminēre "to jut out, project forward" — prō- "forward, forth" + minēre (attested classical Latin). 4. Latin prōmunturium "headland, promontory" — derivative of prōminēre with the suffix -turium "that which projects" (attested from the 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Caesar and Pliny). 5. Latin prōmontōrium "headland" — remodelled spelling under the influence of mōns, montis "mountain" (folk etymology; the -o- spelling prevails in medieval Latin). Note that mōns itself descends from the same PIE root *men- (through the o-grade *mon-), so the folk remodelling accidentally re-connects two genuine cognates. 6. Medieval Latin prōmontōrium → Old French promontoire "headland" (12th–13th c.). 7. Middle English / Early Modern English promontorie, promontory "headland" (borrowed from French; first attested 1547). 8. English promontory — modern form with anatomical, geological, and literary senses added from the 17th c. Note: The spelling history is the principal nuance — the classical prōmunturium and the later prōmontōrium coexist in Latin texts, and the -o- form, reinforced by mons "mountain," became standard in the Romance transmission to English.
