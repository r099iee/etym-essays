lumber — The biography of lumber is a study in the humbling of a word.

The noun that names sawn timber is, in truth, a latecomer and an opportunist: it is recorded in English only from the mid-1550s, and then with a meaning far from any sawmill — "heavy, clumsy, useless objects," the odds and ends that gather in attics and storerooms. Etymologists agree that this sense grew out of the older verb, to lumber, recorded around 1300 as lomere, "to move clumsily," which English apparently borrowed from a Scandinavian source akin to dialectal Swedish loma, "to move slowly, walk heavily," and Old Norse lami, "lame." Behind that Scandinavian cluster stands a reconstructed PIE root *lem-, "to break in pieces," with derivatives meaning "broken, crippled" — the same root that produced English lame itself, along with Old Church Slavonic lomiti "to break" and Lithuanian luomas "lame." The word's extra -b- is what philologists call "unetymological," added by analogy with humble and nimble, so that the very spelling of lumber is a small monument to sound change and spelling drift. At its root, then, lumber begins as a word about lameness and brokenness: what lumbers is what has been broken, crippled, made awkward.

  • PIE *lem- "to break": the reconstructed root; broken → lame → clumsy — the chain of the whole family.
  • Proto-Germanic *lama- "weak-limbed": Old English lama → lame; Old Norse lami → loma "to walk heavily."
  • Middle English lomere (c. 1300): "to move clumsily" — the verb comes FIRST, from a Scandinavian source; the -b- is unetymological.
  • English noun lumber (1550s): "heavy, clumsy, useless objects" — the shift from clumsy motion to clumsy mass; lumber-room, lumber-house coined for the storerooms and pawnshops.
  • American narrowing (c. 1660s): New England seizes the noun for the great export of the forests — sawn timber; a semantic shift of narrowing and commercialization that never fully reached Britain.
  • The industry (18th–19th c.): lumber-yard (1777), lumber-mill (1830), lumber camp (1839), lumberjack (1830s), and the back-formed verb to lumber "to cut forest trees" (1809).
  • The Lombard thread (debated): a folk tradition derives the junk-sense from the Lombard pawnbrokers of medieval London; the OED treats this as an association, not a derivation.

The transformation from verb to noun was a shift from clumsy motion to clumsy mass. By 1550, lumber named the accumulated clutter that is awkward to move — the broken chair, the disused chest, the household bric-a-brac — and English coined lumber-room and lumber-house for the storerooms, and by grim extension the pawnshops, where such goods collected. Here the word collided with a second, very different story. Medieval England's money-lenders and pawnbrokers were predominantly Italians from Lombardy — the Lombards, whose London quarter, Lombard Street, still bears their name — and their shops naturally filled with unredeemed pledges. A persistent tradition, already enshrined in older etymologies, derives the junk-sense of lumber from Lombard itself, on the notion that a pawnbroker's shop is a lumber-house of forfeited goods. Modern scholarship treats this link as an association rather than a derivation: the OED notes that lumber-room is not attested before the noun lumber, so the pawnshop story is likely an attractive guess that grew up around an already existing word. What is certain is that the two threads — the heavy, clumsy "lame" word and the pawnbroker's clutter — reinforced one another in English for two centuries.

The decisive turn came across the Atlantic. In seventeenth-century New England the British noun, meaning accumulated bulky goods, was seized upon for the great export of the New World's forests: from about 1660, Massachusetts records use lumber for "timber sawn into rough planks for use." The semantic shift is one of narrowing and commercialization — from any clumsy discarded bulk to the specific, marketable bulk of sawn wood — and it is a shift that never fully occurred in Britain, where lumber still means attic clutter even as Americans hear sawdust. The whole industrial vocabulary of the trade then grew from the American sense: lumber-yard (1777), lumber-mill (1830), lumber camp (1839), lumberjack (1830s), and the verb to lumber, "to cut forest trees" (1809), which returned to English from its own noun. It is a curious full circle: a word that began as "broken, lame, clumsy" became, through the clutter of storerooms and the coinage of pawnbrokers, the standard American word for the very substance of building.

Sound and register. The phonetics of lumber are as heavy as its meaning — the closed, dark lun- opening and the rumbling -ber closure give the word a bulk that suits both the clumsy verb and the stacked planks. As sawn timber, lumber is flat, utilitarian, commercial — the vocabulary of the yard and the freight ledger; as clutter, it is homely and faintly contemptuous; as a verb of motion it is vivid, physical, and often comic (a lumbering bear). The word's register is always material: it names things that weigh on the ground.

The card's reading. In the passage under study the word is used at the moment a boat is broken into its own boards, so that the old sense of "brokenness" reasserts itself within the modern one. The flatboat's lumber is literally the "broken pieces" of the journey — the clumsy mass that was once a floating home, now sold board by board — and the etymology supplies a quiet, moving gloss on the prose: a word born of lameness and breakage ends by naming the substance of rebuilding. The journey's end is not triumph but a sale of boards; the man walks into the strange city with nothing left of the river but the lumber his boat has become, and the old sense of brokenness, buried in the word's root, rises again in the moment of dismantling.
