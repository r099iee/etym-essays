The present participle and gerund litigating is the product of an ancient compound verb formed within the legal institutions of the Roman Republic. Its ultimate historical roots combine two distinct Proto-Indo-European elements: the verbal root *stel- ("to put, place, set upright, establish"), which yielded the archaic Old Latin noun stlīs, and the fundamental verbal root *aǵ- ("to drive, lead, conduct, act"), which yielded Latin agere.

In Old Latin, stlīs referred to a fixed, established contest or standing legal dispute. By the time of Classical Latin, regular phonological simplification of initial consonant clusters eliminated the initial st- before l, transforming stlīs into līs (with the genitive singular lītis and stem līt-), meaning a lawsuit, judicial controversy, strife, or legal action. The Romans combined the noun stem līt- with the verb agere ("to drive, conduct, carry out") to form the compound first-conjugation verb lītigāre, meaning literally "to drive or carry on a lawsuit, to dispute in court, or to wrangle contentiously."

Roman civil jurisprudence made extensive use of lītigāre and its derivatives, including lītigātor ("a party to a lawsuit") and lītigiōsus ("contentious, fond of disputing"). In Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis, the term codified the formal rights and procedural responsibilities of citizens seeking judicial redress before a magistrate, clearly demarcating formal litigation from extrajudicial violence or private blood feuds.

Following the Renaissance revival of Roman civil law and the rapid expansion of common law legal literature in 17th-century England, the Latin past participial stem lītigāt- was Anglicized into the verb litigate (first attested in English around the 1620s–1630s). The addition of the productive Germanic participial suffix -ing formed litigating, establishing both the active verbal participle ("they are litigating the contract") and the verbal noun/gerund ("litigating is an expensive process").

The semantic evolution of litigating unfolds across several historical and cultural phases:

  • Strict Juridical Context (17th–19th c.): In early common law and chancery courts, litigating was strictly confined to the procedural prosecution and defense of civil actions, property claims, maritime disputes, and corporate liability suits. Legal scholars like Sir William Blackstone used it to describe the formal mechanisms of asserting rights through judicial discovery, witness examination, and pleading.
  • Expansion into Regulatory & Corporate Law (Early to Mid-20th c.): With the rise of the modern administrative state, antitrust legislation, and labor unions, litigating expanded from private torts to colossal institutional clashes between corporate monopolies, regulatory commissions, and organized labor.
  • Metaphorical Transfer to Public Discourse (Late 20th c.): During the political and cultural debates of the late 20th century (especially surrounding elections, constitutional crises, and civil rights), political commentators began using litigating metaphorically. Disagreements were no longer merely discussed; they were "litigated" in the media, implying that public figures were presenting evidentiary briefs and demanding moral verdicts from the electorate.
  • Contemporary Cultural & Retrospective Usage (21st c.): In contemporary discourse, the phrase "litigating the past" has become ubiquitous, describing the endless public or intellectual rehashing, cross-examination, and factional bickering over historical decisions, doctrinal divisions, or cultural controversies (as in the source text's description of theatrical scholars litigating the doctrinal schisms between rival acting teachers).

Through every shift, litigating preserves its foundational Roman concept of bringing a contested matter to an adversarial forum for rigorous, contentious examination.
