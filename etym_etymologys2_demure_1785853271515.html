PIE *mer- 'to delay, hinder, hold back' (reconstructed; the root's shape and even its status are debated — many accept it for Latin mora, some doubt the family) —>
Latin mora (feminine) 'a delay, hindrance' (attested from the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE, e.g., in Plautus and Terence) —>
Latin moror, morārī 'to delay, tarry, linger' (deponent verb; attested in classical prose) —>
Latin dēmorārī 'to tarry, linger, remain, stay' (intensive dē- + morārī; attested in classical Latin) —>
Old French demeurer 'to stay, remain, dwell, linger' (attested from the 9th century, e.g., in the Sequence of Saint Eulalia) —>
Anglo-French demure, past participle of demurer 'sober, settled, composed, grave' (attested in the 14th century; the past participle of 'to remain' developed the sense 'settled, staid') —>
Middle English demure 'sober, grave, sedate' (attested late 14th century) —>
Modern English DEMURE 'reserved, modest, quiet, composed' ◄ TARGET.

Debate note: the OED derives the adjective from the Anglo-French past participle of demurer, but the exact route is debated — some scholars see the influence of Old French meür/mur 'mature, ripe' (shaping the form and reinforcing the sense of a settled, self-possessed person). The adjective demure and the verb demur (via Old French demorer/demeurer, 'to delay, object') are doublets from the same Latin verb that have drifted far apart semantically.
