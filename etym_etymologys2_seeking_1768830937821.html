Linear derivation chain for SEEKING (single root word): PIE *seh₂g- (older notation *sāg-; to track, smell out, seek out, perceive keenly) — reconstructed root, c. 4500–3500 BCE; the hunter's root of following a scent, attested in Latin sāgīre (to perceive keenly, track by scent) → sagāx (keen-scented, shrewd) → English sagacious and presage, and in Old Irish saigid (he seeks, goes toward). → Proto-Germanic *sōkijaną (to seek out, to search for; a class 1 weak verb) — reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE. → Old English sēcan / sēcean (to search, seek, visit, approach; weak verb sēcan–sōhte–gesōht) — attested from the 8th century CE. → Middle English seken / sechen — 12th–15th century. → Modern English seek → seeking (present participle, gerund, and participial adjective) ◄ TARGET. Germanic cognates: Old Norse sœkja, Gothic sōkjan, Dutch zoeken, German suchen (all "to seek"). Note on the debate: Two scholarly nuances — (1) the root is written *seh₂g- in laryngeal notation and *sāg- in the older system; these are the same root. (2) The Greek hēgéomai (to lead, guide; the basis of English hegemony) is sometimes cited as a cognate, but the correspondence is debated and several handbooks treat the Greek form as belonging to a different root. The Germanic chain *sōkijaną → sēcan → seken → seek → seeking is secure.
