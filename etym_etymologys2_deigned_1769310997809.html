Single linear chain for DEIGNED
Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European root *dek- (also *deḱ-), "to take, accept; to be fitting, seemly, proper," c. 4500–3000 BCE. Attested by Greek dékhomai "I receive, accept," Greek dóxa "that which seems right; opinion, glory," and Latin decet "it is fitting."
Stage 2 — Latin decēre, "to be fitting, proper, seemly," attested 2nd–1st c. BCE (Plautus, Cicero); from PIE *dek-. The same root yields Latin decōrus "decorous" and dignus "worthy."
Stage 3 — Latin dignus, -a, -um, "worthy, deserving, meriting," attested 2nd–1st c. BCE (Plautus, Cicero); from PIE *dek-no- "fitting, acceptable." The noun dignitās "worth, dignity, standing" is its companion.
Stage 4 — Latin dignor, dignārī, dignātus sum (deponent), "to deem worthy, to think fit, to condescend," attested 1st c. BCE (Cicero, Livy); alongside the active Late Latin dignāre "to deign."
Stage 5 — Old French deigner (also daignier, deignier), "to deign, to think worthy, to condescend," attested 10th–12th c.; from Vulgar Latin *dignāre (contracted from dignāri).
Stage 6 — Anglo-Norman / Middle English deignen (also dainen), "to think worthy, to condescend, to vouchsafe," attested late 13th c. (c. 1290).
Stage 7 — English deign, 15th c., spelling regularized; the past tense and past participle deigned (16th c.) — FINAL NODE, the exact form used on this card.
Debate note: The Latin chain from dignus to dignārī is uncontroversial, and dignus from PIE *dek-no- is standard. The only nuance is that the PIE root fused two senses — "take/accept" and "be fitting" — and scholars differ on which is original; both are extremely old, and Latin's decēre ("to be fitting") and Greek's dékhomai ("to receive") simply preserve different faces of the same root. No stage of the recorded Latin–French–English chain is in doubt.
