- 4th to 15th Century (Latin Antecedents) - Formulated in Patristic and Scholastic Latin (*indēfectibiliter*, *indēfectibilitās*) by theologians including Saint Augustine, Tertullian, and Thomas Aquinas to establish the divine nature of God's promises and the eternal preservation of the Catholic Church against heresy.
- Early 17th Century - Enters Early Modern English theological prose (c. 1610–1630) during the fierce religious controversies between the Church of England and the Roman Catholic Church regarding papal supremacy, ministerial succession, and saving grace.
- 18th Century - Enlightenment philosophers and rational theologians broaden the adverb to describe the immutable laws of nature, eternal mathematical verities, and the flawless rational order of the cosmos.
- 19th Century - Adopted by high Victorian aesthetic critics, Romantics, and essayists (e.g., Samuel Taylor Coleridge, John Ruskin, Walter Pater) to characterize untarnished artistic perfection, moral saintliness, and imperishable poetic truth.
- 20th to 21st Century - Flourishes in elite literary criticism (Vladimir Nabokov, George Steiner, Frank Kermode) as a dazzling superlative for modern masterpieces whose revolutionary freshness, formal daring, and relevance remain permanently immune to aging or obsolescence.
