- 19th–early 20th century – 'propinquital' emerges as a rare, chiefly learned coinage, formed within English from the noun 'propinquity' (in English since the 15th century) plus the adjectival suffix -al; it lives almost entirely in dictionaries and scholarly prose as a taxonomic label for 'of or arising from nearness' — clinical, almost anatomical, never part of common speech.
- Mid-20th century – the word's rare literary career begins: in Titus Groan (1946) Mervyn Peake uses it of Rottcodd's attachment to his carvings, 'attached in a propinquital way'; the Latinate, filing-cabinet register deliberately ironizes real feeling — affection reduced to a category of contiguity, love by adjacency rather than love by judgment.
- Late 20th–21st century – 'propinquital' remains a dictionary word and a stylistic instrument: a writer's tool for naming proximity-born ties (of place, of shared space, of kin) with wry, pseudo-scientific detachment; its perception has settled as that of a deliberately elevated coinage that makes nearness itself sound like a causal claim.
