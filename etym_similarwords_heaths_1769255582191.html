• moor: Similar because both name open, uncultivated, wild country in Britain. Differs because a moor is typically higher, wetter, and peatier (northern England, Scotland), often a vast boggy plateau, while a heath is drier, lower, and sandy, with heather and gorse rather than blanket bog.
  • heathland: Similar because both denote the same ecological habitat. Differs because heathland is the modern technical, ecological term for the habitat itself, while heath is the old everyday word and the place-name component.
  • fell: Similar because both are open, wild British upland country. Differs because a fell is a high moorland or mountain in the north of England (Lake District), emphasizing height and rock, while a heath is lower, flatter, and heather-clad.
  • downs (downland): Similar because both are open, grass-covered, uncultivated English country. Differs because downland is low, rolling chalk hills grazed to smooth turf (the South Downs), treeless but grassy rather than heathery.
  • common (common land): Similar because both are open, unenclosed land. Differs because a common is land held in common by a community for grazing — a legal and social category — while a heath is a natural habitat category.
  • scrubland: Similar because both are open land covered with low woody vegetation rather than trees. Differs because scrubland is the generic, worldwide ecological term (bushes, shrubs, small trees), while heath specifically connotes heather, gorse, and the British Isles.
  • wasteland: Similar because both denote wild, unproductive, uncultivated land. Differs because wasteland emphasizes uselessness and barrenness (and is often figurative — a cultural wasteland), while a heath is ecologically rich, beloved, and picturesque.
  • wilderness: Similar because both are wild, uncultivated, uninhabited country. Differs because wilderness is a grand, worldwide word for true untamed land (forest, desert, mountain), while a heath is a modest, semi-managed British scrub of human scale.
  • grassland: Similar because both are open, treeless, low-vegetation land. Differs because grassland is the neutral ecological term for grass-dominated habitats anywhere, while heath is dominated by ericaceous shrubs and is geographically specific.
  • prairie: Similar because both are vast, open, uncultivated plains. Differs because a prairie is the grass sea of North America — deep-soiled, fertile, treeless — while a heath is the poor, sandy, heathery scrub of Britain.
  • steppe: Similar because both are open, dry, low-vegetation plains. Differs because a steppe is the vast, semi-arid grass-and-shrub plain of Eurasia, continental and enormous, while a heath is a small, maritime, heather-clad tract.
  • pampas: Similar because both are open, treeless, grass-covered plains. Differs because pampas is the specific temperate grassland of South America, while heath is the British scrub habitat.
  • savanna: Similar because both are open grassy land with scattered low growth. Differs because savanna is the tropical grassland with scattered trees of Africa and elsewhere, while a heath is a cool-temperate, treeless shrubland.
  • tundra: Similar because both are open, low-vegetation, windswept wildlands. Differs because tundra is the treeless Arctic and sub-Arctic biome of moss, lichen, and permafrost, while a heath is a temperate habitat of heather and gorse.
  • barrens: Similar because both are open, infertile, sparsely vegetated tracts. Differs because barrens (North American usage) emphasizes sterility, while heath is ecologically productive and culturally cherished.
  • heather: Similar because both name the heath's characteristic plant and are metonymically linked (heath → heather). Differs because heather is the plant (Calluna vulgaris), a flowering shrub, while heath is the land it covers (or, in gardening, the Erica genus).
  • furze (gorse): Similar because both are associated with heaths, and both name wild uncultivated places by association. Differs because furze/gorse is specifically the spiny golden-flowered plant Ulex, not the land.
  • outback: Similar because both are vast, wild, sparsely settled inland country. Differs because the outback is the Australian interior — enormous, dry, remote — while a heath is a small British habitat.
  • heathen: Similar because both derive from the same Old English root hǣð, and both originally signified the heath-dwellers. Differs because heathen means "pagan, non-Christian" (a person), a total shift from the landscape sense of heath, while heath names only the land.
