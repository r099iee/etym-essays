• Jugoslavia: The same name in its Serbo-Croatian spelling, in which j represents the sound /j/; it differs only orthographically and is common in historical and regional texts.
  • Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes: The state’s official name from 1918 to 1929; it is the same polity before the royal dictatorship renamed it, but it names the three constituent peoples explicitly rather than subsuming them under “South Slav.”
  • South Slavdom: The cultural-linguistic idea behind the name; it is broader and non-political, covering Bulgarians as well, whereas Yugoslavia denotes the state or its territory.
  • Balkans: The wider peninsula containing Yugoslavia; similar as a geographical frame, but it includes Greece, Bulgaria, Albania and European Turkey, and carries different exoticising connotations.
  • Illyria: An older humanist name for the South Slavic lands, revived by the Illyrian Movement; similar as a unifying label, but it is antiquarian and historically inaccurate for the Slavs.
  • Serbia and Montenegro: The successor federation of 1992–2006; similar territory and capital, but it excludes Croatia, Slovenia, Bosnia, Macedonia and Kosovo, and the name abandoned the pan-Slav claim.
  • Pan-Slavism: The wider nineteenth-century movement of Slavic solidarity; similar ideological root, but it embraces Russians, Poles and Czechs too, whereas Yugoslavia is specifically South Slavic.
  • Yugoslav: The adjective and demonym; identical in reference, differing only in grammatical function.
  • Yugosphere: The contemporary cultural-economic network of successor states; similar referent, but deliberately informal and post-national.
  • Czechoslovakia: A parallel twentieth-century compound state of two Slavic peoples; similar as a coined Slavic federation, but it joined West Slavs and dissolved peacefully.
