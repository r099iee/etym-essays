Picts preserves a Roman nickname that became a nation's name. Latin Picti, first securely attested in Eumenius's panegyric of 297 CE ("Caledones and other Picts"), literally means "painted ones," from pingere "to paint," itself from PIE *peyḱ- "to mark, prick, paint" (also yielding Greek poikilos "mottled," Sanskrit piṃśati "he adorns," English picture, pigment, pint). Romans applied it to the tattooed or woad-painted peoples beyond Hadrian's Wall. Whether natives called themselves anything like *Priteni (reconstructed from Welsh Prydyn, Irish Cruithni, both meaning Britons/Picts via Celtic *prit- "form, shape" — i.e., tattooed figures) and Romans merely Latinised it by convenient pun, is the classic Pictish debate. What is certain is that Picti is Latin in form and transparent in meaning, and that English Picts descends through Old English Pihtas, Peohtas (Bede, Anglo-Saxon Chronicle) and Middle English Pictes.

The Picts themselves left no literature and only brief ogham inscriptions; almost everything comes from outsiders — Ammianus, Bede, Adomnán — until modern archaeology recovered their symbol stones, brochs, and monasteries (Portmahomack). They were a confederation of northern British kingdoms (Fortriu, Circin, Fib, Fidach) speaking a Brittonic Celtic language (Pictish), later Gaelicised after union with Dál Riata under Kenneth MacAlpin (c. 843 CE).

  • PIE *peyḱ- to Latin pingere: Root of marking and decorating by pricking or colouring. Latin pingere, pictus (past participle) gives a rich family: pictura (picture), pigmentum (pigment), pictor (painter). Sense is artful marking of a surface — wall, body, cloth.
  • Pingere to Picti (c. 200–300 CE): Plural substantive "the painted (men)." Parallel to Roman nicknaming of barbarians by appearance (cf. Barbari, Flavi). First applied during the Tetrarchy's northern campaigns; popularised by Ammianus's Picts, Scots, Saxons, Attacotti tetrad.
  • Picti to Old English Pihtas (700s–1000s): Bede's Historia Ecclesiastica transmits Picti to the English as Peohtas/Pihtas. Initial P- preserved; -ht- shows Germanic consonantal adaptation. Meaning: the northern neighbours, heathen then Christian, feared raiders then fellow Christians.
  • Middle English to Modern (1200s–1700s): Pictes, Pightes, then antiquarian Picts. After the Picts vanished as a kingdom (absorbed into Alba/Scotland), the word becomes purely historical — mysterious vanished people of standing stones.
  • 19th–20th c. romanticisation: Pinkerton, Skene, and popular histories make Picts tattooed savages, matriarchal relics, or lost tribes. Victorian racial theory briefly claimed them as non-Aryan dwarfs — now abandoned.
  • Durant's usage: Picts as one more cannibal tribe in a universal savage past. This repeats Roman-barbarian slander (St Jerome claimed Attacotti, allies of Picts, ate human flesh) filtered through early anthropology. Modern scholars find no credible evidence for institutional Pictish cannibalism; the charge tells us about Roman and Victorian imaginations, not Pictish diet.

Phonetically the journey is short: /ˈpɪktiː/ > /pɪxtɑs/ > /pɪkts/. One syllable, final cluster -kts, preserving the Latin stem intact. Semantically the journey is from adjective (painted) to ethnonym (a people) to mystery (a vanished civilisation). To say Picts today evokes symbol stones, woad, and northern mist — a Roman adjective that outlived the empire that coined it by seventeen centuries.
