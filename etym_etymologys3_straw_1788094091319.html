Straw is one of the oldest words of English, and its name is a fossil of prehistoric farming. The word descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *stere- / *sterh₃-, "to spread out, scatter, strew" — the same root that gives English "strew" itself, Latin sternere "to spread, strew" (whence English "stratum", and, through strata via, "street"), and Greek στόρνυμι "to spread". The Germanic speakers named the harvest residue not by its botany — the stalks of wheat or barley — but by its use: straw is, etymologically, "that which is strewn", scattered on floors, spread under sleepers and animals. The Proto-Germanic etymon *strawą sits squarely in this family: Old Norse strá, Old Saxon strō, Old High German strō, Dutch stro, and German Stroh are all the same word with the same meaning, and Old English strēaw — recorded in glosses and texts from the eighth and ninth centuries on — is their direct English sibling. The closeness of these forms across a thousand years of upheaval and sound change shows how central the substance was: where a society sleeps, thatches, and beds its beasts on straw, its word for straw does not drift.

Sound changes

Old English strēaw rests on the West Germanic development of *strawą: the spelling ēa represents the Old English diphthong that arose through breaking and diphthongization of the vowel before the following -w. In Middle English this diphthong smoothed and lengthened (straw, stro), and by the early modern period the word had settled at modern /strɔː/ — the exact vowel of "law" and "saw" in Received Pronunciation. Phonologically it is a model one-syllable word: a three-consonant cluster, a long low vowel, and a closing glide, stable now for four centuries. The verb "to strew" (Old English strēowian, strēawian) travelled from the same root by a different path and survives beside the noun; the pair is a standing demonstration of how a single PIE root can split into two English words with visibly different careers.

Medieval material culture

In medieval England straw was infrastructure. It strewed the floors of hall and cottage — fresh straw or rushes laid over the old, stamped in and replaced in layers until the whole floor was dug out and renewed; it filled the pallets on which rich and poor slept, so that "to be in the straw" became the idiom for a woman lying in childbed (still found in seventeenth-century medical English); it bedded horses, cattle, and sheep night and year; it covered roofs as thatch; it was chopped into the clay of walls and bricks — the Exodus image of straw-for-bricks was a daily engineering reality in the building economy; it padded crates, baskets, saddles, and panniers; and in lean winters it was itself fodder, however poor. The word's social universality — the same stuff that served the lord's bed served the ox's stall — is why straw entered so many proverbs: it was the one thing everyone in every class knew the weight, the price, and the worthlessness of.

The age of proverbs and the Bible

The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries made straw the standard emblem of worthlessness. "Not worth a straw" (with the intensified "not worth three straws") is proverbial before 1500, and Chaucer's English already uses "a straw" as the ultimate trifle; "a man of straw" appears in the sixteenth century for a person of no substance — a sham, a scarecrow-like dummy, later specifically the debtor or claimant with nothing behind him. The Bible re-charged the imagery at the same time: in Exodus 5 Pharaoh withholds the straw without which the Israelites cannot make their full tale of bricks, and in 1 Corinthians 3 the King James Version burns the works "of wood, hay, stubble" at the judgment. Straw, the cheapest and lightest of harvest products, carried the whole weight of these images because everyone knew exactly what it was: dry, hollow, and nearly free. The later idiom set refined the physics of the substance — "a straw in the wind" (17th–18th centuries) plays on lightness in a breeze as a sign of direction; "the last straw that breaks the camel's back" (proverbial from the 18th century, fixed in the 19th) turns straw into the negligible addition that overturns a burden, the whole point being how little a straw weighs.

The American expansion and the drinking straw

American English naturalized and extended the family. "Straw man" took on its modern rhetorical sense in nineteenth-century America — from the straw-filled dummies used in target practice, the image of an opponent set up only to be knocked down — and became the standard name for a hollow argument; "straw vote" and "straw poll" (also 19th century) tested opinion by a light, informal sampling; "straw boss" (early 20th century) named a deputy or minor overseer on worksites and farms. Meanwhile the material sense industrialized: sucking liquid through a hollow stalk is an ancient practice, but the manufactured drinking straw dates from the 1880s, when Marvin C. Stone patented a waxed paper tube (1888); plastic straws followed in the mid-twentieth century and became universal. The 21st century completed a strange arc: the plastic straw, once a symbol of convenience, became from 2018 onward the emblem of environmental waste, the object of bans and campaigns worldwide, while the material straw returned to fashion as mulch, insulation, and ecological building blocks. Etymologically, "strawberry" (Old English strēawberige) belongs to the same family by one of two paths — either the plant's runners that stray or "strew" themselves along the ground, or the straw traditionally laid under the plants to keep the fruit clean — and both derivations draw on the same ancient root of spreading and scattering, making the berry a permanent exhibit of how this root kept coining new names for the humble and the useful.
