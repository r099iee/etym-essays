• Indra’s: Similar because Indra is the primary personal name of the exact same deity, sharing all mythological roles, weapons (the vajra), and sovereign titles; differs because Indra’s is the foundational, ubiquitous personal name denoting the storm-god of the Ṛgveda, whereas Śakra’s is an honorific adjectival epithet meaning "the capable, mighty one," which became a specialized proper name in classical epic poetry and the dominant standard designation throughout Buddhist canonical literature.
  • Devendra’s: Similar because both refer to the monarchical supremacy of Indra over the pantheon of gods; differs because Devendra’s is an explicit compound title (deva + indra, "lord of the devas"), emphasizing his political-hierarchical rank above other celestial beings, whereas Śakra’s emphasizes intrinsic personal might, power, and competence.
  • Purandara’s: Similar because both are venerable Vedic epithets applied to Indra in Sanskrit literature; differs because Purandara’s specifically translates to "the destroyer of strongholds/fortresses," highlighting Indra’s martial role as the smiter of enemy citadels, whereas Śakra’s designates general divine power, royal majesty, and capacity to rule.
  • Vāsava’s: Similar because both are classic patronymic/group epithets of Indra; differs because Vāsava’s identifies him as the leader and lord of the eight Vasus (elemental deities representing earth, fire, wind, water, stars, and sky), emphasizing cosmic elemental leadership rather than singular martial potency.
  • Maghavan’s: Similar because both are exalted Vedic titles for Indra; differs because Maghavan’s literally means "the bountiful, generous one" (from magha, "gift, bounty"), highlighting Indra’s role in showering wealth, cattle, and rain upon pious sacrificial patrons, whereas Śakra’s focuses on capability and omnipotent authority.
  • Zeus’s: Similar in comparative Indo-European mythology because Zeus is the cognate Greco-Roman king of the gods, sky deity, and wielder of the lightning bolt; differs because Zeus’s derives from the Indo-European sky-father root *Dyēus and belongs to the Olympian Greek pantheon, whereas Śakra’s belongs to the Indo-Aryan Vedic-Epic tradition and underwent extensive transformation into a Buddhist guardian deity.
  • Jupiter’s: Similar because Jupiter is the supreme Roman sovereign god of the sky and thunder, occupying the same macrocosmic archetypal role; differs because Jupiter’s is situated within Latin religious jurisprudence and Roman civic cults (*Jupiter Optimus Maximus*), lacking the Buddhist doctrinal assimilation and epic narrative roles unique to Śakra.
  • Thor’s: Similar in Germanic mythology because Thor is the storm-god wielding a divine crushing hammer (Mjölnir) against monstrous giants, parallel to Śakra wielding the vajra against asuras; differs because Thor is not the supreme king of the Germanic pantheon (that role belonging to Odin), whereas Śakra is the crowned monarch of Svarga and the devas.
  • Sovereign’s: Similar because both denote the possessive status of supreme imperial rule; differs because sovereign’s is a generic political-constitutional descriptor applicable to any earthly human monarch, whereas Śakra’s is an intrinsically divine, mythological archetype.
  • Celestial’s: Similar because both refer to heavenly, transcendent provenance; differs because celestial’s is a vague, abstract adjective describing anything pertaining to the skies or heavens, whereas Śakra’s specifies the distinct personality, lineage, and attributes of the King of the Gods.
