- [13th–15th c.] - Middle English borrows *chimney* (from Old French *cheminee*, hearth/fireplace), while *pot* designates earthenware vessels; masonry chimneys remain flat-topped stone structures in castles and manors - [perception: luxury domestic heating technology of the feudal nobility and wealthy merchants]
- [16th–17th c.] - Tudor and Jacobean bricklayers develop elaborate molded brick flues and early terracotta extensions to improve draft as coal begins to replace firewood - [perception: advanced craftsmanship and aristocratic architectural prestige]
- [18th c.] - Industrial pottery kilns begin mass-producing standard fireclay and earthenware *chimney-pots*; the compound *chimney-pot* enters written English architectural records - [perception: practical engineering solution to smoky fireplaces and expanding urban density]
- [19th c.] - The Victorian coal-heating boom crowns millions of city roofs with forests of decorative terracotta *chimney-pots*; literature (Dickens, Wilde's *The Selfish Giant*) associates them with rooftop gales and metropolitan skylines - [perception: quintessential hallmark of the Victorian urban roofscape, vulnerable to ferocious gales]
- [20th–21st c.] - Central heating and gas conversion diminish active coal flues, but *chimney-pots* remain cherished architectural heritage fittings on historical buildings and standard measures of storm force - [perception: historic architectural charm, antique pottery collectables, and traditional masonry craftsmanship]
