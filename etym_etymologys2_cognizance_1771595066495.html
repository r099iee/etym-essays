COGNIZANCE — single root word; the chain from PIE to the present noun.

1. PIE *ǵneh₃- “to know, to perceive” (reconstructed; the great Indo-European ‘knowing’ root, source of Greek gignṓskō, Latin gnōscō/nōscō, Germanic *knēaną — English know, and Sanskrit jñā-).
2. Latin gnōscō / nōscō “to get to know, to become acquainted with” (classical Latin, 1st c. BCE); the *g- dropped in Latin, leaving nōscō.
3. Latin cognōscere “to get to know fully, to learn, to recognize” (prefix co- ‘together, with’ + nōscere ‘to get to know’; attested in classical Latin, e.g. Cicero); past participle cognitus ‘known’.
4. Latin cognitio “knowledge, examination, legal inquiry” (noun from cognōscere; also the technical term for a judicial inquiry in Roman law) — the source of English cognition and cognitive.
5. Old French conoissance / conissance “knowledge, acquaintance, recognition” (attested from the 12th century; from conoistre/conoitre ‘to know’, the French descendant of Latin cognōscere).
6. Anglo-French conissance / conisaunce “knowledge, recognition, legal notice” (attested in Anglo-Norman legal records, 13th–14th centuries).
7. Middle English cognisaunce / conisaunce “knowledge, recognition, legal notice” (attested c. 1325 onward; spelling also conysaunce, cognizance).
8. English COGNIZANCE “knowledge or awareness; formal recognition or notice taken by an authority; a distinguishing mark or badge” (modern form and senses; British variant spelling cognisance).

Note on the spelling: the modern spelling cognizance reflects a learned Latinizing tendency — writers re-injected the Latin g and z into the older French-derived form, while the parallel British form cognisance (and the -ant/-ence family) keeps the softer French spelling. Both spellings are correct and mean the same thing; the OED treats them as variants. The semantic narrowing from ‘knowledge’ to ‘formal judicial notice’ happened in English legal usage, which hardened the phrase ‘take cognizance of’ into a term of art.
