- Ancient Greek Mythology (c. 8th century BCE onwards) - A sacred, fearsome river of the Underworld, binding divine oaths and granting invulnerability; viewed with profound awe and dread, serving as the definitive boundary to the afterlife.
- Roman Adaptation (c. 1st century BCE onwards) - Retained its core mythological significance as the underworld river, a potent symbol of death, irreversible passage, and divine justice; continued to be a powerful and frequently invoked literary motif in Latin epic poetry.
- Medieval & Renaissance Literature (c. 12th - 17th centuries CE) - Incorporated into Christian infernal imagery (e.g., Dante's *Inferno*), often recontextualized as a swampy, foul marsh in hell where the wrathful and sullen were punished; maintained its connotation of dread, finality, and a hellish environment.
- Enlightenment & Romantic Periods (c. 18th - 19th centuries CE) - Primarily functioned as a classical allusion in poetry, art, and drama, used to evoke the sublime, the macabre, and the solemnity of death; its mythological origins were widely understood, but direct religious belief in its existence waned, becoming more of a literary and artistic device.
- Modern & Contemporary Usage (c. 20th century CE - Present) - Primarily a cultural reference to Greek mythology, symbolizing a point of no return, an ultimate boundary, or a dark, infernal place; occasionally used in popular culture, scientific nomenclature (e.g., Pluto's moon), and metaphorical expressions ("crossing the Styx"); its connotation remains one of solemnity, finality, and a lingering sense of dread or profound commitment.
