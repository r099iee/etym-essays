Abortive travels from Roman biology to Victorian art criticism without ever losing its image of a birth cut short. Its deepest root is PIE *h₃er- “to set in motion, stir, rise,” which in Latin yields oriri — to rise (the sun rises), to arise, to be born, to originate (whence origin, orient, orientation). Prefixed with ab- “away from, off,” Latin forms aboriri: to go away from rising — hence to disappear (stars aboriri at dawn), to die, and in obstetrics to miscarry, to fail to come to birth. The verbal noun abortus (a miscarrying, a miscarriage) and the agent abortio belong to medical and legal Latin; the adjective abortivus means pertaining to or resulting from miscarriage — premature, born before term.

  • Classical Latin (500 BCE–400 CE): orior, ortus is rising and birth; aborior, abortus is its privation — sinking, abortion. Cicero, Pliny and medical writers use abortus for miscarriage and abortivus for what causes or suffers it. The prefix ab- does moral work: away from life.
  • Late Latin to Old French (400–1400): abortivus passes into learned medicine; Old French takes abortif (feminine abortive) for premature offspring and fruitless labour. Sound shifts are slight: Latin -ivus to French -if, loss of final -us, stress on the last syllable.
  • Early English borrowing (1500s–1600s): English, hungry for Latin adjectives in -ive (active, passive, creative), borrows abortive first in the obstetric sense: abortive birth, abortive child (1540s–1610s). Spelling anglicises abortif to abortive on the model of active, motive. Then, as with many body metaphors, it transfers outward: an abortive enterprise miscarries like a pregnancy (Bacon, Milton: abortive designs, abortive plots).
  • Enlightenment to Victorian expansion (1700–1900): naturalists need a word for arrested development — abortive stamens, abortive petals, abortive instincts (Darwin) — and physicians for therapies that cut disease short (abortive treatment of fevers, later of migraines). Pater in The Renaissance (1873) lifts the word to cultural history: the twelfth-century revival is “brilliant, but in part abortive,” a civilisation’s pregnancy that could not come to term until the fifteenth century. The metaphor dignifies failure as prematurity rather than sterility.

Phonetically the journey is conservative: Latin abortivus /a.borˈtiː.wus/ to French /a.bɔʁ.tif/ to English /əˈbɔː.tɪv/ shows weakening of the first vowel to schwa, stabilisation of -tive as /tɪv/, and RP lengthening of or to /ɔː/. Morphologically English keeps the Latin stem abort- plus productive -ive (having the nature of), allowing abortively and abortiveness. No Germanic root competes; this is a pure learned Latinism, which is why it stays formal. To hear Pater’s sentence rightly is to hear Rome’s nursery and sickroom behind Oxford’s aestheticism: something conceived beautifully, carried hopefully, but born too soon.
