- [Old English] — Originating in mearu ("tender, soft, delicate, frail"), used to describe tender shoots, fragile bodies, or delicate youth — [perception: physically soft, vulnerable to damage or decay]
- [c. 1400 Late Middle English] — Evolved into melwe, specifically denoting ripe, soft, and juicy fruits ready for harvest, as well as rich, crumbly agricultural soil — [perception: fertile, fruitful, ready for nourishing consumption]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English] — Extended to alcoholic beverages (wines and ciders that have matured to shed harsh tannins) and musical sounds (warm, deep vocal tones lacking brassy edge) — [perception: cultivated sensory refinement and aesthetic pleasure]
- [Late 18th – 19th c. Romantic Era] — Adopted into Romantic poetry by Wordsworth (The Prelude) and Keats (To Autumn: "Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness"), capturing the golden, gentle solar warmth of autumn — [perception: sublime, contemplative tranquility and natural fulfillment]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Era] — Widely applied to psychological temperament (softening with age), lifestyle aesthetics (mellow music, ambient lighting), and culinary aging processes — [perception: a universally celebrated virtue of emotional balance, rich maturity, and gentle wisdom]
