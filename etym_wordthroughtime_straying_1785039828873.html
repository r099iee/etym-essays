- 14th century - 'stray' enters English from Old French 'estraier' (to wander beyond bounds); its first senses are pastoral and legal — a stray is a beast that has wandered from its herd or lawful enclosure, and the word carries a precise sense of trespass, of leaving a rightful place.
- 15th-17th century - the moral sense deepens: to stray, and to be 'astray,' is to leave the right road — of sin, of error, of a soul wandering from God — and the word bridges physical wandering and spiritual deviation.
- 18th-19th century - the physical sense stays alive for animals and travellers while the moral sense fades into metaphor; straying becomes a mild word for drifting off course, absent-minded or curious, without the old legal sting.
- Contemporary - 'straying' is standard for wandering from a path, group, or norm — straying thoughts, straying ponies; it keeps a faint trace of its old boundary-crossing and moral overtones, but now reads as gentle and light, closer to a warning than a condemnation.
