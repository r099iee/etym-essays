The history of yammering is the history of a sound becoming a meaning. It begins not in a securely reconstructed Indo-European root but in the expressive, echoic stratum of Germanic vocabulary: a reduplicative base that mimicked the long-drawn moan of grief and pain. In Common Germanic this sound-sign produced a whole cluster of words built on *jēm-, all carrying the sense of lamentation and misery — Old Saxon jāmar, Old High German jāmar, Middle Dutch jameren, and the Old English adjective gēomor, 'sorrowful, sad, dejected'. These are genuine sister formations across the West Germanic spectrum, not descendants of one another: they all grew from the same moaning sound, but each language shaped that sound into its own word for grief. THE GERMANIC SISTERS AND THEIR DIVERGENT FATES The continental branches show how one moan-word could drift in many directions. The German line went on to give Jammer, 'misery, woe' — as in Jammerlappen, 'a miserable wretch' — and the verb jammern, 'to lament, to whine, to moan', with das Gejammer, 'the perpetual wailing', a word of querulous complaint that maps almost exactly onto English yammering. Dutch reshaped the misery-word into the rueful interjection jammer, 'what a pity!', a striking drift from great sorrow to mere regret — the same semantic slide from grief to fuss that English would later make on its own. Old Saxon jāmar, 'sorrow, lamentation', shows the noun form of the base, and the Old Norse sphere contributed its own wailing words to the same acoustic family. What all of them share is the acoustic root: a long, low, moaning vowel wrapped in a soft consonant frame, the sound of a creature — or a person — too hurt or too put-upon to articulate, only to moan. OLD ENGLISH: THE HIGH REGISTER OF ELEGY Old English gēomor, 'sorrowful, sad, dejected', is a word of the highest literary register, and from it, by the ordinary denominative machinery of Old English — the suffix -ian turning adjectives into verbs — came the verb gēomrian, 'to lament, to mourn, to bemoan'. This is the verb of Anglo-Saxon elegy: the mourner of The Wanderer and The Seafarer is gēomor, a man in exile from his lord and his kin, and the word belongs to the same emotional world as the tearful laments that close so much Old English poetry. In this age yammering's ancestor is not a nuisance-word at all but the vocabulary of genuine, dignified sorrow, the grief of a lone wanderer or a fallen lord, the sound of a hall emptied by death. THE SLIDE DOWN THE SCALE OF DIGNITY The passage into Middle English brought both phonetic wear and semantic downgrade. The initial cluster reduced and the vowels shifted: gēomrian became yomeren and then yameren, attested from about 1300 in the senses 'to mourn, to complain, to whine, to chatter'. Where the Old English word belonged to elegy, the Middle English and later forms slid steadily down the scale of dignity: by the fifteenth century yammer was a complaint, often a petty or querulous one; by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries it was the noise of an irritating, persistent fuss; and in the modern period it attaches freely to dogs, telephones, radios, and nagging people. The phonetic change is also a social one: as the sound flattened into the modern yammer, with its slack low vowel and doubled nasal, the meaning flattened from high sorrow to low fuss, and the word crossed from the language of poetry into the language of the street, the kitchen, and the kennel. THE PARTICIPLE AND THE WORD IN THE HOBBIT The present participle yammering, formed with the -ing suffix of continuous action, is the word at full stretch: the sound that goes on and on. In The Hobbit the burning wolves are 'crying and yammering and looking for water', and Tolkien's choice of yammering rather than howling or wailing is precise and cruel: the wolves howl first, in menace, and the word that then describes their fall is the complaint-word, the nag-word, the word of endless undignified fuss. The great beasts are reduced to the register of a yammering dog, and the reader hears in their cries not the dignity of the wild but the querulous panic of creatures in agony — the semantic history of the word, from elegy to nuisance, enacted in a single sentence. KEY ATTESTATIONS AND TURNING POINTS
  • Common Germanic — the echoic base *jēm-, 'to moan, wail', the source of a West Germanic family of sorrow-words.
  • Old Saxon — jāmar, 'sorrow, lamentation'; Old High German — jāmar, 'misery', whence German Jammer and jammern.
  • Middle Dutch — jameren, 'to lament, groan', whence Dutch jammeren and the interjection jammer 'what a pity!'.
  • Old English — gēomor 'sorrowful' and gēomrian 'to lament, mourn, bemoan', the vocabulary of Anglo-Saxon elegy.
  • c. 1300 — Middle English yameren, 'to mourn, complain, whine, chatter'; the slide toward complaint begins.
  • 15th–18th century — yammer becomes a petty, querulous complaint and an irritating, persistent fuss.
  • Modern — the continuous querulous whine of people, radios, and dogs; yammering is the unbroken, insistent form, half-complaint, half-whimper.
