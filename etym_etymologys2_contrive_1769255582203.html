Chain for contrive: 1. PIE *terp- / *trep- "to turn" (reconstructed; attested in Greek trépō "to turn" and in the wide family of turning-words; c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE). 2. Greek trépō "to turn, turn round, direct" (verb; attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE) → noun trópos "a turn, a turning; a way, manner, mode; a turn of phrase, a figure of speech" (attested from c. 6th–5th c. BCE, e.g., in lyric and rhetorical texts; the base of English "trope" and "tropical"). 3. Latin tropus "a trope, a figure of speech; a mode of music" (borrowed from Greek trópos; attested in Latin rhetorical and musical writing from the 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE). 4. Late Latin contropare / contropāre "to compare, to devise, to contrive" — a formation on con- "together, with" + tropus, with the sense "to turn together, compare, work out" (attested in Late Latin and early medieval Latin, c. 5th–9th c. CE; this is the direct Latin ancestor of the French and English verbs; the spelling tropare for "to compose, invent" also appears in the Romance tradition, cf. troubadour). 5. Old French controver "to invent, devise, find out, contrive" (attested from c. 11th–12th c. CE; Modern French controuver "to invent, contrive"). 6. Middle English contreven / contrive "to devise, invent, plan, plot" (borrowed from Old French; attested from c. 1300 CE; spellings include contreve, contryve, contrive). 7. Modern English contrive (verb; the modern form settled by the 16th c.). Debated / nuanced note: The precise Late Latin intermediate is debated. Some etymologists derive the Old French controver directly from Latin contropare "to compare" (with a shift from "compare" to "devise"), while others posit a Vulgar Latin *contropare built on the Romance verb tropare "to compose, invent" (cf. Provençal trobar, troubadour). The semantic pivot is agreed — "turn" (Greek trópos) → "turn of phrase/figure of speech" (Latin tropus) → "work out, devise" (contropare) — but the exact attested spelling and the direction of borrowing at the 4th stage are the points of scholarly nuance. Also note: Greek trópos is the ultimate source of English "trope," so contrive, trope, and troubadour are distant relatives.
