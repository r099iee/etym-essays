Gloated is the simple past tense (and past participle) of the verb gloat, meaning "to contemplate one's own success or another's misfortune with smug, triumphant satisfaction." It is one of English's purest words for unkind self-satisfaction: when someone gloats, they visibly or inwardly savour an advantage — usually a rival's failure or humiliation — with an unpleasant, self-congratulatory relish. The word is always negative in connotation and never describes innocent joy.

Core sense: smug satisfaction

  • Over another's misfortune: to take malicious pleasure in someone else's loss, failure, or embarrassment. This is the dominant modern sense and the closest English single-word relative of the German loan-concept Schadenfreude.
  • Over one's own success: to dwell on one's own triumph in a boastful, self-satisfied way, especially in a way calculated to rub it in.
  • The "stare" ingredient: the word's deep structure is visual — etymologically it meant "to stare or gaze" — so gloating often pictures the gloater's eyes fixed on the loser or on the prize, savouring the sight.

Grammar and usage

  • Intransitive: "he gloated" — used with the prepositions over, at, or about: "gloat over someone's downfall," "gloated about the victory."
  • Forms: gloat / gloats / gloating / gloated; a rare transitive use is jocular and non-standard.
  • Noun use: the same form "a gloat" (19th century) means an act or look of gloating: "a gloat of satisfaction spread over his face."
  • Derivatives: the adjective gloating ("a gloating expression") and the adverb gloatingly.

Semantic components and history in brief

  • Malice + triumph: the emotion combines pleasure with superiority; it requires a loser (or a beaten rival) to feel complete.
  • Lingering: gloating implies dwelling, savouring, prolonging the moment — not a brief flash of joy.
  • Visibility: it often has a public, performative side — gloaters smirk, crow, or taunt; but one can also "gloat inwardly."
  • Judged behaviour: because it feeds on others' misery, gloating is socially condemned; calling someone's behaviour "gloating" is an accusation of pettiness.

Secondary and extended senses

  • In commentary and journalism: newspapers and pundits are said to "gloat" over rivals' defeats, scandals, or collapses; the word is standard in political and sports writing.
  • In game and rivalry contexts: to "gloat" after a victory is the classic unsporting behaviour; games even feature "gloat" or taunt actions.
  • Figurative extension to institutions: "the markets gloated over the central bank's embarrassment," "the opposing camp gloated" — non-human collective subjects are common.
  • Inward or silent gloating: "she gloated silently" — the satisfaction need not be expressed aloud; the word covers the inner emotion.

Register and connotation

  • Informal-to-neutral: frequent in everyday speech and narrative; acceptable in most registers, though always morally negative.
  • Emotional intensity: stronger than "enjoyed" or "rejoiced," less theatrical than "exulted" or "crowed"; it uniquely combines inward relish with a mean streak.
  • Near-synonym boundaries: exult can be a noble triumph without malice; crow stresses the boastful noise; rejoice is general and innocent; relish lacks the targeting of a person's downfall; sneer is contempt's outward sign, not its inward savouring.

Gloated therefore records a specific, universally recognised human failing — the mean little pleasure taken in another's comeuppance — and it does so with a vivid residue of its original meaning, "to stare greedily or fixedly."
