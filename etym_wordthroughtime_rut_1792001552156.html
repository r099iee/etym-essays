- 14th–15th c. — Borrowed into Middle English as rut / ritte from Old French ruit (from Latin rugītus, roaring), designating specifically the annual roaring, fighting, and sexual frenzy of deer and other royal game in medieval hunting manuals.
- Late 16th c. — An independent homophone rut emerges in Early Modern English (probably from French route, a broken path) to denote the sunken track, channel, or furrow worn into dirt roads by repetitive cartwheel traffic.
- 17th–18th c. — Applied colloquially to human behavior, often with coarse or satirical connotations, characterizing raw animalistic lust, indiscriminate coupling, or lecherous appetite.
- 19th c. (Victorian era) — The vehicular track sense generates the enduring psychological idiom "in a rut" or "stuck in a rut," widely popularized in Victorian literature and industrial commentary to condemn mechanical daily routine, mental stagnation, and bureaucratic uncreativity.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains dual prominence: rigorously maintained in wildlife biology and game management for ungulate reproductive cycles, and functioning universally in modern colloquial psychology for personal, creative, or career slumps.
