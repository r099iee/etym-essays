- 16th c.: 'huddle' (of Dutch/Low German origin) means to heap or crowd together in a confused mass, often of troops packing tight in battle; urgent, disordered.
- 17th–18th c.: becomes the verb for people pressing together for warmth or shelter, and for doing things hastily ('huddle through a task'); bodily and hurried.
- 19th c.: in literature, the poignant emblem of the cold, the poor and the frightened gathering close — shared misery softened by mutual comfort (Lewis's rebels huddling under dripping trees).
- Contemporary: still core for tight crowding, extended to the American football huddle and 'huddling up' for private consultation; keeps its note of urgency, secrecy and shared warmth.
