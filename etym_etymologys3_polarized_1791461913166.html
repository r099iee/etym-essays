Polarized descends from a Greek geometrical word for the axis of the heavens. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *kwel-, "to turn, revolve, move around", gives Greek polos — the pivot on which a wheel turns, the axle of the celestial sphere, the point around which the sky appears to rotate. Latin took the word as polus, "pole, axis of the sky", and from Latin it passed into the European vernaculars: Old French pole, English pole, French pôle. For most of this history the word is astronomical and geographical: the poles are the ends of the axis, the fixed points in a turning world. William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600) applied the term to the ends of a magnet, and the image of two opposite poles — north and south, positive and negative — became one of the most durable diagrams in the language.

Malus and the birth of the optical sense. The decisive moment came in 1808, when the French physicist Étienne-Louis Malus, examining sunlight reflected from the windows of the Luxembourg Palace through a calcite crystal, found that the reflected light behaved as if it had been sorted into a single plane. He called the phenomenon polarisation, and the verb polariser followed in French physics around 1811; English polarize is recorded in the same year. The metaphor inside the technical term is exact: the vibrations of light, ordinarily oriented in all directions, are constrained towards one axis, as if the wave had been given poles. A polarizing filter transmits one orientation and absorbs the rest, which is why it can darken a sky, remove the glare from water, and make a stress pattern visible in a piece of transparent plastic.

Nineteenth-century expansion. The term spread rapidly through the physical sciences. Electromagnetism gave it magnetic and electric polarity; chemistry gave it the polarized bond and the polar molecule, in which electron density is pulled towards one end; crystallography and mineralogy used polarized light as a diagnostic tool; biology discovered that cells and membranes are polarized, with distinct ends and a voltage across the membrane. By the end of the century "polarized" was a general technical adjective with a stable core: a system organized around two opposite poles, or a wave constrained to one orientation.

  • Optics: light restricted to a plane, or with a definite elliptical/circular polarization state.
  • Magnetism and electricity: dipoles aligned; plugs, relays and capacitors with a fixed polarity.
  • Chemistry: uneven electron distribution, hence partial charges and directional bonds.
  • Biology: cells with apical/basal or axonal/dendritic ends; membrane potential.
  • Technology: polarizing filters, LCDs, 3D cinema, polarized sunglasses.

The figurative turn. The social sense grows out of the technical one by a simple and almost irresistible metaphor. If light can be forced into one of two orientations, then opinion can be forced towards one of two poles; if a magnet has two ends with a field between them, then a parliament can have two benches with a charged space between them. The figurative use is visible in political commentary from the later nineteenth century and becomes common in the twentieth, especially in descriptions of party politics, class conflict and the Cold War. "A polarized world", "a polarized electorate" and "polarized opinion" treat the body politic as a field whose particles have all been aligned.

Group polarization and the modern diagnosis. In the 1960s social psychologists identified group polarization: deliberation among like-minded people tends to shift the group towards a more extreme position than the average of its members' initial views. The finding gave the word a scientific footing in the study of deliberation, and it dovetails with later work on echo chambers, filter bubbles and affective sorting. Contemporary public discourse uses polarized as a diagnosis of what has gone wrong with democracy, media and even family life; the word now carries an almost medical sense of a condition to be treated.

Active and passive, cause and symptom. English distinguishes polarizing from polarized with some care. A polarizing figure is an agent: their rhetoric or presence produces the split. A polarized public is a result: the split has already happened. The same distinction appears in the technical domain, where a polarizing filter does the sorting and polarized light is what comes out. The grammar of the word therefore encodes a small theory of causation, and careful writers exploit it.

Register and cultural resonance. The technical senses are neutral, precise and largely invisible to the general public, even though the products — sunglasses, screens, cameras — are ubiquitous. The figurative sense is emotionally loaded and almost always pejorative: to call something polarized is to say that it has become unmanageable, tribal or irrational. The word also has a rhetorical use as a claim to neutrality: a commentator who describes a dispute as "polarized" implicitly places themselves above it, in the vanishing centre.

Sound, spelling and variation. RP /ˈpəʊləraɪzd/; the suffix is unstressed but the diphthong of pole is preserved. British English also writes polarised; American English prefers polarized. The participle is fully adjectival and takes comparatives ("more polarized"), which is one sign of how far it has travelled from the physics laboratory into ordinary evaluative language.
