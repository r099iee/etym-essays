Booby is a mildly comic noun for a foolish, stupid, or clumsy person — a simpleton or dunce who blunders about without wit or address. It is lighter in tone than idiot or imbecile: it suggests laughable dullness and awkwardness rather than malice or madness, and it is the kind of word used in mockery, schoolboy banter, or affectionate contempt. The same word names the large tropical seabirds of the genus Sula, so called because their clumsy gait on land and their tameness around sailors made them look stupid; and from that nautical slang come the modern compounds booby-trap and booby prize. Primary senses
  • A foolish, stupid, or clumsy person: a simpleton who blunders; the classic comic insult of mockery and schoolboy banter. “You’re a booby,” said William.
  • The seabird: any of the large gannet-like seabirds of the genus Sula (and related birds), so named because of their apparent stupidity and fearlessness of humans on their breeding islands.
  • Booby-trap: originally (17th century) a practical joke set to catch a blunderer or unwary simpleton; in modern military use, a concealed explosive device triggered by an unwary victim — the “victim” is the booby who sets it off upon himself.
  • Booby prize: a mock award given to the worst performer in a contest or game, the “prize” for the greatest fool — recorded from the late nineteenth century.
  • Booby hatch / booby house (dated slang): an insane asylum; the “house” for simpletons.
Register and tone Booby stands apart from its synonym family as the most childish and bird-like of the insults. Fool is broad and old; simpleton stresses mild, innocent lack of intelligence; dunce names one slow to learn, with scholastic origins reaching back to the followers of Duns Scotus; blockhead imagines the head as a solid, impenetrable block; numskull is a similar folk metaphor; nincompoop is a fanciful, jocular coinage. Booby is the lightest and most playful of the set — the word of the nursery, the schoolyard, and the music-hall rather than of the courtroom or the asylum, which is why it can be used with mock-affection as well as contempt. Word-use note (The Hobbit) In The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the word is hurled in the middle of the trolls’ nightly bickering: “‘Who’s arguing but you, I’d like to know.’ ‘You’re a booby,’ said William.” The exchange occurs while Gandalf, hidden in the dark, is ventriloquising first one troll’s voice and then another’s, setting them against one another. William’s use of booby at once reveals the low register of troll conversation — a blunt, childish, rustic term of abuse — and heightens the comedy of the scene, since the reader can see that the real dupe is not the troll being insulted but the whole band, fools unknowingly held in the grip of a wizard’s trick until the sun rises and turns them to stone. Origin in brief Booby is not an Indo-European inheritance. It comes from the Spanish and Portuguese bobo, “foolish, stupid, naive,” itself a reduplicated baby-talk base (the stammering ba-ba of the tongue-tied fool), related to Latin balbus, “stammering.” Spanish mariners applied pájaro bobo, “the foolish bird,” to the tame, clumsy seabirds of the genus Sula, and English sailors of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries brought the word home in both its senses: the bird and the simpleton. The word has kept one foot in the seabird’s nest and one in the schoolroom ever since.
