The name Theodoric is a compound of two Indo-European roots that travelled through two completely different routes into the Germanic world. The first element, *þeudō-, 'people', descends directly from the PIE root *tewtéh₂-, 'the people as a community', a root with a rich distribution: in Celtic it gave Old Irish túath, 'the people, the tribe', the basic unit of early Irish society; in Balto-Slavic it gave Lithuanian tauta, 'people, nation'; and in Italic it is probably the source of Latin tōtus, 'all, whole', whence our total. In Germanic it became *þeudō, 'people, nation', and its descendants are legion: Gothic þiuda, Old English þēod (still alive in the archaic English thede), Old Norse þjóð. Most remarkably, the same root lies beneath the adjective þiudisk, 'of the people, popular', which Latin writers rendered theodiscus to describe the Germanic vernacular as opposed to Latin — and from theodiscus comes modern German deutsch, 'German', and the Dutch word dietsch, 'Dutch'. The word for the German nation is etymologically 'the people's speech', descended from the very same root that names the 'ruler of the people'. The second element, *-rīks, 'ruler', has a different and more dramatic history, for it is a loanword. The PIE root *h₃rēǵ-, 'to rule, set straight', is native to Indo-Iranian (Sanskrit rājan, 'king', whence our raja and maharaja), to Italic (Latin rex and regere, whence royal, rule, regime), and to Celtic (Gaulish rīx, Old Irish rí, 'king'). But the Germanic peoples, who had no inherited word of their own for a territorial king, borrowed the Celtic *rīg- as Proto-Germanic *rīks — the very name-element that runs through the royal names of the migration age: Alaric, Theodoric, Ermanaric. In Gothic it appears as reiks, and Wulfila used it in his Bible translation for 'ruler'. The loanword then went on to produce, through the adjective *rīkijaz 'powerful, mighty', our word rich — the ruler being the powerful one — and, through Old High German rīhhi, the German Reich. A single borrowed word for 'king' thus underlies Theodoric, rich, and the German Reich. Joining *þeudō 'people' to *rīks 'ruler' gives Gothic *Þiudareiks, 'ruler of the people', and the man who made the name famous is Theodoric the Great, king of the Ostrogoths (reigned 493–526). Raised as a hostage in Constantinople and educated in Roman letters, Theodoric invaded Italy, defeated Odoacer in 493, and ruled from Ravenna for three decades, preserving Roman law and administration through his minister Cassiodorus and maintaining a tolerant balance between his Arian Goths and the Catholic Romans. His age was the last flowering of Roman culture in the West, and his fame was so great that the medieval German Nibelungenlied transformed him into Dietrich von Bern, the legendary dragon-slayer and exile-hero. The name entered the historical record through Procopius, Jordanes, and Cassiodorus as Theodericus/Theodoricus (Greek Theodērichos), and passed into English — where it appears in Macaulay and the historians of late antiquity — alongside its continental reflexes Dietrich, Thierry, Diederik, and Teodorico. In a single name, Theodoric thus gathers the Indo-European notion of the people, a Celtic loanword for kingship, the origin of the word 'German', the etymology of 'rich' and 'Reich', and the biography of the last Roman emperor of the West in all but name. KEY STAGES IN ONE LINE
  • Sound shift: PIE *tewtéh₂- -> Germanic *þeudō shows the regular lowering and loss of the PIE laryngeal; the Celtic loan *rīg- -> *rīks shows the Germanic shift of *g to *k under the influence of following consonants (Grimm's Law effects on loanwords).
  • Semantic drift: 'people' (*þeudō) remained remarkably stable as 'people/nation' across Gothic, Old English, Old Norse; '-ruler' (*-rīks) shifted from Celtic 'king' to Germanic generic 'ruler, mighty one', and in the adjective *rīkijaz 'powerful' produced English rich and German Reich.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the name prospered because the migration-age Goths needed dynastic titles; its survival in Dietrich, Thierry, Diederik reflects the prestige of the heroic legend and of medieval German epic.
  • Literary attestations: Cassiodorus' Variae and Jordanes' Getica (6th c.) fix the Latinized form; the Nibelungenlied (c. 1200) and Þiðreks saga (13th c.) fix the legendary Dietrich; Gibbon and Macaulay (18th-19th c.) fix the modern historical usage.
