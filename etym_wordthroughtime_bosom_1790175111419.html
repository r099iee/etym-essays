- Old English period, ca. 700-1000 - bōsm is a plain bodily word for the chest or breast, and already capable of the second, larger life of the word: the innermost, sheltered, hollow part, as in the Old English sense of a valley or a sheltered place between hills
- 10th-13th c. - the biblical and devotional senses come in, giving the heart, the inner life, the place of secret thoughts, and with them the phrase the bosom of the earth for a deep sheltered hollow or valley
- 14th-15th c. - the metaphor goes social and political: the bosom of the family, of the Church, of the kingdom, meaning the protected inner part; and the attributive adjective appears in the phrase bosom friend, a friend so close that the expression has never been displaced
- 16th-17th c. - theology takes the word over completely: the bosom of God, of the Father, of Abraham, the divine embrace, becomes a fixed part of English religious language; in the same century in the bosom of settles into its common meaning of ‘inside the shelter of’, and the verb to bosom, to embrace and to cherish, is still in use
- 17th-18th c. - the word becomes technical as well as poetic: a bosom is the concealed meeting place of a secret society, the concave inner face of a shield, the chest part of a shirt, and a pocket in it
- 18th-19th c. - Romantic and Georgian poets keep the bosom of the earth and the bosom of nature alive, so the word acquires a specifically outdoor, sheltered, verdant colour; at the same time breast and chest win the ordinary literal senses and bosom starts to sound grand
- 20th c. to present - the word settles into a small set of fixed phrases, bosom friend, the bosom of the family, in one’s bosom, the bosom of God, bosom sins, and everywhere else it reads as arch, poetic or faintly old-fashioned, a mark of deliberate elevation rather than the ordinary word for anything
