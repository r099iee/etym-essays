- Old English (8th–11th c.) - Attested as *dysiġ* / *dysi*, meaning purely 'foolish, stupid, senseless, or ignorant' (cognate with Low German *dösig* and Dutch *duizelig*).
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) - Developed into *disi* / *dysy*; while retaining the meaning of foolish or rash, it began shifting crucially toward physical light-headedness, vertigo, and spatial confusion after trauma, fever, or spinning.
- 16th–17th c. - The physical sense of rotational disequilibrium became the dominant standard meaning in Early Modern English, while the older intellectual sense of foolishness was preserved in poetic and colloquial insults.
- 18th–19th c. - Broadened metaphorically to describe breathtaking heights, immense speeds, and overwhelming cultural or economic changes ('dizzying heights', 'a dizzying pace').
- 20th–21st c. - Pervasively used in medicine (vestibular symptoms, vertigo), colloquial characterization (a silly or frivolous person), and everyday descriptions of rapid sensory overload.
