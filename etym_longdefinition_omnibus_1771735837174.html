In the Modern Library sentence, “omnibus” is a noun of the book trade: a single volume that gathers together works originally published separately. The context explains that “the books contained in this omnibus were each published separately by The Modern Library ... in 1995,” so the word functions as a collector’s term — one binding and one set of pages containing several distinct titles that the reader would otherwise have had to acquire as individual volumes. The nuance is all-inclusiveness: an omnibus implies comprehensiveness, a complete haul bundled into a single vehicle of a book, and for a work on the scale of Gibbon’s Decline and Fall it conveys both scholarly completeness and great convenience — the whole history under one roof, or one cover. Because the Latin word literally means “for all,” the term also carries a faint aura of democratic inclusiveness and of the old horse-drawn public omnibus: something that carries everyone and everything at once. The tone in this sentence is bibliographic and legal, part of the fine print that frames the Modern Library edition, and the word is used with quiet technical precision. As a dictionary word, “omnibus” is a noun with two main families of meaning joined by one idea — a vehicle or gathering that carries everything or everyone. In the book-trade sense, an omnibus (or omnibus edition) is a volume containing several works — novels, stories, plays, reports — previously published separately. In the transport sense, an omnibus is a public passenger vehicle — historically the horse-drawn carriage of the 1820s, today the motor bus; “bus” is in fact the clipped, everyday descendant of “omnibus.” By extension the word is used of parliamentary bills that gather many subjects into one act (an omnibus bill), of broadcasts that combine several episodes or programmes (an omnibus edition), and of any composite, all-inclusive collection. The adjective use (“an omnibus volume,” “an omnibus bill”) is frequent, and the Latin plural “omnibuses” is the standard English plural, with the clipped “buses” for the vehicle. Polysemy — the full range of senses:
  • The book-trade sense (core of this card): a volume containing several works previously published separately — “an omnibus of detective novels,” “the books contained in this omnibus.”
  • The transport sense: a public passenger vehicle, originally the horse-drawn omnibus of the 1820s, later the motor bus — the sense that gave us the clipped word “bus.”
  • The legislative sense: an omnibus bill — a single act of parliament or congress covering many different subjects, “all things” gathered into one law.
  • The broadcasting sense: an omnibus edition — a single programme compiling episodes or segments previously aired separately, a Sunday omnibus of a weekday serial.
  • The general composite sense: any collection or gathering of many items into one whole — “an omnibus of essays,” “an omnibus anthology.”
  • The historical and etymological sense: Latin omnibus, “for all, to all” — the dative/ablative plural of omnis, “all” — preserved in the old carriage motto and in the philosophical idea of something serving everyone.
The boundary words sharpen the profile:
  • “Anthology”: Similar because both gather works in a book; differs because an anthology collects short pieces — poems, essays, excerpts — typically by many hands and chosen thematically, whereas an omnibus reprints whole works in full.
  • “Compendium”: Similar because both are one-volume gatherings; differs because a compendium is a concise summary or abridgment of a subject — it condenses — whereas an omnibus reprints the works complete.
  • “Collection”: Similar because both gather works; differs because collection is the broadest term for any gathering, often of a single author, and does not imply that separate editions are fused into one binding.
  • “Boxed set”: Similar because both assemble previously separate items; differs because a boxed set keeps the works as distinct volumes in a box, whereas an omnibus fuses them into a single volume.
  • “Bus”: Similar because both name public passenger vehicles; differs because bus is the clipped, everyday descendant of omnibus, now the standard word for the motor vehicle, while omnibus is the older, fuller, more formal term.
  • “Complete works”: Similar because both offer the full span of an author; differs because complete works implies everything by one hand, while an omnibus may gather selected works by one or several authors.
  • “Compilation”: Similar because both bring separate materials together; differs because a compilation emphasizes the act of assembling varied material, often abridged or excerpted, while an omnibus reprints whole works complete.
  • “Single volume”: Similar because both describe one binding; differs because “single volume” is a neutral physical description, while omnibus adds the sense of comprehensiveness and prior separate publication.
The word is transparently Latin: omnibus is the dative/ablative plural of omnis, “all, every” — literally “for all, to all” — and the whole history of the word is the history of that prepositional phrase becoming a noun and then a family of nouns. From the carriage that carried everyone (“a carriage for all”) to the book that contains everything (an omnibus volume), the Latin grammar of “for all” survives in every modern sense, and the sentence in this card is using one of the youngest links in that chain — the book-trade omnibus that bundles separately published titles into one all-inclusive volume.
