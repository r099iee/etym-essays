Queer is a word with two radically different histories: a long, quiet Germanic career meaning "transverse" and "odd," and a short, explosive modern career as a term of identity and social theory. In The Hobbit it belongs entirely to the first history, and understanding that older career is essential to reading the dwarves' dialogue correctly. 1. The Germanic root: transverse, crosswise, contrary. The word's deepest recoverable layer is a Germanic idea of the oblique — the transverse, the crosswise, that which cuts across the straight line of expectation. The Proto-Germanic adjective *þwerhaz "transverse, crosswise, contrary, perverse" is reconstructed from its descendants, most notably the German and Low German branches. Old High German had twerah / twerh "crosswise, oblique, contrary," and Middle High German continued the form as twer / twër. The modern German form quer "transverse, diagonal, crosswise" shows a genuine sound change from the Middle High German stage: the initial tw- cluster was simplified, with the t lost and the w vocalized to u, so that twer became quer. A parallel Low German form quer / dwe(e)r carried the same "oblique, crosswise" meaning.
  • Sound shift: Middle High German tw- → modern German qu- (the t dropped and the w became a u-glide): twer → quer.
  • Core meaning: transverse, diagonal, crosswise — that which is not straight-on — later extended to "contrary, perverse."
  • Proposed deeper link: a connection to the Indo-European root *terkʷ- "turn, twist" is often proposed ("twisted" → "crosswise"), but it is debated and not universally accepted.
2. Entry into English: 1500. English borrowed queer around 1500 from the German or Low German area, where it meant oblique, crosswise, and by extension off-center. In English the word immediately generalized from geometry to psychology: a "queer" thing or person is one that departs from the expected axis of behavior or appearance. From the start the adjective meant strange, odd, peculiar, eccentric, and also — importantly — slightly suspect or unwell. "To feel queer" preserves the unease, the sudden oddness of the body, to this day. 3. Four centuries of quiet growth: 1500–1900. For roughly four hundred years queer developed in English along the lines of its "odd and slightly off" core, accumulating subsidiary senses:
  • Strange, odd, eccentric: the dominant sense — things off the expected center.
  • Unwell, faint: "I feel queer" — bodily oddness, near dizziness.
  • Questionable, suspect: "queer business," "queer goings-on" — odd in a way that invites distrust.
  • Counterfeit (cant): "queer money" meant forged notes in nineteenth-century slang, extending "not genuine" into the criminal sphere.
  • To spoil, to put wrong: the verb "to queer" — most memorably in the idiom "queer the pitch" (spoil someone's plans), from the theatrical slang of the nineteenth century.
This is the register Tolkien inherits. In 1930s narrative English, a "queer fit" is transparently a strange spell, a funny turn, a passing deviation from the expected — nothing more sinister than oddness. 4. The twentieth century: from slur to reclaimed identity term. In the early twentieth century, queer was taken up as slang for homosexual men and became, for decades, a slur — the "off-center" root was turned into a label for those whose identity was socially coded as deviating from the norm. From the late twentieth century onward, the word was reclaimed by the LGBTQ+ community as a proud umbrella term, and in academia it generated "queer theory," a critical framework that questions fixed categories of identity and desire. Today queer thus coexists as three things at once: the old everyday "odd," a reclaimed identity label, and, in older or hostile mouths, still a slur. This modern history is English-internal and social — it is not a stage of the etymological chain from *þwerhaz — but it is essential context for reading the word safely across registers. 5. On this card. In the dwarves' praise, "Gets funny queer fits, but he is one of the best," queer is the plain old adjective of eccentricity. The speaker files Bilbo's odd spells under a homely word for "peculiar" and moves on to loyalty and dragon-fierceness. The card must preserve that period sense — strange, peculiar, off the expected axis — exactly as the dialogue uses it, without importing the later identity history into the hobbit's character sketch.
