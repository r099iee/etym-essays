Single linear chain from the earliest reconstructable root to SPOUT: 1. Proto-Indo-European *sp(y)eu- (also *spew-), "to spit, spew, eject fluid from the mouth," an expressive, imitative root whose variants (*spu-, *spl-, *spr-) form a whole family of fluid-ejection words: Latin spuō "I spit" (English sputum), Greek ptúō "I spit," and the Germanic family spit, spew, sputter, spurt, spout. The root is onomatopoeic in origin, modelling the sound of a forced expulsion of liquid. 2. Proto-Germanic *sputōną / *spūtaną, "to spit, spout, spurt" (an extension of the spit/spew root with a -t- suffix typical of expressive verbs), attested indirectly through its descendants: Middle Dutch sputen, spouten, spuiten, "to spout, spurt, squirt," Low German sputen, spüten, Frisian spuitsen. 3. Middle English spouten, "to issue forth in a stream, to gush, to pour from a pipe" (verb, attested c. 1300), probably borrowed from Middle Dutch spouten (or a related Low German or Frisian form) and reinforced by the native English spit/spew family, with which it was cognate or near-cognate. 4. Middle English spoute, spowte, "a tube or lip for pouring; the mouth of a vessel; a jet of liquid" (noun, attested 14th c.), a back-formation from the verb. 5. Modern English SPOUT (noun and verb), with the whale's-blow sense (17th c.), the figurative speech sense ("to spout" = to declaim, 18th c.), and the compounds waterspout (16th c.) and downspout (19th c.). Note on the debated stage: the immediate source of English spout is disputed among the dialectal Germanic forms — OED assigns it to Middle Dutch spouten/spuiten or Low German sputen, with reinforcement from the native spit/spew set — but the deeper connection of all these to the expressive PIE spit/spew root is generally accepted. Because the root is imitative, its reconstruction is approximate by nature: the exact PIE shape (*sp(y)eu-, *spew-, *spu-) varies across the handbooks, and the word may in fact have been re-formed at every level by the universal human gesture of spitting. None of this affects the attested English record from c. 1300 onward.
