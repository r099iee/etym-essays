*passāre
"to step, stride, pace, go through"

"a passage, way through, strait, opening"
↓
French (neologism by Voltaire; im- + passe) (1740)
impasse
"a blind alley, cul-de-sac, dead-end street"
↓
Modern English (c. 1851; borrowed from French)
impasse
"a deadlock, stalemate, unresolvable situation"

Note on historical coinage: The word impasse holds the rare distinction of being a deliberate, documented lexical invention by the French philosopher and author François-Marie Arouet, known to history as Voltaire. In 1740, Voltaire specifically crafted impasse (prefixing the negative Latinate morpheme im-, "not, un-", onto the established French noun passe, "passage") in order to provide the French language with a polite, sophisticated alternative to the traditional street term cul-de-sac (which literally translates as "bottom/arse of the bag"), which Voltaire and his contemporary Enlightenment literati found excessively crude and indecorous for refined Parisian cartography and literature.
