Beggarly is an adjective — and, more rarely, an adverb — meaning like a beggar, or fit for a beggar: miserably poor, shabbily dressed, sordidly mean, contemptibly scanty. It describes both the look of poverty (beggarly clothes, a beggarly hovel) and the spirit of it (a beggarly excuse, beggarly wages, a beggarly spirit), and in both uses it judges: the word never merely reports want but always sneers at it or laments it.

In the source sentence the word is one half of a stable-yard double insult: ‘How now, thou beggarly cob!’ The speaker pairs it with cob — a squat, sturdy, low-bred horse — so that the abuse lands twice: low-born and low-kept, a scrubby horse in scrubby condition, deserving neither oats nor respect. As with starveling hack two lines earlier, the fable's beasts insult each other with the vocabulary of the parish poor-law, and the comedy lies in ragged animals standing on their ragged dignity.

Core meaning in detail: Morphologically beggar + -ly, the adjective-forming suffix meaning ‘having the qualities of’ (as in princely, friendly, cowardly): literally ‘beggar-like’. The force of the -ly is to convert a social station into a quality, so that beggarly can attach to anything — clothes, cottages, portions, excuses, performances — that shows the meanness and inadequacy associated with beggary. As an adverb (‘a beggarly clad traveller’) it is now archaic, the -ly doubling awkwardly on an already adjectival base; modern use is overwhelmingly adjectival.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Resembling a beggar in appearance — ragged, shabby, squalid (primary sense, 15th c. onward): ‘a beggarly fellow in a patched coat’. The bridge is direct likeness: dressed as the destitute dress, whether from real poverty or neglect. This is the fable's sense — the cob looks beggarly.
  • Miserably inadequate in amount — scanty, paltry, contemptible (extended sense): ‘a beggarly portion’, ‘beggarly wages’, ‘a beggarly attendance’. The bridge runs from the beggar's dish to any dish: what a beggar receives — scraps, coppers, dregs — becomes the measure of all insufficiency, and the word carries the recipient's humiliation into every ledger.
  • Mean-spirited, ignoble, sordid in character (moral sense): ‘a beggarly spirit’, ‘beggarly pride’, ‘beggarly excuses’. The bridge converts outward poverty into inward poverty: just as the beggar is low in the street, so the beggarly soul is low in honour — stingy, servile, grovelling, or petty. Shakespeare loves this moral sense: beggarly fears, beggarly thanks.
  • Miserly, stingy — giving like a beggar though rich (figurative): ‘a beggarly host’, ‘beggarly hospitality’. The bridge is ironic reversal: the rich man who entertains with beggarly scraps is judged by the standard of the station he apes — poverty of generosity, not of purse.
  • Base-born, low, vulgar (archaic social sense): ‘beggarly origins’. The bridge is the old equation of worth with birth: to call stock beggarly was to deny it gentility. Democracy has mostly retired this use, but the fable's beggarly cob still faintly carries it — a horse of no breeding.

Register and connotation: beggarly is frank, old-fashioned moral English — Shakespeare, the Authorised Version's margins, Dickens, and the pulpit — and it always takes sides: with the giver against the miser, with dignity against squalor, or cruelly with the comfortable against the ragged. Modern poverty discourse avoids it as stigmatising (preferring deprived, under-resourced), which is exactly why literature keeps it: no modern euphemism sneers, pities, and condemns in a single breath the way beggarly does. As stable-yard abuse it is perfect — short, alliterative with its noun, and instantly picturable.
