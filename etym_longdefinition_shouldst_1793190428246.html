Shouldst is the archaic second-person singular of should (and historically of shall): the verb form that pairs with the pronoun thou, so that thou shouldst means you should, you ought to, or you would have to.

In the source fragment — ‘Why ______ thou not? — the word wears its reproachful biblical dress: a question demanding why thou didst not do what thou oughtest to have done, the grammar of the King James Bible, of Shakespeare’s scolds, and of prayers. The inverted order, verb before subject, doubles the archaism, making the line unmistakably early modern.

Core meaning in detail: Shouldst carries every modal shade of should fitted to thou: obligation (thou shouldst honour thy father), expectation (thou shouldst be weary by now), condition (if thou shouldst fall), polite counsel (thou shouldst rest), and the optative wish (thou shouldst have seen it). The -st ending is the old second-person singular inflection, twin to -est, surviving in this frozen form alongside hadst, wouldst, couldst, and didst. Modern English has shed both thou and its endings, so shouldst now lives only in scripture, liturgy, historical fiction, and deliberate archaism.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • You ought to, you are bound to (deontic, primary): thou shouldst keep thy promise — moral duty laid on the single hearer. The bridge runs from Old English sceal, I owe, to owed duty.
  • You may expect to, you are likely to (epistemic): thou shouldst find him at home — prediction rather than command. The bridge is the softening of obligation into likelihood, as with modern should.
  • If you were to, were you to (conditional): if thou shouldst doubt my word — the tentative supposition. The bridge is the modal’s power to stage unreal or uncertain scenes.
  • Polite advice or recommendation (advisory): thou shouldst see a physician — counsel, not compulsion. The bridge carries ought toward suggestion.
  • Emphatic reproach in questions (rhetorical, the source sense): why shouldst thou fear? — the question that answers itself. The bridge is the prophet’s and parent’s use of ought-questions to shame and stir.
  • Past-tense reporter — said thou shouldst (sequence of tenses): he told me thou shouldst come — shouldst as the past of shalt in reported speech. The bridge is grammar’s backshift, fossilised in old narrative.

Register and connotation: shouldst is solemn, scriptural, and theatrical — pulpits, wedding vows in period dress, fantasy councils, and lovers’ quarrels in doublet and hose. Used straight, it commands reverence; used lightly, it winks. No one writes thou shouldst by accident: the form announces that the speaker has stepped out of the everyday into law, liturgy, or play, and the hearer understands that the ought has been dressed for ceremony.
