Uppish is an informal, chiefly British adjective meaning arrogantly self-assertive, conceitedly superior, or snobbishly putting on airs; acting as if above others or above one's proper station. To be uppish is to display an inflated sense of importance in manner, tone or demands: a pert reply, a condescending look, refusal to be familiar. The source sentence — "Grovel, and the best of them get uppish" — states the classic complaint: excessive deference breeds arrogance in the recipient.

The nuance is specifically upward presumption rather than general pride. An arrogant duke may be arrogant by nature; a clerk who becomes uppish has risen — or imagines he has — and flaunts it. The word thus carries a faintly classed, hierarchical Past: servants, juniors, parvenus getting above themselves. In modern use the class sting has softened to general haughtiness, but the image of rising remains.

  • Arrogant / conceited (core modern): Having or showing an exaggerated sense of one's importance. An uppish tone, uppish manners, don't be so uppish. Implies condescension, dismissiveness, expectation of special treatment.
  • Snobbish / pretentious: Affecting superiority in taste or status. An uppish restaurant, uppish accent. Putting on airs, looking down on what is common. Near-synonym of stuck-up, snooty.
  • Insubordinate / pert (historical): Of inferiors: saucy, cheeky, forgetting one's place. The maids are getting uppish. 18th-19th century masters' complaint. Now dated but still heard ironically.
  • Peevishly self-assertive: In some contexts uppish shades into touchy, quick to take offence from pride. He turned uppish when corrected. Pride as defensiveness.

Polysemy is narrow because the metaphor is tight: UP is superior. From spatial up, English derives social upstart (one who has started up), upwardly mobile, high and mighty, stuck-up. Uppish with dismissive -ish — as in childish, snobbish — packages the judgment as character flaw rather than achievement. To be uppish is not truly to be high, only -ish, like high.

Domain notes: in domestic service history, uppish was a disciplinary word; in schools, an uppish boy is cheeky to masters; in workplaces, an uppish junior oversteps; in hospitality reviews, uppish staff or venues are cold and grandiose. American English largely prefers uppity with heavier, racially loaded history; British uppish is lighter, more comic, Wodehousian. Both are disapproving and informal, never neutral description or praise.

Grammatically regular and gradable: uppish, more uppish, most uppish, a bit uppish, very uppish. Typically predicative (to get uppish, turn uppish, be uppish) or attributive (uppish manner). Collocates with get, turn, sound, look, little. Register is conversational, faintly old-fashioned, often humorous. To use it today signals awareness of hierarchy while mocking it.
