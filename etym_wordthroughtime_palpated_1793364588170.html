- Classical Antiquity — Gentle stroking and coaxing: In ancient Rome, Latin palpāre describes soothing, patting, or stroking someone softly, frequently used metaphorically for flattering or cajoling a patron with smooth speech.
- 17th–18th c. — Early medical Latin revival: Continental anatomists and surgeons writing in Neo-Latin revive palpatio to distinguish living diagnostic examination from post-mortem dissection.
- Early 19th c. — The Paris Clinical Revolution: French physicians formalize la palpation as one of the four cardinal pillars of physical bedside diagnosis, cementing the hand as a scientific instrument.
- Mid-19th c. — English medical borrowing: The verb palpate and past form palpated are adopted into English medical textbooks (c. 1835–1850) as the definitive clinical term for manual examination of internal organs.
- Late 19th–20th c. — Zoological and optical expansion: Naturalists apply palpated to the exploratory actions of insect palps, while historians of science use it metaphorically to describe ancient extramission theories where visual rays physically touched objects.
- Today — Universal clinical descriptor: Palpated is ubiquitous in hospital charts and physical diagnosis, while serving as a refined metaphor in literary and philosophical writing for careful, sensitive investigation.
