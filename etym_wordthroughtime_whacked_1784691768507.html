- 18th-century coinage - whack is an echoic word coined to imitate the crisp crack of a solid blow; a whack is a sharp, ringing strike with a stick, branch or flat object, and to whack is to deliver it
- 19th-century extended senses - the noun spreads from blows to shares and attempts: 'a fair whack' means a fair portion, and to 'have a whack' at something is to make an attempt; the word keeps a breezy, colloquial energy
- Early 20th century idiomatic flowering - whacked comes to mean utterly exhausted (chiefly British, Australian and New Zealand), while American slang gives whacked out the sense of crazy or drug-addled, and out of whack means out of order; the word now spans blows, tiredness, madness and malfunction
- Contemporary sense - the sharp blow remains the core, but the informal family — I'm whacked, out of whack, have a whack — makes whack a small constellation of brisk, homely idioms
