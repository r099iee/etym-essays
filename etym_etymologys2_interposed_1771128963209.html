INTERPOSED is a compound participle built from two historical elements, each with its own linear chain. CHAIN 1 — the element inter- 'between, among': 1. Proto-Indo-European *h₁én 'in' (c. 4000–3500 BCE), extended by the contrastive suffix *-ter to *h₁énter 'inner, between, among'. 2. Proto-Italic *enter 'between, among'. 3. Latin inter 'between, among' (attested from c. 200 BCE, Plautus and Terence). 4. Latin interpōnere 'to place between' (inter- + pōnere), attested from the 1st c. BCE (Cicero), already used for inserting clauses, conditions, and objections. 5. Latin interpositus, past participle of interpōnere, 'placed between' (1st c. BCE onward). 6. Old French interposer, entreposer 'to place between' (13th–15th c.), modelled on Latin interponere but rebuilt on French poser 'to place'. 7. English interpose 'to place between, to insert, to step between' (attested from the 1590s, incl. Shakespeare's interpos'd); the past-tense and participle form INTERPOSED (modern English). CHAIN 2 — the element -pos(e), from Latin pōnere 'to place': 1. Proto-Indo-European *apo- 'away, off' (c. 4000–3500 BCE). The origin of pōnere is DEBATED: the standard account builds it from *po-sinō, i.e. the prefix *po- (from *apo-) plus sinō 'to let, allow, set'; a rival account derives it from *apo-dʰeh₁- 'to put away', from the root *dʰeh₁- 'to put, place, do'. Both routes agree on the core sense 'to set or put down'. 2. Latin pōnere 'to put, place, set down' (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; the irregular forms posuī, positum reflect the original compound). 3. Late Latin pausāre 'to pause, rest, set down', conflated in popular Latin with pōnere; Old French poser 'to put, place' (10th–12th c.) descends from this conflated form. 4. French interposer 'to place between' = inter- + poser (13th–15th c.), the direct source of English interpose, whose participle is INTERPOSED (modern English).
