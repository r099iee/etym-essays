• connoisseurs: Similar because both denote people of deep, refined appreciation of quality; differs because a connoisseur is defined by expert knowledge and discriminating judgment in a specific domain (wine, painting, antiques), while an aesthete is defined by the sensory experience of beauty itself.
  • dilettantes: Similar because both engage with art and beauty; differs because a dilettante dabbles superficially and without commitment, while an aesthete is seriously, often obsessively, devoted to beauty.
  • epicures: Similar because both pursue refined pleasure; differs because an epicure specialises in the pleasures of food and drink and physical comfort, whereas an aesthete pursues the beauty of art and the senses more broadly.
  • sybarites: Similar because both live for luxurious sensory gratification; differs because a sybarite is a voluptuary devoted to luxury and pleasure in general, carrying a strong moral judgment of hedonism, while an aesthete is (in principle) devoted to beauty rather than comfort.
  • hedonists: Similar because both make pleasure a life principle; differs because hedonist names anyone whose guiding value is pleasure of any kind, while an aesthete's pleasure is specifically the refined pleasure of beauty, not appetite or luxury.
  • art lovers: Similar because both love art; differs because 'art lover' is the plain, unpretentious everyday phrase for enjoying art, while aesthete implies an identity, a doctrine, and often a hint of affectation.
  • tastemakers: Similar because both are associated with refined taste; differs because a tastemaker's significance lies in setting trends and influencing others, whereas an aesthete is defined by his own devotion to beauty, not by social influence.
  • cognoscenti: Similar because both denote people of cultivated discernment; differs because cognoscenti stresses membership in an elite of those 'in the know' about a field, while aesthetes stress the emotional-sensory appreciation of beauty.
  • esthetes (variant spelling): Similar because it is the very same word; differs only in spelling — 'esthetes' reflects the unaspirated Greek e- in simplified American spelling, while 'aesthetes' preserves the classical ae- digraph.
