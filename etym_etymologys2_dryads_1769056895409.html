Root core: PIE *dóru / *dréw- / *drú- "tree, wood; firm, solid, true" (c. 4500–2500 BCE) — one of the best-attested roots of the Indo-European family, spread over every major branch; the oak is its default referent in the Mediterranean world. Linear chain to DRYADS: 1. PIE *dóru (oblique *dréw-, *drú-) "tree, wood; firm, solid, true" (c. 4500–2500 BCE). The root's moral branch (firm, true) coexists with its material branch (tree, wood) because wood was the archetypal firm, enduring substance. 2. Greek δρῦς drŷs "oak; (older) tree" (< *dru-s) (Homeric age, c. 8th century BCE and earlier) — for a Greek the oak was the archetypal tree, the sacred wood of Zeus whose rustling leaves spoke oracles at Dodona. 3. Greek δρυάς dryás "tree-nymph, oak-nymph" (plural δρυάδες dryádes), "she of the oak," formed from δρῦς with the feminine noun suffix -άς/-άδ- (classical Attic Greek, attested from the 5th century BCE). 4. Latin dryas, genitive dryadis "a tree-nymph" (borrowed from Greek, 1st century BCE–CE). 5. French dryade (16th century), borrowed from Latin. 6. English dryad (16th century, via French/Latin), plural Dryads — the exact form on this card. Debated / nuanced notes: - The root *dóru is exceptionally well attested (Sanskrit dāru "wood," Old Church Slavonic drěvo "tree," Germanic *trewą "tree" — ancestor of English tree — Latin dūrus "hard" from *dru-ró- "firm as wood," and Greek déndron "tree" from a secondary -n- stem *dréw-n-), so this chain is among the most secure in the field; the only debated detail is the precise status of the -άς suffix in the formation of dryás, which is unproblematic feminine nominal derivation. - Greek δρῦς shifted from "tree" in general to "oak" in particular by classical times; the "oak" sense is the one active in dryás. The English word always enters through the classical (oak-nymph) meaning, so there is no ambiguity in the loan. - The Celtic druid (Gaulish druidēs, "oak-knowers," dru- "oak" + wid- "to know") is a culturally related word built on the same *dóru family but is NOT part of the English chain to dryad.
