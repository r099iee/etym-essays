Scylla — proper noun: in Greek and Roman myth, a sea-monster with six long necks and six heads, each with three rows of sharp teeth, who barked like a young dog and dwelt in a cliff-cave on the Italian (later considered the Sicilian) side of the Strait of Messina, directly opposite the whirlpool Charybdis. She snatched and devoured sailors from passing ships, including six of Odysseus's men in Odyssey Book 12. The name denotes both the monster and, metonymically, the rock or strait named for her; the proverb "between Scylla and Charybdis" means caught between two equal and unavoidable dangers. 1. Primary sense: the Homeric monster.
  • In Homer, Scylla is a goddess-like horror dwelling in a cave high in a smooth rock face, visible only when the fog lifts; with her six heads she seizes six of Odysseus's comrades at one stroke as they pass, rending them at the ship's side — the origin of her traditional name "the Renderer."
  • In later mythography (e.g., Ovid, Metamorphoses), a separate story makes Scylla a beautiful nymph whom Circe, in jealousy, transforms into the monster of the strait — a rationalizing and romanticizing development of the Homeric figure.
  • In the source sentence Gibbon refers to "the Straits of Scylla," using the monster's name for the narrow waterway itself, the classic instance of a perilous strait beside the Laestrygonian cannibals' city.
2. Secondary and extended senses (polysemy).
  • The proverb "between Scylla and Charybdis": the fixed English (and European) idiom for a dilemma with no safe choice — avoiding one danger plunges you into the other. Used in politics, business, ethics, and everyday speech; equivalent to "between a rock and a hard place" or "on the horns of a dilemma."
  • Metonymy for the strait/rock: in geography and navigation, "Scylla" names the rock and the dangerous passage of Messina; the word is a byword for maritime hazard.
  • Modern popular culture: the image of Scylla as a multi-headed, dog-barking predator recurs in fantasy literature, games, and film; the name is also a genus in zoology (e.g., Scylla, a genus of mangrove crabs), preserving the snapping, clawing monster image.
  • Rhetorical figure: "to choose between Scylla and Charybdis" is a stock figure of speech for forced choice between two evils, taught in rhetoric alongside Charybdis.
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges. The Greek name Skýlla (Σκύλλα) was anciently connected with the verb skýllō "to tear, rend, flay, harry," making Scylla literally "the Renderer" — the one who tears sailors apart — which matches her behaviour in the poem and gave ancient readers a satisfying aetiology. Modern scholars, however, are divided: many (e.g., Beekes) suspect a Pre-Greek or otherwise unanalyzable substrate origin, since skýllō itself has no secure Indo-European etymology. Whatever the true etymology, the semantic bridge from monster to idiom is clear: because she sat opposite the whirlpool, any mariner who fled Charybdis fell into Scylla's jaws (and vice versa), so the pair became the fixed emblem of an impossible choice. The modern senses (proverb, metonymic rock, cultural predator) all descend from that one spatial fact. 4. Language, spelling, and pronunciation.
  • Greek Skýlla (Σκύλλα) → Latin Scylla (the y representing Greek upsilon) → English Scylla, a Latin transparent borrowing.
  • Pronunciation: English /sˈaɪlə/ (like "siller"), with the y pronounced as English i; the traditional rendering "SIL-uh" reflects the medieval/learned convention of pronouncing Latin y.
  • Declension: English treats it as an indeclinable proper noun; Latin declines it as a first-declension feminine (Scylla, Scyllae...).
5. Register and connotation.
  • As a mythonym it is literary and classical in register; as the first half of the proverb it is a set phrase known to all educated speakers.
  • The connotation is ominous but also almost comic in its extremity: Scylla and Charybdis together represent the universe of over-determined disaster, the choice in which every option is fatal.
