In the Modern Library sentence, colophon names the registered trademark emblem that identifies the publisher: “Modern Library and colophon are registered trademarks of Random House, Inc.” The word here does the precise work of fine print — it designates the torchbearer device, the little pictorial mark that, like a signature, stands for the entire imprint and is protected by law. The nuance is legal and proprietary: a colophon in this sense is not a description of the book but a badge of ownership and origin, the publisher’s seal stamped into the front matter so that the reader knows, at a glance, whose house has produced the volume. Yet the word is far older than any trademark, and this single sentence is a quiet meeting-point of the ancient and the modern senses of the word.

Historically, the colophon was the scribe’s closing note at the end of a manuscript, the inscription that recorded the title, the author, the copyist, the place and date of production, and often a prayer of thanks (Laus Deo, “praise to God”). In the age before printed title pages existed, this ending inscription was the only formal identification a book carried; a reader could know who had made the book and where and when only because the scribe said so in the last lines. When printing arrived in the fifteenth century, the early printers inherited the custom: the first printed books closed with a colophon naming the printer, the place, and the date, and it is exactly this sense — the crowning statement that seals a finished work — that gave the word its bookish aura for centuries.

The semantic bridge between “crowning statement” and “publisher’s emblem” runs through the Greek source. Greek kolophṓn meant “summit, peak, top,” and in Greek idiom “to put the colophon on a thing” (epitithénai kolophōna) meant “to put the finishing touch on it” — the summit as the capstone that completes a tower or a work. Latin borrowed this as colophōn, “a finishing stroke, a conclusion,” and the word travelled from the idea of completion to the specific closing inscription of a book, and finally, in the twentieth century, to the identifying device that publishers print to mark their editions. The Modern Library torchbearer is thus a direct descendant of the scribe’s signed farewell: what was once a sentence of thanks has condensed into an image, and the legal formula that protects it performs in modern dress the same identifying function the old inscriptions once carried.

Because the word has moved across more than two thousand years, its senses are best mapped as a fan:
  • The manuscript colophon — the closing inscription of a hand-copied book (title, scribe, place, date, often a prayer); the oldest sense, now historical.
  • The early printed colophon — the printer’s closing note in incunabula, later displaced by the title page from the sixteenth century onward.
  • The printer’s device — the emblematic mark (Aldus’s dolphin-and-anchor, Plantin’s compass) that identified a press; the bridge between inscription and modern emblem.
  • The publisher’s emblem / trademark — the pictorial device on the title page, spine, or cover identifying the imprint; the Modern Library torchbearer is a famous instance.
  • The colophon page — a modern bibliographic feature, usually at the end of a book or on the copyright page, recording typeface, paper, and design details.
  • The computing colophon — by loose, bookish extension, the boot-time identification banner of a computer or console; a machine’s “signature.”
Boundary words sharpen the term. An imprint is the official name and address of the publisher printed in the book — a verbal and legal statement — whereas the colophon is a pictorial device. A logo is the general modern word for any company mark and lacks the bibliographic history and bookish flavour of colophon. A device is the old printing term for a printer’s emblem. A trademark is the legal term for any protected name, sign, or symbol; colophon names the symbol specifically within publishing. An ex libris, or bookplate, marks a private library’s ownership rather than a publisher’s identity. In modern usage, therefore, colophon occupies a precise niche at the intersection of bibliography, branding, and law — a word whose long history is compressed into a single identifying picture.
