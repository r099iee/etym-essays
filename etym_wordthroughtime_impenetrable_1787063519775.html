- Latin root penus “innermost part of the house, larder” — the original image is entering the pantry of a home, the deepest recess.
- Latin penetrare “to enter, go into the inmost part”; penetrabilis “that may be entered or pierced.”
- Latin impenetrabilis “not to be pierced or entered” — the negative form, used of forests, walls, and iron.
- English 15th c. (via Middle French impénétrable): first literal — impenetrable forest, fortress, darkness.
- 17th c.: philosophers and theologians extend it to mind and truth — impenetrable mysteries, impenetrable hearts.
- 17th–18th c.: natural philosophy borrows “impenetrability” as a property of matter.
- 19th–20th c.: impenetrable jargon, impenetrable reserve, impenetrable security — the modern figurative spread.
- 1937: Tolkien's Smaug — “the Impenetrable” as a comic-grand epithet for a dragon — cements the word's two faces (armour plus proud self-image).
