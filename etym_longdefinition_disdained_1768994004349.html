disdained (verb, past tense and past participle of disdain; also the inflected form of the noun's sibling verb) — to have regarded with contempt; to have looked upon as unworthy of one's notice, effort, or dignity; to have refused with haughty condescension. The verb sits in the formal, literary register of English, carrying a classical weight inherited through French from Latin. Its core gesture is judgment: the disdainer weighs a person, thing, or action and finds it below a standard of worth that the disdainer believes himself to possess. It is a word of refusal more than of attack.
  • Primary sense — judgment of unworthiness: to disdain someone or something is to consider it unworthy of serious consideration or respect: "she disdained the offer of help". The object is not hated; it is dismissed. The dismissal may be social (rank), aesthetic (taste), moral (scruple), or intellectual (contempt for what seems beneath one).
  • Secondary sense — refusal to stoop: to disdain to do something is to refuse to do it on grounds of dignity: "he disdained to answer the insult". Here the word governs an infinitive and describes an act of abstention performed out of pride — a refusal that itself communicates contempt.
  • Third sense — the noun and adjectives: the same stem yields the noun disdain ("the feeling that someone or something is unworthy"), the adjective disdainful ("showing contempt"), and the adverb disdainfully. In the passage the past participle disdained does the work of the verb: the earthmen "disdained the services of Martian guides" — an accomplished, sealed act of contemptuous refusal performed before the journey began.
  • Literary context: the earthmen — "hard-bitten adventurers" — disdain the services of Martian guides and set out alone into the dangerous Chaur. The nuance is cold rather than hot: disdain does not rage or sneer openly; it quietly files the offered help under "beneath me" and proceeds. The past tense seals the deed as accomplished — the guides were weighed, found wanting, and dismissed — and the sentence carries the reader's foreboding: this is the arrogance that precedes disaster, hubris dressed as professionalism.
  • Semantic boundary versus near-synonyms: scorn is the heated cousin — open mockery, derision, often voiced and directed at persons; despising is the moral heavyweight — an abiding revulsion for what is base or wicked; spurning is physical and active (Old English spurnan, "to kick" — the rejected thing is kicked away); slighting is indifference or neglect without a positive verdict of unworthiness. Disdain alone is the cool, self-aware judgment of superiority, an etiquette of worth in which the disdainer is always conscious of the standard that the despised object fails to meet.
The historical bridge: disdain descends from Latin dedignari, "to deem unworthy," itself a negative form of dignari, "to deem worthy," from dignus, "worthy." The word therefore encodes a double negation — "not deemed worthy" — and retains the Latin sense of measurement against a standard of dignity. In the passage the word also implies an etiquette of worth: the disdainer is always conscious of a standard of dignity that the despised object fails to meet, which is precisely what makes the earthmen's arrogance readable as self-dooming rather than merely rude.
