- Late 16th c. — Coining of the Delegative Compound: Formed in Early Modern English by combining the Latinate prefix in- / en- with the Scandinavian loanword trust, rapidly establishing a verb for formal stewardship and administrative charge.
- 17th c. — Legal and Political Flowering: Becomes a foundational concept in Jacobean and Restoration equity jurisprudence, defining the duties of executors and guardians in the Court of Chancery.
- 18th c. — Neoclassical Dominance of the in- Spelling: Solidified as the premier orthography in political philosophy and constitutional law (Hume, Blackstone, US Constitution, Federalist Papers), used to describe sovereign power granted by the people.
- 19th c. — Victorian Commercial and Domestic Ubiquity: Widely used across novels (Jane Austen, Charles Dickens) and commercial contracts to describe marriage settlements, financial estates, and moral guardianship.
- Early 20th c. — Lexicographical Standardization to en-: Style guides and dictionary editors (such as the Fowler brothers and the OED) begin recommending entrust as the standard form, categorizing intrust as an archaic or dialectal variant.
- Today — Historical, Statutory, and Classical Elegance: While entrusted dominates modern journalism and conversational English, intrusted survives in original historical charters, constitutional jurisprudence, and fine literature.
