- 17th century – English takes dialectal 'wabble' from the North Sea coast (Middle Low German wabbeln, 'to move unsteadily to and fro'); it is a plain, physical working word — the motion of a loose plank, a full barrel, a cart on a bad road.
- 18th–19th centuries – the spelling settles as 'wobble', and the word is used of wheels out of true, jellies on plates, loose teeth: a slackness of fit, motion without purpose; the frequentative suffix -le (as in sparkle, crackle) marks the movement as repeated, small, and purposeless — a rhythm that has gone slack.
- 20th century – 'wobbling' spreads into figurative use: wobbling opinions, a wobbling economy, a wobbling voice — any unstable to-and-fro — and gains a comic-mechanical colour, the gyration of a part loose within its socket (Peake's carving head 'wobbling in a mechanical way from side to side').
- 21st century – both senses thrive: the literal wobble of loose parts and the figurative wobble of uncertain decisions; the word's own phonetics — the labial 'w' and the doubled '-bb-' — keep it feeling loose in the mouth, motion without purpose, rhythm without control.
