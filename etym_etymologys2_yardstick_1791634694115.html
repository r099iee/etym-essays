This compound noun is formed by uniting two ancient Germanic terms: the unit/measuring rod yard + the slender wood piece stick.

[Root 1: yard (measuring rod / 3 feet)]
Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*gʰazdh-o- / *gʰasto-
"rod, staff, pole, stake"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*gazdijō
"rod, staff, goad, measuring stick"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
ġierd / ġeard / ġyrd
"twig, shoot, staff, measuring rod"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.)
ȝerde / yerde
"rod, measuring staff; 3-foot measure"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
yard
"linear measure of 36 inches"

[Root 2: stick (slender piece of wood)]
Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*steig-
"to stick, prick, pierce, be sharp"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*stikkan- / *stikkiz
"stick, peg, pointed wooden staff"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
sticca
"stick, twig, peg, rod"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.)
stikke
"slender piece of wood, staff"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
stick
"wooden rod, piece of timber"

[Compound Formation]
Early 19th Century English (c. 1823)
yard-stick
"graduated wooden measuring rod of one yard"
↓
Late 19th Century to Modern English (c. 1883–present)
yardstick
"graduated 36-inch rod; figurative
standard or benchmark of judgment"

Note: English has two homophonous words spelled 'yard': (1) 'yard' meaning an enclosed ground or court (from PIE *gʰordʰ- 'to enclose', cognate with 'garden' and 'court'), and (2) 'yard' meaning a measuring rod, unit of 3 feet, or nautical spar (from PIE *gʰazdh-o- 'rod, spear', cognate with Latin 'hasta'). 'Yardstick' derives entirely from the second root. The compound 'yardstick' arose in American English in the 1820s to distinguish the physical graduated rod from the abstract unit of measurement, developing its figurative sense of 'criterion' by the 1880s.
