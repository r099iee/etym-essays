The biography of maritime opens in the reconstructed depth of Proto-Indo-European with the root *móri-, which meant "sea" but more originally "standing body of water" — the sea conceived not as the restless open ocean but as a bounded, still expanse of water, the same notion that fed Germanic words for "mere" (lake, pond) and "marsh." The reconstruction is securely attested across Italic, Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic, and its genitive *mr̥-yés explains the varying vocalism of the daughter forms. Two scholarly caveats must be noted: Greek has no reflex of *móri- (Hellenic replaced the sea-word with thálassa, of uncertain origin, and póntos, originally "sea path"), and Indo-Aryan likewise lacks it, Sanskrit using sāgara and samudrá. A supposed link to a root *mer- "to gleam" or to *mer- "to die" is speculative, though the sea-death pun was exploited in the poetic language of Celtic (Old Irish muir "sea" beside marb "dead") and Slavic (Russian more "sea" beside mretʹ "to die"). This is a family that must be reconstructed honestly: four branches, no Greek, no Sanskrit.

Within the Italic branch, *móri- gave Latin mare "sea," and from mare Latin generated two rival adjectives: marīnus (mare + -īnus), which yielded English marine, mariner, and submarine; and marītimus, built with the adjectival suffix *-timus also seen in fīnitimus "neighbouring," which carried the sense "situated by the sea, belonging to the sea." It is marītimus that descends into the Romance vernaculars and, through Old French maritime (attested from the twelfth century), entered English in the late fourteenth to fifteenth centuries. At first the English adjective meant chiefly "bordering on the sea" — a maritime town, a maritime region — but under the pressure of the early-modern mercantile revolution the sense widened to "relating to shipping, navigation, commerce, and naval power."

The widening of sense. The crucial semantic shift happened between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. As England, the Netherlands, France, and Spain built trading empires, "maritime" grew from a word about coasts into a word about power: maritime power, maritime supremacy, maritime commerce. The adjective became institutional, the standard term of geopolitics and international rivalry. It is precisely this developed sense that Macaulay deploys in the opening of the History of England (1848), where "a gigantic commerce gave birth to a maritime power" — the word names the entire sea-borne economic-military system that made England great, and the repetition of "maritime power" turns the category itself into the measure of national achievement. Macaulay's Whig historiography treats sea-power born of commerce as progress, and the adjective carries that triumphant, mercantile glow.

Sound and form. The word passed through Latin, Late Latin, and Old French with its core shape intact: mare → marītimus → maritime. The English stress falls on the first syllable (MA-ri-time), a typically English re-stressing of a French-borrowed word; the final -e of maritime is pronounced (/taɪm/-like in some British usage, /tɪm/ or /ˌmaɪt/-variants across registers), a continuing shibboleth of the word's double allegiance to French and Latin spelling. Its cognates — marine, mariner, submarine, marina, mer, mar, mare, mere, mermaid, marsh — show the root radiating through every branch of Indo-European into modern English.

Modern career. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries maritime became indispensable in international law: maritime law, maritime boundaries, maritime zones, the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. It is the adjective of navigation rights, exclusive economic zones, and coastal states, alongside its older geopolitical use in maritime power and maritime strategy. The word has thus completed a long trajectory from a PIE word for still water, through Latin's sea and its coastal adjective, through French, to become the formal, institutional English adjective for everything that happens on and through the sea.
