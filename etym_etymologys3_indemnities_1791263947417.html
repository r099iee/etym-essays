The plural indemnities presupposes the singular indemnity, whose history runs from Roman law of loss to modern insurance and war reparations. The key is Latin damnum, loss, damage, expense, fine, one of the great Roman legal words alongside culpa and poena. Roman lawyers used damnum for any diminution of patrimony: damnum emergens, loss actually suffered, lucrum cessans, profit foregone. From damnum came damnare, to condemn by inflicting loss, and with negation indemnis, without loss, unharmed, literally not having suffered damnum. The abstract indemnitas therefore meant the state of being without loss, security against damage, and by a natural shift the payment that restores that state.

The ultimate Indo-European source of damnum is commonly reconstructed as a root meaning to cut up, divide, allot, seen also in Greek dapane, expense, and daptein, to devour, to carve and consume. The semantic path from dividing meat or land to expense and then to loss is economically vivid: what is cut away and allotted to another is cost to the giver. Proto-Italic formed a noun with -no- suffix for the result of such cutting: expense, then any loss. Michiel de Vaan accepts this connection as plausible while noting competing details, but the Latin development onward is fully attested and legally precise.

  • Roman to medieval law: In the Digest indemnitas appears for exemption from loss and guarantee against claims. Canonists and civilians extended it to safe-conducts and promises to hold harmless. Old French borrowed indemnité in the 14th century for compensation for damage, and Middle English followed in the 15th century as indemnity, first in legal and diplomatic documents promising to keep a party harmless.
  • Phonetic stability: As a learned legal borrowing, indemnity changed little. Latin indemnis with stress on -demn- yielded French indemnité with final stress and English indemnity with antepenultimate stress. The -mn- cluster simplified in speech but retained in spelling as a learned marker. The plural adds regular -ies, pronounced as a voiced sibilant. Received Pronunciation keeps secondary stress on the first syllable and primary stress on the second.
  • Two modern poles - protection and payment: By the 17th-18th centuries English indemnity splits into two complementary senses that persist: protection against future loss, as in an indemnity clause, indemnity insurance, Act of Indemnity pardoning past illegalities; and compensation for past loss, as in war indemnities, indemnities paid to slave-owners on abolition, indemnities to flood victims. The plural indemnities almost always denotes the second pole: sums exacted or paid. The Franco-Prussian indemnity of 1871, the Boxer indemnity of 1901, and German reparations after the World Wars are textbook examples.
  • Imperial and literary use: Nineteenth-century imperialism made indemnities a euphemism for tribute: victors no longer loot and exterminate but impose legal debts that enslave economically, exactly as the source sentence observes. Abolition debates inverted the term: should indemnities go to the enslaved for their suffering or to owners for loss of property. Literature uses the word for its cold legal aura, contrasting human suffering with accountant language.

Thus indemnities in the source sentence is bitterly ironic: a word that promises making whole and holding harmless becomes the instrument of enslavement by debt. The nation is not killed but kept alive to pay, bound by ledgers rather than chains, and the plural form stresses repeated, grinding payments rather than a single settlement.
