- c. 1200–1300 — "slingen" enters English with the Viking settlers, for hurling with a swinging rotation of the arm; the very first sense is projectile motion, and the throwing weapon is named for the same wind-and-swing: a sling.
- 14th–16th c. — the sense of loose suspension grows beside the hurling sense: what is slung hangs freely from a point of support — a sword slung at the belt, a shield slung on the arm — carried loosely rather than worn stiffly.
- 17th–19th c. — "slung" becomes the verb of habitual, easeful carrying among soldiers and travellers: a musket, a pouch, a knapsack slung over the shoulder in one practiced swing; the word sheds force and keeps only motion and looseness.
- Contemporary — "slung" still names the quick, casual swing that places a thing where the body can use it, and its register is plain and physical; from an archer's quiver to a bag on a commuter's shoulder, it is the muscle-memory motion of the old days.
