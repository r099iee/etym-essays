Laudable means deserving praise, commendable, praiseworthy — worthy of being applauded, approved, or spoken well of. It evaluates a person, action, motive, effort, or sentiment as morally good, admirable, or excellent in a way that merits public acknowledgment. In the source fragment, "A laudable sentiment," a speaker grants that someone's feeling or expressed opinion is noble and worthy of approval, even if — as the clipped, possibly ironic context hints — it may be impractical, naive, or insufficient on its own. The adjective thus performs gracious concession: I praise your motive while reserving judgment on the outcome.

The word belongs to a formal, slightly elevated register. Where nice, good, or great are casual and all-purpose, laudable is judicious and public: committees praise laudable initiatives, obituaries cite laudable service, judges commend laudable motives, teachers encourage laudable ambition. It implies an external standard — virtue, duty, excellence — against which the thing is measured and found deserving. Its noun laudability is rare; far commoner are praise, commendation, and the near-synonym laudatory (which means expressing praise, while laudable means deserving it — a distinction learners must keep).

Two grammatical frames dominate. Attributive: "a laudable goal / effort / sentiment / achievement." Predicative with infinitive or gerund: "It is laudable to volunteer," "Their attempt, though failed, was laudable." Negation is frequent and pointed: "a less than laudable motive," "hardly laudable tactics" — damning by denying praise-worthiness without using overt abuse. Intensifiers are muted (highly, truly, genuinely laudable) rather than slangy, preserving decorum.

  • Morally praiseworthy (motives, sentiments, conduct): the core sense — goodness of character that deserves applause. Example: "Her concern for the homeless is laudable." Bridge: Latin laus (praise) + -abilis (worthy of) compositionally yields "worthy of praise," applied first to persons then to their feelings.
  • Commendable effort despite failure: a pragmatic extension where praise is decoupled from success. Example: "A laudable attempt to mediate, though it collapsed." The reasoning is humane: effort and intention merit honor even when results disappoint, a staple of consolatory rhetoric.
  • Excellent / exemplary in quality (work, achievement): transferred from morals to craft. Example: "A laudable restoration of the historic theatre." Here praiseworthiness rests on skill, care, and fidelity rather than virtue, showing the word's drift from ethics to aesthetics.
  • Institutional-bureaucratic commendation: official jargon in reports, citations, and minutes. Example: "The committee noted the team's laudable progress on safety." Such use is deliberately measured: warmer than satisfactory, cooler than outstanding, safe for formal record.
  • Ironic / faint praise: modern conversational use where laudable damns with faint praise. Example: "A laudable sentiment, but utterly impractical." The bridge is politeness strategy: speakers grant moral credit in order to deny practical credit, exactly the flavor the isolated fragment may carry.

Mastery requires hearing that faint-praise edge. Because laudable praises worthiness rather than effectiveness, adding but after it almost reverses its warmth: "laudable but naive," "laudable but doomed." In isolation it is sincere; in argument it is often the velvet glove before the blow. Collocations to learn: laudable aim / goal / sentiment / motive / effort / initiative / achievement; highly laudable; less than laudable. Avoid using it for trivial things ("a laudable sandwich" is comic bathos) — the word demands a worthy object.
