In earlier English, “apology” meant a speech in self-defence or justification — a formal vindication — and to speak “apologetically” was to argue one’s case.
“Apologetic” from the 16th century onward shifted: an apology became an expression of regret or excuse for a fault, and the apologetic manner became the soft, hedged voice of self-effacement.
“Apologetically” in the 19th–20th centuries settled as the adverb for speech that excuses, softens, or regrets — the tone of the polite host interrupting a louder company.
“Apologetically” today is pure characterization of manner: to say something apologetically is to ask, to hedge, and to shrink rather than to command — a word that paints voice and posture.
