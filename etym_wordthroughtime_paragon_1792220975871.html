- 6th–12th c. — Coined in Byzantine Greek as parakonē (from parakonáō, "to rub against a whetstone / sharpen"), denoting a whetstone or touchstone used by goldsmiths to assay precious metals.
- 13th–14th c. — Adopted into Old Italian as paragone, maintaining the concrete metallurgical sense of a dark touchstone for testing gold purity, while rapidly developing the metaphorical sense of a comparison or trial.
- 15th–16th c. — Italian Renaissance humanists establish *il paragone* as the formal theoretical contest between the visual arts (painting vs sculpture); French borrows it as parangon, applying it to flawless pearls, gems, and human role models.
- 1540s–1600 — Enters Tudor and Elizabethan English; Shakespeare cements its canonical place in English literature in *Hamlet* ("the paragon of animals"), establishing it as the supreme word for an unexcelled pattern of beauty and nobility.
- 17th–18th c. — Institutionalized in typography as a specific 20-point type size; in gemology, established as the technical designation for an immaculate gem of over 100 carats.
- 19th–21st c. — Ubiquitously entrenched in English prose as the quintessential idiom of ideal virtue ("paragon of virtue/justice"), while acquiring a sophisticated second register of gentle journalistic irony.
