Latin grossus (thick, coarse, bulky; of rude physical size), attested in Late Latin; its pre-Latin origin is uncertain — no securely reconstructable earlier Indo-European stage exists, and a substrate, Mediterranean, or expressive origin is possible. [DEBATED — see note below] → Old French gros (large, thick, big; in the commercial phrase en gros "wholesale, in bulk", opposed to en détail "retail"), developing from the 11th century CE → Old French grossier (a wholesale dealer, one who sells by the gross or in bulk; also "coarse, rude" of persons), 12th century CE → Anglo-Norman / Middle English grosser, grossour, grocer (a wholesale dealer in bulk goods; later a dealer in food and household provisions), attested from c. 1275 CE; the Grocers' Company of London was in existence by 1345 → Modern English GROCER — the card's target form: a retailer of food and household provisions. NOTE on the debate: Latin grossus itself has no certain earlier etymology — it may be expressive or substrate in origin — so the chain legitimately begins at Latin rather than at a reconstructed PIE root. Note also that modern French grossier came to mean "coarse, rude" while English grocer specialized commercially; the two words diverged after the borrowing.
