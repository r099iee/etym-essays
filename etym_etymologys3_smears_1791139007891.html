Smears is the plural noun and third-person verb form of smear, whose deep root is PIE *smer- “grease, fat, oily substance,” extended with the causative-iterative sense “to rub with grease.” The root is robustly attested: Sanskrit medas-? Greek smyris? but most directly Germanic — Old Norse smjǫr “butter,” Dutch smeer, German Schmer “grease, lard,” English smear, smirch via the same fat-cluster. Proto-Germanic *smerwjan meant “to anoint, rub with ointment,” a neutral-to-positive act in a world that oiled bodies, leather and axles. Old English continues both branches: the verb smierwan / smyrian “to anoint, daub” and the noun smeoru “fat, grease, ointment.”

Middle English smeren (c. 1200) already shows the moral staining that would dominate: alongside “anoint with chrism” we find “smear with filth, defile.” The broadening is natural — grease both preserves and soils — but Christianity accelerated the negative pole: anointing stayed holy (anoint, unction took over the sacred slot), while smear slid toward dirty daubing. By Caxton (1480s) “smeared with blood, smeared with ink” is standard, and Shakespeare uses both poles within lines: holy balm vs “smear’d with …” shame.

Early Modern English multiplied senses that the plural smears still carries:

  • Physical blotch: a daub of grease, paint, blood, soot. “Ruddy smears upon the brown air” (Dickens, 1843) is a daring transposition: candle-flames seen through fog become grease-blotches on dark air, as if light itself could grease the gloom.
  • Blur / smudge: an indistinct mark where clarity is lost — lipstick smear, ink smear, lens smear. Photography and microscopy (19th–20th c.) made this technical: “blood smear, Pap smear” — a thin film spread on a slide for examination.
  • Defamation: from “daub with mud” to “daub with slander.” “Smear campaign, smear tactic” (first political in 1930s–40s America) treats reputation as a clean surface that can be blotted. The verb “to smear someone” now rivals the noun in frequency.
  • Victory slang: sporting “to smear the opposition” = to crush, rub into the ground (American football, 1950s), and “smear” as oily ointment still in pharmacy (“vaginal smear” preparation).

Phonology is conservative: Old English smierwan /ˈsmi͜orwan/ monophthongised and lost its ending to Middle English smeren /ˈsmeːrən/, then Great Vowel Shift raised /eː/ to /iː/ before /r/ lowered it again in modern /smɪə/ (RP) — hence RP smear /smɪə/, plural smears /smɪəz/. Spelling smear stabilised by 1600; the -s plural/3sg is regular.

Culturally, smears moved from farm and forge (greasing wheels, smearing salve) to printshop (ink smears), to clinic (smear test, 1928 Papanicolaou), to newsroom (smear job). Each era kept the core image of a clean ground — skin, paper, slide, reputation, evening air — spoiled or marked by an oily blotch, whether literal or moral. Dickens’s use is pivotal: he makes city light itself a smear, beauty inseparable from soot, prefiguring Impressionist “smears” of colour and modern “smear of lights” for bokeh.

Literary milestones:

  • Old English Leechdoms: “smire with slyme” — medical anointing.
  • c. 1382 Wycliffe: smeared altars and defiled hands.
  • Shakespeare, Titus, Lear: faces “smeared” with blood and tears.
  • 1843 Dickens: “ruddy smears upon the palpable brown air” — light as grease on fog.
  • 1940s–present: “smear campaign,” “Pap smear” — slander and slide.

In short, smears travelled from sacred grease to dirty blotch to optical blur to moral slur, always a substance out of place made visible. The plural matters: one smear is an accident; smears are a condition — of windows, slides, reputations or a whole London afternoon.
