• Oppenheimer (Franz Oppenheimer): Similar because both were pioneering sociological theorists who demonstrated that the political state originated through the conquest and subjugation of agrarian communities by pastoralist invaders; differs because Oppenheimer explicitly contrasted the "economic means" (peaceful production and trade) with the "political means" (unrequited violent exploitation), whereas Gumplowicz focused more broadly on polygenetic group conflict and legal evolution.
  • Ratzenhofer (Gustav Ratzenhofer): Similar because both were 19th-century Austro-German conflict sociologists who viewed violence, group interest, and conquest as the foundational catalysts of social evolution and political structures; differs because Ratzenhofer developed a more detailed theory of interest-group accommodation and state-directed social harmonization, whereas Gumplowicz maintained a more unyielding, pessimistic conflict determinism.
  • Ward (Lester Frank Ward): Similar because Ward closely studied and adapted Gumplowicz's theories of syngenism and ethnic conflict in state formation; differs because Ward rejected pure social Darwinian fatalism, advocating instead for "telesis"—the conscious, purposeful guiding of social evolution through universal education and democratic state action.
  • Sumner (William Graham Sumner): Similar because both were influential late 19th-century social theorists who emphasized group solidarity, in-group favoritism ("ethnocentrism"), and the role of raw force in sustaining political institutions; differs because Sumner was an ardent laissez-faire individualist and sociologist of folkways/mores, whereas Gumplowicz rejected individualism entirely in favor of strict group-level sociological determinism.
  • Spencer (Herbert Spencer): Similar because both applied evolutionary frameworks to human societies and the development of political institutions; differs because Spencer centered his evolutionary philosophy on individual competition, biological Lamarckian adaptation, and ultimate social harmony, whereas Gumplowicz insisted that individual human will is an illusion and that collective group conflict is eternal.
  • Marx (Karl Marx): Similar because both identified violent struggle and social exploitation as the prime movers of human history and viewed state apparatuses and legal codes as tools of class domination; differs because Marx grounded class antagonism strictly in the ownership of the material means of production within an economic mode, whereas Gumplowicz traced the root of domination to martial conquest, ethnic syngenism, and external tribal subjugation.
  • Machiavelli (Niccolò Machiavelli): Similar because both offered realistic, unsentimental analyses of political power, force, and statecraft stripped of theological or moralistic idealism; differs because Machiavelli wrote primarily pragmatic manuals of governance and princehood in Renaissance Italy, whereas Gumplowicz established a systematic sociological theory of group dynamics and legal origins.
