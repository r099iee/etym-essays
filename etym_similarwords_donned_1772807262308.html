• Put on: Similar because both name the act of dressing; differs because put on is the plain everyday phrasal verb with no ceremonial weight.
  • Wear: Similar because both relate to clothing; differs because wear describes the state of having clothes on, not the act of assuming them.
  • Slip on: Similar because both mean to dress quickly; differs because slip on stresses casual speed and low ceremony, the opposite of formally donning regalia.
  • Throw on: Similar because both name a rapid act of dressing; differs because throw on suggests careless, hurried effort, without the deliberateness of donning.
  • Pull on: Similar because both name putting on garments; differs because pull on suggests tugging on close-fitting or casual clothes, not assuming insignia of rank.
  • Dress in: Similar because both involve putting on clothing; differs because dress in is broader, covering style and full outfit, without the item-by-item focus of don.
  • Attire oneself in: Similar because both are formal ways to say put on; differs because attire oneself is pompously formal and focuses on the whole costume rather than the single act.
  • Clothe oneself in: Similar because both mean to put clothing on one's body; differs because clothe oneself stresses having garments on at all, not the specific assumption of one garment.
  • Array oneself in: Similar because both describe dressing in elaborate garments; differs because array oneself emphasizes magnificent, showy dress and is more archaic.
  • Assume: Similar because both can mean take on; differs because assume is abstract and can mean adopting a role without physical clothing, while don always involves putting something on the body.
  • Adopt: Similar because both can mean taking on something new; differs because adopt is metaphorical and general, unrelated to garments.
  • Invest oneself in: Similar because both can describe putting on official or ceremonial dress; differs because invest is rare and usually used of vestments and offices, with clerical overtones.
  • Doff: Similar because it is the historical partner verb to don; differs because doff means take off (from do off), the exact opposite action.
  • Get dressed in: Similar because both mean to put on clothes; differs because get dressed is neutral everyday speech, while donned is literary and slightly elevated.
  • Robe: Similar because both can describe putting on official garments; differs because robe as a verb is narrower, used mainly of robes and ceremonial dress, not hats or coats.
