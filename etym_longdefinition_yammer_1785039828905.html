**YAMMER (verb) — at its plainest core** At its most basic level, **yammer** means to make a loud, repetitive, high-pitched, or complaining noise; to wail, howl, or chatter on incessantly. It is an imitative or expressive word whose sound already suggests its meaning — the repeated *-m-* and open vowels give a drawn-out, grating, nasal clamour. In the goblins' jeering chorus "Yammer and bleat!" the word evokes the loud, shapeless, high-pitched outcry of creatures in panic and distress — precisely the noise the goblins expect their captives to make as they are roasted and devoured. Its nuance is contemptuous and chaotic: a sustained, grating clamour without dignity or articulation, at once complaining and terrified, reduced by the goblins to an irritating racket they can jeer at. **Primary senses**
  • To wail or lament: the oldest sense, from Old English *geōmrian*, "to lament, mourn, complain, groan" — a grief-stricken, sorrowful crying out.
  • To cry out noisily: from the 1510s, yammering is a loud, annoying noise — the clamour of distress, panic, or complaint, shapeless and unceasing.
  • To chatter on complainingly: the modern colloquial sense — talking loudly, persistently, and complainingly, "yammering on" about grievances.
  • Of animals: wolves yammering into the night, dogs yammering — the prolonged, howling, high-pitched animal cry.
**Polysemy and secondary modern senses**
  • The persistent complainer: in modern prose, *yammer* means talking loudly, persistently, or complainingly ("yammering on"); a person who yammers is a nagging, querulous talker whose words are noise.
  • Mechanical noise: by extension, machinery, alarms, or engines can yammer — a persistent, irritating, high-pitched sound.
  • Germanic cognates: German *Jammer* "lamentation, misery" and *jammern* "to wail, moan"; the expressive compound *Katzenjammer* (literally "wailing of cats") was borrowed into English in 1821 for a hangover and for discordant noise.
**Distinctions from near-synonyms**
  • Wail: similar because both are loud lament; differs because wail is mournful and tuneful, a sustained cry with some melody, while yammer is shapeless, grating, and complaining.
  • Shriek: similar because both are loud outcries; differs because a shriek is a single, piercing burst, not a continuing noise.
  • Screech: similar in piercing loudness; differs because screech is a harsh, high, sudden cry, while yammer is a drawn-out, repetitive clamour.
  • Howl: similar because both suggest prolonged animal or wind-like sound; differs because a howl is full-throated and mournful, while yammer is thinner, more grating, and more complaining.
  • Yelp: similar in sharp animal cries; differs because a yelp is short and sharp, while yammering is persistent and drawn-out.
  • Whine: similar because both are complaining sounds; differs because a whine is thin, nasal, querulous, and low in volume, while yammering is loud and clamorous.
  • Grumble: similar in complaining; differs because grumble is low and muttered, while yammering is loud and high-pitched.
  • Jabber / chatter: similar because both are rapid, shapeless noise; differ because they are rapid talk not necessarily loud, while yammering is emphatically loud and persistent.
**Semantic reasoning and historical bridge** The semantic history of *yammer* is a classic case of semantic pejoration: a word of mourning (Old English *geōmrian*, "to lament") slides over the centuries from genuine grief to mere annoying noise. Old English *geōmor* meant "sorrowful, distressed"; the verb *geōmrian* meant to mourn and complain; but by the 1510s *yammer* could mean simply "to make a loud, annoying noise," and in modern English it means nagging, persistent complaint and chatter. The blending of the native English verb with the borrowed Continental *jammeren* (Middle Low German, "to wail, grieve") reinforced the shift toward irritation. Each modern sense preserves the root idea of a drawn-out, unhappy, attention-demanding sound — which is exactly why the goblins choose it to mock the agonies of their victims: their distress is reduced to an ugly, ignorable racket.
