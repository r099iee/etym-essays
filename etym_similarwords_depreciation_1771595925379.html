• Disparagement: Similar because it also lowers the worth or standing of someone or something; differs because disparagement belittles with contempt and a dismissive sneer, often dwelling on what is trivial, whereas depreciation is cooler and more objective — a judgment, not a sneer, closer to a bookkeeping markdown.
  • Denigration: Similar because it also damages standing and reduces worth; differs because denigration darkens a reputation with aspersions, staining it as one stains cloth — the smear of accusation — whereas depreciation implies a ledger-mark of reduced value rather than a stain of defamation.
  • Devaluation: Similar because it names a reduction in worth; differs because devaluation is strictly monetary and formal (a currency, an asset), whereas depreciation also covers reputation, standing, and non-financial value in a broader and less technical way.
  • Detraction: Similar because it takes away credit or merit from someone; differs because detraction is the active drawing-away of a prior reputation by rivals, presupposing a standing to be diminished, while depreciation can lower value that never carried public esteem.
  • Undervaluation: Similar because it sets too low a value on something; differs because undervaluation is a mistaken or unjust pricing by error of judgment, whereas depreciation is an ongoing downward process, often deliberate and recorded.
  • Belittlement: Similar because it makes something appear small and worthless; differs because belittlement is the plain, modern, overtly aggressive word — the dismissive gesture itself — whereas depreciation keeps the clinical air of a measured markdown or a formal censure.
  • Derogation: Similar because it takes away standing or authority; differs because derogation is more formal and legalistic, from the Roman law of repealing part of a statute, and names the subtraction of formal credit rather than the general fall of value.
  • Depreciation (financial-accounting): Similar because it also means a fall in value; differs because in accounting it is a precise, technical, non-cash charge spreading an asset's cost over its useful life — a ritual of bookkeeping — whereas the general sense covers any reduction in worth or estimation.
