Veddahs is the older English plural of Vedda, the name for the Indigenous hunter-gatherer people of Sri Lanka, and its deeper history leads back to an ancient Indian word for hunter. The ultimate source is the Sanskrit verbal root vyadh, to pierce, wound or strike, which belongs to a family often connected by Indo-Europeanists to PIE *wedʰ-, to strike or pierce. From the verb Sanskrit derived the agent noun vyādha, literally one who pierces, hence hunter, fowler or archer. This was not a tribal name originally but an occupational description applied to forest hunters in classical Indian literature and law, where the vyadha appears as a low-status but recognised forest caste living by the bow.

In the island that became Sri Lanka, Middle Indo-Aryan and then Sinhala inherited this vocabulary. Sinhala väddā, often spelled Veddā or Vedda in romanisation, narrowed from generic hunter to the specific autochthonous forest people of the dry zone, distinguished from the Sinhalese peasantry and Tamil communities. Pali chronicles like the Mahavamsa already contrast the yakkha forest dwellers with incoming Sinhala settlers, and later Sinhala usage fixed väddā as their ethnonym, sometimes extended pejoratively to any wild or rustic person. The singular vädda plus plural väddo was adapted in English with regular -s plural.

  • Piercing root: PIE *wedʰ- to Sanskrit vyadh preserves the core sense of striking through with a weapon, the technological basis of hunting.
  • Occupational noun: vyadh to vyādha creates the hunter by regular vrddhi lengthening, shifting from act to actor.
  • Island narrowing: Sanskrit vyādha to Sinhala väddā shifts from any hunter to the specific Indigenous community, with phonetic simplification of consonant clusters.
  • Colonial transmission: Sinhala väddā to English Vedda via Robert Knox's 1681 account An Historical Relation of Ceylon, after his 19-year captivity, introducing the term to European ethnography.
  • English pluralisation: Vedda to Veddahs adds regular -s, with older -hs spelling reflecting Victorian pluralisation of foreign words ending in -a.
  • Modern standardisation: Veddahs to Veddas or Vedda, as contemporary style drops the h and often uses Vedda as both singular and collective plural out of respect for self-designation.

Phonetically the development shows palatalisation and simplification: Sanskrit vy- to Sinhala v-, loss of aspiration contrasts, gemination and vowel lengthening in väddā, then English stress on the first syllable and reduction of final -a. Orthographically English sources vary wildly, Vedda, Veddah, Wedda, Waddah, reflecting attempts to capture Sinhala long vowels and Dutch and Portuguese intermediaries before Knox. Portuguese Veddos and Dutch Weddas in 16th-17th century accounts show the same root passing through Iberian and Dutch ears.

Socio-culturally the word's connotations have shifted dramatically. In Knox and 19th-century travellers it marked supposedly primitive wild men of the woods, exhibited in colonial discourse as living Stone Age survivals with no houses, a usage echoed in the source sentence grouping Veddahs with Tasmanians and Bushmen. Victorian anthropology made the Veddas famous through the Sarasin brothers and Seligmann, measuring skulls and describing bow, honey and rock-shelter life. Twentieth-century Sri Lankan nationalism and Indigenous activism reclaimed Vedda, now Wanniyala-Aetto, forest-dwellers, as first peoples with land rights, language heritage and cultural pride. Modern usage therefore prefers Vedda people or Veddas without exoticising, and capitalises it as a proper ethnonym, while Veddahs survives mainly in historical texts and older histories of architecture and civilisation.
