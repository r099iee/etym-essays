sawmill — a biography of cutting and grinding, joined. The compound welds two old roots from two families. The first is PIE *sek- "to cut," the root of Latin secāre "to cut" (our section, bisect, dissect, insect — the insect is literally "cut into") and of Old Church Slavonic sěšti "to cut" (Russian sech'), and, in Germanic, of the noun for the tool itself: *sagō "saw," whence Old English sagu, our saw, with its siblings Old Norse sǫg (Swedish såg), Middle Dutch sage (Dutch zaag), and Middle High German sage (German Säge). The second root is PIE *melh₁- "to grind, crush," the family of Greek mýlē "mill, millstone," of Lithuanian malti "to grind," of the native Germanic meal-word *melwą (Old English melu, English meal; German Mehl "flour"), and, in Italic, of Latin molere "to grind," mola "millstone," and Late Latin molīna "mill."

The machine and its spread. Old English mylen borrowed molīna, and the mill-name spread with the machine: French moulin, Italian mulino, Spanish molino, and English mill. For centuries "mill" meant grain-grinding, and the miller was a fixture of every village; a sawmill is that machine-concept turned to timber, the powered saw doing to trees what the millstone did to grain. The compound appears in the early modern period as water- and steam-driven sawing spreads, and in the frontier economy of the passage it is simply the town's engine of production — the first industrial building of the settlement, where forest became lumber and hands were fed into blades. One guardrail matters: this saw, the tool, is not the verb saw, the past tense of see — that saw is from PIE *sekʷ- "to see," a different root that only shares a spelling. The compound's history is thus a double biography — the cutter from *sek- and the grinder from *melh₁- — welded in the 17th century into the machine that cut the New World's timber.



diphtheria — a biography of the hide that became a disease. The story begins in ancient Greece with the preparation of leather: diphthéra meant "a prepared hide, skin, leather," the product of a long process of soaking, scraping, and kneading, and it was the word for the parchment and leather of books as well as of boots. The root is Greek déphō "to knead, soften," probably from a PIE *debh- "to stamp, knead," though the deeper pre-Greek history is not securely reconstructed — the handbooks state this uncertainty rather than inventing a root. For a thousand years diphthéra was a plain artisan's word: the tanner's leather, the scribe's parchment, the soldier's shield-covering.

From hide to throat. The transformation came in the 1850s, when the French physician Pierre Bretonneau — studying the epidemic throat disease of children — recognized the grey, tough, grey-white film that forms over the back of the throat and called it a diphthérite / diphtheria: "the leather disease," named for the membrane that looked like a piece of tanned hide. The coinage was a medical scientist's metaphor made permanent: the leather of the tanner became the membrane of the dying child. English adopted the word quickly, and the disease became the most dreaded child-killer of the Victorian age — the pesthouse, the quarantine, the sudden choking of children, the grey membrane that could suffocate a throat in a day. Then came the victories: diphtheria antitoxin (1890s, Behring and Kitasato), the toxoid vaccine (1920s), and the modern DPT immunization, which made the disease rare in the developed world. The word has therefore passed from a tanner's term to a medical terror to a vaccine-era memory — and in the passage's "diphtheria pesthouse," it is still the old fear, the leathery membrane and the suffocating throat, that gives the sentence its cold weight.
