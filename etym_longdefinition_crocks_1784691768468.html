Crocks is the plural of the noun crock, a word of deep Germanic stock denoting a vessel of coarse, low-fired earthenware — a pot, jar, or dish of unglazed or partly glazed clay, baked hard but never vitrified into porcelain, of the rough, heavy, utilitarian kind that furnished the kitchen, the dairy, and the cellar of the pre-industrial house. In its concrete primary sense the word names the object itself: a crock is a rounded, thick-walled pottery vessel used for storing butter, pickled vegetables, dripping, salted meat, honey, or preserves — the homely ware in which a farmhouse kept its provisions through the winter, sealed with a lid or a cloth. In the plural, crocks denotes the collective clutter of such vessels and, by the domestic extension that gives the word its most familiar modern life, any pile of dirty dishes and greasy cooking pots awaiting the wash-up: to "do the crocks" is to wash up, and a kitchen stacked with crocks is a kitchen that has been cooking hard. The full sense-spectrum of the word is wider than the modern kitchen suggests, and each strand grows from the same root idea of a heavy, hollow, roughly made clay thing:
  • Earthenware vessel (primary, since Old English). A pot, jar, pitcher, or dish of coarse fired clay — the word of the pantry, the dairy, and the larder, for salted butter, pickles, dripping, and preserves; in older usage often of unglazed or partly glazed ware baked in a potter's kiln.
  • Unwashed kitchenware, dirty dishes (familiar modern sense). By extension from the vessels to the accumulated mess of them: "piles of unwashed crocks" are piles of greasy pots and plates awaiting the wash-up; "the crocks" is the everyday name for the soiled washing-up itself.
  • A metal pot or cooking vessel (dialect). In many regional dialects crock could name a pot of any material, including iron or copper, showing that the size, use, and heft of the object mattered more than the exact clay.
  • The sooty pot-hook of the hearth (dialect, archaic). The same word transferred from the vessel to the iron crook on which pots hung over the fire — a homely metonymy of the open hearth, in which the thing that holds the pot takes the pot's name.
  • "Old crock" — a broken-down horse or a sickly, useless person (colloquial, 19th c.). The rough physicality of the worn earthen vessel is transferred to living creatures: an "old crock" is a lame, worthless horse or a person, a machine, or a body past its usefulness — the same degraded solidity of a thing gone to ruin.
  • "A crock" = nonsense, a falsehood (American slang, 20th c.). From "a crock of shit" — a hollow, heavy, smelly container of worthless content — the singular came to mean an absurd or untrue statement: "that's a crock".
  • Verb "to crock" (British informal) and "crocked" (US slang). To crock is to injure, disable, or exhaust (to "crock oneself" at sport); "crocked" in American slang of the mid-twentieth century means drunk — the body itself rendered useless.
  • Crock-Pot (proprietary noun, 1970s). The modern electric slow cooker, named in the early 1970s for its glazed ceramic insert — a late, commercial continuation of the ancient earthen vessel's slow-cooking function.
In the literary context of The Hobbit, Chapter 2 ("Roast Mutton"), the word performs a precise comic and tonal function. The morning after the Unexpected Party, Bilbo Baggins discovers "a fearful mess in the room, and piles of unwashed crocks in the kitchen" — the debris of fourteen dwarves' feasting left behind as an unignorable material accusation. Tolkien chooses crocks rather than "dishes" or "plates" because the word imports an atmosphere of rough, greasy, unrefined domesticity: these are not the dainty teacups of Bag End's habitual order but coarse vessels blackened by dwarven appetite. The noun thereby bridges the grand adventure to come and the absurd, homely chores it has already generated, grounding the heroic in the scullery. Distinctions from near-synonyms sharpen the word. Crockery is the collective term for the whole range of earthenware and china tableware, whereas crocks singles out the coarse, heavy, pot-like members of that range. A dish is a flat or open vessel for serving or eating from; a jar is a narrow-mouthed storage container, typically of glass; a pot covers metal and ceramic cookware alike; and earthenware names the material class itself rather than the objects. Crocks alone combines all three notes — the vessel, its coarse clay, and the accumulating mess of the wash-up. The semantic history is a steady downward drift from dignity to debris. A word that once named the precious storage pottery of a household — valuable enough to be listed in wills — has come, in the plural, to name the dirtiest pile in the kitchen, and in slang to name a worn-out creature and a pack of lies. The bridge in every case is the same heavy, hollow, rough clay thing: solid when whole, hopeless once broken or soiled.
