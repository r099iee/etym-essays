Abstruse means difficult to understand, reconcile, or penetrate — obscure in meaning, recondite in subject, or opaque in expression. It applies to arguments, doctrines, texts, sciences, and questions that withhold their sense beneath layers of technicality, allusion, or depth: an "abstruse point of law", "abstruse mathematics", "abstruse philosophy". The word carries intellectual ambition: what is abstruse is not merely hard, but withdrawn from the common understanding, requiring patient, trained, or strenuous thought. That is precisely its force in Wordsworth's line — "Days of sweet leisure, taxed with patient thought / Abstruse" — where leisure's ease is spent in recondite mental labour.

The literal, physical sense of the classical Latin source, "hidden, concealed, thrust away out of sight", is now archaic and appears only in historical or poetic usage ("abstruse caves", 17th c.). The modern meaning is entirely figurative: what is hidden from the mind's eye rather than from the bodily eye.

The word's semantic field today can be mapped by degree and register:

  • Difficulty: hard to understand — "an abstruse argument"; differs from "difficult" in implying the difficulty is inherent to the subject's depth, not to bad writing.
  • Reconditeness: dealing in little-known, specialised, or esoteric matter — "abstruse scholarship"; the learnèdness itself repels the casual reader.
  • Opacity of style: over-technical or allusive prose — "abstruse jargon"; here the word acquires a mildly pejorative flavour of wilful obscurity.
  • Intellectual register: the word is itself formal and literary — rare in speech, at home in academic and critical prose, where it is a near-synonym of "recondite" and a cousin of "esoteric", "arcane", and "opaque".

Polysemy is minimal but the nuance spectrum is wide: "abstruse" shades from admiring ("abstruse profundity") to exasperated ("needlessly abstruse") depending on whether the reader blames the subject or the author. In theology and philosophy it was a technical companion of "speculative" and "metaphysical" from the 17th century (Locke's "abstruse questions", Hume's "abstruse philosophy").

The historical bridge: Latin abstrūsus is the past participle of abstrūdere, "to thrust away" (ab- "away" + trūdere "to thrust"). Something thrust away from sight is hidden; something hidden from the understanding is abstruse. The English word, borrowed in the 1520s, thus preserves a physical metaphor — pushing truth out of reach — as its permanent conceptual core.

The word also carries a distinctly social meaning: calling a work "abstruse" is usually a judgement about the audience as much as about the text, for it marks the boundary between the learned and the unlearned, between specialists and generalists. This is why the word lives so comfortably in book reviews, academic prose, and serious journalism, and why it can flatter ("profoundly abstruse") or dismiss ("gratuitously abstruse") almost in the same breath. Its antonymic pole is "clear", "plain", or "popular": where a populariser brings the difficulty down, an abstruse writer keeps it — or lets it keep — its difficult altitude.
