Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*stengʷ- / *steng-
"to push, strike, thrust, sting;
sharp sensory impact"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*stinkwaną
"to emit an odor, smell;
to leap, strike, thrust"
↓
Old English (8th c.)
stincan
"to emit a scent or smell;
to rise like dust or vapor"
↓
Old English (10th c.)
stincende
"foul-smelling, offensive, reeking"
↓
Middle English (13th c.)
stynkynge
"foul-smelling, corrupt, loathsome"
↓
Early Modern English (16th c.)
stinking
"foul-smelling, malodorous;
contemptible, offensive"

Note: In Proto-Germanic and early Old English, the root verb 'stincan' was sensorially neutral and could denote a pleasant fragrance (e.g. 'swete stincende' in early texts) as well as an offensive smell. By the late Old English period, semantic pejorization permanently restricted the word to repulsive, offensive stenches.
