Section 1 — the history of “prae-” (the before-prefix) The first element of predecessor is the Latin preposition prae, “before, in front of,” and behind it lies one of the most widespread roots of the Indo-European family. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European *per-, with its vocalic variants *prō and *prē, carried the sense of “forward, forth, through, first, over.” Its reflex is everywhere: Sanskrit prá means “forth, forward,” Greek pró means “before, forward” (prologue, prognosis, programme), Latin pro means “for, on behalf of, before” (propose, provide, pronoun) alongside prae “in front” (precede, prefix, prior), and the Germanic branch underlies English fore, for, first, from, and German vor. A single spatial idea—motion or position ahead—fractured into the prefix systems of every Indo-European language, a testimony to how fundamental the notion of “ahead” is to human thinking about time, space, rank, and purpose. In Latin the two forms pro and prae specialized. pro carried the senses “forward, on behalf of, in place of” (proceed, provide, proconsul), while prae carried “in front of, in advance of” (praeesse “to be before,” praepositus “a prefect,” praecurrere “to run before,” praedictus “aforementioned,” praesens “present,” literally “being before the eyes”). The prefix was supremely productive in administrative and military Latin because offices and ranks are defined by priority: the praefectus is “placed before” others, the praeses “sits in front,” the praesul “dances before” the procession. Priority is the natural metaphor for authority and for time, and prae- carried both: what is “before” is either in front spatially or earlier in time. That double sense is exactly what predecessor needs. The word marks the earlier holder of an office, and “earlier” is here expressed by prae-: the praedēcessor is the one who “has gone before.” The prefix is thus doing chronological work inside an administrative term, and the same prae- continues to live in modern English words that are transparently temporal or spatial—precede, previous, prelude, prehistory, prenatal. Culturally, the choice of prae- rather than some other “before” word reflects the Roman instinct for rank and order: Latin organized the world by who stands in front, and English inherited that organization wholesale through its Latinate administrative vocabulary. In the Ying-lian passage the usher’s word for the previous magistrates is therefore not a native English word for “before,” but the Roman prefix of precedence grafted onto a Roman verb of departure—the language of imperial administration speaking through an English novel about imperial administration. Section 2 — the history of “de-” (the separative prefix) The second element of predecessor is the smallest and least visible: the Latin preposition de, “from, away, down.” Its Indo-European background is a reconstructed separative particle *de, one of the ancient deictic adverbs meaning roughly “away from here,” which Latin hardened into a preposition governing the ablative. In classical Latin de is everywhere: de caelo “from heaven,” de re publica “about the republic,” de muro “down from the wall.” The genius of Latin de lies in its ability to become a verbal prefix, and as a prefix it did three jobs: it marked removal or separation (decedere “to go away,” demovere “to move away”), it marked descent or downward motion (descendere “to climb down,” decidere “to fall down”), and it could mark intensity or completion (declarare “to make clear,” delere “to wipe out”). A single little preposition thus carried the full range of Latin thinking about departure, descent, and thoroughness. For predecessor the crucial formation is de + cedere = decedere, “to go away, to withdraw, to depart.” Roman writers used decedere of a general leaving a province, of a consul leaving office at the end of his term (de provincia decedere, “to leave one’s province”), and, with pathos, of a person departing life—to die. The verb thus already contained the whole administrative drama of succession: the man who goes away makes room. From its perfect stem came decessus, “departure, death,” and the agent noun decessor, “one who departs”—and, in the language of offices, “one who has left a post, a predecessor.” The prefix de- therefore supplies the crucial motion of “going away” that makes the office a place to be vacated; without it there would be no image of the earlier holder stepping down. It is worth noticing how different this metaphor is from the English-native way of talking about succession. English foregoer, forefather, and the phrasal “the man before you” imagine a line of people striding forward, each ahead of the next. Latin decedere imagines the outgoing man retreating or stepping down and away—departure rather than forward motion. When the Church used decessor and decessio for a departed predecessor (a deceased pope was a decessor), the “departure” sense shaded into “death,” and indeed predecessor has always carried a faint undertone of the dead and gone: your predecessor has stepped away, whether into retirement, promotion, or the grave. That undertone suits the Ying-lian passage, where the prior magistrates have effectively vanished from the record of justice, leaving only their collective non-action behind them. The tiny prefix de-, invisible to the casual reader, is the hinge of the whole word: it turns “the one before” into “the one who has gone away,” and so turns a chronological marker into an administrative history. Section 3 — the history of “cedere” (the go-and-yield verb) The body of predecessor is Latin cedere, “to go, to walk, to yield, to withdraw, to give way.” It is a verb of motion that became a verb of concession: the basic sense “to go” softened into “to yield,” “to retreat,” “to withdraw,” “to concede,” and Latin used it of everything from an army yielding ground to a debtor assigning property to an office-holder stepping aside. Its ultimate Indo-European ancestry is debated. The traditional handbooks propose a root *ked-, “to go, yield, fall,” but the evidence is thin, and some scholars treat cedere as an isolated Latin formation of uncertain ancestry; the honest summary is that the PIE stage, if it existed, is poorly attested, and the secure history of the word begins in Latin. What is not debated is the extraordinary productivity of cedere in Latin and in English. Latin built a small army of prefixed verbs on cedere, and every one of them survives in modern English: accedere “to go to” (accede), concedere “to go away with, to yield” (concede, concession), decedere “to depart” (the direct ancestor of predecessor), excedere “to go out” (exceed), intercedere “to come between” (intercede), praecedere “to go before” (precede), procedere “to go forward” (proceed, process), recedere “to go back” (recede), secedere “to go apart” (secede), succedere “to go up under, to follow” (succeed, successor). The verbs, through their perfect stems in -cess-, also gave English cession, process, recess, success, access, excess, and the nouns in -cession. The root’s meaning, “to go,” is so general that it functions as a mere carrier of prefixes—a grammatical motion-word on which Latin’s spatial prepositions ride, rather like the way English “go” and “put” absorb the phrasal particles come-go, out, up, down. The specific formation that matters is de- + cedere = decedere, and its agent noun decessor. In the administrative Latin of the Roman state, decedere had a technical ring: a provincial governor decessit e provincia, “went out of the province,” when his term ended; the official who had departed was remembered as the decessor, the outgoing office-holder. From decessor, Late Latin and medieval chancery formed praedēcessor, adding prae- “before” to make explicit that the outgoing man is also the earlier man: literally “one who has gone away before.” The word then crossed into French (predecesseur), into Middle English (predecessour), and into the administrative vocabulary of Europe, where it has never lost its original double motion—going away plus having done so earlier. In English its family has remained remarkably stable for six centuries: predecessors still means the earlier holders of the same place, and the usher’s use of it in the passage draws on that entire Roman history of provincial governors who, like the previous magistrates of the story, left their offices without leaving justice behind them. Succession, in this word’s bones, is not a line forward but a trail of departures.
