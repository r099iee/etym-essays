Poles, the plural of pole, names in its oldest and most concrete sense long straight shafts of wood or metal set upright in the ground — stakes, posts, and masts that hold something else aloft. In the source sentence the sense is the hop-garden one: coppice woods are cut to yield poles for training hop bines and charcoal for the powder-mills. Hops climb by nature, twining clockwise up whatever stands near, and the Surrey and Kent hop-grounds of Cobbett's day were forests of chestnut and ash poles, ten or twelve feet high, one or two to every hill of hops, replaced as they rotted.

Cobbett the farmer knew the economy behind the line: coppices cut on rotation supplied a cash crop of their own, and the hop-garden devoured poles by the thousand. A pole was graded by length and straightness, barked or left in bark, sharpened at the butt, and driven deep beside the young bine. Good poles lasted several seasons; poor ones snapped in the first autumn gale and dragged the whole bine down with them.

Core meaning in detail: A pole in this stake sense is a stripped sapling or cleft length, taller than a stake and thinner than a post — the hop-pole, the bean-pole, the telegraph pole, the scaffolding pole. It works by length and leverage: planted at one end, bearing weight or wire or climbing plant along its rise. From the bean rows of a cottage garden to the avenues of telegraph poles that later marched beside every railway, the object is one continuous family — timber stood upright to carry civilization a few feet higher.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Upright wooden stake for plants, fences, and wires (primary in this context, from Old English): hop-poles, bean-poles, telegraph and telephone poles. The bridge is direct continuity of form and function — the hop-pole's younger brothers carry copper wire instead of bines.
  • Ship's masts, spars, and oars (nautical): the bare poles of a dismasted ship, sailing under bare poles in a storm. The bridge runs through the shipwright's timber: masts are great poles stepped in the keel, and a vessel stripped to them is reduced to sticks against the sea.
  • The North and South Poles — ends of the earth's axis (geographical, from 14th c. via Latin): the frozen poles of explorers and atlas covers. The bridge is metaphorical and learned: ancient cosmographers imagined the heavens turning on an axis like a mill, its ends fixed at two points they called poli after the Greek pivot; geographers gave the same name to the earth's own ends.
  • Magnetic, electric, and celestial poles (scientific): the poles of a magnet or battery, the positive and negative poles of a cell. The bridge carries the axis-end image into physics: opposite ends where attraction concentrates and between which all action flows, so that every battery and dynamo inherits the geography of the globe.
  • Opposite extremes of opinion or character (figurative): politicians poles apart, temperaments at opposite poles. The bridge runs from geography through magnetism: just as the earth's poles are the farthest points from each other, so two doctrines can share one axis of argument while touching nowhere in between.
  • Vaulting, ski, and punt poles — sporting shafts (modern): the vaulter's fibreglass pole, the skier's paired sticks. The bridge returns to timber in the hand: a length of springy material gripped to lever the body higher, faster, or onward, heir to the leaping pole of ditch-jumping countrymen.

Register and connotation: poles in the coppice sense is earthy commercial English — timber measured by the hundred and priced by length. In the geographical sense it is heroic and absolute, the vocabulary of exploration and extremes. Between them the word spans the whole scale from a bean row to the planet. Note the spelling twin poll, head or vote, which is a different word entirely, though hop-poles are sharpened at the butt and never confused by countrymen.
