DOCTRINES (plural noun; singular doctrine) denotes a set of beliefs, principles, tenets, or teachings held and taught as authoritative by a philosophical, political, religious, economic, or legal system. The word names the intellectual substance of a system — the body of asserted propositions that a group holds to be true and transmits to its members — as opposed to its style, its practices, or its institutional machinery. Three overlapping senses must be distinguished: (1) religious doctrine, the official teaching of a church, creed, or confession (e.g., the doctrine of the Trinity, the doctrine of original sin); (2) political and economic doctrine, the body of principles asserted as valid by a party, state, or school (e.g., the Monroe Doctrine, the doctrine of laissez-faire, the Truman Doctrine); and (3) legal and philosophical doctrine, the settled rules or principles of a branch of law or of a philosophical system (e.g., the doctrine of precedent, the doctrine of consideration, the Cartesian doctrine of innate ideas). What unites the senses is the idea of an authoritative, codified teaching that is received and handed on — a doctrine is not an opinion one holds tentatively but a teaching one asserts as binding.

In the source context — J. S. Mill, writing that his published economic work "owed nothing to her, he tells us, in its doctrines" — the word is used in its intellectual and impersonal sense. Mill is making a careful, quasi-legal delimitation of intellectual ownership: the theoretical content of his economic treatise, the actual principles and analytical claims, was his own, whatever help he may have acknowledged in other respects. The word here points precisely to the substance of the system, not to its presentation or collaboration; "doctrines" is chosen because it denotes asserted principles with authority behind them — the settled analytical claims of a school — and so perfectly suits a scholar demarcating exactly which ideas are whose.

Full polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Religious teaching / creed: the official teaching of a church or faith, often formally defined and dogmatic; "religious doctrines," "the doctrines of the church." The historical first sense in English.
  • Political doctrine: a stated principle or body of principles guiding a state, party, or policy; named doctrines such as the Monroe Doctrine, the Truman Doctrine, the Bush Doctrine.
  • Economic doctrine: the theoretical principles of an economic school or system; "classical doctrines," "the doctrines of political economy" — the exact sense in the Mill passage.
  • Legal doctrine: a settled rule or principle of law developed by courts or stated by authorities; "the doctrine of precedent," "the doctrine of fair use," the canon-law "doctrine of ultramontanism."
  • Philosophical doctrine: a tenet or body of tenets maintained by a philosopher or school; "the doctrines of Plato," "the Socratic doctrine of virtue."
  • Extended / colloquial: loosely, any firmly held personal belief or maxim, often ironic ("his pet doctrines," "the doctrines of the office"); a weakened, non-institutional extension.

Derived forms and collocations: the adjective doctrinal ("of or concerning doctrine"), the nouns doctrinaire ("one who applies doctrine rigidly, often pejoratively," from French), doctrinarianism, and doctrinism. Fixed collocations include "religious doctrines," "political doctrines," "economic doctrines," "the doctrines of the faith," "official doctrine," "doctrine and dogma." Note the tell-tale difference from dogma: a doctrine is a teaching asserted by authority; a dogma is a doctrine so hardened that it is held unquestionably, the term carrying in modern use a pejorative of rigidity.

Register: the word is formal, intellectual, and institutional — the language of theology, law, politics, and scholarship — and is markedly at home in the measured prose of nineteenth-century political economy, where it names the analytical claims of a school with quasi-legal precision, exactly as Mill uses it.
