The story of antedates is the story of two Latin words meeting in English. Neither element is native Germanic; both were borrowed, one as a prefix and one as a noun that became a verb. Their union in the sixteenth century produced a word whose transparency is deceptive: it looks like a simple English compound, yet every part of it carries a long Romance and Indo-European history.

1. The ante lineage

The first element descends from Proto-Indo-European *h₂énti, a locative-adverbial form meaning “facing, in front, before,” built on the root *h₂en- “there, then.” Its Latin reflex is the preposition and adverb ante “before, in front of, earlier than,” attested from the earliest Latin and enormously productive: antecedere “to go before,” anteponere “to place before,” antiquus “former, ancient.” From Latin the word passed into the Romance languages (Italian avanti, French avant, Spanish antes) and into English as the bound prefix ante-, which Middle English adopted wholesale in formations such as antechamber, antecedent, and later antediluvian.

A sound note: Latin ante had a short a and a clean t; in English the prefix is normally reduced to /ænti-/ or /æntə-/, with the vowel of cat, and the e weakens to a schwa in rapid speech. The spelling ante- was fixed by Renaissance classicism, which restored Latin letters to words that had passed through French.

2. The date lineage

The second element is far more surprising. Latin dare “to give” (from PIE *deh₃-) produced the past participle datum “given.” In the stamped formula of Roman letters and documents, data (“things given,” neuter plural) came to mean the place and time from which a letter was dispatched — the “given” circumstances. Old French trimmed this to date, and Middle English borrowed it in the fourteenth century as the word for the time at which something happens or is written. The semantic leap is a textbook metonymy: the written formula that recorded the time became the name of the time itself.

3. Compound and first attestation

English speakers of the late sixteenth century combined the prefix and the noun into antedate, initially in the concrete documentary sense “to put an earlier date upon a letter, deed, or record than the true one.” The verb is recorded from the 1580s, in the same period that produced postdate and a cluster of other administrative compounds. The noun antedate (a date earlier than the true one) is rarer but equally old. The third-person antedates is simply the regular inflection, appearing wherever the verb was used.

4. Semantic drift

  • 1580s–1700: documentary backdating, legal and epistolary, often implying deliberate falsification.
  • 17th–18th century: extension to “be earlier than” in antiquarian, chronological, and natural-historical writing; the neutral sense grows alongside the old suspicious one.
  • 19th century: the verb becomes a workhorse of geology, palaeontology, and comparative philology, where strata, fossils, and reconstructed languages are constantly said to antedate one another.
  • 20th–21st century: the neutral sense dominates in academic prose, while the documentary sense survives in law and bibliography; predate, coined in the same era, has largely taken over the everyday register.

5. Literary and scholarly attestations

Nineteenth-century historical prose is full of the word: Victorian historians argue that a custom antedates the Conquest, that a charter is antedated, that a myth antedates the alphabet. In twentieth-century linguistics the reconstructed ancestor is said to antedate the earliest records — precisely the usage in the source sentence, where “the primitive Teutonic ... antedates the earliest written records.” Darwinian biology and stratigraphy gave the verb a new empirical dignity: to antedate is to make a claim about deep time that fossil evidence can test.

6. Socio-cultural context

Behind the word lies the rise of documentary culture. Medieval and early modern chanceries dated every instrument, and the authenticity of a date could decide the outcome of a lawsuit or a dynastic claim; hence the sharp legal interest in antedating and postdating. The scientific revolution added a second context, in which chronology became measurable and contestable. Antedates thus carries the fingerprints of both the archive and the field notebook.

Its formal flavour today is a residue of that double ancestry: it belongs to people who care about evidence, sequence, and proof.
