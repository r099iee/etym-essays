- [13th c. "savor"] - From Old French savour / Latin sapor "taste"; originally purely literal, meaning to perceive taste or smell, or to give flavor to food.
- [16th c. figurative shift] - Extended beyond the senses: to "savour" an idea, honor, or prospect meant to enjoy it mentally with lingering appreciation.
- [18th-19th c.] - Increasingly tied to refined, deliberate enjoyment; "savour" suggested slow, discriminating pleasure rather than casual liking.
- [Contemporary Sense] - Means to enjoy slowly and fully, drawing out every drop of pleasure from an experience; the British spelling "savour" keeps its literary, elegant register.
