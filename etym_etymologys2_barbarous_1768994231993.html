SINGLE ROOT CHAIN (NON-PIE, onomatopoeic): Onomatopoeic reduplicative base *bar-bar- "imitative reproduction of unintelligible speech" (pre-Greek; no Indo-European ancestry — the template was formed independently wherever speakers met speech they could not parse, cf. Sanskrit barbara- and Hindi बर्बर "barbarian," and English "blah-blah") → Greek barbaróphōnos (βαρβαρόφωνος) "bar-bar-speaking, of unintelligible speech" (Homeric, applied to the Carians in the Iliad, c. 8th century BCE) → Greek bárbaros (βάρβαρος) "non-Greek, foreign; uncivilized, rude" (5th century BCE; Herodotus generalizes it to all non-Greeks) → Latin barbarus "foreign, savage, uncultured, uncultivated" (borrowed from Greek, 2nd century BCE) → English barbarous "foreign; uncivilized; cruel" (15th century, formed from Latin barbarus + the adjectival suffix -ous, possibly via Old French barbareus / barbarous) → barbarous (the card's root word). NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: The chain from Greek bárbaros onward is secure and well attested; the only open question is whether the Greek word reflects a real foreign loan or a purely imitative coinage within Greek. The overwhelming consensus is imitative: the reduplication bar-bar reproduces the sound of unintelligible speech, exactly as Sanskrit barbara- "stammering, lisping" does independently — the two are parallel formations on the same sound-symbolic template, not cognates in the Indo-European sense, since no PIE root can be reconstructed. The parallel Sanskrit/Hindi formation (बर्बर barbar "barbarian") is included here as an independent echo of the same acoustic intuition. Related English loans built on the same Latin/Greek material include barbarian (16th c., via Latin barbaria "foreign land"), barbarism (16th c., "uncivilized condition; an impropriety or mixing of speech"), barbarity (18th c., "extreme cruelty"), and barbaric (19th c., via French barbarique).
