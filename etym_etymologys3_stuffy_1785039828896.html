The story of STUFFY is really the story of the verb stuff — and of the astonishing career of a word that travelled from plugging leaks in boats to describing prim professors and congested noses. The underlying verbal base is Germanic: Proto-Germanic *stoppōną "to plug, stop up," attested in Old High German stopfon (German stopfen "to stuff, plug, darn"), Old English -stoppian (in forstoppian "to stop up, stifle"), and the English verb stop itself. The image is of cramming a hole full — stopping a gap with a plug. Latin made its own verb, stuppāre "to stop with tow," from stuppa "coarse flax, tow, oakum" (a Greek loanword), and this areal network of "plugging" words reinforced one another across Europe; it is no accident that stop, stopper, and stuff all cluster around the same physical act of filling and blocking.

From this Germanic-Latin base, Old French developed estoffer "to equip, stock, pad, quilt" and the noun estoffe — the quilted padding worn under armor. English borrowed the noun as stuffe in the early 14th century, and from "quilted padding" the word generalized with remarkable speed: by about 1400 it meant "material for working with" and "military stores"; by the mid-15th century "goods and possessions"; then "substance or matter of an unspecified kind" — the all-purpose stuff of everyday speech, which by 1570 could even mean "worthless ideas" (in stuff and nonsense) and, by 1929 in American slang, "narcotics, dope." The verb stuffen followed in parallel: "to furnish," "to pad," "to cram full," "to stuff a fowl," "to clog the sinuses" (recorded early 15th century), "to fill a specimen in taxidermy."

  • The adjective is born (16th century): around 1560 the suffix -y — meaning "full of, characterized by" — produced stuffy, which at first simply meant "full of stuff, substantial" (a stuffy cushion, a stuffy meal). This literal sense is now obsolete but it is the semantic seed of everything that followed.
  • The modern physical sense (19th century): in 1831 stuffy is recorded applying to rooms and buildings that are "poorly ventilated, musty from closeness." The bridge is the old image of plugging: a room whose doors and windows are sealed, whose air is crammed in and cannot turn over, is literally "stuffed up" with its own dead air.
  • The person-sense (mid-19th century): at about the same time (recorded from the 1850s onward) the word transferred from air to temperament: a stuffy person is stiff, prim, formal, strait-laced, old-fashioned, humorlessly conventional — a mind sealed against fresh ideas exactly as a stuffy room is sealed against fresh air. The metaphor is the same one that gives us "stuffy institution" and "stuffy etiquette."
  • The congested nose (20th century): a "stuffy nose" completes the semantic family: the sinuses are plugged up, crammed with congestion, sealed against free breathing — the identical image, applied to the body. This sense is now as common as the room-sense.
  • Tolkien's usage: in The Hobbit, "most horribly stuffy" fuses the physical and moral registers at once: the air is literally close and stale in the goblins' tunnels, and the word also whispers of the moral oppression of the underworld — the sealed, lightless, freedomless realm to which the company is being carried. The homely word makes the horror intimate.

Sound-shift notes: the adjective is a simple native English derivation, stuff + -y, with the familiar shortening of the vowel in the closed syllable (stuff /stʌf/ → stuffy /ˈstʌfi/). The interesting linguistic history is entirely semantic: a word that began as "plugging and padding" in the workshops of early medieval Europe ended up, in under two centuries, describing everything from baked-in air to pompous dons. No English word better demonstrates how a single image of "cramming shut" can grow a whole family of meanings.
