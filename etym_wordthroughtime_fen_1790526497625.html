- 8th to 11th Century (Old English) - *Fenn* represented an eerie, desolate, and dangerous marshland wilderness; featured prominently in Anglo-Saxon heroic poetry (such as *Beowulf*) as the monstrous abode of demons, outlaws, and supernatural beasts like Grendel.
- 12th to 15th Century (Middle English) - Shifted from mythological terror toward an everyday geographical, agricultural, and legal descriptor for low-lying wetland tracts across medieval Britain, giving rise to numerous local surnames (e.g., *Fenn*, *Fenton*) and parish boundary markers.
- 16th to 17th Century (The Drainage of the Fens) - The Fens of East Anglia became the epicenter of massive engineering projects led by Dutch drainage experts like Cornelius Vermuyden, provoking fierce social rebellion from the indigenous inhabitants known as "Fen Tigers" who defended their traditional fishing and wildfowling livelihood.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Literature) - Adopted by poets and novelists (William Wordsworth, Alfred Lord Tennyson, Charles Dickens) as a potent atmospheric symbol of melancholy isolation, dreary expanse, and primeval English wildness (e.g., Wordsworth's *London, 1802*: "she is a fen / Of stagnant waters").
- 20th to 21st Century - Formalized in modern wetland ecology as a precise scientific classification for minerotrophic peatlands, alongside global conservation and re-wilding initiatives aimed at restoring historic fenland biodiversity.
