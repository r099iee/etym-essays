Kang is a Sinitic (Sino-Tibetan) word; it has NO PIE ancestry and was borrowed into English in the nineteenth century. The chain below traces the root from reconstructed Sino-Tibetan to the English loanword. (5 attested stages.) 1. Proto-Sino-Tibetan *kaŋ / *kam "to dry, to dry by heat" — a reconstructed root of fire-drying; not directly attested, but inferred from cognates such as Written Tibetan skam "to dry" and skem "to make dry", and from related Tibeto-Burman forms meaning "dry" across the Lolo-Burmese and Kuki-Chin branches. 2. → Old Chinese *kʰˤaŋ-s (Baxter–Sagart reconstruction; Schuessler gives *khāŋ) 炕 "to dry by fire; a heated platform-bed" — written with the fire radical 火 and the phonetic 亢; the verb sense "to dry by fire" is the older, primary sense. 3. → Middle Chinese *khang (departing tone) 炕 — "to dry by fire; the kang platform". 4. → Mandarin kàng (炕) — "the heated brick platform-bed; to dry or roast (grain, cloth) over a fire", the modern standard form of the word. 5. → English kang (also spelled k'ang or khang in nineteenth-century travel and missionary literature; fixed as kang in modern pinyin) — "the heated brick platform-bed of northern China". Final node: English KANG (rootWord) — the loanword, 19th century to present. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The reconstruction of the Proto-Sino-Tibetan root *kaŋ/*kam and its connection with the Tibeto-Burman "dry" forms are inferred rather than directly attested; the Sino-Tibetan family itself is not fully reconstructed, and the internal reconstruction follows Baxter–Sagart while Schuessler gives *khāŋ. (2) The homophonous character 亢 kàng "high, extreme" is a different word, unrelated to the heating sense, and serves in 炕 only as a phonetic. (3) The English form has no native derivatives; only the nineteenth-century gloss "kang bed-stove" occurs.
