**Two roots and a hyphen: the biography of RE-PERUSAL** The word RE-PERUSAL is a meeting of three histories: the history of the Latin prefix re-, the history of the Latin preposition per and its intensive force, and the history of the verb "to use." Each brought its own cargo of meaning, and the compound they formed in the nineteenth century is a small monument to the learned reading culture of the age. **The prefix re-: turning back and doing again** The first component, re-, is one of the oldest and most productive formative elements of Latin. Its classical form was re-, or before vowels and certain consonants red-, as in redīre, "to go back," and reddere, "to give back." Its ultimate ancestry is uncertain: historical linguists have traditionally connected it with the Indo-European root *wert-, "to turn," the same root that yields Sanskrit vártate, "he turns," Latin vertere, "to turn," and the Germanic words for "to become" (German werden) and the English suffix -ward, "turned toward." The underlying idea would be that "to turn back" is the primitive sense behind "again" and "reversed." But some scholars regard the Latin prefix as an Italic isolate of obscure origin, and the connection to *wert- is a reconstruction, not an attested stage. What is certain is the Latin fact: by classical times re- was fully grammaticalized with a stable range of meanings — "back" (motion or direction), "again" (repetition), "against" (opposition), and "anew" (restoration). English inherited the prefix wholesale in thousands of words — return, recover, reconsider, reconstruct — and still treats it as living, freely joining native verbs and nouns to express doing something over again. In re-perusal it carries exactly this sense: a perusal undertaken once more, deliberately, from the beginning to the end of a text. **The per of perusal: through, thoroughly** The second component, perusal, descends from a different Indo-European line. Its core prefix per- comes from the PIE root *per(i)-, "through, forward, beyond," which is visible in Sanskrit pári, Greek perí, and the Germanic words for, forth, and fore; in Latin it appears as the preposition per, "through, throughout," and as an intensive prefix that lends verbs the sense of carrying an action to completion — perficere, "to complete," pervertere, "to turn completely upside down." This "thoroughly" force is the key to the whole family: Latin per- does not merely mean "very," but "right through to the end." The stage between the PIE root and the Latin prefix is regular and well attested; the semantic chain from "through" to "thoroughly" is one of the best-documented developments in Latin word-formation. **User, peruser, peruse, perusal: from using up to reading through** The third strand is the verb of using. Latin ūtor, ūtī, ūsus, "to use, employ, enjoy," produced the noun ūsus, "use, practice," whence Old French user, "to use." The origin of ūtor itself is disputed — some connect it with an Indo-European root of "use" or "enjoy," others treat it as an Italic formation — but its Roman pedigree is certain. In Anglo-Norman and Old French, user combined with the intensive per- to form peruser, "to use up, exhaust, finish": the earliest sense of the verb was physical and destructive, to consume or wear a thing through. From "to use up" it was a natural step to "to go through, to exhaust the contents of," and thence to the sense of reading: to peruse a book was to go through it completely, to read it from beginning to end, to use it up. Middle English took the word in the late fifteenth century — perusen, first recorded around 1470 — in exactly this sense of complete examination, and in the sixteenth century the noun perusal was formed with the suffix -al, meaning "the action of reading through carefully and completely." The Renaissance, the age of humanist scholarship and of print, made perusal one of the standard words of the learned world: to peruse a text was to read it in full, to master it, to know its every point. **The compound and the drift of the modern age** Re-perusal, the compound, joined the two halves in the language of nineteenth-century scholarship. The hyphen — re-perusal rather than reperusal — is itself significant: it keeps the two elements visible, insisting that we register both the "again" and the "thoroughly." In the Mill biography the word stands for the highest standard of classical learning: before reviewing Grote's History of Greece, Mill re-read the Iliad and the Odyssey in the original Greek, and the biographer notes, with deliberate understatement, that this labour "were recreation to the student." The word chosen to carry that claim is deliberately formal and old-fashioned, a marker of the scholarly world in which re-perusal was simply what a serious reviewer did. In the twentieth century the compound's root verb drifted. Ordinary usage weakened peruse toward "read casually, browse, skim" — the familiar "please peruse the menu" — so that perusal, once a word of complete attention, began to mean a lazy glance. Re-perusal, precisely because of its learned, hyphenated shape, has largely escaped the drift, and it survives where a writer wants the precise, unambiguously serious sense of a repeated, thorough reading. The word thus preserves, like a fossil in amber, the Renaissance conviction that to read a book properly is to go through it completely — and that to read it properly twice is the mark of a true scholar. It is a word whose formality is not an accident but the point: no one re-peruses the menu; one re-peruses Homer in the original Greek, and calls it recreation.
