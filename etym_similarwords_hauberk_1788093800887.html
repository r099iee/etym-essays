• Coat of mail: Similar because both denote the mail garment protecting the torso; differs because "coat of mail" is the descriptive English phrase for mail body-armour of any length, while a true hauberk is specifically knee-length, slit for riding, and in strict medieval usage longer than a haubergeon.
  • Byrnie: Similar because both are the old words for a mail shirt; differs because byrnie is the Anglo-Saxon and Norse term (Beowulf's armour) for the mail corselet, typically shorter and less precisely defined than the later Continental hauberk.
  • Mail shirt / shirt of mail: Similar because both name a garment of interlinked rings; differs because these generic terms lack the hauberk's defining length and its knightly, Continental history.
  • Chain mail: Similar because both denote the armour of interlocked rings; differs because chain mail names the material and system (the fabric of rings), while hauberk names the specific long garment made of it.
  • Haubergeon: Similar because both are the same French-derived word family; differs because the haubergeon was the shorter, lighter version reaching only to the hips or thighs — the "little hauberk" — often worn by squires, sergeants or for skirmishing.
  • Habergeon: Similar because both descend from the same Germanic compound; differs because habergeon is the alternative English spelling of the shorter mail coat (Old French haubergeon), and medieval inventories and romances regularly contrast it with the full-length hauberk.
  • Cuirass: Similar because both protect the torso; differs because a cuirass is a rigid breast-and-back piece of plate, while a hauberk is flexible mail.
  • Lorica: Similar because both are classical/medieval terms for body armour; differs because lorica is the Roman legionary's armour (including lorica hamata, the mail type), centuries older and empire-scaled, while hauberk is the medieval knightly garment.
  • Brigandine: Similar because both are medieval torso defences; differs because a brigandine (14th century onward) is a cloth or leather jacket lined with small plates, while the hauberk is entirely of rings.
  • Scale armour: Similar because both can be the material of a knee-length coat (early hauberks were sometimes of scales); differs because scale armour names the overlapping-plate system, while hauberk names the coat itself whatever its rings or scales.
  • Panoply: Similar because both belong to the world of full armour; differs because panoply is the complete suit and equipment of a warrior, or its display, not a single garment.
