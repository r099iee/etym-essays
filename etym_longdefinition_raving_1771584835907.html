Raving is the present participle of the verb to rave, pressed into service as an adjective (and as an intensifying adverb) to describe speech or behaviour that is loud, wild, incoherent, and apparently senseless — the outward sign of delirium, madness, or extreme emotion. In the warning about Bao-yu that reaches Dai-yu, “He can be all honey-sweet words one minute and ranting and raving like a lunatic the next,” the word does the heavy work of the sentence's contrast. The phrase “honey-sweet words” conjures a voice of calculated charm and flattery; “ranting and raving” flips that same voice into its opposite, a stream of loud, ungovernable, meaningless noise. The tone of the passage is fearful, maternal, and cautionary: the speaker is not condemning Bao-yu with moral severity but warning Dai-yu, with alarm and pity, that his emotional volatility crosses into something that resembles madness and cannot be reasoned with. “Raving like a lunatic” frames his behaviour not as anger or argument but as irrational seizure — speech that has abandoned logic and must simply be ignored. The word thus establishes the “nature queer” that the same chapter celebrates as charm, but seen here from its frightening side: a mind in motion too fast for the world to follow. Primary senses.
  • Adjective: talking wildly — of speech or a speaker, wildly incoherent, as if mad or delirious; the card's sense, and the oldest adjectival use.
  • Adjective, intensifying — used to intensify a following adjective of madness or excess: raving mad, raving lunatic; also of things extreme, “a raving success”. The intensifier hardens in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
  • Adverb — in raving mad, “utterly and spectacularly mad”; the adverbial use of the intensifier.
  • Noun (plural: ravings) — the wild, incoherent utterances of a madman or of a person in delirium; “the ravings of a fever”.
  • Positive inversion: raving beauty — since the nineteenth century, a person so dazzlingly beautiful as to border on the unbelievable; the same word of excess turned from fear to admiration.
  • Of the natural world: raving storm / raving sea — raging, roaring, violent; the verb has long been used of wind and water in fury.
  • Modern: raving at a rave — since the 1980s and 1990s, dancing energetically at an all-night electronic dance-music party (a rave); the noun rave and the agent noun raver are the newer members of the family.
The verb to rave and its boundaries. As a verb, to rave means to talk with wild, incoherent enthusiasm or fury; to speak in a frenzy; to be beside oneself. The verb can take a positive turn — to rave about something is to praise it with wild enthusiasm, and “rave reviews” are ecstatic reviews — and it is exactly this double face (mad fury or ecstatic praise) that makes raving such a volatile word. Its near-synonyms draw precise boundaries. Ranting is loud and angry but still coherent: the ranter has a grievance and presses it with repetitive fury, whereas the raver has lost the thread of sense altogether. Babbling is weaker and more pitiable — aimless, childish, weak talk — not the violent shouting of raving. Delirious names the feverish mental state itself, whereas raving names the manner of the speech; one can be delirious and silent, but one cannot rave in silence. Frenzied describes frantic uncontrolled activity generally, while raving is specifically about the mouth. The pairing “ranting and raving” is a fixed, alliterative idiom (since the eighteenth century) for loud, empty, angry noise. The intensifier and its social history. Perhaps the most distinctive life of raving is as an intensifier. Since the sixteenth century, “raving mad” has meant mad beyond all ordinary measure, and “a raving lunatic” has become a set phrase for someone whose madness is spectacular and unmissable. In the nineteenth century the intensifying power was bent to admiration: a “raving beauty” is someone whose beauty is so extreme as to seem incredible, and the phrase preserves the ancient logic that the word always carries — whatever it touches is at the far end of the scale. In contemporary slang the noun rave and its participle have acquired a new lease of life from the acid-house and electronic dance-music culture of the late 1980s, so that a rave is an all-night party and to rave is to dance at one; the participle raving is even used of the party itself (“a raving night”). The word has thus kept, across seven centuries, its core of excessive, ungovernable energy — always loud, always at the extreme, whether the extreme be madness, fury, praise, beauty, or dancing.
