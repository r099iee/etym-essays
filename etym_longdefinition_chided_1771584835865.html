Chided is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb to chide, which means "to scold, rebuke, or find fault with someone - but mildly, briefly, and usually with an undertone of affection or care rather than anger." In the scene from which this card is drawn - Cao Xueqin's The Story of the Stone in the translation of David Hawkes - the decisive word is the adverb indulgently: "Grandmother Jia chided indulgently on seeing Bao-yu back again." Her reproof, "Fancy changing your clothes before you have welcomed the visitor!", is a verbal tap, not a blow. A chiding presupposes intimacy: it is the sort of correction that only a grandmother, a mother, or a close friend may administer without causing offence, because the affection that frames it cancels the sting of the criticism. The word thus carries the whole social weather of the moment. Bao-yu has committed a genuine breach of etiquette - a guest must be received promptly - and yet the "scolding" marks him, paradoxically, as a favourite. It is a privilege to be chided by the Matriarch: her chiding is the sound of special standing, the warm murmur of a household where the darling grandson can be gently faulted and simultaneously forgiven in the same breath. The mildness of the verb is what makes the exchange tender rather than tense, and it quietly signals that Grandmother Jia's authority is exercised through love, not fear. Core senses.
  • Transitive: to scold, rebuke, or reprove someone. The modern core sense, typically mild: "She chided her child for forgetting to say please." The object is the person corrected; the tone is gentler than scold, severer than tease.
  • Intransitive: to complain, murmur, rail. The older, historical sense, now literary or obsolete: to chide against something or to chide with someone was to quarrel or complain aloud, without necessarily addressing the offender. "The wind chides" and "the waves chide" are poetic survivals of this intransitive use.
  • Chiding (verbal noun and adjective). The noun "a chiding" is the act of reproof, and "chiding" as an adjective describes fault-finding or complaining speech; a "chiding wind" is a wind that scolds.
Polysemy, register, and the historical bridges.
  • From "quarrel" to "scold": the narrowing bridge. Old English cīdan meant "to strive, contend, quarrel" - a two-sided fight of words. By the Middle English period (from c. 1175) the sense narrowed to "to scold, reprove," a one-sided correction in which an authority addresses the erring. The bridge is the shared picture of loud complaint: first two voices at odds, then one voice finding fault with another.
  • The mildness, a later development. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries chide could cover reproof of every intensity, from a courtier's sharp rebuke to a lover's gentle teasing. The modern softness - a loving, brief, corrective word - is largely a nineteenth- and twentieth-century drift, as scold took over the loud end of the family and chide retreated to the tender register, becoming increasingly bookish and literary.
  • Archaic strong forms. Because chide was early attracted into the strong conjugation (on the model of ride/rode/ridden), older writers use chid for the past tense (Shakespeare) and chidden for the participle, forms now archaic or purely literary. The standard modern past is the regular chided, the form of this card's rootWord.
  • Chide of nature and of the heart. A distinctive literary extension lets winds, seas, conscience, and parents all "chide": the word lends human reproach to natural and inner forces, giving chided a poetic afterglow even in prose.
Where chide sits among its family. Chide is the gentle member of the "scold" words. To scold is louder, longer, and more public - a sustained flood of complaint that blames the person; chide corrects the act. To rebuke or reprimand is formal, official, hierarchical - the language of the headmistress and the magistrate. Admonish warns for the future; reproach expresses wounded disappointment; upbraid is hot and wordy; berate is the loudest, most continuous scolding of all. What distinguishes chide is intimacy and mildness: a chiding presupposes a relationship warm enough to absorb the rebuke. It is, in short, the word of the grandmother - the affectionate tap that says "you are loved, but you must do better," which is exactly the social meaning the passage depends on.
