- [Old English-Middle English: springing water] - the verb's first English sense is wholly literal and elemental: water wells up out of the earth, rising of its own accord from springs and hidden depths.
- [16th-18th century: tears and fluids] - the sense widens to bodily liquids: tears well up in the eyes, blood wells from a wound — the rise is involuntary, steady, and unstoppable.
- [18th-19th century: emotions rise] - by metaphor the word reaches the heart: pity, understanding, and anger well up, mounting from hidden depths as naturally as spring-water.
- [Contemporary: quiet abundance] - modern use keeps the verb almost always paired with 'up' and prefers it for feelings that swell gently and generously rather than burst — a deliberate opposite of gushing, surging, or flooding.
