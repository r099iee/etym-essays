blotted is the past tense and past participle of the verb blot — "to stain or spot, especially with ink; to smear or disfigure with a blot; to dry or absorb with blotting paper; and, in the phrasal verb blot out, to erase, obliterate or conceal utterly". It is also a participial adjective: "a blotted page", "blotted copy". The noun blot ("a spot, stain, disgrace"), first attested in English around 1300, is the source of the verb, which appears in the early fifteenth century.

Sense 1 — literal: stained or spotted with ink or similar liquid.

  • Ink spilled, dropped or smudged: "blotted manuscripts", "a blotted signature", "blotted pages of a letter".
  • By extension, any absorbed stain: "blotted with tears" (a page spoiled by crying), "blotted with blood".
  • The obverse sense, "to dry with blotting paper": since the sixteenth century, blotting paper absorbs excess ink, so "she blotted the page" means dabbed it dry — a moisture-removing action coexisting with the staining one. The bridge is that both belong to ink and paper: the same noun named the stain and the absorbent paper that removed it.

Sense 2 — figurative: to disgrace, tarnish, sully. From the sixteenth century a blot is a moral stain: "a blot on his name", "a blot on the escutcheon", "the blot of slavery". As an adjective, "blotted" describes honour or reputation that has been stained. The bridge is the oldest of metaphors — cleanliness of the page for purity of character, its disfigurement for disgrace.

Sense 3 — phrasal: blotted out — erased, obliterated, concealed. To "blot out" originally meant to cancel written words by covering them with ink; by transfer it came to mean to destroy or remove utterly:

  • Of writing or records: "blotted out the entry", "blotted out of the ledger".
  • Of sins and offences (biblical): "blot out my transgressions" (Psalm 51:1, KJV), "blotting out the handwriting of ordinances" (Colossians 2:14) — divine erasure of guilt.
  • Of names and memories: names blotted from the book of life (Revelation 3:5); "the memory was blotted out".
  • Of light and sight: darkness that blots out the stars; in Tolkien, "Now it was lost and gone, blotted in the dark" — the thing is swallowed, rendered invisible, annihilated to perception.

Sense 4 — modern technical: the blot in molecular biology. Since the 1970s the noun blot names laboratory techniques that transfer DNA, RNA or protein onto a membrane for detection: the Southern blot (1975, named for its inventor Edwin Southern), the Northern blot and the Western blot. The jargon puns on the original stain sense — the transferred material is visualised as a spot or band. "Blotted" in this context appears in laboratory prose ("the membrane was blotted and probed").

Register and grammar. The literal sense of "blotted" is standard in all registers; the figurative sense is literary; "blotted out" is the high-register idiom for total erasure, frequent in poetry, fantasy and journalism ("blotted out of history"). Note the unrelated homonym in backgammon, where a blot is an exposed piece liable to capture (from Dutch bloot "bare, exposed").
