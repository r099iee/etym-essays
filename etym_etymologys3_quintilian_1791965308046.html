Quintilian is a surname before it is a celebrity. The Roman name Marcus Fabius Quintilianus is built from three ordinary components: a praenomen, a clan name and a family cognomen. The last of these, Quintilianus, is an adjective in -iānus, the suffix of belonging (as in Julianus, “of Julius”), attached to the nomen Quintilius. Quintilius in turn is a gentilicial formation on the praenomen Quintus, “fifth”, from Latin quīntus and the Indo-European numeral *penkʷe, “five”. The name thus began as a label of descent — “one of the Quintilii” — and only later attached itself to the most famous member of that name.

The numerals behind Roman names. Early Roman praenomina included Primus, Secundus, Tertius, Quartus, Quintus, Sextus and Decimus, and it is often assumed that they recorded the order of birth. By the historical period they were inherited names, repeated within families and carrying no arithmetic meaning. Quintilian was not “the fifth” anything; his name is a fossil of an older naming habit. The same is true of the clan name Quintilius, and of the later imperial family the Quinctilii, whose most famous son, Publius Quinctilius Varus, lost three legions in the Teutoburg Forest in 9 CE.

Orthography and transmission of the name. Latin inscriptions and manuscripts show both Quintilius and Quinctilius, and the relation between the two spellings is a matter of gentilicial convention and scribal habit rather than a difference of meaning. The English Quintilian drops the Latin ending, as English does with Cicero, Virgil and Horace; it appears in the sixteenth century, probably by way of French Quintilien or directly from humanist Latin. The adjective Quintilian is used attributively (“Quintilian rhetoric”), and scholars occasionally write Quintilianic for doctrines characteristic of him.

The man and his moment. Quintilian’s career belongs to the Flavian settlement of education. Vespasian’s endowment of a public chair of rhetoric around 71 CE made the teaching of eloquence an imperial concern, and Quintilian’s 100,000-sesterce salary was a visible statement that Rome wanted trained speakers, not merely private tutors’ clients. His later appointment as tutor to Domitian’s designated heirs tied him to the regime’s dynastic hopes, and the consular ornaments he received marked his standing at court. He retired from teaching and spent his last years on the Institutio. The Institutio is therefore both a literary masterpiece and a policy document: it describes the education a governing class should receive.

The Institutio as a cultural programme. Quintilian’s twelve books move from the nursery to the law court, and their central claim is moral: vir bonus dicendi peritus, the good man skilled in speaking. The formula is older than Quintilian — he takes it from Cato the Elder — but he makes it the organising principle of the whole art. Eloquence is not a technique for winning, it is the public exercise of character; the orator must know philosophy, history, law and literature because he must be able to advise on everything. This is why the Institutio became the humanists’ favourite ancient text on education: it justifies a broad liberal curriculum in the name of civic virtue.

  • The good man speaking well: Cato’s formula, adopted by Quintilian, makes moral character the prerequisite of eloquence and turns rhetoric into an instrument of civic responsibility rather than a technique of winning.
  • Education from the cradle: Book 1 treats infancy, nurses, play, grammar and the supporting arts, insisting that the foundations of eloquence are laid long before the boy enters the rhetorical school.
  • Imitation without servility: Book 10 surveys the Greek and Latin canon and teaches the pupil to imitate many models, not to copy one — a warning against the narrower Ciceronianism of later centuries.
  • The complete orator: Book 12 requires philosophy, history, law and ethics, so that the speaker can advise on war, peace and the good life, not merely plead a case.

Textual recovery. The Middle Ages knew Quintilian mainly through excerpts and citations; the complete Institutio survived in a manuscript at the monastery of St. Gall, which Poggio Bracciolini recovered in 1416 during the Council of Constance. The discovery was one of the founding events of Renaissance humanism. Printed at Rome in 1470, the text circulated widely and was mined by educators from Guarino and Vittorino da Feltre to Erasmus and the Jesuit authors of the Ratio studiorum. Its Book 10, with its graded survey of Greek and Latin authors, helped fix the Western literary canon; its Book 1 shaped ideas about childhood, play and punishment for centuries.

Modern reference. Today “Quintilian” names a historical figure and a tradition rather than a living authority. He is cited in the history of rhetoric, of education and of literary criticism; his name signals the ideal of the morally serious, broadly educated speaker and the long European conviction that eloquence and virtue belong together. Bryant’s pairing of Cicero, Quintilian and Aristotle is a precise summary of that inheritance: the model, the method and the philosophy.
