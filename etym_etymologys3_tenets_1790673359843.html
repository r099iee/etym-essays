The story of tenets is the story of a Latin verb-form frozen into an English noun. It begins with one of Indo-European’s hardest-working roots, PIE *ten- “to stretch,” whose semantic field unites tension, extension, thinness, and holding: what is stretched is held taut. From it come Greek teinō (“stretch”), Sanskrit tanoti (“stretch, extend”), and, via Proto-Italic, Latin tenēre “to hold, keep, grasp.” Latin tenēre was enormously productive — continēre (contain), sustinēre (sustain), tenax (tenacious), tenor (course), tenant- in legal French — but the form that matters here is the plain third-person singular present tenet, “he/she holds.”

In classical Latin tenet was just a verb: tenet sententiam meant “he holds an opinion.” The nominalization happened in the medieval schools. Scholastic disputation required masters to state precisely which propositions they would defend, and the formula tenet quod… (“he holds that…”) or tenet dominus… became a label: the tenet was the thesis itself, the thing held. By the 14th century theologians could speak of teneta fidei, the “held things of the faith,” and the step from verb to neuter-like noun was complete. This is why the English noun keeps the odd third-person ending: it is a quotation fossil, a piece of classroom Latin carried over whole.

Early Modern English borrowed it during the age of confessional strife:

  • 1600s theological badge: First OED attestations cluster around controversy — “the tenets of the Papists,” “the tenets of the Arminians.” To name an opponent’s tenets was to itemize error; to recite one’s own was to confess loyalty. The plural dominates from the start because sects were defined by bundles.
  • 1700s political transfer: Whig and Tory pamphleteers, then American revolutionaries, wrote of political tenets, democratizing a church word for party creeds and constitutional first principles.
  • 1800s institutional diffusion: With universities, professions, and sciences codifying themselves, every discipline acquired “tenets” — tenets of geology, of political economy, of nursing — a sign of the era’s faith in teachable foundations.
  • 1900s–2000s ironic flattening: Management literature and lifestyle journalism now proclaim “our tenets” of design thinking or slow food, a usage purists find inflated but which testifies to the word’s prestige: calling a preference a tenet borrows gravity.

Phonetically almost nothing happened: Latin /ˈtenet/ became English /ˈtɛnɪt/ by the regular shortening and reduction of the second vowel, with plural /ˈtɛnɪts/. Orthographically the danger is tenent (Latin “they hold”), a hypercorrection that appears in manuscripts from the 17th century onward and is still the commonest misspelling; careful writers preserve the singular-verb fossil. Semantically the drift is from act (holding) to object (thing held) to badge (thing that marks who holds it). That is why the source sentence’s “behaved according to these tenets” feels slightly accusatory: tenets demand consistency, and professing them while acting otherwise is hypocrisy in a way that merely “having ideas” is not. Literary attestations — Milton’s anti-prelatical tenets, Hume’s sceptical handling of “vulgar tenets,” Orwell’s dissection of the tenets of doublethink — all exploit this normative bite. To master the word is to hear both its promise (principled coherence) and its threat (dogmatic rigidity).
