horny-plated is a hyphenated compound adjective formed from horny ("made of, or resembling, the hard translucent keratin of horn") and plated ("covered with flat sheets or scales of a hard substance"). Its composite sense is: covered with rigid, armor-like sections made of keratin, fitted edge to edge like the plates of a cuirass or the scutes of a reptile.

Primary sense and the passage

In the passage the word describes the vortlups — the grotesque Martian pack-beasts carrying the earthmen's food-supplies and water-barrels. The narrator fastens on the creatures' "elongated legs and necks" and their horny-plated bodies as the two features that make them "some fabulous combination of llama and saurian." The adjective performs a double act of wonder and taxonomy: it names the beasts' most alien feature — a body covering that fuses the keratinous hardness of mammalian horn with the segmented sheet-armor of a reptile — in the measured, clinical register of late-Victorian natural-history prose, while the image it builds remains frankly grotesque. The narrator is a naturalist-observer sorting the unknown by analogy, and horny-plated is the technical-sounding epithet that licenses the hybrid reading. The hyphen welds two materials into one continuous integument, suggesting a skin that is at once protective and inert — harder than hide, yet worn as a garment rather than forged. Because the vortlups must endure the abrasive, sun-scorched Martian desert for months without water, the plating reads as adaptive armor: a biological corselet that makes them seem "fabulous," as if assembled from the parts of several creatures.

Component analysis

  • horny — the adjective of horn, naming the material: hard, translucent, keratinous tissue grown on the heads of cattle and other ungulates. As a material-word it extends to anything "hard as horn": horny-handed labor, a horny callus. A separate, later slang sense (19th-century American English) means "sexually aroused," the aroused body's stiffness compared to the rigid horn; the word thus spans a remarkable range, from the highest point of the animal to its most vital state.
  • plated — the past participle of to plate, "to cover with a thin layer or flat sheets of a harder material," as in gold-plated or armor-plated. The plate of armor and the platen of a press share the image: a flat sheet laid over a surface. In biology, plating names the broad scutes of a crocodile's back or the armored bands of a glyptodon.

Polysemy and related senses

The compound itself is a deliberate literary neologism rather than a mainstream dictionary word, but each member carries a full polysemous history:
  • horny — (1) made of or resembling horn; (2) having horns or horn-like outgrowths (rare; keep distinct from horned); (3) of the skin, thickened and calloused; (4) sexually aroused (slang, 19th c. onward).
  • plated — (1) overlaid with metal, as silver-plated cutlery or nickel-plated steel; (2) covered with plates of armor (a plated dinosaur, a plated warship); (3) in geology, arranged in plates or slabs; (4) in printing and photography, made or printed from a plate.
  • horny-plated — the fused epithet: segmented keratinous body-armor. Its modern technical cousins are scute-covered, armored, and the paleontological armor-plated (of ankylosaurs and glyptodonts).

Differentiating terms

  • horned: merely states that an animal possesses horns; horny-plated names the horn-like material of a segmented covering.
  • scaly: implies many small overlapping flakes; horny-plated implies fewer, larger, fitted sheets jointed edge to edge like armor.
  • armor-plated: foregrounds defensive function and evokes warfare (tanks, knights); horny-plated stays within the biological realm of integument and adaptation.
  • calloused / cornified: describe thickened, hardened skin but without the segmentation of plates.
  • keratinous: the technical material-word for horn-substance; it names the substance, not the way it is arranged.

Register and use

Rare and deliberately technical, horny-plated is at home in genre fiction and descriptive natural history. It preserves its original double act: it names a segmented, rigid, keratinous body-armor, and it keeps the wonder of a creature assembled, like the vortlup, from the parts of several animals. In modern usage it could describe a pangolin, an ankylosaur, or a Martian pack-beast; the word is a license to see armor as grown rather than worn.
