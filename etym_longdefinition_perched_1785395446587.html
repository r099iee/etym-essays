PERCHED is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb to perch: to alight upon and rest upon a high, narrow support - a branch, ledge, rail, sill, windowsill, rooftop, wire, fence, or crag - typically of a bird, which grips its support with its feet and settles there at ease. It also means, by extension, to be set, placed, or situated in a raised and exposed position: a house perched on a hillside, a castle perched on a crag, a man perched on a stool, spectacles perched on the end of a nose. In The Hobbit, Bilbo, dazed and half-starved after the escape from the goblins, sits up and looks anxiously at the eagle who was perched close by: the participle places the great bird settled and at ease on its rock-platform, its bulk unmoved and unbothered, while the hobbit scrambles upright in alarm. The whole contrast between the eagle's calm altitude and the hobbit's anxiety is carried by that single participle. Perched implies elevation, repose, and poise: a body deliberately placed on a support, and the word will serve anything from a sparrow on a telegraph wire to a castle on a crag. Its tone is precise and slightly picturesque - the word of naturalists, of storytellers, and of landscape writing - and it always gives a scene a sense of height and balance. Here it quietly underlines the power relation: the eagle is at home on the ledge, perched; Bilbo is not, and the participle marks the gulf between the high, settled confidence of the bird and the hobbit's scrambling alarm. CORE SENSES AND POLYSEMY
  • Primary sense (ornithological): of a bird, to alight on and rest upon a high, narrow support, gripping it with the feet - the finch perched on the branch; the owl perched on the barn beam; the hawk perched watching for prey.
  • Sense of a person: to sit in a raised or narrow position, often temporarily and with a hint of precariousness - perched on a stool, on the arm of a chair, on the edge of a desk, on a fence.
  • Sense of objects and places: to be set or situated in a raised position - a village perched on a cliff; a cottage perched on the hillside; glasses perched on the end of the nose; a small shop perched on a corner.
  • Passive / participial adjective: used of anything fixed at a height - a perched castle; perched villages; the perched rock that overhung the path.
  • Metaphoric / status sense: a high perch as a position of advantage, and the idiom to knock someone off their perch - to destroy a person's confident superiority or to humble the powerful.
  • Technical noun senses of the base perch: a bird's resting place (the bar, branch, or peg on which birds are set); a long pole or rod; a raised seat or bench (and, in old theatres, a cheap gallery seat, the 'gallery perch'); and a land measure - the rod or pole of five and a half yards, so named because the surveyor's measuring-rod was a pertica.
  • Homonym guard: the perch that is a freshwater fish (Latin perca, from Greek perke, 'perch, the spotted fish') is a completely different word with an unrelated history; the two merely sound alike.
  • Grammar: perch is a weak verb - perch, perches, perched; the participle works both as the past tense of the verb and as a participial adjective (a perched eagle, perched villages), and it forms the verbal noun perching.
SECONDARY MEANINGS AND THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The bridge from 'a pole' to 'a bird's roost' was built in the falconer's mews and the farmyard: the perch was the horizontal bar on which hawks were set to rest between flights and on which domestic fowls roosted at night. A pole is just a pole; a perch is a pole with a creature on it. From the bird's resting bar the word spread to any elevated resting place - a ledge, a rooftop, a crag - and then, through the verb, to the act of settling on any high, narrow thing. The verb's range then widened again from birds to people (to sit on a raised seat) and finally to objects and whole buildings, so that today a whole village can be perched on a hillside as naturally as a sparrow is perched on a wire. Two further developments complete the polysemy. First, the land-measure sense grew out of the surveyor's rod: the pertica of the Romans was a rod used for measuring, and English inherited the name of the rod as a unit of length (one perch = one rod = five and a half yards, still used in British land measurement). Second, the status metaphor developed in the modern period: a person 'perched' at the top of an organisation sits high, and the idiom 'to knock someone off their perch' - recorded from the nineteenth century - means to dislodge them from that eminence. REGISTER, COLLOCATION, AND LITERARY USE Perched belongs to a mid register: it is neither technical nor slang, but it carries a permanent hint of the picturesque and the elevated. It collocates richly with nouns of height - perched on a branch, perched on a cliff, perched atop a hill, perched on the edge - and with the prepositions on, upon, and atop. In nature writing it is the standard word for a bird's resting posture; in travel and landscape prose it describes buildings and settlements in exposed positions; in the literature of the sublime it gives scenes an architectural height. In Tolkien's prose the word does double duty: the eagle is perched, and the participle does quiet work placing the great bird at ease above the anxious hobbit, the very image of the power that has just rescued him. The tone is calm, exact, and slightly formal, never comic, and it is precisely that calm that makes the contrast with Bilbo's flustered 'storks - forks' speech so effective. Across its whole history the word has never lost the two ideas in its Latin core - the narrow support and the resting creature - and both are alive in the single sentence in which the eagle sits perched close by the frightened hobbit.
