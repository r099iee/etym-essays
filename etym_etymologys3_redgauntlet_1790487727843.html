Redgauntlet is a compound proper noun: red + gauntlet (in the glove sense), meaning Red Glove. As a common noun it never existed; as a surname, Border legend, and as the title of Sir Walter Scott's 1824 Waverley novel Redgauntlet, it is fully intelligible only if both elements are traced separately and then recombined in Scots history. The protocol therefore requires two evolution essays in one: red and gauntlet.

Red is among the oldest and most stable words in Indo-European. PIE *h1reudh- "red, ruddy" gives Sanskrit rohita "red", Greek erythros, Latin ruber, rufus, Old Irish ruad, Lithuanian raudas, Russian ruda, and Germanic *raudaz (Gothic rauds, Old Norse raudr, Old English read, modern red). Colour-term theory (Berlin and Kay) finds red universally the first chromatic term after black and white, which explains its mythic load: blood, danger, royalty, Jacobitism. Phonetics are regular: PIE initial *h1r- yields Germanic r-; PIE aspirated *-dh- yields Germanic *-d- by Grimm's law; Old English read with long vowel shortens to modern red /rɛd/ in Middle English. Meaning has barely shifted in 6,000 years — always the colour of blood and fire — though connotations شاخ: socialist red, red tape, in the red, Redgauntlet's bloody glove.

  • Gauntlet (glove): Frankish *want "glove, mitten" (compare modern German Handschuh displaced it, Dutch want retains it) was borrowed into Old French as gant "glove" with regular w- to g- adaptation (cf. werra → guerre). Diminutive suffix -elet (Latin -ellus) gave gantelet "little glove", specialised to "iron-plated glove of armour" by the 13th century. Middle English borrowed it during the Hundred Years War as gauntlet, keeping both spelling gaunt- and pronunciation /ˈɡɔːntlɪt/. Semantic extensions are all from chivalry: to throw down the gauntlet (challenge), to take up the gauntlet (accept), gauntleted hand. Crucially, this gauntlet is etymologically distinct from gauntlet/gantlope "to run between ranks enduring blows", from Swedish gatlopp "lane-run" (1610s). Scott, a Border antiquary, knew the difference and means glove.
  • Red + gauntlet as Border surname: In Liddesdale and Annandale lore, Redgauntlet is explained as a nickname for a Border reiver whose right-hand gauntlet remained stained with blood, or who wore a red-dyed glove as badge. Surnames from coloured clothing are common Scots: Redpath, Reddie, Blackwood, Whiteford. Whether historical Redgauntlets existed is debated; Scott admits in his introduction that he founded the house on traditions of the Herries and Johnstone families and on the penance-glove legend of Sir Robert Grierson of Lag. The compound follows regular English end-stress reduction: RED-gaunt-let with secondary stress on red.
  • Scott's novel (1824): Redgauntlet: A Tale of the Eighteenth Century, the 17th Waverley novel, returns to Jacobitism after the Hanoverian reconciliation of earlier books. Plot: Darsie Latimer (in fact Sir Arthur Darsie Redgauntlet) and Alan Fairford, young lawyers, are drawn into the Third Jacobite plot of 1765–66 to restore Charles Edward Stuart (Bonnie Prince Charlie), embodied in the fanatical legitimist Sir Edward Hugh Redgauntlet, the red-gauntletedLauncelot of the Cause. Episodes include the Solway salmon-spearing (the source of Ruskin's sentence), Nanty Ewart the smuggler, Wandering Willie's Tale (the masterpiece ghost story), and the final rally at Crackenfell where the Prince is revealed as unimpressive and the cause collapses. Scott's theme is the obsolescence of legitimism itself — gallant but doomed, admirable yet unfit for modern Britain.
  • Transmission to Ruskin: Young Ruskin read Redgauntlet as a boy alongside Pope's Iliad. To him Sir Edward was the modern Tydides: a king-lover who speared more salmon than any Solway fisherman, did harder work than common men, and took less. The sentence's and — which was particularly a subject of admiration (breaking off in the excerpt) continues in Praeterita to praise disinterested labour. For Ruskin, Redgauntlet thus means not just a book but a moral type: the Jacobite gentleman who works and dares for loyalty, not profit.

In sum, Redgauntlet joins an unchanging colour PIE root and a Frankish-to-French-to-English armour term into a Scots literary surname and novel title, fixed since 1824 with constant meaning: the bloody-gloved Jacobite and his book.
