A grindstone is a thick revolving disc of natural or artificial abrasive stone, turned by hand, treadle, or machinery, used for sharpening tools, grinding grain, or shaping metal. Typically of coarse sandstone with a central axle and a water trough to cool the work, it cuts by friction: the workpiece is pressed to the turning face until an edge or surface is ground.

In Dickens — "a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone" — the literal tool becomes a moral engine. Scrooge is a hand that grinds: he sharpens bargains and wears men down. The grindstone suggests relentless, joyless, spark-striking labour, not craftsmanship.

  • Core tool sense — sharpening wheel: "The gardener sharpened the shears on the grindstone." Blacksmiths, joiners, butchers, and households kept a grindstone for knives, axes, scythes, and chisels. Modern equivalents are bench grinders and angle grinders, but the principle — abrasive wheel, sparks, water cooling — is unchanged. A wet grindstone runs in water to avoid overheating steel.
  • Milling sense — grain stone: Historically, grindstone overlapped with millstone and quern-stone for flour. Today millstone usually means the paired horizontal stones of a wind- or water-mill, while grindstone usually means the vertical sharpening wheel. Regional usage still blurs them.
  • Figurative labour sense: The grindstone is drudgery itself. Idioms: to keep one's nose to the grindstone (work continuously without rest), to put someone's nose to the grindstone (oppress with work), the daily grind (routine toil). Dickens's phrase belongs here: Scrooge keeps Bob Cratchit's nose to the stone for fifteen shillings a week.
  • Moral-symbolic sense: The grindstone wears away as it sharpens — wheel and blade consume each other. Preachers and novelists use it for systems that sharpen profits while grinding humanity: "the grindstone of poverty," "ground beneath the grindstone of industry." The stone is pitiless, circular, endless.
  • Technical distinctions: A whetstone / oilstone / hone is a small hand-held block for finishing; a grindstone is large and revolving for coarse work. A millstone grinds grain between two stones; a grindstone grinds an edge against one. Grit, diameter, and bond (vitrified, resin) define modern wheels.

Grammar: countable noun, plural grindstones /ˈɡraɪnd.stəʊnz/. Compounds: grindstone grit, nose-to-grindstone. Register neutral-concrete for the tool, literary-moral for the idiom. To modern ears the tool is rustic-nostalgic (farm museums, period films); the idiom is corporate-cliché ("back to the grindstone after holidays").

In short: stone that grinds steel, grain, or men — depending whether farmer, smith, or Scrooge holds the handle.

Extra note on grindstone idioms from village smithy to Sheffield mills to modern office grind, keeping nose to the stone as image of relentless labour and moral critique.
