Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *smei- "to smile, laugh; to look with pleased wonder" (reconstructed; the traditional derivation of Latin mirari from *smei- is accepted by many handbooks, though some scholars prefer an independent expressive origin — this is a debated stage). Stage 2 — Latin mirari "to wonder at, admire, be astonished; to gaze with amazement" (attested from the 1st century BCE, frequent in classical prose and verse, e.g. Cicero and Vergil). Stage 3 — Latin mirandus "to be wondered at, admirable" (gerundive of mirari; the form is well attested, e.g. Vergil, Aeneid 1.731, mirandus Apollo). Stage 4 — Latin miranda, neuter plural of mirandus, "things to be wondered at / admired" (the morphological base of the toponym). Stage 5 — Medieval Latin Mirandula / Mirandola, the fortified town in the Po plain of Emilia (province of Modena); attested in medieval documents in forms such as Mirandula. The traditional explanation derives the name from miranda "admirable things," with local tradition reading the stronghold as a "watchful" or "to-be-looked-at" place; substrate or folk-etymological alternatives remain possible — the exact derivation is debated. Stage 6 — Italian Mirandola, town and comune; seat of the Pico counts, later dukes of Mirandola and Concordia, whose style was della Mirandola "of Mirandola" (the Pico lordship is attested from the 14th century). Stage 7 — English MIRANDOLA, the toponymic element of the name of Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494), as in "Pico of Mirandola" (the form used by Walter Pater in The Renaissance, 1873). Final node: MIRANDOLA. Notes on debate and nuance: - The connection of Latin mirari to PIE *smei- is traditional but not entirely beyond dispute; handbooks vary in confidence. - Mirandola is a place-name derived from Latin miranda/mirari; it is not the ancestor of the abstract concept "wonder" in reverse. - Pico is a separate family name (Italian pico "woodpecker," or related nickname stock) and is not part of the Mirandola etymon tree; only MIRANDOLA is the rootWord here. - The Romance verbs of looking (Italian mirare, Spanish mirar, French mirer "to look at, aim at") descend from the same Latin mirari family and illustrate the same root's semantic reach.
