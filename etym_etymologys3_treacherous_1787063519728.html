Latin base: treacherous descends from a Latin verb meaning "to play tricks." The chain runs: Latin tricae ("trifles, tricks, wiles, deceits," plural, attested in the classical period) → Vulgar Latin *triccare ("to play tricks, deceive") → Old French trichier ("to cheat, deceive") → the Old French adjective trecherous / trecheros ("deceitful, faithless," 13th century) → Middle English trecherous (14th century) → Modern English treacherous. The ultimate origin of Latin tricae is debated: it is often linked to the PIE root *terkʷ- ("to twist, turn"), the same family as Latin torquēre ("to twist") — the sense developing from "twisting" to "entangling" to "tricks, wiles." This connection is plausible but not certain; the safest statement is that tricae meant "tricks, deceptions," with a probable but unprovable link to the "twist" root.

Romance and Old French: Old French trichier gave modern French tricher ("to cheat") and the Italian and Spanish verbs tricare in the same semantic field; the agent noun tricheor ("deceiver") and the adjective trecheros were built on it. English borrowed the adjective in the 14th century — at first spelled trecherous, trechorous, or similar — along with the noun treachery and the now-obsolete noun treacher ("a traitor, deceiver"). The suffix -ous marks the adjective formation ("full of treachery"), the standard medieval pattern for building descriptive adjectives from abstract nouns.

Important distinction: "treacherous" and "traitor" look alike but come from different ancestors. "Traitor" is from Old French traitre, from Latin traditor, "one who hands over" (from trādere, "to hand over, deliver" — also the source of "tradition" and "betray"). "Treacherous" is from the "trick" family (*triccare). The two families converged semantically on "betrayal of faith" but were etymologically distinct from the start.

Semantic history:

  • 14th century: "treacherous" = "faithless, traitorous, given to deceit" — exclusively a moral word for people: a treacherous servant, a treacherous friend.
  • 15th-16th centuries: the word consolidates in political and religious polemic; treacherous rulers, treacherous ministers, treacherous counsellors fill chronicles and drama. It is a strong, emotive accusation.
  • 17th-18th centuries: the first extension to things appears: treacherous seas, treacherous ice, treacherous roads. The extension is metaphorical — a dangerous place "deceives" the traveller by looking safe. Travel writing, exploration narratives, and maritime literature drive the shift.
  • 19th century: the hazard-sense becomes fully ordinary: "treacherous currents," "treacherous weather," "treacherous footing." The word now does two jobs: moral betrayal and deceptively dangerous physical conditions.
  • 20th-21st centuries: in everyday use the hazard-sense is now as common as, or commoner than, the person-sense; weather and traffic reporting use it constantly ("treacherous driving conditions"). The person-sense remains standard in discussion of betrayal, espionage, and politics.

Cultural profile: "treacherous" encodes the fear of trusted things turning against us — whether a friend's hand or a frozen river's surface. Its history from "playing tricks" (Latin tricae) to "betraying faith" to "looking safe but being dangerous" is a textbook case of a moral term extending to the natural world. In the Tolkien context, "dwarves are not heroes, but calculating folk... some are tricky and treacherous," the word keeps both senses in play: the tricky deception of persons and the unreliability that makes trust dangerous.
