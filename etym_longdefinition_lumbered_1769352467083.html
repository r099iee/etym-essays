lumbered — past tense of the verb to lumber — "moved in a slow, heavy, awkward, and often noisy way." In the Of Mice and Men passage, Lennie "lumbered to his feet": the verb captures the immense, uncoordinated mass of a man who moves like a slow, powerful animal, his heavy frame rising with effort and without grace. The word is almost cinematographic — we can feel the thud of each footfall and the labor of the rising. The core semantic content is bulk plus slowness plus clumsiness: whatever lumbers does not glide, does not hurry, and does not move with control; it pushes its own weight forward.

The verb "to lumber" (move heavily) is itself part of a rich polysemous family, because the English word lumber has several distinct modern lives that all share a single root idea of "heavy, bulky, encumbering material." These senses did not all arise at once; they accreted over centuries, and each one has its own historical bridge back to the central image of weight.

1. Primary sense — heavy, ungainly motion (verb)

The core sense, and the one tested here: to move slowly and heavily, often noisily, with a rolling, swaying, or thudding gait. Applied to large animals ("the elephant lumbered across the savanna"), heavy machines, and big-bodied people. The movement is ungraceful and slightly comic or pathetic in its sheer physical effort.

2. Timber, sawn wood (noun, chiefly North American)

"Lumber" as a material — felled trees, logs, and sawn planks prepared for building: a "lumber yard," a "lumberjack," the "lumber trade." This sense arose in colonial North America in the 17th century, from the older noun meaning "disused, bulky household goods." The semantic bridge is the shared idea of heavy, bulky, stacked material that occupies space and must be stored, hauled, and moved about. A pile of timber and a pile of pawned junk are both, to the older eye, "lumber."

3. Burdened, encumbered ("lumbered with")

The British idiom "to be lumbered with" means saddled with an unwanted responsibility, task, or possession: "I got lumbered with the paperwork." Here the meaning shifts from the object (bulky goods) to the victim of bulk (the person who must carry the load). This is a pure metaphorical extension of the noun's sense of "burdensome clutter."

4. Discarded household clutter ("lumber room")

An older noun sense, now chiefly historical: "lumber" = disused furniture, broken objects, and general household junk stored away — hence the "lumber room" of a Victorian house, the place where encumbering things were shut out of sight. This is the ancestor sense that gave rise to both the timber sense (via stacked, stored bulk) and the "burdened" sense (via the load you must carry).

5. Historical semantic bridge — how the senses grew together

Two independent etymological strands collided in Middle English. The first is the native verb lomeren ("to move heavily or clumsily," 14th c.), of uncertain origin, possibly echoic or connected to a Scandinavian word for a slow, heavy gait. The second is the noun lumber ("disused bulky goods"), which is often traced to the Lombards, the Italian banking families whose London pawnshops and storehouses collected heaps of pawned household goods — the "lumber" of London. Over the centuries the verb of clumsy motion and the noun of heavy clutter fused in the popular imagination: a person who moves like a load of lumber is "lumbering," and a room full of lumber is a place of heavy, awkward masses. The timber sense grew out of the same image in the New World, where the piled logs of a settlement's stores were literally the heaviest "lumber" on hand.

6. Modern technical and metaphorical uses

  • Computing/industry: "lumber" remains the standard North American term for timber; "lumberyard," "lumberjack," "lumber industry" are fixed compounds.
  • Idiomatic burden: "lumbered with" is fully current in British English for an unwelcome obligation.
  • Characterization: to describe a person as "lumbering" is to paint them as physically big, slow, and a little awkward — often sympathetically, sometimes comically.
  • Historical register: "lumber room" and "lumber" for junk are now old-fashioned but still recognized in classic literature.

In short, "lumbered" on this card names the heavy, clumsy, ungainly movement of a large body; but the word family around it carries a whole history of heavy, stacked, encumbering things — pawned goods, household clutter, and the timber of the New World — all of which grow from the same root image of weight and bulk.
