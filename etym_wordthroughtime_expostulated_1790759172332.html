- 16th Century: Borrowed into English from Latin *expostulātus*, originally used in formal and legal contexts to mean "to demand vehemently, require as a right, or complain bitterly."
- 17th Century: Semantic shift deepens toward "reasoning earnestly with another to dissuade them from an unwise action" (prominent in Milton, Shakespeare, and devotional writings).
- 18th–19th Century: Becomes a staple of classic English prose (Defoe, Austen, Eliot, Dickens) describing high-minded parental, moral, or philosophical debates and affectionate dissuasion.
- 20th–21st Century: Recedes into formal, elevated literary register, used precisely to describe structured, impassioned argumentation aimed at preventing folly.
