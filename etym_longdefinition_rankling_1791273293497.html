rankling is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb to rankle, meaning to cause persistent, painful irritation of mind or feelings, to fester emotionally, to continue to sting, gall, or embitter long after the original offence. Something rankling has not healed: like a thorn left in flesh or poison in a wound, it works on, throbs, and will not be forgotten. In Wodehouse's sentence, Tuppy's words are still rankling in my bosom: the narrator carries them upstairs, undresses, walks to the bathroom, and still feels their sting. The comedy depends on the dignity of the old bodily metaphor applied to a trivial club insult.

Grammatically rankling functions as verb form (the words were still rankling), as adjective (a rankling injustice, a rankling memory), and as noun (the rankling of old defeats). It governs in (rankling in his breast), with, and against. Typical subjects are insults, slights, injustices, defeats, suspicions, jealousies; typical loci are breast, bosom, heart, mind, memory, conscience. Register is literary but fully current, slightly elevated above nagging or bugging, carrying Shakespearean weight without archaism. To say an issue is rankling implies it deserves attention precisely because it will not fade on its own.

The power of rankling lies in its medical origin. Modern speakers may not know medieval rankle meant to fester, yet they feel the wound-image in the word's sound and use. Psychologists might speak of rumination, intrusive memories, or unresolved grievance; rankling condenses that science into a single ache.

  • Core emotional sense: persistently irritating and embittering. The dominant meaning today is psychological: a memory or feeling that keeps producing resentment each time it is recalled. The semantic bridge from wound to mind is the idea of incomplete healing — just as a sore with matter in it throbs, so an unredressed insult throbs when touched by thought.
  • Active verbal sense: continuing to sting. As participle, rankling stresses ongoing action: it is not a dead past hurt but a live present itch. The bridge is aspect: -ing keeps the wound open, distinguishing rankling from rankled (already festered) or rank (merely foul).
  • Moral and social sense: sign of injustice unaddressed. Rankling grievances often imply the sufferer has legitimate cause: rankling inequality, rankling betrayal. The bridge is ethical: persistence proves seriousness. Politicians invoke what rankles in communities to demand redress.
  • Physical relic sense: festering of sores (archaic). Surgeons once spoke of wounds rankling, meaning breeding corruption. This literal sense is obsolete except in historical novels, but it explains the metaphor's logic and Shakespeare's puns on rank as both festering and overgrown.
  • Comic-literary sense: mock-heroic pique. Wodehouse, Austen, and Waugh use rankling for trivial slights treated in high style (rankling in one's bosom). The bridge is bathos: grand bodily language for clubland vanity, letting readers smile while recognising real feeling.
  • Distinction from neighbours: unlike annoying (momentary), offensive (at the time), or traumatic (overwhelming), rankling names the middle zone of durable, gnawing, half-voluntary remembering that colours mood without destroying function.

In short, rankling is memory with an edge that will not blunt: an insult turned into a small, portable wound carried in the bosom from room to room until apology, laughter, revenge, or time finally draws the thorn.
