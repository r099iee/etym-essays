In Macaulay's sentence “alienated a loyal gentry and __________ from the House of Stuart,” the noun priesthood names the Church of England clergy as a corporate body, an estate of the realm standing alongside the landed gentry. The historical referent is precise: the Anglican priesthood — bishops, clergy, and the whole established church — had been a pillar of Stuart loyalty, but James II's pro-Catholic policies, above all the Declaration of Indulgence of 1687, his attacks on the Test Acts, and his harassment of the Church's colleges and clergy, alienated this loyal body within a few months. The word priesthood here is institutional and corporate rather than individual: it denotes the whole order of ordained ministers, the clerical estate as a force in national politics, and Macaulay pairs it with gentry to evoke the two great conservative powers of English society — the landowners and the clergy — that the king's rashness drove into opposition. The tone is measured, historical, and constitutional: the priesthood is treated as a political body whose loyalty was a national asset and whose alienation was one of James's fatal errors.

Sense 1 — The office, position, or character of a priest (the abstract-singular sense). At its most basic, priesthood is a noun of state formed with the native Germanic suffix -hood: it names the office, function, or character of being a priest. One enters the priesthood by ordination; the priesthood is at once a vocation, a profession, and a sacramental state. This sense is visible in “He was ordained into the Anglican priesthood after years of theological study” and in phrases like “the priesthood of the Old Testament.” The suffix carries its full Germanic force: as childhood is the state of being a child and manhood the state of being a man, so priesthood is the state of being a priest.

Sense 2 — Priests collectively; the whole body of the clergy (the corporate-collective sense, used on this card). The same suffix that makes childhood a “state” also makes brotherhood a “body of brothers,” and so priesthood equally names the entire order of priests as a group — the clergy of a church, the sacral caste of a religion. This is the sense in Macaulay, where the priesthood is a political body, and in sentences like “Members of the priesthood were expected to lead lives of celibacy and devotion.” The two senses are continuous: the office is at once a state each priest holds and the sum of all who hold it.

Sense 3 — The priestly order in a specific religion or tradition. In Christian usage the word is used of the ordained ministry of churches with a priestly office (Roman Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and various others); in historical and comparative-religion writing it is extended to the sacrificial or mediating clergy of other faiths — the priesthood of ancient Israel (the Aaronic and Levitical priests), the priesthoods of pagan antiquity (Egyptian, Greek, Roman), and the priestly castes of non-European religions. In every case the core meaning is the same: an order set apart to officiate at sacred rites, to mediate between the divine and the community, and to possess a special sacral status.

Sense 4 — Transferred and figurative senses.

  • Priesthood of all believers: in Protestant theology, especially Lutheran and Reformed thought, the phrase “the universal priesthood of believers” (German allgemeines Priestertum) denies any special sacrificial caste and asserts that all Christians share direct access to God — a democratization of the word.
  • Secular or metaphorical priesthood: by extension the word is applied to any dedicated, self-sacrificing corps — “the priesthood of medicine,” “the priesthood of the law,” “the priesthood of letters” — stressing devotion, esoteric knowledge, and a calling that transcends mere employment.
  • Celibacy and hierarchy: in common parlance “the priesthood” is often shorthand for the celibate, hierarchically organized clergy of the Roman Catholic Church, carrying the associations of the Roman collar, the seminary, and ecclesiastical authority.

Sense 5 — Historical and political use. In English constitutional and historical prose of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, “the priesthood” denoted the established clergy as one of the two conservative estates of the realm, the clerical counterpart of the gentry and the aristocracy; the word could carry an anticlerical edge in Whig and Enlightenment writers who spoke of “priestcraft” and priestly power. This political sense is precisely the one Macaulay activates in his opening sentence.

Semantic architecture of the word. Two building blocks explain the whole range. The root element priest comes through Latin presbyter from Greek presbýteros, “elder,” the New Testament title of the leaders of the early church — so the very word for the office encodes the principle of seniority and rule by elders, not the “sacrificer” sense of the native Old English sācerd (from Latin sacerdos). The suffix -hood is the old Germanic abstract-forming element (childhood, manhood, brotherhood, knighthood) that turns nouns and adjectives into names of states and corporate bodies. The union of the two, in Middle English, produced a word that could swing between the dignity of the single office and the weight of the whole clerical order — exactly the range this card's sentence draws upon. The distinctions among near-synonyms sharpen the picture: clergy is the broader neutral collective opposed to the laity; ministry stresses service and preaching and was preferred by Protestant writers; holy orders is the rite that confers the office; priesthood alone names the sacral order as a corporate body with its own dignity.
