self-complacency: a feeling of smug, uncritical, and often unwarranted satisfaction with oneself or one's own achievements; quiet, self-regarding contentment that blinds a person to their own faults, limitations, or dangers. The word names a moral and psychological state rather than an outward behaviour — an inner glow of self-approval that typically goes unnoticed by the person who feels it.

Primary sense — smug self-satisfaction: In modern usage, "self-complacency" describes the attitude of a person who is entirely pleased with themselves and their state, and is therefore unreceptive to criticism, improvement, or warning. It is the state of resting on one's laurels, convinced of one's own excellence. It is close in meaning to "complacency" but explicitly directed at the self.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings:
  • Original, milder sense (17th–18th c.): In early usage "self-complacency" was not necessarily pejorative — it meant a legitimate, calm satisfaction with one's own conduct, close to "ease of conscience" or "self-approbation." Christian moralists spoke of the "self-complacency" of a well-ordered soul. Semantic bridge: the word is built on Latin complacere "to please greatly," and the intensifier com- simply meant "thoroughly pleasing to oneself"; the modern pejorative tone emerged only later.
  • Modern moral censure (19th c. onward): By the 19th century, especially in novels of manners, "self-complacency" became a target of satire — a smug, uncritical self-satisfaction that blinds one to one's defects, as in Jane Austen's portraits of complacent characters. It is now almost always used with disapproval.
  • Warning idiom — "smug self-complacency before a crisis": In journalism and rhetoric, "self-complacency" frequently appears in warnings against being lulled into false security — the self-satisfaction that precedes a fall. Semantic bridge: the state of being "thoroughly pleased with oneself" is seen as the psychological precondition for being caught unawares.
  • Psychology and self-help: In modern psychology the concept overlaps with "complacency," "self-satisfaction," and "self-congratulation" — an affective state of uncritical self-regard that can block learning, growth, and adaptability (sometimes discussed under "overconfidence" and "hubris").
  • Distinction from related states: It differs from self-confidence (which is justified and functional), from vanity (which is outward-seeking display), and from conceit (which is active self-importance); it is the passive, quiet, inner satisfaction that asks for nothing and changes nothing.
  • Grammatical and derivational use: The noun "self-complacency" pairs with the adjective self-complacent and is used in formal, literary, and moralizing registers; "complacency" alone (without "self-") is the more common modern word, so "self-complacency" is now somewhat formal and dated, often chosen for its precise, slightly old-fashioned precision.

Historical semantic bridge: The word is a 17th-century English compound: the native Anglo-Saxon pronoun self + the Latinate abstract noun complacency, from Latin complacentia ("thorough pleasure, satisfaction"), from complacēre ("to please greatly"), from com- (intensive) + placēre ("to please"). The whole literally means "thorough pleasure taken in oneself." Over three centuries the phrase "pleased with oneself" hardened from a description of innocent contentment into a sharply critical label for smug, uncritical self-approval.
