- 14th-century English — "a handful of straw or twigs": the practical bundle used to kindle a fire or sweep a floor, a mere wisp of kindling — thin, disposable, and quick to burn.
- 16th century — the bundle becomes a trace: wisp extends to any small, thin bunch or streak of hair, smoke, or mist, and the will-o'-the-wisp (the marsh-light swung as a burning wisp of straw) fastens to the word the menace of a deceptive, luring light.
- 18th–19th centuries — the ghostly sense consolidates: in romantic and gothic writing a wisp is the quasi-material filament that seems to dissolve even as it appears, barely a presence at all.
- Contemporary — a fragile streak of smoke, hair, or mist, and by metaphor anything ghostly and barely present: fibrous, twisted, and still tinged with the folk-treachery of the wisp-light.
waxed
- Earliest recorded English — "to grow, to increase": from the beginning waxed is the verb of organic, smooth, inevitable growth, above all the moon's filling toward fullness, paired with its opposite, wane.
- Early modern — the biblical and prophetic register: the King James "waxed great," "waxed strong and mighty" lend the verb an archaic, fated elevation that plain "grew" lacks.
- Contemporary — waxed survives mainly in the lunar idiom "wax and wane" and in elevated literary use for a luminous, destiny-like filling of light.
