- Classical Greek Antiquity (4th c. BCE – 1st c. CE) — Coined as anōmalos (ἀνώμαλος) to describe rough, uneven terrain, physical asymmetry, and in Hellenistic grammatical scholarship to classify nouns with irregular declensional inflections.
- 17th c. (Early Modern Scientific Revolution) — Borrowed into English through Late Latin anōmalus; applied by natural philosophers like Francis Bacon and Robert Boyle to empirical observations that contradicted traditional Aristotelian natural categories.
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Empirical Physics) — Formalized across astronomy (Keplerian orbital calculations), optics (anomalous dispersion), and thermodynamics (the anomalous expansion of cooling water).
- Mid-20th c. (Generative Linguistics & Philosophy) — Adopted into formal semantics to describe grammatically sound sentences that violate semantic selectional restrictions (e.g., inanimate subjects performing animate verbs).
- Late 20th–21st c. (Information Age & Machine Learning) — Centralized in computer science and data analytics as the foundational concept in cybersecurity and anomaly detection algorithms identifying outlier patterns in big data.
