ENDEAVOUR — comprehensive definition Sense in the given context. In the passage under study, endeavour means a sustained literary undertaking — the serious collective effort of English poets, scholars, and educators, in the sixteenth century, to write verse "in their own language" under the shadow of the freshly pedestalled literatures of Greece and Rome. The plural "endeavours" (here carrying the possessive "literary endeavours of Englishmen") makes it a body of labours rather than a single try. The tone is historical and faintly elegiac: these endeavours are "inevitable," they come "under fresh scrutiny," and they are doomed to be "found wanting." The word carries diligence and earnestness — the respectable striving of a whole culture judged against the classics. Primary lexical senses. The word exists as both noun and verb, sharing one meaning-centre:
  • Noun: a serious, earnest, and usually sustained attempt or effort, directed toward a worthwhile end; an undertaking. "Her latest endeavour is a history of English prosody."
  • Verb: to try hard, to strive, to attempt earnestly. "He endeavoured to restore the classical metres." The verb is always transitive-into-infinitive ("endeavour to do something") and is confined to formal, elevated registers.
Exhaustive polysemy. The word spreads over a small but real family of senses and uses:
  • Collective plural — "endeavours": the gathered labours and enterprises of a person, a group, or a nation (scientific endeavours, humanitarian endeavours, the literary endeavours of Englishmen). This is the sense at work in the context, where the plural names an accumulated body of cultural striving.
  • Proper-noun, nautical and aerospace: The word has been taken up as a name of exploratory vessels — most famously HMS Endeavour, the bark commanded by James Cook on his first Pacific voyage (1768–71), in which he charted New Zealand and the east coast of Australia; and, in deliberate homage, NASA's Space Shuttle Endeavour (1992–2011). The usage taps the word's connotation of earnest, high-purposed enterprise, and gives it a mild patriotic-romantic aura.
  • Legal jargon — "best endeavours" clauses: In contract law, "best endeavours," "reasonable endeavours," and "all reasonable endeavours" are technical phrases defining how hard a party must try to achieve a specified outcome. In this professional register the word has a precise, graded meaning sharper than its ordinary use, and litigators argue about the exact degree of effort each formulation demands.
  • Spelling variant — endeavour/endeavor: Endeavour (British, Canadian, Australian -our spelling, inherited from the Old French word devoir) versus endeavor (American -or, normalised by Noah Webster's spelling reforms in the early nineteenth century, like colour/color and honour/honor). The two spellings are the same word.
  • Historical moral colour — the "duty owed": The word's origin carries obligation with it: Old French en devoir, literally "put in duty," so that an endeavour is etymologically an attempt one OWES — a duty-bound striving rather than a casual try. This grave, dutiful gravity has stayed with the word, from Milton's "life-long endeavour" to the plain official prose of "future endeavours."
  • Register: Endeavour is formal and slightly elevated. It is at home in official prose, education, and literary criticism; it is not a colloquial word. "Try," "attempt," and "effort" are the ordinary members of the family; "endeavour" lends dignity and purpose.
Historical and semantic bridge. The meaning develops from obligation to effort. In Old French, devoir (from Latin dēbēre, "to owe") meant both "to owe" and, as a noun, "duty, that which is owed." The reflexive phrase se mettre en devoir, "to put oneself to one's duty," described the act of setting oneself earnestly to a task. Middle English borrowed the phrase as endeven / endeveren in the late fourteenth century, meaning "to make it one's duty, to strive," and regularised it as endeavour by the fifteenth. The sense then moved from "to put oneself under obligation" to "to strive earnestly" to "a serious attempt": the duty became internalised as purpose. Modern usage preserves the moral colour: an endeavour is an attempt undertaken with seriousness and commitment, as though one owed it to oneself. Synonym boundaries. Effort is the bare expenditure of energy; attempt stresses merely trying an action; enterprise implies boldness or initiative; exertion emphasises strain; undertaking stresses the assumption of a task; venture adds risk; and essay (as a noun, now formal or literary) means a tentative attempt. Endeavour's distinctive colour is its conjunction of seriousness, duration, purpose, and a faint sense of duty — which is exactly why a book about the "literary endeavours" of sixteenth-century Englishmen reaches for it: these were attempts the culture owed to its own self-respect, made under the highest of standards, and found wanting.
