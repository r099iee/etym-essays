COVERLETS is the plural of English coverlet, a compound borrowed into English from the French bed-covering word covre-lit / couvre-lit ("that which covers the bed"). Two separate derivation chains follow, one for each element of the compound. CHAIN 1 — the second element: lit "bed". - PIE *legh- "to lie down, to lay" (reconstructed root of the 'lie/lay' word-family; underlies Greek lekhos/lektron "bed" as cognates) → - Latin lectus "bed, couch" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE; from *legh-to-, "that on which one lies") → - Gallo-Romance / Old French lit "bed" (attested in Old French from the 9th century) → - Old French covre-lit / couvre-lit "bed-cover, that which covers the bed" (compound attested from the 12th century) → - Anglo-Norman coverlet / coverlite "bed-cover" (attested from the 13th century) → - Middle English coverlet "bed-cover, bedspread" (attested from the 14th century) → - English coverlet → plural COVERLETS. CHAIN 2 — the first element: cover. - Latin operiō, operīre "to cover, to close" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE; the deeper pre-Latin etymology is debated — some have suggested a PIE root *ḱeu- / *ḱew- "to cover, conceal", but this is not securely established and the further origin is best treated as unknown) → - Latin cooperiō, cooperīre "to cover entirely, to cover over" (com- intensive prefix + operiō; attested Classical Latin) → - Late Latin cooperīre → Old French covrir "to cover" (attested from the 9th–10th centuries; later couvrir) → - Anglo-Norman / Old French cover- stem, source of the English verb cover "to cover; a covering" (attested in Middle English from the 13th century) → - Old French covre-lit / couvre-lit → Anglo-Norman coverlet → Middle English coverlet → English coverlet → plural COVERLETS. Notes on debated points: - The PIE status of Latin operiō is uncertain; treat any *ḱeu- reconstruction as speculative, not as an attested stage. - The final -let of coverlet is the second element lit "bed" reduced in the Anglo-Norman compound, NOT the diminutive suffix -let seen in booklet and droplet. Speakers commonly reanalyse it as a diminutive, but that is folk etymology; the French couvre-lit shows the true structure. - Latin lectus and Greek lektron/lekhos are cognate through *legh-, which confirms the root of the "bed" element.
