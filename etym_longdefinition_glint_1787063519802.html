Glint is a noun (and verb) denoting a small, quick flash of light — a brief sparkle or gleam produced when light is reflected from a smooth, shiny, often metallic, glassy or watery surface, or caught in an eye. The word stresses brevity and sharpness: a glint appears and is gone in an instant, a momentary catch of light rather than a steady glow or beam. In the source passage, Bilbo stumbles among the golden things of the dragon-hoard and the watchers "caught a glint" — the sudden flash of a golden object caught by his moving light in the dark.

Primary senses

  • Noun — a brief flash of reflected light: "the glint of a knife in the moonlight", "the glint of sunlight on the sea", "a glint of gold in the rock face". The light is reflected, not emitted, and it is small and momentary.
  • Noun — a sparkle in the eye: "a glint of mischief in his eyes", "a glint of determination". A quick, telling flash of emotion or intention that shows in the eye; the eye "catches the light" of the feeling.
  • Verb — to flash briefly: "the glass glinted in the sun", "his eyes glinted with anger". Usually intransitive; the subject is the shining thing itself.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Figurative — a trace or hint: "a glint of hope", "the first glint of dawn", "a glint of humour". From the image of light appearing and vanishing, the word extends to mean the smallest passing sign or beginning of something.
  • Mineral and prospecting contexts: glints of metal or ore in rock; "gold glints" in streams or gravel — the tiny flash by which precious metals betray their presence.
  • Formations: "glinting" (present participle / adjective, meaning flashing), "glinty" (occasional adjective), and the dialectal frequentative "glinter".

Nuance: glint versus the other shine-words

  • Brevity and motion: Unlike "shine" (steady) or "glow" (continuous), a glint is quick, small and produced by movement — it appears as light and object shift relative to one another, so it is caught and lost in a blink.
  • Hard, bright surfaces: Glints characteristically come from metal, glass, ice, water, gems and eyes — smooth, reflective surfaces that flash when the light catches them at the right angle.
  • Literary colour: Writers use glint for menace (a blade glinting), mystery (something glinting in the dark), greed or excitement (gold glinting). Fantasy and adventure prose rely on it heavily; the treasure-hoard passage is a textbook example.

Word family

  • The Germanic "g-l-" cluster of shine-words: glow, gleam, glimmer, glitter, glint, glance, gloss, glare — all built on an ancient root meaning "to shine".
  • Near-synonyms with their own shades: gleam (steady, soft), glimmer (weak, wavering), glitter (scattered, brilliant), sparkle (many tiny points), flash (bright, longer), shimmer (trembling sheen), shine (steady emission).
  • Occasional technical uses: "glint" in radar and optics for a sharp reflected spike of signal; in fashion and jewellery copy for the sparkle of gems and polish.
