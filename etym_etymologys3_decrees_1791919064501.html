The history of decrees begins with a sieve. The reconstructed Indo-European root *krei- meant to sift, separate, or discriminate, and its Latin descendant cernere kept the concrete image while adding the mental one: to sift grain, to sift evidence, to distinguish one thing from another and so to decide. From cernere Latin built decernere, “to decide, determine, settle,” literally to sift a matter through to a conclusion, and its neuter past participle decretum, “a thing decided,” became the everyday Roman word for a determination, an opinion, and eventually a binding ruling.

Roman law

In the classical period the decretum acquired a sharp technical sense. When the emperor heard a case in person and gave judgment, that judgment was a decretum — one of the recognised forms of imperial constitutio, standing beside edicta (general proclamations), mandata (instructions to officials), and rescripta (written answers to petitions). The taxonomy matters because it shows the word already straddling the boundary between “judgment in a case” and “law for everyone”: a decision given in one dispute could be cited as authority thereafter. Later compilations, above all Justinian's Corpus Iuris Civilis in the sixth century, preserved decretum as a term of art and transmitted it to every medieval jurist who read Roman law.

The Church

Western canon law took the word over and made it central. Gratian's collection of c. 1140, properly Concordia discordantium canonum but universally known as the Decretum, gathered and reconciled conflicting authorities; the popes' replies to legal questions circulated as decretales, and Gregory IX's Decretales of 1234 became the second great component of the Corpus Iuris Canonici. From the twelfth century onward a “decree” could mean a papal or conciliar enactment with binding force on the faithful, and the decrees of the Council of Trent (1545–63) gave the term its most durable post-medieval application. The Reformation controversies turned on precisely this question: whether a decree of Rome could bind a national church.

Into English

English acquired the word twice over, through different Old French forms. The ordinary Old French noun was decret, but a diminutive decreet also circulated in Anglo-Norman legal usage, and Middle English texts from about 1300 show both decreet and decree. The modern standard form descends from the shorter variant, while the longer one survives in Scots law as decreet — the decreet of the Court of Session, a decreet arbitral. This split is a useful reminder that legal vocabulary travels along separate scribal routes in England and Scotland and that doublets often preserve older forms in the periphery.

Royal and parliamentary conflict

  • Medieval kings issued ordinances and letters patent, but “decree” remained tinged with Roman and canon-law authority.
  • Under the Tudors the royal proclamation became a legislative instrument, and the 1539 Proclamations Act gave certain proclamations statutory force — the high-water mark of government by decree in England.
  • Stuart claims to rule by prerogative made the word suspect to common lawyers, who insisted that only Parliament could impose taxes or alter the law.
  • Calvinist theology meanwhile used “decree” for the eternal determinations of God, so that the word acquired a double life: an act of arbitrary power in politics, an act of sovereign wisdom in divinity.

Procedure and reform

In the courts of equity, the Chancellor's final determination was a decree, and the word carried the weight of a concluded suit. The Judicature Acts of 1873–75 fused law and equity and rationalised terminology, replacing “decree” with “judgment” and “order”; only matrimonial causes, with their decree nisi and decree absolute, kept the old usage alive in England. Scots law, with its distinct institutional history, retained decreet without interruption. In the United States, the consent decree emerged in antitrust and later civil-rights litigation as a settlement embodied in a court order, and it remains a familiar instrument of regulatory enforcement.

Modern political life

The twentieth century gave the word its darkest associations. Totalitarian regimes legislated by decree, dissolving parliaments or reducing them to acclamation, and “rule by decree” became shorthand for the suspension of constitutional government. The same pattern recurs in emergency rule and military administration. In contemporary usage the word is therefore rarely neutral: to describe a policy as a decree is already to suggest that it was imposed rather than agreed. Canon lawyers, Scots lawyers, and historians of Roman law continue to use it technically, while journalists use it as a verdict.

Cognates and doublets

  • decretal — a papal letter settling canon-law questions, from decretalis.
  • discern, secret, concern — all from Latin cernere, sharing the underlying image of sifting.
  • decretive, decretory — rare adjectives meaning “having the nature of a decree.”

Uncertainty

The reconstructed root *krei- is inferred from Latin and its Italic relatives rather than attested in writing, and the semantic bridge from “sieve” to “judge” is an inference, though a very old and widely accepted one. The precise route by which Old French decreet lost its final -t in standard English is also not a regular sound change but a matter of competing dialectal and scribal forms; the survival of Scots decreet is the clearest evidence of that competition.
