• Uproar: Similar because both denote a loud, confused disturbance; differs because uproar (from Dutch oproer, "a rising up") focuses chiefly on the noise and clamour of a disturbance, while tumult includes the physical surging, swelling motion of the crowd and can extend to inner emotion.
  • Commotion: Similar because both mean a noisy disturbance or bustle; differs because commotion is a broader, blander word for any agitated movement or excitement, often brief and local, while tumult suggests a larger, more violent, swelling disorder.
  • Turmoil: Similar because both denote chaotic disturbance and can be internal ("inner turmoil"); differs because turmoil stresses confused agitation and distress without necessarily implying sound or a crowd, while tumult centres on the loud, surging mass and its noise.
  • Pandemonium: Similar because both mean wild, chaotic din; differs because pandemonium (Milton's coinage, "all-demons-place") implies complete wild madness, near-total disorder, and is more extreme and colloquial, while tumult is more dignified and structured — the swelling confusion of a crowd, not universal bedlam.
  • Clamour: Similar because both denote loud, confused noise; differs because clamour is specifically the noise of voices shouting (often protest or demand), a sustained outcry, while tumult is the more general surge of sound and motion of a disordered mass.
  • Racket: Similar because both mean a loud, unpleasant noise; differs because racket is informal, colloquial, and purely about unpleasant sound, while tumult is literary and implies mass, motion, and disorder, not merely volume.
  • Riot: Similar because both can denote violent public disorder; differs because riot is a legal and concrete term for a specific lawless public disturbance involving violence or damage, while tumult is more general, covering noise, agitation, and emotion without the legal specificity.
  • Agitation: Similar because both can name excited disturbance of mind or mass; differs because agitation is a calm, clinical, or political word for restlessness and stirring up, without the image of surging sound and swelling mass carried by tumult.
  • Chaos: Similar because both denote disorder; differs because chaos (Greek "gap, void") is the abstract state of complete formless confusion, while tumult is concrete, physical, and audible — the swelling noise and motion of a disturbed crowd or heart.
