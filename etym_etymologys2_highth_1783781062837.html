Earliest reconstructable root (debated): PIE *kowk- (also given as *kewk-) "to swell, become strong" — attested with certainty only in a narrow set of daughter forms (Germanic *hauh-, Lithuanian kaũkas "bump, swelling"); the root's shape and even its existence outside Germanic is debated.
Proto-Germanic: *hauhaz "high" — Grimm's Law shifted PIE *k > PGmc *h; the vowel *ow became *au.
Proto-Germanic: *hauhiþō "height, elevation" — abstract noun in *-iþō (cf. *langiþō "length", *breidiþō "breadth") formed on *hauh-.
Old English (c. 725–1100): hēahþu (also hīehþu) "height, summit, high place" — the Germanic abstract noun, with *au > ēa by Old English breaking, and *þ maintained.
Middle English (c. 1150–1500): heihte / heghte / heighte / hyght — scores of scribal spellings; the final -u was lost and the consonant cluster simplified. Alongside these descends the analogical form highth, re-made directly from high + -th on the model of breadth, width.
Early Modern English (c. 1600–1700): highth — used by Shakespeare and Milton; in Paradise Lost (1667) Milton spells it highth for its archaic elevation.
Modern English (archaic variant): highth — survives only as a deliberate archaism; standard spelling is height.
NOTE ON DEBATE: The ultimate PIE etymon is disputed. OED and Etymonline accept a root *kowk- "to swell," but Pokorny's grouping is thin, and some scholars derive *hauh- from *kewk- "to bend" via the notion of a bent-back, raised shape. No intermediate stage between PIE and Proto-Germanic is directly attested; both reconstructions rest on comparative evidence only.
