flocks is the plural of flock, a collective noun whose core sense is a group of animals of one kind gathered or moving together — par excellence a flock of sheep, and, from the earliest records of the animal sense, a flock of birds: starlings, geese, finches. The word has carried a double life since the Middle Ages: it names the animal group with complete neutrality, but it also names — by a metaphor as old as the Gospels — the human community under a shepherd: the congregation of a church, and more loosely any body of people under one leader, “the flock” of a pastor, a teacher, or a party.

Primary sense — animals. A flock is a cohesive group of gregarious animals, most typically sheep, goats, and birds. In modern usage the plural form flocks is the standard way to count multiple such groups: flocks of finches, flocks of geese, flocks of sheep on the hillside. The word covers any animals that “flock” — in ecology, starlings, flamingos, parakeets, and seabirds all form flocks — and it marks a clear boundary against herd: flock is the word for sheep, goats, and birds; herd for cattle, elephants, deer, and whales, though the two words overlap in no species at all. Flocks of birds in particular are studied as complex social systems: the flock's coordination (the swirling murmuration of starlings, the V of migrating geese) is one of the most striking phenomena of animal behaviour.

  • Sense 2 — people and congregations. The psalmist's image — “the Lord is my shepherd” — makes “the flock” the standard English word for a church congregation from the late medieval period onward; the plural flocks extends it to many congregations and, in secular writing, to any gathered, following bodies of people: flocks of tourists, flocks of pilgrims, flocks of commuters. The bridge is the image of the leader as shepherd and of the led as dependent, protective, direction-following animals — a metaphor of care and mild subordination that has proved richly productive in political and pastoral language alike.
  • Sense 3 — the verb and its modern avatars. To flock is “to gather or move in a crowd”: people flock to a sale, birds flock to a feeder. The proverb “birds of a feather flock together” (recorded in English from the sixteenth century) cements the verb in idiom. In computing and robotics, “flocking” has become the technical term for decentralised swarming behaviour (the 1986 “boids” algorithm simulates flocking by steering rules), a modern scientific avatar of the old shepherd's word.
  • Sense 4 — a false friend worth knowing. English has a second, unrelated noun flock — “a tuft or lock of wool, hair, or fibre; a wad of material” — borrowed in the thirteenth century from Latin floccus through Old French, and familiar today in “flocking” (applying fine fibre to a surface) and flock wallpaper. It is etymologically distinct from the animal-collective flock, though the two words have been confused for centuries, and some scholars have speculated, without general acceptance, on a deep prehistoric link between them — a debate worth remembering but not settled.

Usage. Flocks is a high-frequency, register-neutral plural in ordinary writing about farming, birdwatching, and wildlife; in religious prose it retains a warmth no synonym can match; in journalism, “flocks of …” is a vivid way to quantify crowds and crowds in motion. Its rhythm suits both pastoral (flocks on the hillside) and urban scenes (flocks of commuters flooding through the gates), and its long history — from a band of Viking warriors to a murmuration of starlings — makes it one of the oldest collective nouns still in daily speech.
