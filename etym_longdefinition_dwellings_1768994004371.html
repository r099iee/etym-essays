dwellings — Places of residence; shelters or houses; the buildings, caves, tents, or any constructions where beings live. The word is the noun form of dwell, "to live, reside," and it carries the full grammatical history of that verb: the noun was originally the gerund of the verb — literally "the act of dwelling" — and only later hardened into the concrete sense of "a place in which one dwells." That double origin still shows in the word's range: a dwelling can be a physical structure (a house, a cabin, a hut), a natural shelter made habitable (a cave dwelling, a cliff dwelling), or, in the plural, a scattered settlement of such structures.

In the passage the word appears in a sentence that deliberately plays human against animal: the smell of "animal dens" is set beside "the peculiar smell of Martian dwellings." The word is doing quiet, precise work here. It is plain and functional — the everyday noun of habitation — but in this context it is being defamiliarized: applied to Martian homes, it stretches the human word across an alien species, implying that whatever shelters the inhabitants of this world is enough like a house to be called a dwelling. The tone is ethnographic and speculative — a traveler's neutral, clinical word — and the plural form makes the dwellings a scattered settlement rather than a single home, a hint of a society hidden in the waste.

Its near-synonyms each tilt the meaning a different way:

  • Home is emotional and possessive, warm, centered on belonging; dwelling is cold by comparison, a mere place of residence.
  • Residence is formal and official, suggesting status and permanence.
  • Habitation is the archaeological and technical word, the fact of being inhabited.
  • Abode is literary and somewhat archaic, chosen for atmosphere.
  • House names the physical structure; dwelling names the act of living in it — the word is literally the gerund of dwell, "to live."
  • Domicile is legal and technical.
  • At the animal end of the scale, den, lair, nest, and habitat all belong to beasts — precisely the contrast the passage sets up: the "animal dens" belong to creatures, the "dwellings" to beings presumed to have homes in the human sense.

In collocation, dwelling is found in fixed compounds — dwelling place, dwelling house — and in deliberate administrative or archaeological prose: cave dwellings, cliff dwellings, the dwellings of the dead (tombs). The plural dwellings is the standard form in ethnographic and speculative writing for the habitations of a people or a species, which is exactly the register the passage exploits. Register: formal-neutral, deliberately colder than home; the word is a noun of settlement without warmth, chosen when the writer wants to describe where beings live without sentiment, or wants the faint estrangement of seeing even alien structures through the plain glass of the word "dwelling."
