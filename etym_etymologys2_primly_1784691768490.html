PIE *per- “forward, through, before, in front, over” (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; the root of Latin per “through”, pro “before, for”, and of the Germanic words far and first) → Latin prīmus “first, foremost, finest, most excellent” (superlative of prior “former, first”, from the same root; classical Latin, 2nd–1st c. BCE) → Old French prim / prims “fine, delicate, slender, neat” (11th–14th c.) → Middle English prim “excellent, fine” (14th c., borrowed from Old French) → Early Modern English prim “stiffly proper, formal, demure, precisely neat” (16th–17th c.; the sense narrows from excellence of quality to exactness of manner) → English prim + adverbial suffix -ly (from Old English -līce “in the manner of”) → primly “in a stiffly formal, precise, and demure manner” (late 16th c.) => primly (modern English adverb) DEBATE / NUANCE: The descent of prim through Old French prim < Latin primus is the standard account (OED, Etymonline) and the Latin half is secure; the specifically French development of “fine, delicate, neat” from “first, foremost” is well attested in Old French, but the senses “precise, formal, demure” are an English development of the 16th–17th centuries. A minority older view derived prim from Welsh prim “brisk” or from a dialect word, but these are not supported by the attestations. The suffix -ly is native English (Old English -līce), cognate with German -lich and Dutch -lijk.
