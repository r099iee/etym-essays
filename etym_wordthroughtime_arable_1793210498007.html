- Roman farming — the Latin seed: arabilis names land that can be ploughed, the working antithesis of pasture and wood in Virgil and Columella.
- c. 1400 — English takes it up: arable enters farm leases and manor rolls for corn land held by villeins and yeomen under open-field custom.
- Tudor enclosures — a word of contention: arable torn up for sheep runs or sheep runs torn back to arable, with pamphleteers counting every converted acre.
- 18th–19th c. — improvement and Cobbett: turnips, clover, and marl push arable up steep hillsides; Cobbett rides past roof-steep ploughland and makes it evidence of English industry.
- 20th c. — the statistical acre: censuses, subsidies, and set-aside fix arable as a measured category — hectares of temporary crops and fallow against permanent grass.
- Today — guarded ground: the best and most versatile arable is a protected national asset, while gardeners borrow the word for any bed worth sowing.
