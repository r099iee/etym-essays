THE RISE OF SANCTUS: ROMAN RELIGION, ROMAN LAW

The biography of sanctity begins in the ritual and legal life of ancient Rome. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *sak-, traditionally reconstructed as 'to make sacred, to consecrate', the same root that produced Latin sacer, 'sacred, set apart, accursed'. The early Roman conceived of the sacred as a boundary: what was sacer belonged to the gods and was thereby cut off from ordinary use, so that to violate it was to incur divine punishment. From this root the verb sancire was formed with a nasal infix - the stem *sank- - giving 'to consecrate, to make sacred by a solemn act'. Crucially, the same verb was used for the ratification of laws and treaties: a statute or a compact was sancire'd, 'sanctioned', so that it became as binding as a religious vow. The participle sanctus described what the act had produced - the consecrated object, the ratified decree - and from the start the word carried a double force, at once holy and legally unassailable.

THE ABSTRACT NOUN AND THE CHRISTIAN TRANSFER

From sanctus Latin built the abstract noun sanctitas, 'holiness, sacredness, inviolability', a word already current in the first century BCE. Roman prose used it of the holiness of temples and of the moral purity of blameless citizens; Cicero could praise a man's sanctitas as his unimpeachable integrity. The great transfer came with Christianity. The Latin Church took over the whole family: sanctus became the standing word for 'saint', the Vulgate used sanctitas and its kin for the purity demanded of the faithful, and sancire's legal halo was re-read spiritually - the 'sanction' of the old covenant became the sanctification of the new. It was through this Christian Latin that the word reached the Romance vernaculars and then English. Old French had saintete (from saint) alongside a learned sanctite; Middle English borrowed the learned form in the fourteenth century as sanctite, recorded around 1380 in religious contexts meaning 'holiness, sanctity'.

SOUND CHANGES AND THE PASSAGE INTO ENGLISH

  • Latin sanctitas had the cluster -nct- with a velar stop before t; the Romance vernaculars weakened and vocalized the consonant groups (Latin -ct- > Old French -t-, with the preceding vowel lengthened or nasalized).
  • Old French saintete shows the nasalized diphthong from Latin sanctus; the learned borrowing sanctite kept the spelling closer to Latin and furnished the form English actually took.
  • Middle English spelling -ct- (sanctite) preserved the Latin shape, and Early Modern English stabilized the modern spelling sanctity with the -y abstract ending, standard by the sixteenth century.
  • Pronunciation simplified the historic cluster: the modern RP /'saeNk.tE.ti/ keeps a velar /k/ in the first syllable that the French-derived form had long since dropped, a spelling pronunciation imported with the learned word.
SEMANTIC DRIFT: FROM ALTAR TO CONTRACT, LIFE, AND MARRIAGE

The modern career of sanctity is a story of secularization - or, more precisely, of the word's legal sense re-ascending. The religious sense ('the sanctity of the temple') remained constant from the fourteenth century. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, however, English writers began to apply the noun to institutions and principles with the full force of the Roman legal inheritance: the sanctity of oaths, of covenants, of the marriage bond, of royal authority. Eighteenth-century political and legal prose speaks of the sanctity of contracts and of treaties; nineteenth-century moral writing speaks of the sanctity of human life; and in the twentieth century the phrase 'sanctity of life' became a cornerstone of bioethics, invoked in the great debates over abortion, euthanasia, and capital punishment. In every case the semantic bridge is the same one built by the Romans: what is sanctified is set apart, declared inviolable, and shielded from trade, compromise, or convenience. The word supplies exactly what the secular vocabulary of 'value' or 'worth' cannot - an absolute, a thing not to be touched.

SOCIO-CULTURAL FACTORS AND LITERARY ATTESTATIONS

  • Chaucer and the fourteenth-century religious writers used sanctite for the holiness of the saints and the church, the sense in which the word entered English.
  • In the Renaissance the noun migrates into law and politics: the 'sanctity of the person', 'sanctity of kings', and 'sanctity of contracts' appear in Elizabethan and Jacobean prose, reviving the Roman fusion of religion and law.
  • The nineteenth century anchors the word in the domestic and the ethical: 'sanctity of the home', 'sanctity of marriage' become fixed phrases of Victorian moral rhetoric.
  • Twentieth-century law and bioethics give the word its modern centre of gravity: 'the sanctity of life' (in medico-legal debate) and 'the sanctity of contract' (in commercial law) are the two phrases that carry the word today.
  • The ironic shadow, sanctimoniousness, appears in the seventeenth century; a sanctimonious person claims sanctity falsely, and the word family has been split ever since between sincere inviolability and mocked pretence.
DIRECTION OF CHANGE AND REGISTER

Sanctity has not drifted far from its root meaning; what has changed is the balance of its uses. The religious sense is still alive but no longer dominant; the legal-ethical sense, present in embryo in Roman law, has become the word's modern centre. The register is formal and elevated throughout - the noun is at home in law, bioethics, high rhetoric, and religious writing, and rare in everyday speech. Its story is that of a Roman religious-legal term carried by the Church into medieval English, re-borrowed by secular ethics in the modern age, and now standing as the strongest available English word for the claim that something is absolutely inviolable.
