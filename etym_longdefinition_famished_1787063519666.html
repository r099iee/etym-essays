Famished means suffering from extreme hunger; starving; so hungry that one is weak, desperate, or at the point of collapse. The word is the past participle of the verb to famish, 'to starve, to die or cause to die of hunger', and in its modern everyday use it is the emphatic word for being very hungry — 'I'm famished!' — though its older and stronger senses, of starvation and death from hunger, still colour the word with real severity.

Core sense — extreme hunger: The primary modern meaning is simply 'very hungry, ravenous'. A famished person (or animal) has gone long enough without food that hunger has become acute: empty, aching, faint, desperate for anything to eat. In the source context, a man has 'a famished and a savage look like a dog that has been chained and forgotten in a kennel for a week': the word is doing double duty — the hunger is real and extreme, and it has produced the gaunt, wild look of a starved creature.

  • Sense 1 — Extremely hungry, starving (modern, emphatic): 'I'm famished!' — the standard colloquial exaggeration for a healthy but powerful appetite; also used sincerely of real hunger.
  • Sense 2 — Killed or dying of hunger (older, literal): the original English sense, still alive in historical and solemn contexts — 'famished with cold and hunger', 'a famished prisoner'.
  • Sense 3 — Starved, deprived of food (of animals): 'a famished dog' — an animal so underfed it is gaunt and desperate; the sense that governs the source text.
  • Sense 4 — Deprived or starved of something (figurative extension): rare but attested — 'famished for affection', 'famished for news' — hunger transferred from food to any essential thing that is lacking.
  • Sense 5 — Emaciated appearance: the adjective applied to a look, face, or body so thin and hollow from want of food that it betrays starvation ('a famished look').

Intensity scale and near-synonyms: Famished sits at the far end of the hunger scale. In ordinary usage the scale runs from peckish (mildly hungry), through hungry, ravenous, and starving, to famished at the extreme; each step up raises both the appetite and the drama. Starving is the most common everyday intensifier, famished the more literary and emphatic one, and ravenous the word for a hunger that is wild and devouring. Choosing famished rather than hungry always raises the emotional temperature of a sentence — it turns a complaint about a missed lunch into a small declaration of need.

Register and tone: Famished is a strong, rather literary word. In modern speech it is used hyperbolically ('I could eat a horse — I'm famished!'), where it has a warm, emphatic, faintly old-fashioned charm; in writing about war, famine, shipwreck, or animal cruelty it reverts to its serious sense of true starvation. This double register — cosy exaggeration on the one hand, grim starvation on the other — is the word's distinctive feature.

Grammar and collocation: Famished is a past-participial adjective used predicatively ('the children were famished') and attributively ('a famished look'); it combines with 'with' to give the cause ('famished with hunger', 'famished with cold'); and it stands in the fixed pair 'famished and frozen'. The verb to famish is now uncommon, but famished and the noun famine keep the family alive — famine being the great shortage of food that leaves whole populations famished.

Polysemy summary: Famished has narrowed and softened over time but never split: every sense is hunger pushed to an extreme. The older senses — killed or dying of hunger — are still latent in the word, which is why even a joking 'I'm famished!' carries a shadow of genuine desperation; the modern hyperbolic sense is the ancient starvation sense, worn smooth by affection and table manners.
