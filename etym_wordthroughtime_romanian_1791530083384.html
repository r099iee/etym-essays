- 16th century - român names the Romanian-speaking Orthodox population, in contrast with Saxons, Hungarians and the Greek-speaking clergy; the word is also used for a dependent peasant or serf, a social sense now archaic.
- 17th-18th century - the endonym competes with foreign Vlach and Walachian; chroniclers and church writers use român to claim Roman descent and Orthodox faith.
- Late 18th century - the Transylvanian School argues that Romanians are the direct heirs of Roman Dacia; "Romanian" becomes a scholarly and political programme as well as a name.
- 19th century - the principalities unite in 1859-61 and adopt the name România; the Latin alphabet replaces Cyrillic, French loans pour in, and English adopts "Romanian" alongside older "Rumanian".
- 20th century - Greater Romania (1918), the communist period, and the Soviet-sponsored "Moldovan" language project give the word contested political edges; emigration and diaspora spread it worldwide.
- 21st century - EU membership in 2007 and large-scale migration make "Romanian" a familiar label in Western Europe, often entangled with debates about labour, language and identity.
