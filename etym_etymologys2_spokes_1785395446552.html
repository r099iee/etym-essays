PIE *spey- 'sharp, pointed' (reconstructed), extended as *spey-k- (with a -k- suffix) → Proto-Germanic *spaikōn 'splinter, pointed stick; spoke of a wheel' (reconstructed; shared across Germanic — cf. Old English spāca, Middle Dutch speke, Old High German speihha) → Old English spāca 'spoke of a wheel' (attested before 1000) → Middle English spoke 'spoke' (13th c.) → Modern English spoke → plural with -s → SPOKES 'the radiating rods of a wheel' (plural attested from Old English on).

NOTE ON DEBATE / CAUTION: The sibling Germanic form *spīkō 'spike, nail' produced English spike and an early Slavic loan, Russian спица spítsa 'spoke; knitting needle'; Latin spīca 'ear of grain' (English spica, spigot) and spīna 'thorn' (English spine) are sister reflexes of the same root, NOT ancestors of the English word; *spey- has no established Indo-Iranian reflex, so no Sanskrit/Hindi/Odia branch is given rather than invented; English spokesman is unrelated (it derives from spoke, the past tense of speak).
