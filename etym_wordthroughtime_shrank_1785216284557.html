- [Old English-Middle English: physical contraction] - 'shrink' begins as the verb of drawing together: flesh shrinks from cold, cloth shrinks when wet, fruit shrivels — pure, physical, value-free contraction.
- [14th-16th century: figurative recoil] - the word extends to the mind: to shrink from a task or danger is to pull back reflexively, and the body-metaphor of drawing into oneself hardens into a standard sense of fearing withdrawal.
- [17th-19th century: timidity register] - 'a shrinking nature,' 'she shrank from publicity': the participle paints the timid, the bashful, the faint-hearted — modest fear rather than dramatic panic.
- [20th century: slang 'shrink'] - American English clips 'head-shrinker' to 'shrink,' and the old verb of contraction becomes a noun for the psychiatrist who compresses the troubled mind.
- [Contemporary: contraction everywhere] - the physical sense turns economic and quotidian — shrinking economies, budgets, attention spans, retail shrinkage — while 'to shrink back' still preserves the primal, reflexive drawing-together of the body in fear.
