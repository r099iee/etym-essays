The English word jerk (plural jerks) has a rich, polygenetic history characterized by expressive sound symbolism (phonosemantics) in early Modern English, kinematic formalization in the industrial era, and cross-linguistic lexical convergence with indigenous South American vocabulary.

The primary kinetic and mechanical sense emerged in late 15th- and 16th-century English as an expressive, onomatopoeic rhyming variant of yerk (or yirrk), an expressive verb meaning "to strike sharply with a whip, lash with a rod, or kick out violently with the hind legs." In Elizabethan literature, yerk and jerk were used interchangeably to denote the sharp snapping crack of a horsewhip or the sudden, violent defensive kick of a startled stallion. William Shakespeare utilized yerk in Henry V (Act IV, Scene 7) when describing dying warhorses on the battlefield: "and their wounded steeds / Fret fetlock deep in gore, and with wild rage / Yerk out their armed heels." In Othello (Act I, Scene 2), Iago laments that he lacked the wickedness "to yerk him here under the ribs."

Phonosemantically, the initial affricate /dʒ/ or semi-vowel /j/ combined with the central vowel /ɜː/ and terminal voiceless velar stop /k/ mimics a sharp, sudden snapping tension released with instant kinetic impact—aligning with a family of expressive English kinetic verbs such as firk, quirk, smirk, and irk.

During the 17th and 18th centuries, the phonetic variant with initial j- (jerk) completely supplanted yerk in standard English. The meaning shifted from the active infliction of a whip lash to the resulting physical reaction: a sudden, involuntary muscular twitch, sharp start, or sudden pulling dislocation of a mechanical part. In 19th-century railway engineering and mechanics, jerk was applied to the jarring, violent shock experienced by passengers when a steam locomotive suddenly engaged its couplers.

This mechanical reality led directly to the formalization of jerk in classical mechanics and vector calculus. Physicists (notably William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, and modern mechanical engineers) recognized that human physiological discomfort and mechanical material failure are caused not by constant acceleration, but by the rapid change of acceleration over time. Thus, the third time-derivative of position vector $ec{r}$ ($ec{j} = d^3ec{r}/dt^3$) was mathematically baptized as jerk.

Concurrently, in the Americas, the word acquired an entirely independent etymological tributary through cross-linguistic contact. In South America, the indigenous Andean Quechua language used the word ch'arki to designate strips of dried, cured, salted llama or alpaca meat. Spanish conquistadors adopted this into American Spanish as charqui, which English sailors, buccaneers, and North American frontiersmen anglicized as jerked beef and jerky (further giving rise to Caribbean jerk chicken/pork).

In 20th-century American vernacular culture, jerk proliferated into commercial slang through the soda jerk (who rapidly pulled the carbonation levers of pharmacy soda fountains), before solidifying around the 1930s into ubiquitous English slang for an obnoxious, contemptible person.
