In Oscar Wilde's tale The Happy Prince the word appears twice in the Swallow's vision of Egypt: his friends fly up to "the Second _____" — one of the six great rock-barriers of the Nile between Aswan and Khartoum — and the yellow lions' roar is "louder than the roar of the _____", the thundering water itself. That is the word's primary modern sense: a large waterfall, or a long violent stretch of rapids, in which the entire volume of a river plunges over a step of rock or roars down a steep descent. In geography and travel writing "the Cataracts" is used absolutely of the six Nile barriers, which for thousands of years marked the southern frontier of Egypt and the gate to Nubia.

Primary sense — the water. A cataract differs from an ordinary waterfall in scale and violence: it implies the whole river descending at once, deafening and impassable. The word also names the rush of water in general ("a cataract of rain") and, by metaphor, any overwhelming outpouring — "a cataract of abuse", "a cataract of light", "a cataract of information". The permanent core of the meaning is the idea of something bursting or pouring down uncontrollably.

Secondary sense — the eye (now dominant in everyday speech). In ophthalmology a cataract is a progressive opacity of the crystalline lens of the eye. The lens, normally transparent, becomes cloudy and scatters light: vision blurs, colours fade, glare and halos appear, and reading or night driving grows difficult. It is overwhelmingly age-related (nuclear, cortical, posterior-subcapsular types), but may be congenital, traumatic, or provoked by diabetes, prolonged steroid use, or radiation. Cataract is the single leading cause of blindness in the world — roughly half of all global blindness — precisely because it is so common; about nine in ten of those blinded can have their sight restored by surgery. Modern phacoemulsification, which breaks the lens with ultrasound and inserts a folded intraocular lens, is among the most frequent and most successful operations in medicine, and the name is now heard far more often in the clinic than at the riverside.

Tertiary and historical senses. (1) In older architecture: a portcullis or floodgate — a grating that "dashes down" to close a gate or pen water; from it comes the Biblical-sounding phrase "the cataracts of heaven", the floodgates of the sky in the Deluge story, given its most famous English form by Milton in Paradise Lost. (2) Older English could use the word for a waterspout or a heavy shower. (3) Poetic and rhetorical writing keeps the watery sense alive in an exalted register — Wordsworth in "Tintern Abbey" remembers "the sounding cataract / Haunted me like a passion" (1798), and the image of the roaring fall became the Romantics' type of the sublime.

The semantic bridge. Every sense hangs on the Greek verb katarassein, "to dash down, strike down": katá "down" + arássein "I dash, smash". The noun kataráktēs named anything that crashed downward — a waterfall, a falling weight, a dropped portcullis. The transfer to the eye is ancient: physicians believed that a humour or discharge "poured down" into the eyeball and clouded the pupil, exactly as water pours over a cliff — the opacity was a "waterfall" descending over the sight. One image, something coming down with a crash, generated the waterfall, the floodgate and the eye-disease alike.

  • Water sense: a large waterfall or violent rapids; the six Nile Cataracts; a deluge of water.
  • Medical sense: clouding of the eye-lens; the world's leading cause of treatable blindness.
  • Historical sense: a portcullis or floodgate; "cataracts of heaven" = the opened floodgates of the sky.
  • Figurative: any overwhelming torrent of sound, light, words or events.
