PIE *ǵneh₃- 'to know, to be able' (reconstructed, c. 4500-3500 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *kunniz / *kōniz 'knowing, able, bold' (reconstructed; zero-grade *ǵn̥h₃- with adjective suffix *-iz; both -n- and -nn- forms are cited in handbooks) → Old English cēne 'bold, brave, fierce' (attested before 1000; the 'sharp, keen' sense develops from the 13th c.) → Middle English kēne 'bold; sharp, severe; eager' (attested c. 1200) → Modern English keen 'sharp-edged; acute of sense or mind; eager' (16th c.) → comparative formation keen + -er (comparative suffix from Old English -ra) → KEENER 'more keen, more acute, more eager' (comparative attested in Middle English).

NOTE ON DEBATE: The Proto-Germanic intermediate is reconstructed variously as *kōniz or *kunniz (gemination unresolved); the shift from 'bold, brave' to 'sharp, keen, eager' is an internal English semantic development. Sister reflexes of the same PIE root — Sanskrit jā́nāti 'he knows', Hindi jānnā 'to know', Odia jāṇibā 'to know', Latin (g)nōscere, Greek gignṓskein — are cognates, NOT ancestors of English KEENER, whose direct line runs through the Germanic branch alone.
