The first root: *mel-, "bad, wrong, false" Malign is a true compound of two Indo-European elements, and each has its own biography. The first is the base that carries the word's meaning of badness: the Proto-Indo-European root *mel-, "bad, wrong, false." This is a root of corruption in the widest sense — whatever is stained, twisted, false, or crooked — and it left reflexes across the family. In Lithuanian it produced melas, "a lie," and meluoti, "to lie," a decisive witness that the root's core sense was falsity as well as badness. In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit mala, "dirt, impurity, stain," and from Sanskrit it flows directly into the modern languages of India: Hindi मल (mal), "dirt, filth," and Odia ମଳ (maḷa), "dirt," are tatsamas taken from the classical stock, so that a speaker of Hindi or Odia today uses words that a Sanskrit speaker of two and a half millennia ago would have recognised. The Greek word μέλας (mélas), "black, dark," is sometimes brought into this family on the strength of the shared sense of darkening or staining, but modern handbooks generally keep it apart: Greek mélas looks like a colour-root, and the "bad" root of malus is better witnessed by Lithuanian melas, "a lie." The reconstruction itself, however, is not beyond debate. Robert de Vaan treats the prehistory of Latin malus with caution, noting that while *mel- is the traditional reconstruction (Pokorny), the evidence is thin, and some scholars prefer to say only that the origin is uncertain. In Latin the root produced malus, "bad, evil, wicked, wrong," attested from the earliest Latin literature — Plautus uses it constantly in the third and second centuries BCE — and it proved extraordinarily productive. From malus came the adverb male, "badly," the foundation of malediction ("evil-speaking"), malefactor ("one who does evil"), malady ("an ill-keeping," from male habitus), and malevolent ("wishing evil"); from it came malitia, "badness, malice," the ancestor of English malice and malicious; and from it came the compound that matters for our word: malus joined to the suffix -gnus produced malignus, "ill-born, evil-natured." The genius of this family is that Latin did not merely have a word for "bad"; it distinguished the quality of badness in a person from the mere fact of badness. Malus is a plain judgment: the thing is bad. Malignus is a verdict on the disposition: the person is bad by nature, born that way. The same root gives English a complete scale of moral blame — malice (the emotion), malicious (the intention), malign (the nature), malefactor (the doer) — and at the top of that scale stands malignus, the word that makes badness innate, constitutional, a matter of birth rather than of choice.
  • Sound changes: the Latin cluster -gn- (malignus) passed into Old French as -gn- (maligne) and into Middle English as the palatalised gn- of malign(e); in modern English the g is silent and the vowel follows the French development (malign rhyming with "design").
  • Semantic drift: from a root meaning "false, wrong" (Lithuanian melas, "a lie"; Sanskrit mala, "dirt") to Latin malus "bad" to the verdict on character in malignus to the modern verb "to speak evil of" — a migration from falsity to badness to spiteful utterance.
  • Socio-cultural context: the seventeenth century, and especially the English Civil War, politicised the word: the adjective malignant was applied by Parliamentarians to Royalists, and the verbal use "to malign" hardened in the same polemical atmosphere, where calling an enemy bad by nature licensed one's own attacks.
  • Literary attestations: Ruskin's "neglect or malign in the public press" is the high rhetorical use; the adjective appears throughout Shakespeare's contemporaries and Milton, and the verb dominates eighteenth- and nineteenth-century writing about reputation, journalism, and party strife.
The second root: *ǵenh₁-, "to beget," and the suffix -gnus The second element of malign is the tiny suffix -gnus, "born, of such a kind," and it descends from one of the most fertile roots in all Indo-European: *ǵenh₁-, "to beget, to give birth, to produce." This is the root of birth and kind in nearly every branch of the family. In Indo-Aryan it gave Sanskrit jánati, "he begets, brings forth," and jánman, "birth, race," which flow into Hindi जन्म (janma), "birth," and Odia ଜନ୍ମ (janma), "birth," alongside जात (jāt), "caste," literally "birth." In Greek it gave γίγνομαι (gígnomai), "I become, am born," γένος (génos), "race, kind," the ancestor of gene, genetics, genesis, and genealogy, and γονή (gonḗ), "seed, offspring," the root of cosmogony and theogony. In Latin it gave gignere, "to beget," and its vast family: genus, "kind, race," the ancestor of general, generic, and generate; gens, "clan," the ancestor of gentry, genteel, and gentle; and the adjective-forming suffix -gnus itself. The suffix -gnus is a curiosity of Latin morphology: it is the old participle adjective "born, of such a kind" fossilised into an ending, and in classical Latin it survives in only a tiny matched pair — benignus, "well-born, kind," and malignus, "ill-born, evil-natured." The pair is a miniature of Latin social thinking: character is conceived as a matter of birth, of a nature given at the moment one is born. To be benignus is to have a good birth, a good nature; to be malignus is to have a bad birth, an evil nature — the word does not describe a bad act but a bad disposition, a badness in the blood. This is why malignus meant "spiteful, envious, stingy, of evil disposition," an adjective of character rather than of conduct, and why English malign inherits that fatalism: the verb's victim is presumed wicked at heart, so that even criticism of such a person becomes, in the maligner's eyes, a form of legitimate injury — or, in the eyes of the word's user, a deliberate cruelty. The two roots joined in Latin before the empire: malus, "bad," plus -gnus, "born," produced malignus, attested in Cicero and Lucretius in the first century BCE. Old French took it as maligne, "wicked, ill-natured," and Middle English borrowed it in the fourteenth century as the adjective malign, "evil, baleful." Then, in the seventeenth century — the age that gave English malignant, used first of deadly, ever-worsening diseases and then hurled by Parliamentarians at the Royalist party during the Civil War — English speakers made the fateful move of using malign as a verb, "to speak evil of, to defame." The shift from static nature to active deed is precise and revealing: one no longer merely says that someone is bad-natured; one says that someone is actively being bad-natured against another. Because the verb presupposes the adjective's fatalism, to malign someone is never merely to disagree — it is to treat them as though their badness were inborn and their character were the offence. Ruskin exploits exactly this weight when he speaks of the press "neglect[ing] or malign[ing]" the great living masters in public: malign is not criticism but malice, an act of evil-speaking committed against a name, and the word's whole double ancestry — a root of falsity and a root of birth — is compressed into its two syllables.
  • Sound changes: the Latin velar -g- of -gnus (malignus) was palatalised in Old French (maligne), and the English pronunciation drops the g entirely, retaining the French-final "nine" rhyme; the spelling, however, preserves the Latin -gn- to this day.
  • Semantic drift: from the birth-root (Greek genesis, Latin gignere, Hindi janma) to the fossilised suffix "born, of such a kind" to the character-verdict malignus "ill-born" to the verb "to speak evil of" — a migration from begetting to inborn nature to spiteful speech.
  • Socio-cultural context: the Roman conception of character as fixed at birth, mirrored in the matched pair benignus/malignus, is itself a cultural doctrine; the English verbal turn belongs to the age of religious and civil war, when calling an opponent evil by nature was the standard polemical weapon.
  • Literary attestations: malignus is common in Latin moralists (Seneca, Tacitus); English malign as adjective runs from Chaucer and Gower through the Renaissance; the verb emerges in the seventeenth century and reaches its rhetorical peak in Victorian prose, where Ruskin's Preface is a central example.
