ascertaining — the present participle and gerund of the verb ascertain: the action or process of finding something out for certain, of converting uncertainty into settled knowledge through investigation, observation, or enquiry. The -ing form is used both as a verb part ("they were ascertaining the facts") and as a noun ("the ascertaining of the facts"). Unlike casual guessing or haphazard noticing, ascertaining implies deliberate, systematic effort directed at a definite result: the fact is not merely suspected but made sure.

Core senses

  • Primary sense — empirical discovery: to determine the truth of a matter through evidence; e.g. "the ladies had the advantage of ascertaining from an upper window that he wore a blue coat, and rode a black horse." The agent checks against reality — looks, asks, measures — until belief is justified.
  • Gerund / nominal use: naming the activity itself ("the ascertaining of the facts took months") and as an attributive modifier ("an ascertaining procedure").
  • Mental / inferential use: one may ascertain motives, intentions, causes, or meanings — facts not directly visible, established by reasoning from clues.

Polysemy and secondary meanings

  • Legal and administrative sense: historically (and still in legal contexts), to ascertain a fact, amount, boundary, or right means to fix or settle it authoritatively — "ascertain the damages", "ascertain the date of the deed". The semantic bridge: Latin certus means "fixed, settled"; making a thing certain originally meant fixing it firmly (like a boundary), and only later came to mean confirming it in the mind.
  • Statistical / technical jargon: in statistics and the social sciences, "ascertainment" is the determination of facts from data, and "ascertainment bias" names the systematic distortion caused by the way facts are collected.
  • Philosophical / epistemological sense: ascertaining is the epistemic ideal of the empiricist tradition — knowledge secured by evidence, contrasted with mere conjecture. "Ascertainable" (able to be made certain) is a standard predicate in legal and scientific writing.
  • Register: formal, measured, bookish; everyday equivalents are "find out", "make sure of", "discover", "establish", "determine". In Austen's fiction the word carries gentle satirical weight: the Bennet ladies' "ascertaining" of a blue coat and black horse elevates genteel curiosity into a miniature act of empirical science.

Historical semantic bridge (in full)

The verb entered English from Old French acertener / ascertener "to make sure, certify", formed on certain "sure, fixed", ultimately from Latin certus, the settled past participle of cernere "to sift, separate, decide". The root image is agricultural: the sifted grain is what remains when the chaff has been winnowed away — the "certain" truth is what survives testing. The first English sense (14th–16th c.) was outward and legal: to inform someone officially, to establish a fact or title beyond challenge. Under Enlightenment empiricism (17th–18th c.) the direction of the verb reversed: one no longer made a fact certain by declaring it but discovered it to be certain by investigating. This reversal — from declaration to discovery — is exactly the sense Austen exploits in Pride and Prejudice.
