DOUBLY, adverb: to twice the normal degree or extent; in a twofold manner or amount; for two reasons or on two accounts. The word multiplies the intensity of an action or quality rather than counting repetitions of it: to guard a gate 'doubly' is not merely to set two watches but to make the guarding itself twice as strong and thorough. This intensifying, rather than enumerating, function is the heart of the word's meaning and the source of its usefulness. Because it folds a second ground into itself, DOUBLY commonly carries an implicit 'and therefore' or 'because': one is 'doubly glad' because two things have gone right, 'doubly sure' because two independent checks confirm the matter, 'doubly cautious' after a failure. It is an adverb of proportion and consequence, and it nearly always smuggles in a judgment — the doubled care, the doubled stake — rather than a mere doubled count. In the passage from The Hobbit, Thorin laughs that the goblins 'will guard it doubly after this' — after the dwarves' escape and the death of the Great Goblin, the watch on the gate will be redoubled because of the new peril; the word carries the sense of security intensified by recent disaster. The laughter is the telling touch: Thorin makes the doubling a dry, bitter jest, and 'doubly' carries at once the arithmetic of revenge — two guards instead of one — and the emotional weight of a failure that has taught the enemy a lesson. It is the adverb of consequence, of things done again and harder precisely because they were once done badly. Its senses mark out a precise semantic field:
  • Degree sense — to twice the normal degree or extent; twice as much or as strong (a doubly fortified gate, doubly certain).
  • Manner sense — in a twofold manner or way; in two ways at once (an action performed doubly, i.e. in two respects).
  • Grounds sense — for two reasons or on two accounts; with double cause (doubly sorry, doubly anxious).
  • Emphatic intensifier — in rhetorical speech, used to heighten an attitude rather than to compute a quantity ('I am doubly sure of it').
The word is standard and mildly formal, at home in expository prose, journalism, and speech; it is the adverbial partner of the adjective 'double' and the noun 'doubling'. It differs from 'twice', which counts occurrences of an action (he knocked twice) rather than intensifying a single action; from 'twofold', the formal adjectival form (a twofold increase) that cannot itself modify a verb; and from the bare adverb 'double' (to see double), which is confined to fixed phrases of optical confusion and cannot bear the figurative emphasis of 'doubly sure'. Where 'twice' is neutral arithmetic, DOUBLY is nearly always evaluative: it asserts that the care, the measure, or the feeling is proportionate to a doubled stake, and it commonly leaves the second reason implicit, ending the sentence on the attitude rather than the accounting. Its grammatical flexibility mirrors its semantic reach. As an adverb it modifies verbs (guarded doubly), adjectives and participles (doubly sure, doubly afraid), and entire propositions, and it can stand in comparative frames ('doubly so') where the second clause is suppressed entirely. In the medieval period it could even modify nouns in the now-archaic manner sense, 'in two ways at once'. The word also participates in set collocations — 'doubly bound', 'doubly damned', 'doubly indebted' — in which the doubled adverb lends a formal, almost legal weight, and this accounts for its persistence in solemn, moral, and comic registers alike, from sermons to the dry laughter of a dwarf king.
