- Old English (c. 9th–11th Century) - The ancestor verb *spillan* held a fierce and lethal meaning: "to kill, slay, destroy, ruin, or put to death"; *spilt* (or *gespilled*) described living beings slaughtered, mutilated, or irrevocably destroyed in battle.
- 12th to 14th Century (Middle English) - Shifted from the destruction of human life to the wasting or ruining of provisions, time, and physical substances; began to be applied specifically to the shedding of blood and the accidental loss of precious fluids like wine and oil.
- 15th to 16th Century - The modern domestic sense of accidentally upsetting a vessel and losing its liquid contents became fully established in everyday household English, while preserving secondary poetic senses of shed blood.
- 17th Century - The philosophical proverb took root in English folk wisdom; James Howell recorded the earliest print variants around 1659 ("No weeping for shed milk"), soon stabilizing into "no crying over spilt milk".
- 18th to 19th Century - "Spilt milk" became a universal conversational fixture throughout the British Empire and Victorian literature; Oscar Wilde famously satirized its philosophical misapplication in *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888).
- 20th to 21st Century - American English increasingly standardized to the regular form *spilled*, yet *spilt* remains universally recognized across global English within the frozen idiom and in British, Irish, and Australian literary prose.
