In Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince the Swallow tells the Prince that in Egypt "the pink and white doves" watch his companions "building a nest in the Temple of Baalbec". The spelling is the older English form of Baalbek — the ancient city of the Beqaa Valley in present-day Lebanon, some eighty-five kilometres north-east of Beirut, famous throughout the eastern Mediterranean for the colossal temple complex that the Greeks and Romans knew as Heliopolis, "City of the Sun". Wilde is writing romantic oriental geography: his Egypt scene gathers the whole Levant — the Nile, the temple of Baalbek in modern Lebanon — into one dream of the East, and the name "Baalbec" is exactly the spelling a cultivated Victorian reader would recognise from travel books.


The city and its temples. Baalbek stands at an altitude of about 1,170 metres at the head of the Beqaa, the great alluvial plain between the Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon ranges, on one of the ancient routes from the coast to the interior. Its monuments are among the most spectacular of the Roman world:


  • The Temple of Jupiter Heliopolitanus: the largest temple the Romans ever raised — approximately 88 by 48 metres with a forest of monolithic columns, of which six still stand on their platform. Its podium is built of three colossal stones, the famous "Trilithon", each weighing roughly 800–1,000 tonnes, lifted in the 1st century CE with no crane the modern visitor can imagine; a fourth block, the "Stone of the Pregnant Woman", was left half-cut in the quarry a kilometre away.
  • The Temple of Bacchus: built in the 2nd century CE, it is the best-preserved Roman temple in the Middle East — a Corinthian peripteral building whose cella walls, columns and carved portal survive almost intact; its inner doorway bears the celebrated vine-and-putto frieze. In the Byzantine period it was converted to Christian worship, and in the Middle Ages it became part of a fortified citadel.
  • The Temple of Venus: a small, exquisite circular temple of the 3rd century CE, dedicated to the goddess of love, on the edge of the sanctuary.

Religion and history. The site was sacred long before Rome: Baalbek was a cult-centre of the great West Semitic god Baal — the storm- and fertility-lord of the Phoenician and Aramaean world — and the name means, in the standard reading, "Baal of the Beqaa plain". When the Seleucid successors of Alexander the Great consolidated Syria, the city was renamed Heliopolis and its god was identified with the sun; under the Roman empire this became the syncretic cult of Jupiter Heliopolitanus (together with Venus and Mercury in a "Heliopolitan triad" attested on coins and inscriptions), served by a famous oracle and by priests described with astonishment by pagan and Christian writers alike. The sanctuary's surviving grandeur is a monument to the imperial age's love of colossal display: the Romans rebuilt the cult-house on a scale without parallel, and the site remained consecrated ground from Phoenician times through the Roman, Byzantine, Arab and Ottoman centuries — a church within the Temple of Bacchus, then a fortress under the medieval rulers, with earthquakes (notably 1157 and 1759) adding ruin to ruin.


European rediscovery and the spelling. European travellers began printing the ruins in the 17th and 18th centuries; Robert Wood and James Dawkins, who visited in 1751, published The Ruins of Balbec, otherwise Heliopolis in Coelosyria (1757) with engravings that made the site one of the set-pieces of the classical tour. French and English spellings wobbled for a century — Balbec, Baalbec, Balbek, Baalbek — and "Baalbec" was the form current in Victorian books and magazines, which is why Wilde used it. Modern scholarship and cartography standardised on "Baalbek", and the city became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. Its later history has been eventful: the German emperor Wilhelm II visited in 1898 and sponsored the first systematic excavations around 1900; the great stage still hosts the annual Baalbek International Festival; and even the devastating explosion at the port of Beirut in August 2020 was reported to have shaken and damaged parts of the ancient temples at this distance.


In literature. Beyond archaeology, "Baalbec" carries a literary resonance: for the Victorians it was the type of the ruined eastern sanctuary — immense, exotic, mysterious — and Wilde deploys it as the faraway landmark that makes the Swallow's Egyptian idyll vivid: a temple vast enough for a whole colony of nest-building swallows, watched by pink and white doves. The name is a proper noun and place-name, the older spelling of a real city, and its plural history belongs to language as much as to stone: Balbec, Baalbec, Baalbek — three spellings, one city, still standing in the Beqaa.


  • The site: the Beqaa Valley, Lebanon; altitude c. 1,170 m; a Phoenician/Aramaean cult-centre of Baal, Roman Heliopolis.
  • The temples: Jupiter Heliopolitanus (largest in the Roman world; the Trilithon), Bacchus (best-preserved), Venus (circular, 3rd c. CE).
  • The name: Semitic "Baal of the Beqaa"; Greek Heliopolis; English Balbec/Baalbec/Baalbek.
  • The spelling in Wilde: "Temple of Baalbec" — the Victorian form, current in 19th-century travel literature.
