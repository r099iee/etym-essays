The history of chime is a journey from the hollow of a drinking cup to the bell of a clock, the tune of a wind chime, and the interjection of a conversation — a word that has spent two and a half millennia being hollow, then musical, then sociable. The bowl and the cymbal. The oldest layer of the word is Greek κύμβη (kymbē), "bowl, cup, hollow vessel, hollow of a ship." The word's shape — a b-m-b sequence around a rounded vowel — evokes a curved, hollow object, and it belongs to a family of Greek words for hollow, rounded things: κύμβαχον (kymbakhon), κύβη (kȳbē), and the verbs of bowing and bending. From κύμβη the Greeks formed κύμβαλον (kymbalon), "cymbal," literally the "hollow-instrument": a pair of small metal bowls or cups clashed together to make a ringing, shimmering sound. The cymbal was a sacred and ecstatic instrument — used in the worship of Cybele, in Bacchic rites, and in the theatre — and the name simply describes the instrument's shape: two hollow cups. It is this naming-from-shape that later links the cymbal to the chime, because a bowl and a bell are the same geometry: a hollow metal vessel that rings when struck. The late classical period also used cymbala for small bowl-bells strung in rows, an instrument type that already prefigures the chime bar and the wind chime. The status of the Greek root. Whether κύμβη descends from an Indo-European root or was borrowed into Greek from an earlier Mediterranean substrate is debated. A PIE root *(s)geub- or *gembh-, "to bend, to curve, to hollow out," is sometimes cited to explain the family, with cognates claimed in Germanic (camb, "comb," from the curved-toothed shape) and elsewhere; but Beekes and other modern handbooks are cautious and classify κύμβη as possibly Pre-Greek, noting that "hollow, curved" words with this shape pattern are abundant in the substrate vocabulary of the Aegean. The prudent statement is that the word is certainly ancient in Greek but that its ultimate, pre-Greek or Indo-European provenance cannot be fixed with confidence. The rest of the chain — Greek to Latin to French to English — is far better documented. Into Latin and the medieval church. Latin took κύμβαλον as cymbalum, "cymbal," attested in Cicero and Virgil and given enormous currency by the Vulgate, where cymbala appear in Psalm 150: "Praise him upon the loud cymbals." Medieval Latin and the church's ritual use of small bells and cymbala kept the family alive. The crucial transfer into English is documented by the Old French form chimbe / chymbe, "cymbal," attested in the 12th century, which entered Middle English as chimbe and chime. The Middle English word, attested from around 1230, had already widened its sense from "cymbal" to "bell" and to "the ringing of a bell or set of bells," and by the 14th and 15th centuries it was fully naturalized for church bells and the bells of clocks. The sound change French chimbe → English chime reflects ordinary English vowel-lengthening before a nasal-plus-cluster environment and the loss of the final -be, which the modern spelling preserves silently. The clock and the single bell. From the later Middle Ages the chime became inseparable from timekeeping. Cathedral towers and town clocks carried sets of bells tuned to play short melodies — the chimes — and the word settled into the sense "the sound or set of bells of a clock." A single bell of such a set, struck to give a time signal, was itself called a chime, and this is the sense at work in the Red Chamber passage: a warning chime from the inner gate is a single functional bell-strike announcing an arrival. The noun's grammar also produced the verb: to chime, "to ring or sound," and to chime the hour, the phrasing of a clock. Because the sounds of a chime are tuned to fit together, the word naturally developed musical and social metaphors. To "chime in" (attested from the 19th century) pictures a voice joining the sound of others, like a new bell entering a tune; to "chime with" pictures agreement as harmony, two notes sounding together without dissonance. Sound change and form. The English form passed through a set of minor but regular changes: Old French chimbe (with the nasal vowel of -im-) gave Middle English chimbe/chime; the modern pronunciation /tʃˈaɪm/ shows the Great Vowel Shift raising the long vowel to [aɪ], the regularization of the spelling to chime, and the loss of the final silent -e in pronunciation. The word also attracted an analogical relative, the verb and noun chim (obsolete or dialectal for the same ringing idea), and produced the modern compound wind chime, which reintroduces the ancient image of small hollow tubes and cups ringing in the breeze — the descendant of the cymbal rows of antiquity. Socio-cultural trajectory. The word has travelled from sacred ritual to municipal bureaucracy and domestic decoration. It began as the sacred cymbal of ancient cult, became the church bell of medieval Christianity, became the public clock of the modern town, became the warning signal of the great household (the yamen gate chime in the passage), and ended as the wind chime of the garden and the metaphorical voice that "chimes in" with a conversation. Through all of this the core meaning never changed: a clear, musical, hollow metal sound, struck and rung — the sound of a bowl that has learned to speak.
