Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *steh₂- 'to stand, to be standing' (reconstructed root; the source of English stand via Germanic, Latin stō 'to stand' and its whole family, Greek hístēmi 'to stand', Sanskrit sthā- 'to stand'). Stage 2 — PIE reduplicated present *stí-steh₂- 'to cause to stand, to set, to place' (reconstructed; the reduplicated form underlying Latin sistō and Greek hístēmi). Stage 3 — Latin sistō, sistere 'to set, place, cause to stand, stop' (attested from the 2nd century BCE; the reduplicated verb built on the same root as stō). Stage 4 — Latin īnsistō, īnsistere 'to set foot on, stand upon, press upon, urge, dwell on' (formed from in- 'on, upon' + sistō; attested from the 1st century BCE, e.g., in Cicero and Livy). Stage 5 — Latin īnsistēns, īnsistent- (present participle of īnsistō) 'standing upon, pressing, urgent' (classical Latin). Stage 6 — English insistent 'pressing, urgent, unrelenting in demand' (borrowed from the Latin participle, first attested 1620s; the verb insist, from Latin īnsistere, was borrowed slightly earlier, 1580s). Stage 7 — Modern English INSISTENT 'demanding attention, persistence, or compliance in a firm, unrelenting manner' (adjective in continuous use from the 17th century onward; the nouns insistence, insistency and the adverb insistently formed in the 17th–18th centuries). Note on the derivation: every stage is attested or rests on the standard reconstruction of the root, and the only nuance worth noting is that the English adjective comes directly from the Latin present participle īnsistēns rather than through French (French insistant is a later parallel borrowing from the same Latin form). No stage is debated.
