The word is a three-part English formation: the prefix be- + the noun night + the participial suffix -ed. Separate chains follow for each element, then the composite verb and participle. Chain 1 — "night" (the core noun): - PIE *nókʷts "night" (the standard Indo-European word for night; attested through Greek núx, Latin nox, Sanskrit nák, Lithuanian naktis; the o-grade ablaut and the final cluster are securely reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *nahts "night" (reconstructed; attested through Old English niht, Gothic nahts, Old Norse nátt, Old High German naht) → Old English niht, neaht, næht "night, darkness, the time of darkness" (attested from the earliest records) → Middle English night, nihte, nyght "night" (12th–15th centuries) → Modern English night "the period of darkness between sunset and sunrise" → the base of the verb benight. Chain 2 — the prefix "be-": - PIE *h₁epi "near, at, by, on" (the root of Greek epí "on, upon" and Latin ob; the Germanic development is debated, but *bi- is the usual reflex as a preposition meaning "by, near, about") → Proto-Germanic *bi "by, near, around, about" (preposition and prefix) → Old English be- (the unstressed, reduced form of the preposition bī "by," used as a verb-forming prefix with the force "about, all over, thoroughly; around") → Middle English be- → Modern English be-, the verb-forming prefix, as in benight. Chain 3 — the verb "benight" and the participle "benighted": - be- + night → benight (verb, 16th century, first attested c. 1560s) "to overtake or envelop with the darkness of night; to darken, to plunge into darkness" → benighted (past participle and adjective, first attested c. 1570s) "overtaken by night; enveloped in darkness" → figurative sense "in moral or intellectual darkness, ignorant, unenlightened" (attested from the early 17th century onward) → Modern English BENIGHTED. Chain — the participial suffix -ed: - PIE *-tó- (the verbal-adjective and participle suffix, "having been ...-ed") → Proto-Germanic *-daz / *-þaz (the past-participle suffix of weak verbs) → Old English -ed, -od, -ad (the weak past-participle endings) → Middle English -ed → Modern English -ed, giving benighted. Note on debate: the deeper ancestry of the prefix be- involves a reconstruction of how PIE *h₁epi relates to Germanic *bi; this is standard but involves some uncertainty about the ablaut and the exact Indo-European distribution. The verb benight itself is a securely attested English coinage of the 16th century, and the sequence benight → benighted → figurative benighted is fully documented; the only nuance is that the figurative sense begins almost immediately, so that the literal and moral uses overlap from the start.
