ontological is the adjectival form of ontology, meaning pertaining to the nature of being, existence, or reality as such. In philosophy it qualifies questions, claims, insecurities, or commitments that concern what there is and what it means to be, as opposed to what we know (epistemological) or how we should act (ethical). Ontological insecurity — the source phrase, made famous by psychiatrist R. D. Laing — denotes a deep, structural anxiety not about this or that danger but about the very coherence and continuity of the self: am I real, continuous, and distinct in a stable world?

Beyond strict philosophy the adjective has radiated into psychology, sociology, literary theory, and information science, each preserving the core sense of concerning fundamental being while adding local colour. Theologians speak of ontological guilt; psychologists of ontological anxiety; computer scientists of ontological modelling (with a different stress and sense lineage via ontology as a catalogue of what exists in a domain); literary critics of ontologicalmetafiction that foregrounds worlds rather than knowledge. In all cases ontological elevates a problem from the incidental to the constitutive: an ontological insecurity is not nervousness before an exam but dread that the self might dissolve.

  • Strict philosophical sense: relating to ontology as the science of being qua being — e.g., ontological categories (substance, property, event), ontological proof (Anselm's argument that God's essence entails existence), ontological commitment (what a theory says exists, Quine).
  • Existential-psychological sense: relating to the lived sense of being a continuous self — e.g., Laing's ontological insecurity versus ontological security (Giddens), where the adjective marks depth: insecurity about being itself, not about performance.
  • Theological sense: relating to ultimate reality or God as Being — e.g., Tillich's ontological anxiety of non-being, Rahner's ontological existential. Bridge from philosophy via Christian scholasticism identifying God with ipsum esse, Being itself.
  • Information-science sense: relating to a formal ontology — an explicit specification of concepts and relations in a domain — e.g., ontological engineering, ontological mapping. A 1990s semantic shift where the countable noun an ontology (a model) yields an adjective still spelled identically but meaning model-relative.
  • Literary-narratological sense: Brian McHale's distinction between epistemological (modernist, detective) and ontological (postmodernist, fantastic) dominant: fiction concerned with worlds, their making, collision, and dissolution.
  • Grammatical profile: attributive adjective (ontological questions, insecurities, status), rarely predicative except in philosophy (this claim is ontological); forms adverb ontologically and noun ontologist; collocates with argument, difference, insecurity, anxiety, security, commitment, status, turn; register invariably learned, signalling academic gravity.

To call insecurities ontological, as the source does, is thus to dignify and deepen them: our worries about the stability of the self are not vanities but vertigo before nothingness, which a flattering purposive psychology conveniently quiets. The adjective does heavy lifting with quiet economy, importing Heidegger, Sartre, and Laing into a single clause.
