diastole in its primary modern sense belongs to cardiology and physiology, where it denotes the rhythmic dilation or relaxation of the heart, especially of the ventricles, during which the chambers fill with blood after contraction. Physicians speak of diastolic pressure as the lower number in a blood-pressure reading, the pressure during this filling phase, of diastolic dysfunction when stiff ventricles fail to relax, and of the full cardiac cycle as diastole and systole together. The image is of opening out to receive: the muscle lets go, valves open, blood flows in, the heart grows larger to prepare the next thrust.

By ancient analogy the term also belongs to prosody, rhetoric and general poetics, where diastole means lengthening or expansion: in Greek metrics the lengthening of a syllable normally short, in rhetoric the extension or dilation upon a theme. The source context revives this sense with striking beauty: two ways of reading, linear through and imagistic at, taken together constitute the diastole and systole of reading itself, that is, its alternating expansion into image and contraction into line. In wider usage any rhythmic expansion can be called diastolic, from the diastole of lungs or arteries to the diastole of empires that breathe out before contracting.

The pairing with systole is essential and etymological: Greek diastole means a drawing apart or separation, systole a drawing together or contraction. Galen made them technical for pulse and heart in the 2nd century CE, and every later medicine retains the antithesis. To understand one is to feel the other: systole without diastole is a fist that never opens, diastole without systole a hand that never grasps. Literary critics therefore borrow the pair to describe attention itself as cardiac, swelling to take in and narrowing to follow through.

  • cardiac diastole: ventricular relaxation and filling; similar because it is the core clinical sense; differs because it is strictly anatomical and measurable.
  • arterial and pulmonary diastole: recoil expansion of vessels and lungs; similar because it shares relaxation-filling; differs because the organ is not cardiac muscle.
  • diastolic pressure and murmur: clinical signs heard or measured during filling; similar because they index the phase; differs because they are findings, not the phase itself.
  • metrical-rhetorical diastole: lengthening a short syllable, dilating on a topic; similar because it preserves expansion; differs because it is linguistic, not bodily.
  • general pulsation, expansion-contraction: diastole and systole of reading, history or breath; similar because it preserves rhythm; differs because it is metaphorical and evaluative.
  • grammatical diastole mark: in Greek printing, the double dot distinguishing sequences; similar because it marks separation; differs because it is orthographic.

Contemporary medicine refines diastole into early rapid filling, diastasis and atrial kick, while poetry keeps it as emblem of receptive dilation. Both agree: life proceeds by opening and closing, and diastole names the generous half.
