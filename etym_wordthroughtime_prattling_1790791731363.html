- 15th–16th Century: Originated as the active participle for infant babble and the affectionate chatter of young children.
- 17th–18th Century: Broadened in drama and satire to describe frivolous drawing-room gossip, shallow courtly flattery, and pastoral streams.
- 19th Century: Common in Victorian fiction to characterize superficial, trivial conversationalists who avoid deep emotion.
- 20th–21st Century: Adopted in critical theory (notably by Roland Barthes) to define a mechanical, alienated text that babbles without passion.
