• Rural: Similar in relating to the countryside; differs because rural is the neutral descriptive adjective — it tells you where something is, not how it feels — while pastoral carries a literary tradition and an idealization.
  • Bucolic: Similar in evoking herdsmen and countryside charm; differs because bucolic stresses the song-and-dance quaintness of rural life, often with amused affection, while pastoral can be stripped to its bare agricultural bones.
  • Idyllic: Similar in describing an idealized rural scene; differs because idyllic is almost always approving, a place of perfect contentment, whereas pastoral can be flat, empty, and ironic.
  • Rustic: Similar in belonging to the countryside; differs because rustic points to simplicity with a hint of the crude or unpolished — rustic furniture, rustic manners — while pastoral carries the shepherd and the classical dream.
  • Arcadian: Similar in evoking an idealized rural paradise; differs because Arcadian is even more purely mythological and utopian, referring to the real Greek region made legendary by pastoral poetry, while pastoral retains its agricultural, working roots.
  • Agrarian: Similar in relating to farming and the land; differs because agrarian is economic and political — about land ownership, crops, and rural society — while pastoral is aesthetic and literary, about the idealization of that life.
  • Georgic: Similar in being a rural literary genre; differs because the georgic (from Virgil's Georgics) is the didactic poem of agricultural work — ploughing, sowing, beekeeping — while the pastoral is the idealized poem of leisure and song; work versus ease.
  • Country: Similar in meaning of or belonging to the countryside; differs because country as an adjective is colloquial and general — country roads, country folk — with no classical tradition and no idealization attached.
  • Sylvan: Similar in being a poetic word for a rural landscape; differs because sylvan concerns the woods (Latin silva “forest”), not pasture and flocks, and it idealizes shade and wildness rather than shepherding.
