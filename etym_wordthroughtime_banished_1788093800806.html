- 14th century: borrowed from Old French with legal force — 'banysshen' meant to outlaw or exile by proclamation; the participle appears early in religious prose and verse: Adam and Eve 'banysshed' from Paradise (Cursor Mundi, c. 1325).
- 15th–17th centuries: the word of Tudor and Stuart politics — banished from court, banished from the realm; the participle hardens into a sentence-formula of chronicles and drama (Shakespeare's 'banish'd' in Romeo and Juliet and Richard II).
- 18th century: the Enlightenment turns the word inward — one banishes prejudice, melancholy and superstition; the verb migrates from the courtroom into the mind.
- 19th century: Romantic and Victorian prose make 'banished' the participle of longing — banished from home, from happiness, from grace; the theme of the exile-poet dominates the age.
- 20th–21st centuries: keeps its legal sense but is heard most in formal, literary and fantasy registers (Tolkien: 'Yet I wish he were banished from the world'); everyday speech substitutes 'expelled', 'deported' or 'kicked out'. The word has grown more solemn and archaic-perfumed — a verdict-word rather than a plain one.
