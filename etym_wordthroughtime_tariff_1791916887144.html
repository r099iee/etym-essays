- 1590s — the word names a printed arithmetical table or ready-reckoner for merchants: a neutral tool of commercial calculation with no political colour.
- 1660s — “tariff” is applied to the official schedule of customs duties; the table becomes an instrument of state finance, and the word begins its long association with the fiscal frontier.
- Late 18th–19th c. — with the rise of protectionism and free trade, “the tariff” becomes a political slogan: Hamilton’s reports, the Tariff of Abominations (1828), the Cobden–Chevalier treaty (1860), the McKinley Tariff (1890). The word now divides parties.
- Early 20th c. — utility regulation takes up the term: gas, electricity and telephone companies publish “tariffs”, and the word quietly becomes domestic and technical again.
- 1930s–present — Smoot–Hawley (1930), GATT/WTO negotiations and modern trade disputes keep the customs sense globally dominant, while “tariff” survives in British telecom for a price plan and in Indian English for electricity rates.
- Contemporary — the word is politically charged once more in “tariff wars” and retaliatory duties, yet remains a neutral technical term on utility bills and taxi ranks.
