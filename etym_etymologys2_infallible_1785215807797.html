One chain leads from the Latin verb for tripping to the English adjective; the PIE stage is a debated reconstruction noted at the end. Chain — from “to trip up” to INFALLIBLE: - PIE *bhel- “to strike, to strike down; to trip up” (reconstructed; the source conventionally proposed for Latin fallere; c. 4500–3000 BCE, unattested) → Latin fallere “to trip up, cause to stumble; to deceive, elude, let down” (Classical Latin, attested from the 2nd c. BCE; the concrete sense is making someone lose their footing, extended figuratively to deceiving or causing to err) → Latin fallibilis “liable to err, deceitful” (fallere + -bilis, “capable of being”; Late Latin) → Medieval Latin infallibilis “incapable of error” (in- “not” + fallibilis; scholastic Latin of the Middle Ages) → Middle French infallible (borrowed from Medieval Latin) → Middle / Early Modern English infallible (first recorded 15th c., a theological term for authority that cannot err) → Modern English INFALLIBLE “incapable of making mistakes or being wrong; unerring and completely dependable.” Note on debate: The PIE source of Latin fallere is not certain. The traditional handbooks derive it from *bhel- “to strike, strike down,” taking the image of tripping a person as the bridge from “strike” to “deceive”; but some scholars doubt the connection, and the root is sometimes given as *ghwel- or treated as uncertain. This *bhel- “to strike” must not be confused with the homophonous PIE *bhel- “to swell, blow up” that underlies English bellows, belly, and bladder — the two are distinct. The word's modern sense develops entirely within Latin and the Romance languages, so the reconstruction of the PIE stage does not affect the attested Latin–French–English chain.
