Extravagance is a noun meaning the spending of money, time, or resources far beyond what is necessary, reasonable, or affordable — lavish, wasteful excess — and by extension any immoderate, flamboyant excess of behaviour, language, or imagination.

The central financial sense is disapproving: an extravagance is something that costs much more than its function warrants and that could have been foregone. Funerals, weddings, designer clothes, champagne, and palatial houses are prototypical extravagances when viewed from a frugal or utilitarian point of view. To live in extravagance is to habitually spend lavishly; to regard something as an extravagance is to judge it an unaffordable luxury.

  • Wasteful lavish expenditure (core sense): Spending beyond means or need — e.g., private jets, five-star funerals, and daily caviar as extravagance. Implies lack of restraint or prudence. Often collocates with unnecessary, unjustifiable, reckless, sheer.
  • Luxury item itself (countable sense): An article or indulgence that is enjoyable but non-essential — e.g., holidays are our one extravagance, chocolates are my only extravagance. Here the tone can be affectionate and self-forgiving rather than condemnatory.
  • Immoderate behaviour or display (extended sense): Excess beyond propriety — e.g., extravagance of grief, extravagance of praise, sartorial extravagance. Covers flamboyant dress, emotional excess, and showy conduct generally.
  • Exuberant inventiveness (positive literary sense): Rich, unrestrained imagination — e.g., an extravagance of metaphors, Gothic extravagance, baroque extravagance. In criticism this is praise: abundance that overflows rule to delightful effect.

Polysemy follows a single metaphor of going beyond bounds. Money that wanders beyond budget is waste; emotion that wanders beyond decorum is excess; imagination that wanders beyond convention can be either purple bombast or genius, depending on the critic. The same wandering explains the archaic sense still found in older texts where extravagant meant simply irregular or digressive.

  • Connotation and register: Predominantly negative in economic and moral discourse (wasteful, prodigal), neutral-to-positive in aesthetic discourse (lavish, opulent, exuberant). Fiscal conservatives hear vice; artists hear vitality.
  • Domain usages: In economics, extravagance contrasts with thrift and efficiency; in ethics, with temperance and stewardship; in literary criticism, extravagance contrasts with restraint and economy of style; in everyday speech, I can't afford such extravagance is a polite refusal of luxury.
  • Collocations: unnecessary extravagance, reckless extravagance, wild extravagance, sheer extravagance, lap of extravagance, life of extravagance, fiscal extravagance, baroque extravagance.

In your context — funerals seem an unnecessary extravagance — the word activates its puritan-utilitarian critique: ritual display that wanders beyond practical use into costly show, defensible only if one values symbolism over economy.
