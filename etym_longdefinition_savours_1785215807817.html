SAVOURS is the third-person singular present tense of the verb savour (American spelling savor), meaning "to have a specified taste, smell, or quality," and, in its most common figurative use, "to smack of, suggest, or be tinged with" a particular — usually discreditable — characteristic. In the sentence before us — "But it savours almost of impertinence even to attempt to lift the veil on a mystery like this" — the word carries exactly this figurative force: attempting to pry into the private intimacy of a marriage "savours of impertinence," that is, it has the flavor or taint of unwarranted intrusion, an offensiveness that a delicate observer can detect as one detects a taste on the tongue. The nuance is one of detected quality rather than outright assertion: savours does not declare the intrusion to be impertinence outright; it says that impertinence is perceptible in it, unmistakable in its moral aftertaste, and the construction with of marks the quality as something the situation exudes or betrays to any careful sensibility.

  • Sense 1 — literal taste and smell: "to have a specified taste or smell," as in the water savours of sulphur, the cottage savours of woodsmoke and lavender. The verb works directly on the senses, naming the flavor or odor that a thing gives off.
  • Sense 2 — figurative flavour (savours of): "to be tinged with, smack of, suggest, or betray a quality," usually a discreditable one — savours of impertinence, savours of treason, savours of heresy, savours of hypocrisy. This is the dominant modern sense of the construction and the one used in the present sentence; the metaphor is of a taste detected on the tongue that reveals the nature of the situation.
  • Sense 3 — transitive "to enjoy or relish": "to taste or appreciate fully; to enjoy with relish," as in savour the moment, savour the victory, savour every mouthful. Here the verb is transitive and positive, meaning to dwell on and extract full pleasure or understanding from something; it is the sense that survives most strongly in modern speech ("savour the moment"), though the word is still bookish and deliberate.
  • Sense 4 — obsolete/archaic "to give flavour to": older transitive uses meant "to season, to impart flavour to" food; this culinary sense is now largely obsolete, but it underlies the derived adjective savoury ("having a pleasant taste or smell; not sweet; morally edifying or pleasant") and the noun savoury ("a savoury dish served at the end of a meal").
  • Historical bridge: the figurative sense is a metaphorical extension of the literal sensory one. Because a flavor is something one samples on the tongue and judges by taste, a situation that "savours of" a quality is one whose nature becomes perceptible by a kind of moral tasting; and because a flavor detected in a situation is rarely a neutral one, the construction acquired a characteristic note of censure. The word thereby became a delicate instrument of polite moral judgment: it says that something is tinged with a discreditable quality without the bluntness of an outright accusation.
  • Register and connotation: the figurative "savours of" is literary and somewhat old-fashioned, a decorous and allusive way of censuring; the positive "savour (the moment)" is formal but current. The word's sensory metaphor gives it an almost physical concreteness: qualities are tasted, and their aftertaste is telling.
  • Differentiating terms: smacks (of) (colloquial, stresses discernible resemblance), suggests/hints at (neutral, merely point toward a possibility), resembles (likeness of form or appearance), reeks (of) (intensifies to an offensive extreme), savour (of) is the refined, literary middle term — it detects a quality, usually negative, with delicacy.

Etymology and form. Savour descends from Latin sapor "taste, flavour," via Old French savour; the underlying PIE root *sap- ("to taste, perceive by the senses") also produced Latin sapere "to taste; to be wise" — the famous "taste → know" doublet — and hence sapiens, sapient, sapid, insipid, and French savoir. The third-person singular present savours rhymes with "flavours"; the spelling with u is the British/Commonwealth form, while American English uses savor/savors.
