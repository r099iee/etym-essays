- Old English — the verb means to seize, clutch, or take hold; it belongs to heroic poetry and to the language of hands and hawks.
- Middle English — Gripe still means grasp, and the noun means a hold or a handful; the sense “to grip with anxiety or sin” develops in religious prose.
- 16th c. — “The gripes” becomes the everyday name for colic, and the verb means to suffer or cause a sharp stomach pain.
- 17th c. — Grip takes over the physical sense of holding, leaving gripe to pain, archaic clutching, and nautical lashings.
- 19th c. — Proprietary gripe waters for infants make the colic sense familiar in every household, and medical writing uses griping pains routinely.
- 1930s — American English records gripe as “complain,” and the word spreads through journalism, military slang, and campus speech.
- 21st c. — The complaint sense dominates colloquial use on both sides of the Atlantic, while the colic sense survives chiefly in gripe water and veterinary usage.
