Hiccups is a word without a classical pedigree. Unlike most of the learned vocabulary in English, it cannot be traced to a Proto-Indo-European root, a Latin verb, or an Old French noun. It is onomatopoeic — an attempt to write down a bodily noise — and its history is therefore a history of spelling, not of sound laws.

The imitative nucleus

The earliest English forms, hicket and hickock, appear in the sixteenth century. Both begin with hick-, an echo of the sharp catch in the throat, and both end in a diminutive or familiar suffix: -et and -ock. The word is thus a double colloquialism: an imitation of a sound plus an affectionate or dismissive ending. It belongs to the same family of expressive formations as tick, click, and knock.

  • hicket (16th c.): an early imitative noun.
  • hickock (16th c.): a variant with the diminutive -ock.
  • hiccup (1570s): the form that eventually won, with -up perhaps influenced by the abrupt closing of the sound.

The “hiccough” mistake

In the 1620s a learned or pseudo-learned hand respelled the word hiccough, apparently by analogy with cough. The change was etymologically baseless: the word has nothing to do with coughing, and the pronunciation remained /ˈhɪkʌp/ rather than acquiring the /kɒf/ of cough. The respelling nonetheless persisted for centuries and is still found in British formal prose, where it lends a faintly archaic or medical air. It is a classic example of “spelling pronunciation” failing to take hold.

Medical history

Physicians have discussed the condition since antiquity under the Latin name singultus, “a sob, a catching of the breath.” Galen and later writers offered mechanical explanations; early modern medicine linked hiccups to digestive disturbance and to drunkenness, a connection preserved in the comic literature of the period. The vernacular word remained the property of everyday speech while singultus served the learned.

  • Singultus: the clinical Latin term, still used in medical writing.
  • Persistent and intractable hiccups: modern diagnostic categories based on duration.
  • Fetal hiccups: a normal phenomenon observed in utero.

Literature and comedy

Because hiccups are involuntary, audible, and faintly ridiculous, they are a gift to comic writers. Drunken characters hiccup; nervous suitors hiccup; pompous speakers are undone by them. In the source passage the “highly liquid” Bertram hiccups, and the detail does double duty: it signals his condition and undercuts his dignity. The word’s expressive sound makes it naturally funny in a way that singultus never could be.

Semantic drift into metaphor

The figurative sense developed in the twentieth century, especially in journalism and business writing. A “hiccup” in a process is sudden, brief, involuntary, and usually harmless — exactly the profile of the bodily event. The metaphor is thus unusually faithful to its source. It also carries a useful rhetorical function: calling a problem a hiccup minimises it without denying it, which is why spokespeople favour the word.

Phonology and orthography

The modern pronunciation /ˈhɪkʌp/ has short /ɪ/ and final /ʌp/, matching the spelling hiccup. The plural /ˈhɪkʌps/ is regular. The hiccough spelling has never produced a pronounced /f/, a fact that illustrates the primacy of speech over spelling in this word’s history. The double c in both spellings is a later convention; earlier forms often had a single consonant.

Modern usage

Today the literal sense is informal and universal, while the figurative sense is standard in business, politics, and technology reporting. The plural dominates in the figurative idiom (“a few hiccups”), and the singular is usual for a single bodily spasm. The word remains one of the most transparent echoic terms in English, its sound still carrying the sense of the thing it names.

  • Literal: “a fit of hiccups,” “the hiccups.”
  • Figurative: “minor hiccups,” “a hiccup in the schedule.”
  • Technical: singultus, persistent hiccups, intractable hiccups.
