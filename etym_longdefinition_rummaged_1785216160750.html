RUMMAGED is the past tense and past participle of the verb rummage, meaning to search through a mass of objects thoroughly and untidily, turning things over, scattering and disturbing the contents in a hunt for what one wants — and, in the source context, careless of how things lie afterward. The word's essential tone is disorder: a rummage is not a tidy, systematic search but a messy, hasty turning-out of everything, with the searcher caring nothing for the arrangement it destroys. In the source sentence — Tolkien's The Hobbit, where the goblins find "all the baggages and packages lying broken open, and being rummaged by goblins, and smelt by goblins, and fingered by goblins, and quarrelled over by goblins" — the passive construction makes the packages the victims of a rough, greedy pawing-over, and the stacking of verbs (rummaged, smelt, fingered, quarrelled over) fills the scene with the senses of possessive, despoiling touch: the goblins are not looking for anything specific so much as handling everything they find like a mob of looters. Tolkien's choice of rummaged rather than "searched" keeps the scene lawless and tactile: nothing is catalogued, everything is tossed aside. The word also carries a faint archaism befitting the book's mock-heroic adventure-tale voice, recalling pirate cargo and customs men breaking open chests in a ship's hold.

Full polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Messy search (modern dominant sense): to search untidily through a mass of objects, turning everything over; "rummage through a drawer," "rummage in the attic," "rummage in a handbag." The sense Tolkien uses.
  • Historical nautical sense — stowing cargo: originally (c. 1520) rummage (noun and verb) meant the arranging or stowing of a ship's cargo in its hold, from French arrimage; the sense of neat order, now entirely obsolete except in historical writing.
  • Historical customs sense — searching a ship: from the 1560s–1570s the word named the customs search of a vessel for contraband — the breaking-open of chests and turning-out of bales; the pivot from "stow" to "search."
  • Rummage sale: (1808) a charity sale of assorted, often second-hand goods — "odds and ends," originally goods of doubtful origin sold after a ship's cargo was forfeited; the disorder of rummaging turned to good use.
  • Noun uses: rummage as a noun — "a rummage" (an untidy search), "have a rummage"; adjectival "rummage sale," "rummage table."
  • Extended figurative sense: loosely, to hunt through memory, records, or data; "rummaging through the archives," "rummage through one's memory." A gentle, goal-less extension.

Register and collocations: the verb is informal and concrete, common in everyday narrative; fixed collocations include "rummage through," "rummage about," "rummage around," "rummage in," and the noun phrases "rummage sale," "rummage box," "rummage table." It is a word of the hand more than of the eye — rummaging is done by touch, plunge, and scatter — and this physicality is exactly what the goblin scene exploits.

Semantic boundary: rummage is distinguished from search (methodical, goal-directed), ransack (violent, destructive of the place), rifle (furtive, intent on theft), forage (wandering search for provisions), and scour (systematic and thorough) by its carelessness and haste: a rummage is a search without respect for the objects it displaces.
