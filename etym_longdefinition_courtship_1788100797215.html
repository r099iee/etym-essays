Courtship is a noun formed from the verb to court and the suffix -ship (the suffix of state and condition, as in friendship and lordship). In its primary modern sense it means the process, or the period, of courting someone — the wooing of a person, usually with a view to marriage: the time of mutual attention, visits, letters, gifts, and growing attachment that leads from interest to betrothal. The word carries the court inside it: to court is to "pay court" to someone, to attend the beloved as a courtier attends a sovereign — with service, devotion, and the hope of favour. In Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose, the Student's courtship of the Professor's daughter "lasted all through the summer" — the word names the entire romantic pursuit that the Nightingale gives her life to nourish.

Primary sense: the romantic pursuit. In human society, courtship is the socially recognised approach to marriage: the period in which a couple come to know each other with a serious mutual intention, its forms varying with time and culture — the formal "paying one's addresses" and chaperoned calls of the 18th and 19th centuries; the "keeping company" and Sunday visits of Victorian practice; the letter, the serenade, the engagement ring; and, in modern societies, its democratic successor, dating, which the word courtship now describes from the outside, with formality and a touch of old fashion. The courtship is the stage before engagement: it ends when the couple are formally betrothed (or, today, when a partnership is declared), and its cultural function — in every known society, though in endlessly different forms — is to let the two parties and their families test and confirm the match. The word's emotional register is tender and hopeful: courtship is the season of intention, before marriage's fact.

Secondary senses and their bridges.

  • Zoological courtship — in biology and ethology, "courtship" names the display and ritual behaviour by which animals attract and secure a mate: the peacock's train, the bowerbird's decorated bower, the courtship dance of grebes, courtship feeding, courtship song. The transfer is natural: the animal performs for its mate as the human lover performs for the beloved — Darwin's Descent of Man (1871) gave sexual selection its courtship vocabulary, and "courtship display" is now standard scientific prose.
  • Political and commercial courtship — to "court" a constituency, an investor, or the press is to seek its favour deliberately; "the candidates' courtship of the undecided voters" is daily journalism. Here the oldest court-sense resurfaces: to pay court to a prince was to angle for favour, and the modern courtship of votes is the same manoeuvre with the electorate as sovereign.
  • The original, now rare sense: courtly behaviour — the Oxford English Dictionary's first sense, recorded from the 1580s, is "the behaviour or arts of a courtier; courtly manners and intrigue", the state of being a courtier; this sense has faded almost to archaic, but it is the semantic root of the romantic word, and remembering it unlocks the metaphor: wooing is a court-school performance.
  • Figurative extension — "the courtship of danger" (seeking trouble), "the courtship of an idea": any deliberate, gradual winning-over can claim the word; the register is formal and literary.

Boundaries and register. Courtship is formal and literary; in ordinary modern speech "dating", "going out", and "seeing someone" have taken its everyday place, but the word remains standard for the period of wooing with intent, and essential in history, anthropology, and biology. It differs from engagement (the formal betrothal that follows courtship), from flirtation (light, unserious play at attraction), from romance (the whole emotional or literary complex of love), and from the archaic wooing (the active seeking — "courtship" is the state or period, "wooing" the action). In the story the word is used at its tenderest: "This was his courtship, and it lasted all through the summer" — the whole summer of the Student's hope, which the Rose, paid for by the Nightingale's life, was meant to crown.
