From a quarrel to a guess: the biography of surmised. The remote ancestry of surmised lies in a Proto-Indo-European root that may encode the oldest quarrel in the family: *meith-, "to exchange, to change, to go in turns, to alternate." Pokorny's *meit(h)- has left its clearest fossils in the Indo-Iranian languages. In Vedic Sanskrit the root mith- means "to alternate, to pair, to meet, to contend": the Rigveda uses methete, "the two come together, the two contend," and from the same stem come mithas, "mutually, alternately," mithuna, "a pair, a couple" (the name later attached to the zodiac sign of the Twins), and mithyā, "turned the wrong way, false," which survives in modern Hindi as mithyā, "false." The root's core image is exchange and reciprocity — things passing back and forth, taking turns, meeting one another.

Out of that image the Latin verb is traditionally drawn. The identification of Latin mittere, "to send, to let go," with *meith- is an old and honoured equation, though the cautious scholarship of de Vaan flags the matter as uncertain and no further Indo-European cognates are securely established. What the equation proposes is that to "send" is to let something go on its way, to put it into circulation — an exchange, in effect, of the thing sent for the thing received. From mittere Latin built a colossal family, and its perfect stem miss- accounts for more English words than almost any other single Latin source: mission and missionary from missio, "a sending"; missive, "a thing sent"; message and messenger, through the Late Latin missaticum and Old French message; admit, commit, emit, permit, submit, transmit, remit, dismiss, intermittent, and promise (from promittere, "to send forth," hence "to pledge"); and, unexpectedly, the English word mass itself, from Late Latin missa, born of the liturgy's dismissal formula "Ite, missa est," "Go, it is the sending-forth."

Our word begins with a compound. Latin super-mittere, "to send or put over or upon," passed into Old French as surmetre, "to put over; to lay a charge upon, to accuse," and from its past participle came the Anglo-Norman noun sur-mise, "a placing on top" — and, in the law courts, "a charge, an accusation laid upon someone." Middle English took the noun in the 14th century with precisely that legal meaning: a "surmise" was a formal allegation, often brought on slight or even malicious evidence. It was, etymologically, something "mised" (put) "sur" (upon) a person.

  • Sound and form: Latin super + mittere → supermittere; Old French surmetre → sur-mise (past-participial noun); Middle English surmise; modern surmise, surmised. The prefix sur- is the French heir of Latin super- "over, upon."
  • Semantic drift: "to send over" → "to lay a charge upon" (legal) → "a charge, an accusation" (14th c. noun) → "a supposition laid upon a scene" → (verb, 17th c.) "to suppose, conjecture without proof." The leap from courtroom to mind is complete: what was once laid upon a person as an accusation becomes laid upon a situation as a guess.
  • Socio-cultural context: the word's legal origin left it permanently tinged with caution and faint suspicion; its rehabilitation as a respectable intellectual verb tracks the rise of the early modern culture of evidence and the polite science of conjecture — the essay, the natural philosopher, the sceptic.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare uses the noun in the sense of suspicion and suspicion's guilt ("Macbeth does murder sleep... " and in The Tempest, where a "surmise" feeds conspiracy); Milton and the philosophical poets use it of doubtful speculation; and the word reaches its most famous modern line in Keats's "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer," where the explorers "Look'd at each other with a wild surmise — Silent, upon a peak in Darien."

In modern usage surmise is a formal, literary verb for forming a conclusion by intuition on thin evidence; its register is refined and old-fashioned, and in speech it has largely yielded to guess and infer. Yet the full history is preserved in the participle: when Bao-yu "surmised" the delicate girl to be his Aunt Lin's daughter, the word still carries, invisibly, the old legal act of laying something upon someone — the mind putting an identity upon a figure as the court once put a charge upon a defendant.
