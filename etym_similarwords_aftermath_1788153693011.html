• Consequences: Similar because both name what follows from an event; differs because consequences is neutral and logical (one can trace consequences cause-by-cause), while aftermath in modern use implies a turbulent or destructive event and a period, not an argument.
  • After-effects / aftereffects: Similar because both denote results appearing later; differs because after-effects are usually physiological or short-term results of a specific cause, whereas aftermath covers the whole subsequent situation.
  • Fallout: Similar because both denote the disagreeable results of an event (originally radioactive); differs because fallout is colloquial-informal and stresses blame and contamination, aftermath is formal and stresses the following period.
  • Repercussions: Similar because both name results and suggest negative ripples; differs because repercussions re-echo — they are indirect, delayed "rebounds" — while aftermath is simply what comes after.
  • Wake: Similar in the fixed phrase "in the wake of" ≈ "in the aftermath of"; differs because wake is spatial (the trail a ship leaves) and momentary, aftermath covers the whole following state.
  • Upshot: Similar as a term for the net result; differs because upshot is the final outcome of deliberation (the "shot" that ends the argument), singular and positive-or-neutral, not a period of trouble.
  • Outcome / Result: Similar in the broadest sense of what follows; differs drastically in register and scope — results are expected answers, outcomes are end-states, both without the disaster-undertone of aftermath.
  • Sequel: Similar as "what comes after" (also of second crops in old use); differs because sequel is now a narrative term — the literary continuation — while aftermath is real-world consequence.
  • Second crop / aftergrass (agricultural): Similar as the literal sense of aftermath; differs because these are strictly farming terms and have never acquired the figurative, disaster-tinged meaning.
