In this passage hallowed describes the example of great poets as a body of revered, quasi-sacred precedent — the metrical practice of the Greeks and Romans treated with the deference owed to consecrated authority. The word carries a deliberate transfer: classical verse is elevated “on the highest of pedestals,” so its authority is felt as almost religious, a canon too venerable to question. The tone is quietly ironic: the reader is invited to respect these hallowed examples even while noting that they offer no usable rules for English — sanctity without utility. PRIMARY SENSE — religious consecration
  • Hallowed is the past participle of the archaic verb to hallow “to make holy, to consecrate, to set apart as sacred”; as an adjective it means “made holy, consecrated, sanctified, held in deep reverence.”
  • The sense is fixed in the liturgy of the Lord’s Prayer: “hallowed be thy name” — the petition that God’s name be honoured as holy, carried through Wyclif (c. 1395), Tyndale (1526), and the Authorised Version (1611).
  • It describes things set apart for God — hallowed ground, a hallowed church, hallowed relics, a hallowed place of burial — the consecration of persons, places, and objects by ritual.
  • Old English hālgian “to make holy” is the denominative verb of hālig “holy”; both descend from a Germanic root meaning “whole, sound, of good omen.” The underlying logic: what is holy is whole and inviolate, set apart from the broken and the profane.
SECONDARY SENSE — reverent, traditional regard Since the 16th–17th centuries hallowed has loosened from the altar to anything treated as sacred by tradition: hallowed customs, hallowed memories, hallowed institutions, “hallowed halls” of learning. The semantic bridge is a transfer of reverence — what is treated with the awe due to the divine becomes, by long custom, “hallowed” without any claim of divinity. The OED tracks the sense precisely: “made holy,” then figuratively “made the object of reverential awe or regard.” In the passage the “hallowed example of great poets” is exactly this transferred sense: the metrical practice of the ancients, consecrated by veneration and precedent, though it offers the early humanists no practical rules for their own language. TERTIARY SENSES — modern uses
  • Institutional veneration: “the hallowed halls of academia,” “a hallowed tradition,” “hallowed ground” of a battlefield or memorial — places and practices invested with solemn respect.
  • Literal religious: “hallowed be thy name,” “hallowed church,” still used in devotional and liturgical registers, and in hymns and prayers.
  • Literary / archaic-solemn: the word carries a faint archaic solemnity that writers use for elevation, often with irony (as here), and it is common in elevated historical and nature prose.
  • Festival fossil: the frozen compound survives in Halloween, i.e. All Hallows’ Eve — the eve of All Saints’ Day — where “hallow” preserves the old noun meaning “holy one, saint.”
SYNONYM BOUNDARIES
  • Sacred: Similar because both mean set apart as holy; differs because sacred denotes an inherent connection to the divine, while hallowed stresses sanctity conferred by reverence and long tradition.
  • Consecrated: Similar because both are dedicated as holy; differs because consecrated emphasises the specific rite of dedication, while hallowed emphasises the reverence and customary awe that surround the object.
  • Blessed: Similar because both are favoured or sanctified; differs because blessed implies a gift of divine favour, while hallowed implies being made the object of reverential awe or regard.
  • Sanctified: Similar because both mean made holy; differs because sanctified sits closer to consecrated — an act of making holy — while hallowed stresses the state of being reverently regarded.
  • Revered: Similar because both are deeply respected; differs because revered stresses the honour paid, while hallowed adds a layer of superstitious or religious awe.
  • Canonical: Similar because both can describe an officially honoured body of works or authority; differs because canonical stresses official listing and doctrinal authority, while hallowed stresses quasi-religious reverence by custom.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE Hallowed descends from Old English hālgian “to consecrate,” from hālig “holy,” from Germanic *hailagaz “belonging to the sound/intact order of things,” from *hailaz “whole, healthy, of good omen.” The underlying equation is that the holy is the intact, the unbroken, the inviolate. From strict religious consecration the adjective spread to the reverence paid to tradition, precedent, and canon — the very reverence, the passage suggests, that made the “hallowed example” of the ancients unusable for English verse.
