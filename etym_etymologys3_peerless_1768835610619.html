peerless — The life of peerless begins with the act of allotting. The Proto-Indo-European root *per- carried the sense of passing through and, above all, of assigning — dividing a whole into shares and handing them out; it is the root that lies behind Latin pars, "part, a share," and portio, "portion," the slice of a whole. Into this economy of shares Latin placed the adjective par, "equal" — that which is even, level, neither more nor less than its neighbour; the traditional (if debated) derivation reads par as "evenly apportioned," an equality that exists because the shares were divided fairly. From par Latin built a small constellation: paria, "equal things," the neuter plural that became Old French paire and English pair — for a pair is precisely two things of equal standing, two shares that match; comparāre, "to set side by side as equals," hence English compare, the placing of things on a common scale; dispar, "unequal," whose abstract disparitās gave English disparity, the breaking of the level; and the bare par itself, which English took in the eighteenth century as the standard of value and exchange — the financial "par" that later gave golf its par, the number of strokes a perfect equal would take. But the decisive word for peerless is the Old French per, which carried the Latin adjective into the world of feudal rank: a per was an equal, and above all a noble of equal rank — the twelve peers of Charlemagne, the peers of the realm who sat in judgment upon one another, none above the rest. Middle English took the word as pere, and English peer acquired its double life: an equal of any kind, and a nobleman who is the equal only of his fellow peers. From the commoner's sense came the adjective peerless, attested in English by about 1300, in which the suffix -less negates the peer: the peerless thing has no equal at all, standing where no comparison can reach it. The word's dignity is thus inherited from the peerage: to be peerless is to be, not the first among equals, but the one without equals — the member of a rank of one. Its social history then ran a long arc. In the age of chivalry peerless was the word of courtly praise — the peerless knight, the peerless beauty — attaching itself to the heights of love, valour, and devotion, always above the common ladder. From the sixteenth to the nineteenth centuries the word broadened beyond persons to art, skill, and achievement, becoming a standard superlative of rhetoric for anything without rival: peerless craftsmanship, a peerless view. In contemporary usage peerless survives as a formal, literary, and even commercial word for "matchless," stronger and rarer than mere unmatched, retaining its aristocratic air; in marketing it is used to claim an unrivaled excellence, and in careful prose it still means exactly what it always meant — the thing for which no other stands on its level, the word that ends all argument about comparison. When Peake writes of "the most peerless" carvers, he draws on that long history at once: in the hierarchy of Gormenghast, where every craftsman is ranked by his skill, the peerless are the exceptions who break the scale of ranks itself — men for whom, by the word's own logic, no peer exists.
