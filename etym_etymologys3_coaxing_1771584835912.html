The biography of "coax" is a study in how a word can shed its bad reputation. It begins in the 16th century as a slangy word for cheating and fooling, passes through a century of wheedling and flattery, and emerges in the modern language as a tender, patient verb of gentle persuasion — yet the original slyness never quite disappears. The mysterious beginning.
  • "Coax" has no known Indo-European ancestry and no clear etymology. The word first appears in English as the noun "cokes" (also "cocks", "cokeses"), attested from the mid-16th century with the sense "a fool, a simpleton, a dupe"; Shakespeare uses "cokes" for a fool or gull. The origin of "cokes" is unknown: proposals include a personal name, a dialect word, and a connection with "cock", but none is generally accepted, and the OED classes the origin as obscure.
  • From the noun came the verb. Around 1580 "to coax" means "to make a simpleton of, to fool, to dupe" — in the language of the 16th and early 17th centuries, to coax someone was to cajole them into folly, to cheat them by flattery. The verb here is close to "beguile" and "gull": the coaxer is a trickster and the coaxed is a dupe.
The great softening: 17th century.
  • The decisive shift occurs in the mid-17th century. By the 1660s "to coax" has come to mean "to wheedle, to persuade by gentle flattery or fondling", and the outright sense of cheating begins to fade. The bridge is transparent: since duping had always been done with honeyed words and flattery, the word transferred its meaning from the outcome (fooling someone) to the method (using sweet persuasion) — and as the method came to be used for innocent ends (coaxing a child, coaxing an animal), the negative charge drained away.
  • Throughout the 18th century the verb is standard for gentle, wheedling persuasion, and it appears in the great dictionaries and in the sentimental literature of the period. The noun "cokes" meanwhile dies out, leaving "coax" orphaned from its own etymology — a verb whose origin as "to make a fool of" is now visible only to historical dictionaries.
Sound and form.
  • "Coax" is a simple English formation with no recorded sound-shift history; its spelling has been stable ("cox", "coaxe" in early use, then "coax"), and it has produced a small family: "coaxer" (one who coaxes, attested 17th century), "coaxing" (adjective and noun), "coaxingly", and the rare "coaxation". The modern RP pronunciation /kˈəʊksɪŋ/ shows the regular development of the early-modern vowel to the modern diphthong /əʊ/ before the voiceless cluster "ks".
Semantic drift in the modern language.
  • From the mid-19th century "coax" extends from persons to things: Victorians coax fires into flame and coax reluctant machines; and in the 20th century the verb is used for coaxing engines, computers, signals, and answers out of stubborn sources. The modern instrumental sense — "coaxing an old car to start" — treats the object as something obstinate that must be soothed, and it keeps the verb's core picture of patient, gentle, persistent effort.
  • In modern English the word has also softened further into affection: "coaxing" is now almost always tender — a mother coaxing a frightened child, a grandmother coaxing Bao-yu "like a little child". Yet the old strategy remains embedded: to coax is still to persuade by sweetness rather than force, and the word quietly implies that the coaxer is managing the situation, steering the other person toward calm or compliance from behind a screen of gentleness. This faint, persistent trace of manipulation is the word's living memory of its 16th-century trickster days.
Literary and cultural attestations.
  • Shakespeare attests the noun family ("cokes"); the Restoration comic dramatists and the essayists of the 18th century use "coax" for wheedling suitors and flattering servants; the Victorian novel uses it for nurseries and sickrooms; and modern usage has taken the word into journalism and informal speech for the gentle extraction of anything reluctant. In Dream of the Red Chamber the word is perfectly placed: Grandmother Jia's management of Bao-yu is warm, theatrical, and strategic — sweetness deployed as a gentle instrument of control — which is precisely the double nature the word has carried since 1660.
Summary.
  • From an obscure "simpleton", through a cheating verb, through a century of wheedling, to a tender verb of patient persuasion — "coax" is the story of a word reforming its own reputation while quietly keeping its original strategy: honeyed words as the softest form of control.
