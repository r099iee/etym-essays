PIE: *per- "forward, through, in front, before" (attested: Sanskrit purás "before, in front" and purā "formerly", Greek pró "before, in front" and pará "beside, against", Latin per "through", pro "before, on behalf of", prae "in front of"; the root is one of the most ancient particles of the family).
Proto-Germanic: *fura- / *fora "before, in front of" — Grimm's Law shifted PIE *p to PGmc *f, giving *fura-.
Old English (c. 700–1100): foran "before, in front" (adverb and preposition) and fore "before, in front of"; the emphatic compounds æt foran / on foran ("at the front, in the front") are also formed.
Old English: ætforan / onforan — the prepositional phrases "at/in the front (of)" fuse into compounds.
Middle English (c. 1150–1500): afore — contraction of the Old English compounds; first "in front of" (place), then by metaphor "previously, before this time" (time).
Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700): afore — a standard preposition, adverb, and conjunction in the language of law, scripture, and plain prose, fixed in afore-mentioned, aforethought, afore the mast.
Modern English (archaic/dialectal): AFORE — survives in regional dialects and in the legal and nautical fossil phrases; the standard language uses before.
NOTE ON FORM: the a- prefix represents reduced Old English on or æt, exactly as in a-bed, a-new, a-hold; the word is not a direct descendant of the PIE particle but a compound re-formation made within Old English and contracted in Middle English. The spelling is stable because the word's later life was largely oral (dialect) and fixed-phrase (law, sea).
