EXPOSITION is built, quite literally, from the Latin image of placing something out into the open. The word’s body contains two Indo-European roots: the first, *h₁eǥʷs or *h₁eḱs, ‘out, out of, away,’ which became the Latin preposition ex and its prefixal form ex-; the second, the family that produced Latin pōnere, ‘to put, place, set,’ which descends from an older *po-sinō, a compound of the reduced preposition *po- (from PIE *apo-, ‘off, away’) and sinō, ‘to let, to set down.’ When the Romans compounded ex- with pōnere to form expōnere, they produced a verb meaning ‘to set forth, to put out, to display, to expose’ — a ship set out to sea, a child exposed in the wilderness, a goods-store laid open, and, figuratively, an argument laid open for inspection.

Classical Latin and the noun. From the past-participle stem exposit- Latin formed the noun expositio, ‘a setting forth, a display, an exhibition, an explanation.’ In the classical language the word belonged to two worlds at once. In rhetorical and philosophical prose, an expositio was a systematic account — the orderly presentation of a doctrine, a case, or a subject, prized for clarity and arrangement. In public life, an expositio was a showing or display of things. Both strands — the explanatory and the exhibitionary — were present from the beginning, and the entire later history of the English word is the story of those two strands splitting and recombining.

The medieval passage into English. The word reached English through Old French esposicion and directly from Latin. The first English uses, in the late fourteenth century, were overwhelmingly religious: exposicioun meant the explanation of a text, and above all the exposition of scripture from the pulpit and the commentary. The expositors were the commentators and glossators who set out the meaning of the Bible for readers and hearers; the word’s early register was reverent, doctrinal, and homiletic — a preacher’s act of making the Word plain. For two or three centuries the dominant sense remained exegetical, though the public-display sense of ‘a showing of things’ survived at the edges of the word’s range.

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: from the pulpit to the academy. From the early modern period the word broadened into secular scholarship. An ‘exposition of a theme, theory, or doctrine’ became the standard phrase for a methodical explanation — the neutral counterpart of persuasion, the act of laying a subject out for the mind’s inspection. Expository prose became a recognised category of composition, and rhetoricians divided writing into narrative, descriptive, expository, and argumentative modes. It is this scholarly sense — the systematic, architectural setting-forth of a subject, judged by clarity and completeness — that the card’s sentence uses when it speaks of “his exposition of this theme.” The word had travelled from the sermon to the treatise without changing its fundamental action: to set a subject out where it can be seen.

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: story and spectacle. In the eighteenth century the literary critics took up the word for the structure of fiction and drama: the ‘exposition’ of a plot is its opening portion, the part that lays out setting, characters, and prior events before the conflict begins. In music, nineteenth-century analysis gave the word its sonata-form sense: the ‘exposition’ of a movement is the first section, where the principal themes are presented before they are developed. At almost the same time, the old exhibitionary strand of the Latin word exploded into public prominence. The nineteenth-century ‘exposition’ was a great public exhibition of manufactures, art, and invention — the Great Exhibition of 1851, the Paris expositions universelles, the Chicago World’s Columbian Exposition of 1893 — where the world’s goods were literally ‘set forth’ for the gaze of millions. The word swung, in a single century, from the lectern toward industry, display, and mass entertainment.

The modern word. In contemporary English the several senses coexist. In academic prose an exposition is a comprehensive, orderly explanation; in literary and musical study it is the opening material of a narrative or a sonata; in commerce a trade exposition or agricultural exposition is a fair; and in homiletics the old scriptural sense survives in ‘expository preaching.’ Every sense, however, still answers to the Latin image from which the word was formed: to expose, to set forth, to lay open. The same two Indo-European roots — ‘out’ and ‘place’ — that built the verb for putting a ship to sea now build the noun for putting a theory on display and a theme before the reader. The history of the word is thus, in miniature, the history of how ‘laying things open’ came to name both the scholar’s explanation and the world’s fair.
