Drought is an English native, a word shaped on English soil out of English material: it is simply the abstract noun formed from the adjective "dry", and its entire history is the story of a plain word for "dryness" becoming a specialised, and today an urgent, climatic term.

The dry root. English "dry" comes from Proto-Germanic *drūgiz, "dry", which is in turn traced to the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰrewg-, "dry, hard" — a reconstruction supported by Iranian forms such as Persian društ "hard, rough" and Old Church Slavonic drugu "hard, rough". The adjective itself is Germanic-restricted, but its root reaches across the family. From the adjective, Proto-West Germanic formed an abstract noun *drūgōþu, "dryness", built with the common Germanic suffix *-ōþu-; Old English inherited this as drūgaþ (drūgath), "dryness". Its oldest senses are entirely general: the drūgaþ of the ground, of timber, of the air, of a wound, of the throat. In Old English prose and poetry it is a state, not yet a disaster.

Middle English: the word turns to the weather. In Middle English the word appears as droughte (with the side-form drouth), and it now begins to attach itself firmly to weather: a "drought" is a period of dry weather, an absence of rain. In an agricultural society this was no mere meteorological observation: droughts meant failed harvests, dead cattle, dried-up wells, famine, and the collapse of rent and trade. The word therefore quickly gathered heavy economic and moral weight. Biblical language ("there was a famine in the land") fused with native usage; in the Wycliffite Bible and in later sermons "drought" denotes both the natural calamity and, allegorically, spiritual barrenness — "a drought of grace", a dryness of the soul. This double register, the literal and the spiritual, is characteristic of the early modern word.

The spelling split and the -gh- question. The spelling of drought records a curious phonetic history. Old English drūgaþ ended in the sound /θ/, and the h written before it (druhath) eventually became a velar fricative written as "gh" in Middle English, giving spellings like drought(e). Meanwhile the northern and dialectal pronunciation, which kept a dental, produced the form drouth (/draʊθ/), which survives in Scottish and American dialect, notably in American folk speech of the 19th–20th centuries. The standard pronunciation /draʊt/ reflects a reduction of the -gh-; the silent letters in "drought" are thus a fossilised spelling of an old consonant. It is one of the "ough" words English learners dread, alongside "though", "through", and "thought", but it is entirely native, not a Norman borrowing.

Modern scientific drought. In the 20th century, drought became a formal category of climate science, and the single word now covers at least four recognised types: meteorological drought (a period of deficient precipitation), agricultural drought (soil moisture too low for crops), hydrological drought (falling river and reservoir levels, like the sinking of the lake in Tolkien's description), and socioeconomic drought (when water shortage harms people and economies). Famously severe droughts — the Dust Bowl of the American plains in the 1930s, the Sahel droughts of the 1970s–80s, the Australian millennium drought — have made the word a staple of disaster reporting and climate policy. The word has also bred figurative uses: "a drought of goals" in sport, "a drought of ideas" in creative life, "an innovation drought" in business, all preserving the root idea of an abnormal, painful absence.

Literary resonance. Because the word links human catastrophe with landscape, it has strong literary presence. In The Hobbit, Tolkien gives the word a quiet archaeological power: "the rotting piles of a greater town could still be seen along the shores when the waters sank in a drought" — the drought as a recurring natural rhythm that once exposed the ruins of a drowned city. From the Bible through the medieval sermon to modern climate fiction, drought has never lost its sense of collective ordeal.

  • Proto-Germanic *drūgiz "dry" → Proto-West Germanic *drūgōþu "dryness" → Old English drūgaþ.
  • Middle English droughte / drouth: "dry weather, lack of rain"; spiritual sense "dryness of soul".
  • 16th–17th c. – spelling fixed as "drought"; pronunciation diverges (standard /draʊt/, dialectal drouth).
  • 20th c. – scientific typology of droughts; disaster and climate vocabulary.
  • Modern – literal and figurative senses flourish; the word is both meteorological and metaphorical.
