Unknown beginnings. The history of boast is genuinely unusual in English etymology: the word appears in Middle English in the 13th century with no clearly recorded ancestor in Old English, and every serious dictionary labels its origin as "uncertain" or "unknown". It simply arrives — a noun bost (about 1250) and a verb bosten (about 1300) — meaning "boasting, bragging, pride, ostentation" and "to brag, talk proudly". Three main theories have been proposed to explain where it came from.

The Scandinavian theory. The most favoured hypothesis connects the word to a Scandinavian family of adjectives meaning "proud, stiff, swollen with pride": Old Norse baus, Norwegian and dialectal baus, and the Old Norse compound baug-baus. On this view, English borrowed the word during the centuries of intense Norse contact in northern and eastern England, where hundreds of ordinary words entered the language from Viking speech. The semantic core is easy to accept: an animal or person "stiff" or "swollen" with pride is exactly the image behind bragging, and English boast would be the noun built on a "proud" adjective, like pride built on proud. The weakness is that no exact Scandinavian cognate verb or noun for the act of boasting is attested, so the precise route of derivation remains hypothetical.

The native theory. An older school of etymologists derived the word natively from the same Germanic base as Old English belgan "to swell (with anger or pride)" — the idea being that a boaster is one who "swells up" — with an unrecorded Old English ancestor such as *bāst. This theory has largely fallen out of favour: the vowel and consonant correspondences are awkward, and no such Old English form is on record. It survives mainly as a cautionary example of how tempting it is to invent a missing link.

Meaning in Middle English. Whatever its origin, the word's early behaviour is well documented. In the 13th and 14th centuries bost carried more than mere bragging: it meant "noise, clamour, idle and loud talk", and the verb bosten could mean "to make a noise" as well as "to speak proudly". This is a significant clue — it suggests the word entered English as a word about loud, showy speech before it narrowed to self-praise. Chaucer uses the noun in this rich, noisy sense, and medieval sermon-writers already treat boasting as a sin of the tongue, classed with lying and vainglory.

Moral hardening in early modern English. In the 16th and 17th centuries the religious and satirical literature of England did to "boast" what it did to many words of pride: it pinned them down as vices. Preachers denounced "boasting" alongside pride and vanity; "boasting" became a recognized sin of the mouth, and the word hardened into its modern, censorious colour. It was in this period that the reassuring uses also grew up: one could "make a boast" of something worthy, and God or a nation could "boast" (be justly proud) of its works, so the word developed its double life — rebuke and legitimate pride — that it still has today.

The possession sense. A decisive semantic event took place in the 18th and 19th centuries: the verb acquired the "X boasts Y" construction, in which the subject is a town, building, or institution and the object is a feature, so that "to boast" came to mean "to be justly proud of possessing". From the earliest recorded instances — towns boasting churches, libraries, and harbours — the construction spread through travel writing, journalism, and finally advertising. It is now the single most frequent use of the word in English, and it has drained away nearly all the moral disapproval.

The modern word. Today boast (and its inflected form boasts) is split between two nearly opposite worlds. As a noun, "boasts" is still a mild rebuke — "empty boasts", "idle boasts", boasting that will be punished — and in literature it is the natural word for pride about to collapse. As a verb, in the tourism and possession sense, it is cheerfully neutral: every hotel, town, and smartphone "boasts" something. The word has travelled, in about seven hundred and fifty years, from the loud talk of the medieval marketplace to the marketing brochure — carrying all the way a faint memory of the sin it once named.
