The noun reek denotes thick, foul smoke or vapour, or a strong, unpleasant smell; the corresponding verb to reek means to emit such smoke or vapour, and, in its modern dominant sense, to smell strongly and unpleasantly of something. The word's history is a story of narrowing from a neutral physical fact — the giving-off of smoke, steam, or vapour — to a strongly negative olfactory and moral judgement.
  • Sense 1 — thick, foul smoke or vapour (historical and literary): in the passage from The Hobbit in which the fire of the goblins blazes about the trees, the reek is the thick, blinding smoke that pours up from the flames — so dense that it smarts in Bilbo's eyes, so pervasive that he can feel the heat of the fire through it, and so obscuring that the dancing goblins beyond it are glimpsed only through a roiling veil. In this archaic literary register the word's emphasis falls on physical thickness, suffocating density, and rising motion, not on odour: it is the smoke that stings and veils, the smoke that closes the world in, the very substance of the hostile night.
  • Sense 2 — a strong, unpleasant smell (modern dominant): since the nineteenth century the ordinary sense of reek is a strong, penetrating, disagreeable smell: the reek of stale tobacco, of decay, of sweat, of a chemical spill. As a verb, "to reek of X" means to be saturated with a strong smell — and then, figuratively, to be obviously marked by something negative: a plan that reeks of hypocrisy, an affair that reeks of corruption, a sentence that reeks of malice.
  • Figurative and idiomatic use: the modern idiom "to reek of" has become almost wholly metaphorical in educated prose: one reeks of corruption, of privilege, of danger. The metaphor is smell as overwhelming and betraying sign: what reeks of something cannot hide it; the odour, like smoke, gives the truth away. This preserves, in the moral register, the old physical idea of the reek that cannot be mistaken.
  • Historic senses now obsolete or regional: in Old and Middle English the word (verb rēocan, noun rēc) was neutral — to reek was to give off smoke, steam, or vapour of any kind, pleasant or not; a warm room could reek of heat, a horse reek with sweat, a cooking pot reek with steam. The Scottish and northern English tradition kept the smoke-and-steam senses alive long after the south had shifted the word toward bad smells, and it is that northern, ballad-like register that Tolkien deliberately invokes.
  • Jargon and technical uses: in modern technical contexts the word is rarely used formally, but it survives in informal environmental and industrial language for foul emissions (a reeking chimney, the reek of a chemical plant); in wine and food criticism "reek" occasionally appears for an aggressively pungent, off-putting note; and in figuration it is a stock word of political and journalistic condemnation.
  • Grammatical behaviour: reek is both noun and verb. The verb is intransitive and usually takes "of" (reek of smoke) or is used absolutely (the fire reeks); it also appears in the archaic absolute transitive "the land reeks blood". The noun is uncountable in the smoke sense (the reek of the fire) and countable in the odour sense (a reek of garlic). Its register today is strong and sensory, with a literary and slightly archaic colouring.
The boundaries against near-synonyms sharpen the sense. Smoke and vapour are neutral, purely descriptive terms with no moral or emotional charge; steam adds heat but not foulness; fume (usually plural fumes) suggests irritant or chemical gases drifting from some process; stench and stink refer exclusively to bad odour, with stink the cruder, more colloquial word; odour and smell are neutral and general, requiring a qualifier for negativity; aroma is pleasant and chiefly of food; whiff is a faint, passing breath of a smell, not a pervading cloud; and effluvium is a learned word for an unpleasant discharge or vapour. What distinguishes reek is that it unites the visible and the olfactory into a single sensory assault: the old smoke sense and the modern stink sense are two halves of one experience — the thing so thick, so strong, so pervasive that it fills the air, stings the eyes, and cannot be escaped. In the present passage that totality is precisely the point: the reek is not merely unpleasant, it is a hostile atmosphere, the element of the goblin fire itself, and the word's double history lets it mean at once the blinding smoke and the foulness of the place, the physical and the moral reeking together.
