Foundry is a noun with two connected lives. Its core meaning is a workshop or factory in which metal is melted and cast — that is, melted to liquid and poured into moulds, where it cools and hardens into a desired shape. In addition, and historically earlier, foundry names the art or process itself: the skill of founding, metal-casting, whether practised in a great industrial plant or in the small workshop of an artistic caster. The word is the workshop of the verb to found in its metal sense ("to melt and cast"), which comes from Latin fundere, "to pour" — so a foundry is, literally, a pour-house: a place where metal is made liquid so that it can be poured into the shape of tools, bells, cannons, statues, and machine parts.

Primary sense: the casting workshop. The heart of a foundry is the furnace — in an iron foundry, the tall cupola or the electric induction furnace — in which scrap and pig metal are melted; the molten metal is then tapped into a ladle and poured into moulds: traditionally moulds of packed foundry sand shaped around a wooden or metal pattern, and today also permanent steel dies (die-casting), investment moulds (lost-wax), and centrifugal moulds. The vocabulary of the trade is old and precise: sprue and riser (the channels that feed liquid metal into and out of the cavity), fettling (knocking the casting out and cleaning it), and the trades of the moulder, the patternmaker, and the foundryman. Foundries make everything from engine blocks, pipes, and manhole covers to church bells and bronze statues, and the foundry floor is one of the oldest industrial workplaces in the world — heat, dust, and pouring metal at its centre.

Secondary senses and their bridges. From the melting-house the word has spread by one bridge: anything produced by pouring and moulding can claim the name.

  • Type foundry — the firm that designs and produces printing type. From the sixteenth century the letters of print were founded (cast) in metal — Gutenberg's movable type was cast in a hand mould — and a "type foundry" (letter-founding) supplied printers with founts; the term outlived metal type and today means a company that designs and releases digital fonts.
  • Semiconductor (chip) foundry — since the 1980s, a factory that manufactures integrated circuits to other companies' designs: the "foundry model" (epitomised by Taiwan's TSMC, founded 1987) separates design from manufacture, so a fabless chip designer sends its chips to a silicon foundry to be made.
  • Glass foundry — a studio where glass is melted and cast or blown; the word imports the metal-caster's vocabulary (furnace, crucible, annealer) into the glass arts.
  • Figurative use — anything that turns out people or ideas in quantity becomes a foundry: "a foundry of talent", "an idea foundry", "a startup foundry"; here the meaning is the melting-and-moulding production itself, made metaphorical.

Boundaries and register. A foundry differs from a forge (where metal is shaped hot but solid, by hammering, not melting), from a smeltery (which extracts metal from ore, producing the metal the foundry then casts), and from a factory or works (general terms — one says "iron foundry" and "steelworks" rather than the reverse, though a foundry may be part of a works). It is a plain, professional word, at home in engineering, art, and industry equally. In Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince, the ruined statue is taken to the foundry to be melted down: "What a strange thing!" says the overseer of the workmen when the leaden heart, alone among the metal, refuses to melt — the foundry, the pour-house where the beautiful Prince is reduced to a lump of lead, is the story's engine of destruction, and the word's heat and gravity do exactly that work.
