Contiguous is an adjective describing entities that are in actual physical contact along a common boundary, touching at an edge or surface without any intervening space, barrier, or entity. In its most common and concrete modern applications, it characterizes landmasses, nation-states, properties, parcels, rooms, or physical structures that adjoin one another along an unbroken borderline or shared wall. Beyond spatial geography and architecture, the word extends across formal disciplines—most notably into temporal sequencing (unbroken spans of time), computer science (consecutive memory locations in computer hardware), jurisprudence (territorial jurisdiction and municipal annexation), and epistemology (the psychological association of ideas adjacent in experience).

In the source passage from David Hume's The History of England—"The inhabitants of Gaul, especially in those parts which lie contiguous to Italy, had acquired, from a commerce with their southern neighbors, some refinement in the arts, which gradually diffused themselves northwards, and spread but a very faint light over this island"—the word operates with classical historiographical precision. Hume highlights the profound geopolitical and cultural power of physical proximity: because southern Gaul shared an immediate, unbroken land border with the Italian peninsula, it was exposed directly to the advanced commercial networks, urban civic habits, and refined material arts of the Mediterranean world. The absence of a separating sea or vast neutral wasteland allowed cultural innovations to seep organically across the frontier, diffusing northward over centuries until a faint glimmer reached pre-Roman Britain. Here, contiguity is not merely a geometric description; it is the physical conduit of civilizational osmosis.

Grammar, Syntax, and Prepositional Idiom: Morphologically, contiguous is a Latinate adjective formed from contingere (‘to touch mutually’) plus the adjectival suffix -ous. In modern English syntax, it operates both predicatively—typically governed by the preposition to (the traditional and historical idiom, as seen in Hume's prose: ‘parts which lie contiguous to Italy’) or the preposition with (increasingly favored in modern technical and cartographic prose: ‘a parcel contiguous with the forest preserve’)—and attributively (‘contiguous territories’, ‘contiguous memory allocation’, ‘contiguous working hours’). While contiguous to stresses proximity directed toward a primary reference point, contiguous with emphasizes the reciprocal, shared nature of the perimeter.

Comprehensive Polysemy and Domain Usages:

  • Geographic, Cartographic, and Geopolitical Contiguity (Primary Modern Sense): Describes regions, nation-states, administrative provinces, or territories that touch one another along a continuous, unbroken land boundary. The preeminent contemporary example is the geopolitical designation ‘the 48 contiguous United States’ (also known as the conterminous or coterminous United States, or colloquially the ‘Lower 48’), which encompasses all US states on the North American mainland, explicitly excluding the separated exclave of Alaska and the Pacific archipelago of Hawaii. The semantic bridge traces back to ancient Roman administrative geography, where contiguous provinces could be garrisoned and governed without crossing foreign jurisdictions or maritime zones.
  • Real Estate, Cadastral Surveying, and Architectural Layout (Physical Spatial Sense): Refers to parcels of land, town lots, tax tracts, buildings, or internal rooms that share a common boundary line, dividing fence, or structural party wall. In real estate development, urban zoning laws, and property taxation, two parcels must be strictly contiguous to be merged into a single development deed without requiring special easements or rights-of-way. Similarly, contiguous office suites or hotel rooms share an immediate partition wall, enabling direct physical access without crossing public corridors.
  • Computer Science, Systems Architecture, and Memory Management (Technical Sense): Refers to blocks of memory, disk clusters, or data elements that are allocated in consecutive, sequentially adjacent addresses in physical or virtual random-access memory (RAM). In low-level programming (such as C or C++) and operating systems design, storing arrays or buffers in contiguous memory ensures maximum cache efficiency, predictable memory strides, and instantaneous address calculation via pointer arithmetic, avoiding the processing overhead of pointer dereferencing seen in linked lists or fragmented memory heaps. The bridge from geographic territory to computational storage is an intuitive spatial metaphor: memory addresses are mapped like consecutive plots of land, where contiguous blocks share adjacent index borders without intervening gaps.
  • Temporal and Sequential Continuity (Chronological Sense): Describes periods of time, calendar dates, work shifts, or administrative terms that occur in immediate, unbroken chronological succession without any intervening pause, weekend, holiday, or recess (e.g., ‘the employee completed five contiguous days of field duty’; ‘a tenant must maintain three contiguous years of leasehold occupancy’). The semantic bridge treats time as a linear spatial dimension, where contiguous intervals touch end-to-end at midnight or the conclusion of an hour.
  • Jurisprudence, Municipal Law, and Maritime Law (Legal Sense): In municipal law, contiguity is an indispensable statutory requirement for annexation: an incorporated municipality cannot annex a distant, detached enclave; the newly absorbed territory must be physically contiguous with existing city borders to ensure the continuous delivery of police, fire, sewage, and emergency infrastructure. In public international maritime law under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the ‘Contiguous Zone’ refers to a distinct maritime belt extending up to 24 nautical miles from a coastal nation's territorial baseline, immediately adjacent and touching the outer edge of its territorial waters, within which the coastal state exercises customs, immigration, fiscal, and sanitary enforcement.
  • Topological, Mathematical, and Graph-Theoretic Contiguity (Formal Sciences): In planar geometry, cartographic GIS systems, and graph theory, shapes or polygons are contiguous if they share at least one edge (linear contiguity) or at least one common vertex (point contiguity, sometimes distinguished as corner adjacency). This distinction governs topological algorithms used in modern redistricting, census tract aggregation, and computational mesh generation.
  • Philosophical, Cognitive, and Epistemological Contiguity (Metaphoric Sense): In the empiricist philosophy of David Hume (A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739), contiguity represents one of the three universal relations of association—alongside resemblance and cause-and-effect—by which human impressions and ideas become linked in the imagination. If two events or sensory impressions occur contiguously in space or time, the mental perception of one naturally evokes the memory of the other, forming the structural foundation of empirical learning and conditioned response.

Register and Connotative Precision: Contiguous occupies an elevated, formal, and analytical register. It is the chosen term of cartographers, legal drafters, urban planners, software engineers, and scholarly historians who require mathematical and physical exactitude. Unlike loose colloquial equivalents such as next door, touching, or neighboring, contiguous excludes any ambiguity regarding separation: entities described as contiguous cannot be separated by an intervening strip of third-party land, an alleyway, an empty memory address, or an elapsed second. Its tone is objective, clinical, and authoritative, evoking clean boundaries, unbroken continuity, and rigorous spatial or chronological cohesion.
