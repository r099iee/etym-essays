Trembling — present participle and gerund of the verb tremble, and by conversion an adjective: shaking or quivering involuntarily, typically because of anxiety, fear, excitement, or physical frailty. In Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit, the word fixes Peter's state at the story's emotional trough: after escaping the gooseberry net he sits down "out of breath and trembling with fright," a small body advertising the panic the words do not need to spell out. CORE SENSES
  • Bodily tremor of emotion. The fine, involuntary, rhythmic shaking of the body or its parts driven by fear, anxiety, rage, or excitement — the core and oldest sense; "trembling with fright" is the classic collocation.
  • Physical frailty or cold. Shaking from weakness, old age, illness, or low temperature, where the tremor is physiological rather than emotional.
  • Localised trembling. Of a single part: trembling hands, a trembling voice, trembling lips — the body part trembling in place of the whole person.
  • Extended and transferred uses. Non-human things that quiver: trembling leaves, trembling light, a trembling flame; and of the voice or of sound itself, where trembling and quavering nearly merge.
SECONDARY AND MODERN MEANINGS
  • Gerund and noun. "A trembling in the voice"; "the trembling of the leaves" — the act or instance of trembling treated as an abstract thing or natural event.
  • Clinical register. In medicine the stable noun is tremor (from Latin tremor, "shaking"), and tremulousness names the tendency; everyday trembling is the lay word for the same involuntary oscillation — a useful boundary between colloquial and technical speech.
  • Anticipation and excitement. In modern English trembling is as often positive as fearful: trembling with excitement, trembling with anticipation — the shudder of strong feeling of any sign, not only terror.
WHY THIS WORD IN THIS SCENE Potter pairs trembling with "out of breath" and "fright" to complete a triad of helpless vulnerability at the story's trough, before recovery and the journey home. The present participle keeps the action happening in the reader's present tense; the word is child-visible, naming what a small animal's body does when panic and exhaustion combine. FURTHER POLYSEMY AND GRAMMATICAL FACETS
  • Trembling as emotional physiology across cultures. The image of the trembling body as the outward sign of an inner state is universal, but English has folded it into specific idioms: "to tremble at the thought," "to be all a-tremble," "in fear and trembling" (biblical, from Philippians), where the participle has hardened into a noun phrase denoting awe or terror before authority.
  • Trembling of sound and music. In music and acoustics, trembling describes a wavering, vibrating tone or a tremolo effect; a "trembling note" is one that oscillates in pitch or loudness, extending the bodily word into technical description of sound.
  • Degree and intensity scale. Trembling occupies a specific point on the intensity scale of involuntary motion: finer than shaking, more continuous than shuddering, more agitated than a quaver; this fine-grained boundary is what makes the word the precise choice for fear rather than cold.
  • Ageing and frailty. In descriptions of old age or illness, "trembling hands" or "a trembling step" signal decline and pity, the physiological sense detached entirely from emotion — a parallel modern sense with its own collocational set.
  • Figurative trembling. "The foundations trembled," "the market trembled" — the word transfers from bodies to institutions and structures, implying instability on the point of collapse, a metaphoric extension documented in journalism and political prose since the nineteenth century.
REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS Collocations: trembling with fear/fright/rage/excitement, trembling hands/voice/lips, trembling like a leaf, to set someone trembling, all a-tremble, in fear and trembling. Register is ordinary narrative English, vivid but not clinical, equally at home in children's literature and adult fiction — a strength Potter exploits without strain.
