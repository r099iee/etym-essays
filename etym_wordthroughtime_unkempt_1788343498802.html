- [Late 14th c.] - Middle English unkempt / unkembed emerges as a direct participial compound meaning literally "uncombed, uncarded" applied strictly to sheep's wool, flax fibers, and human locks - [perception: functional, technical descriptor in agriculture, textile production, and personal hygiene]
- [16th c. Elizabethan Era] - revived and popularized by Edmund Spenser in 'The Faerie Queene' (1590) to evoke rustic, wild, and ancient pastoral archetypes (unkempt hair and rustic attire) - [perception: admired as a poetic, romantic archaism symbolizing primitive natural purity]
- [17th–18th c.] - broadened into literary criticism and landscape aesthetics, describing unpolished verse, crude rural dialects, and overgrown wild gardens - [perception: viewed by neoclassical critics as a stylistic defect, but celebrated by early Romantic pre-cursors for organic beauty]
- [19th c. Victorian Era] - firmly established in mainstream prose as a general term for disheveled gentlemen, absent-minded professors, neglected estates, and street urchins - [perception: standard social marker of eccentric preoccupation, poverty, or romantic wilderness]
- [20th–21st c.] - stable in modern English as a versatile adjective for messy hair, casual attire, and overgrown yards, devoid of harsh moral condemnation - [perception: accepted as an evocative, slightly elevated descriptor for charming or casual disarray]
