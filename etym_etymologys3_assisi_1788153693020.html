1. An Umbrian hill-town. Assisi begins as one of the many fortified settlements of ancient Umbria, a people whose language and names survive only in fragments. The Romans took the town in the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE and made it the municipium Asisium: a walled, orderly place of merchants and landowners, with a forum, a theatre, and the small temple of Minerva that still stands on the modern Piazza del Comune. For six centuries the name meant nothing more than a decent provincial town on the road between Perugia and Spoleto.

2. Francis and Clare: the name acquires a meaning. Everything changed in the decades around 1200. The merchant’s son born c. 1181 — baptized Giovanni, nicknamed Francesco (‘the Frenchman’) for his father’s cloth-trade with Provence — renounced his inheritance in the bishop’s square in 1206, and from the ruined chapel of San Damiano below the town began the preaching of poverty, joy and brotherhood with all creation that became the Franciscan order. Clare, daughter of the noble Offreduccio family of Assisi, fled to Francis at the Porziuncola in 1212 and founded the Poor Clares. Assisi thus became, within one lifetime, the cradle of the two great Italian mendicant movements; and because Francis died there in 1226 and was canonised in 1228, the town-name was written permanently into the Christian calendar and liturgy.

3. The basilica and the art. The double church of San Francesco (1228–1253) turned a hill-town into a monument of European art. Commonly attributed to Brother Elias, the Lower and Upper Basilicas were painted by Cimabue, by Giotto and his workshop (the twenty-eight scenes of the Legend of St Francis and the allegories of the vows), and by Simone Martini and Pietro Lorenzetti. This made Assisi one of the hinges of Italian painting — a school of naturalism before the full Renaissance — and the reason why art history, not only piety, keeps the name.

  • Papal and communal politics (13th–15th c.): the Sacred Convent (Sacro Convento) made Assisi a papal stronghold in Umbrian wars; the town alternated between Guelph and Ghibelline fortunes and commune self-rule; the name stayed politically weighty.
  • Decline and quiet (16th–18th c.): the friars enriched the basilica, the town stagnated under papal administration; the 1832 earthquake damaged the frescoes, prompting the first restorations — a prelude to the great 20th-century campaigns.
  • Modern sainthood and tourism (19th–20th c.): devotional revival, then mass tourism; the restoration of Giotto’s cycle in the 1960s–70s made Assisi a world art destination and drew millions of pilgrims to the tomb.

4. ‘The spirit of Assisi’ (1986–). On 27 October 1986 Pope John Paul II convened in Assisi the World Day of Prayer for Peace, gathering leaders of the Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu and other traditions in the square before the basilica. From that day ‘Assisi’ has functioned as a diplomatic and ecumenical noun: the name of the inter-faith meetings that followed, of papal ‘Assisi journeys’, and of the ‘spirit of Assisi’ — the conviction that religions meet in prayer for peace without surrendering their identities.

5. The name today. Assisi today is a triple-layered word. In guidebooks it is the pink-stone medieval town of Monte Subasio. In Catholic life it is the shrine of Francis, Clare and the Poor Clares, jubilee goal and place of the Portiuncula indulgence. In world speech it is the symbol of interreligious encounter that was born in 1986. UNESCO recognised the whole Franciscan complex and basilica in 2000, with the surrounding hill-town landscape, making the name also a conservation label. Few towns of 28,000 people have carried so much meaning in five letters.
