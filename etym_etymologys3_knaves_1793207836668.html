The story of knaves is a small tragedy of class: a word that began as an innocent boy ends as a convicted rogue, and the whole of English social history stands written in its fall. No sound law condemned it; society did. Masters distrusted their servants, gentlemen despised the vulgar, and over five centuries that contempt soaked into the syllables until the name of a station became the name of a vice.

The Germanic boy: The reconstruction *knaban- gathers a family of North-West Germanic words — Old High German knabo, Middle Dutch knape, Old Norse knapi — all circling the meanings boy, youth, squire, servant. Whether the root reaches deeper into Indo-European is unknown, and honest etymology says so plainly: attempts to wed it to the great root of knowing remain guesses, however tempting. What is certain is the starting point, a young male of low rank, the lowest piece on the household chessboard, valued for strength and obedience.

  • Old English — the honest lad: cnafa serves in hall and field without disgrace. Charters and glosses use it for boys and young retainers; no text sneers at it. The initial cluster cn- is pronounced in full, two crisp consonants at the word's prow.
  • Early Middle English — the servant: after the Conquest the native word sinks below the imported French servant and valet. A knave is now specifically a menial, and menials, in the literature of their masters, pilfer the pantry and lie about it. The stereotype is born long before the dictionary records it.
  • 13th–14th c. — low birth, low worth: the contrast of knave against knight — two words that once differed only in the career of the youth — hardens into a moral antithesis. Courtesy books warn gentlemen against knavish company; Langland's age already hears dishonesty humming inside the syllable.
  • 16th c. — rogue by definition: Tudor proclamations against vagabonds and Elizabethan rogue-literature fix the new sense: a knave cheats at dice, counterfeits sickness, and picks pockets. Shakespeare rings every change, from the honest knave of comedy to Iago, the honest-seeming knave of tragedy. The silent k begins its long retreat in speech while the spelling holds its post.
  • 17th–18th c. — the Jack and the jobber: card-players christen the lowest court card the knave, freezing the medieval household in pasteboard, while political pamphleteers promote the word to high abuse: knaves in office, knaves on the exchange, knaves at the Crown and Anchor. The servant's name has become the citizen's indictment.
  • 19th c. to today — the archaic thunderbolt: everyday speech abandons the word to Dickensian villains and stage rogues, which only sharpens it: Cobbett's heirs and modern orators reach for knaves precisely because it sounds like justice from an older, sterner England.

Sound changes: The great phonetic event is the simplification of initial kn- to plain n-, complete by the seventeenth century, though spelling never surrendered the k. The vowel travelled the familiar Middle English path from short a to the long a of the Great Vowel Shift, giving the modern diphthong, and the plural now voices its ending as /vz/ after the voiced stem. Every change is regular; the scandal lies entirely in the meaning, not the mouth.

Why servants became scoundrels: The mechanism is pejoration by association, the linguist's name for guilt by company. Words for low persons — villain (farmhand), churl (freeman farmer), lewd (lay person), knave (boy servant) — slide downward with terrible regularity, because the powerful describe the powerless and dictionaries record the powerful. Knaves is among the purest specimens: no knave did anything to the word except occupy, century after century, a station his betters despised. Cobbett, radical that he was, turns the weapon round: his knaves are the rich and the prosecuting, proof that a word forged by contempt can be seized and aimed back at the powerful.
