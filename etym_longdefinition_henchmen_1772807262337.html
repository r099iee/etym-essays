henchmen — the plural of henchman; faithful followers or political supporters, especially those prepared to engage in crime or dishonest practices on behalf of a powerful master. In the source text, the plaintiff reports that Xue Pan, 'a powerful Nanking boss', set a crowd of his henchmen on Feng Yuan and beat him so badly that he died, and that 'Xue and his henchmen' then disappeared without trace. The word frames Xue as a strongman whose power rests on money, influence, and men willing to beat a rival to death and then vanish.

In this literary context the term strips any residual dignity from 'follower' or 'retainer' and marks the killers as instruments of a criminal will rather than agents of justice or mere hirelings of a legitimate household. A henchman is defined by blind personal allegiance yoked to readiness for dirty or illegal work: the loyalty is to a person, not to law or office, and the readiness for violence is understood rather than stated. Collocations favour the plural and the possessive of a powerful or villainous figure: 'his henchmen', 'a crowd of henchmen', 'set his henchmen on' someone, 'loyal/hired/political henchmen'.

Polysemy — senses in English:
  • Historical horse-servant (now obsolete): a groom or attendant who tends a lord's horse or stands at his side — the original sense of the compound 'hengest-man' (see etymology); 'henxman' appears for a page of honour or personal attendant of rank.
  • Archaic personal attendant: a loyal personal attendant, squire-like follower, or political supporter standing close beside a man of power — a sense common in early modern prose before the pejorative reading took over.
  • Modern pejorative enforcer (primary current sense): a trusted subordinate of a criminal, villain, or unscrupulous politician, willing to do violent or dishonest work; the source-text sense.
  • Political operative (journalistic use): in modern political prose, 'henchmen' often means ruthless party operatives or loyal underlings who do the leader's unglamorous or unethical work, not necessarily physical beaters.
  • Fiction, comics, and games (cultural sense): 'the villain's henchmen' is a fixed trope of adventure fiction, comics, and video games — interchangeable, uniformed, loyal muscle that appears in numbers and is defeated in crowds.
  • Figurative/team sense (informal, rarer): jocular use for a person's loyal inner circle or entourage, draining the word of violence but keeping the flavour of uncritical personal service.
Boundaries. Retainers can still suggest paid household staff without criminal implication; minions stress subservience and smallness of status more than physical brutality; thugs or goons foreground street violence but lose the bond of personal loyalty to a particular master; bodyguards imply defensive protection, whereas henchmen act offensively as an assault squad; accomplices name shared guilt without the boss-and-gang hierarchy the context needs.

Register. Literary-to-journalistic, almost always disapproving; one rarely claims the label for oneself. In the novel the plural form does heavy narrative work: it converts a beating into a category of organized power — a boss's private force — and leaves the reader in no doubt that Xue Pan's money bought men, not merely followers.
