- mid–late 1600s — stingy appears in English as adj. from sting + -y: ‘sharp, biting, pungent’; also of persons ‘ill-tempered, mean-spirited’ (sharpness of manner, not yet fixed on money).
- c. 1690s–early 1700s — economic sense crystallizes: ‘niggardly, unwilling to spend or share’; the sting becomes the smart of refusal rather than of temper alone.
- 18th century — novel and essay usage normalizes financial stinginess as a comic and moral type (the stingy host, the stingy merchant); older ‘pungent/biting’ sense recedes in standard prose.
- 19th century — fully established colloquial pejorative; extended to meager portions and stinted supplies (‘a stingy allowance’); solidly distinct from mere thrift or frugality in educated usage.
- 20th century — mass media and everyday speech cement stingy as the default informal word for tight-fistedness; applied to tips, wages, government benefits, and emotional withholding; children’s speech and family rebuke keep it highly salient (‘Don’t be stingy!’).
- late 20th–21st century — consumer culture and review writing amplify portion and service contexts (‘stingy pour,’ ‘stingy freebies’); psychological self-help may discuss ‘scarcity mindset,’ but public perception of the bare word remains simple moral judgment: mean with what one has. No major taboo attaches to stingy itself (unlike niggardly, which suffers sound-association stigma).
