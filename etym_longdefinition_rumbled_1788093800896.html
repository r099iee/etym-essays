Rumbled is the simple past and past participle of rumble, a verb of sound and motion whose core meaning is “to make a continuous low, heavy, rolling noise,” and by transfer “to move while making such a noise.” In “Winter thunder on a wild wind rolled roaring up and rumbled in the Mountain,” the word gives the deep, prolonged, chest-thumping voice of thunder shaking inside the mountain — a sound with no sharp edge, felt as much as heard.

Primary senses — continuous low sound.

  • Of nature: thunder rumbles (the archetypal use since the 14th century); so do avalanches, earthquakes, distant gunfire, and falling rock.
  • Of vehicles and machines: carts, wagons, trains, engines, and lorries “rumble over bridges” or “rumble past” — sound fused with locomotion from the 16th–17th centuries onward.
  • Of the body: a hungry stomach “rumbles” — the same low churning noise, half digestive gurgle, half growl; by the 20th century this is the most familiar everyday use.
  • Of voices: a bass voice “rumbles,” threateningly or magnificently — the diction of thunder-gods and villains.

Secondary sense — movement with sound.

  • As an intransitive verb of motion: “the convoy rumbled across the plain” — the sound-word became a motion-word because heavy things that rumble are usually moving (carts, trains, tumbrils).
  • As a transitive verb, “to utter with a rumble”: “he rumbled a warning” — a low-throated utterance; attested but rarer.
  • Figurative: unrest, war, and discontent “rumble” in the distance — barely audible, growing trouble; journalism leans on this transfer from sound to mood constantly.

Slang senses — a separate branch.

  • British “to be found out”: “we’ve been rumbled” = our secret is discovered, we have been seen through; “to rumble (someone)” = to detect their game. This is 20th-century slang rooted in 16th–17th-century thieves’ cant, where “to rumble” meant “to perceive, understand, detect” — a word of the London underworld, probably (but not certainly) of Romani origin, with nothing to do with thunder.
  • American “gang rumble”: from the 1940s–1950s, “a rumble” is a street fight between rival youth gangs, popularised by the musical West Side Story; “they rumbled” = they fought. The bridge is the noise and menace of a scuffle, though the slang may be independent growth.

Domain and technical uses.

  • Rumble strip: rows of raised bumps on road verges that vibrate and roar when crossed, waking drowsy drivers — the sound-word built into infrastructure.
  • Rumble seat: an American term for the folding rear seat of a carriage or early automobile, named for the noise of the ride.
  • Rumbling as noun and adjective: “the rumbling of the avalanche,” “rumbling discontent” — the figurative rumbling of society.

Why so many senses? Rumble is onomatopoeic: English created the word to imitate a sound — low, repeated, rolling — and the imitation proved so apt that it was reused for every noise resembling the model: thunder, carts, stomachs, bass voices, distant cannon. Once the word meant “to sound like heavy motion,” it took one natural step to “to move heavily” itself (a wagon rumbled away). The slang senses then attached to the same form by different routes (cant and gang language), so a single word now carries one literal sound family and two unrelated slang families under one roof — context decides which “rumbled” you mean.
