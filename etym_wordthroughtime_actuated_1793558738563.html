- Late 16th to Early 17th Century - Entered Early Modern English directly from Scholastic Medieval Latin *actuātus*; primarily employed in metaphysical, theological, and natural philosophy texts to denote "actualizing" latent potentiality or infusing living, spiritual animation into passive physical matter.
- 17th Century Mechanistic & Philosophical Shift - Under the influence of Cartesian physics and English empiricism (Hobbes, Locke), human psychology was conceptualized as a delicate internal clockwork; *actuated* transitioned from abstract metaphysics to describing how passions and motives act as physical springs impelling human behavior.
- 18th Century Enlightenment & Historiographical Golden Age - Flourished as the preeminent analytical keyword of 18th-century rationalist prose and historiography (David Hume, Edward Gibbon, Samuel Johnson), admired for its cool, dignified objectivity when dissecting human motives ("actuated by fear," "actuated by the love of liberty").
- 19th Century Industrial & Mechanical Evolution - With the proliferation of steam power, railway engines, and automated industrial machinery, the verb gained a literal, mechanical application among engineers to describe valves, levers, and regulators set into physical motion by fluid pressure or mechanical linkage.
- 20th Century Electromechanical Specialization - As electrical engineering and aviation developed, *actuator* became a standard technical noun; in language, the mechanical and automated senses surged in technical and patent literature, while the psychological sense receded into judicial opinions, historical treatises, and formal literature.
- Contemporary Usage - Today *actuated* maintains a vigorous dual existence: universally prevalent in robotics, automation, and control engineering for mechanisms physically moved by electronic or pneumatic actuators; while in literature, jurisprudence, and scholarly prose it remains an elevated, highly refined term for the deep-seated motives governing human action.
