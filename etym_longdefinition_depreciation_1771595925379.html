depreciation: the action or process of lowering, or the fact of being lowered, in value — whether the value of money, of property, of an asset, of a commodity, or of a person's reputation, standing, or worth. At its core the word names a downward movement in price or estimation: something is marked down, judged to be worth less than it was before, or treated as of diminished account. The noun is the abstract companion of the verb depreciate, and it carries two interlocked faces — an objective, measurable face (value really does fall, in a ledger or in a market) and a subjective, judgmental face (someone asserts that a thing or a person has been reduced in worth, even when the intrinsic value has not changed).

Primary senses

  • Financial / accounting sense (the modern technical core): In bookkeeping and corporate finance, depreciation is the systematic allocation of the cost of a tangible fixed asset (machinery, buildings, vehicles) over its estimated useful life. A machine bought for a large sum is not charged to the year of purchase but "depreciated" — its cost is spread as an annual expense, matching the value that is being used up. Here depreciation is a non-cash charge that reduces reported profit while preserving capital. The accountant's model treats value as a finite stock that decays in time.
  • Currency / monetary sense: Depreciation is the fall in the value of one currency relative to another in a floating exchange-rate system — the pound "depreciates" against the dollar when fewer dollars buy one pound. Distinct from devaluation, which is the deliberate official lowering of a fixed exchange rate.
  • Goods and real estate sense: The fall in the resale value of a possession — a new car "depreciates" the moment it leaves the showroom; a house may depreciate through wear, neglect, or a collapsing local market.
  • Reputation / critical sense: The lowering of someone or something in esteem; a belittling, a marking-down of worth in print or speech. It is this sense that Ruskin deploys in the Preface to Modern Painters when he writes: "In three instances only have I spoken in direct depreciation of the works of living artists." The accountant's word is pressed into the service of art criticism, and the context is one of scrupulous self-measurement — a critic stating his liabilities with the exactness of a man entering figures in a ledger.
  • Obsolete / rare wider sense: In older usage, depreciation could simply mean the act of undervaluing or the contemptuous dismissal of worth in general, without any financial frame at all; the word could also once be used of a decline in the value of time or of human life.

Secondary and figurative extensions

  • Linguistic / rhetorical depreciation: In lexicography and rhetoric, "depreciatory" language is language that systematically lowers the status of its object — the vocabulary of belittling, sneering, or dismissal used as a deliberate instrument.
  • Environmental / resource depreciation: Natural-resource economics speaks of the depreciation of mineral deposits, forests, and ecological capital — the using-up of assets that are not replaced; the word here is a metaphor transferred from the factory ledger to the planet.
  • Intellectual / moral depreciation: The wearing-down of an idea, a doctrine, or a school of thought through sustained criticism, so that it loses prestige in the currency of opinion; "the depreciation of the old masters" in modern taste is a running theme of art history.

The semantic bridge

All these senses hang together on a single Latin model. The prefix de- means "down, away, off," and the stem pretium means "price, value, worth"; to depreciate is etymologically to "price down," just as to appreciate is to "price up" (from Latin appretiare, "to set a price on"). The Romans themselves used depretiare for the lowering of the price of a thing and, by extension, for the undervaluing of a person's worth. The two directions of the same verb — the accountant's markdown and the critic's belittlement — are not homonyms but one movement of mind applied to two fields: in both, value is something that can be measured and that can fall.

Near-synonyms and boundaries

  • Disparagement is the closest verbal rival: it belittles with contempt, often by dwelling on what is trivial or poor, and always carries a dismissive, sneering tone; depreciation is cooler and more objective — a judgment, not a sneer.
  • Denigration, from Latin denigrare, "to blacken," means to darken a reputation with aspersions, staining it as one stains cloth; it implies the smear of accusation, whereas depreciation implies a ledger-mark of reduced value.
  • Detraction is the active taking-away of someone's credit or merit — the fountain-metaphor of Latin detrahere, "to draw away"; it is what rivals do to the great, and it presupposes a prior reputation, which depreciation need not.
  • Devaluation is strictly monetary and formal — the official or market reduction of a currency's worth; depreciation is broader and covers non-financial value as well.
  • Undervaluation names the mistaken or unjust setting of too low a price without the process sense: you undervalue by error of judgment, you depreciate through an ongoing downward process.
  • Derogation, from Latin derogare, "to repeal part of a law," is the legal-to-rhetorical taking-away of standing or authority; it is more formal and legalistic than depreciation.
  • Belittlement is the plain, modern word for making something seem small; it is more overtly aggressive and personal, while depreciation keeps the clinical air of a measured markdown.

The word is thus a pivot on which the language turns from money to manners: the same mechanism that lowers the value of a stock in a ledger lowers the value of a reputation in the press, and Ruskin's sentence is the perfect literary demonstration — a financial term, handled with the precision of an accountant, doing the work of severe and dignified critical censure.
