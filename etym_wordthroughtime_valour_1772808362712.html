- Medieval chivalric courage - From the 13th century valour names prowess and worth in arms, the courage of knights in romance and chronicle, intimately tied to honour and the code of chivalry.
- Early Modern military sense - In the 16th-17th centuries it settles into disciplined martial courage, the bravery of soldiers in battle rather than of single heroes, as armies become organised and professional.
- Imperial and ceremonial register - In the 18th-19th centuries valour belongs to imperial historiography and military citations (medals for valour), an elevated, ceremonial word that assumes battlefield nobility.
- Modern literary survival - Today valour is rare in speech, where bravery and courage dominate; it survives in ceremonial English, historical prose, and poetry, keeping its elevated, almost old-fashioned dignity.
