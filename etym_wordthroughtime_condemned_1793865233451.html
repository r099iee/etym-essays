- c. 1300–1400s (Judicial Sentencing) - Strictly legal and solemn; denoted being formally found guilty by an authorized magistrate and sentenced to death, outlawry, or corporal forfeiture, carrying profound religious terror and civic finality.
- c. 1500–1600s (Theological Damnation) - Acquired intense spiritual weight during the Reformation; applied to souls judged unworthy of salvation and doomed to eternal perdition by divine decree, shifting from human courts to cosmic judgment.
- c. 1650–1780s (Secular Moral & Literary Censure) - Softened into secular literary and conversational registers to denote severe public disapproval, intellectual rebuke, or moral condemnation in civic discourse and Enlightenment criticism.
- c. 1830s–1900s (Bureaucratic & Sanitary Condemnation) - Institutionalized into public health, urban planning, and civil engineering; specifically used for structures, tenements, ships, or foodstuffs officially certified as dangerous, blighted, or unfit for human use.
- Late 20th–21st Century (Existential & Inevitable Doom) - Broadened philosophically and metaphorically to express inexorable entrapment in tragic cycles, inescapable psychological patterns, or unavoidable historic fates (e.g., Santayana's aphorism: 'Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it').
