bolted — In Prince Caspian, at the crash of thunder overhead, the horse Destrier “bolted in good earnest,” and Caspian, a good rider but not strong enough to hold him, keeps his seat while his life hangs by a thread among the trees of the dusk. “Bolted” names a specific kind of motion: not a gradual gallop but a sudden, irrevocable breakaway — the horse converts a moment of terror into one explosive, headlong flight, like a missile released. The phrase “in good earnest” sharpens it: no longer merely startled, the horse is now truly committed to flight, and “bolted” captures the violence of the ignition — the instant an animal’s terror becomes motion, straight, fast, and out of the rider’s control.

Primary sense. The core meaning of bolted (past tense and past participle of to bolt) is to have run away suddenly, violently, and uncontrollably — used above all of a horse or other animal that, startled by fear, breaks into a headlong rush and cannot be stopped. When applied to people, it means to have fled abruptly, to have dashed away, or (in older use) to have made off without ceremony. The word always implies suddenness, speed, and a loss of control or self-possession: a bolting horse is not merely running fast; it is running away.

Polysemy & secondary senses of “to bolt”.

  • To bolt one’s food: to swallow food hurriedly, without chewing, “as if shot down the throat”; “he bolted his breakfast” means he wolfed it down in a rush. The image of rapid, straight passage connects this sense to the projectile “bolt.”
  • To bolt a door or window: to fasten it with a sliding metal bar (the “bolt”); “the door was bolted” means it was secured, not left running — an instructive opposite to the running-away sense.
  • To bolt (a screw / parts together): to join or fix with metal bolts; “the frame was bolted to the wall.”
  • To bolt (political / formal): in American political usage of the 19th–20th centuries, to bolt a party or convention is to break away from one’s party, to defect or revolt — the running-away sense transferred to politics.
  • To bolt (of plants): in horticulture, a plant that “bolts” runs prematurely to seed, shooting up a flower stalk before its time — another sudden, straight, uncontrollable upward rush.
  • The noun “bolt” family: a bolt of lightning (the forked flash, like a missile), a bolt of cloth or paper (a rolled length), a crossbow bolt (the short stout arrow), a bolt of a gun (the sliding mechanism) — all share the root image of a straight, driven, thick rod or shaft.

Register and usage. “Bolted” is an ordinary, vigorous English word, equally at home in narrative prose, journalism, and speech. It is the standard verb for a horse’s uncontrollable flight, and it is the natural word for the scene in Caspian precisely because it fuses mechanical and animal imagery: the horse goes off “like a bolt from a crossbow.” In the past participle it can also be adjectival — a “bolted door” is one that is fastened, so context always decides which of the two great senses (fastened firmly, or fled wildly) is meant; the two live side by side as a neat pair of opposites built on the same metal bar.

Historical bridge. The whole family grows out of the physical object: the bolt, the short, heavy, straight-flying arrow discharged by a crossbow. From that missile come both the bolt of lightning that shoots across the sky and the verb’s central image — a sudden, straight, unstoppable trajectory. The horse that bolts is imagined as a released missile, and the terrified animal in Caspian’s passage is exactly that: a thing launched, no longer governable, carrying its rider into the darkness of the wood.
