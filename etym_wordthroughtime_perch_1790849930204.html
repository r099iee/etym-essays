- 13th c.: Entered Middle English from Old French *perche*, initially denoting both a measuring rod/pole and a bird's elevated roosting bar.
- 14th c. to 15th c.: Evolved into an intransitive verb (*perchen*) describing birds landing and gripping a roost.
- 16th c. to 17th c.: Broadened in Early Modern English to describe humans sitting high up or objects positioned atop heights.
- 18th c. to 19th c.: Widely used in landscape aesthetics and Romantic poetry to portray dramatic clifftop castles and mountain vistas.
- 20th c. to Present: Flourished in psychological, literary, and semiotic imagery to capture transient, poised states of contemplation and delicate textual dwelling.
