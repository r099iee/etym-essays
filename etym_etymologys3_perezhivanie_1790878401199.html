The Russian concept of perezhivanie (переживание) occupies a singular place in the history of performance theory and psychology. Constructed from ancient Slavic and Indo-European morphemes of life and endurance, the term evolved from an everyday description of emotional suffering into the theoretical cornerstone of 20th-century realism in theater and psychology.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Slavic Morphological Composition:

  • The prefix pere- (пере-) descends from the Proto-Indo-European spatial root *per- ("through, across, beyond, forward"), which produced Greek peri- / para-, Latin per- / trans-, and Germanic *far- / through. In Slavic languages, pere- denotes movement through a medium, crossing a threshold, repetition, or endurance over time.
  • The verbal root zhit’ (жить, "to live") descends from Proto-Slavic *žiti, which traces directly to the fundamental Proto-Indo-European root *gʷeyh₃- ("to live"). This root is the ancestor of Greek bios ("life") and zoon ("animal"), Latin vivere / vita ("to live / life"), Sanskrit jīvati ("lives"), and English quick (originally "alive").
  • In Old East Slavic, the prefixed verb perežiti meant "to outlive, survive, endure a period of time, or experience a trial." By adding the deverbal noun suffix -anie (-ание), Russian formed the abstract substantive perezhivanie, literally denoting "the process or state of living through something."

2. 19th-Century Russian Literature and the Soul’s Crucible:

  • In 19th-century Imperial Russia, in the great literary works of Leo Tolstoy, Fyodor Dostoevsky, and Ivan Turgenev, perezhivanie was used to describe intense, soul-stirring internal crises, moral awakenings, and profound tribulations. Unlike simple "feeling" (chuvstvo) or "emotion" (emotsiya), perezhivanie implied an existential process: an experience that penetrates deep into the human spirit, demanding contemplation and leaving a permanent psychological imprint.

3. Konstantin Stanislavski and the Theatrical Revolution (1898–1938):

  • When Konstantin Stanislavski and Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko founded the Moscow Art Theatre (MAT) in 1898, European acting was dominated by mechanical declamation, vocal histrionics, and formulaic gestures. Stanislavski sought a revolutionary alternative that would match the psychological subtlety of Anton Chekhov's plays.
  • Stanislavski codified his system around the dichotomy between two acting philosophies: the "art of representation" (iskusstvo izobrazheniya) and the "art of experiencing" (iskusstvo perezhivaniya). In perezhivanie, the actor does not present an external caricature of jealousy, grief, or ambition; instead, by entering the "given circumstances" of the play, the actor creates a living psychological process on stage, experiencing genuine, organic emotions that emerge organically in the present moment.
  • When Stanislavski's treatises were translated into English in the 1930s (by Elizabeth Reynolds Hapgood in An Actor Prepares), perezhivanie was rendered simply as "experiencing." However, later theater scholars (such as Jean Benedetti and Sharon Carnicke) pointed out that "experiencing" lost the vital Russian connotation of an active, arduous "living-through," prompting modern practitioners to preserve the original Russian term perezhivanie.

4. Lev Vygotsky and Cultural-Historical Psychology:

  • Simultaneously in the 1930s, the pioneering Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky adopted perezhivanie as a foundational concept in child development. Vygotsky recognized that a child’s development cannot be understood merely by measuring environmental factors; rather, what matters is the child’s *perezhivanie*—how the child emotionally experiences, interprets, and internalizes that environment.
  • Today, in both international theater conservatories and cognitive psychology, perezhivanie stands as a vital concept uniting emotion, cognition, art, and lived human experience.
