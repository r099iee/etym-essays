The biography of damask is not the usual story of a root growing a family of words; it is the story of a city lending its name to everything fine that passed through its markets. There is no Proto-Indo-European root to trace, because the word's ancestry is a toponym — a place-name — and the whole history of damask is a history of metonymy: the name of Damascus transferred, again and again, to the goods for which the city was famous.

1. The ancient city and its ancient name. Damascus is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities on earth, and its name is correspondingly ancient. The earliest records are Egyptian: in the fifteenth century BC the texts write the city as t-ms-ḳw, one of the oldest attestations of the name in any language. In the fourteenth century BC the Akkadian of the Amarna letters has Dimašqa, the form through which the city enters the diplomatic correspondence of the ancient Near East. Biblical Hebrew writes it דַּמֶּשֶׂק (Damméseq), and Arabic, the language of the city for over a millennium, has دِمَشْق (Dimashq). Scholars suspect the oldest layer of the name may be pre-Semitic — that is, older than the Semitic languages that later fixed it — and its original meaning, if it ever had one recoverable, is unknown. None of this matters for the history of the word damask as an English term except as backdrop: the city itself was already ancient and already famous for trade when the name began its career as a label for cloth.

2. The city of silk and steel. Damascus stood for millennia at the crossroads of the great trade routes — the Silk Road from China, the spice routes from India, the caravan routes of Arabia — where the merchandise of the East met the markets of the Mediterranean. Its weavers produced figured silks, its smiths produced blades of hardened steel with the distinctive watered pattern that European armourers could not replicate, and its markets supplied the roses and plums that European gardeners would come to call by the city's name. For the European merchant and noble, "of Damascus" was a phrase of automatic luxury, and the adjective damascenus in Medieval Latin already labelled the choicest oriental goods.

3. From city-name to cloth. The decisive step was taken in Medieval Latin, where the noun damascus came to mean the figured cloth of Damascus, and in Old French, where damas named the fabric itself. English borrowed the word in the sixteenth century, in the great age of imported luxury, and damask entered the language as a noun and adjective for the rich silk whose pattern was woven into the cloth. The technical principle is worth restating because it explains the word's lasting prestige: damask is not printed and not embroidered — the design is produced by the weave itself, glossy areas of warp-faced satin against matt areas of twill, all in a single colour, so that the pattern reverses in the light. In an age before mechanical printing of cloth, a figured damask was proof of a weaver's craft at the highest level, and the word carried that proof with it.

4. The family of the city-name. Once the city's name had become a fabric, the same toponym began to spawn a whole lexical family, each member a separate loan or extension from the same source. The damask rose came to Europe through the same eastern trade, named in Latin rosa damascena; the damson plum is etymologically the "plum of Damascus" (Latin damascenum prūnum); the patterned steel of the city's blades gave Damascus steel; and the metalworking technique of inlaying gold and silver into steel to imitate the watered pattern gave the verb to damascene (also damaskeen) and the craft of damascening. It is an extraordinary feat of semantic productivity for a single place-name: the same word wears a fabric, a flower, a fruit, a metal, and a craft, all descendants of the same ancient city.

5. The age of industry and the democratisation of luxury. The nineteenth century transformed the word's social reach. The invention and perfection of the Jacquard loom made figured weaving cheap and automatic, and damask cloth moved from the wardrobes of princes to the table-linen cupboards of the middle class. A "damask tablecloth" became a standard household phrase, and the word lost some of its oriental glamour while gaining a new, domestic precision. In the modern textile industry "damask" is a technical term for any reversible figured fabric — silk, linen, cotton, or synthetic — produced on Jacquard or similar looms, and the word's older exoticism survives chiefly in literary and historical usage, where a narrator still reaches for damask to signal old-fashioned, quietly sumptuous luxury.

6. The word in literature and modern life. For the novelists of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, damask was a shorthand for wealth and taste: an interior furnished with damask hangings and laid with damask napery was a room that announced its owner's cultivation without a word. The card's own sentence is a perfect example of this code — the slate-blue silk damask jacket with its medallions and tasselled borders is chosen to establish a character's opulence and discernment in a single phrase. Today the word has three lives: a technical textile term, a rose in the perfume trade (the damask rose remains the principal source of attar), and a literary adjective of old-fashioned luxury. From an Egyptian scribe's transcription of a Bronze-Age city-name to a modern tablecloth and a rose garden, damask has spent four thousand years as a word for the finest things the East could send — and the city of its birth still glitters inside it.
