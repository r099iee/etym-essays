Latin(attested 3rd c. BCE)
cadere
"to fall, sink, die"
(earlier PIE origin debated — see note)
↓
Latin(attested classical, 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE)
dēcadere
"to fall away, decline, decay"
(de- "down from" + cadere)
↓
Medieval Latin(attested 12th–13th c.)
dēcadentia
"decay, deterioration, falling away"
(from cadentia "a falling")
↓
Middle French(16th c.; Montaigne)
décadence
"fall, decay, decline (of empires, morals)"
(Montesquieu 1734: "grandeur et décadence des Romains")
↓
Early Modern English(attested 1540s)
decadence
"moral or cultural decline"
(rival form decadency now rare)
↓
Modern English(19th c. – )
decadence
"cultural decline; artistic over-refinement"
(Decadent Movement 1880s–90s; modern "indulgent" sense)

Note on the debate — Latin cadere has no universally accepted PIE reconstruction: some scholars posit a root meaning "to fall" with possible Celtic connections, others a substrate origin; the attested line of decadence therefore begins safely at cadere, and no deeper stage should be asserted as fact.
