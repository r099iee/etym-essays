- Old English: the two elements have stood side by side in the language from the beginning — nīwe "recent, fresh" and hēawan "to cut, strike, shape by cutting" — but they remain separate words; freshly cut stone and timber are described by the participle alone
- Middle English: "hewen" is the regular word for cutting and shaping stone and wood in building accounts and devotional prose; collocations pairing "new" with participles multiply (new-built, new-made), and "new hewen" occurs naturally of masonry and timber
- 16th–17th c.: the King James Bible fixes the monumental register of "hewn" forever — Christ's tomb is "his own new tomb, which he had hewn out in the rock" (Matthew 27:60), Israel is bidden to look "unto the rock whence ye are hewn" (Isaiah 51:1), and "hew thee two tables of stone" rings through Exodus 34:1; around these phrases, hewn compounds gain scriptural solemnity, and new + hewn acquires epic weight
- 18th–19th c.: Romantic and antiquarian prose exploits "new-hewn" for freshly raised monuments and freshly cleared forest — the word smells of axe-strokes and quarry dust
- 20th c.: fantasy and adventure prose (as in Tolkien's The Hobbit, from which this very sentence comes) revives "new-hewn" for dramatic effect: walls raised at need, gates sealed overnight
- Today: rare, literary, deliberately antique; builders say "freshly cut" or "newly dressed", but a "new-hewn" wall still raises the archaic dignity of the axe and the quarry
