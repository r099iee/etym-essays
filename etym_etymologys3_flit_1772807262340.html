1. The PIE source: a world of flowing water. English flit belongs to one of the most productive roots in Indo-European: PIE *pleu- "to flow, float, swim". The root is reconstructed with confidence from cognates across the whole family — Sanskrit plavate "swims, floats", Greek pléō "I sail, float", Latin pluere "to rain", and the Germanic group behind English flow, flood and fleet. Its core image is buoyant, liquid motion: things carried on or through water, moving without effort and without resistance. Even before the word reaches English, then, the notion of effortless, gliding movement is already embedded in the root. 2. Germanic inheritance and the Norse causative. - Proto-Germanic *fleutaną "to float, flow" descends directly from *pleu-. Old English flēotan "to float" yields the adjective fleet, while the wider family gives flood and flow. - The specific ancestor of English flit, however, is Old Norse, not Old English. Norse fljóta "to float, flow, swim" (from *fleutaną) was turned into a causative, flytja, meaning "to cause to float; to carry, convey, remove". A causative adds the idea of making something move, and it is precisely this causative flytja that the English verb descends from. - Sound changes worth noting: the Germanic cluster *fleut- surfaces in Norse as fljó- through the breaking and fronting effects around the liquid and glide; the causative -j- triggers umlaut on the root vowel, giving flytja. These are the phonetic fingerprints of the Norse form in the English loan. 3. Borrowing into English. Viking settlement left a deep Norse layer in the dialects of northern and eastern England, and countless everyday verbs entered Middle English from that Scandinavian contact. Middle English borrowed flytja as flitten/flytten, meaning "to move, migrate, change residence, depart", first recorded around 1200–1300. For centuries the word's core sense in English was moving house or travelling onward, and it remained strongest in the North and in Scots, where "to flit" still means to change one's dwelling and "the flitting" is the removal itself — the Scottish mover's lorry is still colloquially "the flit van". 4. Semantic drift toward lightness. Alongside the "move house" sense, English developed a finer sense of flit as passing lightly and rapidly from place to place: birds and butterflies flit, glances flit, thoughts flit. This sense emphasizes motion so easy that it barely disturbs the medium it passes through — the very buoyancy inherited from the watery PIE root. Some scholars suspect reinforcement from continental verbs (Middle Dutch vletten "to glide, float", Middle High German vlitten "to move"), though the standard derivation runs through flytja and the light-motion sense is a natural extension of "moving from place to place" applied to smaller, quicker subjects. 5. The nineteenth-century idiom of the midnight exit. - From the dialectal "move house" line grew the informal phrase "do a (moonlight) flit": to leave a dwelling secretly and hurriedly, above all to avoid paying the rent or escaping creditors. - "Moonlight" (19th century) marks departure by night, when the landlord cannot be confronted; the idiom retains the same lightness as the bird's dart but applies it to evasion of obligation. - Socio-cultural context: urban tenancy, rapid population movement in industrializing Britain, and the figure of the "furniture-flitting" debtor all fed the idiom. It passed quickly into journalism and fiction as shorthand for a cowardly, unscrupulous exit that leaves unpaid debts and unfinished business behind. 6. Literary attestation and the present word. The word's double life is visible in the passage from David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone: the kidnapper hopes to "pocket the money from both parties and then do a flit". Etymologically he is still "causing himself to float" away — buoyant motion as escape — but morally he is the opposite of an honest house-mover, using lightness and speed to erase accountability. Modern English thus carries, in a single short word, the PIE river, the Norse ship, the Scots removal van and the debtor's midnight exit: no other common English verb packs flow, float, migration, escape and betrayal into so small a compass.
