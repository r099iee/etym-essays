The plural commandments carries three thousand years of authority in a single suffix. Its ancestor is Latin mandare, to entrust or commit to someone charge — literally to give into someone hand, from manus (hand) + dare (to give). With the intensive prefix com- it becomes commandare in Late Latin, shifting decisively from entrust to command: to order with the full weight of the person entrusting. Old French inherits this as comander, to order or rule, and Middle English borrows it around 1300 as comaunden. The noun commandment, an order or decree, follows in the fourteenth century via Old French commandement, and the plural commandments is established by the age of Chaucer and Wycliffe.

The word would have remained a routine term for orders had it not been seized by religion. The Latin Vulgate renders the biblical precepts as mandata and praecepta; English Bibles from Tyndale to King James fix them as commandments, above all the Ten Commandments delivered to Moses in Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5. By the seventeenth century the Commandments with a capital C needs no numeral: every hearer supplies ten, stone tablets, Sinai, thou-shalt-not. The Book of Common Prayer catechism, church walls, schoolrooms, and courtrooms inscribe them, giving the plural a monumental, memorized, half-remembered quality that secular uses ever after exploit.

  • 1300–1500: feudal and chivalric orders. In Middle English a commandment is any authoritative bidding — a king, lord, or captain commandments. It is weightier than a request and more personal than a law: it binds because of who speaks. Romance and chronicle use it for knightly and royal orders without necessary religious colour.
  • 1500–1700: Reformation fixation. Tyndale, Coverdale, Geneva, and King James Bibles, plus catechisms and homilies, make the Ten Commandments the dominant association. To break a commandment is to sin; to keep them is duty. The plural becomes quasi-proper: Commandments. Schoolboys memorize them; divines expound them verse by verse. Secular rulers borrow the aura by calling statutes commandments.
  • 1700–1900: moralized extension. Enlightenment and Victorian writers speak of commandments of reason, of nature, of health, of grammar — any set of non-negotiable rules stated with biblical gravity. The irony is already available: to call schoolroom grammar rules commandments both elevates them and hints they are obeyed from fear rather than understanding. Thackeray, Dickens, and school memoirs use it so.
  • 1900–present: the hazily recalled set. As religious observance and rote learning recede, commandments survive as the name for rules learned by heart in childhood and imperfectly retained: grammatical commandments (never split an infinitive, never end with a preposition), journalistic commandments, parental commandments. The source sentence depends exactly on this stage — a few grammatical commandments hazily recalled from high school — where the biblical echo makes half-memory comic and slightly oppressive: stone tablets now prescribing commas.

Formally the word is transparent: command + -ment (result or instrument of an action, as in judgment, parliament) + -s (plural). The suffix -ment came with the French borrowing and never varied. Pronunciation moved from Old French /ko.mɑ̃ˈde/ to Middle English /koˈmaundement/ to modern RP /kəˈmɑːndmənts/, with reduced first vowel, long a in the stressed syllable, and a consonant cluster /ndmənts/ that invites the very mumbling schoolroom recitation the source sentence evokes. Spelling settled early as commandment; the doubled m distinguishes it from commendment, which died out.

Literary attestation is dense because the referent is ubiquitous: Wycliffe Ten Commandments, Shakespeare thou-shalt-not jokes, the King James Exodus 20 tablets of stone, Sterne and Fielding ironic uses, Shaw commandments of the maker, Orwell commandments of Animal Farm (All animals are equal, rewritten on the barn wall — the great modern parody of monumental plural rules), and countless style guides that list commandments of good writing only to demolish them. The source passage joins that debunking tradition: grammatical commandments are what ordinary citizens carry away from school — absolute, moralized, vague — and a serious account of written discourse must go beyond them toward description of what prose actually does.
