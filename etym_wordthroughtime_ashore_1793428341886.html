- c. 1400 — Late Middle English prepositional phrase: a schore appears in maritime records and chronicles for motion toward or landing on the sea-bank.
- 16th c. — Fusion during the Age of Sail: The elements coalesce into the single adverb ashore, entering naval logs, admiralty law, and sea ballads.
- 17th–18th c. — The great maritime binary: Established in literature (Defoe, Swift) as the opposite of *afloat*, defining the alternating rhythm of maritime adventure and landward observation.
- 19th c. — Naval service bifurcation: The Royal Navy formally divides personnel duties and commands into "sea duty" and "shore duty / service ashore."
- 20th c. — Metaphorical and ecological extension: Expands into broader figurative usage for escaping tumultuous situations, as well as marine biology (marine mammals coming ashore).
- 21st c. — Contemporary nautical and general usage: Universal term for disembarking from watercraft or being situated on dry land after maritime transit.
