LINEAR CHAIN (single root word HALLOWED) Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *kailo- “whole, uninjured, of good omen” (c. 4000 BCE), the root of wholeness and soundness; its descendants include Sanskrit kevala “entire, alone” (a proposed cognate), Old Prussian kailas “healthy, whole,” and Old Church Slavonic cělъ “whole” (Russian tselyy). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hailaz “healthy, whole, sound; of good omen” (the ancestor of English whole, hale, heal, health, and the greeting hail). Stage 3 — Proto-Germanic *hailagaz “holy” (the adjective *hailaz + suffix *-aga-, “belonging to the sound/intact order of things”); its attested forms include Old Saxon hēlag, Old High German heilag (German heilig), Old Norse heilagr, and Gothic hailags. Stage 4 — Old English hālig “holy” (recorded from the earliest Old English texts, 8th–9th c.), the adjective of divine sanctity. Stage 5 — Old English hālgian “to make holy, consecrate” (the denominative verb on hālig, recorded in Old English), the direct ancestor of the modern verb to hallow. Stage 6 — Middle English halwen / halowen “to consecrate, to honour as holy” (from Old English hālgian, with the regular Middle English development of the root vowel and consonant cluster), whose past participle halowed gives the adjective. Stage 7 — English hallowed (adj.) “consecrated, made holy; (fig.) revered by long tradition” — the modern form HALLOWED, as on this card. NOTES - The same Old English hālgian produced the noun halwe “holy one, saint,” whence All Hallows’ Eve (the eve of All Saints’ Day), contracted to Halloween — the word survives in daily use centuries after the verb hallow became archaic. - The Germanic root *hailaz and its derivative *hailagaz also underlie whole, hale, heal, health, and the greeting hail; the religious and the physical-health senses are two branches of one concept of soundness. - The Indo-Iranian and Balto-Slavic cognates (Sanskrit kevala, Old Prussian kailas, Old Church Slavonic cělъ) are traditionally adduced for *kailo-; the exact shape of the PIE etymon is debated (some scholars set *kéh₂i-lo- with a laryngeal), but the Germanic and Balto-Slavic sets are secure.
