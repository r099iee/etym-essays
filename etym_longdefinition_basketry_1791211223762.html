Basketry is both the craft of weaving baskets and the baskets so made collectively. As an activity it denotes the art, technique, and trade of interlacing pliable plant materials into containers and structures; as a product noun it denotes woven ware in general, especially hand-made vessels.

In the Durant sentence, Akin to basketry, perhaps born of it, was the art of pottery, basketry means the primeval woven-container technology from which fired clay vessels allegedly developed. Clay daubed on wicker hardened when heated, suggesting the path from flexible basket to rigid pot. Here basketry stands for a whole technological stage of humanity.

Principal modern senses:

  • Craft and technique: the skill of weaving, twining, coiling, and plaiting rushes, willow, cane, rattan, bamboo, grasses, and synthetic analogues into functional forms. Textbooks distinguish coiled basketry, twined basketry, plaited basketry, wicker basketry. Classes teach basketry as heritage skill and therapy.
  • Collective product: baskets as a category: Native American basketry, African basketry, fine basketry in the museum. Dealers and curators speak of a piece of basketry, basketries in plural for styles.
  • Industry and trade: commercial manufacture of hampers, creels, cane furniture, and decorative storage. Willow basketry remains a rural industry in Somerset, Poland, and China.
  • Architectural and artistic extension: by analogy, open lattice weaving in other media, wicker furniture called basketry weave, and contemporary fiber art that uses basketry techniques for sculpture rather than vessels.

Materials and process nuance: true basketry uses unspun flexible elements, unlike weaving which uses spun yarn on a loom, and unlike pottery which shapes plastic clay. Ethnobotany matters: willow withies for wicker, oak splints for Appalachian carriers, sweetgrass for coils, rattan cane for commercial frames. The word evokes hand, natural material, and irregular human touch versus molded plastic.

Figurative and institutional uses:

  • Anthropological stage: basketry culture, basket-maker Indians, pre-pottery horizons where archaeologists infer baskets from impressions in clay. Implies ingenuity before wheel and kiln.
  • Aesthetic value: fine basketry praised for pattern, tightness, symmetry, and beauty more than modern forms, as in Durant. Collectors value Pomo feathered baskets or Japanese bamboo hanakago as high art.
  • Metaphor of interweaving: occasionally writers speak of a basketry of ideas, meaning interlaced strands forming a holding structure, though this is rare and literary.

Grammar: uncountable for the craft to teach basketry and countable for types basketries of the world. Collocates with traditional, coiled, twined, willow, cane, art of, revival of. Register is standard, slightly technical-craft and museum-like, never slang. It differs from wickerwork which stresses the wicker material and lattice look, and from wicker which can mean the material itself.
