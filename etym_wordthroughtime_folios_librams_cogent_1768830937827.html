- 15th century (folio, from Latin folium "leaf"): originally a single sheet of paper, the written leaf
- 16th–17th century: book-trade format — in folio, a sheet folded once, the largest book size; the grand, unwieldy volume
- 17th–18th century: the age of the Shakespeare Folios — folio as a monument of print, a library piece proper to the raw wisdom of ages
- Modern: any large-format book; the word keeps its leaf-heritage, a book that still remembers it is a stack of vegetable leaves

- 1950 (libram, coined by Jack Vance in The Dying Earth): a deliberate archaism built on Latin liber, "book" — reaching past the modern Romance words to the ancient sense of the inner bark of a tree
- Within the fiction: a formal, forbidden text, leather-bound, setting forth the syllables of powerful spells — a book that must be unpeeled layer by layer like bark
- Modern: not a standard dictionary word — a Vancean coinage recognized by readers for its manufactured Latin depth

- Mid-17th century (cogent): enters English meaning "compelling, forcibly convincing" — an argument that drives assent before it
- 18th–19th century: restrained to reasoning — cogent evidence, cogent reasoning; the force of logic rather than of emotion
- Modern: the standard term of argumentation — clear, logical, and compelling, applied almost always to reasons, proofs, and spells
