SAWMILL — two linear chains, one for each element, joined at the compound:
Element 1 — saw:
1. PIE *sek- "to cut, cut off" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the root of Latin secāre "to cut" (English section, bisect, dissect, intersect, insect, secant) and of Old Church Slavonic sěšti "to cut" (Russian sech').
2. Proto-Germanic *sagō "saw (the tool)" (c. 500 BCE–CE 500; o-grade *sag- + noun suffix).
3. Old English sagu "saw" (attested c. 8th–11th c.).
4. Middle English sawe "saw" → English saw (n.), the cutting tool.
5. Compound: English saw + mill → sawmill (17th c.) (TARGET, first element).
Element 2 — mill:
1. PIE *melh₁- "to grind, crush" (c. 4000–3000 BCE) — the family of Greek mýlē "mill, millstone," Lithuanian malti "to grind," and the native Germanic meal-word *melwą (Old English melu, English meal; German Mehl "flour").
2. Latin molere "to grind"; mola "millstone" (classical).
3. Late Latin molīna "mill" (imperial and medieval).
4. Old English mylen "mill" (borrowed, c. 8th c.) → Middle English mille → English mill (n.).
5. Compound: English saw + mill → sawmill (17th c.) (TARGET, second element).
Anti-merging note: this saw (the tool, from *sek- "to cut") is a different word from the verb saw, past tense of see, which descends from PIE *sekʷ- "to see" — two roots that only share a spelling.

DIPHTHERIA — linear chain for diphtheria:
1. Greek déphō "to knead, soften" (classical; PIE ancestry debated — possibly *debh- "to stamp, knead," but no secure reconstruction is agreed).
2. Greek diphthéra "a prepared hide, skin, leather; leather parchment" (classical, e.g. Herodotus) — the leather made by soaking, scraping, and kneading.
3. Modern Latin / Greek diphtheria "the leather-membrane disease" — coined in the 1850s by the French physician Pierre Bretonneau from diphthéra + the disease-suffix -ia.
4. English diphtheria (1850s) (TARGET) — named for the grey, leathery membrane the disease forms over the throat.
Debate/nuance note: Bretonneau's coinage (originally French diphtérie / diphthérite) settled in English as diphtheria with the pathological suffix -ia; the deeper pre-Greek history of déphō is unknown, and the handbooks state this explicitly rather than reconstructing a root on speculation.
