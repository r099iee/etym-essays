- Late 14th c. — English takes "proclamacioun" (from Old French proclamacion, from Latin proclamatio) as a legal-technical word: a solemn public announcement by a king, bishop, or town authority, cried aloud by criers and sheriffs.
- 15th–17th c. — the royal proclamation matures into an instrument of state: printed, posted, and cried in the streets; Henry VIII's 1539 statute briefly gives proclamations the force of law, and in 1610 the Case of Proclamations rules they cannot create new offences — the word carries real constitutional weight.
- 18th–19th c. — proclamations go global and democratic: colonial proclamations (the Royal Proclamation of 1763), independence proclamations, and the Emancipation Proclamation (1863); the word still means official announcement of state, now increasingly republican and international.
- 20th c. — international bodies proclaim rights and decades (the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948, is "proclaimed" in its own text); clubs, societies, and causes appropriate the word for solemn announcements of their own.
- 21st c. — the legal core survives, while everyday speech inflates the word into affectionate hyperbole: "I hereby issue a proclamation that..." — a joke made possible precisely by the word's solemn history.
