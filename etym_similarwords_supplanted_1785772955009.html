• Superseded: Similar because both mean that something newer occupies the place of something older; differs because superseded stresses official or qualitative obsolescence (a standard superseded by a new edition), while supplanted more readily allows craft, rivalry, or evolutionary takeover and not only authorized update.
  • Replaced: Similar because it is the general verb of substitution, and "X replaced Y" often says exactly what "X supplanted Y" says; differs because replaced is the blandest and most neutral term with no historical color, whereas supplanted is more formal and carries the heel-tripping image of competitive displacement.
  • Displaced: Similar because both involve something being removed from its position; differs because displaced focuses on removal or forced relocation and does not always assert that the displacer fully occupies the same functional niche the way supplanted does.
  • Ousted: Similar because both mean removing someone from a position of power or place; differs because ousted is overtly forceful and usually personal or political — an open expulsion — whereas supplanted can be quiet, gradual, or systemic and need not involve a dramatic purge.
  • Usurped: Similar because both mean seizing another's position or role; differs because usurped implies illegitimate seizure of authority and is always wrongful, whereas supplanted can be legitimate market or evolutionary succession as well as underhanded ousting.
  • Overthrown: Similar because both describe someone removed from a position of power; differs because overthrown implies violent or revolutionary removal of a ruler or regime, while supplanted is usually non-violent and about occupying the vacated place rather than destroying the old order.
  • Deposed: Similar because both mean removing someone from office or rank; differs because deposed is specifically about formal removal from a throne or high office, whereas supplanted emphasizes the successor taking over the role.
  • Dispossessed: Similar because both involve taking something (a position, property, place) away from someone; differs because dispossessed stresses the loss of what was owned, while supplanted stresses that another now occupies the functional place.
  • Dislodged: Similar because both mean forcing something out of its position; differs because dislodged suggests a physical or mechanical removal without necessarily an occupying successor, while supplanted always implies a replacement that takes the place.
  • Shouldered aside / Elbowed out: Similar because both are vivid phrases for pushing a rival out of the way; differs because these are informal and image-driven, while supplanted is formal, and they do not carry the full Latin heel-tripping or systemic-succession history.
  • Succeeded: Similar because both describe coming after someone in a position; differs because succeeded is neutral and often orderly (a successor succeeds a predecessor by rule or appointment), while supplanted implies the predecessor was displaced, not simply followed.
  • Relegated: Similar because both describe something being moved down or out of a place; differs because relegated means demoted to a lower position or rank, whereas supplanted means removed from the position entirely and replaced by another.
