From the pig's wallow to the tarnished name. The history of sully is a small masterpiece of metaphor, a word that travels in four short steps from a pig rolling in mud to the destruction of an honoured reputation. The journey begins in the reconstructed Indo-European root *sū-, the expressive, probably onomatopoeic word for the pig, a root that rings through the whole family: Latin sūs "swine," Old English sū "sow," Old Norse sýr, German Sau, and Sanskrit sūkará- "wild boar." From Latin sūs the Romans derived the adjective suīllus, "pertaining to swine," used of pork and of things pig-like; and from that stock, in the Gallo-Romance that would become French, grew the noun souil, the wallow — the muddy pool in which a pig churns and slops — and the verb souiller, to soil, to make dirty, originally with the vivid force of "to wallow in mire like a pig." The word thus entered the world already wet and filthy, carrying its animal image on its back. The sound and sense of a French loan. Old French souiller belonged to a crowded family of verbs of defilement, and its particular niche was the mud of the sty. Phonologically the word was shaped by the reduction of the Latin stem: the consonant s followed by the glide of souil produced a sound English borrowed in the 14th century as sulien, later sullen, spelled and pronounced with the plain u that gives modern "sully." Middle English religious prose took the word up eagerly: to "sully" was to defile, to pollute, to render impure in a moral and spiritual sense, and the verb appears alongside "defile" and "pollute" in sermons and devotional treatises condemning sin as a stain on the soul. It is at this moment — in the moralizing vocabulary of medieval religion — that the figurative career of the word is decided: sully entered English already meaning "to tarnish morally," not merely to dirty cloth. The 16th century, the age of Shakespeare, fixed the modern spelling and the modern register: "sully" became the dignified verb of dishonour, the word for what scandal does to a good name. A genuine scholarly debate. The derivation just given — Latin sūs → suīllus → souil → souiller → English sully — is the traditional account, and the OED, Etymonline, and the standard handbooks present it as the primary etymology. But honesty requires recording the alternative. Old English possessed a native verb sylian "to sully, defile, pollute," attested in religious texts and continuing Proto-Germanic *suljaną "to make muddy," with cognates in Old Saxon and, notably, German sühlen "to wash, soak, wallow in mud," whose noun Suhle still means "wallow, mud puddle." This Germanic word is itself a member of the same far-flung *sū- family, connected through the pig's habit of wallowing in wet mud. Because the French loan and the native word both existed in Middle English and both meant "to make dirty," scholars disagree whether modern "sully" descends from the French verb, from the Old English verb, or from a blending of the two that reinforced one form. The phonology favours the French source (the English u and the absence of any trace of the Old English y are hard to reconcile with a purely native descent), but the question is genuinely open. What is not open to doubt is the deep root: whether by the Gallo-Romance road or the Germanic road, the word ultimately grunts back to *sū-, the pig, and to the wallow of mud that named the oldest metaphor of defilement. Semantic drift and the modern word. Once "sully" was established in the 16th century, its centre of gravity moved steadily from the literal to the moral. The literal sense — "Mud had sullied her white dress" — remained available and is still perfectly correct, but from the beginning the figurative sense dominated: to sully a reputation, a name, an honour, a memory, a legacy. The word's power lies in the transaction it implies: something is pure, bright, or white, and a single act of scandal, betrayal, or vice dirties it. This is why "sully" is the natural verb of the political scandal and the historical disgrace — a "sullied reputation" is one that was once untarnished and is now irrecoverably dimmed. In the 19th and 20th centuries the negated form "unsullied" became a fixed epithet of praise, meaning spotless, pristine, untouched by corruption or stain, used of reputations, characters, snowfields, and ideal states. In environmental prose the word slides back toward the literal, describing air, water, and landscapes defiled by human waste. Throughout, the register has remained formal and literary: sully is a word for tragic drama, political rhetoric, and moral essay, not for the kitchen. The card's sentence — "Will you so easily sully our ancient honor?" — catches the word at its full height, in the tragic register where honour is a treasure that can be stained beyond repair.
