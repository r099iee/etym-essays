affability is a noun meaning "the quality of being pleasantly easy to approach and to talk to; courteous friendliness; warmth and openness of manner." It denotes a social grace: the ease with which a person can be spoken to, the absence of standoffishness, and a readiness to receive others with kindliness. The word carries a distinctly aristocratic history — affability is the friendliness of a person in high position who chooses to be approachable rather than aloof — and its root is the Latin verb fari "to speak": an affable person is literally one "able to be spoken to."

In the supplied context. The passage describes Jia Zheng's inherited "affability with men of letters and readiness to help them in any sort of trouble or distress." Here the word names a specifically cultivated, hereditary social virtue: a high official who is warm and accessible to scholars, willing to converse with them and to help them. It is not mere cheerfulness; it is approachability with substance — the combination of being easy to talk to and actually ready to assist. The context places affability within a system of patronage and literary culture, where the powerful received the learned, conversed with them, and helped them in distress.

Range of senses and uses.

  • Approachability in manner — the core sense: easy to speak to, without pride or reserve; a quality of kings, officials, hosts, and public figures.
  • Courteous friendliness — pleasantness of address, genial warmth in conversation; near-synonymous with "amiability" but with a stronger note of deliberate graciousness toward inferiors.
  • Readiness to listen and help — as in the reading context, affability often implies not just pleasant talk but a genuine willingness to receive requests and assist.
  • Historical and aristocratic sense — from Renaissance courtesy literature, the graciousness proper to princes and great men, a quality that humanizes power: "the affability of kings."
  • Modern general sense — pleasant, friendly, easy conversational manner, without the aristocratic overtones: "known for his affability."
  • Related forms — affable (adjective), affably (adverb), and the rare negatives inaffability and unaffable.

Register and nuance. Affability is a formal, slightly literary word, more elevated than "friendliness" and less gushing than "warmth." It describes a stable, cultivated disposition rather than a passing mood, and it has a performative, public dimension: one is affable to guests, subordinates, petitioners, and strangers, not merely to intimates. Unlike "geniality" (festive good humour) or "sociability" (love of company), affability specifically means the quality of being easy to approach and speak with — the removal of barriers between a person of rank and the person they deign to address.

Usage notes. The word is almost always positive, though it can be used ironically to suggest a calculated, condescending charm ("his famous affability"). It is frequently paired with nouns of rank or office (king, prince, magistrate, host) and with qualities like courtesy, condescension, and readiness to oblige. The adjective affable is far more common in speech than the noun, which belongs to a slightly bookish register.
