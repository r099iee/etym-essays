• Ur-Nammu: Similar as a Mesopotamian lawgiver whose code survives on a tablet, and older than Hammurabi’s by roughly three centuries; differs in scale, in literary form (the laws are casuistic but the prologue is a hymn), and in the fact that its penalties are mostly pecuniary rather than bodily.
  • Lipit-Ishtar: Similar as an earlier Sumerian law collection from Isin, also framed by a royal prologue; differs in being fragmentary, far shorter, and much less influential in the history of legal scholarship.
  • Moses: Similar because the Mosaic law is the third member of the source’s triad and shares the talion formula; differs in being presented as revelation to a prophet rather than as a royal enactment, and in its monotheistic framework.
  • Solon: Similar as an ancient legislator remembered for a founding code; differs in being an Athenian reformer whose laws concerned debt, citizenship and constitution, with no talion and no divine relief.
  • Draco: Similar as a byword for harsh written law; differs because Draco’s Athenian code was famous for imposing death for almost every offence, whereas Hammurabi’s tariffs and compensations are markedly more graded.
  • Justinian: Similar as a ruler whose name is attached to a codification that shaped later law; differs in being a Christian emperor whose Corpus Juris Civilis was a compilation of existing jurists’ writings, not a new statute book.
  • Sargon of Akkad: Similar as a Mesopotamian king who unified the region and became a legend; differs in being remembered for conquest and empire, not for legislation.
  • Nebuchadnezzar II: Similar as a Babylonian king familiar from the Bible and from monumental building; differs in belonging to the Neo-Babylonian period a millennium later and in having no law code attached to his name.
  • Amraphel: Similar because nineteenth-century scholars proposed identifying this king of Shinar in Genesis 14 with Hammurabi; differs in being a biblical name of disputed reference, and the identification is now generally rejected.
  • Shamash: Similar as the figure who guarantees the code — he is carved on the stele handing the king his insignia; differs in being a god, not a legislator, and in representing the divine source of justice rather than its human administration.
