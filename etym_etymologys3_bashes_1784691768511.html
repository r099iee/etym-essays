Bash is a word of the expressive undergrowth of English — the stock of short, blunt impact-words (bang, bump, thump, whack, crash, mash, smash, dash) that English has accumulated by imitating the sounds and sensations of violence rather than by inherited descent. The verb is first recorded in the seventeenth century (1640s) with the sense “to strike violently,” and its origin is genuinely obscure. Scholars point to a possible Scandinavian source in Old Norse *basca, reflected in Swedish basa and Danish baske “to beat, thump,” while others see a native blend of bang and mash, or a word shaped by its whole sound-family — the same mouth-filling, splatting consonants repeated across the impact-verbs of the language. Both channels are plausible, and both may have worked together: the word’s body is native onomatopoeia, while its ancestry may be partly Viking, carried into English by the Danish settlers whose speech so enriched the northern dialects. For its first two centuries bash lived as a verb — to bash a head, to bash a door in — the plainest possible word for putting force behind a hard object. It belongs to the class of “echoic” words described by the philologist Otto Jespersen: they enter the language not through a chain of inherited sound changes but through a direct imitation of the noise and feel of an event. Where a word like tear descends in an unbroken line from Proto-Indo-European, bash appears suddenly, at a datable moment in the seventeenth century, already fully formed. Its explosive initial b-, its short open vowel, and its hissing final cluster -sh all conspire to enact the thing they name. The noun came late and usefully: from 1818 a bash is a heavy blow, and the plural bashes soon extends naturally to the marks the blows leave — the bruises and swellings that rise on flesh after a beating. This is metonymy of the most ordinary kind: the blow and its bruise share a single word, exactly as a cut names both the blade’s act and the wound it leaves. In this double sense the word entered the popular literary language of the nineteenth century, appearing in adventure fiction and comic writing as the robust, schoolboyish word for physical rough-and-tumble. The nineteenth century also saw the word go sociable in Britain, where “have a bash” (recorded by the mid-twentieth century) turned the sense of “take a blow at” into “make an attempt at”: the word had gentled from violence to enterprise. Meanwhile American slang performed a stranger reversal, using bash for a lively party (1930s) — the same bundle of energy and force, redirected from fighting to festivity. And in the late twentieth century the verb acquired a new figurative career in journalism: to bash someone is to attack them in public speech, a kind of verbal cudgelling that the word’s earliest sense would have recognised at once. The compound -bashing (as in gay-bashing, union-bashing) has become a fixed formative element of modern political English. Through all these changes the physical core has remained: a bash is what you get when something big hits you. The word never entered the polite classical vocabulary of English; it stayed in the register of blows and scuffles, and that is exactly why Tolkien chose it. In The Hobbit (1937), the trolls nursing “burns and bashes to remember” after Thorin’s fight are given a word of the nursery and the schoolyard, which keeps their violence comic and their resentments childish. The word thus performs a double service in the passage: it names the physical injuries in a deliberately unsolemn way, and it prepares the reader for the petty, squabbling, easily-duped trolls who will be turned to stone by the rising sun. In sum, bash and bashes illustrate three great tendencies of English word-formation: onomatopoeia (the word is a sound-picture of its own meaning), metonymy (the blow and its bruise share a name), and semantic drift under social pressure (violence gentling into attempt and festivity, then hardening again into verbal attack). Its debated Scandinavian origin and its strong native feel show how porous the boundary is between borrowed and home-grown vocabulary in English.
