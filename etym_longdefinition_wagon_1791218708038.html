Wagon is a noun for a heavy four-wheeled vehicle for carrying goods or people, drawn originally by horses or oxen and later applied to railway trucks and motor cars. The prototypical wagon is an open, high-sided farm or freight cart — hay wagon, covered wagon, chuck wagon — but the word has travelled from farmyard to railway to motoring, so that a station wagon and a goods wagon share the name though not the shape. In the source sentence it is used figuratively and humorously: in southern and eastern Asia man is wagon and donkey and all — i.e. he himself does the carrying, hauling and pulling.

Core physical senses cluster around load-bearing wheels:

  • Horse-drawn freight sense: a strong four-wheeled cart with high sides or hoops and canvas: farm wagon, hay wagon, Conestoga wagon, prairie wagon, chuck wagon, ammunition wagon. Example: they loaded the harvest onto the wagon. This is the historical and still first-image sense.
  • Child's and hand sense: a small four-wheeled pull-toy or handcart: red wagon, toy wagon, garden wagon. To pull a child in a wagon belongs here; lightness does not cancel wagonhood if four wheels and an open bed remain.
  • Railway sense (chiefly British): an open goods truck: coal wagon, goods wagon, tank wagon, wagonload. British railways say wagon where Americans say freight car or gondola. Spelling waggon survives here as archaism.
  • Motor-vehicle sense: station wagon — estate car — a car with a long roof and rear hatch for loads; paddy wagon — police van; chuck wagon as food truck. American English in particular keeps wagon alive through the station wagon even as SUVs displace it.
  • Figurative burden sense: as in the source, man is wagon — man serves as his own carrying vehicle. Related joking extensions: to be the family wagon, office wagon — the one who hauls everything.
  • Idiomatic senses: on the wagon — abstaining from alcohol; off the wagon / fall off the wagon — relapsed into drinking; on the bandwagon — joining a popular cause; hitch one's wagon to — attach one's fortunes to; circle the wagons — adopt a defensive posture; fix someone's wagon — get revenge. Each preserves motion-with-load imagery: staying aboard, climbing aboard, defending the train.

Distinctions of vehicle vocabulary: a wagon has four wheels and carries goods; a cart prototypically has two wheels; a carriage carries people elegantly; a car is motorised and enclosed; a truck is a large motor lorry — though American railways call goods wagons freight cars. Size and dignity descend from carriage through wagon to cart, which is why calling a man a wagon in the source is both comic and demeaning: he has been reduced to farm equipment.

Polysemy bridge: all senses share the schema of a wheeled bed that bears a load along a route. Horse cart → rail truck is transfer by function — carrying freight on a fixed way. Rail truck → station wagon is transfer by marketing — the family car that carries like a wagon. Vehicle → teetotal wagon is metonymy via the water wagon that accompanied temperance parades. The humorous man-is-wagon in the source is metaphor: a human doing a vehicle's work, collapsing agent and instrument.
