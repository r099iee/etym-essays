In the sentence at hand, RESOLVED is the past tense of the verb resolve: the besiegers 'resolved to strike while they debated' — they made a firm, deliberate decision to attack. The word's deep structure is revealing: Latin resolvere means 'to loosen again, untie, unfasten' (re- 'back/again' + solvere 'to loosen'), and the modern sense 'to decide' is the endpoint of a long chain of metaphorical loosening — from untying a knot, to dissolving a problem, to settling a question, to fixing one's own will on a course of action.

1. The verb senses

  • To decide firmly: 'resolve to do something' = to make a firm decision, often after deliberation — 'she resolved to leave at dawn'. This is the sense in the context.
  • To settle (a problem, doubt, question): 'resolve a dispute', 'resolve the issue', 'resolve a mystery' — to find a solution or bring to a conclusion; the question is 'resolved' = answered.
  • To break up into parts / analyse: 'resolve into' = to separate into constituent elements — 'light resolved into a spectrum', 'the committee resolved into subcommittees'. This is the oldest English sense (15th c.), preserving the Latin 'loosen into parts'.
  • Technical / scientific: optics — the 'resolution' of a lens (the power to separate fine detail); computing — 'screen resolution' (pixel density) and 'resolving' a domain name (turning it into an IP address); music — a dissonant chord 'resolves' into a consonant one; mathematics — 'resolve' a vector into components.
  • Parliamentary / legal: 'Resolved, that...' as the formula of formal decision — the motion sense of 'resolution'.

2. The adjective and past-participial uses

  • Resolved (adj.) = determined, firm in purpose: 'he was resolved to win'; 'a resolved spirit' — carrying the older participial sense 'freed from doubt, settled'.
  • Distinguish resolute (the adjective, from Latin resolutus) — 'determined, firm' — and the noun resolution — 'a firm decision; a formal motion; the act of resolving'.

3. Semantic bridges

  • Latin resolvere 'to loosen, untie' → the mental metaphor: a doubt or problem is a knot; to 'resolve' it is to untie it, to dissolve the tangle of possibilities. From 'untie the problem' it is a short step to 'settle the question' and then to 'settle one's own mind' — i.e., to decide. The decision sense is recorded in English from the 16th century and fully dominant by the 17th.
  • 'Resolved' (loosened, dissolved) → 'reduced to elements': the alchemical and scientific tradition of analysing compounds into their parts keeps the literal sense alive in technical language.
  • 'Resolved' as a parliamentary formula: a body 'resolves' (decides formally) that something be done — the collective version of personal resolve.

4. Register and connotation

  • Personal: 'resolved' is firmer and more deliberate than 'decided'; it implies overcoming hesitation, often after weighing options — precisely the tone of the context, where the besiegers strike 'while they debated'.
  • Modern usage: extremely common in all registers — news ('the government resolved to act'), business ('we resolved the issue'), science, computing, and law; the past participle 'resolved' doubles as a status label in issue-tracking ('bug resolved').
