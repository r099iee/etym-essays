Siberia is the vast northern Asian region stretching from the Ural Mountains eastward to the Pacific Ocean and from the Arctic Ocean southward to the Mongolian and Chinese borders, comprising some three-quarters of Russia's territory. The name denotes both a physical immensity of taiga, tundra, steppe, and mountain, and a cultural idea: remoteness, extreme cold, exile, endurance, and untapped resources. In the source sentence snowflakes falling thick in Siberia figures evaluation as abundant, natural, and overwhelming where true description is scarce, invoking Siberia as the proverbial land of endless winter profusion.

As a proper geographical name Siberia functions like a common noun for the remote and the severe. We say it is Siberia in here of an over-air-conditioned room, or a cultural Siberia of intellectual exile. Historically it names the Russian conquest and settlement from Yermak's sixteenth-century expedition through Soviet industrialisation and the Gulag system. Economically it means oil, gas, diamonds, timber, and fresh water; ecologically it means permafrost, mammoth remains, Lake Baikal, and the lungs of boreal forest; humanly it means Tatars, Yakuts, Buryats, Evenks, and generations of Russian settlers and exiles.

  • Core geographical sense: northern Asia within Russia from Urals to Pacific; differs from Russia as a whole because it excludes European Russia and the Caucasus.
  • Climatic-symbolic sense: archetype of cold, snow, and endurance; differs because it is metaphorical rather than cartographic.
  • Historical-political sense: zone of conquest, colonisation, exile, and camps; differs because it foregrounds state power and suffering.
  • Economic-resource sense: storehouse of minerals, hydrocarbons, and timber; differs because it views land as inventory and prospect.
  • Ecological-wilderness sense: vast taiga-tundra biome and refuge of megafauna; differs because it values intact nature over extraction.
  • Figurative-exile sense: any isolated, punishing posting or cultural margin; differs as a transferred use for social coldness and distance.

Usage notes: the adjective is Siberian, the inhabitant a Siberian, the Russian form Sibir'. English stress falls on the second syllable /saɪˈbɪəriə/. The region has no single administrative boundary; Siberia is historical-geographical rather than federal, overlapping the Siberian and Far Eastern Federal Districts. Writers exploit its scale for sublime and for bathos: falling thick as snowflakes in Siberia turns abundance into weather, impersonal and irresistible, precisely the force the author grants to evaluative chatter over patient description.

Literary and popular resonance is immense, from Chekhov's Sakhalin journey and Dostoevsky's Siberian exile to contemporary climate writing where thawing permafrost makes Siberia a global warning. To call description's absence Siberian is paradoxical: the emptiest mapped space is fullest of talk. The keeper status of this name reflects that double weight: everyone knows Siberia without having been there, and the word carries cold on its tongue.
