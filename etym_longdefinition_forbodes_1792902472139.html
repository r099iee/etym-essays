forbodes, the archaic third-person singular of forbode — the older spelling of modern forebode — means at its core to sense beforehand that ill is coming, to carry a heavy presentiment that some misfortune, loss, or fateful turn lies ahead even when no evidence yet proves it.

In the source sentence Gildor the Elf tells Frodo that his heart forbodes a dark enlightenment awaiting him, that ere all is ended Frodo will know more of fell things than the Elves themselves. The verb here is not casual prediction but sorrowful intuition, spoken by an elder being whose long sight makes dread a form of knowledge, and it casts the whole parting in shadow, so that adventure and doom arrive together in one breath.

Core meaning in detail: To forbode is to bode beforehand, and to bode is to be an omen of something. A black sky forbodes storm, a silence forbodes bad news, a heart forbodes grief. The subject may be the sign itself, as in the clouds forboding rain, or the mind perceiving the sign, as in my heart forboding disaster. In both patterns the grammar reaches forward in time, joining present perception to future sorrow, and the tone stays grave, literary, and slightly archaic, which is why Tolkien chooses it for an Elf rather than a plain word like expects or fears.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To have a gloomy presentiment, to feel evil coming (primary modern sense): she forbodes no good from the delay. The bridge is inward, from outer omen to inner feeling, so the heart itself becomes the prophet, registering dread before reason can name its cause.
  • To portend or presage as an omen (older transitive sense): dark birds forboded defeat. The bridge is the original sense of bode as an announcement, carried forward in time by the prefix, so that ravens, dreams, and clouds are read as letters from the future.
  • To predict or foretell calamity aloud (literary): he forboded the fall of the city. The bridge runs from silent feeling to spoken prophecy, and the word takes on the robes of Cassandra and Jeremiah, solemn and unwelcome, truth-telling without comfort.
  • To have a general uneasy intuition, not necessarily of disaster (weakened modern use): I forbode we shall be late. The bridge is bleaching by frequency, as dread softens into mere expectation, though careful writers still reserve the word for genuinely dark outcomes.
  • Archaic spelling variant of forebodes (orthographic sense): Tolkien writes forbodes where modern editions often print forebodes. The bridge is purely historical, the prefix shifting between for- and fore- in Middle English before standardisation fixed fore- as before in time.

Register and connotation: forbodes is high, mournful, and old-world, at home in ballads, scripture, Shakespeare, and fantasy, never in memos or chat. It dignifies fear by giving it foresight, turning anxiety into a kind of tragic wisdom, and it always darkens the air around it, so that even a small sentence wearing it sounds like the opening of an elegy.
