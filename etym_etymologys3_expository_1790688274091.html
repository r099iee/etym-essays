Expository belongs to one of the most durable Latin rhetorical families in English — exponere, expositio, expository, exposition, exponent — all built on the spatial metaphor of setting something out so it can be seen. Its story runs from a Roman verb for physical display through medieval biblical commentary to the modern schoolroom label for factual writing.

Latin exponere transparently joins ex- “out, forth” (from PIE *eghs) with ponere “to put, place.” In Classical Latin (Cicero, Caesar, Quintilian) it already spans the physical and the intellectual: soldiers exponunt copias (set out troops), merchants exponunt merces (display goods), and orators exponunt causam (set forth a case, explain). The past participle expositus yields the noun expositio “a setting-forth, explanation,” a standard term in Quintilian’s Institutio Oratoria for the plain statement of facts before argument. Late Latin derives the adjective expositorius “pertaining to exposition,” rare but attested in grammatical and patristic writers for commentary that clarifies scripture.

Key stages:

  • Classical Latin exponere / expositio (1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE): rhetorical term of art. Quintilian distinguishes narratio/expositio (clear statement) from argumentatio (proof) and peroratio (emotional close). Connotation is neutral-professional: the good orator first makes things plain.
  • Patristic and medieval expositio (4th–14th c.): the word migrates to biblical hermeneutics. An expositio evangelii is a line-by-line commentary (Bede, Aquinas). Expositor means commentator. The adjective expository is not yet English, but the concept dominates schooling: to expound is to unfold authorized meaning. Perception is reverent; exposition serves doctrine.
  • Renaissance borrowing into English (16th–17th c.): exposition enters c. 1400 via Old French; expository follows in the 1620s–30s (OED first attests expository c. 1628 in theological writing: “expository sermons”). Early use is almost entirely religious: an expository sermon expounds a text verse by verse, as opposed to topical preaching. Connotation is learned, Puritan, serious.
  • Rhetorical systematization (18th–19th c.): Scottish and English rhetoricians (Blair, Campbell, Whately) codify four modes of discourse — description, narration, exposition, argumentation/persuasion — that dominate Anglophone composition teaching to this day. Expository discourse, exactly the phrase in the source sentence, becomes textbook jargon for writing whose aim is to inform or explain rather than to move or delight. The older religious sense persists but is overtaken by the classroom sense.
  • Modern composition and linguistics (20th–21st c.): “expository writing,” “expository essay,” “expository text structures” (compare/contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution) are staples of US Common Core and UK curricula. Stylists distinguish expository from expressive, poetic, and persuasive functions (Jakobson, Kinneavy). In music, exposition independently names the opening section of sonata form, reinforcing the idea of laying out material. The adjective now feels institutional — sometimes dry or reductive (“merely expository”) — which is why the source sentence treats it as only one motive (purpose/information) among play and competition.

Phonetic evolution is straightforward borrowing, not sound-law descent: Latin ex- /eks/ → English /eks/; -positorius anglicized to -pository/- pository with stress shift to /ɪkˈspɒzɪtəri/ (RP) under the English Latinate stress rule (antepenultimate). Spelling retains x and y as learned markers. The doublet expound (via Old French espondre) shows the popular route with intrusive -d, while expository preserves the learned Latin stem.

Literary and pedagogical attestations trace the narrowing: Donne’s “expository” sermons; Coleridge on Shakespeare’s “expository soliloquies”; Bain’s English Composition and Rhetoric (1866) entrenching exposition as a mode; and modern textbooks contrasting “expository vs. narrative vs. persuasive” prompts. The source sentence — “studying the forms of expository discourse” — invokes that whole tradition: purpose enters criticism through the schoolroom analysis of how information is set forth.
