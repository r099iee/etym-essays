all-but — the biography of a boundary. The compound is made of two words with very different biographies, and its meaning is the sum of both. The first element, 'all', descends from Proto-Germanic *allaz, 'all, entire, whole'. The deeper origin of *allaz is uncertain: the most widely accepted reconstruction connects it to the Indo-European root *h₂el-, 'beyond; other', the family that also produced Latin alius, 'other', and English else. If that derivation is right, the ancient concept behind 'all' is a totality that reaches beyond itself — everything 'else' included — which in Germanic hardened into the simple quantifier. Old English eall carried this meaning into Middle English al and so into modern all. The second element is more transparent. Old English būtan meant 'on the outside, except, unless', and was built from the prefix be-, 'by, at', and ūtan, 'outside', from the Indo-European root *ud-, 'up, out' — the root of Sanskrit ud, German aus and English out. A thing 'būtan' the group was literally outside it, excluded from it. When the two elements were first placed together, 'all but X' meant 'all except X' — a piece of spatial arithmetic. The sentence 'all but the eldest son perished' named the one person excluded from the total. From this arithmetic of exclusion the language distilled its most elegant word for 'almost'. The reanalysis happened in late Middle English: instead of pointing to what is excluded, the phrase began to describe how far a condition had been reached. 'All but' came to mean 'everything up to the final step', 'everything short of the end'. What had been a sentence about a missing member became a degree word for near-completeness. This is semantic bleaching at its purest — the 'except' kept its excluding force, but the phrase as a whole stopped counting outcasts and started measuring thresholds. The further step, the hyphen, belongs to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. As English increasingly used the phrase attributively before a participle — 'all-but forgotten', 'an all-but-impossible task' — writers began to bind the three words into a single prenominal adjective, and the hyphen registered the fusion. Modern usage shows all three stages living side by side: the quantifier ('all but two'), the adverbial near-synonym of 'almost' ('all but finished'), and the hyphenated attributive compound ('all-but forgotten'). The Gormenghast sentence exploits the middle sense with full awareness of the paradox at its core: totality minus one step. The outer folk stand at precisely that asymptote of existence — everything except finally erased — and the phrase's ancient pairing of totality with exclusion gives their half-forgetting its precise, ceremonial dread. It is no accident that Peake, a painter as well as a novelist, reached for a phrase that draws a line rather than stating a fact: all-but is not a fact about them, it is the geometry of their disappearance.
recrudescent — a wound that will not stay closed. The word is a direct import from Latin, but its story reaches much deeper into the Indo-European past. At the root is the reconstructed Indo-European *kreuə-, 'raw flesh, gore, blood', which left its mark across the whole family. In Sanskrit it produced kravíṣ, 'raw flesh, carrion', and kravyá, flesh for devouring; in Greek it produced kréas, 'flesh, meat', from which nineteenth-century chemistry coined créatine, the substance extracted from raw muscle; in Germanic it gave *hrawaz, the ancestor of Old English hrēaw and modern raw. In Italic the same root produced Latin crūdus, 'raw, bloody, undressed' — the source of English crude, material in its unrefined state — and, a stage further, crūdēlis, 'cruel', one whose harshness is imagined as raw flesh-spirit. The adjective crūdus was the foundation on which the medical vocabulary built. From crūdus, Latin formed the inchoative verb crūdēscere, 'to grow raw, to become sore', using the -sc- infix that marks the beginning of a state. To this the prefix re-, 'again', was joined, producing recrūdēscere: 'to become raw again'. In the Roman medical writers of the first century CE this verb had a precise, technical use — it described wounds and diseases that broke open afresh after appearing to heal. A fever that returned with renewed violence 'recrudescuit'; an ulcer that tore open again 'recrudescuit'. The present participle recrūdēscēns, genitive recrūdēscentis, 'becoming raw again', was the seed of the English adjective. It is important to state clearly that English took the participle directly, in the eighteenth century, into scientific and medical writing: the word has no French or Germanic intermediate, which is why it kept the exact Latin participial form. From that clinical start the word spread sideways. By the nineteenth century it was fixed in pathology for fevers, ulcers and inflammations that recur with renewed severity after apparent healing. Then the metaphor escaped the hospital. In the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries, writers transferred recrudescence to collective evils: the recrudescence of war, of plague, of revolt, of superstition, of sectarian hatred — each imagined as a wound in the body politic tearing open again. The transfer worked because the image was already physical: whatever recrudesces does not merely return; it reopens a scar. In the twentieth century the metaphor reached inward, to the mind: memories, dreams and suppressed desires recrudesce, breaking surface with the raw force of the original injury. Peake's 'the unreality of a recrudescent dream' draws on this whole history in two words. The memory of the outer folk is not a memory that returns; it is one that reopens. The word's rawness is literal at its root — crūdus is the raw, bloody, undressed flesh — and every extension since has preserved that shock. Where 'all-but forgotten' names the asymptote of erasure, 'recrudescent' names the eruption that tears the asymptote open. The two words together are the whole physics of the passage: a disappearance held just short of completion, and a return that bleeds.
Together in the sentence — a dialectic of memory.
  • Contrast of direction: all-but moves toward oblivion and stops; recrudescent moves out of oblivion and erupts.
  • Contrast of register: all-but is formal and paradoxical but everyday English stock; recrudescent is a rare clinical-learned Latinism that startles by its technical precision in a gothic context.
  • Contrast of root image: all-but is spatial arithmetic (a set minus one member); recrudescent is bodily injury (a scar tearing open).
  • Shared cultural logic: both words belong to the nineteenth-century vocabulary of decay and recurrence that the Gothic and the medical imagination shared — Peake places the outer folk exactly at their intersection.
