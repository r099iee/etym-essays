• Drawing-room: Similar because both are rooms for receiving guests; differs because the drawing-room is the later genteel “withdrawing” room used after dinner, more formal than the parlour.
  • Living-room: Similar because both are everyday sitting rooms of a house; differs because the living-room is the modern, less formal equivalent, without the parlour’s guest-facing Victorian connotations.
  • Sitting-room: Similar because both are rooms for sitting and conversation; differs because the sitting-room is the plainer, everyday name with none of the parlour’s period flavour.
  • Study: Similar because both are private rooms of a house; differs because the study is for solitary work and books, not for receiving guests in conversation.
  • Salon: Similar because both are rooms for social reception; differs because the salon (French) implies a fashionable, elegant gathering or a high-end service shop (hair salon), with Continental cachet the homely parlour lacks.
  • Lounge: Similar because both are relaxed domestic or hotel sitting spaces; differs because the lounge is informal, modern, and often public (hotel lounge), without the parlour’s historical guest-room dignity.
  • Hall: Similar because both are domestic reception spaces; differs because the hall is larger, more public, or transitional (the entrance passage), while the parlour is a dedicated furnished sitting room.
  • Front room: Similar because both name the best guest-facing room of a house; differs because “front room” is the plain colloquial British term without the parlour’s Latinate, historically rooted dignity.
