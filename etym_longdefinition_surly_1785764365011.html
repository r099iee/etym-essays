SURLY: Bad-tempered, churlish, and unfriendly in manner or appearance; gruffly hostile, sullenly ill-humoured, and abrupt in one’s dealings with others. Surly describes a person, demeanour, mood, or even weather characterized by gruff hostility, sullen ill-humour, and abrupt unfriendliness. Beyond mere moodiness, surliness conveys an underlying threat of hostility or contemptuous resentment — often expressed through brief, clipped responses, dark glares, or an unapproachable presence. In the card’s sentence, the guard at the entrance “remained surly and refused to return our greetings” despite the host’s warm welcome: the word paints active, almost bristling discourtesy, as if the guard resents the intrusion and will not grant even the minimal social oil of a returned greeting. The tone of the adjective itself is critical and slightly archaic-elevated: writers use it to judge manners, not merely to report emotion. Senses and uses (full polysemy).
  • Sense 1 — of a person’s manner or temper (the primary sense): rude, ill-tempered, and unwilling to be pleasant or helpful; the guard of the card’s sentence is the classic instance — surly servants, clerks, doorkeepers, and officials populate novels and newsrooms as the type of obstructive ill-nature.
  • Sense 2 — of speech and response: a surly reply, surly tone, surly nod — the clipped, grudging, or contemptuous quality of what is said or done, an edge of rudeness in the manner of response rather than its content.
  • Sense 3 — of appearance and demeanour: a surly look, surly countenance — an unapproachable, scowling, or threatening outward bearing that warns others away before a word is spoken.
  • Sense 4 — of weather and natural forces (transferred sense): a surly sky, surly sea, surly weather — heavy, forbidding, threatening, and unwelcoming; the transferred sense retains the same dark, storm-threatening quality as the human one, so that a surly sky is a sky that looks hostile to those beneath it.
  • Sense 5 — historical: “lordly, arrogant” (the etymological ancestor): the older English form sirly meant “lordly, majestic, befitting a sir”; through social pejoration “lordly” came to be read as “harrogant, haughty, overbearing”, and “harrogant” finally narrowed to “bad-tempered, churlish”. The word no longer means “noble” in any positive sense; any residual lordliness is now ironic.
Near-synonyms and semantic boundaries.
  • Churlish: similar in rude, ungracious behaviour; differs because churlish stresses boorish lack of breeding and the refusal of courtesy, with a class-coded history (the churl as peasant), while surly stresses dark, resentful ill-temper and an almost threatening unapproachability rather than vulgar manners.
  • Sullen: similar in gloomy unfriendliness; differs because sullen is quieter and more withdrawn — gloomy silence and sulking — while surly often carries an active edge of hostility or rude speech, an outward cut rather than inward brooding.
  • Gruff: similar in rough, abrupt address; differs because gruff may describe a rough exterior that is benign underneath (the gruff but kindly uncle), while surly implies genuine ill will or bad temper, not mere vocal roughness.
  • Morose: similar as a negatively valenced mood word; differs because morose emphasizes long-lasting gloomy bitterness and introspection, while surly is more situationally interactive — it describes how one treats others in the moment.
  • Irascible: similar in relating to bad temper; differs because irascible means easily angered, quick to flare, while surly describes the already-present, brooding unfriendliness of manner, whether or not an explosion follows.
Collocations and register. The word collocates with waiter, clerk, guard, reply, silence, mood, look, nod, sky, and weather. Register is standard literary and journalistic English, somewhat more formal or bookish than grumpy or rude; the adjective is a compact instrument of character judgement, letting a single word condemn a person’s entire manner, and it has been a favourite of novelists and moralists ever since the early modern period.
