• Pony: Similar because both name small horses; differs because pony is affectionate and neutral, a child's mount or a breed term, while nag is contemptuous, stressing age, worthlessness, or wear.
  • Jade: Similar because a jade is exactly a worn-out, ill-tempered horse, the literary twin of the sorry nag; differs because jade is now rare and high-literary, while nag is common and colloquial.
  • Hack: Similar because both name tired hire-horses and, by extension, tired drudges; differs because hack stresses hire and routine — the hack writer, the hack cab — while nag stresses the animal's sorry state.
  • Steed: Similar because both are horse-words of old story; differs because steed ennobles — the knight's charger — while nag degrades, and the source sentence's sneer depends on refusing the grander word.
  • Cob: Similar because both name sturdy smallish horses; differs because a cob is valued — strong, short-legged, sensible — while a nag is deplored.
  • Pester: Similar because both verbs mean to harass with repeated demands; differs because pester is general and often physical or importunate, while nag is specifically the drip of repeated verbal complaint.
  • Scold: Similar because both name fault-finding speech; differs because scold is the single angry rebuke, while nag is the campaign — small complaints repeated until surrender.
  • Henpeck: Similar because both describe domestic persistent fault-finding; differs because henpeck names the power pattern of a dominated spouse, while nag names the speech tactic itself, usable by anyone upon anyone.
  • Gnaw (as image-kin): Similar because nagging pain and doubt gnaw exactly as complaints nag; differs because gnaw is the physical biting image itself, while nag is its human-voiced equivalent.
