allegorizing (verb participle / gerund & verbal noun): The act or process of interpreting, explaining, representing, or translating literal events, characters, texts, or historical phenomena into symbolic, allegorical narratives that convey underlying abstract, moral, spiritual, or ideological meanings.

I. Core Lexical & Hermeneutic Senses

  • Interpretive Exegesis (Allegorical Reading): The practice of analyzing a text, myth, or historical narrative to uncover deeper, figurative levels of meaning (such as moral, theological, or philosophical truths) that are intentionally or unintentionally encoded beneath the literal surface wording.
  • Literary & Artistic Composition: The deliberate creative technique of structuring a story, poem, painting, or dramatic work so that its concrete characters, settings, and events systematically correspond to abstract concepts, ethical virtues/vices, or socio-political realities (e.g., in Spenser's The Faerie Queene or Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress).
  • Conceptual Rationalization & Systematic Myth-Reading: The intellectual habit of translating raw, random, or unsettling historical experiences and ancient mythology into tidy, coherent philosophical or ideological frameworks to make them intelligible and pedagogically useful.

II. Modern Polysemy, Literary Theory & Critical Jargon

  • Theological Hermeneutics (The Fourfold Sense of Scripture): In patristic and medieval biblical exegesis (Origen, Augustine, Aquinas), allegorizing constitutes the primary method of discovering the sensus allegoricus (spiritual and Christological significance) alongside the literal (historical), tropological (moral), and anagogical (eschatological/heavenly) dimensions of holy scripture.
  • Deconstructive & Postmodern Literary Criticism: In 20th-century critical theory (notably Walter Benjamin and Paul de Man), allegorizing was rehabilitated as the fundamental condition of human language. De Man argued that all critical reading is inherently an allegorizing process because words can never achieve direct, unmediated identity with physical reality, forever producing a narrative of their own structural unreliability.
  • Ideological Over-Interpretation (Pejorative Sense): In contemporary cultural analysis and literary reviewing, allegorizing is frequently used pejoratively to describe the heavy-handed, reductionist imposition of rigid modern ideological, political, or psychological agendas onto nuanced, ambiguous artistic works, flattening multidimensional characters into mere mouthpieces for dogma.
  • Psychological Projection & Myth-Making: In depth psychology (Jungian analysis), the spontaneous psychic process by which the unconscious mind allegorizes internal emotional conflicts and archetypal drives into dreams, fantasies, and personal mythologies.

III. Historical Semantic Bridges & Epistemological Shifts

  • From Athenian Oratory to Philosophical Defense: The Greek etymon allēgorein combined allos ("other") and agoreuein ("to speak in the public assembly"). It originally meant "to speak in public in another way"—i.e., to use figurative veiled speech. Hellenistic philosophers (such as the Stoics) adopted allegorizing to defend Homer's and Hesiod's scandalous stories of bickering, adulterous Olympian gods against rationalist critics by claiming the myths were actually sophisticated physical allegories of cosmic elements (Zeus as fire, Hera as air).
  • The Romantic Rejection vs. Modern Revival: During the Romantic era (Goethe, Coleridge), allegorizing fell into disrepute, condemned as a dry, mechanical, intellectual exercise that artificially attached concepts to arbitrary labels, contrasted with the organic, transcendent revelation of the living "symbol." In the late 20th century, structuralist and post-structuralist theorists reversed this verdict, showing that narrative allegorization is reason's essential tool for organizing historical contingency into intelligible semantic patterns.
