- [13th–14th c.] - [Middle English Germanic Borrowing] Entered Middle English as crulle / curle from Low German/Dutch sources, originally describing naturally curly, wavy, or spiraled human hair.
- [15th–16th c.] - [Transitive & Material Generalization] Shifted from an exclusive trichological descriptor to an active transitive verb, describing the manual rolling of paper, curling of leaves in drought, and bending of physical materials.
- [17th–18th c.] - [Postural & Physiological Extension] Developed the reflexive and intransitive sense of bodily coiling (to curl up in bed or around a hearth for warmth), as well as affective facial grimaces (curling the lip in disdain).
- [19th–21st c.] - [Sporting Specialization & Modern Idioms] Formalized in the Scottish winter sport of curling on ice, while idioms like curled up with a book became ubiquitous cultural tropes for cozy domestic relaxation.
