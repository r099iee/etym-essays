purloined (adj./v. past participle) — taken secretly and without permission, especially by artful or stealthy means; the past participle of to purloin, "to steal, especially in a secret, quiet, or skilful way."

1. Core sense — secret or artful theft. At its heart purloined names the act of stealing, but a particular kind of stealing: the removal of property quietly, cleverly, and so deftly that the owner may not notice the loss until much later. Unlike open robbery or violent seizure, purloining implies subtlety, concealment, and often a degree of cunning or even elegance in the thief. It is the perfect word for a gentleman thief, a pickpocket, a spy, or an embezzler who operates with finesse rather than force. The word is recorded in English from the fourteenth century and has always carried a faintly literary, slightly elevated, even mock-heroic flavour.

2. Secondary sense — "to remove or divert for one's own use." Beyond literal theft, purloined extends to the surreptitious appropriation or diversion of anything that is not one's own: a purloined hour is an hour stolen from duty; a purloined glance is a furtive look; a journalist's purloined documents are files removed without authorization, whether from a desk, a computer, or an archive. In this broader use the element of outright crime recedes and the element of secrecy or unauthorized taking predominates.

3. Figurative and ironic senses. Because the word is bookish, it is a favourite of ironic or comic register: a sleepy student "purloins" a nap, a greedy guest "purloins" the last biscuit. The mock-dignity of the term — dressing a trivial theft in a stately Latinate word — is exactly the effect Tolkien exploits when the narrator imagines a first-class burglar who would have "purloined the beer." The word also appears in legal and historical writing for the taking of funds or goods with fraudulent intent, where it overlaps with embezzlement and misappropriation.

4. Historical bridge — the etymology that explains everything. The modern meaning is a narrowing of an older, more literal sense. The verb entered English as purloinen (fourteenth century) from Anglo-Norman purluigner, itself from Old French porloignier / purloignier, "to put far off, to remove to a distance, to postpone." That French verb is composed of the prefix por-/pur- (from Latin pro-, "forth, forward, away") joined to a stem built on loign "far" (from Latin longē, "far off," the adverb of longus, "long"). To purloin is thus, etymologically, "to put far away" — to spirit something off to a distance. In English and in Anglo-Norman usage the neutral sense "to remove, to put aside" narrowed to "to remove (someone else's property)" and finally to "to steal secretly," so that the word's history is itself a miniature of how a plain act of transportation became a euphemism for theft.

5. Register, style, and usage today.

  • Formal and literary: the word is at home in detective fiction, journalism, and academic prose, but rarely in casual speech.
  • Detective tradition: Edgar Allan Poe's story "The Purloined Letter" (1844) fixed purloined in the popular imagination as the connoisseur's term for artful stealing.
  • Archaic-flavoured humour: the verb retains an antique dignity, so modern speakers use it to raise a trivial theft to a mock-serious height (as Tolkien does).
  • Distribution of forms: the past participle purloined is the commonest form in writing; the base verb and gerund purloining also occur, and the noun purloiner ("one who purloins") is recorded from the sixteenth century.
  • Collocation: typical objects include purloined documents, purloined letters, purloined goods, purloined funds.

6. Distinctions from near-synonyms. The word is best separated from its neighbours by the ideas of secrecy and craft: where steal is the plain, neutral, universal word; pilfer implies repeated petty theft; filch is sly and quick; pinch and nick are British slang for the same act; appropriate is bureaucratic and euphemistic; and purloin is the literary, faintly theatrical member of the set, suggesting a theft carried out with quiet cleverness and a degree of elegance.
