A mace is a heavy club, typically with a metal head, carried as a weapon and transformed into a ceremonial staff symbolizing authority; in modern civic life the mace is the ornamented emblem borne before magistrates, parliaments, and universities to signify lawful power in session.

The weapon sense is oldest in English. A medieval mace comprised a shaft of wood or metal, a flanged or knobbed head designed to crush armor where blades glanced off, and a pommel and grip for horsemen and foot soldiers. From the 12th to 16th centuries it was the sidearm of knights, sergeants, and civic watchmen, effective against plate and mail, cheap to make, and easy to wield in close press. The passage opposes sword to mace and magistrate's or king's mace to earlier shepherd and priestly staves to show the same stick hardening into coercion.

The ceremonial sense grows directly from the weapon. Sergeants-at-arms who guarded kings and magistrates with maces gradually transformed the crushing head into gilded, engraved, crowned ornament. A great mace laid on the table of the House of Commons, a university mace carried in procession, or a mayoral mace in council all declare that authority is present and protected. To be under the mace is to be in session; to have the mace removed is disorder. The symbolism is frank: law rests ultimately on force made decorous.

  • Blunt weapon: flanged, knobbed, or spiked club for crushing; horseman's mace, footman's mace, morning-star variant; e.g. Richard III's mace, ecclesiastical exemption allowing clergy to use maces rather than shed blood with swords.
  • Ceremonial emblem of office: great mace of Parliament, mayoral mace, university mace; silver-gilt, crowned, with arms and mottoes; carried by mace-bearer, displayed to open proceedings.
  • Spice homonym (distinct etymology): dried aril of nutmeg used in cooking; same spelling and pronunciation but unrelated origin from Latin macis; cooks distinguish blade mace versus ground mace; must not be confused in reading.
  • Chemical irritant (proprietary extension): Mace as trademark for tear-gas spray, from the notion of a stunning club in chemical form; to be Maced, Mace spray; capitalized in trade use.
  • Athletic and مجازی uses: mace-bell or gada for Indian club training; juggling mace; drum-major's mace for parade flourish, twirled rather than wielded.
  • Figurative power: the mace of justice, the mace of office; to wield the mace means to exercise authority; heraldic and poetic shorthand for coercive majesty.

Polysemy bridges are material and social. Weapon to symbol follows the universal path of arms becoming insignia once the bearer no longer fights: sword to sword of state, spear to processional spear, mace to civic mace. Spice and spray are accidents of homophony and branding, not semantic growth, but they complicate dictionary entries and must be fenced off. The durable core is heaviness at the end of a handle concentrating force, whether to break armor, to impress spectators, or in the spice to add weight of flavor.

Usage: countable noun, plural maces; compounds mace-bearer, mace-head; verb to mace (to strike with a mace, or to spray with Mace). Register splits: weapon-historical versus civic-ceremonial versus culinary. In British parliamentary reporting the Mace is almost a character; in American campuses it appears at commencement. Pronunciation is a single syllable rhyming with face. Distinguish from maze homophone by spelling and context.
