- [Antiquity: c. 8th c. BCE–1st c. CE] - Greek ἠχή / Ἠχώ & Latin echo - The voice of the mountain nymph who repeated Narcissus's words; also the physical reflection of sound in caves and theatres - [perception: poetic, tragic mythology and early architectural acoustics]
- [Late Middle Ages: c. 1350–1500] - echo - Enters Middle English via Old French and Latin translations of Ovid's Metamorphoses (Chaucer, Gower) - [perception: courtly mythological trope of unrequited love and vanishing beauty]
- [Renaissance & Elizabethan Era: c. 1500–1650] - echo - Popularized in dramatic "echo songs" (in pastoral plays by Sidney, Spenser, Webster, and Shakespeare) where stage echoes provide uncanny answers - [perception: theatrical virtuosity, spiritual reflection, and acoustic mystery]
- [Enlightenment & 19th Century: c. 1700–1900] - echo - Systematized in wave physics, while Romantic and Aesthetic poets (Shelley, Keats, Wilde) elevate Echo as a symbol of nature's sympathetic mourning - [perception: harmonious synthesis of physical science and exquisite melancholic poetry]
- [20th–21st Century] - echo - Standardized across radar, sonar, echocardiography, computer networking (ping/ICMP), and modern sociology ("echo chamber") - [perception: indispensable technical metric in diagnostics and communication; cultural metaphor for political tribalism]
