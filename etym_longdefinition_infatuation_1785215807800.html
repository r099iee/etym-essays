INFATUATION is a noun denoting an intense, short-lived, or unreasoning passion or admiration for someone or something — a state of being carried away by feeling that overrides judgment. The word descends from Latin fatuus, "foolish," and its core idea is that of being made foolish: to be infatuated is to have one's reason temporarily displaced by a consuming attachment.

Primary sense: romantic and personal infatuation

In its modern, dominant sense, infatuation is a powerful but brief romantic attachment, typically characterized by idealization — the lover projects onto the beloved a perfection that impartial observers cannot see — and by a fantasy-driven intensity that fades as acquaintance grows. The word is carefully distinguished from love: love is taken to involve knowledge of the other person, mutuality, and endurance through difficulty, whereas infatuation is blind, idealizing, and short-lived. This is the sense in which the biographical passage behind this card uses the word: "Mill's infatuation was the cause of considerable trouble to his acquaintances and friends" — the Victorian biographer's judgment that John Stuart Mill's devotion to Harriet Taylor was unreasoning, disproportionate, and self-blinding, a folly that estranged friends, produced awkward social situations, and fed whispered gossip.

Older and secondary senses

  • Deprivation of reason (now archaic): the original English sense, "the act of making foolish or depriving of reason," attested from the sixteenth century; early writers could speak of a person "infatuated" by greed, by false doctrine, or by bad counsel, with a flavour of spiritual and mental ruin.
  • Theological delusion: in religious writing of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, infatuation was used of a divinely or diabolically induced blindness of judgment — a mind given over to error.
  • Crush: in modern colloquial speech, infatuation is the serious, literary counterpart of the light adolescent "crush"; both name the same short-lived, idealizing state, but the colloquial word is playful where infatuation is solemn.
  • Psychology and clinical vocabulary: modern psychology describes the state of being infatuated as an intense, projection-driven attraction distinct from mature attachment; some researchers call the early, obsessive phase of romantic love "limerence," a near-synonym of infatuation coined in the 1970s.
  • Non-romantic fascination: the word extends to unreasoning enthusiasm for things as well as persons — an infatuation with an idea, a hobby, a fashion, a celebrity, or a luxury — always with the suggestion that the enthusiasm is disproportionate and likely to fade.

Metaphoric shift and historical bridge

The word entered English in the sixteenth century as a learned borrowing from Latin infatuātiō, "a making foolish," built on fatuus, "foolish, insipid." Its earliest senses were moral and intellectual censure: to be infatuated was to have one's judgment deliberately destroyed. Only in the eighteenth century did the lover replace the madman as the typical subject, and the word shifted from "depriving of reason" to "an intense romantic passion that clouds reason" — a shift that kept the old idea of folly while applying it to the heart rather than the head.

Register, grammar, and usage

The noun is countable ("an infatuation," "a passing infatuation") and uncountable in the abstract ("a state of infatuation"). It is usually followed by with or for: "an infatuation with a colleague," "an infatuation for a cause." The verb infatuate (17th c.) and the adjective infatuated (16th c.) belong to the same family, and the related adjective fatuous (17th c.) and noun fatuity (16th c.) preserve the root sense of foolishness. In the Mill passage the word carries the full weight of Victorian disapproval — the tone is amused, affectionate, yet slightly scandalized biography.
