Fir-tree is a folk compound, and each of its halves is a deep-rooted Germanic word whose history reaches back through the Germanic languages and, in the case of tree, to a firmly established Proto-Indo-European root.

The first element, fir, is the more obscure of the two. Its Germanic ancestry is secure: Old English fyrh / furh (with genitive fyrhwe) names the fir or pine, and close relatives appear across the West Germanic languages (Old Norse fyri / fýri, and continental forms), pointing to a Proto-Germanic base *ferhw- / *furh-. What is debated is whether that Germanic base has an Indo-European etymology. Some scholars connect it to the PIE root *perkʷ-u-, the word for a hard-wood tree of stony ground, whose most famous descendant is Latin quercus "oak"; under this view the Germanic branch shifted the sense from a hardwood (oak-like) tree to the evergreen conifer, a plausible but unprovable change. Other scholars reject the connection and treat fir as a Germanic word of unknown, perhaps non-Indo-European, origin. The honest statement is: the Germanic stages are certain, the PIE stage is a widely repeated but debatable hypothesis, and no scholar claims a documented link between the Old English form and any attested Indo-European ancestor. The word's Middle English shape, firre, stabilised in the 13th century, and in modern English the noun fir names the genus Abies with great precision while the plain compound fir-tree keeps the older, folk, storybook register.

The second element, tree, is one of the most solid etymologies in the language. It descends from PIE *deru-, "wood, be firm, tree," a root so widely attested that it is a standard exhibit in every Indo-European textbook. The derived grade *drew-o- (or *drēu-o-) produced Proto-Germanic *trewą, and from it Old English trēow / trēo, which in early English meant not only "tree" but "wood, timber, beam, gallows, cross." The modern vowel and spelling settled in Middle English. The same root appears in Sanskrit dāru "wood", Greek drûs "oak" and dôru "spear-shaft, wood", Latin dūrus "hard" (from the notion of firm as wood), and in the Celtic and Baltic oak-words (Old Irish daur, Welsh derwen, Lithuanian dervà "resinous wood"). The history of tree is thus a history of the Indo-European forest itself: the same root that gave the Greeks their oak and the Romans their hardness gave the English their most fundamental plant-word.

  • Sound change (tree): PIE *deru- → *drew-o- → PGmc *trewą → OE trēow/trēo (initial *d- becomes voiceless *t- by Grimm's Law) → ME tre → NE tree.
  • Sound change (fir): PGmc *ferhw- → OE fyrh/furh → ME firre → NE fir; the medial *-h- of the Old English form was lost, and the short vowel settled as -ir-.
  • Semantic drift (tree): "wood, timber, material" (OE, where trēow covered both the living tree and the worked wood) → "the living woody plant" (NE), while the material sense was taken over by the related wood.
  • Semantic drift (fir): a West Germanic conifer name whose sense remained stable — the evergreen — while its Indo-European connection (if real) shifted from a hard-wood oak-like tree to the softer conifer.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the fir became the Christmas tree in 19th-century Britain and Europe (popularised by Prince Albert and German custom), and the compound fir-tree thereby gained its warm, domestic, festive halo; it is the "ever-green" of winter folklore, the tree of constancy planted in churchyards.
  • Literary attestations: fir-tree appears in medieval English place-names and documents; in the 19th century it is ubiquitous in nursery and landscape writing (including translations of Hans Christian Andersen's The Fir-Tree, 1844, in which the tree itself is the narrator); and in Potter's tale it is the fixed home-marker at the wood's edge.
  • Contrast: modern scientific and commercial English prefers the bare fir or the Latin Abies, while fir-tree survives as the plain, nostalgic, storybook form — exactly the register Potter needs for a burrow under the roots.

In Peter Rabbit, then, fir-tree is not a botanical term but a home-word. Etymologically it joins a debatable Germanic conifer-name with one of the oldest Indo-European roots of the forest; culturally it joins the dark ever-green of folklore with the warm tree of Christmas; and narratively it is the one fixed point — big, rooted, green — that stops the running.
