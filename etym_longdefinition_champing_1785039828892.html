CHAMPING (present participle of the verb champ; also the noun champ) CORE SENSE. To champ is to chew or bite down vigorously and noisily, working the jaws with audible, rhythmic crunching — either on feed or on a metal bit. The word describes the eager, repeated closing and working of the jaws, whether an animal is mashing fodder with evident appetite or worrying a bit in impatient readiness to move. As a noun, a champ is the act of biting repeatedly. Two threads run through every sense: noise (the crunching is audible) and eagerness (the chewing is vigorous and driven). Champing is never the steady, quiet chewing of contented rumination; it is the emphatic, working-the-jaws motion of an animal (or person) with appetite or impatience in it. LITERARY CONTEXT (The Hobbit, ch. 3). After the travellers find the dry, warm cave, the ponies stand "steaming, and champing in their nosebags." In this passage champing is a sound of life and comfort: after the terrifying, drenched night in the storm, the rhythmic crunching of grain in the ponies' nosebags is the homely music of safety. The word carries a note of eager, satisfied appetite — the animals are hungry, warm, and finally at ease, and their noisy feeding signals to the reader that the crisis is over. Tolkien pairs it with "steaming" to complete a picture of bodily warmth and release: wet, frightened animals have become dry, contented, feeding ones. The tone is one of cozy, physical relief after supernatural terror — and the word's eager, gripping energy is still audible even in the feeding sense. POLYSEMY: THE FULL RANGE OF SENSES. - Feeding sense (primary here). To chew or mash food noisily and vigorously, especially of horses, cattle, and other animals working fodder or grain: champing hay, champing oats, champing corn. This is the sense in the Hobbit passage, where ponies champ in their nosebags. - Bitting sense. To bite down upon and work a bit between the teeth, as a horse held back from movement does: champing the bit. The motion is the restless, jaw-working bite of impatience, and it is the source of the most famous idiom (below). - The idiom "to champ at the bit" (figurative). To be restlessly, impatiently eager to begin or to be off. The idiom, recorded from 1640, transfers the impatient horse's jaw-working to human longing: a person champing at the bit cannot wait for something to start. Historical bridge: the physical restlessness of the bitted horse = the emotional restlessness of the eager person. (Commonly misspelled or reshaped as "chomping at the bit" in American English.) - Personified / humorous use. To champ can describe a person chewing with exaggerated vigour and noise — champing one's food, champing loudly through a meal. Bridge: transfer of the animal's emphatic jaw-work to human eating, usually with comic or disapproving force. - Extended metaphorical use. Anything that works, gnaws, or bites repeatedly and emphatically can champ: waves champing against the rocks, machinery champing at its work. Bridge: the core image of repeated, forceful biting. ON THE SOUND AND THE WORD'S SHAPE. The verb is echoic at heart: cham-cham-cham is the noise of jaws working, teeth closing, and grain being crushed. Its close relative chomp, the common American variant, is heavier and more casual, and the two words are frequently confused; purists prefer champ for both the feeding and the bit idiom, but chomping is widespread and now generally accepted in informal American use. The OED cautiously connects champ with jam ("to press tightly"), suggesting both spring from the same sound-symbolic impulse to imitate the gripping, squeezing action of jaws. REGISTER AND CONNOTATION. Champing is a vivid, onomatopoeic word of the stable and the farm. In the feeding sense it is homely and reassuring; in the bit sense it is full of tension and impatience; and in the human, humorous use it is comic. It carries more energy than chewing or munching and more noise than gnawing, and it always pictures the jaws actively, vigorously at work. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS (summary). - munching — steady and quiet, closed mouth, without urgency; champing — noisy, vigorous, working the jaws. - crunching — stresses the brittle breaking sound of food, no idea of eagerness; champing — stresses the eager, rhythmic working of the jaws. - gnawing — slow, persistent biting on something hard, to wear it down; champing — quick, repeated, emphatic biting. - gnashing — grinding the teeth together in rage or pain; champing — working the jaws in feeding or impatience, not anger. - chomping — the heavier, more casual American variant; champing — the standard British form of the same sound-family. - chewing — neutral, general mastication; champing — the emphatic, noisy, eager subset. - masticating — the clinical, technical word for chewing; champing — the vivid, onomatopoeic, animal word. IN THE HOBBIT, the word is the sound of safety itself: the eager, noisy working of the ponies' jaws in their nosebags after the terror of the storm, the homely crunching music that tells the reader the crisis is over.
