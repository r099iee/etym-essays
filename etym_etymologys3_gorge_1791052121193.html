The modern English word gorge descends from a vivid bodily metaphor in which the throat is imagined as a whirlpool or abyss that seizes and drags down what it swallows. Its deepest reconstructable ancestor is usually given as PIE *gʷer- / *gʷergʷ-, a root expressing swallowing, gulping, choking and whirling water. This root also feeds Sanskrit gargara (whirlpool), Greek borkana (?), Latin vorare (to devour), and English carouse-adjacent forms. The connection is semantic as much as phonetic: swallowing and whirling share a spiralling inward motion.

In Classical Latin this idea is fully lexicalised as gurges, gurgitis, masculine noun meaning whirlpool, eddy, abyss, gulf, and by transferred use a spendthrift or devourer, and poetically the throat or maw. Cicero, Virgil and Ovid use gurges for a sea-abyss that swallows ships, and also for the gullet. The double sense — water that swallows and throat that swallows — is crucial. Late Latin popular speech simplified the stem to *gurga, feminine, meaning gulf and throat, losing the learned -it- inflection. This is not directly attested in large numbers but is securely inferred from Romance outcomes.

  • Old French gorge: By the 11th century, Old French has gorge as throat, neck, upper chest, bosom, and by metonymy the contents of the throat, a narrow passage, and the crop of a bird. Charters, romances and medical texts use gorge for both human anatomy and topography.
  • Middle English borrowing: Borrowed c.1375-1400, first as anatomy (throat) and falconry (crop, food eaten by a hawk). Chaucer and Gower use it for throat. The phrase to fill his gorge belongs to hawking.
  • Verb development: From the noun comes the verb to gorge (c.1500): to fill the throat, to cram with food, to eat greedily. Reflexive to gorge oneself becomes standard by the 16th-17th centuries, exactly as in the source context Savages gorge themselves or fast.
  • Topographic sense: From narrow throat to narrow passage between mountains. By the late 18th century, especially in travel writing about the Alps and America, a gorge is a deep narrow ravine. This is metaphor, not a new etymon.
  • Technical extensions: Architecture (throat of a chimney, gorge of a bastion), heraldry, fortification, and engineering retain throat-sense. Medicine retains gorge as rising of stomach contents.

Phonetically the development is regular: Latin u before r + Vulgar lowering gives Old French o (gurga > gorge), with softening of g before e to /dʒ/ in both French and English. English preserves French spelling but anglicises pronunciation to /ɡɔːdʒ/. Semantically the drift is from CONCRETE WHIRLING WATER to BODY PART (throat) to ACTION (swallowing greedily) to LANDSCAPE (earth that swallows like a throat). Each step preserves the image of a narrow devouring passage.

Literary attestations track this: 14th-century surgical treatises (gorge of the throat), 15th-century hawking manuals (hawk's gorge), Shakespeare (gorged maw), Milton (gorgeous from same root via splendid bosom/clothing), 19th-century geology (gorge of the river). The modern verb is mildly disapproving: to gorge implies excess, loss of control, animal appetite, contrasting with dine, feast positively, or nibble. That pejorative colour explains its use in early anthropology to describe supposed savage improvidence — eating to excess when food is present rather than storing it.

Debate note: Some older dictionaries derived gorge directly from Latin gurges without intermediate *gurga, while Romance philologists insist *gurga is required to explain Old Provençal gorja, Spanish garganta (with nasal infix), Italian gorga. The PIE root itself has variants cited as *gʷer- (swallow) vs *gʷergʷ- (whirlpool); both are reconstructions of the same imitative complex, not competing etyma. No serious alternative origin is proposed.
