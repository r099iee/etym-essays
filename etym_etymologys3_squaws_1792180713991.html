The English plural noun squaws possesses a linguistically documented and historically tragic etymological evolution. Emerging from an ancient Proto-Algonquian morpheme signifying simply "woman," it was adopted into early colonial American English through peaceful trade and missionization in Massachusetts Bay. Over subsequent centuries of violent westward expansion, however, the word underwent extreme semantic pejoration, degenerating into one of the most vitriolic racial and misogynistic slurs in North American history.

Proto-Algonquian Ancestry and Cross-Linguistic Cognates:

  • The Ancestral Root (*eθkwe·wa): Comparative Algonquian historical linguistics firmly reconstructs the Proto-Algonquian ancestor word as *eθkwe·wa ("woman, female"). This ancient morpheme was remarkably resilient across the entire Algonquian language family spanning North America:
    • In Plains Algonquian: Blackfoot áákiikoan ("girl, young woman").
    • In Central Algonquian: Cree iskwêw (ᐃᐢᑫᐧᐤ, "woman"), Ojibwe ikwe ("woman"), and Menominee mɛqnɛ·niw.
    • In Eastern Algonquian: Abenaki paskw or skwa, Delaware (Munsee) oxkwéew, and Mohegan-Pequot sqwáás ("young girl").
  • Southern New England Apheresis and English Contact: In the Southern New England Algonquian languages spoken by the Massachusett, Wampanoag, and Narragansett peoples, an unstressed initial short vowel underwent regular phonological apheresis (loss of the initial unstressed syllable). Thus, Proto-Eastern Algonquian *əhkwe·w was realized as squàw [skwaːw] or ussqua. In 1643, colonial founder Roger Williams published his seminal study A Key into the Language of America, where he explicitly documented Narragansett squàw as meaning "woman," recording: "Squàwsuck: Women" (displaying the native Algonquian animate plural suffix -uck).

Colonial Semantic Pejoration and the 1970s Pseudo-Etymology Controversy:

  • Anglicized Pluralization and Semantic Degradation: English colonists rapidly replaced the Algonquian plural suffix -uck with the standard English regular plural marker -s, yielding squaws. In early seventeenth-century documentation (such as William Wood's New England's Prospect, 1634), the word was descriptive and matter-of-fact. However, as frontier conflicts intensified during King Philip's War (1675–1676) and subsequent Indian Wars, white settlers increasingly deployed squaws to strip native women of civilized standing. By the nineteenth century, it became an engine of dehumanization, framing native women as subjugated beasts of burden or moral outcasts.
  • The "Mohawk Genitalia" Myth Refuted: In the 1970s, a widely publicized folk etymology emerged (popularized in books and television interviews), claiming that squaw did not descend from an Algonquian word for woman, but was instead an obscene corruption of a Mohawk (an Iroquoian language, completely unrelated to Algonquian) word for female genitalia (ojiskwa'). Leading Algonquian historical linguists (such as Ives Goddard of the Smithsonian Institution) published exhaustive philological refutations demonstrating that this claim was factually and linguistically false: squaw derives unequivocally from Proto-Algonquian *eθkwe·wa ("woman"). Nevertheless, linguists noted that this folk etymology itself reflected the profound real-world pain and sexualized trauma that the slur had inflicted upon generations of Native American women.
  • Contemporary Institutional Eradication: Today, the term is categorized as hate speech. In 2021, the U.S. Department of the Interior enacted historic federal reforms to expunge the slur from all national place-names, ensuring that the legacy of indigenous women is honored through accurate ancestral nomenclature rather than colonial degradation.
