Zoilus (Greek: Ζώϊλος, Zōílos; c. 400–320 BCE) was an ancient Greek Cynic philosopher, grammarian, and rhetorician from Amphipolis in Thrace, who achieved immortal literary infamy for his bitter, pedantic attacks upon Homer, earning him the enduring epithet Homēromastix (Greek: Ὁμηρομάστιξ, "Scourge of Homer"). In literary history, rhetoric, and criticism, his name became the definitive antonomasia for a carping, envious, and hypercritical fault-finder who attacks monumental genius out of personal malice and pedantic vanity.

In the source passage from John Ruskin's Modern Painters (1843), Ruskin explicitly pairs Zoilus with Charles Perrault to illustrate two complementary forms of critical incompetence and malice. While political tyrants like Nero and Caligula manifest malice through violent oppression, literary critics manifest the identical psychological pathology through ungenerous detraction. Zoilus represents the futile effort to degrade the dead—specifically, the supreme epic poet Homer—by manufacturing trivial logical objections, just as modern Victorian reviewers were attempting to degrade the living genius of J. M. W. Turner. Ruskin highlights that whether a critic attacks Homer or Turner, the driving impulse is the same: the sour resentment that mediocrity feels in the presence of transcendent achievement.

Core historical identity and critical method: Living in the fourth century BCE and possibly studying under Polycrates or Isocrates, Zoilus authored a famous nine-volume treatise entitled Against Homer's Poetry (Kata tēs tou Homērou poiēseōs). His critical method was characterized by aggressive literalism and pedantic fault-finding: he mocked Homer for having Achilles weep excessively over the death of Patroclus, which he considered unmanly; ridiculed the transformation of Odysseus' companions into swine by Circe as weeping piglets; and scoffed at Homeric divine interventions as absurd logical contradictions. Because Homer was revered throughout the Greek world as the supreme educator and poet of Hellas, Zoilus's attacks were regarded not merely as eccentric literary dissent, but as insolent, bad-faith iconoclasm designed solely to win notoriety through contrarian exhibitionism.

All senses, primary and extended usages:

  • The historical Greek rhetorician (proper historical sense): Zoilus of Amphipolis, the 4th-century BCE Cynic critic and orator whose name became synonymous with Homeric vilification and who, according to Vitruvius, was executed or driven into exile after presenting his disparaging critiques to King Ptolemy Philadelphus in Alexandria.
  • Archetype of the envious, carping critic (literary antonomasia): Any reviewer, critic, or commentator who systematically seeks out minor blemishes, flaws, or grammatical irregularities in a masterpiece while remaining completely blind to its transcendent beauty and intellectual grandeur.
  • Abstract noun derivative (zoilism): The practice, spirit, or habit of bitter, captious, and envious fault-finding in criticism; petty, hypercritical carping driven by personal resentment of an author's success.
  • Agent noun derivative (zoilist): A malicious, ungenerous critic; one given to habitual zoilism.
  • Adjectival derivative (Zoilean): Pertaining to, resembling, or characteristic of Zoilus; intensely captious, spiteful, and pedantically ungenerous (e.g., "subjected to a barrage of Zoilean reviews").
  • Modern figurative usage in intellectual and media discourse: A label applied to bad-faith online commentators, pedantic forum polemicists, or cynical reviewers who seize upon inconsequential technical discrepancies in film, literature, or scholarship to dismiss works of monumental ambition.

Register and literary legacy: Zoilus belongs to high literary, classical, and academic registers. For over two millennia—from Martial and Ovid through Erasmus, Alexander Pope, and Ruskin—invoking Zoilus has served as the classic defensive weapon of artists and poets against ungenerous detractors. It captures a precise moral diagnosis: that excessive, venomous fault-finding is rarely an expression of superior taste, but rather the desperate, noisy compensation of creative impotence.
