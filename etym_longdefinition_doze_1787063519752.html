Primary senses. Doze is both a verb and a noun. As a verb it means to sleep lightly or briefly, hovering on the edge of waking, usually in an unintended or easily broken sleep: to drowse, to nod, to be only half asleep. As a noun, "a doze" is a short, light sleep of this kind. In the source sentence Bilbo passes from a dream to "a doze" — a shallow, hovering state between the uneasy dream and full waking — and then from the doze to wide waking: the word marks the exact middle stage of the threefold descent from deep dreaming to full alertness.

Full range of senses.

  • To sleep lightly (verb, intransitive). The core sense: "he dozed in the armchair", "she dozed through the film". The sleep is shallow — a dozer can be woken by a whisper — and the word is the opposite of the deep, heavy sleep of "slumber" or "sleep soundly".
  • A short light sleep (noun). "He had a doze after lunch": a brief, light, pleasant sleep, usually in a chair or in the daytime. In this use it overlaps with "nap" but is lighter and often more accidental.
  • To doze off (phrasal verb). The most common modern use: to fall involuntarily into light sleep — "I dozed off during the lecture", "she dozed off in front of the television". The sudden, uncontrolled transition from waking to sleeping is the key feature.
  • To doze away (phrasal verb). To spend time sleeping lightly: "he dozed away the afternoon" — the sense of time passing in a drowsy half-sleep.
  • To pass into dullness or stupefaction (older sense). The verb's earliest English meaning was "to be dull, sluggish, stupefied" — a dozing mind was a thick, slow mind, not necessarily asleep. This older sense survives in the adjective "dozy" (chiefly British: drowsy, and also stupid or slow — "a dozy student") and in "dopey"-type slang.
  • Figurative uses. Anything dull, slack, or half-alive can "doze": "the town dozed through the long summer", "the committee dozed over the report". The figurative use trades on the half-awake quality: not dead, not asleep, just not quite operating.

Register and tone. "Doze" is a mild, gentle, slightly comic word. It has none of the dignity of "slumber" or "rest", none of the clinical neutrality of "sleep", and none of the decisiveness of "nap". A doze is soft, warm, and a little silly — the sleep of grandfathers in armchairs, of students in back rows, of cats in sunbeams. The word almost always suggests comfort or harmless drowsiness, except in "doze off" when it suggests a small public embarrassment (falling asleep in a meeting) or a real danger (dozing at the wheel).

Derived forms. The family is small but busy: "doze" (verb/noun), "dozes" (third person / plural), "dozed" (past), "dozing" (participle/gerund), "dozy" (adjective, British, "drowsy, slow"), "doziness" (noun), "dozer" (one who dozes) — and, by a different route, "doze" is unrelated to "bulldozer", whose "doze" comes from the noun "dose" (a quantity), not from this word.

Grammar note. As a verb, doze is regular and intransitive: doze / dozes / dozed / dozing. As a noun it is countable: a doze, a doze of an hour, little dozes. The participle "dozing" works as adjective ("a dozing cat") and noun ("the dozing of the old men"). The phrasal forms "doze off" and "doze away" are fully standard and, in modern English, more common than the bare verb.
