GRATUITOUS is an adjective with two sharply different lives: an older, neutral-to-positive sense, "given freely, without charge or payment," and the modern dominant sense, "done without reason, necessity, or justification; uncalled-for." The two senses are joined by a single idea — something offered without obligation — and the modern sense arose precisely because what is given freely, with no reason required, came to seem, in many contexts, like something that has no business being there at all. In the passage from William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity, GRATUITOUS characterizes the "puzzles of definition" that the author has resolved to sweep away: difficulties that are artificial, self-imposed, and lacking any genuine justification. The tone is sharp and corrective — the writer concedes that certain ambiguities are real and worth pursuing, but insists that the added complexities of definition are unwarranted inventions, introduced without necessity and obscuring rather than illuminating the genuine questions. The senses in full:
  • Free of charge (historical, technical, legal). Given or done without payment or compensation: a gratuitous service, a gratuitous bailment (law, where a bailee receives no reward), a gratuitous contract (law, where only one party is bound, without consideration). In theology, "gratuitous grace" (gratia gratis data) is grace freely given by God, unearned and unowed.
  • Voluntary, spontaneous (older neutral sense). Done of one's own free will, without obligation or expectation of return: a gratuitous act of kindness. This is the sense closest to the Latin source and to the sibling noun gratuity, "a tip given freely."
  • Uncalled-for, unjustified (modern dominant sense). Done or added without cause, reason, necessity, or justification; unwarranted and often morally or aesthetically censured: gratuitous violence, gratuitous nudity, a gratuitous insult, gratuitous detail, gratuitous complication.
  • Adverbial and nominal forms. Gratuitously, "without justification"; gratuitousness, "the quality of being uncalled-for."
The characteristic modern collocations are worth spelling out:
  • Gratuitous violence / sex / nudity. In film and media criticism, violence or nudity added for its own sake, serving no dramatic or thematic purpose — the textbook case of the word's modern censure.
  • Gratuitous insult / remark. A remark that is unprovoked, added without cause, and usually wounding.
  • Gratuitous detail / complication. In scholarly and technical prose, complexity added without necessity, obscuring rather than clarifying — exactly Empson's "gratuitous puzzles of definition."
The historical semantic bridge: the Latin grātuitus meant "done freely, without pay," built on the adverbial base grātīs, "out of favour, without reward." For roughly two centuries in English (from c. 1650) gratuitous meant simply "freely given." During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word was increasingly applied to things added without a sufficient reason, and the free gift that needed no justification became the addition that lacked all justification — the modern sense, with its moral and aesthetic censure, triumphed in the twentieth century. The sibling words gratuity (a tip) and gratis (free) belong to the same family and keep the older, positive meaning alive.
