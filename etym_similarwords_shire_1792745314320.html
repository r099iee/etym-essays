• county: Similar because both denote the primary administrative territorial subdivision in Great Britain and other English-speaking nations (with many shires formally functioning as counties); differs because county is a Norman-French loanword (from comté, the domain of a count) introduced after 1066 representing continental feudal administration, whereas shire is the native Anglo-Saxon Germanic term carrying deeper pre-Conquest rural resonance.
  • district: Similar because both refer to a defined geographical zone demarcated for administrative, legal, or electoral governance; differs because district is an abstract, neutral, and utilitarian administrative term (from Latin districtus, "restraint/jurisdiction"), lacking the rich historical, pastoral, and cultural connotations inherent in shire.
  • province: Similar because both represent territorial divisions within a larger kingdom or nation; differs because a province is typically a vastly larger geographic territory (often possessing its own regional government, as in Canada, Roman provinces, or imperial domains), whereas a shire is a localized, close-knit county-level agrarian community.
  • bailiwick: Similar because both historically originated as the territorial jurisdiction under the charge of a specific royal officer (a bailiff for a bailiwick; a shire-reeve for a shire); differs because bailiwick has shifted largely into an idiomatic metaphor for one's personal sphere of expertise or authority, whereas shire remains an active geographic and literary toponym.
  • hundred: Similar because both were foundational administrative territorial units in Anglo-Saxon England; differs because a hundred (originally land sufficient to sustain 100 households or furnish 100 warriors) was a subordinate subdivision contained inside a shire, with each shire encompassing numerous hundreds.
  • fiefdom: Similar because both refer to an estate or land unit held under a ruler's sovereign authority; differs because a fiefdom is fundamentally bound to the feudal system of military service, vassalage, and noble lordship, whereas the historic Anglo-Saxon shire and Tolkien's Shire were communal, self-governing agrarian entities.
  • parish: Similar because both represent ancient English territorial divisions deeply tied to local rural life; differs because a parish was primarily an ecclesiastical division centered around a single local church and village pastor, functioning on a much smaller geographical scale than the overarching shire.
  • canton: Similar because both denote localized, semi-autonomous territorial subdivisions within a federal state (such as the cantons of Switzerland); differs because canton represents a sovereign constitutional state within a confederation, whereas a shire is historically an administrative arm of a centralized royal kingdom.
