1. PIE *ǵenh₁- "to beget, give birth to, produce" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3500 BCE; cf. Sanskrit janati, Greek gígnomai). 2. Latin gignere "to beget, bring forth, produce" (from *ǵenh₁-; attested from the 3rd c. BCE). 3. Latin ingenium "inborn nature, innate talent, natural disposition, genius" (in- "in" + the root of gignere; attested from Plautus, 2nd c. BCE). 4. Latin ingeniosus "talented, clever, gifted, inventive" (ingenium + adjective suffix -ōsus; attested 1st c. BCE). 5. Old French ingénieux / engignous "clever, talented, skilful" (12th–14th c.). 6. Middle English ingenious / enginous "possessed of natural talent, clever" (borrowed 15th c.). 7. English ingenious "cleverly inventive, resourceful, original" (sense fixed by Johnson's Dictionary in the 18th c.) → INGENIOUS. Debate / nuance notes: - The PIE root *ǵenh₁- is one of the most securely reconstructed roots in Indo-European; the chain from it to ingenium is uncontroversial. - The parallel branch of ingenium produced Old French engin "contrivance, war-machine" → English engine / engineer; this mechanical branch is historically distinct from but constantly cross-influenced with the mental "clever" sense. - The historical confusion with ingenuous (Latin ingenuus "free-born, candid") affected English usage from the 16th to the 18th centuries but is now firmly resolved.
