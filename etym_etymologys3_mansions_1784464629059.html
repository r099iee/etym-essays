Mansion is a Romance dwelling-word built on the Latin verb of staying, and its history is a rise in social scale: a word that once meant a night's lodging on a road became, over two thousand years, the name of the grand house of the wealthy. The biography of the word is also the biography of the idea that where you stay reveals who you are — from the humblest halting-place to the palaces of the great. 1. The PIE root and the Latin verb. The chain begins with the Indo-European root *men-, "to remain, stay, wait," a root distinct from its homophone *men- "to think" (source of mind and memory). From *men- comes Latin manēre, "to remain, stay, abide," attested from the second century BCE and used both of persons staying put and of things enduring. The participle stem is māns-, and on that stem Latin builds the noun mānsiō (accusative mānsiōnem): "a staying," then concretely a place where one stays — a night's lodging, a halting-place, a station on a journey. In the Roman Empire the mansiō was also an official stopping-station on the road system, with stables, inns, and lodgings for travellers and officials; the word thus carried a whole infrastructure of travel and rest. 2. Through Romance to French: mansion the dwelling. Late Latin and Romance transmit mānsiōnem as a general word for a dwelling-place or abode. Old French mansion (and Anglo-Norman forms) mean "dwelling, habitation, residence" without any insistence on size; the word is the everyday noun for a place where people live. With the Norman Conquest the word enters Middle English as mansion / mansioun in the thirteenth century, with the wide sense of "dwelling-place, abode," and also "a stage or station on a journey," keeping the Roman travel sense alive in English. 3. The biblical anchor: "many mansions." A decisive event in the word's English biography is the Bible. In John 14:2, Jesus says that in his Father's house there are "many mansions" (Latin multae mansiones; Greek monai pollai), and the King James translation of 1611 fixes the plural mansions in the language with the sense of "many dwelling-places, many abodes." The phrase is so familiar that it anchors the older wide sense — mansion as any place of abiding — in elevated English, even after the everyday word had begun to rise in social rank. Every later use of "many mansions" is a bridge between the humble lodging and the heavenly dwelling. 4. The rise in dignity. Over the 17th–19th centuries the everyday word climbs. A "mansion" ceases to be any dwelling and becomes specifically a large, impressive house — the great country house of the gentry, the grand town house of the wealthy, the residence of a person of rank. The shift is social and economic: as towns and country houses grew more differentiated, the language needed a word for the imposing residence, and mansion, which had meant merely "a place of staying," was promoted to the task. The older "abodes" sense survives only in archaic, biblical, or consciously elevated register. 5. Secondary senses and compounds.
  • Mansions of the moon: the 28 divisions of the zodiac in which the moon "stays" — an astronomical use of the lodging image.
  • Mansion block: in British English, a large, well-built apartment building.
  • Mansion House (capitalized): the official residence of the Lord Mayor of London, and, generically, a grand town residence.
  • The mansion of the soul / heaven's mansions: figurative abodes in theological and poetic writing.
6. On this card. In The Hobbit, the catalogue of Smaug's routing runs "halls, and lanes, and tunnels, alleys, cellars, mansions and passages." The plural sets the great residential chambers of the dwarven Mountain beside the meanest cellars and alleys, so that the destruction is total — high and low alike. The word's biblical resonance ("many mansions") sharpens the irony: these are the dwelling-places of a people, now scoured and emptied. The card keeps the plural of the text, grand dwellings named only as the dragon empties them.
