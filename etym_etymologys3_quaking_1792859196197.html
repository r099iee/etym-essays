The word quaking descends from one of the oldest and most visceral verbs in Germanic, a word that seems to have been shaped by the body it describes. Its ancestor is the Old English cwacian, meaning to shake, tremble, or quake, itself from the reconstructed Proto-Germanic *kwakōną, a verb of shaking with cognates across the northern languages. Philologists have long suspected the base is imitative: the blunt kw- onset followed by a breaking vowel seems to enact the jolt it names, the way quake, quiver, and quaver all cluster phonaesthetically around rapid small vibration. If so, the word was never borrowed from anywhere but coined from the human frame itself, a sound the mouth makes the way the knees knock. No convincing Proto-Indo-European root stands behind the Germanic family, and the dictionaries are honest about the gap: beyond Germanic the trail dissolves, which is exactly what one expects of sound-symbolic vocabulary, always younger in form than the feelings it voices.

Old English used cwacian broadly for anything that shook: limbs, leaves, the earth. The great specialisation toward fear came with Middle English Christianity, whose preachers made quaking the signature motion of the soul confronted with judgement. Manuscripts describe sinners quaking before the doom, the temple veil quaking, the earth quaking at the Crucifixion; mystery plays put quaking speeches into the mouths of devils and damned souls. The physical verb became a moral thermometer: to quake was to know one’s guilt in one’s knees. Chaucer and his contemporaries use quaken freely for fear, cold, and spiritual awe, and by 1400 the association with dread was fixed.

Notable later chapters:

  • The Quaker naming (1650s): when George Fox’s followers trembled, wept, and shook under the power of the Spirit in worship, hostile observers called them Quakers in derision, a name recorded in court proceedings and pamphlet wars. The insult became an identity: the Society of Friends carried quaking in its nickname for three centuries, a permanent memorial to the verb’s religious charge.
  • Augustan and Gothic careers (18th c.): polite writers used quaking for thrones, senates, and guilty consciences, while Gothic novelists made it the furniture of terror: heroines quake in corridors, castles quake in storms. The participle quaking became stage shorthand for visible dread, the knees-knocking prelude to fainting or flight.
  • Seismology’s loan (19th–20th c.): the noun quake, clipped from earthquake (itself from earth plus quake), gave science a terse term for seismic shocks, and headlines learned to speak of quakes and quaking ground. The ancient fear-word thus returned to its oldest literal object, the shaking earth, now measured on the Richter scale.
  • Tolkien’s usage: the source sentence shows the word at its most tender. Sam Gamgee, the story’s emblem of endurance, quakes as he cries out, and the participle honours both his terror and his refusal to yield to it. Tolkien the Anglo-Saxonist knew cwacian from the Old English corpus, where heroes quake before monsters and still stand; Sam quaking is Beowulf’s heirs in miniature, fear felt in the frame and mastered by the heart.

Phonetically the journey from cwacian to quaking records the Norman Conquest in spelling: the Old English cw- cluster was refashioned by French-trained scribes into qu-, and the two-syllable infinitive wore down to the monosyllabic modern verb, with the -ing participle carrying the continuous shudder the root always implied. Fourteen centuries on, the word still does its first job: naming what the body does when courage and terror occupy it at once.
