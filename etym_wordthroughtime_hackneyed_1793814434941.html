- [12th to 14th c. / Middle English] - Toponymic and equine origin: originated as the place-name *Hackney* in Middlesex, an area of rich marshland meadows where riding horses were bred; the noun *hackney* quickly came to designate a medium-sized, docile riding horse or ambler suitable for ordinary travel, distinct from noble war chargers (*destriers*).
- [15th to 16th c. / Tudor Era] - Commercial hire and common utility: shifted from a specific breed to any horse kept for public hire; the term took on connotations of commonness, heavy labor, and commercial availability, being used of horses, vehicles, and hired servants who were worn out by relentless daily drudgery.
- [Late 16th to 17th c. / Early Modern English] - Verbalization and prostitution: converted into a transitive verb (*to hackney*), meaning to expose to common hire, to overuse, or to prostitute; the participial adjective *hackneyed* emerged to describe anything—whether a horse, a hireling, or a phrase—exhausted, degraded, and stripped of dignity through endless public service.
- [18th c. / Augustan Age & Enlightenment] - Literary and critical standard: adopted by prominent literary critics and satirists, including Alexander Pope, Jonathan Swift, and Samuel Johnson, to excoriate unimaginative poets who relied on derivative rhymes, tired classical tropes, and worn-out metaphors, cementing its modern critical sense.
- [19th to 21st c. / Modern usage] - Universal aesthetic and intellectual critique: firmly established across literature, film, politics, and journalism as the definitive term for tired tropes, stale media soundbites, formulaic narratives, and uninspired cliches, while the root *hack* persisted independently for commercial scribblers and hirelings.
