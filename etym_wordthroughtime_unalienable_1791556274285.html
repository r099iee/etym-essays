- Early 17th Century: Legal term describing property, feudal titles, or sovereign prerogatives that could not lawfully be transferred, sold, or mortgaged to another party - Perceived as a rigid technical constraint safeguarding lineage, aristocratic estates, and royal patrimony.
- Mid to Late 17th Century: Expansion into political philosophy under John Locke and natural rights theorists, transitioning from feudal property to fundamental human liberties - Acquired radical, emancipatory resonance as a check against royal absolutism and tyrannical authority.
- Late 18th Century (1776): Immortalized by Thomas Jefferson in the United States Declaration of Independence ("endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights") - Elevated to a sacred, revolutionary civic creed, sanctifying rebellion against tyranny and establishing moral foundations of democracy.
- 19th Century: Crucial battleground in the Abolitionist movement, where Frederick Douglass and anti-slavery orators wielded "unalienable rights" to indict chattel slavery as a monstrous crime against natural law - Regarded as a stinging moral indictment of national hypocrisy and a rallying cry for universal emancipation.
- 20th Century: Formalized in global human rights covenants (though frequently styled as "inalienable" in international law) and invoked in civil rights marches, decolonization movements, and constitutional jurisprudence - Celebrated as the ultimate baseline of human dignity against totalitarian encroachments.
- 21st Century: Invoked in debates surrounding digital privacy, bodily sovereignty, reproductive rights, and artificial intelligence ethics - Connotes fundamental, non-negotiable entitlements essential to preserving human agency in an increasingly commercialized and automated world.
