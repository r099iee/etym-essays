- c. 1275 — "padlok" appears in Middle English for a portable lock, a lock that can be carried and clapped onto anything; the "pad-" element is of obscure origin, but the object is already the travelling lock of chests, hampers and panniers.
- 14th–17th c. — the word settles into its plain, practical register: homely iron hardware, the lock of the common chest and gate, entirely at home in English speech — a contrast to courtly French borrowings.
- 18th–19th c. — "padlocked" hardens as an adjective and participle describing a finished state of security: doors, shops, gates and yards stand padlocked; the image is of layered, mercenary defence — a thing doubly secured against tampering as well as force.
- 20th–21st c. — the sense widens and softens by metaphor: a "padlocked" door means shut and sealed, a padlock icon on a website means a connection is secure, and love-locks clipped to bridges make the little portable lock a symbol of the modern world.
