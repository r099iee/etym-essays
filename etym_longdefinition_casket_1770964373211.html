casket: A small, ornamental box or chest used for storing jewels, valuable heirlooms, coins, or precious documents; by extension, a protective inner repository that guards something of worth, and—chiefly in North American English since the mid-19th century—a burial container for human remains, used as a euphemistic, ornamented alternative to coffin. In the literary context of Dream of the Red Chamber ("The jewel in the casket bides till one shall come to buy"), the word operates as an evocative poetic metaphor: the casket is the unacknowledged scholar's protective exterior—a quiet, self-contained vessel that safeguards his inner brilliance, virtue, and genius ("the jewel") until a buyer of true discernment appears. The word conveys privacy, high value, and aesthetic refinement: true genius does not flaunt itself on the open market but remains preserved in its enclosure until recognized. 1. Core senses
  • Jewel-box sense (chief historical and British sense): A small, finely made box—often of precious wood, silver, ivory, or inlaid work, lined with velvet or silk, sometimes lockable—for keeping jewels, rings, letters, and other treasured small objects. This is the sense in Shakespeare and in English poetry generally, and it is the sense required by the Dream of the Red Chamber couplet.
  • Treasure-chest sense (literary/romance register): In romances and fairy tales, a casket is frequently the plot device that contains a precious object, a will, a ransom, or a hazardous gift; "The Merchant of Venice" famously uses three caskets (gold, silver, lead) as the test of a suitor's judgment, making the casket a symbol of hidden value and of choosing wisely.
  • Burial-container sense (chiefly North American, 19th c. onward): In American English, casket became the standard term for a coffin, generally rectangular with a hinged lid and cushioned interior, chosen by the funeral industry as a dignified, softened word. In this sense it collocates with the entire vocabulary of mortuary commerce (casket showroom, casket price, casket spray).
  • Figurative/emblematic sense: A "casket" can metaphorically be any enclosing structure that protects hidden worth—a person's breast as the casket of the heart or soul (common in 17th–18th century verse), a sealed chest of valuables in a will, or a hidden cache of knowledge.
  • Rare archaic sense (container of unspecified contents): In earlier English (15th–17th c.), casket could denote any small chest or box, including containers for money, papers, spices, or medical supplies, before the jewel-and-burial senses dominated.
2. Register, collocation, and usage notes
  • Collocations: jewel casket, silver casket, wooden casket, love-casket (in Shakespeare), three caskets (The Merchant of Venice), burial casket, steel casket, casket bearer, casket spray.
  • Register and geographic split: In British English, casket remains primarily a literary and somewhat archaic word for a jewel box; in American English it is the ordinary, commercial word for a coffin. This transatlantic split is one of the most striking facts about the word's modern life: the same form means a delicate treasure box in London and a funeral product in Chicago.
  • Distinction from coffin: A coffin is the tapered, six-sided, shoulder-shaped burial box; a casket (in the American mortuary sense) is the rectangular, upholstered, four-cornered container. The two are functionally identical but formally distinct, and the funeral industry deliberately promoted casket as the softer, more elegant term.
  • Register caution: Because of the American funerary sense, British or literary readers should not be startled by the word in a burial context; conversely, using casket for a jewel box in American speech today can sound quaint or poetic.
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge The word descends from the family of Latin capsa, "box, chest" (from capere, "to take, hold, contain"), which gave Old French casse and Anglo-Norman cas/case. English casket is a 15th-century formation: the noun cask (itself from the same word family, meaning a barrel or a wooden container) plus the diminutive suffix -et, giving "little cask"—a small chest. The original sense was purely a small container for valuables; the jewel-box sense hardened into a poetic emblem of hidden worth. The burial sense is a 19th-century American development: the expanding, professionalized funeral industry needed a word that distanced the product from the blunt coffin, and casket—with its jewel-box connotations of preciousness and care—was the perfect candidate. The historical bridge from treasure box to coffin runs through the shared idea of a protective container that holds something precious, gently: the same reverence that locks a jewel away was repurposed to describe the respectful enclosing of the dead. In the Dream of the Red Chamber couplet, the word taps the oldest, richest layer of that history—the casket as the guardian of a jewel awaiting recognition.
