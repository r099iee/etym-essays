Derivation chain for CLOSETED (the target root word is bold at the final stage): 1. PIE *kleh₂u- / *klāu- "hook, peg, nail; fastening or locking device" (reconstructed). The ultimate origin of Latin claudere is debated, but this root, via the idea of fastening with a bolt or bar, is the standard proposal (compare Greek kleíō "to close"). 2. Latin claudere "to shut, close, bar, enclose" (attested from c. 3rd c. BCE; e.g. Plautus, 2nd c. BCE). 3. Latin clausus, past participle of claudere "shut, closed"; noun clausum "enclosed place, enclosure" — the Vulgar/Late Latin clausa feeds the Romance words for "enclosed place". 4. Old French clore "to close" (verb) and clos "closed; enclosure, enclosed space" (past participle and noun; attested from the 9th–12th centuries). 5. Old French closet, diminutive of clos "little enclosure; small private room, study, oratory" (attested from the 12th century onward). 6. Middle English closet "private room, chamber for study, prayer or confidential business" (borrowed c. 1330; in use by Chaucer's time). 7. English closet (noun) → closet (verb) "to shut up in a closet or private room" (first recorded 1604). 8. English closeted (past participle / adjective) "shut away in a private room; engaged in private conference" (in use from the 17th century; the modern hidden-identity sense is 20th-century). Debated / nuanced stages: The PIE reconstruction is the least certain link — Latin claudere is securely an Italic verb, but its deeper PIE anchoring (whether to *kleh₂u- "peg/hook" or to a root meaning "to bar/fasten", with possible Celtic and Germanic relatives) is debated in the handbooks. All stages from Latin claudere onward are well attested.
