- Middle English - smirch/smirchen circulate as verbs of soiling and darkening with dirt, soot, or grease; perception is concrete and domestic/physical
- Late 16th century - besmirch appears as intensive be- + smirch; Early Modern drama (notably Shakespeare) links unsmirched/besmirched brows and honor, shifting perception toward moral purity
- 17th–18th centuries - figurative use dominates: to besmirch a name, fame, or reputation becomes the expected reading in elevated prose
- 19th century - Victorian honor culture keeps the word strong in novels and moral commentary; physical sense retreats to poetic or comic archaism
- 20th century - journalistic and political English retain besmirch for attacks on character; near-synonym smear grows more colloquial and partisan
- Contemporary usage - almost exclusively reputational; speakers hear it as formal, slightly old-fashioned, and condemnatory toward the mud-slinger; often paired with name, honor, legacy, or memory
