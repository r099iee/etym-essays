- 15th c.: "contented" enters English as the past participle of the borrowed verb "to content" — literally "made satisfied"; a quietly moral word. - 16th–17th c.: in Christian and moral writing, a contented man is one whose desires are "contained" within his lot; contentment is a virtue preached against envy and greed. - 18th c.: the word softens into everyday use — a contented countryman, a contented child; it loses religious sharpness and gains homely warmth. - 19th c.: frequently applied to animals and rustic scenes ("the contented cow", "contented herds"); it connotes placid, well-fed ease. - Modern: used both attributively ("a contented sigh") and with the back-formed noun "contentedness"; still peaceful and positive, but less exalted than "fulfilment" — mild, steady satisfaction.
