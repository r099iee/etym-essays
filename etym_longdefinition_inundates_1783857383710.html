INUNDATES (v., 3rd person singular present) — Overwhelms with a flood of water, or overwhelms with an excessive burden, volume, or onslaught of things, people, or messages. The word names both the literal act of water overflowing land and the figurative act of swamping anything with more than it can absorb: "the river inundates the valley each spring," "the office is inundated with calls during the holiday rush."

Core senses

  • Literal, hydraulic sense (primary): to overflow and cover with water — a river, sea, or storm inundates land, fields, or settlements, submerging them beneath water that has risen above banks or barriers. This is the classical Latin meaning (inundare, "to overflow") and the sense used in hydrology, flood reporting, and environmental writing.
  • Figurative sense of overwhelming volume (dominant in modern English): to deluge a person, office, or institution with more work, information, requests, or demands than can be handled — "inundated with emails," "inundated with applications," "inundated with complaints." The water image is retained as the governing metaphor: the receiver is swamped, submerged, drowned in quantity.

Polysemy & secondary modern meanings

  • Overwhelm with people or visitors: "tourists inundate the historic centre every summer," "fans inundated the stage" — a crowd arrives in such numbers that it floods a place, the figurative sense applied to human masses.
  • Overwhelm the senses or attention: "the festival inundates the visitor with colour and sound," "the report inundates the reader with data" — an onslaught of sensory or intellectual input, the volume sense applied to perception and cognition. This is precisely the use in the card's context: an epic catalogue or priamel deliberately inundates the reader with a dense, rapid sequence of references.
  • Media and information overload: in the internet age, "inundated" is the standard verb for being flooded with notifications, posts, advertisements, or news — digital quantity conceived as water.
  • Legal and administrative sense: "the courts are inundated with cases," "the agency is inundated with claims" — institutional overload, where the metaphor describes capacity exceeded by demand.
  • Technical hydrology (noun, inundation): in flood engineering and emergency management, "inundation" is the technical term for the extent or depth of flooding — inundation maps, inundation zones, inundation modelling — where the noun carries no metaphor at all, only measurement.

Register and connotation — inundates is a formal, Latinate verb (the plain Anglo-Saxon alternatives are floods and swamps). It always implies excess and powerlessness: the subject acts like water, the object is passively overwhelmed. The word is strongly associated with quantity and volume rather than with danger or violence per se — one is inundated by work, mail, or demands, not attacked. The connotation is of being submerged, covered, or drowned in sheer amount.

Grammatical note — INUNDATES is the third person singular present indicative of inundate: "it/he/she inundates." The related noun is inundation ("the act of overflowing; a flood"), and the participle/adjective inundated ("overwhelmed, flooded") is extremely common: "inundated with requests." The verb is transitive and typically takes a human or institutional object: X inundates Y (with Z).

Historical semantic bridge — From Latin inundāre, "to overflow, flood," built on in- ("into, upon") + undāre ("to rise in waves"), from unda ("wave"). The literal sense — water rising in waves and covering land — extended very early to the figurative: already in Latin, inundare could describe overwhelming anything by sheer quantity. English borrowed the verb in the 17th century with the literal sense first, then the figurative sense rose to dominance through the 19th and 20th centuries as correspondence, paperwork, and finally digital information created ever-new floods to be overwhelmed by. The single thread is quantity conceived as rising water.
