Obsolete enters English in the 1560s as a learned humanist borrowing of Latin obsoletus, past participle of obsolescere "to fall into disuse, wear out". Its Latin prehistory is unusually tangled, and honesty requires stating the debate. The older schoolroom analysis — still in most dictionaries including the OED and Etymonline — segments ob-so-letus as prefix ob- "away, off" + solere "to be accustomed, to use" + inchoative -scere + participial -tus: literally to grow out of habitual use. Thus obsoletus meant "that which has grown out of use". Michiel de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin (2008), however, observes phonological and semantic difficulties with solere and prefers connection to opus, operis "work" and verbs of growing (olescere, adulescere): obsoletus as "worn out by work, grown shabby", parallel to exoletus "grown up, outgrown". On either account the PIE base is the widespread root for work and increase, *op- "to work, produce" (Latin opus, opera, opulentus, Sanskrit apas "work") or the related growth-root *al-. The practical difference matters little for English: by Cicero's time obsoletus already meant both "shabby, soiled by age" and "out of use, old-fashioned".

  • Classical Latin range: Cicero calls old words verba obsoleta (words fallen out of use); Suetonius calls a dirty toga obsoleta (worn, stained). The participle thus bridges physical shabbiness and social disuse — a thing looks old because it is no longer maintained, and is no longer maintained because it looks old. This double sense survives in English obsolete versus obsolescent.
  • Phonetic borrowing: Latin obsoletus /ob.soˈleː.tus/ with stress on le entered Middle French as obsolete (learned, 16th c., keeping Latin spelling) and then English with anglicised stress. Early English spellings obsolite, obsolete show hesitation; stress settled on first syllable in RP /ˈɒbsəliːt/ by analogy with absolute, desolate. No consonant change occurred; the borrowing is purely orthographic and prosodic. The verb to obsolete (to make obsolete) and noun obsolescence are later English back-formations (18th–19th c.).
  • Early-modern English (1560s–1700s): First used of words, customs, and laws: obsolete words, obsolete statutes, obsolete ceremonies. Puttenham (1589) warns poets against obsolete terms; Hooker defends rites not yet obsolete. The connotation is antiquarian and legal: what is obsolete has lost force but may retain dignity. Ruskin's use — ideas about kings which I find for the present much obsolete — belongs exactly here: moral ideas, not machines, grown out of fashion.
  • Industrial and Darwinian expansion (1800s): With rapid technology, obsolete shifts from words to things: obsolete machinery, firearms, ships, processes. The OED records obsolete applied to army rifles, printing types, and railway stock by mid-century. Darwin in the Origin (1859) calls rudimentary organs obsolete in the Latin sense of vestigial — e.g. wings of flightless birds — giving biology a technical sense still in dictionaries as vestigial, rudimentary.
  • Twentieth-century standardisation: Economics and computing sharpen the distinction: obsolete (wholly disused) versus obsolescent (becoming so) versus deprecated (discouraged but functional). Planned obsolescence (1930s–50s, Brooks Stevens) makes obsolete a business strategy: products designed to become obsolete. Military obsolete becomes a classification stamp. The adjective gains a verbal use in American business: to obsolete a product line.

In Ruskin's sentence much obsolete (with degree adverb much, now slightly archaic) means largely fallen out of present belief: his boyhood faith that kings labour hardest seems Victorian-naive in an age of constitutional monarchy and industrial capital. The etymology — whether "grown out of use" or "worn shabby by work" — ironically fits: an idea worn out by history itself.
