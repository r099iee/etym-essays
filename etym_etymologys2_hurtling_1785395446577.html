Old French hurter 'to strike, knock against, run into, collide' (attested from the 11th–12th c.; origin disputed — possibly echoic/imitative of a knock, or borrowed from Frankish/Germanic *hūrt- 'a battering ram; to butt, ram'; no secure PIE etymon has been established) → Middle English hurten 'to strike, knock, wound' (attested from c. 1200; borrowed via Anglo-Norman; source also of English hurt 'to injure', whose 'pain' sense grew out of 'to hit') → Middle English hurtelen 'to strike against, clash, dash, collide' (attested from c. 1290; a frequentative formation in -el, the suffix of repeated or intensified action, built on hurten) → Early Modern English hurtle 'to move or rush with great violence and speed; to collide' (the intransitive sense of violent flight is well established from the 16th c., alongside the older transitive 'to dash, fling, cause to collide') → English hurtling (present participle of hurtle, used both as a progressive verb and as an attributive adjective; the -ing form in mid-flight) → HURTLING DEBATE / NUANCE: The ultimate origin of Old French hurter is uncertain. The traditional view sees a Germanic loan from a Frankish *hūrt- 'to ram, butt' (related to English to hurtle by inheritance from the same continental contact), while other scholars prefer an imitative, sound-symbolic origin — the verb capturing the thud of collision. Both hypotheses are live; neither has been proved, and there is no reliable PIE ancestor. What is certain is that English hurt, hurl, and hurtle are all, ultimately, different continuations of this single Old French verb: hurl and hurtle are sister frequentatives, and their modern split (throw vs. violent flight) is an English-internal development.
