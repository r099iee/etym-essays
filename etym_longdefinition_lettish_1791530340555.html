Lettish is an English adjective and noun that historically named the Latvian language, the Latvian people, and the culture of Latvia. It belongs to the vocabulary of older British, German-influenced, and comparative-philological writing rather than to modern official usage. Since the founding of the Republic of Latvia in 1918, and especially since the Second World War, English writers have generally preferred Latvian; Lettish now survives mainly in historical, bibliographic, and linguistic contexts. It is thus a dated exonym with a precise referent, not the name of a separate language or a distinct ethnic group.

The primary sense: the language. In its central use, Lettish denotes the East Baltic language spoken in Latvia, the official language of the Republic of Latvia and one of the official languages of the European Union. It has roughly 1.5 million native speakers in Latvia and perhaps 100,000 more abroad. Latvian is closely related to Lithuanian, but the two are not mutually intelligible: they preserve different archaic features and have followed separate paths since the break-up of Proto-East-Baltic. Within Latvian, the three traditional dialect areas are Livonian, Middle, and High Latvian or Latgalian; the modern standard is based mainly on the Middle dialect, while Latgalian has its own written tradition and is sometimes treated as a separate language. The name Lettish was once used for all of these varieties and occasionally, in older sources, specifically for Latgalian.

Secondary senses: person and adjective. As a count noun, a Lettish (more commonly a Lett) meant a Latvian person; as a mass noun, the Lettish could mean the Latvian people collectively. The adjective described anything belonging to Latvia or its inhabitants: Lettish folklore, Lettish peasants, Lettish songs, the Lettish coast. In nineteenth-century travel writing and ethnography these phrases carried a neutral, descriptive, sometimes slightly exotic tone. The corresponding adjective Lettic and the combining form Letto- (as in Letto-Lithuanian) belong to the same family. None of these forms implies contempt; their effect today is simply antiquarian or old-fashioned.

Tertiary and technical senses. In comparative philology, Lettish was used for the eastern branch of Baltic, as opposed to Lithuanian and the extinct Old Prussian. The phrase Old Lettish refers to the earliest written Latvian, from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, including the Lord's Prayer printed in Sebastian Münster's Cosmographia (1544), the Catholic catechism of 1585, and the Bible translation associated with Ernst Glück (1685-1694). Library catalogues and older encyclopaedias still contain headings such as Lettish language or Lettish literature, now normally cross-referenced to Latvian. The word also appears in the history of the Baltic German nobility, who called the peasant language Lettisch and the province Lettland.

Modern usage and the semantic bridge. The shift from Lettish to Latvian is a clear case of endonymic standardization. Nineteenth-century Latvian national awakening writers promoted latviešu valoda, literally "the language of the Latvians", and after independence the new state's English-language diplomacy used Latvia and Latvian. Lettish was left to older books, emigre publications, and etymological dictionaries. It is not a slur, but it can sound redolent of Baltic German administration, Tsarist ethnography, or Cold War library practice. In present-day corpora it is rare; a careful writer uses it only when quoting an older source or explaining the older name. The semantic bridge is therefore from an external, German-mediated label for a Baltic peasantry to the modern national language name, with the older term receding into the status of a historical synonym.

  • Language sense - the Latvian language, especially in older English usage.
  • Ethnic sense - the Latvian people, or a Latvian individual (now usually Latvian or Lett).
  • Adjectival sense - of or relating to Latvia, its language, or its culture.
  • Philological sense - the East Baltic branch or its older written monuments.
  • Modern status - archaic or dated; replaced by Latvian in official and general use.
