- Verb 'wound' (past tense of 'wind' /waʊnd/)
- Early Germanic/Old English (windan) - Described the physical act of turning, twisting, coiling, or moving in a circuitous path. Connotation was practical and descriptive, relating to weaving, rope-making, or natural phenomena like rivers.
- Middle English (winden, past tense wounde/wound) - Continued the descriptive use for movement and coiling. The shift in past tense form from 'wand' to 'wound' began to solidify, reflecting ongoing phonetic changes and regularization tendencies in English verbs.
- Modern English (wound) - Retains the core meaning of twisting, coiling, or following a non-linear course. Its connotation remains largely neutral and descriptive, used for geographical features, mechanical actions (winding a clock), or intricate movements. The perception is of natural flow or deliberate construction.
- Noun/Verb 'wound' (injury /wuːnd/)
- Old English (wund noun, wundian verb) - Referred directly to physical injury, a break in the skin or flesh, often associated with battle, accidents, or violence. Connotation was stark, painful, and often life-threatening.
- Middle English (wounde noun, wounden verb) - Maintained the literal sense of physical injury. Began to develop early metaphorical extensions to emotional pain, reflecting a growing capacity for abstract thought in the language. Societal perception was still heavily tied to physical suffering and the dangers of life.
- Early Modern English (Shakespearean era) - Figurative use for emotional or psychological hurt became more common and sophisticated in literature. The word gained a deeper emotional register, moving beyond mere physical trauma to encompass the pain of betrayal, grief, or insult.
- Modern English (wound) - Continues to be used for both physical injuries and, very prominently, for emotional, psychological, or spiritual pain. The connotation is often one of deep, lasting damage, whether visible or invisible. Societal perception acknowledges both the medical reality of physical wounds and the profound impact of emotional ones, with a growing emphasis on mental health and healing from non-physical 'wounds'.
