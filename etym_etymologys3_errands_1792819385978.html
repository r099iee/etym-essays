The history of errands is the history of humble service made into a word — a term that began with kings and angels and ended with shopping lists, without ever losing the thread that connects them.

Germanic foundations: The reconstructed Proto-Germanic form *airundija- already meant something like a message or mission — business entrusted by one person to another. Its relatives across the Germanic languages confirm the antiquity: Old High German ārunti meant a message or embassy, Old Norse eyrendi (modern Icelandic erindi) meant a mission or errand, and Old Saxon had its own cognate. What the ultimate pre-Germanic source was remains honestly uncertain — older dictionaries sometimes guessed at links with words for going or ploughing, but modern etymologists decline to force the connection, and the responsible chain starts with the Germanic noun itself rather than an invented root.

Old English dignity: In Anglo-Saxon England ǣrende was no trifle. Laws, homilies, and poems use it for messages between kings, for embassies, and for divine commissions — Christ and the apostles bear ǣrende in religious verse. The word belonged to the world of loyalty and lordship: to carry a man’s ǣrende was to be his trusted mouthpiece, and to fail in it was dishonour. Alongside it stood the messenger himself, the ǣrendwreca or ǣrendfæst, titles with real standing.

  • Embassy and scripture: royal messengers and biblical angels alike carry ǣrende — the word moves comfortably between court and church.
  • Trust as substance: the errand is inseparable from the trust placed in the bearer, a theme that survives faintly in the modern phrase errand of mercy.
  • No certain PIE root: unlike many Germanic words, errand cannot be securely traced beyond the Germanic family, and scholarship rightly leaves the gap open.

Middle English broadening: After the Norman Conquest the word lived on in English while the court spoke French, and it broadened downward: alongside embassies it now covered pilgrimages, merchants’ business, lovers’ messages, and servants’ fetchings. Chaucer’s world is full of characters sent on erandes of every rank. Sound changes regularised the form toward erand and then errand, with the doubled r settling in spelling.

Early modern mockery and morality: The sixteenth century gave the word its most famous phrase, the fool’s errand — the prank mission on which apprentices and greenhorns were sent for imaginary goods. At the same time moralists used errand for life’s purpose itself: man’s errand on earth, the soul’s errand. High and low, the word stretched to cover both cosmic purpose and practical joke.

Modern domestication: By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the solemn senses had retreated into fixed phrases and the plural had conquered everyday use. To run errands — for the household, the office, the neighbour — became a universal experience of childhood and adult routine alike, motorised but structurally unchanged: a list, a round, a ticking-off. Tolkien’s line, where many paths and errands meet, deliberately reaches back past the shopping list to the older sense, letting the word carry both its ancient commission and its modern humble round in a single breath.
