1. The Latin base: cadere, "to fall". At the root of decadence stands Latin cadere, "to fall, sink, die", one of the most productive verbs of the Latin lexicon (source of case, casualty, accident, cadence, chance). Its pre-Latin history is genuinely disputed: no secure Indo-European reconstruction commands universal assent, and serious comparative scholars (de Vaan among them) treat the word's deeper etymology as uncertain, with suggestions ranging from a root connected to the Celtic casad to a substrate origin. The honest statement is that cadere is the attested starting-point of this word's line; beyond Latin, etymology is speculation. From cadere, Latin formed the transparent compound dēcadere, "to fall away, fall down, decline" (with the privative-downward prefix de-), attested in classical Latin prose.

2. Medieval Latin: the noun crystallises. The noun dēcadentia is the creation of the Latin of the learned and of the church, attested from the twelfth–thirteenth century onward with the meaning "decadence, deterioration, falling away" — a formation in the abstract-noun pattern (-antia, from the present participle, cf. cadentia "a falling"). At this stage the word is a general moral-administrative abstraction; it has not yet attached itself to any particular history.

3. French makes it a word of history. The word entered Middle French as décadence in the sixteenth century and was given its long life by French historiography. Montaigne uses it of the falling state; but the decisive text is Montesquieu's Considérations sur les causes de la grandeur des Romains et de leur décadence (1734), "Reflections on the causes of the grandeur and decadence of the Romans", which canonised the idea that empires have organic life-cycles — youth, maturity, and decadence — an analytical framework that Gibbon then made English and world-famous. From that point "decadence" is the technical word for the terminal phase of civilisation, bound up with luxury, corruption, effeminacy, and the triumph of pleasure over duty.

4. English adoption. English borrowed the word in the 1540s (first records are mid-sixteenth century), at first in the religious-political sense of decay and falling off. For three centuries it is a sober noun of moral history and homiletics: the decadence of the Church, of states, of manners. The alternative form decadency competed in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries in legal, ecclesiastical, and American usage and then faded, leaving the French-shaped decadence triumphant.

5. The Decadent Movement (1880s–1890s). The late nineteenth century performs the word's great reversal of sign. In France, poets gathered round the idea of decadence as a style rather than a failure: Verlaine's sonnet "Langueur" (1883) opens famously "Je suis l'Empire à la fin de la décadence" — "I am the Empire at the end of its decadence" — and embraces the role; Huysmans's À rebours (1884) makes the decadent sensibility a complete programme of artifice; the journal Le Décadent appeared in 1886. In England the movement arrived as the aestheticism of the 1890s: Arthur Symons's essay The Decadent Movement in Literature (1893), Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890), the notoriously daring The Yellow Book (1894–97), and Beardsley's art. The word becomes double-edged: an insult from the pulpit, a badge of honour among the avant-garde. Pater's "refined and comely decadence" (his Renaissance was published 1873, second edition 1877, enlarged 1888) is the missing bridge — he finds the decadent aesthetic already embodied in the late Renaissance, and his sympathy made him, retrospectively, the English movement's godfather.

6. Nietzsche and the verdict of modern thought. On the philosophical side, Friedrich Nietzsche made decadence a central term of cultural diagnosis (chiefly in the notes published as The Will to Power and in The Case of Wagner, 1888), declaring Wagner's art and Christian morality alike decadent — the word as a scalpel against everything that weakens life. The twentieth century thereafter used "decadence" freely in cultural criticism: Spengler's The Decline of the West (1918), the "decadent West" of Soviet polemic, and the intermittent jeremiads of every civilisation against its own over-refinement. The word's polarity remained unstable: it is always an accusation, and always, to the accused, half a compliment.

7. The lightening of the word (20th–21st c.). Post-war consumer culture domesticated the adjective: "decadent" became the cliché of the food pages and the travel pages — a decadent dessert, a decadent weekend — meaning luxurious, indulgently rich, guiltily pleasant. The moral fall has been replaced by a price tag. The noun, however, has kept its gravity, and continues to serve historians, moralists, and art critics for the twilight of ages and styles. Few words carry such a complete inversions-history: from "falling" in Latin, to the fall of Rome, to the badge of Wilde's circle, to the chocolate truffle.
