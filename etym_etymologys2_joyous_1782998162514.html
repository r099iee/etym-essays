The English word JOYOUS is built from two elements, joy and the suffix -ous; the chain below follows the base joy to its PIE root and then shows the suffix separately. Chain for the element JOY: 1. PIE *gau- (also reconstructed as *gau-/*gu-, "to rejoice, take pleasure in") — not directly attested; reconstructed from cognates such as Greek ganos "delight, brightness" and the verb gaio "I rejoice" (Homeric), c. 4th millennium BCE. 2. Latin gaudere "to rejoice, take pleasure in, delight" (attested 3rd-2nd c. BCE in Plautus and Terence). 3. Latin gaudium "joy, gladness, delight" (neuter noun derived from gaudere; attested in Cicero, 1st c. BCE). 4. Latin gaudia (originally the neuter plural of gaudium, "joys") — in Late/Vulgar Latin (ca. 4th-7th c. CE) this plural was reinterpreted as a feminine singular noun, the source of the Romance words for "joy". 5. Old French joie "joy, gladness, delight" (9th-11th c.; from *gaudia, with monophthongization of /au/ to /o/ and palatalization of /g/ to /dʒ/ before the front vowel). 6. Old French joios / joious / joyous "joyous, happy, full of joy" (11th c.; joie + the suffix -os, from Latin -osus "full of"). 7. Middle English joyous "joyful, glad, happy" (attested c. 1300, e.g. in the Cursor Mundi, c. 1325; borrowed via Anglo-Norman). 8. Modern English JOYOUS (continuous from the 14th century). Chain for the suffix -OUS: 1. Latin -osus "full of, abounding in" (e.g. glorio-sus "glorious," vino-sus "full of wine"; Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE). 2. Old French -os / -ous / -eus (from Latin -osus; 9th-12th c.). 3. Middle English -ous / -us (13th-15th c.). 4. Modern English -ous, attached to joy to form JOYOUS. Debate note: the ultimate source of the Latin suffix -osus is disputed. Some scholars compare it with the PIE adjective-forming element *-h3es-/*-os-, while others see a composite of *-o- plus *-so-; no certain PIE reconstruction is available, so the suffix is best described as Latinate without forcing a speculative PIE stage.
