Bisected is the past tense and past participle of the verb bisect, which means to divide into two parts — and, in its strict and primary mathematical sense, into two equal parts. The word is built transparently from Latin bi- ("two, double") and the participle sectus ("cut"), so that to bisect is literally "to cut in two." In the sentence "It was bisected by a long, low table piled with books and tea things," the verb performs a precise act of domestic geometry: a room is cut into two halves by a single piece of furniture, and the table becomes the axis around which the whole space is organized — "long" because its line must span the room to make the cut, "low" so that it divides without obstructing. The effect is one of order, proportion, and balance: the description radiates the quiet discipline of a room governed by a single central line.

  • Core mathematical sense. In geometry, to bisect is to divide an angle, a line segment, a chord, or a plane figure into two equal parts. A diameter bisects a circle; a median bisects a triangle's area; the angle bisector is the ray that cuts an angle into two congruent halves. This is the sense in which the word entered English in the 1640s and the sense that still anchors its meaning: bisection is an exact, ratio-preserving operation, not a rough splitting.
  • Transfer to physical space and geography. From mathematics the verb spread to describe anything that cleanly divides a surface or space into two: a river bisecting a valley, a road or highway bisecting a field, a wall bisecting a room, an avenue bisecting a city. In this extended sense the "equality" of the two parts is often only approximate or rhetorical — a road rarely divides a valley into equal halves — but the word still insists on a single decisive line of division, a cut that runs the full way across. This is the sense of the passage: the table bisects the room, whatever the exact proportions of the two resulting halves.
  • Anatomical and biological use. In biology and anatomy, "bisected" describes organs, specimens, or whole bodies cut into two (usually symmetrical) halves, especially for examination or illustration. A bisected heart, a bisected brain, a bisected flower show internal structure by being opened along a single plane.
  • Figurative and literary use. Metaphorically, anything divided into two sharply separated parts can be said to be bisected — a career "bisected by the war," a society "bisected by a river," a life "bisected into before and after." Here the word's exactness does rhetorical work: it promises not mere division but a decisive, clean line of separation, often with a faintly dramatic or ominous charge.
  • Registered nuance. Bisect is more formal, technical, and clinical than its everyday alternatives. It claims precision and often implies equality or symmetry, where "divide" is neutral and "split" suggests force or raggedness. Its tone is surgical — the cut is decisive but serene, without violence.

Etymology and anatomy of the word. The verb is a New Latin coinage of the seventeenth century, formed from bis "twice" and sectus, the past participle of secare "to cut." It entered English mathematics as the precise verb for dividing angles and lines into equal parts, and from that classroom kept its association with exactness, order, and straight lines. The passage's effect depends entirely on that heritage: the table does not "divide" the room as a wall might, or "split" it as a fault-line might — it bisects it, imposing a geometric order that turns the room into two balanced answering halves, a domestic reflection of the couplet's geometry of proportion and harmony.

Discriminations. "Divided" is the neutral all-purpose verb, with no guarantee of equality or straightness. "Split" implies force, a break along a natural seam, and often leaves uneven parts. "Partitioned" implies the interposition of a wall or barrier that encloses one space into two chambers. "Intersected" describes two things crossing each other, each continuing past the other. "Dissected" means cut apart for examination, into many parts, not necessarily two. "Bifurcated" means divided into two forks or branches — used of rivers, roads, and tubes, not of a single clean cut across a whole surface. "Halved" is the everyday word for division into two equal parts, lacking the geometric formality of "bisected." Only "bisected" combines the idea of two with the idea of a single straight cut, which is why it is the exact word for the table's work in the passage.
