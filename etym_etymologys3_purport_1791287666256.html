Purport enters English as a French borrowing built from Latin bricks. Old French purporter meant "to contain, to carry along, to convey," formed from pur- (a northern French form of pour < Latin pro "forth, forward") + porter "to carry" (< Latin portare "to carry, bear"). To purport was thus literally "to carry forth" a meaning, as a vessel carries contents. Latin portare itself continues PIE *per- "to lead, pass over, carry through," also seen in port, import, transport, porter, and fare.

In Anglo-Norman and early Middle English purporten (late 14th century) first meant what a document "carries" or "holds": its tenor, substance, or effect. Legal and parliamentary rolls are full of it: the purport of a charter, the purport of a petition. From contents the sense slid to intention: what the writer meant to carry across. By the 15th-16th centuries the verb had developed the crucial modern duality: neutral "to mean, to signify" and suspicious "to profess, to claim (falsely or ostensibly)." To purport to be a doctor could be true or pretended; context decided.

Early Modern English multiplied uses. As a noun (stressed on the first syllable, /ˈpɜːpɔːt/) it meant gist, drift, or substance, especially of speech or writing: "the purport of his remarks." As a verb (stressed on the second, /pəˈpɔːt/) it meant to have as its meaning and, increasingly, to lay claim to a false character: "a letter purporting to come from the king." The law loved the word because it distinguished ostensible from proven: forged wills, counterfeit orders, and disputed confessions purported to be what they were not.

  • Phonetic development: Latin pro + portare > purporter [purporˈter] with northern French rounding of pro to pur; Middle English borrowing preserves /u/ as pur-; modern stress split (noun initial, verb final) follows the regular English noun-verb stress alternation (record, import, transport).
  • Morphological stability: no further affixation; purport remains a bare stem used as both noun and verb, with regular inflections purports, purported, purporting.
  • Semantic drift: to carry contents > what a text contains > what it means > what it claims to mean > what someone falsely claims to be. The bridge is the gap between carrier and contents: a container can mislabel its goods.
  • Socio-cultural context: the rise of bureaucracy, diplomacy, and print made purport indispensable for summarizing documents; the rise of forgery, espionage, and journalism made its suspicious sense equally indispensable.
  • Literary attestations: parliamentary rolls ("the purport of the bill") from the 1380s; Shakespeare uses purport for meaning and intention; 18th-century newspapers for letters purporting to be from gentlemen; modern news style guides still prescribe "purported" for unverified claims.

Modern usage keeps noun and verb sharply distinct but related. The noun is almost always neutral and formal: the purport of a message is its essential meaning, equivalent to import or gist but more official. The verb is often wary: to purport to be X invites doubt, equivalent to claim or profess, and is a favourite of lawyers and journalists who must report an allegation without endorsing it.

The source sentence "I did not get the purport" uses the noun in its classic sense: I did not grasp the gist, the point being carried. The humour or pathos lies in failed carriage: something was carried forth, but it did not arrive.
