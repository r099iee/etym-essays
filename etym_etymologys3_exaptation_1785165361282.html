Exaptation is one of the youngest words in this batch, and its entire biography can be dated: it was coined in a single paper, "Exaptation — a Missing Term in the Science of Form," by Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba, published in the journal Paleobiology in 1982. Yet the word is built from two very ancient materials, and the story of how they came together is itself a study in the recycling the term describes. The prefix ex-, "out of, from," descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h1eghs, which appears across the family: Greek ek/ex (as in eclipse, "a leaving-out" of the sun), Latin ex (as in exclude, exit), Old Irish ess-, and the Slavic iz, "out of." The second half reaches back further still: Latin aptus, "fitted, joined, suited," is the past participle of the Latin verb apiscor, "to reach, attain, grasp," which descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h2ep-, "to grasp, reach, fasten." That root has remarkable descendants: Sanskrit ap-, "to reach, obtain"; Greek haptein, "to fasten, touch, kindle," which lies inside synapse — the junction where neurons "join together." From aptus Latin built the verb aptare, "to fit," and from that, with the prefix ad-, "toward," the verb adaptare, "to fit to." Adaptare crossed into English through French as adapt in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and adaptation settled into the language of natural history, where by the nineteenth century it had become one of the master-words of evolutionary thought.

By the twentieth century, adaptation had hardened into orthodoxy: on the strict adaptationist reading, every feature of every organism is what it is because selection shaped it for its current role, and the business of the biologist is to find the function that explains each trait. It was against this programme that Gould and Vrba wrote. The older term preadaptation — used for a trait that would one day prove useful — struck them as doubly wrong: it implied that evolution could look ahead and design a part for a function it had not yet acquired, smuggling a kind of foresight into the Darwinian machine. Their 1982 paper proposed a cleaner vocabulary. Let aptation be the umbrella term for any fit between organism and environment, whether or not selection produced it. Then an adaptation is an aptation built by selection for its present function, and an exaptation is an aptation now serving a function other than the one that shaped it — whether it began as an adaptation (feathers, insulation, then flight) or as a non-adaptive spandrel (a structural byproduct later seized on). The ex- prefix does precise grammatical work: it names the drawing of a feature out of its original role, exactly as exclude names the shutting out of an intruder. The coinage was deliberately analogical, and the authors were explicit that the term's value lay in separating the history of a trait's origin from its current utility — the very distinction the present passage draws between "speech processing and threat detection" and "art, story, and bonding."

The coinage succeeded immediately, and its later career illustrates the very process it names. Within a decade exaptation was standard in the evolutionary literature, used for the classic cases — feathers, the jawbones of the mammalian middle ear, the panda's pseudo-thumb, the human chin — and it became a banner of the anti-adaptationist turn of the 1980s and 1990s, sitting beside spandrel as a reminder that evolution recycles parts without foresight. From paleobiology the word spread outward, as so many biological terms have: into evolutionary psychology, where cognitive machinery is described as exapted for language, music, and religion; into linguistics, where Roger Lass and others used it for the recycling of obsolete grammatical material; and into archaeology and the study of culture, where artifacts and institutions are said to be exapted for new social functions. In the twenty-first century the word has loosened further, becoming a fashionable term in technology and design for the repurposing of tools and platforms — an "exapted" technology is one whose best use was never intended by its maker. Each of these borrowings keeps the original kernel: function after the fact, without design in advance. The word has thus done in culture what it describes in nature — an old instrument taken up for a new job, its popularity outrunning the biology that coined it. In the present passage it is used in exactly the 1982 sense, and the gloss offered — "a mechanism that evolved for one basic purpose... that got hijacked for another" — reproduces Gould and Vrba's central claim with remarkable fidelity.
