- c. 1300 — the brave knight: Middle English borrows Old French estout meaning bold, proud, and fierce — a word of chivalry and battle, describing warriors rather than waistlines.
- 14th–15th c. — fierce and formidable: stout describes strong men, hard blows, and valiant resistance; Chaucer's age knows nothing of fat stoutness, only of stout hearts and stout arms.
- 16th c. — from spirit to body: the brave man is imagined as big-framed, and the sense drifts toward strongly built, thick-limbed, sturdy — Shakespeare's stout fellows are both courageous and broad.
- 17th–18th c. — the sturdy object: boots, ships, walls, and beer go stout; the drink sense (strong porter) fixes stout as a noun in every tavern in England.
- 19th c. — the kindly euphemism: as fat grows ruder, stout becomes the polite word for corpulence — tailors advertise stout sizes, and the moral sense retreats into literature.
- Today — solid and dependable: stout praises whatever holds firm — stout shoes, stout defence, stout fellows — warm, old-fashioned, faintly heroic, with Tolkien's Maggot as its perfect portrait.
