- Late 17th to mid-18th century: Pungent Maritime Import - Encountered by British East India Company traders in Southeast Asia as an exotic fermented fish or brine seasoning; perceived as a rare, pungent, oriental culinary luxury for flavoring bland naval rations.
- Mid-18th to early 19th century: Domestic English Extract - Adapted across British and colonial American households into concentrated homemade table liquors pressed from salted mushrooms, green walnuts, oysters, or anchovies; prized in Georgian dining as an indispensable umami base for gravies and roasted meats.
- 1800s to 1870s: The American Tomato Transformation - Re-engineered in the early United States by blending native tomatoes with vinegar and spices, shifting in public perception from a medicinal tonic to a beloved domestic staple.
- 1880s to 1980s: Industrial Packaged Condiment - Standardized by industrial food processors and marketed prominently under the label 'Catsup' (notably by Del Monte and regional packers); viewed as an everyday, democratic American table staple ubiquitous in lunchrooms and diners.
- 1980s to Present: Regional and Nostalgic Variant - Progressively supplanted in commercial packaging and mainstream marketing by the spelling 'ketchup', leaving 'catsup' perceived as a charmingly vintage, retro, or regional dialectal spelling.
