Catiline is a proper noun, the English form of the Latin cognomen Catilīna, borne most famously by Lucius Sergius Catilina (c. 108–62 BCE), the Roman patrician who plotted to overthrow the Republic in 63 BCE. English uses the name in two connected ways. Historically it denotes that man, his followers and his conspiracy; rhetorically it denotes a type — the well-born, debt-ridden revolutionary who turns against the state that raised him. The source context uses the historical sense: Cicero, as consul, “suppressed the uprising headed by Catiline”.

The name is not a neutral label. In the Latin historians and in English political writing it carries the charge of aristocratic decadence, reckless debt and a willingness to burn the city for power. To call a politician “a Catiline” is to accuse him of plotting against the constitution, usually from inside the governing class. This double life — individual and emblem — is why the word still appears in histories of Rome, in school curricula and in political invective.

Who the name denotes

  • Family and background: born c. 108 BCE into the patrician gens Sergia, one of Rome's oldest houses, but into a branch that had not produced a consul for generations; the family's relative poverty and its Sullan connections shaped his career.
  • Public career: served under Sulla in the civil war of the 80s BCE; praetor in 68 BCE; governor of Africa in 67–66 BCE; returned to stand for the consulate but was barred after a prosecution for extortion, a humiliation that fed his grievance.
  • The conspiracy of 63 BCE: he gathered indebted aristocrats, Sullan veterans and dispossessed farmers; the plan combined arson in Rome, the murder of Cicero and leading senators, and an armed rising in Etruria under Gaius Manlius.
  • Detection: the Allobrogian envoys, approached for cavalry, informed against him; sealed letters were intercepted at the Milvian Bridge; the Senate passed its emergency decree and Cicero had the leading conspirators arrested in Rome.
  • Death: Catiline left the city, took command of the Etrurian force and fell with most of his men at Pistoria (Pistoia) in early 62 BCE, fighting in the front line, as Sallust famously has it.

How a surname became a political type

Sallust's Bellum Catilinae (c. 42–40 BCE) turned the episode into moral history, setting Catiline's ambition against the austerity of Cato and the indecision of the Senate. Cicero's four Catilinarian Orations — above all the First, with its opening “Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra?” — fixed the name in the schoolroom as the paradigm of sedition. From the sixteenth century English writers used “a Catiline” for any suspected plotter, and “Catilinarian” for the speeches, the conspiracy and its participants.

Senses and uses in modern English

  • The individual: Lucius Sergius Catilina, Roman senator and conspirator of 63 BCE.
  • The type-noun (literary): “a Catiline”, a would-be tyrant or domestic traitor, especially one of noble birth and ruined fortune.
  • The attributive uses: “Catilinarian conspiracy”, “Catilinarian oration”, “Catiline's revolt”, “the Catiline affair”.
  • The academic sense: a set text in Latin syllabi and a case study in Roman Republican politics and in Cicero's self-fashioning.
  • The dramatic sense: the title of plays and operas, from Ben Jonson's Catiline His Conspiracy (1611) to Ibsen's Catilina (1850).

Register, connotation and collocations

The word is strongly pejorative and slightly archaic. It connotes patrician arrogance, financial ruin and the readiness to use violence against one's own city; it is not applied to ordinary criminals or to foreign enemies. Typical collocations are “the Catilinarian conspiracy”, “Catiline's conspiracy”, “Cicero and Catiline”, “a second Catiline”. In political rhetoric the name implies an inside job: the enemy is a senator, not a barbarian.

Why it matters in this reading

The passage is a capsule biography of Cicero: at forty-three, as consul, he crushed Catiline's rising, delivered the Philippics, and was hailed Father of the Country. Catiline is the foil that makes the consulate the summit of Cicero's career. The summary executions that followed — the conspirators strangled in the Tullianum — became the charge that drove Cicero into exile in 58 BCE. The name therefore marks both his greatest triumph and the legal ambiguity that undid him.

The name itself

Catilīna is a cognomen, the third element of the Roman name, distinguishing a branch of the Sergii. Its morphology is only partly transparent: the ending -īna is a common Latin name-forming suffix (compare Latīnus, Secundīnus), but the stem Catil- has no agreed Latin etymology. Ancient writers gave the Sergii a Trojan ancestor, Sergestus, a companion of Aeneas; that is genealogical myth, not linguistic evidence. The cautious modern view treats the cognomen as Etruscan or otherwise pre-Latin, and therefore of unknown meaning. English Catiline is a Renaissance Latinism in which Latin -a has been replaced by English -e.
