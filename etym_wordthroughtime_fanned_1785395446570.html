- [Winnowing origin (Old English fann)] - A fan was a winnowing basket; to 'fan' grain meant tossing it so the wind lifted the chaff, a purely agricultural, functional act.
- [Domestic air-stirring (late medieval)] - Extended from winnowing to any hand-held fan or blowing motion; the verb lost its barnyard sense and became a household word for cooling a face or feeding a fire with air.
- [Figurative stirring (16th-17th c.)] - 'To fan the flames' turned the verb abstract: rousing anger, hope, or desire; public perception shifted from neutral mechanics to deliberate incitement.
- [Radiating spread 'fanned out' (18th-19th c.)] - Alongside the fire sense, the shape sense grew: feathers, cards, and peacock trains 'fanned out' into semicircular arrays, giving the participle its decorative, geometrical meaning.
- [Modern register] - Today 'fanned' is most alive in the flame sense (fanned controversy, fanned embers) and the shape sense; the winnowing past is forgotten, but the participle still carries its deliberate, instrumental, escalating energy.
