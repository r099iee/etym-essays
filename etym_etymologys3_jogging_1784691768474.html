Jogging begins in the sound of motion itself. Middle English shoggen, recorded around 1400, meant to shake or jolt, to move something with a jerk, and it belongs to a family of expressive, onomatopoeic verbs — words that imitate the short, percussive movement they describe, the sh- / j- + vowel + -g / -k pattern that English shares across its history (shake, shove, jolt, jounce, jostle). Shoggen may itself be related to Old English sceacan, "to shake", and it survived in English dialect as shog, "to shake", and in the phrase "to shog along", "to move slowly on one's way". In the 1540s English produced jog, either as an alteration of shoggen or as a parallel formation, meaning first "to shake up and down" and "to nudge" — the sense that survives in "to jog one's memory". The frequentative joggle followed in the same century, shaking with small repeated motions. The deeper ancestry of the family is unrecoverable: these are phonesthetic creations, not descendants of a reconstructable Indo-European root, and honest etymology must simply say so. From the physical shake the word stepped sideways into locomotion. By the seventeenth century a horse that moved with a little bouncing trot was said to jog, and the phrasal verbs jog off, jog along, and jog on meant to depart or proceed at an easy trot; Shakespeare's Autolycus sang "Jog on, jog on, the foot-path way" in The Winter's Tale (c. 1610), catching the light, rhythmical sound of the word forever in English verse. The noun jogtrot arose for the steady, monotonous trot, and then, by metaphor, for any humdrum routine of life — the "jogtrot of existence" that carries a man through his days — while the verbal noun jogging described this easy travelling and remained an equestrian and pedestrian word for two hundred years, exactly the sense in which Tolkien's dwarves "jog off" from the Green Dragon Inn. The equestrian world is where the word lived longest: from the seventeenth through the nineteenth centuries, jogging named the unhurried, bouncing progress of horse and rider along country roads, and no other sense competed with it. In the twentieth century the word performed an extraordinary transfer. From 1948, jogging began to be used in English — first in New Zealand — for running slowly and steadily for exercise: the human runner took over the trot of the horse, the jog-trot became the morning jog, and by the late 1960s jogging was a mass pursuit, spread by coaches such as New Zealand's Arthur Lydiard, who trained champions on steady aerobic road running, and by books like Bill Bowerman's and W. E. Harris's Jogging (1966), which sold hundreds of thousands of copies and launched the American jogging boom. The word's older senses never died — horses still jog, memory is still jogged, and the "jogtrot" of routine still plods on — but the modern citizen's jog is now the sense most people first think of. The old sense, however, is the one Tolkien draws on. When the company "came to start, jogging off from the inn one fine morning just before May, on laden ponies", the word's light, bouncing, two-hundred-year-old rhythm does the work of the whole departure: unceremonious, comfortable, faintly comic, and entirely at ease with itself.
