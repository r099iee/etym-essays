- Lonely persons (14th c.): ME 'desolat' means deprived of comfort or companionship — a learned word from Latin, applied first to people
- Ruined places (15th–16th c.): the word extends to land laid waste and abandoned; the KJV (1611) fixes the biblical register ('your country is desolate', Isaiah 1:7)
- Divine abandonment (17th–18th c.): sermons and epic poetry load 'desolate' and 'desolation' with the terror of God's withdrawal — Milton's 'seat of desolation, void of light'
- The sublime wasteland (19th c.): Romantic and Victorian writers make desolation awesome — empty mountains, moors and ruins inspire awe as well as grief
- Today: for places, 'empty, bleak, lifeless'; for people, 'utterly alone, inconsolable'; the verb 'to desolate' is rare and literary; ecology uses it for lifeless habitats — all senses still radiate from Latin solus, 'alone'
