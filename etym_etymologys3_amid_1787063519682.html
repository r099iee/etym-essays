A root that sits at the centre of everything. Amid descends from one of the most far-reaching Indo-European roots, *medhyo- (“middle”), the same root that produced Latin medius and English middle, mid, midst, and mediæval. In the Germanic branch it yielded Proto-Germanic *midjaz (“middle”) and Old English midd, an adjective whose dative forms supplied the building blocks of a phrase that would one day harden into a preposition.

The fusion of on middan. Old English expressed “in the middle of” with the prepositional phrase on middan — literally “in the middle”, with middan the dative of midd. Over the Old English and early Middle English periods this phrase eroded: the on reduced to a mere a-, and the whole fused into a single word. By the 13th century the preposition amidde (later amid) was an established member of English grammar, meaning “in the middle of, among”. A parallel development produced amidst, with an excrescent -st added by analogy with other prepositions and adverbs such as whilst and amongst.

Medieval and early modern space. For most of its history amid was a preposition of physical and social space. A knight stood amid his enemies; a hermit dwelt amid the woods; a saint appeared amid the assembly. The word placed its object at the centre of a surrounding mass — enclosed, flanked, or, in the social sense, indistinguishable among a crowd. Chaucer, Malory, and the makers of Middle English romance use it freely; it is a perfectly ordinary literary preposition, with amidst preferred in the most elevated or archaic-sounding passages.

From space to time to atmosphere. The great drift of the modern word is the move from space to circumstance. From the 17th century onward amid increasingly places an event inside a period or process: things happen “amid the war”, “amid the rejoicing”, “amid the growing panic”. The preposition’s object shifts from a physical surround to a temporal, emotional, or institutional one. This is the development that powers the modern journalistic formula:

  • “amid reports of...” — the event takes place in an atmosphere of rumour and disclosure.
  • “amid growing concern...” — the event is framed by a rising collective mood.
  • “amid chaos / celebration / protests...” — the event is set within an ongoing state of affairs.

Amid and amidst today. In modern English the two forms remain synonymous, but their registers have split. Amid is the plain, efficient preposition of journalism, headlines, and everyday prose — short, neutral, and tirelessly productive. Amidst carries a poetic, antique, or mannered flavour and survives in verse, in historical novels, and in the high style of writers like Tolkien, whose Middle-earth prose alternates comfortably between the two. The near-synonyms among (used with plural countable members) and between (used for paired or separated things) have never displaced amid for the simple job of “in the middle of” a mass or a moment.

Sound and form. Amid is a single unstressed-then-stressed syllable, /əˈmɪd/, the same vowel as in mid — a word that is, etymologically, its closest relation. Its shortness and its soft centre suit its function: the word slips an object into the middle of a scene without any fuss, which is exactly what it has done in English for almost eight hundred years.
