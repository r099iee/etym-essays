Evolution of "drat." Few words carry so much history in so small a package. Drat is a curse that has been through the wash: three hundred years ago it was a genuine imprecation calling divine rot upon an enemy, and today it is a comic, harmless syllable of stage vexation. Its history is the history of English swearing, of the euphemism, and of the theatre, all compressed into one beat of four letters.

1. The parent phrase: "God rot him." The story begins not with a word but with a phrase, the imprecation "God rot (him)!", "may God cause him to decay," a curse in the same family as "God confound him" and "God damn him," calling a divine punishment of corruption and putrefaction upon the offender. In the 17th and 18th centuries such oaths were common enough in speech but sat awkwardly between religion and politeness: the profane use of the divine name was itself a sin, and the culture of the drawing room, the stage, and the pulpit all conspired against it. The result was the minced oath, the oath that gestures at profanity without committing to it.

2. The erosion of the curse. Everyday speech did the rest. The phrase "God rot" was clipped in the mouth: the divine name, the most dangerous and most expendable element, fell away first, leaving "'od rot," and the contraction then tightened further. By about 1800 the form "drat" was in use, first recorded in writing between 1800 and 1815. The verb rot had lost its verb, god had lost its god, and what remained was a single explosive syllable that still carried the grammar of an imprecation, "drat him!", but none of the substance. This is the whole art of the minced oath: the speaker keeps the shape and the energy of the curse while emptying it of its meaning, so that the anger is vented and no blasphemy is committed.

3. The Victorian stage and the comic novel. It was the Victorian period that gave drat its permanent character. The music hall, the farce, and the comic novel adopted it as the stock exclamation of the blustering but harmless figure, the uncle, the father, the housekeeper, the tradesman. "Drat it!", "drat the boy!", "drat these stairs!" became the vexation of the inconvenienced and the incompetent, always comic, never threatening. The word's datedness was already beginning to set within a generation of its birth: it was old-fashioned almost as soon as it was current, and the theatre loved it all the more for that. To use "drat" was to put on the costume of the bluff old English character, and the word became a shorthand for period grumbling.

4. The twentieth century: quaintness as function. As spoken profanity loosened across the twentieth century, drat only grew more antique. Writers reached for it precisely because it was no longer live speech: it could signal period, humour, or gentleness in a way that a real swearword never could. Tolkien's use in The Hobbit (1937) is a masterclass in this function. Thorin's "drat him, I say" sits beside the other dwarves' "confusticate him!", and both words mark the dwarves as bluff, homely, hearth-side figures whose fury is entirely theatrical. The word does in the narrative exactly what it had done on the Victorian stage: it lets a character sound angry while guaranteeing that no real harm is meant, and it colours the anger itself as comic and deflated.

5. The contemporary state. Today drat survives as a deliberately quaint mild irritation, euphemistic and comic, the phonetic ghost of a curse. It is confined to the historical novel, the period drama, the costume comedy, and the self-consciously gentle swearer who vents feeling while keeping it deliberately small. Its full etymology, the descent from "God rot" through "'od rot" to "drat," is known to every etymological dictionary and to very few speakers, which suits the word perfectly: it is a disguise that has outlived its secret.

  • Morphological change: the aphesis of God- in "God rot", the contraction of the two remaining syllables, and the re-spelling with short a; no regular sound law is involved, only jocular erosion.
  • Semantic drift: from a genuine imprecation (divine putrefaction), to a mild oath of annoyance, to a comic stage interjection, to a consciously dated, playful exclamation; the force has steadily drained while the gesture has been preserved.
  • Social register: born in colloquial speech, adopted by the theatre and the comic novel, retained as a period marker, and now confined to historical and deliberately old-fashioned usage.
  • Literary attestations: first recorded c. 1800–1815; Victorian farce and comic fiction; Dickens and the novelists of the mid-century; Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937); modern period drama and historical fiction.
