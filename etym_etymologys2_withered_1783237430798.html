PIE *h₂weh₁- "to blow, breathe" — reconstructed (full grade *h₂weh₁-); source of words for wind and weather across the family: Latin ventus "wind", Greek áēmi "I blow" and aḗr "air", Sanskrit vāti "he blows" and vāyu "wind, air", Proto-Germanic *windaz → English wind → PIE *h₂wé-dʰro- "blowing, weather" — reconstructed; the *-dʰro- suffix forms state/instrument nouns ("the blowing state") → Proto-Germanic *wedra- "weather, wind, storm" — reconstructed (neuter *wedram) → Old English weder "weather, storm, sky" — attested c. 8th–11th c. (cognate: English weather, Dutch weder, German Wetter, Old Norse veðr) → Middle English wederen "to expose to the weather, air, dry, season" — attested 14th c.; variant widren / wideren (c. 1300) "to become dry and shrivelled, to waste away" (the drying done by wind and sun) → English wither (v.) "to shrivel, dry up, lose vitality, decay" — attested from c. 1300 (early text: Cursor Mundi); transitive "to cause to shrivel, blast" likewise → WITHERED (past participle → adjective) "shrivelled, shrunken, dried up" — current Note on the chain: wither is etymologically "to weather" — the -dʰro- derivative of the PIE blow/breathe root means the verb and the noun weather are near twins, not distant relatives. The wind family (Latin ventus, Greek aḗr, Sanskrit vāyu, English wind) shows the same root in its "blowing" branch; the weather/wither branch specializes the "state of blowing" into weather, and weather into drying. No stage between PIE and Old English is directly attested; all forms before Old English weder are reconstructions from comparative Germanic and Indo-European evidence.
