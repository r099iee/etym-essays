BEHELD is the past tense and past participle of the verb behold, meaning to see, look at, gaze upon, or perceive, especially with attention, wonder, or reverence. When a speaker exclaims she is the most beautiful creature I ever beheld, the claim is that among all persons the speaker has ever seen or witnessed, none equals her beauty. The word is now distinctly literary, archaic, or biblical in flavour: everyday speech says saw or have seen, while beheld elevates the act of seeing into witnessing, contemplating, or marveling. It implies not a casual glance but a held gaze.

Grammatically beheld serves both simple past (I beheld the sunset) and participial uses (the sight I had beheld, lo, I beheld a new heaven). It survives robustly in hymns, scripture, Shakespeare, fantasy, and romantic hyperbole, and in the fixed imperative behold! meaning look, see, consider. Its register is solemn, poetic, or deliberately old-fashioned; using it in casual conversation creates irony or grandeur.

  • Saw, perceived with the eyes (core literary past): To have seen something, especially something striking. The prefix be- originally intensified hold in the sense of hold with the eyes, keep in view. Thus I beheld her is stronger than I saw her: it suggests the sight arrested attention. Austen's character uses it in amorous hyperbole typical of early-19th-century gallantry.
  • Gazed upon, contemplated, witnessed (attentive sense): To look at steadily and with feeling — wonder, awe, love, horror. Biblical beheld the glory, beheld the city and wept imply spiritual witnessing, not mere optics. Modern fantasy retains this: he beheld the dragon coiled below invites the reader to pause and stare.
  • Regarded, considered, held in mind (archaic mental sense): Old English behealdan also meant to hold, keep, guard, regard, consider. Traces survive in beholden (obliged, indebted — literally held by obligation) and in the imperative behold as a discourse marker meaning consider this, mark my words. The semantic bridge is from holding an object, to holding it in view, to holding it in thought.
  • Experienced, encountered (extended sense): The most beautiful creature I ever beheld can imply not only seen but encountered in life. Similarly, such joy as eye hath not beheld means experienced. This is common in superlatives and oaths where seeing stands for total life-experience.
  • Imperative behold! — lo! see! (discourse function): Though not the beheld form itself, the paradigm must be understood together: Behold the man! (Ecce homo), Behold, I bring tidings. Here behold functions like look or listen to command attention before revelation. The past beheld gains solemnity by association with this prophetic frame.
  • Passive marvel — to be beheld (aesthetic sense): In art criticism and tourism, a sight to behold means a sight worth seeing, spectacular. Beauty to behold treats the object as demanding contemplation. This idiom is the main modern survival of the participle outside scripture.

In the Austen-era sentence the word is youthful masculine rapture: Bingley (or his admirer) declares a young woman the most beautiful creature he ever beheld. The diction is intentionally a touch inflated — gallant, romantic, slightly comic to Austen's sharper observers — contrasting with Elizabeth's cooler vocabulary of seeing and observing. To master beheld is thus to hear both sincere awe and period performativity: the speaker truly stares, but also performs staring in the language of sermons, ballads, and love-letters. Modern writers deploy it for the same double effect, whether in epic fantasy without irony or in everyday hyperbole with a wink.
