The name Toledo is a place-name of Iberian antiquity that has been spoken for over two thousand years, and its biography runs from a lost Celtiberian root through Roman, Visigothic, Islamic, and Castilian history to a byword for a fine sword in the modern world. Each layer of the name is a chapter in the history of Spain itself.

The uncertain root. The oldest form we can reach is Latin Toletum, attested from the 2nd–1st centuries BC in the Roman historians and geographers as a town of the Carpetani, the Celtiberian people of the upper Tagus valley. Behind it lies a pre-Roman base that Latin recorded but never explained. The traditional derivation connects Toletum with a Celtic word for a hill or rising ground — the family of Old Irish tulach ("hillock, mound") and Welsh twl ("hill") — which fits the city's setting superbly: Toledo sits on a steep promontory almost completely ringed by a great bend of the Tagus, a naturally fortified height above the river plain. But the surviving evidence is too thin for certainty: the original pre-Roman form and meaning are lost, no Indo-European root can be securely demonstrated, and any full reconstruction would be guesswork. Honesty requires stating that the chain begins with an attested Latin name whose deeper origin is unknown.

Roman, Visigothic, and the Councils. Under the Empire Toletum was a Roman municipium, an administrative and military town on the great western road, but its first glorious age came after the fall of Rome. In the 6th century the Visigothic kings made Toledo the capital of their kingdom of Spain, and its bishops met in a long series of national councils — the Councils of Toledo, 400–702 — which shaped the Spanish church. The most famous, the Third Council of 589 under King Recared, marks the formal conversion of the Visigoths from Arianism to Catholic (Nicene) Christianity; it is precisely this moment that Gibbon has in mind when he pairs Toledo with Ravenna and the Nicene theology. For Gibbon, Toledo is the emblem of the German prince who lays down his Arian heresy, listens to his bishops, and enters the Roman Catholic world.

The Arabic centuries and the sound change. In 711 the Muslims conquered the peninsula, and Toledo entered its most brilliant period as Ṭulayṭulah (طليطلة), a great city of the Caliphate of Córdoba and later of the taifa kingdoms, famed for its artisans, its scholars, and its large Jewish community. The Arabic form preserves the Latin base in a distinctive guise — the Andalusian pronunciation of the old Toletum — and it is from this period that the city's renown as a centre of learning and craft dates. When Alfonso VI retook the city in 1085, the Spanish name settled into its modern form, Toledo, with the old Latin ending gone. The 12th-century School of Translators, working at Toledo under Archbishop Raimundo and later Alfonso X, turned Arabic and Hebrew science into Latin and Spanish, carrying Aristotle, Avicenna, and the astronomy of the Islamic world into western Europe; Toledo's name is thus written into the history of European scholarship.

From capital to sword: the metonym. From the 16th century Toledo was famous for the quality of its steel, and the name became a metonym: "a Toledo" meant a superb blade, a sense well established in Elizabethan and Jacobean English and still alive in phrases like "Toledo steel". The word's final chapter is global: as Spain's capital shifted to Madrid (1561), Toledo became a proud provincial city and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, while its name travelled to the New World, giving the American city of Toledo, Ohio, its name in 1833. A Celtiberian root we can no longer translate has thus produced a word that names a Visigothic capital, an Islamic golden-age city, a Christian seat of learning, the world's most famous sword, and an American metropolis.
