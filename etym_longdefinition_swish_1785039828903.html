**SWISH (noun / interjection / verb) — at its plainest core** At its most basic level, **swish** is an onomatopoeic word that reproduces the sharp, sibilant, whirring sound of something cutting rapidly through the air, and, by extension, the movement itself that makes that sound. It is a phonetic, imitative formation — the shape of the word IS the sound: the hissing *s*, the breathy glide *w*, the high front vowel *i*, and the soft closing *sh* reproduce the friction of a flexible body moving against the air. In the goblin chant of the Misty Mountains, *swish* is the first half of the whip-song's most brutal couplet — "SWISH, smack!" — in which the goblin drivers compress into two syllables the entire arc of a blow: the *swish* of the lash slicing through the cavern air, and the *smack* of it landing on a prisoner's back. **Primary senses**
  • The sound: the noun *swish* (1820) names the noise of something moving through the air — a whip, a cane, a blade, a sweeping skirt, a scythe, an arrow. It is thin, fast, and airy, the sound of speed rather than weight.
  • The movement: the verb *swish* (1756, intransitive) means to move with such a sound or flourish; from 1799 it is transitive — "to cause to swish," hence to flourish or brandish a blade.
  • The blow: from 1856, in slang, *to swish* means to flog or lash, and a *swish* is the stroke of a whip or its sound — the exact usage Tolkien's goblin drivers enact.
  • The exclamation: as an interjection in the chant, *swish* is the pure auditory image of a stroke in motion, the sound before the impact.
**Polysemy and secondary modern senses**
  • Basketball slang: a *swish* is a shot that passes cleanly through the net without touching the rim — the only sound is the ball brushing the net, a perfect, silent-movement throw.
  • Fashion slang: *swish* as an adjective means fashionable, smart, stylish ("a swish restaurant") — the confident, sweeping, elegant movement of high style.
  • The adjective "swishy": meaning rustling or ostentatious; from the 1930s, in homosexual slang, *swishy* (and the noun *swish*) described an effeminate man, drawn from the notion of a mincing, swishing motion.
  • The expressive family: *swoosh* (19th c.) — a broader, deeper, fuller rush of air; *whish* — a lighter, more abrupt rustle; *swash* — liquid surging against a surface; *whoosh* (20th c.) — an emphatic variant of swish.
**Distinctions from near-synonyms**
  • Swoosh: similar because both are sounds of air in motion; differs because swoosh is a broader, deeper, longer and fuller rush, while swish is thinner, faster, and more sibilant.
  • Whish: similar because it is swish's nearest kin, a sw- rustling sound; differs because whish is a lighter, more abrupt rustle, while swish implies a definite stroking movement.
  • Swash: similar in the sw- onset; differs because swash is reserved for liquid splashing or surging against a surface, not air.
  • Hiss: similar because both are sibilant noises; differs because a hiss is a prolonged sound from a steady breath with no movement, while swish is produced by rapid motion through air.
  • Whistle: similar because both are sounds of air; differs because a whistle is a musical, pitched tone, while swish is an unpitched friction sound.
  • Smack: similar because both belong to the whip's sound-pair; differs because smack is the sharp sound of impact, while swish is the preceding sound of the stroke in flight.
**Semantic reasoning and historical bridge** Every sense of the word is generated from the one imitative core: the sound of a body brushing through air. The basketball swish names the identical acoustic experience (cloth against air, no impact); the fashion sense transfers the graceful, sweeping movement to elegance and style; the slang adjective transfers the mincing motion to human mannerism. The verb-to-noun derivation (1756 verb, 1820 noun) and the transitive extension (1799, "to brandish") show the word building its polysemy within a few generations, all while keeping its purely imitative force — which is why it remains so vivid as the goblins' whip-song: the hissing s and gliding w put the listener in the path of the blow, waiting for the smack.
