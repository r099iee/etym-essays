- From its first appearance in Middle English (bost, c. 1250) the noun meant noise, tumult, and idle talk as much as proud self-assertion — "boast" and the din of boasting went together.
- In Chaucer's age it was a plain, frank word: men made their "boast" (their solemn promise or claim) in public, and it still carried a hint of swaggering but not yet of contempt.
- By the 1500s the moralists took hold: "boasting" became a recognized sin of pride, paired in sermons with vainglory and lying, and the word darkened toward its modern, censorious tone.
- The 17th-18th centuries refined it: you could "make a boast" (foolish) but also have "a proud boast" (defensible), and the verb quietly acquired the possession sense ("the city boasts fine buildings"), still in use today.
- In modern English it is split in two: as a noun it is usually a mild rebuke ("empty boasts"), but as a verb it is cheerfully neutral in tourism and journalism ("the hotel boasts a rooftop pool") — a word that once meant vanity has learned to market itself.
