- 15th century: borrowed from Latin via French, 'resolved' (as part of resolve) means 'loosened, dissolved, broken up into parts' — a physical/alchemical word for analysis (a substance 'resolved' into its elements)
- 16th century: the mental metaphor takes hold — doubts and questions are 'knots' that can be untied; 'resolve' shifts to 'settle a question, free from doubt', and 'resolved' begins to mean 'having one's mind made up'
- 17th century: the decision sense becomes dominant — 'resolved to...' = firmly decided; parliamentary usage fixes 'Resolved, that...' as the formula of collective decision; the adjective 'resolved' (determined) is fully established
- 18th–19th centuries: the word spreads across every formal domain — law, politics, science ('resolution' of forces, of spectra) — while the personal sense keeps its deliberative gravity ('she resolved to endure')
- 20th–21st centuries: 'resolved' becomes a status word in technology (a 'resolved' bug, screen 'resolution', DNS 'resolution'), and in ordinary speech it sits comfortably beside 'decided' — the old 'untied knot' image now invisible to most speakers, but still the reason why 'resolving a conflict' feels like untying it
