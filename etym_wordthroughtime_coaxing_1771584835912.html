- [16th century: to make a fool of] - 'To coax' first means to cajole or wheedle a person, originally to make a fool or dupe of them; connotation is sly and faintly dishonourable - persuasion with a trickster's edge.
- [17th-18th century: the turn to tenderness] - The verb softens from trickery to gentle, persistent persuasion - prevailing upon someone by fondness and soothing talk rather than by force, argument, or authority; connotation becomes warm and patient.
- [Contemporary sense] - 'Coaxing' means persuading gradually and gently, as one calms a frightened child, a nervous animal, or a sulking loved one; connotation is affectionate and soothing, though a hint of strategy remains - sweetness as a gentle instrument of control, the soft management of another's mood.
