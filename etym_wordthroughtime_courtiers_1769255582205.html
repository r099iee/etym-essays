- Court-of-the-monarch sense — 'courtier' enters English in the 1300s–1400s as a plain occupational word for one who attends the royal court: a companion, attendant, or adviser of the household, neutral and matter-of-fact in tone.

- The Renaissance ideal — In the 1500s the word is lifted into a cultural emblem: Castiglione's The Book of the Courtier (1528) turns the courtier into the ideal of polished excellence — wit, courtesy, self-presentation, the art of pleasing the great — so that the word glows with refinement.

- The satirical turn — Through the 1600s–1700s the courtier darkens into the flatterer, the intriguer, the parasite who lives by royal favour; 'courtier' becomes a near-synonym for insincere calculation, and satire attacks the courtly art of bending to power.

- Historical and figurative drift — By the 1800s–1900s the word recedes from everyday speech into the period vocabulary of kings and castles, while keeping a lively figurative life: any insider clustered around a powerful figure — 'courtiers of power,' the 'courtiers' who whisper around a leader — keeps the note of conspiratorial proximity.

- Contemporary sense — Modern English uses 'courtier' chiefly as a historical or literary word (as C. S. Lewis does of the whispering Narnian attendants); its tone now balances polished privilege with the whisper of calculation that has clung to it since the seventeenth century.
