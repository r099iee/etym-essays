Derivation chain for CRACKLING (with the verb CRACKLE and the noun CRACK): Stage 1 — No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon is recoverable. The root is an imitative (onomatopoeic, sound-symbolic) base of the form *krak- / *krVk- 'sharp, harsh noise', reconstructed on the evidence of the Germanic cognates alone; the Proto-Germanic verb is *krakōną 'to make a sharp noise, to crash, to crack' (parallels: Old High German chrahhōn 'to crack, resound', Middle Dutch kraken 'to crack, crash', modern German krachen). Parallel but independent formations exist elsewhere in Indo-European (Latin crepāre 'to rattle, crack, creak'; Greek krazō 'to croak, scream') and are typological parallels, not evidence of shared descent. Stage 2 — Old English cracian 'to resound, crack, snap' (recorded c. 1000, in glosses and the Old English Gospels). Stage 3 — Middle English craken 'to make a sharp noise, to break or split with a sharp sound' (12th–14th century). Stage 4 — Middle English crakelen 'to crack repeatedly' — a frequentative verb formed on craken with the iterative suffix -el- (cf. sparkle, twinkle, snuffle), recorded in the 14th–15th centuries. Stage 5 — Early Modern English crackle (verb) 'to emit a rapid series of small, sharp noises' (16th century), with the noun crackle 'a crackling sound; a network of fine cracks (as in a glaze)'. Stage 6 — Modern English crackling: present participle and gerund of crackle, and, as a noun, 'the crisp skin of roast pork' (16th century), plural cracklings 'crisped scraps of pork skin'. Final node: CRACKLING. Note on debate: the imitative base *krak- cannot be assigned a confident PIE etymon; the connection with Latin crepāre and Greek krazō is a matter of typology rather than inheritance, and the reconstruction *krakōną rests entirely on the attested Germanic cognates, which are themselves independently imitative. The spelling crackle (with -ck-) reflects the later standardised orthography of the vowel-plus-k cluster.
