Evicted is the past tense and past participle of the verb evict: to expel someone formally, and usually legally, from land, a building, or a dwelling they occupy — most often because rent has gone unpaid, a lease has expired, or a court has ordered possession returned to its owner.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses the word with his tongue firmly in cheek: three young hobbits burrowing holes in a cellar wall are evicted the way rabbits are bolted from a warren — seized, lifted out, and sent packing by indignant property-owners, with none of the bailiffs, notices, and county-court solemnity the word usually drags behind it. The comedy depends on the reader feeling the full legal weight of evict and then watching it applied to small furry-footed vandals in a pantry.

Core meaning in detail: At law, eviction is the landlord's ultimate remedy. The tenant's right to occupy is extinguished and possession is recovered, by court order where necessary, with officers empowered to remove persons and belongings if they will not go. The noun of action is eviction, the writ or notice is an eviction notice, and proceedings are called eviction proceedings. Because a court stands behind it, evicted always implies authority and finality: nobody is merely asked to leave, they are compelled to leave by a power they cannot argue with.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Legally expelled from property (primary modern sense, from 15th c.): the tenant evicted for arrears, the squatter evicted by court order. This is the sense every dictionary lists first and every newspaper reaches for, and all the figurative senses below borrow their force from it.
  • Thrown out of any premises by authority (extended everyday sense): rowdy supporters evicted from the stadium, a drunk evicted from the pub. The bridge is the authority figure — steward, bouncer, referee — standing in for the landlord, and the doorway standing in for the property line.
  • Voted or ordered off a show (reality-television sense, late 20th c.): the housemate evicted on Friday night. The bridge is the house itself: the Big Brother house or the jungle camp is treated as a dwelling, the producers as landlords, and the public vote as the court order.
  • Removed from a team, post, or position (figurative professional sense): the captain evicted from the side after a run of defeats, a minister evicted from office. The bridge runs through office as property: a post is imagined as rooms one occupies, so losing it becomes a kind of eviction.
  • Expelled from memory or storage (computing sense, 1960s–70s): pages evicted from the cache, entries evicted from a translation buffer. The bridge is the address space imagined as lodgings: when the cache is full, the least-recently-used page is turned out to make room, exactly like a tenant who must go so another can move in.
  • Driven from the mind (literary and psychological sense): to evict grief, fear, or care from the heart. The bridge is the ancient metaphor of the mind as a house with rooms: unwelcome thoughts are lodgers, and resolve is the landlord serving notice on them.

Register and connotation: evicted is a cold, official word — notices pinned to doors, padlocks, belongings on the pavement. It carries sympathy for the removed party more often than not, since headlines pair it with families, pensioners, and arrears rather than with villains. Used playfully, as Tolkien uses it, the chill of the courtroom collides with the warmth of the burrow, and that collision is the joke. Grammatically it is almost always passive or participial — people are evicted — because the word's whole point is that a greater power does the evicting.
