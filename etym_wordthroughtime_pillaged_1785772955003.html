- [Latin → OF background: Bare-stripping] - Before English owns the form, the root idea is peeling or stripping bare; French military speech turns that into armed robbery of civilians and towns.
- [1300s–1400s ME: War-raid verb] - Pillaged enters English as the proper word for what soldiers and free companies do to farms, villages, and baggage; hearers link it to chevauchée and living off the land.
- [1500s–1700s: Chronicle & empire] - Histories of Vikings, conquistadors, civil wars, and colonial campaigns use pillaged for formal devastation; the word sounds official and condemnatory, not slangy.
- [1800s: Romantic & Victorian narrative] - Novels and school histories fix ‘pillaged and burned’ as a set scene of barbarity or tragic conquest; public perception is vivid, visual, and moralized.
- [1900s–present: War crimes, heritage, metaphor] - News of occupied cities, museum theft, and archaeological looting keeps the core; occasional figurative uses (markets ‘pillaged’) borrow the violence without always matching the old military scene.
