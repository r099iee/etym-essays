smote is the past tense of smite, one of the oldest verbs of striking in English, and its history is a dramatic example of a word whose meaning narrowed, whose register became archaic, and whose participle went on living a vivid everyday life.

Prehistory and the meaning of the root

Behind the verb stands the Indo-European root *smei- (extended *smeid-), whose original meaning appears to have been 'to smear, wipe, or rub'. This may seem startling for a word meaning 'to strike', but it is well documented: cognates include Greek smēris 'rubbing or polishing powder' and smēkhō 'to wipe, cleanse'. The semantic path runs from 'to smear' through 'to rub, wipe, work by hand' to 'to throw, fling, dash', and finally 'to strike' — a chain of shifts that also explains why Old Norse smíða 'to forge' and English smith belong to the same family: the smith is, in origin, a man who works material by blows.

The Germanic and Old English verb

Proto-Germanic *smītaną 'to throw, fling, smear' produced Old English smītan, attested from the 8th century, which already carries both strands of meaning: 'to smear, daub, defile' and 'to smite, strike, beat, kill'. The Old English strong past was smāt, but in later Middle English the class-I pattern of write – wrote generalised to smīte – smōt, giving the modern smote, recorded from the 13th-14th centuries.

  • Old English smītan (8th c.): 'to smear, defile; to strike, kill' — both senses present side by side.
  • Middle English smiten / smote (12th-15th c.): 'to strike, beat, slay, punish' — the 'smear' sense dies out and pure striking dominates.
  • 16th-17th centuries: the Authorised Version of the Bible (1611) fixes smite / smote / smitten in the language of religion and law — 'smite the rock', 'I will smite', 'smitten with a plague'.
  • 18th-19th centuries: the verb retreats from ordinary speech into poetry and archaism; 'smitten' stabilises in the emotional sense ('smitten with love', first recorded in the 17th century, from the image of love as a blow or Cupid's arrow).
  • 20th-21st centuries: 'smitten' is common and playful in everyday English; the infinitive smite and the past smote survive mainly in the Bible, hymns, proverbs ('the wages of sin'), and in fantasy literature and games, where Tolkien and his successors deliberately revive them for epic colour.

Literary afterlife

In the Hobbit, 'a blow smote the side of the Mountain like the crash of battering-rams' uses smote to make the reader hear a single, gigantic, annihilating stroke; the archaic form supplies a grandeur no modern verb like 'hit' could carry. The word's whole history — from smearing hands, to striking enemies, to divine punishment, to the playful 'smitten' of a crush — is compressed into that one old past tense.
