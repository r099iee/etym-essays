Bilge begins as a bag and ends as an insult, passing through the belly of every ship in between. Its voyage from swollen leather sacks to foul hull water to worthless words is one of English’s most satisfying smell-to-sound metaphors.

1. Swelling bags: PIE to Rome. The root is PIE *bhelgh- “to swell, bulge, bag up,” which also gives English belly, bellows, bag, bulge, budget (via French bougette “little bag”), German Balg “bellows, brat,” and Sanskrit barha-. Celtic speakers formed Gaulish *bulga “leather bag, knapsack” — the soldier’s swollen pack. Latin borrowed it as bulga (Festus glosses it as a Gallic word), used by Plautus and Lucilius for purses, wombs, and paunches. The semantic core never moves: a soft container swollen with contents.

  • Phonetics: PIE *bh- to Latin b-, vocalic l to ul; Gaulish u preserved into Latin bulga.

2. From bag to belly to hull. Old Northern French keeps the word as boulge, bouge “bag, belly, cavity, swelling,” and Middle English borrows it in the 13th–14th centuries as bulge, bouge, bowge for any hump or swelling — including the swelling curve of a hull or barrel. Shipwrights need a name for the round of the bilge where bottom turns to side; coopers need one for a cask’s fattest hoop. Both call it the bulge. In Tudor dockyards (first record 1510s, in naval accounts) the nautical spelling splits off as bilge — a familiar East Anglian and Kentish raising of u to i before l (compare bulge/bilge like pulp?), закреплённая by sailors who love a distinctive technical term. From then on bulge means swell in general, bilge means hull-bottom in particular.

3. Life in the sump. For three centuries bilge belongs to seamanship manuals: to pump, sweeten, sound, cleanse the bilge; bilge water as the enemy of health, cargo, and morale; bilged as the verdict on a holed ship (Lloyd’s lists are full of “bilged on the Goodwins”). Wooden walls sweat, oakum weeps, livestock and ballast foul the sump; the smell — salt, tar, rot, urine, diesel after steam — becomes proverbial. Patrick O’Brian, Conrad, and Forester all use the black-ribbed open-boat bilge of the card as shorthand for misery: wet feet, bruised back, no dignity. Naval architecture formalises it with bilge keels (side fins at the turn of the bilge, 19th century), bilge blocks in dry dock, and bilge alarms on modern yachts.

4. From sump to sneer. Around World War I Royal Navy wardrooms shorten bilge water — useless, stinking, to be pumped overboard — to bilge meaning rubbish-talk: “Don’t give me that bilge.” By the 1920s–1930s it is in print (novelists of naval life, then newspapers), and after World War II it spreads ashore as all-purpose British slang for nonsense, later globalised by film and television. The metaphor is exact: speech that, like bilge water, is dirty, stale, and best expelled. The card’s steward, sprawled in literal bilge, and the slang “talking bilge” thus meet: both are where what is worthless ends up.
