The biographical history of lexicon reveals a profound semantic journey spanning thousands of years: starting as a physical manual action of gathering crops or pebbles, transforming through classical rhetoric into speech and writing, crystalizing in the Byzantine Empire as a manuscript wordbook, and culminating in the twentieth century as an abstract architecture of the human mind. The ultimate ancestor of lexicon is the fertile Proto-Indo-European verbal root *leǵ-, whose foundational concrete meaning was "to gather, collect, cull, or pick out by hand."

From Agricultural Gathering to Rhetorical Speech in Greek:

  • Bifurcation across Indo-European Branches: In Latin, PIE *leǵ- developed through the sense of "gathering with the eyes" into legere ("to gather, choose, read"), spawning an enormous family of Western borrowings including lecture, legible, collect, and elect. In ancient Greek, however, the root followed an equally profound metaphorical pathway: from gathering physical items, it shifted to "gathering one’s thoughts, picking out words, recounting, and speaking," embodied in the primary verb legein (λέγειν, "to say, speak, tell, recount"). From legein emerged the paramount philosophical concept logos (λόγος, "word, speech, reason, cosmic order").
  • The Nominalization into Lexis: In classical Athens during the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, literary theorists and philosophers—most notably Aristotle in his Rhetoric and Poetics—coined the feminine noun lexis (λέξις). While logos emphasized the logical content, truth value, and rational argument of a speech, lexis denoted the verbal surface: the style, diction, phrasing, word choice, and vocabulary employed by an orator or poet.

Hellenistic Lexicography and the Byzantine Word-Book:

  • The Coining of the Relational Adjective: By the Hellenistic era (c. 3rd century BCE), Greek scholars derived the relational adjective lexikos (λεξικός), meaning "pertaining to words or vocabulary." When scholars began compiling alphabetical guides to decipher the obscure, archaic dialectal vocabulary of Homer, the lyric poets, and Athenian dramatists, they paired this adjective in its neuter singular form with the noun for scroll or book (biblion), creating the compound phrase lexikon biblion (λεξικὸν βιβλίον, literally "a word-book").
  • The Ellipsis into a Substantive Noun: Over centuries of scholarly scribal practice in Alexandria, Constantinople, and Antioch, the noun biblion was dropped through grammatical ellipsis, leaving the nominalized neuter adjective to lexikon (τὸ λεξικόν) to stand independently as the substantive name for an alphabetical reference work. Monumental achievements of Byzantine scholarship, such as Hesychius of Alexandria’s fifth-century glossary and the massive tenth-century encyclopedic dictionary known as the Suda (which contained over 30,000 entries), institutionalized the lexicon as the pinnacle of lexicographical erudition.

Renaissance Latin and Early Modern English Reception:

  • The Humanist Revival of Greek: Following the fall of Constantinople in 1453 and the flight of Byzantine scholars to Italy, Renaissance humanists ignited a fervent revival of Greek literature. In Renaissance Latin, the Greek neuter singular lexikon was transliterated directly as lexicon. Throughout the sixteenth century, printer-scholars like Aldus Manutius and Henri Estienne published monumental Greek and Hebrew lexicons. Because Western scholars already possessed native Latin terms like dictionarium (coined in the 13th century) and vocabularium, the imported term lexicon carried distinct scholarly prestige: it was exclusively reserved for dictionaries of Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, and other ancient learned tongues.
  • Adoption into English: The word entered English prose around 1600 (attested in John Florio’s translations and seventeenth-century university catalogues). For over two centuries, calling a reference work a lexicon instantly signaled that it treated a classical or oriental language. Only gradually in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries did English writers extend the word metaphorically to denote the entire wordstock of English or the vocabulary peculiar to an author like Milton or Shakespeare.

The Chomskyan Revolution and Modern Computational Theory:

  • Internalization of the Lexicon: The most radical conceptual revolution in the word's history occurred in the mid-1960s with the rise of generative grammar led by Noam Chomsky. In works like Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965), Chomsky transferred the term from external paper book volumes into human neurobiology. The mental lexicon ceased to be an alphabetical reference tool and became an active, biological module of the human mind containing all arbitrary linguistic signs and their subcategorization frames. Today, this cognitive model interfaces seamlessly with computer science, where automated lexicons drive natural language processing, neural machine translation, and compiler design.
