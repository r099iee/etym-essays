Thither is a pure descendant of the great pronominal system of Proto-Indo-European, and its biography is really the biography of the word that. The ancestral base is the demonstrative stem *to-, the oblique form of *so-, which pointed at something away from the speaker. From this single deictic seed grew the entire "that/there" machinery of the daughter languages. In the Indo-Iranian world it produced Sanskrit tat ("that"), tatra ("there"), and tatas ("thence"); in Greek it hardened into the article to — Homer's demonstrative use of ho and to slowly crystallized into the definite article — together with tote ("then"). In Latin the same deictic function was carried by iste ("that one of yours") and its adverb istic ("there"), which in turn produced modern Romance forms such as French cette and Spanish este. But it was the Germanic branch that kept the *to- stem itself as the everyday machinery of English.

Proto-Germanic inherited *þa- ("that"), and from it Old English drew an astonishing number of words: þæt ("that"), þe ("the," the article), þær ("there"), þonne ("then"), and the directional adverbs. The key formation for this card is the suffix -der, which built the three motion-words: *þadēr ("thither"), beside the mirror forms *hidēr ("hither," to this place) and *hwidēr ("whither," to which place). Old English spelled the word þider or þæder, and it is attested throughout the Anglo-Saxon period, including in Beowulf. In Middle English the form thider gained a final -r by analogy with here, there, and where, becoming thither — the shape the word has kept for six hundred years.

The semantic history is a study in how languages organize space:

  • The three-way grid: for a millennium English ran two parallel three-way systems — static (here/there/where) and directional (hither/thither/whither) — plus an ablative set (hence/thence/whence). Thither occupied a precise cell in this grid: "to that place," the goal of motion away from the speaker.
  • Standard currency in early Modern English: through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries thither was ordinary formal prose. The King James Bible uses it freely ("and thither shall all thy cattle be brought," "they journeyed thither"), and Shakespeare exploits its cadence; it was no more archaic in 1600 than "thereto" is rare today.
  • The collapse of the system (18th–19th centuries): as English simplified, the motion-words lost their distinct niche. "Go there" absorbed the work of "go thither," just as "go here" and "go where" absorbed hither and whither. Whither lingered in questions and after prepositions; thither, having no interrogative function to protect it, retreated fastest and was archaic by the Victorian age, surviving chiefly in the fossilized phrase "hither and thither" and in deliberately lofty prose.
  • Literary afterlife: the very archaism made the word precious. Nineteenth-century historical novelists, and later writers of fantasy and ceremonial prose, deployed thither precisely because it was gone from speech — it now signals a scene dressed in the robes of the past, which is exactly the effect Hawkes achieves when he sets the adverb at the head of the sentence in The Story of the Stone, giving a young official's departure the gravity of an ancient progress.
  • Dialectal persistence: alongside the literary survival, pockets of regional speech in England and Scotland kept thither (and hither, thence, whither) alive as living direction-words into the modern era, a reminder that the collapse was a matter of standard usage rather than of the language as a whole.

The word thus charts a perfect linguistic arc: from a PIE demonstrative that organized the grammar of distance, through the elaborated grid of Old and Middle English, to standard formal currency in the age of the King James Bible, and finally to the elegant relic it is today — a word that still means exactly what it meant a thousand years ago, and that owes its survival to being, in ordinary speech, gloriously obsolete.
