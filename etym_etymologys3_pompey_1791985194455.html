A plebeian name from Picenum

The Pompeii were not part of the old Roman aristocracy. They came from Picenum, on the Adriatic side of Italy, and rose through wealth, military command and the civil wars of the first century BCE. The name Pompeius is a gentilicium, the clan name that in Roman nomenclature sits between the praenomen and the cognomen; the gens Pompeia was plebeian, and its first consul, Quintus Pompeius, held office in 141 BCE. By the time of Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus the family had arrived, but its name still carried the flavour of the Italian countryside rather than of the Forum.

The Oscan hypothesis

The most persistent etymology connects Pompeius with the Oscan numeral pompe “five”, the Italic cognate of Latin quinque. Oscan, the language of the Samnites and of Pompeii, preserves the Indo-European numeral *penkʷe with initial p- where Latin has qu-, so pompe is exactly the form expected. The city of Pompeii appears in Oscan as púmpaiians, and the resemblance has encouraged the view that both the city and the family name contain the numeral — perhaps a reference to a five-part division, a fifth son, or a founding group of five. The hypothesis is attractive but cannot be demonstrated: no ancient source explains the name, the morphology of -eius is not fully understood, and the numeral may be a coincidence. Dictionaries of Latin personal names accordingly mark the origin as uncertain, with “perhaps Oscan” as the cautious formula.

  • Oscan pompe: “five”, from Italic *kʷenkʷe, the same numeral that gives Latin quinque.
  • Pompeii: Oscan púmpaiians; the city's name may share the root, though the connection is debated.
  • Caution: the numeral is genuine, but its use in a personal name is a conjecture, not a recorded fact.

Magnus: the adopted epithet

Pompey's third name was not inherited but acquired. According to Plutarch, Sulla saluted him as Magnus, “the Great”, and Pompey adopted the agnomen, which his sons later used as a hereditary title. The gesture was both honour and irony: Pompey was in his twenties, had held no magistracy, and had just won a triumph for victories in Africa. The epithet stuck because his achievements in the 60s BCE were genuinely extraordinary — the pirate command of 67 and the eastern settlement of 66–63 — but ancient and modern writers have often measured him against it and found him wanting. Caesar, by contrast, needed no such label.

Englishing the name

English did not take the name directly from the Latin classroom in its full form. The traditional English forms of Roman names — Tully for Tullius, Virgil for Vergilius, Pompey for Pompeius — arose in the Middle Ages from Anglo-Norman and Middle English usage, which trimmed the Latin ending to a familiar -y. The result is a name that sounds English rather than Roman, and that has kept a popular life independent of scholarship. Shakespeare uses it without explanation in Julius Caesar (1599) and in Antony and Cleopatra (c. 1607), where “Pompey” is first the great general and then his son Sextus Pompeius, master of the sea.

Literary and historical afterlife

  • Cicero's letters: the indispensable contemporary source for Pompey's political manoeuvres, by turns admiring and exasperated.
  • Caesar, Bellum Civile: the adversary's account, in which Pompey's forces are numerous but his cause is hollow.
  • Lucan, Pharsalia: Pompey becomes the tragic defender of the Republic, a man whose defeat is nobler than victory.
  • Plutarch, Life of Pompey: the biographical portrait that supplied Shakespeare and later dramatists.
  • Shakespeare: Julius Caesar and Antony and Cleopatra keep the English name on the stage.

Monuments and nicknames

Two very different afterlives show how far the name has travelled from the man. “Pompey's Pillar” at Alexandria is a granite column erected in honour of Diocletian at the end of the third century CE; medieval and early modern travellers, finding a Roman monument in Egypt, attributed it to Pompey, and the misnomer has stuck. In England, “Pompey” is the nickname of the city of Portsmouth and its football club, of uncertain origin — suggestions include a map abbreviation, a sailors' joke and a reference to a local pub. Neither usage has anything to do with the Roman general, but both show the name's capacity to become a familiar label.

Semantic summary

In historical prose Pompey denotes the man and his achievement: the conqueror of the pirates, the organiser of the East, the senior partner in the First Triumvirate. In political narrative he denotes a type: the indispensable general whose prestige a civilian regime cannot do without and cannot control. In English literature he denotes dignified failure, the man who lost to Caesar and whose cause was nonetheless the better one. And in popular usage he is simply a nickname, proof that a classical name can survive as a local joke.
