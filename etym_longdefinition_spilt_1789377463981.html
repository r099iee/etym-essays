Spilt is an inflected past-tense and past-participle form, as well as a participial adjective, of the verb spill. At its core physical definition, it designates liquid, semi-liquid, or granular matter that has inadvertently or accidentally flowed, tumbled, fallen, or escaped from its intended vessel, container, or boundary, dispersing across a surface and often resulting in irrevocable loss, waste, mess, or ruin. Beyond this physical sense, the word carries deep metaphoric resonance, signifying blood violently shed in warfare, secrets heedlessly revealed, or emotional energy squandered on hopeless causes.

In the source passage—taken from Oscar Wilde's satirical fairy tale The Remarkable Rocket (1888)—the word appears within one of the English language's most famous and enduring proverbial idioms: "I hate people who cry over spilt milk." Wilde deploys the proverb with exquisite comic irony. The self-important, hypochondriacal Rocket creates an entirely fabricated, ludicrous hypothetical catastrophe: he imagines that the newlywed Prince and Princess might one day have an only son with violet eyes, that his nurse might fall asleep under an elder-tree, and that the child might fall into a river and drown. When the pragmatic Roman Candle points out that no such child exists and no tragedy has occurred, the Rocket indignantly retorts that if it had already happened, he would not bother weeping, because he hates people who cry over spilt milk. The humor stems from the Rocket's absurd inversion: the proverb normally urges stoic composure and rational acceptance regarding past, unalterable mishaps, but the Rocket cites it to justify indulging in theatrical, self-indulgent grief over an event that hasn't happened and never will.

The participle and adjective exhibits extensive polysemy across physical, idiomatic, legal, and nautical domains:

  • Physical Escape and Dispersion of Matter (Primary Literal Sense): In everyday domestic, industrial, and laboratory contexts, spilt describes fluids, powders, grains, or loose particulates that have tumbled out of cups, tanks, barrels, or sacks. One speaks of spilt wine staining a linen tablecloth, spilt oil contaminating an estuary, or spilt grain attracting pests on a granary floor. Unlike controlled pouring or intentional emptying, matter that is spilt escapes through clumsiness, mechanical failure, structural leakage, or turbulence, universally conveying an aura of accidental disorder and waste.
  • Proverbial and Idiomatic Wisdom ("No Use Crying Over Spilt Milk"): As crystallized in the centuries-old aphorism, spilt embodies the philosophical concept of temporal irreversibility. Once milk has escaped the jug and soaked into the earthen floor or floorboards, no amount of sorrow, lamentation, anger, or recrimination can gather the droplets back into the jug or undo the mistake. The idiom serves as a pragmatic injunction against futile dwelling on past errors, urging individuals to accept irreversible losses and direct their focus toward constructive future action.
  • Violent Bloodshed and Warfare ("Spilt Blood"): Across epic poetry, tragedy, and historiography, spilt is frequently collocated with blood to denote slaughter, carnage, and violent death in combat or murder. To speak of spilt blood evokes the physical reality of severed arteries and spilled life-force soaking into the earth, carrying profound moral, sacrificial, and retributive gravity. In international diplomacy and treaty negotiations, statesmen speak of avoiding the tragedy of "spilt blood," where the fluid loss symbolizes the irreversible human cost of war.
  • Metaphorical and Psychological Leakage: In psychological and social registers, the participle denotes personal secrets, concealed information, or suppressed emotions that escape containment. Just as physical liquid leaks from a fractured pitcher, a careless confidant who reveals a confidential plan has "spilt the beans" or allowed confidential details to become spilt across the public square, irreversibly damaging privacy or political advantage.
  • Nautical and Maritime Maneuvers (Spilt Wind): In traditional seamanship and sailing terminology, the verb spill has a specialized active and passive register. To spill the wind from a sail means to turn the vessel or adjust the sheets and clewlines so that the wind spills out of the belly of the canvas, reducing forward propulsion or preventing a knockdown in sudden squalls. Canvas that has spilt its wind hangs luffing or fluttering, relieved of destructive aerodynamic pressure.
  • Morphological and Dialectal Variation (Spilt vs. Spilled): In modern English morphology, spilt represents the traditional British, Commonwealth, and literary irregular dental-preterite form (terminating in -t), formed along the same Germanic pattern as learnt, burnt, spoilt, and dreamt. While American English predominantly regularizes the verb to spilled in both past indicative and past participial functions, spilt remains universally entrenched across the global Anglophone world within the frozen proverb "spilt milk" and preserves a distinctive literary, historical, and poetic cadence.

The semantic bridge linking ancient Germanic slaughter, medieval agricultural grain spills, modern oil slicks, and Oscar Wilde's theatrical fireworks rocket is the underlying conceptual shift of the verb itself: originating as an active Germanic verb meaning "to destroy, mutilate, or put to death," the destruction of life inevitably involved the violent spilling of bodily blood. Over centuries of domestic usage, English speakers attenuated this lethal destruction to the physical loss, ruining, or accidental escaping of domestic liquids and provisions, crystallizing in the enduring image of valuable milk lost upon the ground.
