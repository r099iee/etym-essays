• arteries: Similar because both refer to high-capacity routes essential for continuous transit and circulation through a city or region; differs because arteries is a vivid biological metaphor emphasizing vital function and organic branching, whereas thoroughfares is a classic architectural and legal term describing wide, unobstructed open streets.
  • boulevards: Similar because both denote broad, major city avenues; differs because boulevards (historically derived from bulwarks/ramparts) typically feature grand landscaping, central medians, tree-lined pedestrian sidewalks, and leisurely promenades, whereas thoroughfares focuses functionally on unobstructed through-traffic.
  • avenues: Similar because both signify wide, important roadways; differs because avenues historically originated as formal, tree-lined approaches to grand country estates or ceremonial civic axes, whereas thoroughfares emphasizes functional passage from one end to another.
  • highways: Similar because both facilitate travel between distant points; differs because highways usually denotes high-speed, long-distance intercity roads with controlled access, while thoroughfares applies frequently to busy urban streets, canals, and metropolitan corridors.
  • conduits: Similar because both serve as channels through which flow occurs; differs because conduits is an engineering and technical term typically describing enclosed pipes, channels, or abstract flow pathways, whereas thoroughfares denotes open public passageways.
  • streets: Similar because both designate paved vehicular paths in towns; differs because streets is a general term encompassing quiet residential lanes, dead-end cul-de-sacs, and alleys, whereas thoroughfares strictly requires open access at both ends with heavy through-traffic.
  • passages: Similar because both allow movement from one place to another; differs because passages often suggests narrow, enclosed corridors, covered pedestrian arcades, or tight mountain defiles, lacking the vast, open scale of major thoroughfares.
  • promenades: Similar because both are public spaces for transit; differs because promenades are designed primarily for leisurely walking, social strolling, and sightseeing (often along a seafront or park), rather than rapid through-transit.
