- Early 19th century - “Impressionable” (capable of being impressed or influenced) is current in psychological and literary language — the mind as wax or paper receiving stamps; its negative “unimpressionable” is the natural contrary.
- Mid-19th century - Applied especially to character: the unimpressionable man is sealed against influence and impervious to emotional currents; in the associationist psychology of James Mill the term is precise — a mind that takes no stamp.
- 20th century - Extends to aesthetic and social contexts — unimpressionable audiences, unimpressionable natures — always naming resistance to being shaped by feeling or opinion.
- Contemporary - Formal and uncommon; the “wax that will not take the seal” image keeps the word precise: not merely unemotional, but genuinely unaffected by external impressions.
