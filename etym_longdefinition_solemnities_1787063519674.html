solemnities is the plural of solemnity, a noun denoting formal, dignified ceremonies and occasions of grave seriousness. In its primary modern sense a solemnity is a rite, ceremony, or public observance performed with deliberate gravity and ceremonial order — a wedding, a coronation, a state funeral, a religious feast — and solemnities is used when several such observances, or the whole apparatus of a grand occasion's formal proceedings, are referred to collectively.

Primary meaning: ceremonial occasions.

  • Most commonly, solemnities = the formal ceremonies and ritual proceedings that mark a special event, especially religious ones.
  • In Christian liturgical usage, a solemnity is the highest rank of feast (Christmas, Easter, Pentecost, Corpus Christi), outranking "feasts" and "memorials" in the calendar.
  • In secular contexts, solemnities covers the whole run of official observances: investitures, oath-takings, coronations, funerals, the opening of parliament, and other grand public rites.
  • The word very often appears with the definite article — "the solemnities" — meaning the full programme of formal proceedings of an event.

Secondary meaning: gravity and seriousness as a quality.

  • The uncountable sense "deep, earnest seriousness" is also solemnity: "the solemnity of the occasion silenced the hall."
  • In older prose solemnities can mean "serious, grave proceedings" just as much as "festive ceremonies."
  • The historical bridge: because formal ceremonies are performed gravely and without levity, the word that named the rite came to name the earnest, dignified mood that surrounds it — a classic metonymic transfer from the event to its atmosphere.

Tertiary and specialised senses.

  • Liturgical (Roman Catholic): a "solemnity" is a first-class feast; the plural solemnities refers to such high holy days and their observances.
  • Legal-historical: the "solemnities" of an act are the formalities required by law to make it valid — e.g. the solemnities of marriage (publication of banns, witnesses, ceremony).
  • Rhetorical/personal: "solemnity" of manner = weighty, serious, dignified bearing or speech.
  • Mock-formal and ironic: in modern literary usage solemnities can denote grand, slow-moving formalities that strike onlookers as tedious or pompous — exactly Tolkien's use when Fili bursts out that he is "getting impatient at these solemnities."

Etymological bridge and semantic reasoning.

  • Latin sollemnis "annual, fixed by religious custom" (traditionally from sollus "whole" + annus "year") named rites that recurred every year — "whole-year" observances.
  • The Latin neuter plural sollemnia hardened into a noun meaning "the religious ceremonies" themselves; that plural survives semantically in English solemnities, most naturally used of a plurality of ceremonies or a full programme of observances.
  • By the 19th century the plural had come to suggest not merely multiple rites but the whole imposing, slow-moving business of a formal occasion — which is precisely the sense in Tolkien's dwarf-dialogue.

Morphology and grammar.

  • solemnity (singular) + regular plural in -ies: solemnities.
  • Both countable ("a solemnity") and uncountable ("with solemnity") uses exist; the plural is overwhelmingly commoner than the singular in real usage.
  • Derivatives: solemn (adjective), solemnly (adverb), solemnize (verb, to perform a ceremony, especially a marriage), solemnization (noun).
  • Note the pronunciation trap: in solemn the final n is silent (/ˈsɒl.əm/), but in solemnity it is pronounced (/səˈlɛm.nə.ti/).

Register and usage notes.

  • Formal and literary; uncommon in casual speech, where "ceremonies," "celebrations," or "formalities" are preferred.
  • Frequently plural; often preceded by "the."
  • In modern writing "solemnities" tends to appear in journalism (state funerals, royal occasions), liturgical texts, and fiction seeking a grand or ironic tone.
