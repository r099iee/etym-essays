The biography of jeer opens, unusually, in obscurity. The word surfaces in English around 1550 with the sense "to deride, to make sport of," and every major authority — the OED, Etymonline, the great historical grammars — describes its origin as unknown. Three hypotheses are current, none of them proven. The first is that the word is imitative: a coarse, guttural cry of contempt felt to resemble the sound of jeering itself, related perhaps to an obsolete English ger or geer, "a cry, a shout," and to a dialectal gare, "to cry out." The second sees a Dutch loan: modern Dutch gieren means "to roar, to howl, to scream with laughter," and the image of mockery as a howling wind or a screaming crowd fits the word's social meaning perfectly; a jeering mob is, etymologically, a roaring one. The third points to German scheren, "to vex, to plague, to trouble," whose root sense is "to shear, to cut" (the same root as English shear): on this theory a jeer is something that "cuts" at its victim, trimming away his dignity or reputation with a sharp, wounding edge. Scholars have never been able to choose among these, and the honest verdict remains that jeer is a word of "uncertain noise," born in the streets and cant of early modern England without a verifiable pedigree. What is certain is that it is NOT related to cheer: cheer descends from Old French chiere, "face, countenance," from Greek karā, "head," and the resemblance between the two words is pure accident. The pair are a standing warning against trusting sound alone.

Within English the word developed with quiet efficiency. The verb appeared first, in the 1550s, meaning "to mock, to deride"; the noun followed within about twenty years, meaning "a taunt, a mocking cry," and with it the participial forms that are now the word's most durable workhorses — jeering as adjective and noun, jeeringly as adverb, and jeerer for the person who jeers. The semantic core never wavered: a jeer is always loud, always contemptuous, and always public. It carved for itself a precise niche in the field of mockery, standing apart from the silent superiority of the sneer, the witty point of the gibe, and the articulate insult of the taunt. Its distinctive feature, fixed from the sixteenth century to the present, is collectivity — the jeer is the sound of a crowd, a mob, an audience, a faction, and it presupposes a public stage and a single humiliated target. It is the noise of the social machine registering disapproval, and it has always carried with it the implication that the jeered-at person stands alone against the many.

The socio-cultural life of the word has been rich. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries jeers belonged to the theatre and the pillory — the groundlings of the Globe jeered the player, the mob jeered the offender in the stocks — and the word's history is bound up with the public, punitive ritual of early modern shame. In the eighteenth century it entered the language of journalism and parliamentary controversy, where a speaker could be "jeered down." In the nineteenth century the novel made it the sound of the crowd against the individual, a staple of Dickens and the Victorian melodrama; and in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries it moved naturally to the football terraces, the hustings, and the arena, where massed voices still jeer the player, the politician, or the loser. The verb's grammar has likewise settled: modern English jeers at a person, or jeers a person from a stage, while the noun forms the twin collocations "jeers and catcalls" and "jeers of derision."

That is exactly the role the word plays in the verse that introduces Bao-yu in The Story of the Stone. The catalogue of his faults — "harum-scarum," "doltish," "acts outlandish," "nature queer" — is designed to provoke the jeer, and the couplet obliges by supplying it in the crowd's voice: "Yet not a whit cared he how folk might jeer!" The point of the sentence is not the jeer but the indifference that meets it. Bao-yu's refusal to care "a whit" (itself a word that reduces the whole of public opinion to a particle of zero size) turns the crowd's noise into a mere weather event — sound without authority. The jeer, etymologically a "roaring" or a "shearing," fails to cut because its victim has declined to enter the social field where such sounds have power. Four and a half centuries after the word first appears, it still does this double work: it names the derisive voice of the many, and it invites us to measure the courage or eccentricity of the one who declines to hear it.
