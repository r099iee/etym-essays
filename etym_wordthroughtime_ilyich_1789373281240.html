- 10th to 14th Century - Emerges in Kievan Rus' following the Christianization of the East Slavs (988 CE); Church Slavonic *Iliya* (from Hebrew *Eliyahu*) generates patronymics denoting descent from Ilya, originally restricted to princely, boyar, and merchant elites.
- 15th to 18th Century - The patronymic suffix *-ich* becomes a prestigious badge of aristocratic and official recognition in Muscovite Russia and the Tsarist Empire; formalizing under Peter the Great's Table of Ranks as the indispensable standard for polite, dignified address among the educated classes.
- Late 19th Century (1886) - Tolstoy publishes *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, transforming the patronymic across world literature into the universal archetype of the bourgeois professional confronting physical agony, mortality, and existential dread.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - The patronymic explodes in global political consciousness as the ubiquitous, affectionate Soviet shorthand for Vladimir Ilyich Lenin (*лампочка Ильича*, state honors, poetry by Mayakovsky), while Tolstoy's novella is adopted by Martin Heidegger and Western existentialists as the foundational text on death.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Cemented worldwide in medical humanities, bioethics, and literary curricula as the ultimate meditation on palliative care and the authentic life, while retaining its Russian sociolinguistic role of formal respect and familiar affection.
