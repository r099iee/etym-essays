The biography of elapse is a study in how an image of physical slackness became a neutral word for time. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *slebh-, "to be slack, hang loose, be limp," a root whose meaning is almost tactile: things that sag, droop, and hang loose. Its clearest reflexes outside Latin are Balto-Slavic — Lithuanian slẽbnas "slack, weak" and slỹbti "to hang down, become slack," Old Church Slavonic slabъ "weak," which lives on in modern Russian slábый and Polish słaby — and Greek preserves ólibros (ὄλιβρός) "slippery." Many scholars also group the Germanic "sleep" words here (Proto-Germanic *slēpaną, English sleep, German schlafen), on the ancient guess that to sleep is to "fall slack," though that link is debated.

From this sense of slackness Latin developed lābī, "to glide, slip, fall" — a verb whose image is of things losing their footing and sliding: bodies slipping, feet sliding, smooth surfaces gliding. The semantic bridge is a loose fabric or a loose footing: what is not taut slips. The Romans used the root for the literal sliding of the body and for the figurative sliding away of opportunity and time, and Latin poetry made the image of fleeting time a standing formula — tempus fugit, "time flees," and the lapsus temporis, "the lapse of time." From the simplex lābī Latin built the participle lapsus, "a slipping, a fall," which English imports in the fifteenth century as lapse — first literally, a fall or slip, then figuratively a moral lapse, a lapse of memory, and finally the lapse of time. Meanwhile the adjective lābilis "slippery, fleeting" gave English labile, "unstable, changeable."

More important for our word is the prefixed compound ēlābī (ex- "out" + lābī): "to slip out of, to glide away," whose participle ēlapsus gives English elapse around 1590 — a direct Latin borrowing, not a French one. The word entered English already pointed at time — "to slip away (of time)" — though it could also be used of life and of opportunities slipping out of reach. In modern English elapse is completely specialised to time and carries no hint of its origin in slackness or falling: it is the neutral, dignified "to pass (of an interval)" that survives in elapsed time and the legal-administrative formula "let a period elapse." Its cognate family still shows the whole range of the old root — the Balto-Slavic "weak" words, Greek "slippery," and, if the old guess holds, Germanic "sleep" — going slack, slipping, and gliding their way from an ancient idea of looseness to the measured flow of modern minutes.

That specialization to time is the word's whole modern identity. In the sciences and in sport, "elapsed time" is a term of art — the measured interval between the start and the finish of an experiment, a voyage, or a race — and computing uses "elapsed run time" for the actual duration of a process; the adjective has become as standard as the verb. In the law and in administration the phrase "let a period elapse" is a standing formula for due process, requiring that time be allowed to pass so that rights may vest or notices be served. All of this flows, by a now invisible route, from a Proto-Indo-European picture of something slack and hanging loose. The older poetic use of the verb for life and opportunity slipping away — "how quickly the hours elapse!" — is the one place where the Latin image of gliding still glimmers through, and it is the same image that Hawkes quietly releases in The Story of the Stone, where the interval between a meal and its tea is gently "allowed to elapse," time gliding by like water through fingers, in a household where even minutes are managed with measured patience.
