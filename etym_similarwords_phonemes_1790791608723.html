• Morphemes: Similar because both are foundational structural units of language; differs because a morpheme is the smallest unit of *meaning* or grammatical function (e.g., 'un-', 'break', '-able'), whereas a phoneme is the smallest unit of *sound* and carries no inherent meaning on its own.
  • Phones: Similar because both refer to speech sounds produced by human vocal organs; differs because a phone is a raw, physical, acoustic realization measured in phonetics without regard to whether it alters word meaning, whereas a phoneme is an abstract, functional category in a specific language's sound system.
  • Allophones: Similar because both involve the sounds belonging to a language's phonological inventory; differs because allophones are the various phonetic surface realizations or non-meaning-altering variants of a single underlying phoneme (e.g., the aspirated [tʰ] in 'top' vs. the unaspirated [t] in 'stop').
  • Graphemes: Similar because both represent foundational distinct units in communication systems; differs because graphemes are the smallest contrastive units in a *written* language (letters or letter combinations), whereas phonemes belong to *spoken* language.
  • Syllables: Similar because both are units of spoken sound; differs because a syllable is a larger phonological organizing unit typically composed of a nucleus (usually a vowel) flanked by optional consonantal onsets and codas, containing multiple phonemes.
