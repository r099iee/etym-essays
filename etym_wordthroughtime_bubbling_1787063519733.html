- 14th c., echoic verb — water heated over a fire rises in bubbles; the word is domestic and kitchen-bound.
- 15th–17th c. — extends to springs, brooks, wells, and fermenting beer; bubbling becomes the sound of living, moving water in nature.
- 18th–19th c. — figurative transfer to temperament: “bubbling laughter”, “bubbling wit”, a person “bubbling over” with spirits; the word turns from cooking to character.
- 20th c. — a “bubbling” personality and “bubbly” (champagne, then people); effervescence becomes a social virtue.
- 21st c. — “bubbling under the surface” for suppressed tensions and near-breakthrough artists; still the friendly word for a noisy pot.
