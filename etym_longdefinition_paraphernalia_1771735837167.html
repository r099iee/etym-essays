PARAPHERNALIA, plural noun (in origin a neuter plural, but now usually construed as singular or mass noun). In the Gibbon passage the word describes the entire institutional apparatus that has grown up around the historical profession: learned societies, prestigious academic specializations, journals, chairs, and credentials. The sentence "The historical profession, with all its paraphernalia of learned societies and prestigious academic specialties, has grown up only since Gibbon's day" contrasts the modern organized guild of professional historians with the solitary, aristocratic scholarship of Gibbon himself. Paraphernalia here names the whole kit of institutions and trappings — the supporting machinery — that now surrounds, dignifies, and bureaucratizes historical study. The core senses of PARAPHERNALIA:
  • Miscellaneous articles and equipment, especially the accessories needed for a particular activity. This is the everyday modern sense: the photographer's paraphernalia, the magician's paraphernalia, fishing paraphernalia. It names the assortment of gear, props, and tools that belong to a craft or pursuit.
  • The apparatus, trappings, and supporting institutions of an activity or profession. The institutional sense used in the passage: learned societies, journals, chairs, credentials, conferences — the organized machinery that surrounds and sustains a discipline.
  • Belongings, personal possessions, or bits and pieces associated with a person or place. The writer's paraphernalia scattered on a desk, the paraphernalia of a gentleman's study. Here the word shades toward miscellaneous personal effects.
  • Historical-legal sense (archaic): a married woman's personal property beyond her dowry. In medieval English law, paraphernalia designated the goods — clothing, jewels, and personal effects — that a wife retained as her own besides the dowry she brought to the marriage. This is the etymological sense (from Greek "beside the dowry"), now obsolete in law but the key to the word's history.
Secondary and extended senses, with historical bridges:
  • Drug paraphernalia. In modern police and legal usage, the equipment associated with drug use (pipes, syringes, scales). A narrowing of the general sense "equipment for an activity."
  • Figurative and often ironic use. "All the paraphernalia of modern life" — the clutter of objects and procedures that surround an activity. The word often carries a slightly amused or dismissive tone, implying more apparatus than is strictly needed.
  • Ceremonial and official trappings. The paraphernalia of royalty — crowns, scepters, robes. Here it overlaps with regalia and trappings, stressing the visible apparatus of office.
The historical bridge that explains the polysemy: the word is built on Greek παρά (para-, "beside") + φερνή (pherne, "dowry"). In Greek and Roman law, the parapherna (plural) were the goods a bride carried "beside the dowry" — her own property beyond what she brought to her husband. Latin legal writers took the word over as parapherna, and medieval Latin formed paraphernalia bona, "paraphernal goods," which English law borrowed in the 17th century as paraphernalia: the wife's personal goods beyond the dowry. In the 18th century the legal sense decayed and the word broadened to mean simply miscellaneous belongings or equipment — the supporting stuff that accompanies anything. The modern institutional use is a further broadening: the paraphernalia of a profession are its supporting apparatus, the surrounding machinery of the core activity. Usage and collocations:
  • Common collocations: all the paraphernalia, the paraphernalia of, drug paraphernalia, photographic paraphernalia, writing paraphernalia, ceremonial paraphernalia.
  • Register: neutral to slightly formal; in everyday use often humorous or gently disparaging, implying clutter or excess.
  • Family words: paraphernal (obsolete adjective), parapherna (Greek plural, the legal origin); no common English verb or adjective derived from it.
  • Form: plural in form and origin (Latin neuter plural) but treated as singular or uncountable: "the paraphernalia is kept in the cabinet."
