COGNIZANCE, noun (British spelling cognisance). At its core, cognizance denotes knowledge or awareness — but more precisely, and more interestingly, it denotes the formal recognition or notice taken of something by a person, court, or authority. The word lives at a high register: it is the vocabulary of the law court, the herald’s office, the scholarly commentator, and the bureaucratic record. To “take cognizance of” a fact is not merely to know it; it is to notice it deliberately, to acknowledge it officially, and to enter it upon the record so that it counts in the proceedings. In the sentence from the editorial apparatus surrounding the novel — “He noted that cognizance of these other excellences can only be taken in the future parts of his work” — the word works at its most formal register. The writer is promising that certain “excellences” will be granted formal recognition only later, when the “future parts of his work” reach them. The nuance is of knowing institutionalized — awareness raised to the level of an official act, the way a court takes “judicial cognizance” of a fact so that no further proof is needed. The sentence deliberately withholds recognition even as it promises it, and cognizance is the word that makes that gesture of deferred acknowledgment possible. The primary senses of COGNIZANCE:
  • Knowledge or awareness. The general sense: perception, consciousness, or understanding of something. A person “beyond the cognizance” of a fact is beyond knowing it; the word here is a formal synonym for awareness or knowledge.
  • Formal notice or recognition by an authority. The distinctive legal and institutional sense. A court “takes judicial cognizance of” a fact — accepts it as proven without requiring further evidence; an official body “takes cognizance of” a matter — agrees to consider it formally. This is the sense in the Ruskin-adjacent editorial sentence above: cognizance is something taken, a deliberate act of acknowledgment.
  • Jurisdiction; the right to take judicial notice. In law, a court’s cognizance is its right and power to hear and determine a case or matter; to be “within the cognizance of” a court is to fall within its jurisdiction.
  • A distinguishing mark or badge; a coat of arms or device. The heraldic sense: a cognizance is a badge, crest, or device by which a person or family is recognized — the visual emblem of identity, from the same root as ‘recognize’.
A secondary, technical family of senses extends the word into formal and procedural domains:
  • Judicial cognizance / judicial notice. In Anglo-American law, the principle that a court will accept certain facts (laws, calendars, geography, notorious facts) as true without proof. The phrase is the modern descendant of the older “take cognizance of.”
  • Heraldic and ceremonial cognizance. In medieval and Renaissance heraldry, a cognizance (or livery badge) was worn by retainers and servants of a noble house as a mark of identity and allegiance — literally a way of being ‘known’.
  • Cognitive and psychological transferred uses. In philosophy and psychology, cognizance may appear as a formal synonym for cognition or mental grasp — the taking of something into the mind as an object of knowledge. Modern British legal usage also writes cognisance without the ‘z’.
The word’s meaning is built on the verb to know raised to the level of official act. Unlike knowledge, which is a static possession of facts, cognizance is active and deliberate: it is knowledge put to use, knowledge that recognizes and acknowledges. Its formal register makes it the natural companion of legal, scholarly, and administrative prose, and it is nearly always found in the company of the verb take — “to take cognizance of” — which treats knowing as something one performs, records, and acts upon. COGNIZANCE must be set against its neighbours in the field of knowing. (1) Knowledge is the possession of facts and understanding — static, passive, and vast (“his knowledge of botany”); cognizance is narrower and more active, the taking of notice, knowledge directed at something specific. (2) Awareness is the everyday, perceptual counterpart (“she was aware of the noise”); it is informal and bodily, while cognizance is formal and institutional. (3) Recognition overlaps closely — “to recognize” a quality is to acknowledge its existence or validity — but recognition can be instantaneous and personal, while taking cognizance is a considered, recorded act. (4) Notice is the general term for attention paid; cognizance implies the formal, official, deliberate acknowledgment of a fact, often by an authority. In the sentence at hand, the word’s legal and administrative gravity is precisely the point: the excellences will not merely be known about, but formally recognized and recorded — and only in the future parts of the work, when the writer chooses to grant that recognition.
