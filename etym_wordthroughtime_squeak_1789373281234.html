- Late 14th to 15th Century - Emerges in Middle English (*squeken*) as an imitative onomatopoeia denoting the shrill, diminutive distress cry of mice, rats, bats, or wounded game animals.
- 16th to 17th Century - Expands rapidly into mechanical and domestic registers to describe the sharp, grating frictional sounds generated by unoiled hinges, leather boots, wheelbarrows, and dry wagon axles.
- Late 17th to 18th Century - Enters underworld cant and criminal lexicography (canting dictionaries) as slang for confessing under interrogation, turning king's evidence, or betraying comrades to the bailiffs ("to squeak").
- Late 18th to 19th Century - Crystallizes in British colloquial speech into the durable idiom "a narrow squeak" (popularized in Regency sporting literature, schoolboy slang, and Victorian adventure fiction) to designate a close call, narrow escape, or hair's-breadth avoidance of death or defeat.
- 20th Century to Present - Generalizes in political journalism and modern vernacular as a versatile verb and noun ("to squeak by," "to squeak through," "a squeaker") for razor-thin electoral contests, sports victories, and diagnostic tribology ("squeak and rattle").
