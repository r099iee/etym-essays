The pedigree of besieged is a study in how one metaphor — an army "sitting down" before a city — travelled from Indo-European antiquity into the Romance languages and then into English. The chain begins with the PIE root *sed- "to sit", one of the most productive roots in the family: it yields English sit, seat and settle (via Germanic), Latin sedēre "to sit", sedes "seat" and sessio "a sitting" (whence session and sedentary), Greek hédra "seat, chair" (cathedral), and Sanskrit sad-. From sedēre Latin raised a whole family of military compounds by prefixing prepositions: insidēre "to sit in, occupy", and above all obsidēre — ob- "in front of, against" + sedēre — literally "to sit before", used classically (in Caesar and Livy, for instance) of armies blockading and besieging towns. The associated noun obsidio "a blockade, siege" and the agent noun obses "a hostage" (one who 'sits' as a pledge) belong to the same picture: the Roman imagination of siege is of an army encamped and waiting in front of the walls. Native English, tellingly, used the identical metaphor with its own words — Old English besittan (be- + sittan "to sit") meant "to besiege", and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle repeatedly records that an army besæt a town.

Vulgar Latin and the Romance reshaping. The classical verb obsidēre did not survive unchanged into Romance; the spoken Latin of the late Empire reshaped it as *absediāre (with the ab-/ob- prefix blurring common in Vulgar Latin), and from this reconstructed form descend Old French assegier/asségier (Modern French assiéger), Italian assediare and Spanish asediar — the convergent forms of the Romance languages justify the reconstruction. The French noun sege / siège took two lives: first "seat, throne" (from Vulgar Latin *sedicum, the seat of office or power), and then, by the same sitting metaphor, "the encampment of an army before a place" — a besieging army "holds a seat" before the city. English borrowed both: siege in the sense "seat, throne" is recorded from the thirteenth century, and the military sense ("a blockade and investment of a town") appears alongside it; "to lay siege to" is recorded from the fourteenth century.

Middle English formation. The English verb besiege, recorded from about 1300 as bisegen, was not borrowed whole from French but formed on English soil: the prefix be- (here intensive: "to cause to be sieged") was attached to the French-derived verb segen "to besiege", itself from Old French asegier. The formation is thus a hybrid — Germanic prefix + Romance base — of exactly the kind English specialises in. By the fourteenth century the participle besieged was standard in chronicle prose, and the homiletic literature of the period besieges the soul with devils, temptations and worldly cares, opening the figurative career of the word at the same time as its literal one.

Early Modern expansion. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries did two things to besiege. First, siege warfare became the dominant form of European war — the great sieges of the Italian Wars, the Eighty Years' War and the English Civil War made besieged a headline word of the news-sheets. Second, the verb leapt decisively into figurative use: people and passions are besieged in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (hearts besieged by love or grief, consciences besieged by guilt), and this metaphorical habit was quickly naturalised. By the eighteenth century besiege described anything that surrounded a person with pressing importunity — "besieged with petitions and applications" is a stock phrase of Johnsonian prose.

The modern profile. In contemporary English both lives flourish. Literal use remains factual and journalistic — the besieged city, the besieged garrison — and the sieges of Leningrad and Sarajevo in the twentieth century kept the word in the world's headlines. Figurative use has become, if anything, more common: governments are besieged by crises, celebrities by paparazzi, ministers by reporters; in economics and computing, markets and systems are besieged by demand or attacks. The word's emotional valence is of relentless, inescapable pressure from all sides, with the implicit promise that the encirclement may outlast the defenders — which is exactly the dramatic weight Bard's proclamation carries in The Hobbit when he "declares the Mountain besieged" and leaves the dwarves to eat their gold.
