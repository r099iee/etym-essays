Chain for CONNOISSEUR: 1. Proto-Indo-European *ǵneh₃- "to know, recognize, come to know" (one of the great "knowing" roots of Indo-European; also cited as *gen-, *gno-). 2. Latin nōscere "to come to know" (the inchoative "to begin to know"; classical Latin). 3. Latin cognōscere "to get to know thoroughly, to recognize" (com-, intensive, + nōscere; classical Latin). 4. Old French conoistre / connoistre "to know" (the Latin cluster gn palatalized to ɲ and then simplified toward n; 9th–11th c.; modern French connaître). 5. French connaisseur "one who knows; an expert in matters of taste" (connaître + the agentive suffix -eur; the "expert in taste" sense develops in the 17th c.). 6. English CONNOISSEUR "an expert judge in matters of taste" (borrowed early 18th c., first recorded c. 1710) — OUR CARD. Notes: - The same PIE root *ǵneh₃- produced Greek gignōskein "to come to know" and gnōsis "knowledge" (English gnosis, diagnosis, prognosis, gnomic), Sanskrit jñā- "to know" (Hindi gyān ज्ञान "knowledge, spiritual insight"), and the Germanic stem *knē- / *kun- behind English know, ken, can, cunning, and German kennen, können. - Spelling nuance: English kept the spelling connoisseur (with -oi-) while modern French reformed it to connaisseur (with -ais-); the English spelling reflects the older French orthography current when the word was borrowed. - No dispute attaches to the root itself; the only nuance is orthographic.
