The word withal is a fossilised compound of two of the oldest Germanic function words, with + all, literally “with all (that)” or “with it all.” Its long evolution essay must therefore track both elements and then their fusion in Middle English into an adverb, preposition and conjunction that once did the work now divided among besides, moreover, also, with, thereby and notwithstanding. In Old English the two parts were still fully independent. Wið meant primarily “against, opposite, toward” — the adversarial sense still preserved in withstand, withdraw, withhold — and only later bleached into the comitative “together with.” Eall meant “whole, entire, every.” The collocation wið ealle / mid ealle meaning “with all, altogether, in addition” is already common in Old English prose and glosses.

Middle English regularised the phrase as one word: withalle, with all, wythall (c. 1200 onward). Ormulum, Layamon, Chaucer and Wycliffe use it freely in three overlapping grammatical roles that Modern English has since split apart:

  • Adverbial “moreover, besides, in addition”: “He was a lord withal” = he was a lord in addition to all else. This is the Dickensian use — “foggy withal” = foggy in addition to being cold, bleak and biting.
  • Instrumental / comitative “therewith, with it, by it”: “smite withal” = smite with it; “content him withal” = content himself with it. Shakespeare is full of this: “buy the fee-simple of my life for an hour and a quarter … withal …”
  • Concessive / adversative “notwithstanding, nevertheless”: “poor withal honest” = poor and yet honest. This bridges to withal as a discourse connector signalling reservation.
  • Prepositional “with” (now obsolete): “withal his sword” = with his sword. By 1500 this use was already receding before plain with.

Phonetically almost nothing happened: Old English wið /wiθ/ voiced to /wɪð/ in Middle English under Scandinavian and southern influence, while eall /æɑll/ monophthongised to /al/ and then /ɔːl/. The stress settled firmly on the second syllable in the adverbial use, /wɪˈðɔːl/, distinguishing it from the phrase with all. Spelling fluctuated between withal, withall, wythall until Johnson’s Dictionary (1755) and 19th-century printers fixed withal for the single word and with all for the open phrase.

Socio-culturally, withal rose with bureaucratic and biblical Middle English — Wycliffe’s Bible uses “withal” dozens of times to render Latin cum, insuper, quoque — became a high-style elegance in Spenser, Shakespeare, Milton and the King James Bible (“a man of war … withal …”), then fell steeply after 1700 as prescriptive grammarians and plain-style essayists preferred besides, also, too, moreover. By Jane Austen’s time it was already archaising; by Dickens’s time (1843, A Christmas Carol) it was a deliberate archaism that lent fog, law and pulpit gravity. Dickens uses “foggy withal” precisely to sound like an older chronicle voice, thickening the weather catalogue: cold, bleak, biting — and foggy into the bargain.

Literary attestations anchor the drift:

  • c. 1200 Ormulum: “witt all …” in the fused sense “altogether.”
  • c. 1387 Chaucer, Canterbury Tales: “Ther was also a Reve … withal …” to add a trait.
  • 1611 King James Bible: “And withal they learn to be idle …” (1 Tim. 5:13).
  • c. 1600 Shakespeare, Henry IV, Merchant of Venice: instrumental “I will … pay … withal.”
  • 1843 Dickens, A Christmas Carol: “foggy withal” — additive, bookish, weather-heaping.
  • Modern survival: almost only in legal formulae, hymns, crossword puzzles and conscious pastiche; otherwise replaced by also, too, as well, besides.

In short, withal travelled from a transparent Germanic phrase “with everything” to a compact connective that could add, equip or concede, then shrank under competition from simpler adverbs into a stylistic relic. Understanding that trajectory explains why a modern reader stumbles on Dickens’s colon — “foggy withal:” — where a 17th-century reader would have glided: it means “foggy too, foggy as well as all that just listed.” No PIE root beyond the two Germanic pieces is needed, and no new sense has emerged since 1900 except ironic-archaic reuse.
