Slinking is the present participle of slink, meaning to move quietly, stealthily, and furtively, with the body drawn low or close and an air of guilt, fear, or predatory caution. In the literary context of this note, from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the narrator explains that the deepest caves "go back in their beginnings to ages before the goblins," and that the "original owners are still there in odd corners, slinking and nosing about." The word's nuance here is primordial and animallike: these are not walkers but creepers, ancient things that keep to corners, shrink from notice, and glide rather than step — Tolkien sets them before us as a patch of unease moving in the dark, and the pairing "slinking and nosing" fuses stealthy locomotion with blind, questing curiosity. The tone is hushed and slightly ominous, carrying the narrator's half-warning that what belongs to the deep places has never been displaced, only endured.

Primary sense (literal). The core meaning is a mode of movement: to move stealthily, especially in a crouched, gliding, self-effacing way, so as not to be seen or heard. Unlike walking, slinking is deliberately unobtrusive; unlike running, it is slow and careful. The body draws in on itself, shoulders hunched, as if trying to become smaller than it is.

Secondary senses and uses.
  • Guilt and shame: the most distinctive modern use is moral — slinking is how one moves after defeat or disgrace; the idiom "slink off with one's tail between one's legs" (of a beaten dog, applied to humans) fixes the word in the realm of shame and defeat.
  • Predation and animal movement: in zoological description, slinking describes the low, quiet approach of a predator or a wary wild animal (a slinking fox, a cat slinking through grass).
  • Technical/rural sense: from the 17th century, a "slink calf" is one born prematurely; the semantic bridge is the idea of something that "slips away" before its time — the root sense of gliding, withdrawing motion applied to birth.
  • Register: the word is vivid and sensory, appropriate to narrative; it is not formal or technical but belongs to storytelling, animal description, and reproachful idiom.

Grammar note. As a present participle, slinking denotes an ongoing or habitual activity rather than a single completed act; in Tolkien's sentence the -ing form makes the furtive motion a permanent condition of the cave-owners — they are always slinking, in odd corners, about. The verb slink is intransitive in this sense and is often followed by a directional adverb (slink away, slink off, slink about), matching the sentence's "slinking and nosing about."

Historical note and kinship. The word is purely Germanic in ancestry, descending from Old English slincan, "to creep, crawl," a verb used of serpents and creeping things, with a close cognate in Old High German slinkan. Its semantic history is one of steady darkening: from neutral, physical crawling it narrowed to stealthy movement and then, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, acquired the unmistakable colour of guilt and shame that it carries today. That moral charge is the whole point of its literary use — a slinker is never merely quiet but quietly ashamed. In the sentence before us the participle therefore does not just describe the creatures' gait; it insinuates that these original owners skulk about as beings who know they have been displaced, yet refuse to go — unease given legs, moving forever in odd corners.
