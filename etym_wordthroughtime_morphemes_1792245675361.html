- 1896 (Late 19th c.) — Coined in French as morphème by Polish-Russian linguist Jan Baudouin de Courtenay, modeled on phonème to establish a rigorous taxonomy of linguistic building blocks.
- 1920s–1930s — Adopted into American structural linguistics and popularized globally by Leonard Bloomfield in his seminal textbook Language (1933), defining it as the minimal meaningful grammatical unit.
- 1950s–1960s — Reconceptualized in generative grammar and formal morphology by Noam Chomsky, Morris Halle, and later distributed morphology, formalizing the interface between syntax and phonological spell-out.
- Late 20th c. — Integrated into psycholinguistics, cognitive neuroscience, and reading science, proving that the human brain parses complex words via internal morpheme recognition during reading.
- 21st c. — Crucial foundation in computational linguistics, natural language processing, and modern subword tokenizers (e.g. BPE, WordPiece) powering large language models.
