Old English origins (c. 1000). The verb bealcan "to belch, eructate" is first recorded in Old English, appearing in glossaries and medical-manuscript contexts where bodily functions were named without embarrassment. It belongs to a tight Germanic family: Old English bealg/belg "bag, skin, bellows" (a thing that swells), Old English bealgor "anger, fury", Old High German belchan "to belch", and later German bölken. The whole cluster radiates from a Proto-Germanic root of the shape *belg-/*belk- built on the sense of "swelling and bursting", which etymologists connect to the PIE root *bhel- "to blow, swell, grow" (the same family that yields English blow, ball, bellow, and blade).

Sound shifts into Middle English. Through normal Old English development the vowel and consonants changed: bealcan (with its diphthong ea, pronounced roughly /bæɑlkɑn/) became Middle English belchen/belken, with the velar stop -k- palatalising to the affricate -ch- /tʃ/. The modern pronunciation /beltʃ/ descends directly from this Middle English form. By the 13th–14th centuries the verb had already acquired its second, figurative sense of "discharge violently", appearing in alliterative verse and romance where smoke, fire, and even breath "belch" out of vents, hearths, and monsters.

Semantic drift: from digestion to conflagration. The bridge between senses is force and pressure: a burp is a burst of gas under pressure, and medieval writers transferred exactly that profile to furnaces, dragons, and cannon. By the 16th century the figurative sense was fully standard — Shakespeare and his contemporaries used "belch" of fire and gunfire. The literal sense simultaneously slipped down-market: as table manners hardened in the 17th–19th centuries, the word was increasingly judged coarse and vulgar in polite society, a judgement that survives in modern etiquette handbooks even though the word is now mildly comic rather than obscene.

Industrial and environmental age. The 19th-century factory and railway world adopted the word with relish: engines, chimneys, and furnaces "belch" smoke and soot, and environmental journalism has used it ever since. The 20th century added internal-combustion exhaust and industrial pollution imagery, making "belching" almost a technical descriptor for choked particulate emission — "the stacks belched black smoke".

Modern status. Belch today is a word with three coexisting registers: (1) everyday-colloquial for the bodily burp, often comic; (2) grand-literary for dragons, volcanoes, and fire, exactly as in the Tolkien sentence here; (3) neutral-technical in engineering and environmental writing. The past tense belched is a regular formation (verb + -ed) and has been since Middle English; the form was stable for centuries, so the inflected word carries the full weight of the base verb's history.

Key literary attestations run from Old English glosses (c. 1000) and alliterative romance through Renaissance drama and Victorian industrial prose to modern fantasy — Tolkien's The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings use the word repeatedly for Smaug and the crack of the world's fires, cementing its dragonish, fiery flavour in the popular imagination.
