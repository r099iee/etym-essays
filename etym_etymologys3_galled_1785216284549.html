The biography of galled begins, improbably, with colour. The word descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *ghel-, which meant "to shine, be bright," and which named the yellow-green tints of new spring growth and of things that gleam with the freshness of life. From that single root the daughter languages built an enormous family. In Greek it produced khlōros "greenish-yellow," the ancestor of chlorophyll, chlorine, and the name Chloe; and, because bile is yellow-green, it produced cholē "bile," the source of English choler and melancholy — in the old humoral medicine, the man of black bile was the melancholic, the man of yellow bile the choleric. In Latin the same root gave fēl "bile, gall." In the Indo-Aryan branch it gave Sanskrit hari- "yellowish, tawny" and harita- "green," which live on in Hindi and Odia harā "green." Everywhere the root points to the shimmering yellowness of the natural world.

In the Germanic branch the root produced Proto-Germanic *gallōn "bile," and Old English inherited it as gealla, the everyday word for the bitter liver-fluid. In Old and Middle English, gall was simultaneously a bodily fluid and a moral metaphor: "gall and wormwood" named the bitterness of spirit, and a bitter man was said to be full of gall. The great shift came in the fourteenth century, when the noun took on a startlingly concrete new sense — the raw, inflamed sore raised on a horse's back by a chafing saddle. The abraded flesh of such a wound reddens and weeps a yellowish discharge, and medieval understanding connected that yellowish fluid with the bilious humour; a sore that seemed to fill with a kind of bile was therefore called a gall. The word thus moved from the inside of the body to its surface, from a fluid to a wound — a classic instance of semantic transfer by perceived similarity of colour and substance.

From the wound the verb grew. In the 1550s English formed to gall, "to chafe, to make sore by rubbing," and within two decades the figurative sense had flowered: by the 1570s one could speak of a galling disappointment, a galling injustice, a remark that galled a man's pride. The semantic transfer is transparent — the same steady, wearing pressure that abrades the skin, applied to the spirit, abrades the self-respect. Shakespeare used the verb in both registers, and the Elizabethans loved the word for its blend of the physical and the psychological. The sense of "to have the gall" — to possess impudent, brazen boldness — belongs to the nineteenth century: the bitterness of bile is turned outward into bitter nerve, so that a man who "has the gall" is one whose effrontery is as thick and bitter as the yellow humour.

By the twentieth century the participle galled had settled into its modern double life: literally of skin abraded by friction, figuratively of a spirit rubbed raw by humiliation. Its grittiest power is that it holds both senses alive at once, which is exactly how Tolkien deploys it when the Ring galls Gollum. The Ring is a hard, heavy object in a pouch against the skin, and it galls him literally; but it is also an obsession, a secret, a loss that rubs against his mind, and it galls him in the older, bitter sense — a bilious wound to the soul. The word's thousand-year journey from a colour root, to a bodily fluid, to a saddle sore, to a verb of humiliation, and finally to a figure of obsessive torment is compressed into that single participle, still carrying the ancient yellowness of bile at its core — a reminder that the sharpest irritations, physical and moral alike, have been described since antiquity in the language of a bitter, yellow-green fluid.
