FORBEARANCE is the noun formed from the verb forbear ("to hold back, to refrain, to endure patiently"), and it names the act or quality of holding oneself back: patient self-control, restraint, and tolerance exercised under provocation, difficulty, or temptation; and, in specialized legal and financial English, the deliberate refraining from enforcing a right, collecting a debt, or taking legal action. It is a word of dignified restraint — the power not to do what one could do, not to take what one could take, not to punish what one could punish.

Core senses
  • Patient self-control (primary sense): Forbearance is the quality of enduring provocation, delay, or hardship without losing composure or retaliating — a near-synonym of patience, but patience exercised specifically when one has the power to act and chooses not to.
  • Refraining from retaliation: In ethics and religion, forbearance is the virtue of not paying back evil with evil, of forgiving rather than avenging — a cardinal virtue in Christian and other moral traditions, praised as "long-suffering."
  • Legal sense ("forbearance to sue"): In contract law, forbearance is the refraining from bringing a lawsuit or enforcing a legal right, and it is often itself the "consideration" that makes a contract binding — the promise not to sue given in exchange for payment or other performance.
  • Banking and finance ("mortgage forbearance"): In modern finance, forbearance is the temporary postponement or reduction of loan payments granted by a lender to a borrower in financial distress — a formal relief program in which the lender agrees not to foreclose or to collect for a defined period.
  • Tolerance of others' faults: By extension, forbearance is the tolerant putting-up-with of others' weaknesses, mistakes, or offensive behavior without complaint or punishment.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge
The verb forbear is native English, from Old English forberan, built from the prefix for- "away, off, refraining" and beran "to bear, carry." Its oldest sense was "to hold oneself back, to abstain, to endure." The noun forbearance simply nominalizes this: it first meant "abstinence, the act of holding back," then "patient endurance," and then, as English law and commerce developed, "the deliberate not-doing of a thing one has a right to do." The modern financial sense (a lender forbears from collecting) is a direct, contemporary application of the same ancient idea: to forbear is to refrain. The word's whole career is one consistent image — hands held back, power unexercised.

Related words and grammar
  • forbear (v.): to refrain from doing; to hold back; to be patient. (Careful: distinct from the rare noun forebear "ancestor," differently spelled.)
  • forbearing (adj.): patient, tolerant, long-suffering.
  • forbearingly (adv.): with forbearance.

Register and use
Forbearance is a formal, dignified word — at home in religious and ethical writing, in legal documents, and in financial news ("the bank granted a mortgage forbearance"). It is rarer in casual speech than patience or restraint, and it always carries the moral flavor of self-control exercised in the face of temptation or provocation.
