Derivation chain for coronets (base: coronet) — a single linear chain from the Indo-European root to the present English form: 1. Proto-Indo-European *sker- "to turn, bend, curve, circle" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the root of curves, rings, and wreaths; the same root gives English "curve," "circus," "circle," and "ring." 2. Greek κορωνός (korōnós) "curved, bent" (derived adjective; attested from the classical Greek period, c. 5th c. BCE) → Greek κορώνη (korṓnē) "anything curved; the curved end of a bow; a garland or wreath" (feminine of κορωνός; attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE). 3. Latin corōna "garland, wreath, crown" (borrowed from Greek κορώνη; attested from the 2nd c. BCE) — first a wreath of laurel or flowers, then the metal crown and badge of honour of emperors. 4. Late Latin coronella "a small garland" (diminutive of corōna; late antique period) — the first morphological "small crown." 5. Old French coronete / coronette "small crown" (diminutive of corone, from Latin corōna; attested from the 12th c.) — the word of chivalric and heraldic usage. 6. Middle English coronet "small crown worn by nobility below the sovereign" (borrowed c. 1400). 7. English coronets (plural; the note's exact inflected form) — in modern heraldry, historical fiction, and equine terminology. Note on debate and nuance: Latin corōna is itself a loanword from Greek, and the precise route of the English borrowing (directly from Old French coronete, or via Middle French couronnette, possibly reinforced by Italian coronetta) is a minor point of scholarly debate; the OED gives Old French as the immediate source. The Greek word κορώνη "the crow (the bird)" looks identical but is a homophone from a different root (*ker-, an onomatopoeic bird-call) and does not feed this word.
