• Kingston upon Hull: Similar because it is the formal, full civic name of the English city commonly called Hull; differs because 'Kingston upon Hull' is the official ceremonial and legal designation created by Edward I's charter, whereas 'Hull' is the standard conversational and historical shorthand.
  • Keel: Similar because both are fundamental components of a ship's structure; differs because the keel is the central longitudinal structural beam running along the very bottom of the vessel, whereas the hull is the entire outer watertight shell and framework.
  • Fuselage: Similar because both represent the central structural body of a transportation craft; differs because a fuselage is the aerodynamic body of an aircraft housing passengers and cargo, whereas a hull is the hydrodynamic buoyant body of a marine vessel.
  • Husk: Similar because both refer to the dry protective outer layer of seeds and fruits; differs because 'husk' is predominantly applied to corn, cereal grains, and coconuts, carrying connotations of dryness and fibrous texture, whereas 'hull' is also applied to soft calyxes (like strawberries) and vessels.
  • Chaff: Similar because both denote the outer casing of cereal grains; differs because 'chaff' specifically refers to the discarded, lightweight husks separated during threshing and winnowing, often used metaphorically for worthless matter.
  • Pod: Similar because both are botanical structures enclosing seeds; differs because a pod is a specialized dehiscent seed vessel (like a pea or bean pod) that splits along seams, whereas a hull is a general protective outer casing.
