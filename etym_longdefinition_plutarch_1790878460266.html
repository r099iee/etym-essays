Plutarch (c. 46 – 119 CE; Ancient Greek: Πλούταρχος, Ploútarkhos; later named Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus upon acquiring Roman citizenship) was a preeminent Greek biographer, essayist, Middle Platonist philosopher, and magistrate whose voluminous literary works served as one of the paramount intellectual bridges transmitting classical Hellenic culture, ethics, and Roman historical memory to the Western world.

Born into an ancient, aristocratic family in the small Boeotian town of Chaeronea, Plutarch lived during the zenith of the Roman Empire under the Flavian emperors, Nerva, and Trajan. Deeply loyal to his provincial homeland, he chose to remain in Chaeronea throughout his life ("lest it should become even smaller," as he quipped), while maintaining active cultural, intellectual, and political ties in Athens, Rome, and Alexandria. For roughly three decades, he served as one of the two senior priests of Apollo at the sacred Oracle of Delphi, overseeing religious rites, philosophical discussions, and architectural restorations at the Panhellenic sanctuary.

Major Literary Corpora:

  • Parallel Lives (Bioi Paralleloi): Plutarch's most famous and enduring masterpiece consists of a series of paired biographies comparing prominent Greek and Roman figures of comparable achievements or character (such as Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar, Demosthenes and Cicero, Pericles and Fabius Maximus, Theseus and Romulus, Alkibiades and Coriolanus). Rather than writing exhaustive political or military chronicles, Plutarch explicitly pursued ethical portraiture, declaring in his life of Alexander that he was writing lives (bioi), not histories (historiai), and that a casual word, a minor habit, or an unthinking jest often reveals a person's moral character more transparently than massive military campaigns involving tens of thousands of casualties.
  • Moralia (Ethika): An astonishingly eclectic collection of over seventy surviving treatises, philosophical dialogues, speeches, and open letters covering moral philosophy, religious theology, daemonology, psychology, political advice, pedagogical theory, marital ethics, dietary habits, and scientific curiosities. Prominent essays include On the Delays of the Divine Vengeance, On the Obsolescence of Oracles, On the Daemonic Sign of Socrates, Sayings of Kings and Commanders, Whether an Old Man Should Engage in Public Affairs, and On the Cleverness of Animals.

Extended & Metaphorical Senses:

  • The Archetype of Comparative Biography: In literary criticism, "a Plutarch" has become a recognized eponym denoting any author who composes paired or comparative character studies to extract moral and psychological insights from history.
  • Ethical Historiography & Character Study: In the philosophy of history, the "Plutarchan method" denotes an approach that views history not as the product of impersonal socioeconomic mechanics, but as the lived drama of individual moral choices, virtues, vices, and ethical education.
  • Fountainhead of Renaissance Literature & Drama: Plutarch served as a cornerstone of Renaissance humanism. Translated into French by Jacques Amyot in 1559 and subsequently into Early Modern English by Sir Thomas North in 1579, Plutarch's vivid psychological sketches directly inspired William Shakespeare, who adapted whole speeches and narrative arcs almost verbatim into Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus, and Timon of Athens.
  • Political Catalyst of the Enlightenment & Revolutions: In 18th-century political philosophy, Plutarch's portraits of Spartan austerity, Athenian democracy, and Roman civic virtue profoundly shaped thinkers such as Michel de Montaigne, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Alexander Hamilton, Thomas Jefferson, and the ideologues of the French Revolution, who regarded his biographies as sacred secular scriptures of republican liberty and civic devotion.
