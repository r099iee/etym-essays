- Late 14th c. - Borrowed into Middle English as impetuous (from Old French), initially used to describe violent physical phenomena like raging storms, cataracts, and unbridled elemental forces.
- 16th c. - Tudor & Elizabethan drama transferred the term to human emotions and royal temperament, describing fierce, uncurbed martial zeal or fiery amorous passion.
- 17th-18th c. - The noun impetuousness crystallized in moral and philosophical prose as an explicit personality defect, contrasted systematically against classical virtues of prudence, temperance, and stoic self-governance.
- 19th c. - Romantic and Victorian literature romanticized the quality in youth and revolutionary heroes as an endearing, spirited ardour, while warning of its tragic outcomes in domestic and financial life.
- 20th-21st c. - Settled into standard literary and psychological registers, frequently collocated with youth, executive haste, and dramatic narrative turning points.
