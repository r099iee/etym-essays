Anarchism is a comprehensive political philosophy, social theory, and historical movement that advocates for the abolition of all involuntary, coercive state authority, institutionalized hierarchy, and compulsory governance in favor of a society organized entirely through voluntary cooperation, mutual aid, decentralized federation, and direct self-management. Rooted etymologically in the concept of a community existing without an overarching ruler or coercive master, anarchism rejects the assertion that sovereign state power is necessary to preserve public peace, enforce justice, or maintain social order. Instead, anarchists contend that the state is an inherently oppressive instrument that protects class privilege, concentrates wealth, wages imperialist warfare, and stifles human autonomy.

The philosophical scope and historical expressions of anarchism encompass several major classical traditions, modern technical frameworks, and contemporary offshoots:

  • Classical Mutualism: Systematized by Pierre-Joseph Proudhon in the mid-nineteenth century, mutualism proposes an anti-capitalist, free-market socialist order based on the labor theory of value, reciprocal credit institutions (people's banks offering interest-free loans), and worker-owned cooperatives. In mutualist theory, private property based on absentee ownership is replaced by "possession"—the right to occupy and use land or productive tools—ensuring that workers retain the full fruits of their labor without exploitation by landlords or capitalist employers.
  • Collectivist Anarchism: Championed by the Russian revolutionary Mikhail Bakunin during his fierce debates with Karl Marx inside the First International, collectivist anarchism insists that the means of production must be collectively owned and democratically managed by producer associations. Unlike state socialists who advocated a "dictatorship of the proletariat", Bakunin warned that seizing state machinery would merely create a despotic "red bureaucracy", arguing that the state must be dismantled immediately upon the outbreak of social revolution.
  • Anarcho-Communism (Communist Anarchism): Formulated most cogently by Peter Kropotkin, Errico Malatesta, and Emma Goldman, anarcho-communism extends collective ownership from the means of production to the products of labor themselves. Rejecting wage systems, labor vouchers, and market exchange, communist anarchists envision a society operating on the principle "from each according to their ability, to each according to their needs", sustained by the natural human propensity for voluntary solidarity, which Kropotkin documented biologically in Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902).
  • Anarcho-Syndicalism: A militant labor-oriented current that emerged in late nineteenth-century France and achieved massive historical influence in early twentieth-century Spain (exemplified by the National Confederation of Labour, or CNT, during the Spanish Revolution of 1936). Anarcho-syndicalists view revolutionary industrial trade unions as both the weapon of class struggle (wielding direct action, sabotage, and the general strike) and the embryonic framework of a future libertarian socialist society.
  • Individualist and Egoist Anarchism: Rooted in the radical philosophy of Max Stirner (author of The Ego and Its Own) and American individualists such as Benjamin Tucker and Lysander Spooner, this tradition places absolute emphasis on the individual sovereign self. Stirner rejected all abstract "spooks" (the state, morality, religion, rights, nationalism) that demand individual submission, advocating instead an unconstrained union of egoists based on immediate mutual self-interest.

Beyond its traditional historical doctrines, the term has undergone extensive polysemic expansion into diverse contemporary domains, technical fields, and metaphorical registers:

  • Popular Colloquial Sense vs. Philosophical Sense: In popular media, sensationalist journalism, and common parlance, anarchism (or anarchy) is frequently used pejoratively as a synonym for violent chaos, nihilistic vandalism, mob rule, lawless looting, and the breakdown of civil order. Anarchist theoreticians have fiercely contested this semantic conflation for over two centuries, pointing out that Proudhon explicitly defined anarchy as "Order without Power" and maintaining that true anarchism represents organic, cooperative organization rather than disorganization.
  • Epistemological Anarchism (Philosophy of Science): Introduced by Austrian philosopher of science Paul Feyerabend in his seminal 1975 work Against Method. Feyerabend argued that no single prescriptive scientific method or rulebook can account for the historical development of science; true intellectual and scientific breakthroughs require epistemological anarchism—encapsulated in his provocative maxim "anything goes"—wherein scientists freely break dogmatic methodological constraints.
  • Cryptographic Anarchism (Crypto-Anarchy): A late twentieth- and twenty-first-century technological movement spearheaded by the cypherpunks (formalized in Timothy C. May's 1988 The Crypto Anarchist Manifesto). It asserts that strong public-key cryptography, anonymous communication networks (like Tor), and decentralized cryptographic currencies (such as Bitcoin) can empower individuals to engage in economic transactions and free speech entirely outside the surveillance, taxation, and regulatory purview of sovereign nation-states.
  • Pedagogical Anarchism (Libertarian Education): An educational philosophy advocating the abolition of coercive grading, standardized state curricula, and authoritarian school discipline (exemplified by Francisco Ferrer's Modern School movement, A. S. Neill's Summerhill School, and Ivan Illich's concept of "deschooling society"), emphasizing autonomous, child-led inquiry and voluntary collaborative learning.
  • Aesthetic, Cultural, and Musical Expressions: From the artistic disruptions of Zurich Dada and Situationist International detournement to the explosive mid-1970s punk rock movement (ignited by the Sex Pistols' anthem "Anarchy in the U.K."), anarchism has repeatedly served as a potent countercultural symbol of anti-establishment defiance, DIY (do-it-yourself) ethics, and resistance to commercial commodification.
