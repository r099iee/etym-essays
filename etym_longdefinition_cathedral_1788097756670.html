1. The core sense. A cathedral is the principal church of a Christian diocese or archdiocese — the "mother church" — and, strictly, the church that contains the bishop's cathedra, his official chair or throne, from which the diocese is governed. The word is inseparable from the idea of the see: a bishop's seat gives the building its identity, and the see of a diocese is exactly the cathedra concept under a different name, since the English word see comes from Latin sedes, "seat". A church without a cathedra is not, canonically, a cathedral, however grand it may be.


2. The seat made stone. Because cathedrals house the teaching chair (cathedra is the Greek word for a seat or armchair, and teaching "from the chair" is ex cathedra, the very phrase used of the Pope's infallible pronouncements), the building is simultaneously a worshipping house and a seat of authority: the cathedra traditionally stands behind or beside the high altar, and the building's scale, choir, chapter and endowments express the bishop's dignity and the diocese's wealth. Cathedrals are therefore not merely large churches; they are the juridical headquarters of a church region, and their architecture of towers, spires, rose windows, flying buttresses and vaulted naves grew up precisely to embody that authority in stone.



  • Governance: cathedrals are staffed by a chapter of clergy — the dean and the canons — who maintain daily worship and the cathedral's ancient offices; cathedral chapters historically elected bishops and ran cathedral schools, the medieval forerunners of the universities, from Paris to Oxford.

  • Original adjectival sense: early English usage treats cathedral as an adjective — "the cathedral church", "cathedral clergy", "cathedral see" — and this adjectival core survives in everyday phrases: cathedral city (a city with a cathedral), cathedral close (the enclosed precinct around the church), cathedral school, cathedral music.

  • Music sense: cathedral music denotes the great Anglican choral tradition — professional choirs of men and boys, pipe organs and daily choral evensong — centred on foundations such as St Paul's in London, Durham and York, and on the collegiate choirs of Oxford and Cambridge that share the same repertory and training lineage.

  • Architectural and design senses: cathedral ceiling, cathedral window and cathedral glass (thick, heavily textured glazing) are building-trade terms that exploit the pointed-arch and soaring-height associations of Gothic cathedrals; in real-estate language a "cathedral ceiling" is a high, vaulted, light-filled room ceiling.

  • Metaphorical sense: "cathedrals of industry", "cathedrals of learning", "cathedrals of sport" — any monumental institution or edifice that inspires awe. The word functions as the superlative of grand building, so that the greatest banks, libraries, railway termini, stadiums and museums are routinely called cathedrals of their kind.

  • Variety across traditions: Roman Catholic cathedrals (bishop's cathedra and chapter), Orthodox cathedrals (often governed by senior priests and tied to the bishop's throne), Anglican cathedrals (which absorbed many monastic churches after the Reformation) and modern civic cathedrals such as Liverpool and Coventry show the word stretching from ecclesiastical law through architecture into civic identity and heritage.


3. The semantic bridge. The entire modern career of the word is a widening from chair to church to monument. First the cathedra, the chair, named the church that contained it; then the church of the chair became, by extension, the head church of a whole region; then the architectural grandeur of such churches made cathedral mean "a very grand church"; and finally grandeur detached from any bishop at all, so that any awe-inspiring building or institution, even a stadium or a skyscraper, can be called a cathedral of its world. Authority became monumentality, and monumentality became metaphor.
