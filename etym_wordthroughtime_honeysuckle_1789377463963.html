- Old English Period (c. 9th–11th Century) - Known in Anglo-Saxon herbal texts as *hunigsūge* ("honey-suck"), applied broadly to clover, privet, and wild plants whose blossoms yielded sweet nectar.
- 13th to 14th Century (Middle English) - The frequentative suffix was attached, evolving into *honisoukel*, *honysocle*, or *hunisucle*, gradually narrowing from general nectar-bearing plants to the climbing *Lonicera* vine.
- 16th to 17th Century (Tudor & Elizabethan Eras) - Celebrated in herbal treatises (William Turner, John Gerard) and Renaissance literature (Shakespeare) alongside its poetic synonym *woodbine*, becoming a staple of domestic cottage garden porches.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Firmly established in Victorian floriography as the emblem of generous affection and fidelity; featured in fairy tales and rural fiction (Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*) as the hallmark of idyllic rural contentment.
- 20th to 21st Century (Modern Botanical Era) - Retains beloved status in temperate horticulture for sensory gardening and wildlife landscaping, while environmental agencies monitor aggressive non-native species like Japanese honeysuckle.
