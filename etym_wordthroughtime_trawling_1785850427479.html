- 16th–17th centuries (English establishment) — trawl/trawling perceived as a concrete fishing method: dragging a heavy net to take groundfish; occupational, maritime, largely neutral-technical among seafarers and merchants.
- 18th–19th centuries — expands with commercial fisheries and improved vessels; public association with industry, harbors, and market supply of fish; still primarily literal.
- Late 19th–20th centuries — industrial scale and gear sophistication; “trawling” can index modern efficiency. Growing ecological critique attaches especially to bottom trawling (habitat damage, bycatch), so the word gains contested moral coloring outside industry circles.
- Late 20th century — figurative extension solidifies in journalism and policing/investigation prose: trawling through files, tips, databases.
- 21st century — dual default meanings in educated English: (1) commercial net-tow fishing; (2) systematic broad search through information or places. Perception of the metaphor: thorough, sweeping, sometimes indiscriminate—exactly the net image.
