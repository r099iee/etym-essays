- [Middle English Period (c. 1200–1450)] — Borrowed from Old Norse gapa into Northern and East Midland dialects as gapen, signifying opening the mouth wide to eat, breathe, or stare in astonishment — [perception: earthy, physical verb describing rustic hunger, simple astonishment, and visceral animal reactions]
- [Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700)] — Expanded in Elizabethan drama and translation (Shakespeare, Spenser, Marlowe) to describe ravenous abysses, chasms, and hell-mouths opening wide (hell gapes for him) — [perception: acquired mythic and dramatic power, describing both terrifying voids and dumbfounded crowds]
- [18th c. Enlightenment & Augustan Satire] — Used satirically by Swift, Pope, and Johnson to mock the gullible, slack-jawed London mobs captivated by quacks, street performers, and optical spectacles — [perception: associated with rustic foolishness, naive credulity, and lack of aristocratic decorum]
- [19th c. Victorian Medicine & Geology] — Systematically applied in clinical surgery to lacerations and open wounds (a gaping wound) and in geology to seismic faults and canyon clefts — [perception: established rigorous technical utility alongside ongoing romantic and dramatic use]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Usage] — Ubiquitous across literary fiction, journalism, and fantasy worldbuilding (such as Tolkien's startled hobbits) to depict involuntary awe, shocking news, or physical fissures — [perception: powerful, visceral descriptor of slack-jawed wonder and raw physical openings]
