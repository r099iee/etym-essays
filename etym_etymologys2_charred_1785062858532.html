Proto-Indo-European *gers- ("to turn, bend, wind"; attested in Greek γῦρος "circle, ring, turn, orbit" from *gers-o-, and in Germanic; c. 4000–3000 BCE reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *karzijaną ("to turn, cause to turn," causative formation; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) → Old English cierran / cerran ("to turn, change, convert, go"; attested 8th c.) → Middle English charren / charen ("to turn, to do a turn of work, to convert"; 12th–15th c.), which formed the compound charcole ("turned coal," i.e. charcoal; 14th c.) = charen "to turn" + cole "coal" → Early Modern English charcoal (the blackened residue of smothered wood) → back-formation of the verb char ("to burn to charcoal, scorch, blacken"; 17th c., formed from charcoal) → past participle charred ("scorched, blackened to carbon"; 18th c.). Note on debate and parallel branch: the derivation of charcoal from charren + cole ("turned coal") is standard and widely accepted, but the semantic link is sometimes questioned because charcoal is not literally "turned" in the modern sense; the medieval idea is that wood is "converted" or "turned into" coal by the smothered burning of the charcoal-burner. The same Middle English verb charren produced an independent, parallel branch meaning "a turn of work": char ("an odd job, a chore," 16th c.), charwoman ("a woman hired by the turn or job"), and the 18th-century American chore — a semantic offshoot that shows how polysemous the underlying "turn" verb was. The PIE connection of charred is therefore indirect, arriving entirely through charcoal and the earlier "turn" sense; no stage between PIE *gers- and Proto-Germanic *karzijaną is directly attested.
