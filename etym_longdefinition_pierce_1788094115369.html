PIERCE is a verb meaning "to make a hole in or through something with a sharp instrument; to penetrate; to force a way through or into". In the Tolkien context ("Thorin drove right against the bodyguard of Bolg. But he could not ___ their ranks"), the word carries its full military and physical force: Thorin cannot thrust his way through the dense formation of the enemy bodyguard — the ranks resist penetration like a wall. From this concrete core, pierce has developed a wide field of figurative senses in which a sharp sensory or emotional stimulus penetrates a surface, an organ, a silence, or a state of mind.

Primary senses:

  • To make a hole in (transitive, physical): "to pierce a balloon", "to pierce leather with an awl", "a nail pierced the tyre". The instrument is typically pointed; the result is a perforation.
  • To penetrate, to pass through (transitive): "an arrow pierced his armour", "the tunnel pierces the mountain", "to pierce the ranks" (the Tolkien sense: to break through a line of troops). Here the emphasis is on passing through a resisting medium, not merely making a hole.
  • To affect keenly, to penetrate emotionally (figurative): "a cry that pierced the silence", "the cold pierced him to the bone", "grief pierced her heart". The bridge is the metaphor of a sharp point entering the body or senses: an intense sound, sensation, or emotion "stabs" or "cuts into" the perceiver.
  • Of sound and light (figurative): "a piercing shriek", "piercing eyes", "piercing light" — sensory stimuli that are sharp, penetrating, and intense, as if physically cutting through the air or the senses.
  • To understand, to see through (figurative, now largely literary): "to pierce a mystery", "to pierce the veil" — to penetrate obscurity with the mind; the mind's eye "pierces" what is hidden. This intellectual sense was strong in 16th–18th century English ("piercing wit") and survives in fixed phrases.
  • Body piercing (modern cultural sense, noun and verb): since the late 20th century, piercing names the deliberate perforation of the body (ears, nose, navel, tongue) for jewellery; "to get pierced" is a standard colloquial use.
  • Technical uses: in metallurgy, piercing is the process of making a hole in hot metal (as in seamless-tube manufacture); in medicine, "to pierce a vein"; in tunnelling, a bore or piercing through rock.

Semantic bridges. The single Latin source sense — "to thrust through, to bore" (Latin pertundere, from per- "through" + tundere "to beat, strike") — already contained both "making a hole" and "passing through". English developed the emotional and sensory extensions by analogy: what is sharp enough to enter the flesh is sharp enough to "enter" the ear (a piercing cry), the eye (a piercing gaze), or the heart (piercing grief). The intellectual sense ("to pierce a secret") extends the same image to the mind. The modern body-piercing sense re-concretizes the ancient physical sense after centuries of figurative drift — a return of the word to its literal root in contemporary culture.

Register today. Pierce is neutral-to-formal: perfectly normal in everyday speech ("the thorn pierced my finger") yet strong enough for epic prose — which is why Tolkien uses it for a heroic assault on an enemy formation. Its figurative uses (piercing sound, piercing cold, piercing glance) are standard and vivid, while the intellectual sense is archaic-literary. The derived adjective piercing is extremely common; the noun piercing (body art) is a late-20th-century coinage that has thoroughly entered mainstream vocabulary.
