- Middle English ratelen (c. 1300) - coined directly out of the noise it names: a rapid, unbroken succession of short, sharp sounds; an imitative word with no traceable ancestor.
- 16th c. - the noun rattle appears for the instrument that rattles (a baby's toy, a watchman's alarm); the verb turns figurative, 'to rattle' meaning to disconcert or alarm; and the intensifier 'a rattling good yarn' or 'a rattling pace' is recorded.
- 17th c. - the rattlesnake is named from the horny rings of its tail that rattle as a warning, and the grim collocation death-rattle is fixed.
- Modern - sound and sense have changed remarkably little in seven centuries; the register runs from machines, weather, and alarm (rattling windows, chains, trains, hailstones) to the layered, mechanical percussion of a mountain-side shaking itself apart.
