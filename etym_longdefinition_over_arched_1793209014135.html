Over-arched is the past-participial form of the verb to overarch: spanned by an arch passing overhead, covered from above by something that curves or meets like the stones of a vault. Where something is over-arched, the sky is hidden by a roof that was never built by hands — or by one that was.

In the source sentence the travellers stand in a deep dim-lit gully over-arched by trees high above them: the branches lean in from both banks and knit together overhead, turning the gully into a green tunnel. The hyphen marks the word as a working participle rather than a settled adjective — the trees are actively doing the arching — and the dim light below is the proof of the arch above, since only a closed canopy could steal the day.

Core meaning in detail: To overarch is to make an arch over, whether deliberately or by growth. Masons over-arch doorways with rings of voussoirs; branches over-arch lanes by reaching across until twig meets twig; eyebrows over-arch eyes in the fixed curve of bone and hair. The resulting state — over-arched — always implies both shelter and shadow: what is over-arched is protected from the sky and darkened by the same gesture, which is why poets love the word for bowers, cloisters, naves, and forest paths.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Spanned by an arch overhead (primary physical sense, from the 17th c.): a walk over-arched by elms, a gully over-arched by trees, a doorway over-arched with stone. The bridge from the verb is direct: the arch stands over, and what lies beneath is over-arched.
  • Formed into an arch above something (of the arching thing itself): trees that over-arch the road, eyebrows that over-arch the eyes. Here the grammatical subject is the canopy rather than the sheltered hollow — the same scene viewed from above the grammar instead of below it.
  • Vaulted in architecture (technical): naves, crypts, and bridges described as over-arched where ribs or rings of masonry carry the load overhead. The bridge is constructional: the arch is load-bearing, and the space beneath exists because the arch holds.
  • Overarching — all-embracing in scope (figurative, modern, usually without hyphen): an overarching theme, principle, or narrative that spans a whole field the way an arch spans a roadway. The bridge runs from stone to abstraction: just as the physical arch covers everything beneath it, so the overarching idea covers every case beneath it — beloved of mission statements, prefaces, and literary criticism.
  • Dwarfing or dominating from above (extended): a career over-arched by one great achievement, a childhood over-arched by war. The bridge is the shadow side of shelter: the arch that covers also looms, and what stands beneath it never quite escapes.

Register and connotation: over-arched with its hyphen is literary and slightly archaic — guidebooks, country-house poems, and fantasy novels keep it alive, while engineers say spanned and botanists say closed canopy. Its unhyphenated child overarching belongs to a different world entirely: committees, strategies, and syllabuses, where it is sometimes mocked as jargon precisely because the physical arch has been forgotten. The source sentence uses the older soul of the word, and the dim light in the gully is its credential: no one writes over-arched without meaning the darkness too.
