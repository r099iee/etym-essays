The reconstructed deep history. English mast descends from Old English mæst, "the mast of a ship; a pole", attested in tenth-century prose and glosses and cognate with Old Norse mastr, Old Saxon and Old High German mast, and perhaps Latin mālus, "mast". Behind the Germanic set stands a reconstructed Proto-Germanic *mastaz, and behind that the traditional PIE reconstruction *mazdo-s, "a pole, a staff, a mast", recorded in Pokorny's great comparative dictionary. Caution is required at exactly this point: the PIE root rests on the Germanic and Italic forms alone, its semantics are generic ("pole"), and several modern authorities treat Latin mālus as uncertain or even unrelated, since the sound correspondence (*zd to Latin l) is irregular and the word's formation is opaque. The honest summary is that the Germanic branch is solid and ancient, while the reconstruction beyond it is plausible but debated. What the cognates tell us is social history in miniature: the word is shared by all the Germanic sea-peoples — Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, Saxons, Franks — who sailed the North Sea and the Baltic, which strongly suggests that the mast as a rigging concept belonged to the common Germanic nautical inheritance before the Viking Age, when the square-rigged ship with a single mast and a huge yard was the supreme technology of the northern seas.

Old and Middle English. In Old English, mæst sits within a small constellation of ship words — scip, bāt, naca, wudu — in a poetry where the ship itself is often called a "sea-wood" and its mast, sail, and prow are named with relish. The word appears in glosses, charters, and homilies, and the church's ancient image of the church as a ship under sail carried the mast into devotional prose and verse. Middle English keeps mast unchanged in form, while the two-masted and then three-masted ship arrived in English waters with the cogs and carracks of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries, so that by the age of the great voyages — Columbus's three-masted Santa María, the English galleons of Elizabeth — the mast had acquired its full typology: fore, main, and mizzen, each stepped into the keel, stayed, and rigged. The word's phonetic stability across a thousand years is remarkable: Old English mæst, Middle English mast, modern mast differ only in the vowel of the earliest form.

Age of sail and the word's golden age. The sixteenth to nineteenth centuries are the mast's golden age in language. English seamanship vocabulary exploded: lower mast, topmast, topgallant, royal mast, jury mast (an emergency replacement), dismasted, masthead, before the mast. The naval novel and the sailor's memoir carried these words into general literacy; Richard Henry Dana Jr.'s Two Years Before the Mast (1840) made "before the mast" a permanent phrase for the common sailor's hard life. The idioms "nail one's colours to the mast" (never surrender) and "at half-mast" (mourning) show the mast functioning as the ship's pole of honour — the pole from which identity and grief are flown. On land, meanwhile, the word was borrowed by engineering: the "mast" of a derrick is a seventeenth-century extension, and the "mast" of a windmill or crane followed as tall timber structures multiplied.

Modern migrations. The twentieth century detached the mast from the sea for good: radio masts from the 1890s onward, television masts, antenna masts, and cellular towers are the word's new referents, each carrying invisible cargo where sails once carried visible cloth. The maritime sense has become partly historical — few commercial vessels now carry masts — yet it remains fully alive in yachting, in naval tradition, in art, and in literature, where the mast still stands as the emblem of the sea voyage itself. The word's final migration is abstract: a "mast" is anything tall, straight, and load-bearing, from a derrick to a maypole. Alongside this main line runs the ancient homonym mast, "acorn crop, pig fodder" (Old English mæst, a word of separate origin), a reminder that the sea of language keeps two islands with the same name, unconnected beneath the surface — and that the shipwright's mast, alone among them, still carries the sky.
