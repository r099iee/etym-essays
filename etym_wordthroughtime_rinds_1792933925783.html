- Old English — the bark of the tree: rinde names the rough coat of oak and ash, stripped by tanners and warmed-over by poets.
- Middle English — from wood to orchard: the word spreads to thick fruit peels, the orange and melon coats that resist the knife.
- 15th c. — into the dairy: cheese rinds appear as pressed, salted coats that guard the wheel while it ages.
- Early modern — the farmhouse scrap: bacon and ham rinds become the classic leftover, gnawed by dogs at every hearth in the land.
- 19th–20th c. — the cook's ingredient: candied peel, marmalade, parmesan broth, and watermelon pickle turn the despised coat into delicacies.
- Today — honest and artisanal: rinds smells of cheese counters, craft butchery, and zero-waste kitchens, with a figurative crust (the rind of the earth, a rind of reserve) kept alive by writers.
