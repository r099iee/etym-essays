- 8th–10th century CE — Emerged from Western Gupta Brahmi under the Karkota and Utpala dynasties of Kashmir, establishing itself as the paramount royal and philosophical script of northwestern India — perceived as the elegant medium of high Sanskrit literature, poetics, and Vedic recensions.
- 11th–13th century CE — Attained pan-Indian fame through the intellectual prestige of the Śāradā Pīṭha temple-university along the Kishanganga River; visited by wandering scholars from as far as Tamil Nadu and Bengal — perceived as the living script of the Goddess of Learning.
- 14th–16th century CE — Began evolving regional offshoots across the western Himalayas (ancestor of Takri, Gurmukhi, and Landa); within Kashmir, administrative records gradually transitioned to Persian under the Shah Miri sultanate — perceived increasingly as a specialized sacred script.
- 17th–19th century CE — Contracted into an exclusive socio-religious script preserved primarily by Kashmiri Pandit families for genealogical records, liturgical manuals, and astronomical horoscopes (*patras*) — perceived as an esoteric heritage marker.
- Late 19th–20th century CE — Discovered by Western and Indian indologists (Georg Bühler, Aurel Stein) on birch-bark scrolls, revolutionizing textual criticism of the Rigveda, Atharvaveda, and Kalhana's Rajatarangini — perceived by modern scholars as an indispensable archive of South Asian civilization.
- 21st century — Formally encoded into the international Unicode Standard (Version 6.1, 2012) and championed by diaspora preservationists through digital fonts and manuscript digitization — honored as an endangered cultural monument of global significance.
