- [c. 1300: "to put down"] - Deposen enters Middle English from Old French; at first simply "to set down, lay aside", quickly specialized to removing people from office.
- [14th–16th centuries: the political word] - The word of coups, rebellions and papal schisms: deposing kings, popes and bishops raises the great questions of legitimacy; the deposition of Richard II (1399) haunts English politics.
- [15th century: the legal sense] - A witness "deposes" (lays down) his testimony under oath; both meanings of "putting down" now coexist.
- [1595: the tragic register] - Shakespeare's Richard II makes the deposed king the tragic centre of English drama; "deposed" gains pathos as well as politics.
- [18th–21st centuries: headline and courtroom] - The newspaper word for overthrown rulers everywhere ("deposed dictator", "deposed monarch"); in American law, "deposed" is the everyday term for recorded sworn testimony.
