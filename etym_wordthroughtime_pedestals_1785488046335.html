- [1560s: architectural term] - Borrowed from Italian piedistallo (via French) during the Renaissance vogue, the word is purely architectural: the base supporting a statue, column or vase.
- [17th-18th c.: general literal use] - It spreads through classical-architecture handbooks for any statue- or column-bearing block, remaining a technical, material term.
- [19th c.: the exalting metaphor] - The idiom "put on a pedestal" emerges and takes over: to raise someone or something to a position of supreme, reverent honour.
- [Modern: idealising idiom] - The literal sense recedes behind the metaphor; today "on a pedestal" means uncritically idealised and worshipped, often with an implicit warning against such reverence.
