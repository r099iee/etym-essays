deferred (adjective / past participle of the verb defer) — put off to a later time; postponed; also, in the construction deferred to, yielded or submitted to another's opinion, judgment, or authority. The word is a chameleon of formality: in finance, law, taxation, education, computing, and the military it names technical kinds of postponement; in polite and literary use it can also mean “respectfully yielded to.” It is most often encountered as the past participle (“the meeting was deferred”) or as an attributive adjective (“deferred taxation”), and its homograph relationship with defer “to yield” makes it one of the more nuanced words in the English vocabulary of delay.

Grammatical profile and usage
  • Function: past participle of transitive defer (passive: “was deferred”); also adjective (“deferred payment”); also part of defer to + object (“deferred to his elders”).
  • Register: formal, institutional, and literary; the postponing sense dominates in technical writing, while the yielding sense appears in polite and respectful contexts.
  • Collocations: deferred payment, deferred taxation, deferred revenue, deferred compensation, deferred prosecution, deferred admission, deferred gratification, deferred execution, deferred maintenance.
  • Word family: defer (verb), deference (noun), deferential (adjective), deferral (noun, “the act of postponing”), deferment (noun).
Polysemy — the full range of senses
  • Sense 1 — postponement (primary, 15th c.–present): to put off to a later time; to postpone. “The meeting was deferred until next week,” “the answer arrived which deferred it all” (Austen).
  • Sense 2 — submission (16th c.–present): to yield to another's opinion, judgment, or authority; to defer to. “He deferred to his seniors' wishes,” “the younger members deferred to the chairman.” This is a separate verb of identical spelling, from a different Latin compound (see the derivation chain).
  • Sense 3 — technical, finance/accounting (20th c.): deferred taxation, deferred revenue, deferred income, deferred compensation — money or tax whose recognition is postponed to a later accounting period.
  • Sense 4 — technical, law (20th c.): deferred prosecution (an agreement to postpone criminal proceedings), deferred judgment, deferred sentence.
  • Sense 5 — technical, education/employment (20th c.): deferred admission (entering college a year late), deferred acceptance, and historically deferred from military service (draft deferment).
  • Sense 6 — technical, computing (20th c.): deferred execution, deferred rendering, deferred binding — actions or computation postponed until a later phase of a program or pipeline.
  • Sense 7 — cultural/psychological (20th c.): deferred gratification — the willing postponement of reward, a concept central to psychology (the marshmallow test) and literature (Langston Hughes, “Harlem”: “What happens to a dream deferred?”).
Connotation
In its postponing sense deferred is neutral-to-euphemistic — softer than “cancelled,” more formal than “delayed” — and it is used precisely when a delay is deliberate, institutional, or scheduled. In its yielding sense it conveys respect, civility, and the submission of one's own judgment to another's, as in the deference one shows to elders, experts, or superiors. The two senses share a faint undertone of gravity: something deferred is held in abeyance, neither done nor abandoned.

Semantic boundaries against near-synonyms
  • postponed: similar because both mean put off to a later time; differs because postponed is the neutral everyday word for rescheduling, while deferred carries formal legal, financial, or institutional weight.
  • delayed: similar because both mean happening later than intended; differs because delayed usually implies obstruction or lateness, while deferred implies a deliberate decision.
  • adjourned: similar because both mean suspending a session; differs because adjourned applies to official meetings, courts, and assemblies that will resume.
  • suspended: similar because both mean holding something in abeyance; differs because suspended can mean stopped indefinitely, while deferred keeps a scheduled future time in view.
  • shelved: similar because both mean setting something aside; differs because shelved often implies indefinite or permanent abandonment.
  • yielded: similar because both describe submitting to authority; differs because yielded implies giving up resistance, while deferred to implies respectful submission of judgment.
  • procrastinated: similar because both involve putting things off; differs because procrastinated implies lazy or habitual delay, while deferred is deliberate and principled.
Historical bridge
English has two verbs spelled defer that merged in form. The postponing defer descends from Latin differre “to carry apart, to scatter, to delay” (dis- “apart” + ferre “to carry”), via Old French differer, entering English in the 14th century. The yielding defer descends from Latin dēferre “to carry down, to submit” (dē- “down, away from” + ferre), via Old French déférer. Two verbs “to carry” in opposite directions — one carrying the matter away from its time, the other carrying oneself down before an authority — collapsed into a single word, which is why deferred can mean both “postponed” and “respectfully yielded.”

Literary attestations
“…when an answer arrived which deferred it all” (Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, 1813); “What happens to a dream deferred?” (Langston Hughes, Harlem, 1951) — the century's most famous use, where postponement becomes an engine of tragedy.
