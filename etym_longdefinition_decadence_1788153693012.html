decadence (noun) — the process or state of falling away from an earlier standard of excellence: moral deterioration, cultural decline, or the over-refined late stage of an art or civilisation. The word carries a double modern accent: a general one (decay, decline, corruption — "the decadence of the empire") and a specialised historical one (the Decadence, the late-nineteenth-century European literary and artistic movement that gloried in refined, exquisite, world-weary beauty). Walter Pater's "a refined and comely decadence" deliberately plays the two together: the French Renaissance phase is a late, over-subtle sweetness — a beautiful decay, the aesthetic movement avant la lettre.

Primary sense — decline and deterioration. At its simplest, decadence is the falling off of a people, culture, institution, or organism from vigour and virtue into weakness, luxury, and corruption. It is the noun of decadent and sits beside decay, but with a moral-historical flavour: decadence names a process of history — the terminal stage of a civilisation after its golden age. Gibbon's great narrative is the model: Rome's decline and fall is the textbook decadence, complete with luxury, vice, and effeminacy. In this sense the word has been a polemical instrument since antiquity's own moralists.

Secondary sense — the artistic-literary Decadence. In the 1880s and 1890s "decadence" was claimed, half-ironically, as the name of a movement. French poets and novelists (Baudelaire's heirs: Verlaine, Mallarmé, Huysmans, the Symbolists) and English aesthetes (Arthur Symons, Oscar Wilde, the circle of The Yellow Book) made a programme of the very qualities their critics loathed: artifice over nature, the exotic, the morbid, the exquisite, sensation refined past the healthy. "Decadent" became a badge of the ultra-modern: des Esseintes (Huysmans's hero in À rebours, 1884) lives wholly inside an artificial paradise, and Wilde's Dorian Gray is the type of beautiful corruption. Pater, writing in the 1870s–80s, uses the word just as this meaning is crystallising; his "refined and comely decadence" reads like a prophecy of the movement he partly inspired.

Tertiary senses.

  • Biological / medical — rarely, the decay of an individual organism in late life; the adjective decadent was even used in nineteenth-century natural history of organs that regress. This sense is now obsolete in science.
  • Everyday hyperbole (20th–21st c.) — "decadent" as a compliment for luxurious indulgence: a "decadent chocolate cake", a "decadent spa weekend". The moral censure has been lightened to playful self-indulgence — an interesting inversion of the word's gravity.
  • Political taunt — "decadent West" in anti-Western rhetoric (Soviet and post-colonial usage), where the word resumes its full moral charge against capitalism, luxury, and softness.

Word family and register. The family is French-based: decadence noun, decadent adjective/noun, decadently adverb; the nominal rival decadency is now rare (nineteenth-century American legal and religious use: "the decadency of the church"). Register runs from the historical-formal ("the decadence of Rome") through the art-historical ("French Decadence") to the playful ("decadently rich dessert").

The semantic bridge. Latin dēcadere is literally "to fall down, fall away" (de- "down from" + cadere "to fall"). The whole conceptual history of the word — falling empires, falling morals, falling standards, even the "falling" of late flowers into over-bloom — is contained in that single Latin verb, and Pater's "refined and comely decadence" reclaims the falling as something exquisite: a decline so polished it deserves the word comely.
