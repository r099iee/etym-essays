- Mid-16th c. "to hang limp, flap loosely" - Emerges in northern English and Scots dialects to describe cloth, bird wings, or sails hanging loosely without wind or energy.
- Late 16th–17th c. "to droop in spirit and courage" - Rapidly metaphorized in Elizabethan drama (Shakespeare) and Jacobean sermons for human vitality, military courage, and enthusiasm sinking into exhaustion.
- 18th–19th c. "conversational and physical decline" - Solidified in English prose as the standard idiom for waning physical stamina in athletics and sputtering social conversation; parallel growth of the noun 'flag' in naval signaling generates the transitive verb 'to flag'.
- Early 20th c. "comic social paralysis" - British humorous novelists (such as P. G. Wodehouse) adopt 'conversation flagged' as a comedic staple to signal acute social embarrassment and drawing-room deadlock.
- Late 20th c.–Present "digital surveillance and technical boom" - The intransitive 'drooping' sense remains cherished in literary prose, while the transitive signaling sense explodes into computer science ('feature flags'), fintech compliance ('flagged transactions'), and content moderation ('flagged for review').
