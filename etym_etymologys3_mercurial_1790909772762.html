The adjective mercurial was adopted into Middle English in the late 14th century from Old French mercurial, which directly continued the Classical Latin adjective mercuriālis ('pertaining to the god Mercury or the planet Mercury'). The Latin adjective is formed from the divine proper name Mercurius (Mercury, the Roman messenger god of commerce, trade, travel, eloquence, cunning, and thievery) combined with the adjectival suffix -ālis ('pertaining to'). The name Mercurius is historically derived from the Latin noun merx (genitive mercis, meaning 'goods, merchandise, wares'), which also gave rise to merchant, commerce, market, and mercenary. The root merx is believed by historical linguists to be an Italic borrowing from an Etruscan substrate term associated with trade and market exchange. In Roman mythology, Mercury was identified with the Greek god Hermes, famously depicted wearing winged sandals (talaria) and a winged petasos, emphasizing his supreme speed, agility, and ability to move effortlessly between the heavens, the earth, and the underworld.

The evolution of mercurial across astrology, alchemy, literature, and psychology demonstrates a profound synthesis of astronomical, chemical, and human attributes:

  • Astrological and Alchemical Origins in the Middle Ages (14th–15th Centuries): In medieval Europe, the word mercurial first functioned within the twin pseudo-sciences of astrology and alchemy. Astrologically, those born under the celestial governance of the planet Mercury were believed to inherit the god's swift, restless traits—exhibiting intellectual curiosity, rapid speech, eloquence, inventiveness, and inconstancy. Simultaneously, alchemists used mercury (or quicksilver) as one of their primary elemental principles (alongside sulfur and salt). Because liquid mercury moves with uncanny, slippery rapidity and divides into shimmering globules that instantly recombine, the metal became the ultimate physical symbol of transformative volatility.
  • The Elizabethan Theatrical Re-imagining (16th–17th Centuries): In the English Renaissance, playwrights translated these astrological and chemical qualities into psychological character studies. William Shakespeare immortalized this temperament in Romeo and Juliet (c. 1595) through the character of Mercutio—whose very name embodies the mercurial spirit of quicksilver wit, dazzling puns, explosive temper, and rapid shifts between playful mockery and fatal combat. In Cymbeline, Shakespeare explicitly refers to 'a mercurial breath,' denoting swift, lively speech.
  • Scientific and Medical Specialization (18th–19th Centuries): During the 18th and 19th centuries, mercurial developed a prominent medical usage. Preparations containing elemental mercury or its salts were widely employed in pharmacopeias to treat syphilis and digestive disorders. Patients undergoing these toxic treatments often displayed the neurological and psychiatric symptoms of mercury poisoning (erethism)—tremors, severe emotional swings, excitability, and anxiety—which reinforced the psychological understanding of mercurial as erratic, nervous unpredictability.
  • Modern Metaphorical and Cultural Application (20th–21st Centuries): In modern English, while its medical and astrological usages have receded into historical interest, mercurial has flourished as one of the most expressive literary descriptors for brilliant, volatile human genius. It is routinely used to describe gifted artists, directors, athletic prodigies, stock market fluctuations, and elusive abstract forces (such as artistic inspiration or political public opinion) that shift with dazzling and unpredictable speed.
