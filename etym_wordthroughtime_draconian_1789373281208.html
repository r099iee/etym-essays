- [7th c. BCE Classical Greece] - Draco (Δράκων) is commissioned in 621 BCE to codify Athens' first written law code, establishing public legal accountability while prescribing death for almost all offenses - [connotation: revolutionary milestone in written law, yet immediately infamous for merciless severity]
- [Classical & Roman Antiquity] - Preserved through Greek and Roman historiography (Aristotle, Plutarch, Aulus Gellius) as the universal archetype of legislative brutality; remembered through Plutarch's famous epigram that Draco's laws were written not in ink, but in blood - [connotation: philosophical cautionary tale against disproportionate penal justice; superseded by Solon's humane reforms]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Britain] - Naturalized into English, initially as *draconic* (c. 1770s) and subsequently *draconian* (c. 1870s), deployed by legal reformers (such as Jeremy Bentham and Samuel Romilly) to denounce Britain's "Bloody Code", which executed citizens for minor property theft - [connotation: barbaric legal anachronism, disproportionate state terror, urgent need for penal reform]
- [20th c.–Present] - Enters global political journalism, international diplomacy, corporate governance, and everyday speech to denounce any excessively harsh sanctions, emergency decrees, censorship measures, or interpersonal ultimatums - [connotation: authoritarian overreach, excessive systemic cruelty, heavy-handed coercion]
