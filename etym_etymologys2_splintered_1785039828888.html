• Stage 1 — (Debated) Proto-Indo-European *(s)pley- "to split, cleave" (reconstructed; the connection of the Germanic split-family to this root is widely assumed but not certain — see note).
  • Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *splītaną "to split, cleave" (reconstructed; attested through Middle Dutch splitten and modern Dutch splijten, Low German splitten, German spleißen).
  • Stage 3 — Middle Dutch splinter / splinte and Middle Low German splinte "a splinter, a thin fragment of wood" (attested 13th–14th c.).
  • Stage 4 — Middle English splinter (noun) "a thin sharp fragment of wood or bone" (attested 14th c.).
  • Stage 5 — English to splinter (verb) "to break into splinters" (attested from the 16th c.).
  • Stage 6 — Modern English splintered (past tense / past participle / adjective) "broken into thin sharp fragments; figuratively, divided into factions" — the root word itself.
Note: The connection to PIE *(s)pley- "to split" is plausible and often cited (the family has been linked to Greek σπλήν, splḗn, "spleen," and to words for splitting across several branches), but the precise PIE reconstruction is debated and no single attested chain below Germanic is certain. The form splinter is either a direct borrowing of Middle Dutch splinter/splinte or the English noun splint (itself borrowed) plus the suffix -er; either way, splint, splinter, and split all belong to the same Germanic splitting-family, and those Germanic/Middle Dutch stages are secure.
