- Classical to Scholastic Latin — 'Perceptum' serves in philosophical Latin as the neuter noun for 'that which is perceived', translated in English by vague terms like 'idea' or 'sensation'.
- Late 1830s — Coined into English as 'percept' by Scottish metaphysician Sir William Hamilton to provide an exact linguistic twin to 'concept', separating the object of perception from the act ('perception').
- Late 19th c. (1870s–1890s) — Popularized by evolutionary philosopher Herbert Spencer and psychologist William James, establishing 'percept vs. concept' as the foundational duality of cognitive psychology.
- Mid-20th c. (1950s) — Adopted into Gestalt theory and visual art aesthetics by Rudolf Arnheim (demonstrating dynamic structural tensions within visual percepts).
- Late 20th to 21st c. — Formally codified in artificial intelligence and robotics (Russell & Norvig) as the instantaneous sensory information received by an autonomous agent from its operational environment.
