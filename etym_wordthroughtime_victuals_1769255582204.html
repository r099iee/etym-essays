- Late Middle English "vittle" - the word enters English with its French pronunciation ("vittle"), meaning food supplies laid in for a journey, army, or household; the spelling-pronunciation gap already begins to form.
- 16th-18th century standard provisions - "victuals" is the plain, everyday word for food-as-stores across armies, inns, ships, and kitchens; its register is homely, sturdy, and practical, dignified without being fine.
- 19th-century rustic dialect - as standard speech moves on, "vittles" survives as a colloquial, folksy form - the food of campfires, frontiersmen, and simple folk - giving the word a warm, unpolished, even affectionate tone.
- Contemporary literary archaism - modern usage is chiefly period-flavored: "victuals" appears in historical fiction, fairy tales, and fantasy for a prince's provisions and road-supplies, and it still delights in the trap of its silent c - spelled "victuals," spoken "vittles."
