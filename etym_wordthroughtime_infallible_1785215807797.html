- 15th century theological adoption - enters English as a term for religious authority that cannot err in matters of faith; the register is sacred and scholastic, applied first to scripture and to the Church's teaching.
- 16th-17th century broadening - extends to human reasoning, proofs, and remedies; "infallible" logic and "infallible cures" appear in science, medicine, and practical claims, shedding some of their divine weight.
- 18th-19th century moral and intellectual trust - attaches to persons whose judgment is trusted without reserve; as in Mill's "almost infallible counsellor," the word is now used of intimate advisers, often with a tell-tale hedge like "almost."
- Contemporary use - everyday and slightly hyperbolic ("the recipe is infallible"), yet it never quite sheds its theological echo of certainty beyond the reach of error.
