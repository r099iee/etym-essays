- [Early Middle English, c. 12th–14th c.] — derived from the Old Norse loanword *thrall* (slave, bondsman); functioned as a brutal legal verb meaning to reduce a human being to physical slavery, debt servitude, or chattel serfdom under a feudal lord — [perception: terrifying legal degradation signifying the absolute loss of personal liberty, civil status, and human rights]
- [Early Modern English / Renaissance, 16th–17th c.] — adapted by Elizabethan and Jacobean poets and dramatists (Spenser, Shakespeare, Milton) into moral, psychological, and romantic allegories, depicting the human soul enthralled by sinful vices, demonic spells, or unrequited romantic passion — [perception: viewed with moral gravity as a dangerous condition of spiritual vulnerability, or poetically as amorous devotion]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Romanticism] — shedding its punitive and legalistic associations as feudal serfdom vanished from Western society; celebrated by Romantic poets (Keats, Shelley, Wordsworth) to characterize sublime aesthetic transport before nature, poetry, and orchestral music — [perception: idealized as an exquisite emotional condition of transcendent wonder and voluntary surrender to creative genius]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — fully secularized into high-register literary and critical discourse describing audiences spellbound by theatrical performances, cinematic masterpieces, or charismatic oratory — [perception: universally positive marker of captivating beauty, exquisite storytelling, and rapturous engagement]
