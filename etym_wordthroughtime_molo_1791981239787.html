- 1st c. BCE — the name denotes one man: the Rhodian rhetorician whom Cicero names as his teacher and corrector, a byword for the disciplined Greek training available to Roman aristocrats.
- Imperial and medieval — preserved only in citations: Quintilian, the scholia on Homer and the Christian polemic of Josephus keep the name alive as an authority to be quoted or refuted.
- 16th–18th c. — Renaissance handbooks of rhetoric and the biographies of Cicero restore the figure as a model teacher; the name circulates across European learned prose, always as a personal name.
- 19th c. — English histories of rhetoric and of the late Republic use the short form Molo, and the figure becomes a stock example of Greek influence on Roman style.
- 20th–21st c. — confined to classical scholarship and to biographies of Cicero and Caesar; outside those fields the string survives only in unrelated place names and surnames.
