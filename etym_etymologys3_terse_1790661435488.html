Latin tergere (also tergere, tersi, tersum) meant to wipe, rub, scour, or polish, applied to floors, weapons, and bodies. Its past participle tersus meant wiped clean and, by easy metaphor, neat, spruce, correct, and refined. Roman critics already applied tersus to language: Horace and Quintilian praise tersum diction for freedom from stain. The image is domestic and tactile: style as a surface that can be dusted, burnished, and kept bright, a moral as well as aesthetic labour.

Romance languages preserved the adjective: Italian terso for clean sky or polished verse, French terse for neat and spruce. English borrowed it in the seventeenth century in both spellings terse and terse, first for material neatness (terse arms, terse linen) and swiftly for style (a terse writer, terse expression). Augustan criticism made it a keyword: Dryden, Pope, and Johnson oppose the terse to the luxuriant. Nineteenth-century school rhetoric drilled terseness as a virtue of the plain English sentence, and the spelling terse gradually yielded to terse in dictionaries and print.

  • Latin wiping: tergere as household and military cleaning; tersus as the cleaned result.
  • Roman stylistic transfer: clean surface becomes clean diction, an ethics of care.
  • Romance mediation: terso / terse carry neatness into vernacular poetics.
  • Early modern borrowing: terse/terse enter English for persons, things, then prose.
  • Augustan canonisation: terse becomes a standard term of praise for condensed elegance.
  • Orthographic settling: terse wins over terse; terse survives as archaic variant and period colour.

Phonology is simple: RP /tɜːs/ with a long central vowel and voiceless sibilant, rhyming with verse and purse. The final -se spells /s/ on the French model. Morphology gives tersely, terseness (rarely tersely from the archaic base). Semantics narrowed from general neatness to specifically verbal economy, while retaining the approving glow of polish. That is why terse never quite means curt: curtness wounds, terseness shines.

Attestation history shows the evaluative world the source sentence inhabits: handbooks praising Addison's terse periods, reviews commending Macaulay's terse narrative, Strunk-like maxims urging writers to be terse. Set beside turgid in the same list, terse is its pointed opposite: wiped versus swollen. Recovering the archaic spelling reminds modern readers that concision was once literally cleanliness, and that our casual blurbs about lean, lucid, hard-hitting prose inherit a centuries-old fantasy of language as a well-kept room.

Lexicographically the variant matters because early dictionaries and printing houses had not yet fixed the modern form, so concordances to seventeenth- and eighteenth-century criticism must be searched under both spellings. Editors modernising texts regularly normalise terse to terse without comment, a quiet orthographic wiping that mirrors the very sense of the word. Noting the older form therefore trains attention on transmission as well as style: how polish of spelling and polish of sentence advance together.
