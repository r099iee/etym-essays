- Old English 'crammian' → Middle English 'crammen': everyday verb for stuffing and tight packing; neutral and unrefined.
- 17th–18th centuries: the culinary extension hardens — 'cramming' geese and capons for the table, and stuffing oneself greedily.
- c. 1800: Oxford and Cambridge slang hijacks it — 'to cram' = to drill facts into a student's head for examinations; 'crammer' tutors become a Victorian institution.
- 19th–20th centuries: American college slang takes it over ('cram for finals', 'cram course'); the verb stays robust and colloquial.
- 1937: Tolkien's 'The Hobbit' converts the noun into a fictional travel-biscuit — dense, sustaining, boring — the comic ancestor of elvish lembas.
- Today: 'cram' means last-minute study or rough packing; the fantasy food survives in Tolkien fandom; dialect 'cram' ('a crumb') quietly endures.
