Wilful is an English compound of the noun will + the adjective-forming suffix -ful ("full of"), built entirely inside English. The chain below traces the root of will from PIE to the compound. (5 attested stages.) 1. PIE *welh₁- "to wish, will, choose" — a verbal root of deliberate preference, reconstructed with a laryngeal; the zero-grade variant *wel- "to want, choose, prefer" underlies the Germanic forms. 2. → Proto-Germanic *weljaną "to want, choose, wish" (the verb) and *wiljōn "will, wish, desire" (the noun, from the same root) — the Germanic pair that gives English will. 3. → Old English willan "to want, desire" (verb) and will "will, desire, purpose" (noun) — the Old English forms; the related wela "wealth, well-being" comes from the same root. 4. → Middle English wil / wille "will, desire" — the noun to which -ful is appended to form the compound. 5. → Middle English wilful "self-willed, headstrong, deliberate" (14th c.) — the compound of wil + -ful, attested by Chaucer's age; the direct ancestor of the modern word. Final node: English WILFUL (rootWord) — British spelling with one l; American English uses willful with two l's. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The laryngeal in *welh₁- is reconstructed on comparative evidence and is debated in detail, but the root's meaning "to wish, will, choose" is uncontroversial. (2) The compound wilful itself (stage 5) is fully attested in Middle English; its earliest date of attestation is given as the 14th century. (3) The spelling difference (wilful vs willful) reflects a nineteenth-century regularization of the doubling after the British/American split, not a change in meaning.
