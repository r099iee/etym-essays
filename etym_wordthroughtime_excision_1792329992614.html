- Classical Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — *excīsiō* signified a physical felling, cutting down of trees, breaking down of walls, or military destruction and eradication of a fortress — perceived as violent physical destruction.
- Late Latin & Medieval Vulgate (4th–14th c.) — Used in theological and legal texts for extirpation, excommunication, or divine cutting off of sinners from the community (*kareth* translation) — perceived with profound moral and spiritual dread.
- Early Modern English (15th–16th c.) — Borrowed via Middle French to describe the utter destruction, razing, or slaughter of a people or city — perceived as catastrophic ruin.
- 17th–18th century — Shifted into medical and surgical terminology to denote the precise operative cutting out of diseased tissue, tumors, or gangrenous limbs — perceived as a scientific life-saving intervention.
- 19th–21st century — Adopted in textual criticism and philology to denote the critical deletion of spurious verses, and later in molecular genetics to describe enzymatic DNA excision repair — perceived as rigorous purification and corrective restoration.
