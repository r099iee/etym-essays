• chronological: The exact English equivalent, identical in sense and register; it differs only in being an English adjective with the suffix -ical and in having no feminine agreement.
  • temporal: Similar in relating to time; it differs by naming time as such (temporal succession, the temporal world) rather than the order of dates, and it carries a philosophical or theological flavour.
  • sequential: Similar in following one thing after another; it differs by describing any ordered succession, not necessarily one measured in dates, and it belongs to technical and computing registers.
  • successive: Similar in marking one item after another; it differs by emphasising mere consecutiveness and by lacking any reference to calendars or dating.
  • consecutive: Similar in denoting unbroken sequence; it differs by stressing that nothing intervenes (three consecutive years) rather than that the order is temporal.
  • serial: Similar in implying a series; it differs by belonging to publication and broadcasting, where it names instalments, and to criminology, where it describes repeated offences.
  • annalistic: Similar in recording events year by year; it differs by being a term of historiography for bare, date-by-date record-keeping, often with a suggestion of dryness that chronologique does not have.
  • diachronic: Similar in studying change over time; it differs by being a linguistic term opposed to synchronic, and by concerning development rather than mere date-order.
  • dated: Similar in attaching dates to items; it differs by being a participle of the verb date and by meaning either "marked with a date" or, figuratively, "out of fashion".
  • historical: Similar in belonging to history; it differs by naming the subject-matter rather than the arrangement, and a historical account need not be chronological at all.
