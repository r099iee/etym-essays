Two roots for one trembling sound

Trill entered English by two streams that eventually merged into one word. The first stream is native Germanic: Middle English trillen to turn, spin, whirl, roll (attested from the 13th–14th centuries), borrowed from Scandinavian and ultimately descending from the PIE root *terh₁- to turn, rub, bore, pierce — the same ancient root that yields English turn, drill, and through, and Latin tero to rub. The turning-rolling idea is the semantic seed: a thing that trills moves in a quick, trembling, rotating way, and the connection between twirling and a trembling voice is easy to feel.

The Italian detour

The second stream is the musical one. Italian trillare to quaver, warble and its noun trillo a trill entered 16th-century Italian musical vocabulary, and English musicians of the Baroque borrowed them in the 17th century — exactly as they borrowed aria, sonata, and fugue. Since Italian music writing dominated European practice, the ornament name reached English as trillo (1630s) and then as trill. The ultimate origin of Italian trillare is debated: it may be imitative of the bird-like quavering sound itself, or it may be a Romance relative of the Germanic twirl-turn family, imported through medieval intermediaries. The OED cautiously allows either route, and the family resemblance to trembling and twirling words across Europe makes both options plausible.

Fusion and expansion

Once both strands were in English, they pulled toward each other. By the 17th–18th centuries trill had settled as the standard word for the rapid trembling alternation of notes in singing and on instruments. Eighteenth-century poetry and nature writing cemented the bird-song sense: the liquid trill of larks and thrushes became a stock phrase of the period, and essayists from Gilbert White to the Victorians used it interchangeably with warble and quaver. In the 18th–19th centuries the verb trilled appears in early novels for high, bright, affected speech — a lady trills a greeting — keeping the musical overtone alive even in dialogue. The word was also adopted into 19th-century linguistics as the technical term for a rolled or vibrated consonant, and from there it spilled into general sensory vocabulary: a trill of laughter, the trill of insects, the trill of a telegraph or telephone.

Modern profile

Today trill does triple duty: a precise musical ornament (performers trill; piano tutors drill their students in the trill), a phonetic term (Spanish rr is a trill), and an everyday word for bright fast sound (a bird trills in the hedge; a girl lets out a trill of laughter). In the source text the old thrush gives a sudden trill — a brief, bright burst of warbling song, the very sound the word has named for three centuries. Beneath all three senses the two-thousand-year root still glimmers: something that trills is something that turns and rolls and shakes — quickly, brightly, and alive — through the air.
