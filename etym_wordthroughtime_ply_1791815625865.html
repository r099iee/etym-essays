- 14th c. — plien means “to bend, fold, twist”, and by extension “to apply oneself”; the physical sense is still dominant.
- c. 1400 — “ply a trade” appears: to work at or exercise a calling, at first of artisans and craftsmen.
- 16th c. — the nautical “ply to windward” (to tack) and the weapon sense “ply a sword” give the word an energetic, martial colour.
- 1620s — “ply with” develops: to press someone with food, drink or questions, with a tone hovering between hospitality and importunity.
- 1803 — ships are said to “ply” a route; the usage extends to coaches and then to taxis “plying for hire”, a commercial and regulatory register.
- 20th c. — the noun multiplies in technical compounds: plywood, two-ply, multi-ply, ply yarn.
- 21st c. — “ply a trade” survives as a deliberate archaism; “ply with questions” and “taxis plying the streets” remain fully current in news prose.
