• Throw: Similar because both mean propelling something through the air, and cast is the formal-literary double of throw. Differs because throw is the neutral everyday verb of any projection, while cast is elevated and has branched into moulds, actors, colours, and computing.
  • Hurl: Similar because both mean throwing with violence and force. Differs because hurl stresses speed and rage of the throw; cast is calm and controlled (cast a net, cast lots) and far wider in senses.
  • Fling: Similar because both mean energetic throwing. Differs because fling suggests careless abandonment; cast suggests deliberate aim or ritual (casting lots, casting a vote).
  • Toss: Similar because both mean light throwing. Differs because toss is casual and short-range (toss a coin); cast can be long-range, formal, or entirely figurative.
  • Pitch: Similar because both can mean throwing with aim and are used of ships’ motion. Differs because pitch (of a ship) is fore-and-aft rocking, while cast is not a ship-motion sense; and pitch has none of the mould, theatre, or plaster senses.
  • Sling: Similar because both mean throwing with force. Differs because sling implies a looping, whipping action; cast implies a straight, released throw.
  • Launch: Similar because both mean setting something in motion. Differs because launch is used of ships, rockets, and careers, suggesting beginnings; cast is the throw itself.
  • Propel: Similar because both mean driving something forward. Differs because propel is mechanical and scientific; cast is human, ritual, and metaphorical.
  • Chuck: Similar because both are informal throw-words. Differs because chuck is casual British slang for small objects; cast is never casual.
  • Lob: Similar because both are throws with a high arc. Differs because lob is gentle and sports-specific (tennis, cricket); cast is general and forceful.
  • Heave: Similar because both can mean throwing with effort, and both are usable of ships and weights. Differs because heave stresses muscular strain and rising motion; cast stresses the release and the aimless flight.
  • Mould / Model: Similar because “to cast metal” means to form it in a mould, and a cast is a moulded object. Differs because mould is the general verb for shaping; cast specifically means shaping by pouring into a hollow form.
  • Assign / Allot: Similar because casting a play means assigning roles, as lots are allotted. Differs because assign/allot are the abstract bureaucratic terms; cast keeps the game-of-chance flavour of the original lot-throw.
  • Troupe / Company / Ensemble: Similar because “the cast” names the actors of a production. Differs because troupe and company can mean the permanent acting group or theatre company itself; the cast is specifically the actors in one play or film.
  • Tinge / Shade / Tint: Similar because “a cast” is a tinge or shade of colour. Differs because tinge/shade/tint are the general colour words; cast of colour is the faint, surface suggestion — and cast has fifty other senses they lack.
  • Squint / Strabismus: Similar because a “cast in the eye” is a squint. Differs because squint is the everyday word and strabismus the clinical term; cast in this sense is idiomatic, archaic-flavoured, and noun-only.
  • Outcast / Castaway (derived nouns): Similar because both name people “thrown out” — the throw-sense frozen into social exile. Differs because they are separate derived words with their own narrowed meanings; cast itself is not used of persons in this way.
