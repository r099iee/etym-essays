Tarry is a verb meaning “to delay, linger, or stay; to remain in a place longer than necessary or longer than intended; to wait before acting.” When the Elvenking says “Long will I tarry, ere I begin this war for gold,” he means he will wait a very long time indeed before choosing war — the word stresses voluntary postponement seasoned with patience, even reluctance.

Core senses.

  • To stay or remain in a place: “tarry a while,” “tarry overnight at an inn” — the oldest and simplest sense, of remaining somewhere.
  • To delay or linger: to spend time over something, to be slow in coming or doing; “do not tarry on the way” warns against loitering during a journey.
  • To wait (for something): to bide one’s time before acting — “tarry for the tide”; the sense in our context, where waiting is deliberate and patient.
  • Archaic transitive — to retard, hold back: Middle English could tarry someone else (“tarry the messenger”), delaying another person or thing; this transitive use died out in the modern period, leaving only the intransitive senses.

Register and survival.

  • Tarry is now literary and archaic in English. Its chief living habitats are the King James Bible (“Tarry ye here, and watch with me,” Matthew 26:38; “If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee?” John 21:22), hymns, historical novels, and fantasy prose — including this very passage from The Hobbit.
  • In ordinary speech it has long been replaced by wait, stay, linger, and delay; hearing tarry today immediately signals elevated or period diction.

Idioms, patterns, and derivatives.

  • Simple, unhurried phrases: “tarry a while,” “tarry here till I return,” “tarry over one’s meal,” “tarry behind the company”; negative imperatives such as “tarry not” are especially characteristic of scriptural and solemn style.
  • Derived forms: tarriance (rare noun, “lingering, delay”), tarrier (“one who tarries”, rare), and the gerund tarrying (“his long tarrying in town”) are attested from Middle English onward.

Frequently confused forms.

  • The adjective tardy (“slow, late”) is possibly — but not provably — a distant relative, since both may go back to Latin tardus “slow”; the link is commonly repeated but not universally accepted.
  • The noun/verb tar (pitch; to cover with pitch) and the adjective tarry meaning “covered with tar, tar-like” are completely unrelated homographs. The verb tarry and the adjective tarry are pronounced identically (/ˈtæri/), which makes the collision easy to trip over: “tarry rope” is tarred rope, while “to tarry” is to wait.

Emotional colour and modern usage. To tarry is to choose slowness. The word is gentler than delay: tarrying is unhurried, patient, sometimes idyllic, tinged with forbearance rather than reproach — which is why kings, elves, and scriptural speakers tarry while schedules and deadlines “are delayed”. When writers reach for it today, they usually do so to slow the reader down: a character who tarries is pausing in a world that does not, and the word carries the feeling of long evenings, unhurried journeys, and holy patience, all compacted into one softly archaic verb with an unbroken seven-century pedigree. In grammar, tarry is a regular weak verb (tarry – tarried – tarried) with a gerund tarrying; it is intransitive in all living senses, and it takes no direct object in modern English. Its meaning is always time-relative: to tarry is to stay beyond the moment when one could have left, which is why the word so often appears with a stated or implied deadline (“ere I begin this war”).
