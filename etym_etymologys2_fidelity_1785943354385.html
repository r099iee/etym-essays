FIDELITY is a Latin-derived loanword whose entire history is the story of one Indo-European root meaning "to trust." The chain below runs in a single, attested line from the reconstructed root to the modern word. 1. Proto-Indo-European *bʰeydʰ- "to trust, confide, be persuaded, be obedient" (reconstructed; projected c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the root of a whole family of trust-words; secure cognates include Greek peíthomai "to trust, obey, be persuaded" (source of English persuade via Latin persuādēre) and Avestan baēš- "to bind, pledge." 2. Latin fīdere "to trust, have confidence, rely on" (attested verb, with fīdus "faithful, trustworthy") — from the Italic continuation of *bʰeydʰ-, with the regular shift of PIE initial *bʰ- to Latin f- and PIE *ey to Latin ī. 3. Latin fīdēs, fīdeī (f.) "faith, trust, confidence, belief; a pledge of loyalty" (attested from the earliest Latin literature, e.g. Plautus, 3rd–2nd c. BCE) — the central noun, formed on the stem of fīdere; also the name of the Roman goddess Fides, the divinity of sworn good faith, whose temple vowed in 258 BCE stood on the Capitoline. 4. Latin fidēlis "faithful, loyal, trustworthy" (adjective from fīdēs + the suffix -ēlis; attested from the 2nd century BCE) — the adjectival base from which the abstract noun is built. 5. Latin fidēlitās, fidēlitātis (f.) "faithfulness, fidelity, loyalty" (abstract noun in -tās from fidēlis; attested in classical prose, e.g. Cicero) — the exact ancestor of the English word. 6. Old French fidelité (later fidelite) "loyalty, faithfulness" (attested from the 12th century) — the regular Romance continuation of Latin fidēlitātem (accusative), with the characteristic loss of final syllables and the ending evolving -tās > -tātem > -teit > -té. 7. Middle English fidelitee / fidelite "fidelity, loyalty" (attested from the 15th century; borrowed from Old French, often spelled fydelite) — the first appearance in English, in religious, legal, and chivalric contexts. 8. English FIDELITY (modern form, settled from the 16th century onward) ◄ TARGET — used first for loyalty to a person or sovereign, later extended to marital, textual, and (from the 1920s) technical "high-fidelity" accuracy. NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: (1) The reconstruction of the root as *bʰeydʰ- is standard and well-supported by Greek peíthomai and Latin fīdere, but its exact laryngeal shape and full vowel grade are inferred, not directly attested. (2) Latin foedus "treaty, covenant, league" is traditionally placed in the same family (a treaty as a formal pledge of trust), though some scholars keep it separate; the connection is debated and does not affect the line to fīdēs. (3) The technical "high fidelity" sense is a 20th-century English extension of the word's old metaphor "true to the original"; it did not exist in Latin, French, or earlier English. (4) The 1999 coinage "Wi-Fi" was marketed as short for "Wireless Fidelity" although it is not a true abbreviation of anything; the acronym is a branding exercise built on the prestige of the word.
