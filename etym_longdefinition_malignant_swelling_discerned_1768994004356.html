The three root words are malignant, swelling, and discerned — the vocabulary of the passage: "The malignant column of the zoorth, twisting and swelling toward them, was the first sign of animation they had discerned in that lifeless land."

1. MALIGNANT

Core sense: evil in nature, influence, or effect; disposed to harm; of a disease or growth, tending to spread and destroy — the medical opposite of benign. In the passage the word is a biological metaphor blown up to the scale of weather: the "malignant column" of the zoorth is not merely destructive but willingly so — the storm is imagined as a living malignancy of the air, a tumorous evil that grows as it pursues the travelers across the lifeless land. The Latin anatomy of the word (malus, "bad," + gignere, "to beget") makes malignancy a matter of nature: it does not choose evil; it is born evil. Polysemy:
  • of persons and dispositions: ill-disposed, spiteful, full of hatred (the 16th-c. sense);
  • of fevers and diseases: resistant to treatment, spreading, destructive (17th c.);
  • of tumors: invasive, metastatic, tending to recur — the clinical opposite of benign (modern medicine);
  • of influences, forces, and phenomena: destructive and deadly by nature (malignant weather, a malignant influence).
Differentiating terms: malevolent wishes harm and implies a conscious agent; malicious harms from spite; baleful and baneful mean "bringing harm," often through ominous influence; sinister suggests lurking wrongness; virulent describes poison or disease that spreads rapidly. Only malignant joins inborn badness to unchecked, organic growth.

2. SWELLING

Core sense: expanding in size or volume; becoming fuller; of water or air, rising and surging; also, as a noun, an abnormal enlargement of tissue. In the passage it captures the storm column mid-birth: the zoorth is "twisting and swelling toward them" — a pressurized surge of atmospheric violence inflating itself as it approaches, like a boil filling or a wave lifting. The participle gives the motion a body: the column does not merely move; it grows, drawing substance into itself as it advances. Polysemy:
  • physical, of flesh, wounds, rivers, sails: internal expansion under pressure;
  • noun: an abnormal enlargement of tissue (a swelling, an edematous lump);
  • of sound: a swelling chord or swelling music — gradual increase in volume;
  • social: a "swell" (19th c.), a person puffed up with fashion and importance; "swell" as an adjective meaning "excellent, fashionable";
  • of quantities and populations: swelling crowds, swelling numbers.
Differentiating terms: expanding is neutral and geometric; distending implies unnatural, strained fullness (a belly, a vein); billowing suggests soft, cloth-like folds of cloud or sail; bulging is local and lumpy; rising loses the inward pressure entirely. Only swelling implies growth from within, under pressure, toward rupture.

3. DISCERNED

Core sense: detected, perceived, or recognized, especially by effortful observation through distance, clutter, or darkness; to separate (one thing) from another by close examination. In the passage it is the precise verb of perception: the first sign of animation in a lifeless land — something picked out of the dead dust by careful looking. The etymological force of discernere, "to sift apart" (dis-, "apart," + cernere, "to sift"), is the patient separation of signal from noise. Polysemy:
  • to perceive (see, hear, make out) something indistinct: to discern a shape in the fog;
  • to distinguish or recognize a difference: to discern right from wrong;
  • to understand or judge something subtle: to discern a motive;
  • in theology, "discernment": the spiritual capacity to judge spirits and callings.
Differentiating terms: perceive is neutral reception; detect is technical and instrumental; observe is steady attention; distinguish separates one from another; recognize identifies as known. Discerned carries the full etymological sense of sifting — the effort of separation is part of its meaning.
