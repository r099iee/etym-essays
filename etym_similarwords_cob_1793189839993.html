• Pony: Similar because both name a small, sturdy equine for ordinary riding; differs because a pony is defined by short height (under 14.2 hands) of any build, while a cob is defined by its thick-set, powerful build at horse-like height.
  • Nag: Similar because both are dismissive stable-yard words for a common low-grade horse, exactly the fable's register; differs because nag stresses worthlessness or age, while cob stresses the short stout shape (and is often affectionate).
  • Hack: Similar because the fable pairs them — the starveling hack and the beggarly cob are fellow scrub-horses; differs because a hack is a hired-out horse worn by work, while a cob is a type defined by sturdy compact build.
  • Packhorse: Similar because both are sturdy working horses valued for strength over speed; differs because a packhorse carries goods on its back in trains, while a cob is chiefly a riding horse for heavy or nervous riders.
  • Cart-horse: Similar because both are thick-set, strong, unglamorous working horses; differs because a cart-horse is a heavy draught breed for pulling, while a cob is a lighter riding animal.
  • Lump: Similar because the inanimate cob is literally a rounded lump — cob of coal, cob loaf; differs because lump is the general formless mass, while cob implies compact roundness of fist- or head-size.
  • Nugget: Similar because both name a compact rounded mass (gold nuggets, coal cobs); differs because nugget stresses small precious solidity, while cob stresses homely fist-sized roundness.
  • Hunk: Similar because both name a thick solid piece — a hunk of bread beside a cob loaf; differs because a hunk is cut or torn off roughly, while a cob is round by nature or baking.
  • Boulder: Similar because cobbles and boulders are both rounded stones on the same scale-ladder; differs because a boulder is a great rock mass, while cob-origin stones are man- or fist-sized lumps.
