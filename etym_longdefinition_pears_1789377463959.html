Pears is the plural botanical and pomological noun denoting the edible fruits produced by deciduous trees of the genus Pyrus within the rose family (Rosaceae), as well as the trees themselves. Cultivated across temperate regions for over four millennia, the pear is characteristically pyriform (narrow at the stem and bulbous at the base), though varieties may range from globose to oblong. Anatomically classified as a pome fruit, its tender, juicy flesh surrounds a central core containing seeds. A unique defining characteristic of pears is the presence of sclereids or "stone cells"—hard, lignified cellular clusters scattered throughout the flesh that impart a distinctive, subtle grittiness to the fruit before it achieves melting ripeness.

In the source text—from Oscar Wilde's heartbreaking allegorical fairy tale The Devoted Friend—the narrator describes Little Hans's winter suffering: "During the spring, the summer, and the autumn he was very happy, but when the winter came, and he had no fruit or flowers to bring to the market, he suffered a good deal from cold and hunger, and often had to go to bed without any supper but a few dried pears or some hard nuts." Here, "dried pears" serve as a poignant literary emblem of pastoral hardship, rural frugality, and extreme physical vulnerability. Hans's meager winter subsistence on dried fruit contrasts sharply with the gluttonous, fire-warmed comfort of his wealthy "friend" the Miller, who refuses to visit or feed Hans during the snowy months under the absurd, hypocritical justification that visitors only bother people in trouble.

The term displays extensive polysemy, specialized technical applications, and cultural resonance across varied domains:

  • Pomology, Horticulture, and Taxonomy: In systematic agronomy and fruit breeding, pears encompass two primary cultivated species: the European pear (Pyrus communis) and the Asian pear (Pyrus pyrifolia, also known as the nashi pear, sand pear, or apple pear). European cultivars (such as 'Williams' Bon Chrétien' or 'Bartlett', 'Conference', 'Bosc', and 'Doyenné du Comice') are typically harvested mature but unripe, as they ripen to peak buttery texture off the tree. Asian cultivars, by contrast, are tree-ripened and prized for their crisp, watery crunch and round shape.
  • Culinary Gastronomy and Preservation: Throughout culinary history, pears have been consumed fresh, preserved, or cooked across savory and sweet preparations. Due to their high pectin and sugar content, dried pears (such as those eaten by Little Hans) provided an essential winter calorie source across rural Europe for centuries. Classic gastronomic dishes include poached pears in spiced red wine (poires Belle-Hélène), pear tarts (tarte Bourdaloue), pear chutneys paired with sharp blue cheeses like Roquefort, and distilled fruit brandies, notably eau-de-vie de poire (often featuring a whole pear grown inside the bottle).
  • Fermentation and Cidery ("Perry"): In beverage production, fermented pear juice yields perry (traditionally known in France as poiré). Produced primarily in traditional orchards of Gloucestershire, Herefordshire, Worcestershire, and Normandy, authentic perry is crafted from specialized, tannic, unpalatable "perry pears" (such as the 'Hendre Huffcap' or 'Blakeney Red') that possess high concentrations of non-fermentable sorbitol, imparting a distinctive residual sweetness and delicate effervescence.
  • Fine Woodworking and Instrument Making: Pearwood (the timber of Pyrus communis) is one of the most highly prized hardwoods in cabinetmaking, woodturning, and lutherie. Extremely dense, fine-grained, stable, and pinkish-brown in tone, pearwood is resistant to warping and splinters, making it the preferred historical medium for architectural drawing instruments (T-squares and rulers), woodcut printing blocks, precision recorder wind instruments, violin fingerboards, and luxury furniture veneers.
  • Gemology and Jewelry ("Pear Cut"): In lapidary and gemstone cutting, a pear cut (or teardrop cut) denotes a modified brilliant-cut gemstone featuring a hybrid silhouette: a softly rounded circular bottom tapering smoothly into a sharp, pointed tip. Typically cut with 58 facets, the pear cut is prized in diamond engagement rings, pendants, and teardrop earrings for elongating the wearer's fingers and maximizing internal light reflection.
  • Anatomy, Somatotypes, and Biometrics ("Pear-Shaped"): In human anthropometry and clinical nutrition, a pear-shaped body describes a gynoid somatotype where adipose tissue is concentrated predominantly around the hips, thighs, and buttocks, with a narrower waist and shoulders. Epidemiologists contrast pear-shaped fat distribution with the "apple-shaped" (android/visceral) fat distribution, noting that pear-shaped fat carries significantly lower cardiovascular and metabolic disease risks.
  • British Colloquial Idiom ("Gone Pear-Shaped"): In British, Australian, and Commonwealth English, the informal colloquial phrase "to go pear-shaped" signifies that a plan, enterprise, or situation has gone terribly wrong, collapsed into confusion, or failed completely (e.g., "the whole project went pear-shaped after the lead developer quit"). Theories regarding its twentieth-century origin trace to Royal Air Force flight instructors describing student pilots whose attempted circular aerobatic loops distorted into saggy, asymmetrical pear shapes.
