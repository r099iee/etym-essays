• Nītiśāstra: Similar because both are authoritative classical Sanskrit instructional treatises composed for kings and scholars; differs because nītiśāstra focuses strictly on pragmatic politics, statecraft, mundane diplomacy, and worldly wisdom (nīti), whereas dharmaśāstra is the overarching sacred jurisprudence of moral, cosmic, and religious righteousness (dharma).
  • Arthaśāstra: Similar because both constitute canonical pillars of ancient Indian normative philosophy; differs because arthaśāstra (epitomized by Kauṭilya) deals exclusively with wealth acquisition, political economy, statecraft, espionage, and warfare, whereas dharmaśāstra governs ethics, personal righteousness, sacramental duties, and spiritual salvation.
  • Dharmasūtra: Similar because both belong to the sacred legal literature of Hinduism; differs because dharmasūtras are archaic, compact prose aphorisms embedded directly within Vedic school lineages (c. 6th–2nd c. BCE), whereas dharmaśāstras are later, fully metrical (verse), encyclopedic treatises aimed at a pan-Indian audience.
  • Smṛti: Similar because dharmaśāstras constitute the preeminent textual category within smṛti; differs because smṛti ("that which is remembered") is the vast umbrella category encompassing epics (Mahābhārata, Rāmāyaṇa), purāṇas, and legal texts, whereas dharmaśāstra designates only the specific treatises dedicated to law and duty.
  • Śruti: Similar because both are foundational sources of Hindu authority; differs because śruti ("that which is heard") is primordial, authorless, eternal revelation consisting solely of the Vedas and Upaniṣads, whereas dharmaśāstra is humanly authored, interpretive tradition subject to historical adaptation.
  • Vyavahāra: Similar because both pertain to legal disputes and adjudication; differs because vyavahāra specifically denotes the judicial process, litigation, and courtroom trials, forming merely one of the three major subdivisions contained inside a complete dharmaśāstra.
  • Śāstra: Similar because dharmaśāstra is a specific exemplar of śāstra; differs because śāstra is the broad generic Sanskrit term for any systematic, authoritative science, manual, or technical treatise (e.g., vāstuśāstra for architecture, kāmaśāstra for erotics).
  • Mīmāṃsā: Similar because both are deeply concerned with Vedic ritual and legal interpretation; differs because mīmāṃsā is a formal philosophical school of textual hermeneutics and linguistic exegesis used to interpret Vedic injunctions, whereas dharmaśāstra is the codified substantive body of laws and customs.
