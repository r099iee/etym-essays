breeches: A garment covering the body from the waist to the knee, or just below it, and — in older and British usage — trousers generally; a pair of breeches. The word is grammatically plural, a fossil of its origin as a garment made for two legs, and it always takes a plural verb ("breeches are," never "breeches is"). In the present context — "She wore loose white breeches to the knee and a yellow cape flapping in the wind" — the garment is part of a rider's costume that is at once practical and striking: loose enough for freedom of movement on horseback, white against the wind, cut short to the knee, so that the rider's legs are free and bare below, with the yellow cape streaming above. The details work together to create an image of athletic wildness — a woman who rides a black horse break-neck and dresses for motion, not display. The word's historical register is important here: for centuries breeches were the everyday garment of men (and of women only when riding or working astride), so the rider's breeches mark her as one who rides astride, self-possessed and unconventional. Primary sense: Short trousers fastened just below the knee.
  • Of riding and sport: riding breeches — the fitted knee-length garment worn for riding, with its origins in the split garment that allows a leg on each side of the saddle.
  • Of historical costume: the knee-breeches of the gentleman (16th-early 19th c.) — stockings below, buckled knee-breeches above, the dress of the master of the house and of courtiers.
  • Of children's clothing: knee breeches as boys' wear; "breeching" was the old ceremony of a boy's first being dressed in breeches instead of skirts.
Secondary and figurative senses:
  • "To wear the breeches": to rule the household — the idiom from the era when the breeches were the mark of the master, applied to a wife who dominates her husband.
  • "Too big for his breeches": conceited, presumptuous — the modern idiom for someone puffed up beyond their station.
  • Breech (singular, technical): the hind part or rear — as in breech-birth (a baby born bottom-first) and breech-loading (a gun loaded from the rear of the barrel).
  • Britches (dialectal variant): the colloquial American form of breeches, used in the same idioms ("too big for his britches").
Modern technical and jargon senses:
  • In firearms engineering: the breech of a gun is the rear part of the barrel, behind the bore, where the cartridge is loaded — a transfer from the hind part of the body to the rear of a firearm.
  • In obstetrics and medicine: a breech birth or breech presentation is delivery of a baby buttocks or feet first.
  • In equestrian costume: modern riding breeches (and jodhpurs) remain knee- or calf-length fitted garments; "breeches" is standard in the vocabulary of horse sports.
  • In heraldry and old dressmaking: breeches appear as items of blazoned costume; "breeches pattern" survives in tailoring as a technical term.
Semantic bridges and historical development: The word's ancestor, Proto-Germanic *brōkiz, named the garment that covers the place where the body splits into two legs — breeches are, etymologically, "the split thing," the clothing of the fork. The plural shape is a fossil: Old English brēc (with plural brēċ, the two leg-coverings taken together) was re-pluralized in Middle English by adding -es, so that modern breeches is a double plural — the legs, so to speak, twice over. Against its neighbors: trousers (from Irish triubhas, the Gaelic trews) covers the legs to the ankle and is the modern general word; pants means underpants in British English but trousers in American; knickerbockers are loose breeches gathered at the knee; culottes are skirt-like divided garments; breeches is the oldest of them all, tied to the knee, to riding, and — in the idiom "to wear the breeches" — to authority in the household, since the breeches were the mark of the master. The allied singular breech (the hind part, as in breech-birth and breech-loading gun) is the same word specialized, a reminder that breeches always named the part of the body where the frame divides.
