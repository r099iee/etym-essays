desolation: noun. A state or condition of complete and utter emptiness — barrenness, ruin, or abandonment in a physical place, and the corresponding overwhelming loneliness, grief, or hopelessness in a person. The word is unique among words for sadness in fusing landscape and feeling: it describes a place that has been emptied of life and a heart that has been emptied of comfort, using one vocabulary for both.

PRIMARY SENSE (physical ruin and barrenness). The earliest and most concrete sense is the ruin or laying-waste of a place: a city reduced to rubble, a countryside burned bare, a house standing empty. "The desolation of the war-torn provinces," "a land of desolation," "the desolation of a desert" — in all such uses the word describes a geography stripped of inhabitants, cultivation, and life. This sense is closely bound to the word's Latin root (dēsōlāre, "to leave alone, to lay waste," formed from dē- "thoroughly" + sōlus "alone"), so that the very idea of a wasteland is etymologically the idea of an emptied or lonely land.

SECONDARY SENSE (emotional and psychological emptiness). From the sixteenth century onward desolation developed an equally powerful inner sense: the desolation of the heart — an extreme, desolate loneliness, the feeling of being utterly abandoned, or grief so absolute that it becomes a barren interior landscape. In the card's source sentence ("the sense of desolation left by the death of their only heir, which had turned their home into a quiet and empty place"), the word operates at the intersection of both senses: the home has become a "quiet and empty place" (physical desolation) and the parents have been emptied of hope (emotional desolation). This fusion is the word's distinctive power.

FURTHER SENSES AND EXTENSIONS.

  • Religious and apocalyptic usage — in biblical language desolation names the punishment of an abandoned land, culminating in the New Testament's famous phrase "the abomination of desolation" (Matthew 24:15), a prophetic image of the temple rendered desolate; the word thus carries a solemn, prophetic, almost catastrophic tone.
  • Romantic and sublime landscape usage — eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Romantic writers prized desolation for describing awesome, lonely, overwhelming scenes — moors, ruins, mountains, frozen wastes — that inspire both fear and awe (the "sublime" rather than the merely pretty).
  • Literary and rhetorical intensifier — because of its absolute character, the word is used as a hyperbolically serious term for emptiness of any kind: "the desolation of the empty ballroom," "a deep sense of desolation in the crowded city."
  • Historical description of lands — in historical writing, "desolation" names deliberate destruction (scorched earth, the sack of cities) as a documented event, and "desolations of war" survives in reportage.

GRAMMAR, COLLOCATION, AND REGISTER. desolation is an abstract (usually uncountable) noun, though it is occasionally countable when naming distinct scenes of waste ("the desolations of war"). It collocates with utter, complete, absolute, barren, howling, lonely, sense of, scene of, years of, waste and. The register is high and grave: the word belongs to literary prose, journalism, the Bible, and formal speech, and it is felt as considerably stronger, more absolute, and more desolate than ordinary sadness words.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES. Its nearest rivals stake out surrounding territory: devastation stresses the violence of destruction; ruin the brokenness of what was once built; loneliness the ordinary ache of being alone; solitude a neutral or even pleasant aloneness; grief the active process of mourning; melancholy a pensive, often poetic sadness. Desolation alone combines the ruin of a place with the emptiness of a soul, offering no comfort and no beauty — only the blank aftermath. Its historical bridge runs from the Roman soldier "leaving a land utterly alone" to the modern mind "left utterly alone by what it has lost."
