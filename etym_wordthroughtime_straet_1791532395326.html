- 5th–7th century: a Latin loanword in the Germanic dialects of the North Sea coast - *strātā means a paved road, a piece of Roman engineering rather than an everyday lane.
- 8th–11th century: Old English strǣt - the word names Roman roads and paved ways, and is used in boundary clauses and place-names; it still carries the prestige of Roman engineering.
- 12th–14th century: Middle English strete - the sense narrows toward a road in a town, and the word becomes ordinary rather than technical.
- 15th–17th century: the rise of the built-up street - street comes to mean a row of houses with a roadway, and street names and street life become features of urban description.
- 18th–19th century: the industrial and imperial city - the street becomes a site of commerce, poverty, spectacle and reform, and "the streets" acquires a social and moral charge.
- 20th–21st century: metonymy and compound explosion - "the Street" means finance, "the street" means homelessness or public opinion, and "street style," "street food" and "street smart" turn the word into a marker of authenticity and urban culture.
