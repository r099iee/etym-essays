- [14th c.] - Borrowed from Old French paillete (diminutive of paille 'straw'), Middle English palet denotes a coarse mattress or pad filled with straw, chaff, or rushes - [connotation: peasant poverty, servitude, rustic simplicity]
- [15th–16th c.] - Established in Early Modern English and Tudor drama (Chaucer, Shakespeare) as the standard poetic contrast to royal canopies and featherbeds - [connotation: humility, weary but sound sleep, ascetic virtue]
- [17th–18th c.] - French technical homophone palette (from Latin pala 'spade') is adopted into English craft terminology as pallet for flat potter's tools, bookbinding gilding irons, and clock escapement pawls - [connotation: artisan precision, mechanical ingenuity]
- [19th c.] - Victorian literature (Dickens, Brontë, Wilde) uses pallet as an evocative descriptive marker of garrets, workhouses, student rooms, and prison cells - [connotation: pathos, bohemian austerity, modest student romance]
- [1920s–Present] - American warehousing and logistics invent the standardised wooden shipping pallet, revolutionising modern global transport - [connotation: industrial efficiency, supply chain backbone, logistics staple]
