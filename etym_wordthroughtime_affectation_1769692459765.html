- [15th–16th century] - "Affectation" enters English from Latin affectatio ("a striving after"), meaning the deliberate assumption or pursuit of a quality, manner, or status one does not genuinely possess.
- [16th–17th century] - In an age of courtly display, the word curdles: "affected" style and "affectation" become code for studied artificiality, the theatrical performance of refinement that observers delight in mocking.
- [18th–19th century] - Austen makes affectation a central social vice: the "affectation of candour" or of feeling — a universal social mask worn to appear agreeable and win prestige, condemned precisely because it replaces genuine character with posture.
- [Contemporary sense] - "Affectation" still means artificial behavior, speech, or mannerism designed to impress; it now also lightly covers pretentious tastes (an affectation for obscure wines), but always implies a straining after effect rather than naturalness.
