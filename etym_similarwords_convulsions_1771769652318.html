• Upheaval: Similar because both denote violent, sudden disturbance; differs because upheaval implies a structural overturning — a geological or social lifting and displacing — and is heavily social-political in colour, lacking the physiological shudder and the clenched, spasmodic image of a convulsion.
  • Cataclysm: Similar because both denote overwhelming disaster; differs because cataclysm is apocalyptic and terminal, suggesting a world-ending catastrophe far grander than Gibbon's contained fit of nature, and it need not be shaking or muscular at all.
  • Turbulence: Similar because both denote chaotic, violent motion; differs because turbulence names restless, disorderly movement — of air, water, or crowds — without the sudden, clenched, involuntary violence of a spasm; turbulence is ongoing chaos, a convulsion is a violent fit.
  • Spasms: Similar because both are involuntary muscular contractions; differs because spasms remain purely medical and localized — a twitch or cramp of particular muscles — whereas convulsions are violent, generalized, and have been extended into political, natural, and emotional upheaval.
  • Paroxysm: Similar because both denote a violent sudden outburst or attack; differs because paroxysm is primarily the acute fit or attack of a disease (and by extension of laughter or rage) — a single eruptive episode — while convulsions emphasize the violent shaking itself and their plural form covers a series of such fits.
  • Eruption: Similar because both suggest violent bursting-forth; differs because eruption is anchored to volcanoes (and, in metaphor, to the bursting-out of pent-up feeling) — a matter of matter breaking out — whereas a convulsion is a shaking or wrenching, not an outpouring.
  • Agitation: Similar because both denote disturbance; differs because agitation is milder — a mere stirring, commotion, or restlessness — lacking the sudden, violent, uncontrollable force of a convulsion.
  • Commotion: Similar because both denote noisy disturbance; differs because commotion is chiefly public noise and confusion, a social stir, without the involuntary, physical, spasm-like violence that convulsions carry.
  • Shock (as in seismic): Similar because both denote a sudden violent impact or jolt; differs because shock names the single impact or the state produced by it, while convulsions name the repeated, wrenching, uncontrolled shaking itself.
  • Struggle / convulsion as body metaphor (familial parallel): Similar because both involve violent uncontrolled movement; differs because struggle implies a deliberate contest of effort against resistance, whereas a convulsion is by definition involuntary — no one 'convulses' on purpose.
