• Heath: Similar because both name open uncultivated country of heather and poor soil; differs because a heath is typically lowland and drier (southern heaths of Surrey and Dorset), while moors are upland, wetter, and peatier, with a wilder northern character.
  • Moorland: Similar because it is the same landscape under its collective singular name; differs only in grammar and scale — moorland is the mass noun for the whole habitat, while moors counts the separate upland tracts themselves.
  • Fell: Similar because both name high bleak northern uplands beloved of walkers; differs because fell is the Norse-flavoured word of the Lake District for a single hill or mountain side, rocky and steep, while moors are the broad rolling plateaus between the peaks.
  • Bog: Similar because blanket bog is the wet beating heart of most moors; differs because a bog is specifically waterlogged peat ground that sucks at boots, while moors take in the dry heather banks and grassy ridges around it too.
  • Marsh: Similar because Old English used one word for both, and both are wet uncultivated waste; differs because a marsh is low flat wetland of reeds and standing water, while moors in modern use are upland and heathery, wet from rain rather than rivers.
  • Wasteland: Similar because both describe land left outside cultivation; differs because wasteland is the legal and historical category (common waste, industrial dereliction), cold and colourless, while moors paints the actual purple-green place with its birds and weather.
  • Upland: Similar because nearly all moors are upland country; differs because upland is the geographer's neutral altitude band (any high ground, farmed or wild), while moors names the wild uncultivated habitat itself.
  • Tundra: Similar because both are vast treeless expanses of moss, sedge, and dwarf shrub under huge skies; differs because tundra is the Arctic biome frozen half the year, while moors are temperate and man-haunted, shaped by grazing, burning, and peat cutting.
  • Mire: Similar because mires and moors share the same sucking peat and cotton grass; differs because a mire stresses the dangerous sinking wetness itself (to be mired is to be stuck), while moors embrace the whole walkable landscape around the wet patches.
