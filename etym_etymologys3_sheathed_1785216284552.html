The biography of sheathed begins, counterintuitively, with cutting. The word descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *skei-, "to cut, split, separate, divide," one of the most productive roots in the entire family. Its children are scattered across the Indo-European world: in Greek it produced skhizein, "to split," the ancestor of English schism — a splitting of a church — and of the schizo- of psychiatry, the splitting of the mind; in Latin it gave scindere, "to cut, split," behind rescind, scissile, and abscission; and in the Indo-Aryan branch it gave Sanskrit chid-, "to split, cut off," whose derivative chidra- "hole" descends into Hindi chhed, "hole, opening." Everywhere the root is about division, separation, and the clean cut.

In the Germanic branch the root was extended into Proto-Germanic *skaid-, "to separate, divide," and from that base the ancestors of the English built one of their most brilliant coinages: the sheath. A blade-cover was conceived not as a box or a bag but as "that which keeps apart" — the piece that separates the sharp steel from the flesh of the wearer, the divider that stands between the weapon and the body. The Old Norse had skeið, the Old High German sceida, and the Goths even derived their verb for "to separate" from the same root, skaidan. Old English had scēaþ, recorded from the early centuries of the written language, meaning "scabbard, sheath." This is why the modern words for the thing still rhyme with the words for dividing: the sheath is the divider, and the concept travelled with the object from the northern forests into every Germanic tongue.

Middle English carried the noun down as shethe, and around the fourteenth century English performed a quiet piece of word-making: it turned the noun into a verb. To sheathe meant "to put into a sheath," and its past participle sheathed named both the completed act — the sword slid home into its scabbard — and the resulting state of being covered and concealed. For centuries the word lived at the heart of martial life. "Sheathe the sword" was the soldier's and knight's gesture of ending combat, and the phrase carried chivalric weight: a sheathed blade meant peace, control, and stored menace, the promise of violence held in reserve. When a medieval chronicler wrote that a king sheathed his sword, he meant that the fighting had stopped and the king had chosen mercy — or at least a pause.

The seventeenth to nineteenth centuries generalised the word far beyond weapons. Anything enclosed in a close-fitting protective covering came to be described as sheathed: electrical cables are sheathed in rubber or metal, a wire sheathed in plastic, a branch sheathed in ice, a hillside sheathed in snow. The technical sense took hold in the vocabulary of the industrial age, where sheathing became an act of engineering as much as of war. In anatomy and biology the same idea found its perfect home: a sheath is the protective tissue layer enclosing a structure, most famously the myelin sheath that wraps and insulates the axons of nerve cells — the nervous system's own scabbard for the swift current of the nerve impulse. The word's opposite, unsheathed, kept the blade's menace alive: an unsheathed sword or unsheathed claw is the danger released.

In Tolkien's sentence the participle carries the full weight of this history. Bilbo "sheathed his sword, which was now glowing faintly again" — Sting, the little blade whose light has just betrayed danger in the darkness of the goblin tunnels, is pushed back into the scabbard that "separates" its glow from the gloom around him. The ancient act of covering the splitter with the divider, hiding the blade so that he might follow, unseen, through the dark, is exactly what the word has meant for a thousand years — the fitted covering, the concealment, the stored menace of a weapon returned to its case. From the PIE root of cutting, to the Germanic divider, to the knight's gesture of peace, to the myelin sheath of the nerve, the word has never lost its core: what is sheathed is kept apart, covered, and safe — until it is drawn again.
