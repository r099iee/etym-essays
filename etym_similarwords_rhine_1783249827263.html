• Rhenish: Similar because both refer to the same river and its world; differs because Rhenish is the learned, archaic adjective (from Latin Rhenanus) still heard in 'Rhenish wine', while Rhine is the plain modern noun.
  • Danube: Similar because both are great boundary-rivers of Europe that framed the Roman frontier (Rhenus et Danubius); differs because the Danube formed the empire's eastern line and was far more multilingual and porous than the permanent Rhine frontier.
  • Elbe: Similar because both mark a Germanic limit of the ancient and medieval world; differs because the Elbe represented a later, purely Germanic boundary rather than the lasting Romance frontier the Rhine became.
  • Rhône: Similar because its name is built on the same Celtic hydronymic pattern (Latin Rhodanus, 'the flowing one'); differs because the Rhône lies inside the Romance world and never became a civilisational frontier as the Rhine did.
  • Rhineland: Similar because both name the river's domain; differs because Rhineland is the modern political-geographical region (from German Rheinland), while Rhine is the river itself.
  • Rhinegold: Similar because both carry the river's name into compounds; differs because Rhinegold is the mythological treasure of the Nibelungen (Wagner's Das Rheingold), a literary-musical extension, not a geographical synonym.
  • Rhein / Rijn: Similar because they are the same hydronym in German and Dutch; differs because they are the modern vernacular forms, while Rhine is the English (Latinized) name.
