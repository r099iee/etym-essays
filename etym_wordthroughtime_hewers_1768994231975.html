- Old English (hēawan): to hew means to cut, chop, or strike with a blade, and the agent noun hewer is a plain, neutral craft-name — the man who squares timber or dresses stone, no more weighted than 'miller' or 'smith'.
- 1611: the King James Bible freezes the plural into the phrase “hewers of wood and drawers of water” (Joshua 9:21, 9:23), naming the Gibeonites condemned to the two most menial labours — the moment hewers becomes a class label, the lowliest manual worker seen through a scriptural lens.
- 17th–18th c.: the collocation turns proverbial; in political and religious prose “hewers of wood and drawers of water” is shorthand for the whole labouring class — honest, biblical, and fixed below the learned — giving hewers an archaic dignity that plain ‘woodcutter’ never had.
- 19th–20th c.: industrial senses branch off — the ‘coal-hewer’ is the miner who cuts coal from the seam, and hewer is a recognized trade-name — the word gains a working-class heroism while the biblical sense continues its slow drift toward archaism.
- 20th–21st c.: outside the fixed phrase hewers is now distinctly archaic and literary; to use it is deliberately to invoke the Old-Testament class epithet, the emblem of inherited manual station, not to describe an actual modern woodcutter.
