Patron is a word that turned a family relation into a social institution. Its root is the Indo-European kinship base *ph₂tér-, "father," one of the oldest and most stable reconstructions in the whole of Indo-European linguistics: Sanskrit pitṛ (पितृ), Greek patēr (πατήρ), Latin pater, and the Germanic *fadēr behind English father all descend from it with a fidelity that shows how central the father figure was to the ancient world. In the Italic branch the root produced the fateful derivative patronus, formed from pater with a suffix denoting office or relationship. In Roman law a patronus was a former master who, after freeing a slave, remained that man's legal protector and guardian; the institution was called patrōcinium, "patronage," and it bound both men in a web of duties and rights — the patron owed protection, the client owed service and deference. From this narrowly legal beginning the word widened across the empire: any influential protector of clients, of a city, or of a cause could be a patronus, standing to his dependents as a father stands to his children. The entire modern idea of patronage is contained in this Roman gesture: support given in exchange for loyalty, protection understood as a kind of fatherhood.

Latin patronus passed into Old French as patron, and English borrowed it around 1300, at first meaning "a protector, a defender." But the French word had also developed a second sense — "a model, a sample, an example," perhaps because the patron who commissions a work is also its pattern — and English carried both senses inside one word. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the two meanings had drifted so far apart that they split in spelling: patron kept the protector, while pattern became the model. This is one of the most celebrated word-splits in English, and it explains why a sewing "pattern" and a "patron of the arts" are etymologically the very same word. Meanwhile the religious sense, patron saint, fixed the idea of a heavenly protector standing over a church, a town, or a trade, and the Renaissance flooded the term with new life as "patron of the arts" named the princes and merchants who sheltered painters, poets, and musicians — the Medicis and the popes, the dukes and the guilds.

The wider family of patron shows how fertile the root proved. Patrimony (Latin patrimonium) is the estate a father hands down; patrician (Latin patricius) names the Roman senator descended from the patres, "the fathers"; paternal and paternity keep the pure father-sense; patriot, through Greek patriṓtēs, "fellow-countryman," turns the father into the fatherland, patria; and patriarch, from Greek patriárkhēs, "the ruler of a family," gives the root its most elevated office. The modern verb patronise has itself undergone a revealing shift: once it meant simply "to act as a patron," but in the eighteenth century it acquired the condescending sense, "to treat someone as an inferior" — a reminder of how easily protection slides into superiority, and how the word's fatherly root could curdle into paternalism. The social history is continuous: the Roman client-patron system, the feudal lord's protection of his dependents, the Renaissance prince's sheltering of artists, the modern business customer, all repeat the same pattern of protection in exchange for loyalty. Key stations:

  • PIE *ph₂tér- "father" → Latin pater → patronus "one who stands in a father's place" (Plautus, 2nd c. BCE).
  • Roman patrōcinium — the legal institution of the patron-protector and the client.
  • Latin patronus → Old French patron (protector and model) → Middle English patron (c. 1300).
  • The patron/pattern split (c. 1600) — one word divides into protector and model.
  • Renaissance patronage of the arts; patron saint (16th c.) — the father-protector becomes heavenly and princely.
  • patronise (16th c. "support") → (18th c. "condescend") — protection curdles into superiority.
  • 19th-century American "patron" = regular customer — the benefactor becomes the client's support base.

In this novel's scene all the layers are present at once. When Yu-cun bows elaborately and thanks his patron profusely before daring to ask his question, he is performing the ancient Roman transaction of client and patronus — the father-figure, the protector, the model, the one whose favour, once given, must be answered with gratitude and used with care.
