Dike is, in standard modern use, a long raised bank built to hold water back or out — the earthen wall between river and field, sea and pasture — but the word carries its own opposite inside it, for in northern England and Scotland a dike is the ditch itself, the hollow the bank was dug from. Bank and trench, wall and drain: the two senses are one act of digging seen from either side, and the source sentence walks the reader across both, the lane crossing a deep dike before climbing onto the high-banked causeway beyond.

Core meaning in detail: A dike in the defensive sense is typically a long, low, grassed embankment of compacted earth, sometimes with a clay core, engineered to resist water pressure along rivers, estuaries, and coasts — from the sea-dikes of the Netherlands to the fen-dikes of East Anglia to the levee systems of the Mississippi. In the northern sense it is a field drain or boundary ditch, often stone-faced, carrying the same name because the spoil of the ditch became the bank beside it. Offa’s Dyke and the Devil’s Dykes preserve the ancient fortified sense: ditch-and-bank as frontier, defence dug into the landscape itself.

The Dutch mirror: no country has made the dike its destiny like the Netherlands, where a third of the land lies below sea level behind sea-dikes, and where English travellers from the 17th century onward learned to see embankments as national architecture. Vermuyden’s drainage of the Fens with Dutch labour imported both the technique and the vocabulary; the 1953 North Sea flood, which drowned polders in Holland and coasts in England in a single night, fixed the breached dike in both nations’ memory as the image of catastrophe; and the proverb of the finger in the dike made it the universal emblem of vigilance against slow disaster.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Flood-defence embankment (primary standard sense): sea walls of earth, river banks, polder dikes. The bridge is function: whatever keeps water off land by height rather than by depth.
  • Ditch, drain, watercourse (northern and Scots sense, oldest in feel): field dikes, stone-faced lane dikes as boundaries. The bridge is origin: the trench is the dike’s other half, and dialect kept the half that standard English dropped.
  • Defensive earthwork (historical): Offa’s Dyke, frontier dykes of the dark ages. The bridge carries bank-and-ditch from water to war: the same profile that stops the sea stops armies, and early kingdoms drew their borders with the spade.
  • Stone boundary wall (Scots extension): a dry-stane dyke round the field. The bridge is material substitution: where stone replaces heaped earth, the boundary keeps the old name and the old line.
  • Unrelated slang homographs (noted only to exclude): modern slang spellings share letters but no history with the embankment, and lexicographers keep them in separate entries; the bridge is spelling only.

Usage, grammar, and register: dike is plain, rural, and workmanlike — engineers’ reports, walkers’ guides, estate maps — with the dyke spelling favoured in place names and Scots contexts. Collocations run wet: sea dike, river dike, fen dike, drainage dike, breach a dike, hold the dike, patrol the dikes. In dialect speech it is still the ditch by the road; in standard speech it is the bank that saves the field — and fluent readers, like the lane-walkers of the source sentence, keep both pictures ready at once, crossing the hollow and climbing the bank in a single stride.
