Wormed belongs to one of the great word-families of English, built on the Proto-Indo-European root *wer-, 'to turn, to bend, to twist, to coil'. For the earliest speakers the root named anything that turned or coiled: the rotating joint of the wrist, the writhing of a wounded body, the wrenching of a tool, the winding of a wreath, the twisting root of a plant (Greek rhiza). The root's unifying image is circular or spiralling motion, and out of it each branch of the family carved its own vocabulary of turning. It is from this root that the Germanic peoples built the word for the archetypal coiling creature: the worm.

Proto-Germanic *wurmiz meant 'a serpent, a dragon, a creeping thing' - and we must be careful not to read the modern, diminutive sense back into it. For the early Germanic and Norse peoples, a wyrm (Old English wyrm, Old Norse ormr) was a creature of terror: the great Midgard Serpent Jormungandr, which encircles the world in Norse myth; the dragon that guards the treasure-hoard and is slain by Beowulf; the firedrake of countless legends. The word belonged to the vocabulary of heroism and dread, describing a majestic, sinuous, crushing beast whose nature was defined by its coiling motion. In the Old English poetic tradition the wyrm could breathe fire and hoard gold; it was a fitting antagonist for heroes, not a pest for gardeners.

The catastrophe, for the word's dignity, was the Norman Conquest. After 1066, French and Latin began to displace the native vocabulary of the English elite, and the great serpents of legend came to be called 'dragons' (from Greek drakon via Latin and Old French). What remained to the humble word wyrm - now spelled worm - was only the smaller truth: the earthworm, the maggot, the grub, the parasite. A word that had named Beowulf's dragon was reduced to the blind creature that turns the soil. This downsizing is one of the great semantic collapses in English history, and it is precisely the shrinking that made the word available as a metaphor for low, creeping, subterranean behaviour.

From the noun came the verb. In the literal sense, to worm was to move or crawl like a worm - to creep on the belly or to wriggle through narrow places; the past tense wormed is recorded in this physical sense from the early modern period. Then the metaphor leapt from the body into society. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century writers, inheriting a culture fascinated by courts, favour, and intrigue, spoke of men who 'wormed themselves' into royal confidence, into a household, into a party - moving invisibly and persistently through the cracks of power. By the seventeenth century the verb had already acquired the modern extraction sense: to 'worm out' a secret was to draw it from a person by the same blind persistence with which a worm forces its way through earth.

  • - c. 4500-3500 BCE: PIE *wer- 'to turn, bend, twist' - the root of the coiling.
  • - c. 500 BCE: Proto-Germanic *wurmiz 'serpent, dragon, worm'.
  • - c. 700-1000 CE: Old English wyrm - Beowulf's dragon, the serpent of legend.
  • - 1066 onward: Norman French 'dragon' displaces wyrm in its grand senses; worm shrinks to the earthworm and the maggot.
  • - 12th-15th c.: Middle English worm; the creeping creature.
  • - 16th-17th c.: the verb worm - first 'to crawl like a worm', then 'to worm oneself into' and 'to worm a secret out of'.
  • - 20th c.: the computer worm; the noun's metaphor of burrowing through networks completes the cycle.

Coda: wormed is thus the past tense of a verb whose noun lost a dragon and gained a metaphor. The animal's very lowliness - its blindness, its slowness, its underground motion - became, in human hands, a description of the stealthiest form of intelligence. To have wormed something out of someone is to have outwitted them without ever seeming to fight; the word carries the whole strange history of the wyrm in its syllables, from the terror of the dragon to the slyness of the secret. In the supplied sentence, the information about the reinstatement was not obtained by right or by force but wormed out - and that single word connects the character's quiet, subterranean triumph to a thousand years of English words for creatures that move unseen beneath the surface of things.
