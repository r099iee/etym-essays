The English word Macedon is a short word with a long pedigree: it is the bare nominative of the Greek ethnonym Μακεδών (Makedōn), ‘a Macedonian,’ taken into English as a fixed, archaizing name for the ancient kingdom. Behind that single form stands a chain that begins, like that of Macedonia, in the Proto-Indo-European root *meh₂k- ‘long, tall,’ an adjectival root of height and length that also produced Greek μακρός (makrós) ‘long’ and Latin macer ‘thin, lean.’ The immediate Greek parent is the adjective μακεδνός (makednós) ‘tall, slender, tapering,’ applied in Homeric usage to high, slender things; from this the Greeks formed the ethnonym, so that the Macedonians were, in the common reading, “the tall ones” or “the highlanders” of the Pierian and Olympean uplands. Greek tradition reinforced the etymology by myth: Makedōn was the eponymous hero, son of Zeus and Thyia in the Hesiodic Catalogue of Women, the ancestor from whom the Makedónes took their name. The history of the word is the history of the kingdom. From its shadowy emergence in the seventh century BC, the Argead state of Macedon grew steadily, absorbing the mountain cantons of Upper Macedonia and the coastal plains of Lower Macedonia. In the fifth century BC the name enters Greek literature — Herodotus and Thucydides treat the Makedónes as a northern people whose Greekness was doubtful; in Athenian oratory of the fourth century, Demosthenes could sneer at Philip II as not even a Greek. Yet in the second half of the 4th century BC the same name was carried to the centre of world history: Philip II (r. 359–336 BC) unified and militarized Macedon, and his son Alexander (r. 336–323 BC) led the Macedonian phalanx and cavalry across the Hellespont, defeated Darius III, and built an empire from Greece to India. “Macedon” thus came to stand, in a few generations, for the power that ended the classical Greek city-state world and opened the Hellenistic age. Roman writers adopted the Greek nominative as Macedo, genitive Macedonis, and the fall of the kingdom to Rome in 168 BC (Pydna) and its annexation as a province in 148 BC gave the name its terminal historical date — the lifespan the gloss on this card records. In Latin literature — Livy, Florus, the historians of the Macedonian Wars — Macedo names the soldier and the state; the adjective Macedonicus was borne by victorious Roman generals such as Q. Caecilius Metellus Macedonicus. English received the name through the humanist revival of classical learning. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, as English humanists translated the classical historians, the Greek nominative Makedōn and the Latin Macedo were adopted directly in the truncated form “Macedon,” while the wider territorial name “Macedonia” came in by the same route (via Latin, with Old French mediation). The two forms soon specialised: “Macedonia” named the land and region, while “Macedon” — shorter, stonier, more resonant — named the kingdom as an actor in history. Eighteenth-century neo-classical prose and verse made the split canonical: Pope, Gibbon, and the historians of antiquity habitually wrote “Philip of Macedon,” “the arms of Macedon,” “the fall of Macedon,” reserving the fuller form for geography and administration. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries “Macedon” acquired a further, deliberate function. As “Macedonia” became entangled in the modern political dispute — the Greek–Yugoslav quarrel over the name of the successor state, settled only by the Prespa Agreement of 2018–19 — historians who wished to speak neutrally of the ancient kingdom found in “Macedon” a form free of nationalist freight. Today the word survives in two registers: the literary-archaic (“the phalanx of Macedon” in histories and epic-tinged prose) and the scholarly-neutral (the ancient kingdom as distinct from the modern region and state). A word that began as the Greek for ‘the tall one,’ became the name of the power that conquered the known world, and was kept alive in English as the poetic, unsullied name of a vanished kingdom — a rare case of a toponym surviving as a fossil precisely because a modern political controversy made its longer sister form awkward to use.
