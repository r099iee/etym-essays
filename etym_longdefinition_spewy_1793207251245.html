Spewy is a rare dialectal English adjective meaning oozing with moisture, marshy, and spongy underfoot — soil, and especially gravel or loam, that seems to spew water back at the boot when it is trodden on. In the source sentence William Cobbett, riding through Middlesex in Rural Rides, distinguishes the sound gravel-and-loam ground near the Thames from the land further back, which he calls a sort of spewy gravel: hungry, waterlogged drift that never properly drains and squelches under the horse's hooves.

Core meaning in detail: The word is built transparently as spew + -y (‘characterised by, given to’), exactly like runny, oozy, or weepy. Just as a spring spews water from the hillside and a man spews vomit from his mouth, so spewy ground spews water upward around every footprint. The image is active and faintly disgusting: the earth does not merely lie wet, it discharges. Cobbett the farmer uses it as a term of agricultural condemnation — spewy land is cold, sour, late land, the opposite of the kind deep loam a husbandman loves.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Waterlogged and oozing, of ground (primary dialectal sense, 19th c.): marshy gravel or clay that wells up with water when pressed. The bridge is direct composition: ground characterised by spewing water, as rainy is sky characterised by rain.
  • Discharging or running, of sores and ulcers (secondary, provincial): recorded in dialect glossaries for wounds that weep matter. The bridge runs from the earth to the body: as the bog discharges water, so the sore discharges pus — both are surfaces that will not stay sealed.
  • Given to spewing or vomiting, of persons or animals (rare): a spewy drunkard, a spewy dog. The bridge is the verb's oldest human sense: one characterised by the act of spewing itself, the -y turned back on the agent rather than the ground.
  • Spouting or gushing, of springs, pipes, and chimneys (transferred): a spewy spring, a spewy chimney pouring smoke. The bridge extends the discharge image from water in soil to any vent that will not stop emitting — the same picture of an unsealable mouth.
  • Showery, spit-drizzling, of weather (dialectal, rare): a spewy morning that spits rain in fits. The bridge carries the verb skyward: clouds that spew droplets the way the marsh spews water underfoot.

Register and connotation: spewy is rustic, provincial, and faintly contemptuous — a word of the farm and the stable-yard, never of the drawing room. It belongs with Cobbett's whole vocabulary of agricultural scorn: hungry soils, bleak wastes, tax-eaters’ boxes. To call land spewy is to damn it as undrainable and ungrateful, fit neither for corn nor for building. Modern readers meet it almost nowhere else, which is why dictionaries mark it dialectal and rare: it survives in the English Dialect Dictionary, in the OED with a handful of citations, and in the one immortal sentence of Cobbett that keeps it breathing.
