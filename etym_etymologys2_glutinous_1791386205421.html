Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*gley-
"to stick, cleave; clay, glue"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*glūten-
"that which sticks, glue"
↓
Classical Latin (1st c. BCE)
glūten
"glue, viscous substance"
↓
Late Latin (3rd–4th c. CE)
glūtīnāre
"to glue together, cement"
↓
Late Latin (4th–5th c. CE)
glūtīnōsus
"gluey, sticky, viscous"
↓
Old French (13th c.)
glutinos
"sticky, viscous"
↓
Middle French (14th–15th c.)
glutineux
"viscous, adhesive, glue-like"
↓
Middle English (c. 1530s–1540s)
glutinous
"gluey, sticky, viscous"
"(later fig.) cloying, sentimental"
Note: The PIE root *gley- (with extensions *gleit- and *gloy-) generated an enormous family of words relating to adhesion across Indo-European languages, including Germanic *klajjaz (English *clay*), Old English *clīfan* (English *cleave* to / cling), *clamm* (English *clam*), and Greek *gloia* (glue). The Latin noun *glūten* produced the adjectival derivation *glūtīnōsus* via the productive suffix *-ōsus* ("full of"), which entered English through French and direct Latin borrowing.
