The compound well-flogged is a small but revealing specimen of English word-formation: a degree adverb joined to a participle, one element ancient Germanic, the other a late, obscure, and vigorously colloquial verb of punishment. Its history is really two histories, which is why a responsible account treats each root in isolation and then shows how they lock together in Gibbon's ironical phrase.

Root 1: WELL — the Germanic adverb of thoroughness
The first element is ancient Germanic stock. Behind it stands the Proto-Indo-European root *wel- / *welh₁-, reconstructed with the sense "to wish, to want, to will" — the root of the entire will/wish family, including Latin velle "to wish" (source of voluntary, volition) and its cognates across the Indo-European languages. From this wish-root, Proto-Germanic developed an adverbial formation *wel- / *wela- meaning "as desired, well, in a good manner." Old English wel continued it, meaning "well, thoroughly, properly," and already in Old English it served as an intensifier of adjectives and past participles: to be "well taught" is to be taught thoroughly. That intensifying use survives in a long series of English compounds — well-bred, well-read, well-worn, well-done, well-known — of which well-flogged is a darker member. The adverb's semantics are stable across a thousand years: well- before a participle always means "thoroughly, completely, in a proper degree," and it is this ancient thoroughness that gives the compound its force: not merely flogged, but thoroughly flogged.

Root 2: FLOGGED — the late, obscure verb of the lash
The second element is historically messier and far more recent. English flog surfaces in the later 17th century in naval, criminal, and school contexts, meaning "to beat with a whip or rod." Its origin is genuinely uncertain, and etymologists divide between two main hypotheses. The first connects it to Latin flagellum "little whip" and the learned verb flagellāre "to whip" — perhaps as a slang or cant shortening in school or criminal argot, perhaps influenced by the broad similarity of the sounds. The second treats it as imitative, echoing the crack of a whip. Neither account yields a clean PIE verb tree for "flog" itself, and no Germanic ancestor has been established, so responsible description separates the secure learned Latin flagellum family (English flagellate, flagellation, and the biological flagellum of bacteria and sperm cells, so named for its whip-like motion) from the English cant verb whose exact birth remains disputed. What is secure is social history: from the later 17th century, flogging was routine in the Royal Navy (where the cat-o'-nine-tails was the instrument of discipline), in criminal punishment, and in the classical grammar school, where the birch was an instrument of Latinity as much as of order. The past participle flogged ("beaten") and the agent form flogger are attested alongside the verb.

The compound: well-flogged
Joining the ancient thoroughness of well- to the modern participle flogged yields a compound whose literal sense is simply "thoroughly beaten." But the schoolroom context gave it a second, figurative life. In the 18th- and 19th-century grammar school, rigorous training and corporal punishment were the same practice: the birch that punished also drilled, and a boy's mastery of Latin grammar and Greek prosody was understood to have been literally beaten into him. A "well-flogged" scholar was therefore not merely a beaten one but a thoroughly drilled one — one whose precision, it was half-proudly, half-ironically claimed, had become second nature through the whip. It is precisely this cultural logic that Gibbon exploits. Lamenting that his own "desultory" Greek training left him without "the scrupulous ear of the well-flogged critic," he uses the compound to pay a backhanded compliment: the victim of the birch may well possess a finer ear for quantity and idiom than the self-taught amateur, even as Gibbon flinches at the method. The irony is structural — "well" elevates the beating into a mode of accreditation, while "flogged" keeps the brutality in view.

Later life of the phrase
In the 19th and 20th centuries the literal force gradually faded, and well-flogged could be used of any thoroughly drilled or disciplined performer, while the nautical and penal senses (a well-flogged crew or deserter) remained available in historical writing. A separate figurative use applies to arguments, topics, or jokes that have been "flogged to death" — overworked by repetition, as in the related idiom "flog a dead horse." In memoir and biography, however, the phrase retains its period flavour: it is a self-deprecating, darkly comic shorthand for the brutal thoroughness of traditional classical education, and it is Gibbon's usage that keeps the compound alive for modern readers of the Memoirs. The two etymological roots — ancient Germanic thoroughness and obscure 17th-century cant punishment — meet exactly in that phrase, and the compound's whole meaning is the friction between them.
