- [Early English: the animal cry] - To bellow originally named the deep, resonant, full-throated roar of animals, especially bulls and deer — a natural, instinctive cry of great volume.
- [15th-17th century: transfer to the human voice] - The verb crosses over to people: to bellow is to shout with a deep-voiced, angry or urgent roar, clearly set apart from the higher-pitched shout or yell.
- [18th-19th century: command and authority] - In nautical and military writing a captain or sergeant bellows orders to cut through noise and danger, so the word gains firm overtones of power, authority, and being used to obedience.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still strongly tied to the bull, bellowed now works as a vivid, literary speech-tag for a deep, chest-shaking, commanding shout, deliberately opposed to the neutral "said" of ordinary narration.
