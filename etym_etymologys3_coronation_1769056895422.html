“Coronation” is a biography of the circle. Its deepest ancestor is a Proto-Indo-European root, *(s)ker-, meaning “to turn, bend, curve,” whose zero-grade *kor- produced a whole family of curved things across the ancient languages: bent staffs, rounded baskets, and crooked limbs. In Greek this produced “korṓnē,” a word that wriggled between senses — “anything curved,” a garland of twisted foliage, the circular wreath set on a victor’s head, and even the hooded crow, named for its curved beak. Already the link between “a curved thing” and “a wreath” was alive: the crown began, not as gold and jewels, but as a twisted ring of leaves. The Greek word passed into Latin as “corōna,” the garland of victory, and once Rome’s emperors began to be crowned, the garland of leaves hardened into the crown of the state. Beekes, it should be said, has argued that Greek “korṓnē” may be a Pre-Greek substrate word, in which case the PIE assignment is traditional rather than certain; but the recorded history from Greek onward is unaffected.

From Latin “corōna” the word spread along two great routes. In the Romance languages it never left home: Italian “corona,” Spanish “corona,” Portuguese “coroa,” French “couronne,” and Romanian “coroană” all descend directly, and from Old French “coroune” English took “crown” in the twelfth century, building “crowned,” “coroner” (the officer of the crown), and “coronet” upon it. Through Christianity the Latin word was also borrowed into the Germanic world, giving German “Krone” and Dutch “kroon” — loanwords, not inheritances, carried by the Church’s image of the King of Kings and by Charlemagne’s imperial crowning at Rome in 800 CE, which made the coronation the defining ritual of medieval kingship. Meanwhile the Latin verb “corōnāre” (“to crown”) grew a noun, “corōnātiō,” the act of crowning, which reached English by way of Old French “coronacion” in the late fourteenth century as “coronation.” The English word has kept its Latin spelling and its grand Latin ring, even as the everyday object was handed over to the simpler native “crown”; the two words from the one source — one short, native-sounding, and for the object, one long, learned, and for the ceremony — are a small monument to the social history of English.

The coronation ritual itself drove the word’s meaning. In the medieval and early modern West the coronation was the hinge of a reign: the oath, the anointing with chrism, the investiture with orb, sceptre, and ring, and the placing of the crown — the moment costume becomes authority, exactly as Lewis puts it in the Narnia passage (“coronation rings,” the rings of the day they were made “Kings and Queens of Narnia”). The word therefore carried a sacred, public, ceremonial weight that the plain verb “to crown” never had, and it remains the one word reserved for the full state ritual. In the twentieth century the sense loosened outward into metaphor and everyday life: “coronation chicken” (1953) domesticated the word into cookery; Coronation Street (1960) lodged it in popular culture; and politics borrowed it for a leadership contest that was never really contested (“a coronation”).

Timeline:
  • pre-3rd millennium BCE — PIE *(s)ker- “to turn, bend, curve”; zero-grade *kor-.
  • Classical Greek — korṓnē “anything curved; wreath, garland, crown”; possibly Pre-Greek substrate (Beekes).
  • 1st c. BCE — Latin corōna “garland, wreath, crown of victory,” borrowed from Greek.
  • Roman period — verb corōnāre “to crown”; Late/Medieval Latin corōnātiō “a crowning.”
  • 13th c. — Old French coronacion; English “crown” already present from the 12th c. via Anglo-Norman.
  • late 14th c. — English coronation first recorded for the ceremony of crowning a sovereign.
  • 15th–19th c. — institutionalized as the great state ritual of monarchy; Charlemagne’s crowning (800 CE) and the English coronations set the pattern.
  • 20th–21st c. — metaphorical widening: coronation chicken (1953), Coronation Street (1960), and the political metaphor of a foregone conclusion.
