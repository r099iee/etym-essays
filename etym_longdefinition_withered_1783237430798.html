withered: Macaulay's image of Ireland in the History of England is one of the most arresting in the book: "but a withered and distorted member, adding no strength to the body politic, and reproachfully pointed at by all who feared or envied the greatness of England." The governing metaphor is the body politic — the state conceived as a living organism — whose limbs are its constituent nations, and Ireland is a member (a limb) that is withered: shrivelled, atrophied, drained of vitality, so that it neither contributes strength nor carries life but hangs upon the body as a public reproach. The tone is anatomical, visceral, and morally pointed. Crucially, withered denotes desiccation — a leaf, a branch, a limb dried and deadened by loss of moisture and vigour — rather than decay by rotting. It pictures a slow draining of life from within, not an external blow or an infection. Macaulay's Ireland is not merely poor or weak; it is a dead weight, an embarrassment, "reproachfully pointed at," the visible shame of the imperial body. The adjective therefore works on two registers at once: the physical (a shrivelled limb) and the moral (a member that fails the whole and discredits it).

Core meaning. Withered is the past participle of the verb wither, used as an adjective: dried up, shrivelled, shrunken, or wasted away, especially from loss of moisture or vitality. Applied to plants, it describes leaves, flowers, and branches that have lost their sap and crumbled; applied to living things, limbs, faces, and persons that have lost their fullness, strength, and life; applied to institutions, hopes, and powers, it describes something that has drained away to nothing. The unifying image is slow desiccation — the withdrawal of the life-giving moisture — rather than sudden injury or putrefaction.

Polysemy and shades of meaning.

  • Of plants and vegetation (the physical base sense): "withered leaves," "a withered flower," "withered crops" — dried and shrivelled by lack of water, heat, or season; the oldest and most literal use.
  • Of bodies and limbs: "a withered hand," "a withered face," "a withered limb" — shrunken, wasted, and atrophied, whether by age, disease, or paralysis; the sense Macaulay activates for Ireland.
  • Figurative — of institutions, nations, hopes, powers: "a withered empire," "withered hopes," "a withered reputation" — drained of vitality, strength, or promise, by the same slow desiccation extended to the abstract. This is the moral-political sense of "a withered member of the body politic."
  • Withering (present participle → adjective): "a withering glance," "withering scorn," "withering criticism" — literally "that which blasts or shrivels," so devastating, scorching, or annihilating in effect; the active counterpart of withered, describing the power to dry up and kill.
  • As verb — transitive and intransitive: "the heat withered the crops" (to cause to shrivel); "her courage withered" (to lose vigour gradually); "wither away" (to waste and disappear, a phrase made famous in Marxist accounts of the state).

Historical bridge and semantic reasoning. The verb wither is, etymologically, "to weather": it descends from the Old English verb for exposing something to the weather, and from the fourteenth century the same word came to mean "to become dry and shrivelled," because that is exactly what wind and sun do to a leaf or limb left out. The sense has since extended from plants to bodies to institutions along a single line: whatever loses the moisture of life — sap, vigour, vitality, strength — withers. In Macaulay, the bodily and the political senses fuse: Ireland as a dried, deadened limb of the imperial body, adding no strength, a public reproach.

Register and the synonymic field. Shrivelled is the closest synonym but stresses surface contraction and wrinkling and carries far less moral weight. Wizened is reserved for persons, especially the aged, evoking a dry, wrinkled face, and is grotesquely affectionate rather than accusatory. Sere (also sear) is the archaic, poetic word for dry and withered — Shakespeare's "the sear, the yellow leaf" (Macbeth, V.iii) — and is purely literary. Parched describes dryness from heat or thirst and may be temporary, whereas withered is terminal. Dried-up is the colloquial equivalent, flat and literal. Blighted implies attack from without — frost, disease, or curse — while withered describes slow wasting from within. Atrophied is the medical-scientific term for a wasting limb or organ, close in force to Macaulay's image. In the essay, withered is chosen because it alone unites the botanical, the bodily, and the political in one slow, irreversible drying.
