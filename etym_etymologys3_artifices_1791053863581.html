Artifices is the regular plural of artifice, and its essay must therefore tell two intertwined stories: the compounding of two ancient roots into a Latin word for craftsmanship, and the later pejoration of craftsmanship into trickery, of which the plural form marks the proliferation of devices.

First root: ars. PIE *h2er- meaning to fit, join, put together underlies Greek arariskein to join, harmos joint, harmonia joining, and Latin ars, artis, skill, craft, art. Originally joining planks or fitting words, ars broadened to any learned skill: ars medica medicine, ars oratoria rhetoric, artes liberales liberal arts. English art, artisan, artist, artificial all descend from it. The semantic core is skilled fitting.

  • PIE *h2er- to PIE *h2r-tu- fitting > Latin ars. Cognates: Sanskrit rta order, cosmic fit; Greek artys friendly joining. The root thus already links technique to order.
  • Latin ars as cultural institution: guild craft, fine art and cunning all covered. Ars est celare artem it is art to conceal art already hints at deception latent in skill.

Second root: facere. PIE *dheh1- meaning to do, make, put, set is among the most productive: English do, deed, doom (thing set), Sanskrit dhati puts, Greek thesis placing, Latin facere to do/make, factum deed, factura making, -ficere in compounds (magnificent, efficient). Latin facere covered both doing and making, action and manufacture.

  • PIE *dheh1- reduplicated and suffixed > Latin facere (perfect feci, supine factum). Phonological change dh > f initially in Latin is regular (cf. *dhumos > fumus smoke).
  • Compounding: ars + -fex maker (from facere, as in aurifex goldsmith) yields artifex, artificis, craftsman, artist, author of a trick. Suffix -ium forms abstract artificium: the craft, the product, the trick.

Latin artificium in classical authors (Cicero, Livy, Pliny) is ambivalent but predominantly neutral-positive: craftsmanship, workmanship, professional skill, ingenious construction, with occasional sense of contrivance. Pliny praises artificium in bronze; Cicero contrasts natura and artificium nature and artifice. Only in contexts of rhetoric and war does trick emerge: artificium dicendi contrived style, artificia belli stratagems.

Old French artifice (13th-14th c.) borrowed the Latin noun wholesale, retaining both senses: métier and ruse. Middle English artifice (1530s) initially meant workmanship, skilled construction — Tyndale and Elyot use it for human craft versus nature. The plural artifices appears early in this neutral sense: artifices of building, artifices of husbandry, as in the source sentence innumerable artifices for fishing and hunting, where artifices means ingenious devices, inventions, appliances, without moral censure.

  • 16th-century positive stage: artifice as art. Spenser, Sidney praise poetic artifice. Artificer is honoured as maker.
  • 17th-18th-century bifurcation: art (fine) vs artifice (contrived). Moralists contrast sincere nature with artifice as cunning. Hobbes, Locke use artifice for human construction (the Commonwealth as artificial man) neutrally, but also for deceitful stratagem.
  • 19th-present pejoration in singular, retention in plural-technical: singular artifice increasingly means trick, ruse, wile (by artifice, not force), while plural artifices can still be neutral-technical in history and anthropology (prehistoric artifices, fishing artifices) alongside deceitful sense (political artifices, amorous artifices).

Phonologically, Latin artificium /ar.tiˈfi.ki.um/ lost its ending in French to /ar.ti.fis/; English borrowed spelling artifice and anglicized stress to ART-i-fiss /ˈɑːtɪfɪs/, plural artifices /ˈɑːtɪfɪsɪz/ with regular /ɪz/ plural, spelled -ces by French analogy (cf. precipice/precipices). No great sound shift beyond loss of Latin quantity and French final devoicing.

Literary attestations chart the moral slide: 1531 Elyot the artifice of Carpentrye (neutral craft); 1590s Shakespeare artifice as skill and as stratagem; 1667 Milton Pandemonium rose by strange artifice (engineering marvel with hint of diabolic cunning); 18th-century Chesterfield warning against artifices of courtiers; 19th-century anthropology reverting to neutral plural for primitive tools, as in the source sentence. Modern style keeps both: engineers admire artifices of design (rare, literary), critics denounce artifices of advertising.

Thus artifices today hovers between inventions and tricks, and context decides. In prehistory passages it redeems the older craftsman sense: ingenious fitted-makings (ar- + -fex) for survival. In moral-political passages it condemns clever fitted-makings for deception. Both descend faithfully from joining + making.
