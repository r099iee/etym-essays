A footman (/ˈfʊt.mən/; plural: footmen) is a male domestic servant employed in large aristocratic households, royal palaces, and stately homes. Historically ranked below the butler and under-butler and above the hall boy, the footman performed ceremonial, domestic, and transport duties while wearing the master's elaborate heraldic livery. In addition to its primary domestic meaning, the word encompasses significant historical military, physical transport, decorative antique, and entomological definitions.

1. Domestic Hierarchy and Aristocratic Role:

In 18th- and 19th-century European and British high society, footmen were essential symbols of wealth, aristocratic prestige, and social grandeur. Great households often employed multiple footmen, designated as First Footman, Second Footman, etc.:

  • Primary Domestic Duties: Answering the front door, receiving callers, delivering formal invitations and handwritten letters across town (as depicted in Tolstoy's War and Peace, where Anna Pavlovna sends morning notes by a footman in red livery), waiting at table during formal dinners, carrying heavy dishes, attending drawing rooms, and pouring wine under the supervision of the butler.
  • Carriage Attendance: Accompanying the family's carriage, standing on the rear footboard, opening carriage doors, letting down the carriage steps, and escorting ladies with umbrellas or lanterns at night.
  • Aesthetic Standards and "Powdered Footmen": Footmen were deliberately selected for their physical appearance—specifically tall stature, symmetry of legs, handsome features, and upright posture. They wore powdered hair, tailored livery coats with brass or silver crest buttons, plush knee breeches, white silk stockings, and buckled shoes. In Great Britain, a special tax on male servants (*Servants' Tax*, 1777–1852) and a hair powder tax made maintaining a retinue of tall, liveried footmen one of the most expensive and conspicuous displays of aristocratic luxury.

2. Historical and Military Polysemy:

  • Running Footman (*curseur* / running servant): In the 16th, 17th, and early 18th centuries, before road paving and modern carriages, running footmen were athletic servants who literally ran on foot ahead of or beside their master's horse-drawn coach over rough country roads. Their duty was to clear obstacles, scout roads, pay toll-gates in advance, steady the coach around sharp turns, and prepare lodgings ahead of the party.
  • Military Infantry Soldier (Foot Soldier): In late-medieval and early modern warfare (14th–16th centuries), a footman was an armed soldier who fought on foot (an infantryman), as opposed to a mounted knight or horseman (*cavalier*).

3. Decorative Arts, Domestic Furniture, and Zoology:

  • Hearth Trivet / Antique Footman: In 18th- and 19th-century metalwork and decorative arts, a footman is a four-legged brass, iron, or steel stand or trivet placed before or over a fireplace hearth to keep kettles, dishes, or plates warm (humorously named after the servant who stood waiting by the fire).
  • Footman Moth (Entomology): Any of various slender, drab-colored moths of the subfamily Arctiinae (tribe Lithosiini, such as the Common Footman, Eilema lurideola), so named because their long, folded wings when at rest resemble the straight, narrow tails of a footman's livery coat.
