Slings are in the source sense primitive throwing weapons made of a pouch with two cords, whirled to launch stones or lead missiles, and in modern use any looped strap that supports, suspends, or carries weight, plus the act of throwing with such force. The plural covers both weapons in museums and supports in hospitals because the same loop schema underlies both.

The weapon consists of a cradle of leather, a release knot, and a retention loop. The slinger places a smooth stone, loads the pouch, whirls it in vertical or horizontal circles to build centrifugal force, and releases one cord at a precise moment, the pouch opening and the missile flying flat and fast. Effective range exceeds one hundred metres, impact can fracture helmets, and ammunition is free river pebbles. Ancient Balearic, Greek, and Roman armies fielded thousands of slingers, and the biblical David remains the archetype of the weak defeating the strong with this cheap arm.

  • Weapon sense: shepherd's sling, war sling, staff sling for heavier shot. Museum slings are displayed with piles of sling-stones and lead glands, exactly the relics in the source passage.
  • Medical support sense: arm sling, baby sling, orthopaedic support that cradles a limb or infant against the body. Soft fabric replaces leather, gentleness replaces violence, but the loop-around support remains.
  • Carrying and lifting sense: rifle sling over the shoulder, cargo sling under a helicopter load, climbing sling as sewn runner for anchors. Industrial and outdoor registers.
  • Furniture and clothing sense: sling chair with fabric slung between frames, slingback shoe held by an ankle strap, sling bikini held by cords. Design borrows the suspension image.
  • Verb and slang senses: to sling means to throw, hang, or serve hastily: sling mud, sling hash, sling a hammock. The plural noun rarely carries this sense but implies repeated throws.
  • Catapult and playground senses: slingshot as Y-forked variant, water-balloon slings, gym slingshot for bench press. Modern play keeps the hurl.

Grammatically slings is a regular count plural: one sling, two slings. Collocations disambiguate domain: sling stones, sling bullets for weapons; in a sling, wear a sling for medicine; cargo sling, climbing sling for industry. Verbs include whirl, release, hurl for weapons and support, carry, suspend for aids.

Domain usages span military history with treatises on Balearic training and Roman lead inscriptions; emergency medicine with protocols for elevation in slings; climbing with rated nylon runners; and colloquial slang where slinging denotes drug dealing or fast food service. Literary nuance contrasts heroic archaism with humble care: weapon slings evoke underdog triumph and primitive ingenuity in the source list, while medical slings evoke vulnerability and nursing. That polarity makes slings a word that can wound or cradle with the same loop.

Polysemy bridges through the strap: a strap that throws becomes any strap that holds, because both wind around and bear tension. The twist-to-throw etymology explains why support senses feel natural rather than homonymous, and why figurative to sling keeps violent motion even in kitchens and newsrooms.
