- [14th century: "to speak as a prophet"] - Prophecien enters Middle English from Old French; meaning is strictly religious — to speak by divine inspiration, to foretell as a prophet; used of biblical prophets and saints.
- [16th–17th centuries: the KJV register] - The Reformation and the King James Version fix the word in Scripture: "prophesying" means inspired preaching and prediction alike (1 Corinthians 14); it carries weighty, sacred authority.
- [18th–19th centuries: secularisation] - Anyone can now "prophesy" a political or scientific outcome; the word keeps a lofty, solemn flavour even in non-religious use.
- [20th century: the ironic turn] - Journalism and ordinary speech adopt "prophesying doom/disaster" as a stock mock-solemn phrase; the sacred register fades into irony or respectful archaism.
- [Contemporary: two lives] - In religious communities it retains full sacred meaning; elsewhere "prophesying" usually signals confident, often unwelcome prediction — weather, elections, crashes — with a faint archaic or dramatic colour.
