blister — noun and verb; (1) a small bubble on the skin filled with serum (clear fluid), caused by friction, burning, rubbing, or irritation; (2) a raised bubble or swelling on any surface — paint, metal, glass, or ceramic; (3) in packaging, a transparent plastic shell ("blister pack") sealed to a backing card; (4) figuratively, a source of irritation. In the source context, Trumpkin's boast "a jibe won't raise a _____" means a verbal taunt is too insignificant to wound him — words, unlike physical friction, cannot raise a blister on his thick hide.

Primary sense — the skin lesion. A blister is the skin's protective response to damage: friction, heat, cold, or chemical irritation separates the epidermal layers, and the body fills the gap with serous fluid, cushioning the injured tissue beneath. Blisters form on hands and feet from repeated rubbing (new shoes, oars, spades), from burns and scalds, from frostbite, and from allergic or infectious reactions.

  • Mechanism: rubbing or burning damages the epidermis; fluid (serum) pools between the skin layers; the raised pocket cushions and protects the raw tissue while it heals; the fluid is eventually reabsorbed or the blister bursts.
  • Medical terms: a small blister is a vesicle; a large one a bulla; an infected, pus-filled one a pustule. "Blistering" in medicine can also mean the deliberate raising of blisters (as in old counter-irritant therapy).
  • Verb senses: intransitive "my skin blisters in the sun" (to form a blister); transitive "the rope blistered his hand" (to cause a blister); extended "the heat blistered the paintwork" (to raise bubbles in a surface).
  • In context: the proverb-like sentence "a jibe won't raise a blister" uses the blister as the benchmark of trivial damage: a jibe (cruel taunt) cannot even raise a blister — it cannot wound at all. The image suits Trumpkin's gruff, leather-skinned dwarf persona.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The word spread early from skin to materials and then to modern industry:

  • Materials sense: a blister is a raised bubble in paint, varnish, glass, metal, or ceramic glaze, caused by trapped air, gas, or heat — the same bubble image transferred from skin to surfaces.
  • Packaging sense: a blister pack is a pre-formed transparent plastic bubble (blister) sealed to a card or foil backing, used to display and secure small products — the dominant 20th-century sense in retail.
  • Metaphoric sense: a blistering pace, blistering criticism, or blistering heat — intensification via the blister's association with burning; "blistering" as a fiery, scathing adjective is a standard modern extension.
  • Technical compounds: blister copper (rough cast copper with a blistered surface), blister steel (blistered bar steel), blister beetle (insect that raises blisters), blister rust (plant disease).
  • Slang: "blister" as a mild term for an annoying person ("you little blister") — an extension of the irritation sense, chiefly British.

Historical bridge & semantic reasoning. The word comes from Old French blestre (a swelling, pustule), from Germanic roots meaning "to swell or blow up" — the same family as blow, blast, and bladder. The semantic core is the image of something inflated or swollen — a bubble. From the skin bubble, the word naturally extended to any surface bubble (paint, metal, plastic) and to anything that stings like a burn (blistering heat, blistering criticism). The proverb's logic — words raise no physical bubble — is as old as the word itself.
