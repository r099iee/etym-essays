- Late 14th to 15th Century - A precise diagnostic and characterological term in Galenic medicine identifying an individual dominated by yellow bile (hot and dry), predisposing them to ambition, restlessness, and sudden violent fury.
- 16th to 17th Century - A pervasive archetype in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (exemplified by Shakespeare's Hotspur and Tybalt), capturing the martial, honor-obsessed nobleman whose wrath is ignited by the slightest perceived insult.
- 18th to Early 19th Century - Transitioned from literal humoral physiology to moral, political, and domestic portraiture, describing blustering patriarchs and tyrannical commanders whose bilious outbursts disturbed civil decorum.
- Late 19th to 20th Century - Preserved as an elevated, erudite literary descriptor, often carrying slight historical irony or period atmosphere when describing imperious authority figures.
- 21st Century - A specialized, highly expressive literary adjective used in scholarly, historical, and sophisticated analytical prose to designate an inherently combustive, hair-trigger temperament.
