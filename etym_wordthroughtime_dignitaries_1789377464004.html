- 1st Century BCE to 4th Century CE (Classical & Imperial Latin) - Latin *dignitās* designated personal worthiness, moral esteem, and high public office; during the Christian empire, *dignitārius* arose in canon law for high church officeholders.
- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French *dignitaire*, originally applied in ecclesiastical and legal records to high-ranking clergy (bishops, deans, archdeacons) holding benefices with jurisdiction.
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor Era) - Expands from church hierarchy into the royal household and state governance, designating high officers of state, royal chancellors, and court officials.
- 17th to 18th Century (Stuart & Georgian Eras) - Enters diplomatic protocol to describe visiting ambassadors, state envoys, and high judicial figures; recognized as the formal collective body attending state ceremonies.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Reaches widespread literary and civic prominence; frequently satirized in Victorian fiction and fairy tales (as in Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*) to mock the hollow pomp and vanity of self-important courtiers.
- 20th to 21st Century - Universal in international diplomatic relations, journalism, and state ceremonial (e.g., 'foreign dignitaries attending the summit'), while occasionally used with light irony for pompous local officials.
