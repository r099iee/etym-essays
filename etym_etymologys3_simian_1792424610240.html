The etymological and historical evolution of simian presents a remarkable linguistic journey, tracing how an Ancient Greek physical adjective describing a snub or concave facial contour became the definitive taxonomic moniker for the monkey and ape lineages across world science. The word's history encapsulates classical Roman animal observation, medieval Christian moral allegories, nineteenth-century Darwinian evolutionary battles, and modern biomedical nomenclature.

Ancient Greek Origins and Physical Semantic Grounding:

  • The Greek Adjective *sīmós* (σῑμός): At its earliest attested linguistic stratum, simian originates not in an animal name, but in the Ancient Greek adjective sīmós (σῑμός), signifying "snub-nosed," "flat-faced," "bent upward," "concave," or "sloping upward." In Greek prose and medical texts (such as the Hippocratic corpus and Xenophon), sīmós described horses with dished facial profiles, people with upturned noses, hollow terrain, or concave landscapes. Historical linguists (including Robert Beekes and Edzard Furnée) consider sīmós to be of Pre-Greek substrate origin, as anatomical anomalies, physical deformities, and facial profiles in Greek frequently lack Indo-European cognates and preserve the phonological hallmarks of the indigenous Aegean language that preceded the arrival of Proto-Greek speakers.
  • Application to Primates in Classical Greece: To the ancient Greeks, non-human primates—primarily North African Barbary macaques (Macaca sylvanus) imported via Mediterranean trade—were striking precisely because their facial anatomy diverged from the idealized straight Greek nasal profile. Because monkeys possess a flattened, nasal aperture lacking a prominent cartilage bridge, Greek speakers referred to the monkey substantively as he sīmī́ (ἡ σῑμή) or ho sīmías (ὁ σῑμίας), literally "the snub-nosed one."

Transmission into Latin and Roman Literature:

  • Adoption as *sīmia* and *sīmius*: The Romans borrowed the Greek term directly during early cultural contact with Magna Graecia, adapting it into Latin as the feminine noun sīmia (and masculine sīmius), meaning "an ape" or "a monkey." The underlying adjective sīmus ("flat-nosed, snub-nosed") was also retained in Latin poetry (as in Virgil's descriptions of rustic satyrs and goats).
  • The Roman Perception of Grotesque Mimicry: In Roman cultural life, the ape was legendary for its mocking, slavish imitation of human actions. The early Roman poet Quintus Ennius (239–169 BCE) penned the famed hexameter preserved by Cicero in De Natura Deorum (1.35): "Simia quam similis turpissima bestia nobis!" ("How exceedingly similar to us is the monkey, that foulest of beasts!"). This iconic line cemented the dual Roman perception of the simian: unsettlingly human-like in posture and gesture, yet viewed as a grotesque caricature of humanity. Consequently, Latin sīmia developed a secondary figurative meaning: "a slavish imitator, a plagiarist, an unoriginal pretender."

Medieval Moral Symbolism to Renaissance Science:

  • The Medieval Ape of God (*Simia Dei*): Throughout medieval Christian bestiaries and theological treatises, the ape maintained its Latin reputation for deceitful mimicry. The devil was routinely styled Simia Dei ("the ape of God"), an entity incapable of authentic divine creation who could only mimic and distort God's holy works into sin. The animal symbolized lust, pride, and fallen spiritual blindness.
  • Neo-Latin Adjectival Derivation (*simiānus*): During the Renaissance revival of classical natural history, Renaissance humanists and early taxonomists created the Neo-Latin relational adjective simiānus by appending the suffix -ānus ("pertaining to") to the stem simia.

English Adoption and the Evolutionary Revolution:

  • Early Modern English Entry: The word entered the English language around the turn of the seventeenth century as the learned adjective simian, initially appearing in natural history compendia and anatomical treatises to denote traits belonging to apes and monkeys.
  • Nineteenth-Century Primatological Taxonomic Codification: The decisive transformation of simian occurred during the nineteenth century. In zoological taxonomy, the French naturalist Henri Marie Ducrotay de Blainville and later British anatomists formalized the suborder Simia and infraorder Simiiformes to classify the higher primates.
  • The Darwinian Upheaval: With the publication of Charles Darwin's The Descent of Man (1871), simian was catapulted into the center of global intellectual and theological conflict. Darwin demonstrated that humanity's genealogical lineage did not descend from any living monkey, but from an ancient, extinct simian ancestor. The noun simian became the standard scientific term used by evolutionary biologists to discuss the common ancestor shared by modern humans and the great apes.
  • Twentieth-Century Biomedical Codification: In modern clinical and epidemiological science, simian was enshrined in virology and genetics with the identification of SV40 and SIV, bridging classical zoology with cutting-edge medical genetics.
