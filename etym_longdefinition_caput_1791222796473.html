caput is a Classical Latin neuter noun (genitive capitis) meaning head — and by systematic extension top, chief, person, life, source, mouth, capital, heading, and unit of counting.

It is one of the most prolific roots in English: capital, captain, chief, chef, head (via Germanic cognate), cattle, chattel, capitalise, decapitate, precipitate, occiput, sinciput, cap, cape, chapter, capital punishment and dozens more derive from it directly or via French. To know caput is to hold the head-word from which Western vocabularies of leadership, topography, law and economics grow.

In the source sentence caput appears as ultimate etymon: capitale (property) derives from caput (head), i.e. of cattle — wealth counted by heads.

  • Core anatomical sense — head of man or beast: Caput is the skull with face, from crown to chin: caput aperire (to bare the head), caput velare (to veil it), a capite ad calcem (from head to heel). Physicians (Celsus, Galen in Latin) describe ossa capitis, dolores capitis. Diminutives capitulum, capellus, cappa give chapter, chapel, cap, cape — little heads covering heads.
  • Life, person, civic existence: Because beheading kills and the head counts as the person, caput means life and legal personality: capitis damnare (to condemn to death/loss of citizenship), capitis deminutio (loss of civil status), caput liberum (free person). Census counts capita (heads/souls): per capita (per head) survives in English statistics. The bridge is pars pro toto: head stands for whole living bearer.
  • Chief, top, source, origin: The head is highest and controlling, so caput means leader, source, origin, mouth: caput rei (chief point), caput familiae (head of household), caput mundi (Rome, head of the world), caput fluminis (source of a river), caput cenae (main course). Adjective capitalis and noun capitale systematise this: chief city, principal sum, capital offence. English chief, captain, capital, chapter (heading), cape (headland), precipitous all continue this top/chief metaphor.
  • Economic unit — head of cattle: Livestock, slaves and taxpayers were counted in capita. Hence capitale (stock counted by heads), capitatio (poll tax), cattle/chattel/capital in English. The semantic step is metonymic counting practice: the herdsman or tax-collector counts heads, not bodies, because heads are visible above a crowd or herd and horns identify beasts. Modern head of cattle, headcount, per capita, livestock capital preserve the practice verbatim.
  • Topographical and textual senses: Caput gives headland, cape (head of land into sea), head of a river, header, heading, headline, chapter-heading (via capitulum — little head marking a section). Architecture's capital (head of a column) continues the body metaphor in stone. Anatomy keeps caput femoris, caput mandibulae, occiput (back of head), sinciput (forehead-half).
  • Idiomatic and proverbial range: Caput generates Latin idioms alive in English calque: perdere caput (to lose one's head), caput inter nubila (head in the clouds), caput rerum (head of affairs), de capite (from the top/systematically). Legal English retains capital (deadly), decapitate, decapitation (remove head), precipitate (hurl head-first), recapitulate (re-head, summarise by headings).

Polysemy is therefore not random: every sense is head-as-top, head-as-person, or head-as-counted-unit. Whether a general loses his caput to the executioner, a city becomes a capital, a publisher uses capitals, or a farmer counts capitale in heads of cattle, the same skull presides — commanding, countable, severable.
