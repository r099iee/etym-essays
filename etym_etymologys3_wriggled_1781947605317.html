Wriggled is the past tense of wriggle, a word whose recorded history in English is comparatively short but whose inner structure — the frequentative -le — is one of the most characteristic and ancient patterns of the Germanic verbal system. The verb enters English in the late 15th century, around 1490, and it is not a native Old English inheritance but a loan from the continental West Germanic languages, specifically from Middle Low German or Middle Dutch wriggelen, itself a frequentative built on the base verb wriggen "to move to and fro, twist." The frequentative suffix, visible in English in pairs like drip–dribble, crack–crackle, spark–sparkle, nibble, and wriggle itself, marks action done repeatedly, in small broken increments, or with a playful or continuous quality. This grammatical machinery is the whole point of the word: to wriggle is not to move once, but to move again and again, in quick, small, twisting strokes.

Once in English, the verb developed in two directions. The first was physical and whole-bodied: wriggle came to mean the twisting, squirming motion of worms, eels, snakes, insects, fish, and — above all — children and small animals trying to free themselves. The -le frequentative made it the natural word for the worm, whose entire locomotion is a series of repeated small twists, and for the child who cannot keep still. The second direction was figurative: by the 18th and 19th centuries the verb fixed its signature collocation wriggle out of, meaning to extricate oneself from a garment, a task, a promise, or a commitment by squirming — verbally, morally, or physically. A debtor "wriggles out of" payment; a witness "wriggles out of" answering; a rabbit "wriggles out of" his jacket. The physical image is never lost: every such escape is imagined as a small, quick, twisting withdrawal.

  • Morphology: wriggle = wrig(g)- (the Germanic twist-base) + -le (frequentative suffix); wriggled adds the regular past-tense -ed. The doubled g in wriggled reflects the English rule that short-vowel stems double a final single consonant before the suffix.
  • Loan path: Middle Dutch / Low German wriggelen (14th–15th c.) → English wriggle (c. 1490) → English wriggled (regular inflected past).
  • Possible deeper root: some scholars link the Germanic wrig- base to a PIE root *wreik- / *wer- meaning "to turn, twist, bend," which would connect it to a large family of turn-words; but the link is debated, the intermediate Germanic stage (*wrigōną) is reconstructed rather than attested, and the safest historical statement is that the word's documented ancestry begins with the attested Low German and Dutch frequentatives of the 14th century.
  • Semantic drift 1: repeated twisting motion (15th–16th c.) → specifically the squirming of worms and small animals (16th–17th c.) → escape by squirming (wriggle out, 18th–19th c.).
  • Semantic drift 2: physical escape (wriggle out of a garment) → figurative evasion (wriggle out of an obligation, wriggle-room), both recorded by the 19th century.
  • Socio-cultural context: the word has always belonged to plain, homely, spoken English and to children's literature; it is the verb of the nursery, the fishing-bait tin, and the worm in the garden, and its comic, elastic quality made it natural for Victorian and Edwardian writers of tales for children — including Beatrix Potter, who needed a word that could let a rabbit slip out of his jacket in a split second.
  • Literary attestations: wriggle appears in 16th-century natural-history and didactic prose; its figurative "escape" sense is well attested in 18th- and 19th-century fiction and journalism; and in Potter's tale the preterite wriggled is the pivot of the whole escape scene, a word so quick and squirming that the reader feels the narrow margin.
  • Offshoots: wriggly (adjective, "given to wriggling"), wriggler (a person or creature that wriggles), wriggle-room (chiefly British, "room for manoeuvre"), and in embroidery the interlacing wriggle-work.

In the sentence from The Tale of Peter Rabbit, the etymology and the grammar cooperate perfectly. The frequentative -le supplies the repeated twisting; the loan from the Dutch and Low German wriggelen supplies the homely, practical, non-heroic register; and the figurative capacity to "wriggle out of" something supplies the escape. Peter does not fight, break, or burst — he wriggles, and the word is exactly as quick, elastic, and determined as the rabbit himself.
