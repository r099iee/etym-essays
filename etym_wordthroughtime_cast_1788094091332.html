- (c. 1200, Middle English casten, borrowed from Old Norse kasta “to throw”) - A vigorous, physical verb that begins replacing native weorpan: cast stones, spears, nets, dice; tone energetic and concrete — the Viking word for a decisive fling.
- (13th–14th c.) - The noun “cast” = a throw; figurative throws spread into games, commerce, and law: cast of dice, cast lots, cast the eye, “cast accounts” (to reckon, by throwing counters) — the word multiplies around a single gesture.
- (14th–16th c.) - Industrial sense: “to cast metal” = to shape by pouring molten metal into a mould; “cast” as the moulded object; “cast-iron” (17th c.) — tone shifts from sport to workshop.
- (16th–17th c.) - The theatre: “to cast a play” = to allot parts (as lots are cast); “the cast” = the company of actors; “cast of characters” follows in the 19th century — the throw becomes the lottery of the stage.
- (18th–19th c.) - Medicine adds the plaster cast for broken bones (from the mould); fishing casts a line; “a cast in the eye” (a throw of the gaze) and “a cast of colour” settle in; worm casts and hawk casts keep the excrement-sense alive — the word becomes quietly ubiquitous.
- (20th c.–today) - Computing adopts “cast” for converting a value from one data type to another — a throw across type boundaries; cinema makes “the cast” a daily word; “castaway” and “outcast” keep the exile-throw — one Norse verb now runs from the elves’ barrels to every compiler in the world.
