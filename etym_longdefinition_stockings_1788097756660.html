A stocking is a close-fitting knitted covering for the foot and leg, reaching from the toes up the calf to the knee, or in older fashion to the thigh, worn inside shoes or boots and held up by garters, suspenders, or an elasticated top. The plural stockings is the ordinary word for the pair of garments. The context — “She has no shoes or stockings, and her little head is bare” — shows the word in its plainest clothing sense, paired with shoes as the two items of footwear that poverty takes away first.

Etymologically the word is a derivative of stock, formed with the suffix -ing. The traditional explanation is that the leg was regarded as the “stock” (trunk or stem) of the body, so that the cloth covering the leg was the “stocking”; an alternative older theory derives it from the “stock”, the bolt of cloth from which hose were cut. Either way the word appears in English records from the fifteenth century and gradually displaced the older word “hose” as the name of the leg-garment.

  • Everyday legwear sense (the core sense): a knitted or woven covering for the leg and foot worn by men and women alike until the twentieth century; in the plural, a pair of these garments, as in the note’s context.
  • Women’s hosiery sense: from the twentieth century the word narrowed socially: stockings became specifically the sheer silk or nylon legwear of women, reaching the thigh and held up by suspenders, in clear contrast to men’s short socks.
  • Christmas stocking: an oversized stocking-shaped cloth hung by the fireplace on Christmas Eve to be filled with small gifts — a custom traced to the legend of St Nicholas, who reportedly dropped gold through a chimney into the stockings of a poor man’s daughters hung up to dry.
  • “Stocking feet” / “in one’s stockings”: wearing stockings but no shoes — “he stood in his stocking feet”, a state of undress, stealth, or domestic ease.
  • Knitting-trade terms: stocking stitch (also stockinette) is the plain knit fabric so named because stockings were made in it; stockinette is the machine-knit textile of the same construction used for bandages and underwear.
  • Historical clothing sense: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries “stocking” meant the lower, leg-covering part of a pair of hose (the “nether stocks”), while “upper stocks” or “trunk hose” covered the trunk; when the two-part hose was abandoned, the word stockings took over the whole garment.
  • Compounds and idioms: stocking-cap (a close knitted winter cap, chiefly American), stocking-stuffers (small gifts small enough to fill a Christmas stocking), stocking frame (the knitting machine), blue-stocking (a learned woman, from the blue worsted stockings reputedly worn in an 18th-century London literary circle).

Semantic bridge. Every modern sense flows from the same core: a knitted tube that clothes the leg. Because the leg was seen as the body’s “stock” — its trunk-like support — the cloth tube was the “thing of the stock”, the stocking. When hosiery manufacture became strongly gendered in the twentieth century, the word narrowed in ordinary speech to the feminine garment, while the Christmas and knitting-trade senses preserved the older, wider garment meaning. The plural STOCKINGS now carries the whole of that history at once: a pair of plain knitted garments, the glamorous “nylons” of mid-century, and the nostalgic cloth hung by the fire at Christmas.
