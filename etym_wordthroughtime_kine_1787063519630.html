- Old English (c. 8th-11th c.) - Attested as cȳ (the regular front-mutated i-umlaut plural of cū "cow"), serving as the universal everyday plural for dairy cows in Anglo-Saxon England.
- Middle English (c. 12th-14th c.) - Southern and Midland speakers reinforced the mutated plural with the weak noun suffix -en, creating cy-en, kyn, and kine as the standard collective plural.
- Early Modern English (c. 16th-17th c.) - Immortalized in the 1611 King James Bible (Genesis 41) and Shakespearean verse, even as the analogical regular plural cows began dominating everyday colloquial speech.
- 18th-19th c. - Transitioned fully into a deliberate pastoral and bucolic archaism in Romantic and Victorian poetry, evoking pristine rural landscapes, pre-industrial innocence, and biblical gravity.
- 20th-21st c. - Preserved exclusively in historical literature, fantasy worldbuilding (such as Tolkien's legendarium), liturgical readings, and agricultural folk music.
