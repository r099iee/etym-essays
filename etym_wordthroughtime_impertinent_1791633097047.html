- Late 14th–15th Century: Pure Logical Non-Pertinence - Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman and Late Latin as a technical term of scholastic logic and legal pleading, signifying strictly 'not pertaining, unconnected, irrelevant, or having no logical bearing on the proposition.'
- 16th–17th Century: Semantic Slippage to Conversational Transgression - Began to describe social interactions: someone who introduced irrelevant, unasked-for, or inappropriate topics into a serious conversation was judged as meddlesome, impolite, and ill-mannered, establishing the bridge to social rudeness.
- 18th–19th Century: Solidification as Social Insolence - Became a quintessential term of moral and domestic etiquette in Georgian and Victorian literature (e.g., Jane Austen, Charles Dickens), designating ill-bred, disrespectful, or nosy behavior, particularly from subordinates, young people, or domestic staff.
- 20th–21st Century: Legal Archaism & Refined Literary Reproof - Preserved its ancient sense of pure irrelevance exclusively in legal jurisprudence (such as Rule 12(f) motions to strike 'redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous' matter), while in contemporary literary English it remains an elegant, dignified rebuke for presumptuous intrusion.
