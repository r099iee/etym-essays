[Root 1: CLERGY (Ancient Greek klēros "lot, divine inheritance")]
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*kelh₂- / *klā-
"to break, beat, strike off"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th c. BCE)
klêros (κλῆρος)
"shard of wood/pottery used in casting lots; allotment of land, divine portion"
Note: Originally a broken twig or potsherd used for casting lots to divide land or choose leaders; adopted in biblical Greek for God's chosen inheritance (Deuteronomy 18:2; Acts 1:26).
↓
Hellenistic / Ecclesiastical Greek (c. 1st–3rd c. CE)
klērikós (κληρικός)
"belonging to the sacred allotment/heritage of God; ordained churchman"
↓
Late Latin / Ecclesiastical Latin (c. 3rd–5th c. CE)
clericus / clericātus
"clerk, priest, minister, person in holy orders"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
clergie
"body of clerics; clerical order; learning, scholarship"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th–14th c.)
clergie / clergé
"ordained churchmen; priestly order, learning"

[Root 2: MAN (Proto-Germanic *mann- "human being, adult male")]
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*mon- / *men-
"to think, remember, have mental power"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 1000–500 BCE)
*mann- / *manwaz
"human being, person, mortal, man"
↓
Old English (c. 5th–11th c.)
mann
"human being, person; adult male"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.)
man
"human being, adult male person"

[Compound Synthesis]
Early Modern English (c. 1570s)
clergy + man → clergyman
"an ordained Christian minister or priest"
Note: Coined during the English Reformation to designate ordained ministers of the Church of England, distinguishing them from Roman Catholic monks and friars.
↓
Modern English (present)
CLERGYMAN
