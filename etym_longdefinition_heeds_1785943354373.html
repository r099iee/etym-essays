HEEDS: The third-person singular present of the verb heed; pays careful attention to, takes notice of, listens to, and acts upon advice, warnings, or instructions. PRIMARY SENSE: To heed is more than to hear and more than to listen: it is to take something seriously enough to let it shape behaviour. A person who heeds a doctor's warning changes course; a captain who heeds a storm alert alters the ship's route; a government that heeds public opinion adjusts policy. The word therefore joins perception to obedience — the attentive ear is inseparable from the responsive action. In ordinary modern English it is a formal, slightly literary verb, at home in warnings, proverbs, journalism, and religious or ceremonial prose, and less common in everyday speech, where "listen to" or "pay attention to" have largely displaced it. SENSES AND CONTEXTS:
  • Advice and counsel: "heeds his doctor's advice," "she rarely heeds criticism" — taking counsel to heart.
  • Warnings and danger: "heeds the warning signs," "heeded the storm warning" — conforming behaviour to an announced hazard.
  • Instructions and law: "heeds the rules," "heeded the injunction" — observing a command or requirement.
  • The noun usage: "give heed," "take heed," "pay heed," and their negatives ("pay no heed," "unheeded") — heed as attention itself, in both concrete and abstract senses.
  • Legal and safety register: disclaimers and protocols that say hazards must be heeded; statutory language in which failing to heed a notice creates liability.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE: The verb descends from Proto-Germanic *hōdijaną, "to guard, watch over, protect." In Old English hēdan meant to keep, take care of, guard, or observe — an act of protective attention. The historical shift is from guarding a thing physically (watching over sheep, guarding a place) to guarding oneself mentally (watching one's conduct against warning), and from there to the purely cognitive act of giving attention to advice. The guard has become the listener: what was once the shepherd's vigilance is now the hearer's alertness. The word's formal register preserves this older gravity — to heed is to keep watch with the mind. REGISTER, COLLOCATIONS, AND THE NOUN: Heed is the language of warning and counsel, of the proverb and the statute, and it is markedly more formal than listen or pay attention. It collocates with the objects it governs — heed a warning, heed advice, heed counsel, heed the signs, heed the call — and, in its noun form, builds the idioms give heed, pay heed, take heed, and lend heed, together with the negatives pay no heed, take no heed, and the adjective heedless ("heedless of the danger"). The pair heedful and heedless keeps the word's ancient guardianship imagery alive: to be heedful is to be watchful, to be heedless is to be unguarded and exposed, and in biblical and Shakespearean English "take heed" is a standing command of vigilance. In law and safety English the verb survives in formal notices — "failure to heed this warning" — and in journalism it is the stock verb for officials responding to reports and recommendations ("the minister heeded the committee's call"). Its register explains why the word has not been displaced: in the sentence "Aegon still heeds me," the verb does the work of both attention and obedience — the speaker's words are taken seriously enough to govern conduct, and the ancient guardian's alertness is still audible in the modern verb.
