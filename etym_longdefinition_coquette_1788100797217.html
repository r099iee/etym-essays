A coquette is a woman who flirts: one who habitually seeks to attract and hold the admiration of men through playful charm, teasing attentions and artful refusals, without necessarily intending, or sincerely feeling, romantic attachment. The word entered English in the early seventeenth century (c. 1610s) directly from French, where coquette is the feminine form of coquet, a diminutive of coq, “cock, rooster”: the coquette is, literally, “a little (female) rooster”, one who struts and preens and displays her plumage for an audience. The underlying image is agricultural and barnyard-honest — courtship as display — and it explains most of the word's tone: the coquette is understood as a performer, never as a dupe.

Unlike the plain word flirt, which describes a behaviour, coquette describes a social type with a technique and a repertoire — the withheld glance, the half-promise, the careful calibration of encouragement and denial. Eighteenth-century conduct literature treats coquetry as a moral category; the theatre turned the coquette into a stock character of Restoration and Georgian comedy (Millamant in Congreve's The Way of the World, 1700, is the supreme type — witty, self-possessed, playing at love while guarding her freedom), and fiction produced outright warnings such as Hannah Webster Foster's epistolary novel The Coquette (1797), whose heroine's flirtation leads to social ruin and death. By the nineteenth century the word had also flown far from the drawing-room into the ornithology of the New World: “coquette” became the standard English name of the hummingbirds of the genus Lophornis, whose males flash iridescent crests and ruffs in extraordinary display flights — courtship again, this time performed by birds with jewel-coloured plumage.

  • Sense 1 — the flirtatious woman (primary): a woman who cultivates admirers by coquetry, that is, by playful, non-committal amorous behaviour. In older usage the word is mildly derogatory (it implies insincerity and vanity); today it is partly literary and often affectionate or ironic.
  • Sense 2 — the stock character of comedy and fiction: the coquette of Restoration and eighteenth-century drama embodies the social performance of femininity; her refusal to choose among suitors drives both plot and satire. The semantic bridge is the shared idea of deliberate theatricality: flirtation as a staged art.
  • Sense 3 — hummingbirds of genus Lophornis (ornithological jargon): the “coquettes”, tiny New-World hummingbirds whose males perform flamboyant courtship displays with fan-like crests and ruff collars. The name transfers the human sense directly through the notion of showy self-display in courtship.
  • Sense 4 — the word family: the adjective coquettish (“playfully flirtatious”, early 18th c.), the abstract noun coquetry (“the art of flirting”, 1670s, from French coquetterie), and the verb to coquet(te), “to act the coquette; to dally”, recorded from the early eighteenth century, complete the set; the French phrase grande coquette denotes the consummate practitioner of the art.
  • Sense 5 — modern figurative and cultural uses: in analytical prose, coquetry is used metaphorically for any teasing alternation of invitation and withdrawal — of an argument, a price, a policy (“the coquetry of the pre-election budget”). In the 2020s the noun “coquette” was revived as the name of a social-media and fashion aesthetic (bows, lace, ribbons, studied demureness), a playful reclamation of the word's flirtatious femininity for self-presentation.

Pronunciation and morphology: English /kɒˈkɛt/, with second-syllable stress and a final /t/; the French source is pronounced [kɔkɛt] with a silent final e. The suffix -ette, a feminine diminutive, is shared with brunette, statuette, soubrette; the spelling shows the double t and terminal e of the French original.

Register: coquette is not slang — it is a cultivated loanword with three centuries of literary pedigree — but it has receded in everyday speech, where flirt, tease or player now do most of the work. When coquette is used today it carries deliberate period flavour or stylistic irony, and that is precisely its value in fiction and essays: the barnyard etymology is invisible to most speakers, yet the strutting little rooster still animates the word from inside.
