Rotund is a Latin word that English borrowed whole, and its history is the history of a single root of motion — *ret-, "to run, to roll, to turn" — being domesticated into a polite word for a fat man. Everything about the word, from its shape to its tone, is explained by the wheel. The chain begins in Proto-Indo-European with the root *ret-, "to run, roll, turn," a root of dynamic motion. Its most spectacular descendant is the chariot: Sanskrit ratha, "chariot," literally "the runner" — the swift wheeled vehicle that transformed warfare and language in the Indo-Iranian world. The Germanic languages used the same root for the wheel itself: Old English rāð meant "wheel," and the root also gives ride (to move along). But the decisive branch for our word is the Latin one. Latin built from *ret- the noun rota, "wheel," attested from the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE. From rota, Latin formed the adjective rotundus, "wheel-shaped, round," with the suffix -undus (as in facundus, "eloquent"). Plautus, the comic playwright of the 2nd century BCE, already uses rotundus of a round, well-fed figure — the very sense our word keeps. A rotundus in Plautus is a man so round he looks like a wheel. From this point the Latin word took two separate roads into English, and English keeps both as near-synonyms with different tones. The first road ran through French. Vulgar Latin *retundus became Old French reont, ront, which entered Middle English in the 13th–14th century as round — the ordinary, everyday word for any circular thing. The second road ran straight from the classical page: in the early 18th century, English writers, in the fashionable classical taste of the age, borrowed rotund directly from Latin rotundus as a learned word for roundness, especially of the body. Where round is flat and neutral, rotund arrived wearing its Latin dignity. The two words, siblings from the same Latin parent, now occupy different registers: a circle is round; a bishop is rotund. A third member of the family came by way of Italy. Italian rotonda, the feminine of rotondo ("round"), was taken into English in the 17th–18th century as rotunda, a round building or hall crowned with a dome — from the round temples of antiquity, most famously the Pantheon in Rome, to the Capitol rotunda. So the same Latin root gives English round (through French), rotund (directly from Latin), and rotunda (through Italian), each keeping a different slice of the wheel. The semantic development of rotund in English is a steady widening from geometry to the human body to the human voice:
  • 18th century: the learned adjective. Rotund enters meaning simply "round, circular, spherical," applied to shapes and objects, and from the beginning to plump persons — the round, full figure that the Latin comic poets had already celebrated.
  • 19th century: the polite synonym for fat. As Victorian and Edwardian letters cultivated a genial, euphemistic vocabulary, rotund became the standard literary word for a stout, well-fed, contented man — the landlord, the alderman, the bishop — carrying warmth and good humor rather than reproach.
  • 20th–21st century: voice and style. By extension from fullness of shape to fullness of sound, rotund came to describe a full, sonorous, rounded voice or a stately, flowing prose style — "rotund periods" of oratory. The related orotund (from Latin os, "mouth" + rotundus, "round-mouthed") deepened this sense into "grandiloquent, pompously round."
In the Gibbon passage, rotund does exactly the work the 19th century assigned it. The engraving of Gibbon's "rotund face" gives the great historian a human, lovable presence — the round-cheeked, well-fed scholar rather than the abstract author of the Decline and Fall. The word is affectionate and slightly amused, precisely because it pictures an even, full roundness without any of the clinical or insulting force of fat. It is the classical, dignified word that only a round and prosperous subject can wear. Through the wheel of *ret-, from the Sanskrit ratha to the Roman rota to the Rotunda and the rotund bishop, the word has always been about turning and rolling — and the fullest, most self-satisfied turning of all is the round belly it now names.
