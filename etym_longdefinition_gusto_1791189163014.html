Gusto is a noun meaning vigorous, hearty enjoyment or relish, especially as displayed in action. To do something with gusto is to do it eagerly, energetically, and with evident pleasure, throwing one's whole self into eating, singing, laughing, arguing, or applauding. The word belongs to a warm, approving register; it praises uninhibited vitality rather than cool discrimination. Where taste judges, gusto devours.

  • Core adverbial idiom: with gusto: Overwhelmingly used in the frame with gusto (eat with gusto, sing with gusto, join in with gusto). It functions as a manner adverbial signalling zestful, wholehearted participation. Bare uses (his gusto was infectious) are rarer and more literary.
  • Energetic relish vs. mere pleasure: Pleasure can be passive and private; gusto is active and visible. A person may take quiet pleasure in poetry but applaud with gusto, laugh with gusto, tuck in with gusto. It implies bodily expression: open mouth, loud voice, animated gesture.
  • Historical sense: sense of taste: In early seventeenth-century English gusto could mean literal taste or flavour, as in Italian. This sense is now obsolete except in the adjective gustatory and scientific gustation. Modern dictionaries mark it archaic.
  • Aesthetic sense: vigorous style: In art, music, and literary criticism gusto denotes forceful, passionate execution rather than delicate finish. Hazlitt's On Gusto (1817) defined it as power in conception revealing itself in the object, e.g. Rembrandt's gusto for craggy faces, Liston's gusto for comic appetite. This critical sense persists in reviews praising a performance's gusto.
  • Appetite and food collocations: To eat, drink, devour, tuck in, fall upon food with gusto signals healthy appetite and lack of squeamishness. Durant's vegetarians take to blood with gusto exploits this: even those presumed averse display carnivorous heartiness.
  • Enthusiasm, zeal, and relish distinguished: Enthusiasm is forward-looking excitement; zeal is principled fervour, often religious or ideological; relish is anticipatory savouring; gusto is present-tense, embodied delight in the act itself. One can have zeal without joy, but not gusto without joy.
  • Countability and modification: Usually uncountable (much gusto, great gusto) but occasionally pluralised in literary prose (the gustoes of youth). It grades with great, real, evident, surprising, infectious, hearty, boyish and negates as without gusto, with little gusto to damn with faint energy.
  • False friend with gust: Gust of wind comes from Old Norse gustr and is unrelated. Gusty meaning windy should not be confused with gusto meaning relish, though advertisers pun on both (gusty flavour).

In context, with gusto completes Durant's argument that blood-drinking is not endured from famine but savoured as delicacy. The vegetarians, who might be expected to recoil, embrace it with the same hearty appetite as everyone else, proving that horror at blood is a late, local European scruple, not a universal human instinct. To grasp gusto here is to hear unembarrassed, full-bodied appetite — the opposite of dutiful nibbling — and to feel Durant's mildly provocative pleasure in overturning his readers' assumptions about primitive disgust.
