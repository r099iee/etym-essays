slighted (verb, past tense and past participle of slight; also adjective) — to be slighted is to be treated as of little worth or account: insulted by indifference, neglect, or the withholding of the attention and respect one is due. The word is built on the adjective slight, "small, slender, insignificant", so that to slight someone is literally to make them small. 1. Insulted by neglect or disrespect (primary sense)
  • To slight a person is to treat or speak of them without proper respect or attention — to ignore them deliberately, cut them, or pass them over as beneath notice. "She felt slighted when she wasn't invited."
  • The insult lies in the act of omission rather than commission: the slight is delivered by what is withheld (attention, invitation, greeting) more than by what is said.
  • Stronger than ignored or disregarded (which can be neutral oversight) because a slight is perceived as deliberate and wounding to pride; quieter than insulted in its most direct sense, because a slight need not involve open verbal attack.
2. To treat as of little worth or account (the verb generally)
  • The verb to slight means to treat someone or something as small, insignificant, or unworthy of consideration: "He slighted her contribution".
  • Applied to things rather than persons, it means to disregard, undervalue, or pass over: "to slight one's duties" is to neglect them.
  • The noun a slight is an act of studied indifference or disdain — an insult delivered by neglect. "I take it as a slight that I was not consulted."
3. Historical military sense: to raze a fortress
  • In military engineering of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, to slight a fortress or town meant to demolish or level it — to make it flat, to reduce its defences so that it could no longer be held.
  • This sense preserves the oldest meaning of the adjective slight, "smooth, flat, level", inherited from Germanic: to slight a fort is to make it flat.
  • The pejorative human sense grows directly out of this physical one: to make a person flat is to reduce them, to render them small and level with the ground.
4. Related adjective senses of slight (context for the participle)
  • Slight as an adjective means small in degree, slender in build, or insubstantial: "a slight figure", "a slight improvement", "a slight headache".
  • These senses are the semantic bridge: what is slight is thin, weightless, beneath notice — and to slight a person is to treat them as thin, weightless, and beneath notice.
  • The past participle slighted, "treated as slight, rendered small", is the natural meeting point of the physical and the social senses.
5. The semantic bridge
  • The whole family descends from the Germanic adjective for "smooth, level, flat" — a word about surfaces and ground.
  • From "flat" came "plain, ordinary" and then "small, slender, of little account" — a slight figure is a slender one, a slight reason a weak one.
  • From "of little account" the social meaning was inevitable: to slight a person is to declare them of little account, to make them small. The word travelled from levelling forts to levelling pride.
Register and usage notes
  • Slighted is literary and reflective; it belongs to the language of wounded pride and social estimation. In Austen's world, where attention was the currency of worth, to be slighted was to be publicly diminished.
  • Common collocations: "feel slighted", "a slighted guest", "slighted pride". The noun slight ("a deliberate slight") and the verb to slight are the family's other living forms.
  • Do not confuse with the adjective slight ("small"), though the two are historically the same word: "a slight error" does not imply that anyone was slighted.
