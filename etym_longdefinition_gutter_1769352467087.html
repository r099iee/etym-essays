gutter — a shallow trough or channel for carrying off water, either along the eaves of a building (a roof gutter) or at the side of a street, between the sidewalk and the roadway (a street gutter). On this card the word is tested in the context of John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men: George reminds Lennie of "settin' in that gutter on Howard Street and watchin' that blackboard" — the gutter of the town street, the low channel where rainwater and debris collect. The word's primary sense is plain and architectural, but "gutter" has grown a large family of secondary meanings, from bowling to journalism to candle flames, all of which flow from the same root idea of a channel that collects what runs down or is swept along.

1. Primary sense — the roof gutter and the street gutter (architecture)

A trough or channel that catches and carries off rainwater: (a) along the lower edge of a roof or under the eaves, discharging water away from the walls; (b) at the side of a street, between the sidewalk and the roadway, carrying runoff to drains. This is the concrete, everyday sense — the gutter of the card's context, a physical place in a small California town.

2. Secondary sense — the gutter as a place of degradation (metaphor)

"The gutter" is the figurative bottom of society — the lowest, most squalid, most degraded social sphere. Because the street gutter collects filth, garbage, and debris, it became the image of moral and social filth: "gutter press" (sensationalist newspapers), "gutter language" (vulgar, foul speech), "gutter politics," "in the gutter" (morally or socially fallen). This is a powerful and still fully current metaphor: from the channel that collects dirt to the society that collects its refuse and outcasts.

3. Tertiary sense — the bowling gutter

In bowling, the gutters are the sunken channels along either side of the lane; a ball that rolls into them is a "gutter ball," scoring nothing. The word is a direct, playful transfer from the street channel: the lane's side channels literally carry the ball away like water in a drain. "To bowl a gutter ball" and the mockery of "in the gutter" (scoring zero) extend the metaphor into sport and failure.

4. Verb sense — to gutter (of a candle)

As a verb, "to gutter" describes a candle or flame that flickers, splutters, and burns unevenly, with the melted wax running down in channels — the flame "guttering" as if flowing in gutters of its own wax. This is a specialized but vivid transfer from the channel sense: the candle's dripping wax carves little gutters, and the flame gutters with them. "Guttering" is a favorite word of literary description for a dying or troubled flame.

5. Publishing sense — the gutter of a book

In printing and bookbinding, "the gutter" is the blank margin at the inner edge of a page where two facing pages meet — the channel of the spine where the book "folds." Book designers speak of "gutter margin" and text "falling into the gutter." Again, a physical channel; the word runs from streets and roofs into the very architecture of the printed page.

6. Historical semantic bridge

Latin gutta means "a drop (of liquid)." From it, Late Latin and medieval French formed words for a channel that catches falling drops — a rainwater spout or gutter. Old French gotiere/goutiere ("water spout, gutter") entered Middle English as gotere and became modern "gutter." The whole meaning family hangs from that single image: a channel that carries off drops of water. The street gutter collects what washes down; the metaphor collects what society washes away; the bowling gutter carries the ball off; the candle's gutter carries the wax. Every sense is a channel.

7. Register, nuance, and modern uses

  • Neutral architecture: the literal sense is plain and everyday — roof guttering, street gutter, drain gutter.
  • Pejorative metaphor: "the gutter," "gutter press," "gutter language," "gutter politics" carry strong class-loaded contempt.
  • Sport: "gutter ball" and "in the gutter" (scoring nothing) are standard bowling vocabulary.
  • Literature: "guttering flame," "guttering candle" are fixed poetic-phrase uses of the verb.
  • Publishing: "gutter margin" is technical book-design vocabulary.
  • Compounds: gutter press, gutter snipe (a street urchin), gutter ball, gutter punk, gutter politics, rooftop gutter, eaves gutter.

In summary, "gutter" on this card is the street channel where Lennie and George once sat — but the word's family runs from rooftops and bowling lanes to the lowest reaches of society and the flickering of a dying candle, all channels, all carrying what flows downward.
