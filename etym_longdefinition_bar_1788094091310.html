Core sense in context. A bar in the reading sentence is the stout rod of wood or iron with which a door is fastened — the door-bar that secures a gate against the outside; the dwarves search the face of the hidden door for "any sign of bar or bolt or key-hole". The root meaning of the word is a long, rigid piece of material — a rod, beam or rail — and from that single image a remarkable fan of senses has grown, in law, in taverns, in music, in geology and in physics.

Primary senses — the rod and the barrier.

  • The rod itself: a bar of iron or steel, a crowbar, a bar of soap, a bar of gold, a chocolate bar. Any long rigid piece, especially one with a use: a gate bar, a space bar, a parallel bar in gymnastics, the bars of a prison cell.
  • The barrier: anything that blocks or fences: a toll bar (the bar across a road where a toll is paid), a sandbar (the ridge of sediment that bars a river mouth or harbour), a barricade. The verb to bar means to fasten with a bar, to block — and hence to forbid: "barred from the club", "no bar to progress", "bar none" (with no exception), "barring accidents". This excluding force is the bridge to every abstract barrier in the language.

The legal bar. In Anglo-Norman courts the bar was the wooden railing that separated the judges and advocates from the public and the prisoners — the barrier of justice. From that physical rail, the Bar became the body of barristers and advocates admitted inside it: to be "called to the bar" is to be admitted to practise; a barrister is one who pleads at the bar; "bench and bar" names judges and advocates together; the bar exam, the bar association, the American Bar follow. The bridge is metonymy: the place where a profession works gives its name to the profession.

The tavern bar. In sixteenth- and seventeenth-century inns the counter over which drink was served was itself a bar — a rail or board dividing server from customer, exactly as the court's rail divided pleaders from public. By the nineteenth century the counter had given its name to the whole establishment: "the bar" is the pub, the room, the shop where drinks are poured — barman, bartender, bar stool, cocktail bar, wine bar. The bridge is again metonymy plus the persistent image of the dividing rail: the bar both serves drinks and marks the boundary between the licensed space and the street.

  • Music. A bar is the segment of written music between two bar-lines — the vertical lines that "bar off" equal lengths of time. The word first named the line (seventeenth century) and then the measure itself (eighteenth century): "a four-bar phrase", "a bar of rest".
  • Geology and navigation. A bar is a ridge of sand or gravel at a river mouth or across a harbour entrance — sandbar, harbour bar, "the bar of the Mississippi" — a barrier built by the water itself.
  • Heraldry and pattern. A bar is a horizontal band across a shield, and a "barred" pattern is striped with bars — the rod laid flat as ornament.
  • Physics and meteorology — a separate word. The bar is the unit of pressure equal to 100,000 pascals, roughly atmospheric pressure at sea level: millibars in weather forecasts, barograph, barometer. This word is not the rod at all — it was coined in the early twentieth century (meteorology, 1911, commonly attributed to Vilhelm Bjerknes) from Greek báros, "weight" — see etymology.
  • Computing and modern life. A bar chart (bar graph), a bar code (barcode, everyday from the 1970s), the space bar, the menu bar, the address bar, the progress bar — every new interface rediscovers the rod and the divider. "Bar" also names a shelf of a rack or grate, an ingot, a stripe of light or colour (a bar of sunlight), and a stretch of beach (a bar of sand).

The semantic bridge, condensed. One Romance word, Vulgar Latin *barra "bar, barrier", carried the rod-and-barrier image into English in the thirteenth century; every sense above except the pressure unit is a development of that single image — the rod that blocks, the rail that divides, the line that measures, the counter that separates, the ridge that bars the channel. The pressure unit is an independent Greek word (báros, "weight") that homonymised with the rod by design, not accident — it was deliberately coined. Modern English sorts "bar" by context: law, drink, music, sediment, physics — five universes behind one spelling.
