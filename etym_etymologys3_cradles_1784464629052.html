Cradles is the plural of the native English noun cradle, and its story is unusual among English words: the word's documented history is long and steady, but its deepest root is genuinely unknown, and a responsible etymology must say so. The essay that follows traces the word from its Old English attestations through Middle English into the modern age, examines the proposed (but unproven) deeper connections, and then follows the semantic career of the infant-bed word as it spread into furniture, tools, boats, and metaphor. 1. Old English: a word already complete The noun is attested from the late tenth century in the form cradol (with variant spellings cradel, cradul), and it already means exactly what it means today: a small bed for a baby, and by extension a frame that holds something. The very earliest uses, in glosses and in the writings of Ælfric, name the child's bed, and the word appears to have been a fully formed member of the vocabulary, not a recent borrowing. This matters: cradle is native English, part of the old Germanic inheritance of the island, and it shows no trace of the Latinate importation that shaped so much of English household vocabulary. 2. The mysterious deep root: what we do and do not know Standard etymological dictionaries mark the origin of cradle as uncertain, and the honest account must respect that. Three proposals recur. The most popular links the word to a Germanic family meaning 'woven basket' or 'crate' — Old High German kratto and Middle Dutch krade are the usual comparanda — on the historically plausible ground that early cradles were often basket-like or woven frames; the baby was literally cradled in a structure that resembled a big basket. A second proposal reaches to Celtic, comparing forms such as Welsh crud 'cradle.' A third, older line of thought connected the word to Latin cratis 'wickerwork,' the ancestor of English crate and grate, again on the basket analogy. None of these has won universal acceptance: the consonant structure of cradol does not line up cleanly with any one of the proposed sources, and the semantics (a rocking bed) are general enough to have been coined independently in more than one place. A careful chain therefore begins at Old English and treats all pre-English stages as speculative. 3. Middle English: the word settles down Middle English continues the noun as cradel (also cradel, cradele), with no radical change of sense. The infant-bed meaning dominates, and the word develops the collocations that will define it for centuries: a cradle rocks, a mother rocks a cradle, and the verb form begins to appear beside the noun. The Middle English period also produces the earliest figurative extensions, in which the cradle stands for infancy itself — a person 'from the cradle' is a person from birth. The framework sense continues as well, since the same word names any supporting frame shaped like a child's bed. 4. Early modern and modern English: the frame takes over From the sixteenth century onward the 'supporting frame' sense grows steadily, and it is here that the word's modern polysemy takes shape. In the trades and on the water, a cradle is a framework that holds a ship while it is built, repaired, or launched — a massive wooden cradle cradling a hull on the slipway. In the workshop and the home, cradles appear as stands that hold fragile or awkward objects: a rifle cradle, a telephone cradle, later a charging cradle for a phone or tablet. In construction and cleaning, a cradle is a suspended work platform hung from a building — the 'window-washer's cradle.' In each case the semantic logic is the same: a cradle is a holder that supports and protects something fragile, exactly as the rocking bed supports and protects the infant. 5. The figurative career: birthplace and nurture Alongside the concrete senses runs a powerful metaphor. A place where something begins or is nurtured becomes 'the cradle' of that thing: the 'cradle of civilization,' 'the cradle of democracy,' 'the cradle of the Renaissance.' The image is the infant bed as the source of life and growth — from the cradle to the grave stretches a whole human life between its two containers. The verb to cradle develops in parallel: to cradle something in one's hands is to hold it with the protective gentleness of a cradle, and the gesture has become a fixed modern image of care. Compounds multiply: cradle-song (a lullaby), cat's cradle (the string game, whose woven loops resemble a bed or frame), cradle-robber and cradle-snatcher (slang for someone who pursues a much younger partner), from the cradle (from infancy), cradle-to-grave (a whole-lifetime span used of welfare and care systems), and cradle cap (the infant scalp condition). Each compound preserves the deep structure: cradle = the place where something begins and is protected. 6. Tolkien's shields as cradles In the passage from The Hobbit, Gandalf's comic inventory 'shields as cradles or dish-covers' plays precisely on the concrete domestic sense. The plural stacks multiple repurposed shields across a peaceful land: war-gear demoted to baby-furniture and kitchen lids, swords blunt, axes used for trees, dragons comfortably far-off. The image is not nursery sentiment but anti-epic inventory — a proof that the neighbourhood has no spare heroes, which is why the wizard settled on burglary and on Bilbo. The plural form matters: it is not one cradle but many, one for every household that has converted a shield to a gentler use. 7. The plural and its form Cradles is the regular plural of cradle, and like all such plurals it is formed by simple addition of -s to the noun. The card form keeps the text's plural because the passage lists several shields used as several cradles. In sound the plural shows the voiced -lz ending typical of English, so the spoken form rhymes with 'tables' and 'battles,' while the singular keeps the unvoiced release of the final -le. The plural is thus as regular as the singular is old, and the whole word remains a compact example of a native English noun that never crossed the Channel to reach the island.
