Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*h₁lengʷʰ-
"light, agile, not heavy"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*legʷis
"light, having little weight"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE)
levis
"light, swift, nimble"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
levāre
"to lift up, raise, relieve"
↓
Vulgar Latin (c. 6th c. CE)
*levāta
"a rising, a lifting up"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c. CE)
levee
"raising, lifting, collection"
↓
Middle French (c. 15th–16th c. CE)
levée
"embankment; morning rising"
↓
Louisiana French (c. 1718–1727)
levée
"river dike, raised riverbank"
↓
Modern English (c. 1770)
Levee
"flood embankment; river wharf"

Note: English borrowed levee twice in completely different semantic registers from the identical French participle levée: first in the 17th century as a royal/political morning reception (from the king rising from bed), and second in the 18th century from Louisiana French civil engineering as a river embankment (from the raising of earth).
