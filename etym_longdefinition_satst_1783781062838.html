satst (v.) — an archaic second-person singular past-tense form of the verb sit ("thou satst"), the early modern equivalent of modern "you sat." The -st ending marks the verb for the pronoun thou (subject singular), so satst means "you sat," with the intimate, reverential, or archaic register that the thou form carries in Early Modern English. It is a strong-verb (class 5) past form and therefore shows the characteristic vowel alternation of the sit / sat / sat pattern.

Grammatical senses. Because satst is an inflected verb form rather than a lexically polysemous word, its "meanings" are the full range of senses of sit in the second person singular past, namely:

  • To be in a seated posture — "thou satst on the throne": the concrete, literal sense of resting on the buttocks with the body upright.
  • To be positioned, to rest, to be set — "thou satst beside the stream": a body, object, or place occupying a fixed position (compare "the house sits on a hill").
  • To brood, to be settled over something — the Miltonic sense: the Spirit "satst brooding on the vast Abyss," i.e. hovered, rested, and brooded over the waters like a bird over its eggs, combining posture with watchful incubation.
  • To sit in judgment or council, to preside — "thou satst in judgment": the institutional sense of occupying a seat of authority, a bench, a throne, or a seat in assembly.
  • To remain, to stay, to be in a condition — "thou satst long in idleness": the durative sense of continued presence or state.

Historical & stylistic senses. Beyond its verbal content, satst carries grammatical and stylistic meaning:

  • Person and number: it is unambiguously second-person singular; the -st suffix (with variant -est) is the marker of the thou-paradigm, inherited from Old English and still fully productive in Early Modern English.
  • Register: in the King James Bible (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton, the thou forms express intimacy, reverence, or solemn address; satst is therefore found in prayer, invocation, and elevated poetry, addressing God, a beloved, or a single soul.
  • Archaism today: in modern English satst is a fossilized, deliberate archaism — it appears in biblical quotation, liturgical language, and archaizing verse, instantly signalling an early modern or scriptural diction.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word is built transparently: the past stem sat- (from Old English sæt, the strong past singular of sittan) plus the second-person singular ending -st. The bridge from Old English to satst is the steady morphological history of the strong verbs: in Old English the second person singular past was sǣte (stem sæt + ending -e); as final -e was lost in Middle English, the ending -st (already present in the present tense and in the preterite-present verbs) was generalized to the past, giving satte(s)t → satst. Thus satst is a perfectly regular form of its era, and its fall from use has nothing to do with morphology: it disappeared because the whole thou-paradigm retreated from standard English in the 17th–18th centuries. The metaphor at work is grammatical personification — the word encodes a singular addressee with a degree of familiarity or reverence that modern "you" no longer signals.

Current status. In present-day English satst is obsolete outside deliberate archaism. Dictionaries label it archaic; it is used chiefly in quotations from the Authorized Version, in the Prayer Book, and in the study of Early Modern English grammar, where it serves as a textbook example of the -st conjugation of the strong verbs.
