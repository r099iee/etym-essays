ROOT WORD 1 — earthen 1. PIE *h₁er- "earth, ground" (c. 4000 BCE) — a root of the Germanic, Celtic, and Hellenic periphery; prehistory debated, possibly connected with *h₁er- "to dig or plow." NOTE: Latin terra is from PIE *ters- "dry" and Sanskrit/Hindi earth-words come from *dʰǵʰem- (Hindi zamin) and *bʰuH- (Sanskrit bhūmi, Hindi bhoomi) — none of these is *h₁er-, so no Italic or Indo-Iranian node belongs here. 2. Proto-Germanic *erþō "earth, ground, soil" (c. 200 CE) — giving German Erde, Dutch aarde, Icelandic jörð, Gothic airþa. 3. Old English eorþe "earth, ground, soil" (attested 8th–11th c.). 4. Old English eorþen "made of earth" (eorþe + the adjectival suffix -en, the Germanic "made of" suffix also in wooden, golden, silken). 5. Middle English erthen "made of earth" (11th–15th c.). 6. English EARTHEN "made of earth or clay" — the form on this card. ROOT WORD 2 — causeways 1. Latin calx, calcis "heel" (classical; 1st c. BCE) — of uncertain Indo-European origin (possibly related to Greek kṓlēps "hollow of the knee"); no reliable PIE reconstruction, no Indo-Iranian or Germanic cognates. 2. Latin calcare "to tread, trample, press with the heel" (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE). 3. Latin calcātus "trodden, pressed down" (past participle). 4. Vulgar Latin *via calciāta "a trodden / paved road" (Late Latin, 4th–6th c. CE) — the road pressed down by feet and heels. 5. Old North French cauciee "paved highway" (12th c.). 6. Anglo-French causee "raised road across wet ground" (14th c.). 7. Middle English causey / causeye "a raised way" (14th c.). 8. English causeway "a raised road over low or wet ground" — causey + the native word way (15th c.). 9. English CAUSEWAYS (plural) — the form on this card. NOTE: Latin calx "limestone, lime" (a Greek loanword from khálix "pebble, gravel") is a DIFFERENT homonym — the source of calculate, chalk, calcium — and must not be merged with calx "heel." ROOT WORD 3 — marshland (a compound of two chains, given in isolation) CHAIN A — MARSH: 1. PIE *mori- "body of water, sea, lake" (c. 4000 BCE) — the sea-root, also the source of Latin mare "sea," Greek (via *mori-? no — Greek thálassa is a different root), Welsh môr "sea," and Lithuanian marios "lagoon." 2. Proto-Germanic *mari "sea, lake, standing water" (c. 200 CE) — Old English mere "lake, pool," Old Norse marr "sea," Gothic marei "sea." 3. Proto-West-Germanic *marisk- "watery, swampy" (the -isk adjective "of the water") — giving the "marsh" words. 4. Old English merisc / mersc "marsh, swamp, bog" (attested 8th–11th c.). 5. Middle English mersche / mersh "marsh" (11th–15th c.). 6. English marsh "soft, wet, low-lying land" (14th c. onward). 7. English MARSHLAND "marsh + land" (16th c. onward) — the compound on this card. CHAIN B — LAND: 1. PIE *lendʰ- "open heath, uncultivated ground, steppe" (c. 4000 BCE) — the root of "land." 2. Proto-Germanic *landą "land, ground, territory" (c. 200 CE). 3. Old English land / lond "land, ground, earth, country" (attested 8th–11th c.). 4. Middle English lond / land (11th–15th c.). 5. English land "the solid surface of the earth; ground, territory" (14th c. onward). 6. English MARSHLAND (marsh + land, 16th c. onward) — the compound final node on this card. NOTE: Latin terra and Greek khthṓn and Sanskrit bhūmi are from *ters-, *dʰǵʰem-, and *bʰuH- respectively — none is *lendʰ- — so no Italic, Hellenic, or Indo-Iranian node belongs in this chain.
