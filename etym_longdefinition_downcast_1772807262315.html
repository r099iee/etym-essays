When the wife tells Ying-lian, “You’ve no cause to be downcast,” the adjective names a complete posture of despair: spirit and often the eyes thrown downward, hope suspended, the body itself reading as grief. In the surrounding narrative Ying-lian has just been purchased, promised a kinder life with Mr Feng, and then held in anxious suspense; the woman’s speech tries to lift her by arguing that Feng’s care, his looks, his wealth, and his unusual readiness to spend on her are all proofs of genuine affection. Downcast captures the heavy, low emotional state that the speech is trying to counteract—the look of a girl whose gaze and whose heart have both been cast down. The later catastrophe, her sale to Xue the Oaf King and the beating that followed, retrospectively deepens the pathos of this fleeting comfort, so that the adjective carries the full weight of hope raised and then violently dashed. Core meaning. Downcast, as an adjective applied to a person, means feeling despondent, dejected, or low in spirits, and it very often describes the outward physical signs of that state: eyes that look toward the ground, a face whose expression has lost its brightness, a head slightly lowered. The word is at once emotional and visual. It is the ordinary modern word for the inner condition and for the tell-tale look that betrays it, and it is fully current in standard English, though it carries a slightly more literary or novelistic weight than plain sad or low. Etymological and conceptual bridge. The literal core is spatial: “cast down”—thrown downward, directed toward the earth. Something downcast has been turned from an elevated or open orientation to a low one. Applied to the gaze (to cast down one’s eyes) the idiom is ancient and almost instinctive: lowered eyes read as submission, shame, grief, modesty, or defeat. The emotional sense grows out of that bodily metaphor. To feel downcast is to feel as though one’s spirits have been thrown down from a height, exactly as a thing overthrown falls. The historical bridge is therefore a chain of metaphors: physical overthrow → lowered eyes and gaze → dejection of spirit → a person’s whole bearing and mood. Polysemy and secondary senses (exhaustive):
  • Emotional sense (dominant today): of a person, despondent, dejected, low in spirits; “He looked downcast after hearing the bad news.”
  • Visual or physiognomic sense: of the eyes, gaze, look, or face, directed toward the ground; “She tried to hide her downcast expression.”
  • Literal and archaic sense: thrown down, overthrown, brought low—used of fortunes, edifices, or institutions; the earliest recorded uses (14th–15th centuries) are physical rather than emotional.
  • Fused sense describing the whole person: a downcast man is one whose posture, face, and mood together announce low spirits; the most common modern use.
  • Modern psychological register: near-synonymous with mildly depressed, discouraged, or disheartened; used of temporary low mood as distinct from clinical depression.
  • Transferred use on objects or scenery (rare but attested): a downcast or downcast-looking street, sky, or room that seems sunken, gloomy, or without light.
  • Mining terminology (a different word): a “downcast” (noun) names the shaft or passage through which air is forced downward in a mine—a homographic technical development to distinguish from the adjective.
  • Idiomatic base: the transitive phrasal verb “to cast down,” meaning to discourage, dispirit, or abase, from which the adjective is derived.
Register and collocations. Standard, literary, and narrative register; fully current, yet more novelistic than plain sad. Common collocations: downcast eyes, a downcast look / expression / face, to feel downcast, to look downcast, a downcast air or manner, a downcast gaze. The word describes temporary mood (as in the wife’s pep talk) or a more settled dejection, and it can name either the inner feeling or its visible signs. In this passage it is the emotional target of consolation: the speech act aims to un-downcast Ying-lian, and the text notes that she did cheer up—briefly—before fate struck again. Against its near-synonyms (dejected, despondent, crestfallen, depressed, disheartened, dispirited, glum, melancholy—set out fully in the similarWords list), downcast stands out as the most concrete and visual: it never forgets that the sad person is literally facing the ground.
