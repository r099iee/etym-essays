• Abu Bakr: Similar because both were intimate companions of the Prophet Muhammad, early converts, fathers/sons-in-law to the Prophet, and Rightly Guided Caliphs; differs because Abu Bakr was the first caliph (632–634 CE) who preserved the community through the Ridda Wars, whereas Uthman was the third caliph who canonized the standardized Qur'anic text.
  • Umar: Similar because both were senior companions who succeeded to the caliphate and oversaw immense imperial conquests; differs because Umar ibn al-Khattab was the austere, disciplinarian second caliph renowned for establishing the state bureaucracy (Diwan), whereas Uthman was an affluent Umayyad patrician celebrated for gentleness, charity, and naval warfare.
  • Ali: Similar because both were close companions, sons-in-law of Muhammad, and Rashidun caliphs; differs because Ali ibn Abi Talib was the fourth caliph (and the first infallible Imam of Shi'a Islam), whose accession followed Uthman's murder and whose reign was consumed by civil conflict against Uthman's vengeful Umayyad kinsmen.
  • Mu'awiya: Similar because both belonged to the prominent Umayyad clan of Mecca and held supreme executive power; differs because Uthman was an early convert belonging to the pious inner circle of Rashidun caliphs in Medina, whereas Mu'awiya I was a later convert who established the dynastic Umayyad monarchy in Damascus.
  • Zayd ibn Thabit: Similar because both played paramount, inseparable roles in preserving the written text of the Qur'an; differs because Zayd was the scholarly scribe and amanuensis who chaired the editorial committee, whereas Uthman was the caliphal sovereign who commissioned the project and enforced its imperial adoption.
  • Rashidun: Similar because Uthman is an essential member of this collective historical grouping; differs because Rashidun is a plural collective designation for all four 'Rightly Guided' caliphs, whereas Uthman denotes the specific third individual ruler.
  • codifier: Similar because Uthman's defining historical legacy was compiling and codifying the definitive Islamic canon; differs because codifier is a general legalistic noun applicable to any compiler of legal or religious statutes (such as Justinian or Napoleon), lacking Uthman's personal religious sanctity.
  • companion: Similar because Uthman held elite status as a Ṣaḥābī who lived alongside Muhammad; differs because companion is a broad category encompassing tens of thousands of early believers, whereas Uthman attained the supreme political and theological leadership of the empire.
  • canonizer: Similar because both describe authorities who establish the official, immutable list of sacred scriptural books; differs because canonizer is an ecclesiastical function often associated with Church councils (e.g., Council of Nicaea), whereas Uthman achieved scriptural uniformity through imperial executive decree and scribal recension.
  • Osman: Similar because Osman is the direct Turkish phonetic descendant and equivalent of the Arabic name Uthman; differs because in historical contexts Osman typically designates Osman I Ghazi, the 14th-century founder of the Ottoman Empire, rather than the 7th-century companion of the Prophet.
  • sovereign: Similar because Uthman reigned as the absolute executive monarch over an imperial territory spanning three continents; differs because sovereign is a secular title of royal statecraft, whereas Uthman's authority was bound to the religious covenant of prophetic succession.
  • martyr: Similar because Uthman was assassinated in an act of political-religious violence and is revered in Sunni tradition as a Shahīd; differs because martyr is a general religious status shared by anyone who dies for their faith, whereas Uthman was the reigning head of the Islamic empire.
