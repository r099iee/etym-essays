Latin (1st c. BCE)
rupta (via)
‘broken way, forced road’
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th–6th c.)
ruta
‘beaten way, course’
↓
Old French (11th–12th c.)
rute, route
‘road, path, course’
↓
Middle English (14th–16th c.)
rut
‘wheel-track in a road’
↓
Modern English (current)
rutted
‘grooved by wheels’

Note: English has a second unrelated rut meaning oestrus in deer, from Old French ruit meaning roaring; it shares spelling only and plays no part in the wheel-track lane of the source sentence.
