- 14th–15th c.: 'whisk' begins as a brush-like tool — a small bunch of twigs or straw for sweeping — and the verb 'to sweep with a whisk'; humble, domestic, workmanlike.
- 16th–17th c.: the kitchen whisk (for beating eggs and cream) cements the sense of rapid, brisk, circular agitation; quick, energetic, light.
- 18th–19th c.: the sense of sudden, nimble movement takes over — to whisk away, to whisk someone off — with a comedic, almost conjuror's flair in novels (Trumpkin's beard whisking to and fro); lively and faintly humorous.
- Contemporary: 'whisked' lives mostly in swift flits — whisked away, whisked into the car — and in cooking (whisked eggs); light, swift, playful, and faintly glamorous.
