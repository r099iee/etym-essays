Wearisome is a transparent English compound, and its history is the history of its two parts. The first part, weary, is ancient: Old English wērig meant “tired, exhausted, sorrowful”, descended from a Proto-West Germanic *wōrig of the same meaning. Beyond that the trail is obscure — the word’s ultimate origin is uncertain, and the most popular guess links it to the Germanic root behind “worry” (Old English wyrgan, “to strangle, to oppress”), so that “weary” would carry the buried image of being crushed or worn down by a burden. That connection is plausible but debated, and honest dictionaries label the deeper ancestry unknown.

The second part: -some

  • The suffix -some descends from Old English -sum, from Proto-Germanic *-samaz, from the Indo-European root *sem- “one, together, whole”.
  • Its function has always been to form adjectives meaning “having the quality of” or “tending to”: Old English wynsum (joyful, winsome), langsum (long-lasting, slow).
  • English never stopped coining with it — tiresome, loathsome, quarrelsome, wholesome, burdensome, and of course wearisome.

The compound is born (late 1300s)

  • Wearisome is attested from the late 14th century, meaning “causing weariness”. Its first life was physical: wearisome journeys, wearisome labour.
  • The pattern is identical to tiresome and mirrors the double life of weary itself, which could mean both “tired” and, from the same period, “causing tiredness”.

From muscles to patience (1400s–1800s)

  • During the 15th and 16th centuries the word spread from the body to the mind and ear: wearisome talk, wearisome arguments, wearisome length.
  • For a time it was also used of the person who felt the weariness (“I was wearisome” for “I was weary”) — a looser sense that modern English has largely abandoned, leaving wearisome firmly on the side of the cause rather than the sufferer.
  • By the 19th century the dominant shade was tedium: wearisome is what drags, repeats, and outlasts the attention — the word of long waits, long lectures, and long journeys.

The word in Tolkien

  • In The Hobbit (1937), Bilbo is “lonesome and wearisome” sitting on the doorstep of the Lonely Mountain — the adjective describes the slow, draining boredom of waiting day after day with nothing happening.
  • The pairing is exact: “lonesome” is the ache of being alone; “wearisome” is the drain of the endless, tedious wait. Together they render the full misery of that vigil.
  • Tolkien, a medievalist, uses the word’s older flavour: a wearisome spell is heavy, oppressive, almost moral — the world-weariness of waiting itself.

Sound and form

  • RP pronunciation /ˈwɪə.rɪ.səm/ — three syllables, with the long vowel of weary in the first.
  • The word is unfailingly regular: wearisome has no inflected forms, and derivatives (wearisomely, wearisomeness) are formed normally.
  • It has remained remarkably stable across six centuries — same spelling, same construction, same core meaning of draining tiredness.

Modern profile

  • Today wearisome is standard but literary; in speech most people reach for tedious or tiring, which is precisely why the word keeps its stately, slightly old-fashioned weight in prose.
  • Its strength is that it names the whole drain — body and patience together — with a single dignified adjective, which is why authors still choose it for atmospheres of slow, oppressive waiting.
