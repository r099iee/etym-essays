BENIGHTED is the past participle of the verb benight, "to overtake or envelop with the darkness of night," used both literally and figuratively. Literally it means "overtaken by night; caught out of doors after nightfall, so as to be lost, stranded, or exposed to peril in the dark." Figuratively it means "plunged into moral, intellectual, or spiritual darkness; ignorant, unenlightened, or deluded," with an overtone of tragic helplessness rather than of mere lack of learning. In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses the word in its literal, narrative sense: the goblins of the Misty Mountains kept a main gate on an easier pass, and "often caught people benighted near their gates." The nuance is sudden, overwhelming exposure, not choosing ignorance but being swallowed by darkness. The travellers are not merely late or lost; they are caught by night itself, overtaken while still on the road, so that darkness falls upon them as an active agent and delivers them into the goblins' hands. The participle gives the state as suffered, not chosen: these are people to whom night has happened, and the word carries a quiet, pitying helplessness even as it marks them as prey.

Formation. The word is built from three parts: the intensive prefix be- (which turns the noun night into a verb meaning "to cover with night"), the noun night, and the participial suffix -ed. The verb benight is a 16th-century coinage; its participle benighted follows within a century, and the figurative sense, from darkness of night to darkness of unreason, is attested almost from the start.

  • Sense 1 (literal, core sense): "overtaken by the darkness of night; caught or stranded in the dark," especially on a journey, a benighted traveller, a benighted village cut off by dusk. This is the sense in the Tolkien passage and the earliest attested use.
  • Sense 2 (figurative, ignorance): "in a state of moral, intellectual, or spiritual darkness; unenlightened, ignorant, or deluded," benighted heathens, benighted ages, benighted minds. The sense developed in the 17th and 18th centuries and became the dominant use in Enlightenment and missionary prose.
  • Sense 3 (pejorative historical and colonial colouring): as a term of condescension, applied to peoples, places, and eras deemed backward or superstitious, "the benighted natives," "a benighted province." The word carries a pitying, often smug superiority, and modern readers may sense its colonial baggage.
  • Sense 4 (substantivised, rare): used absolutely with the definite article, "the benighted," for a class of people, the ignorant, the unenlightened, those wandering in darkness; a rhetorical, semi-archaic use.
  • Sense 5 (modern literary and ironic register): today the literal sense is largely archaic, and figurative use survives chiefly as literary, mock-archaic, or ironic colouring, a consciously old-fashioned way of saying "lost in the dark" or "hopelessly ignorant," and a favourite of stylists who want a word with shadows in it.

Register and stylistic bridge. The bridge between the literal and figurative senses runs through the shared image of being overtaken against one's will: just as night can fall on the traveller without warning and leave him helpless in the dark, so ignorance and delusion can fall on the mind and leave it groping. The participle benighted carries the shape of a completed misfortune, the darkness has already come, the person stands within it, and the word looks on them with a mixture of pity and reproach. In the Middle Ages and the early modern period the same image had a religious form, the soul benighted in sin before the light of grace, and it is from that long tradition of darkness as moral blindness that the modern, secular, Enlightenment use descends.
