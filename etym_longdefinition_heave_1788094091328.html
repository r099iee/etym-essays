Heave (verb; also noun) is a forceful, effortful word for lifting, raising, or throwing something heavy, and by extension for any slow, powerful rising-and-falling motion, for the deep expulsion of breath or stomach contents, and (in nautical tradition) for hauling on ropes and cables. Its core semantic gesture is upward effort against weight: where lift is neutral and raise is orderly, heave always implies strain, mass, and a sudden release of muscular force. In the source context — “Heave ho! Splash plump! Down they go, down they bump!” — the cry is the traditional chant of sailors hauling together on a rope, here hurled gleefully by the elves as they tip the dwarves’ barrels through the trapdoors into the river.

Primary senses

  • To lift or raise with effort — “they heaved the boulder onto the cart”: the effort is central; the object is heavy, the posture is straining, and the motion often involves a coordinated surge of shoulders, legs, and breath. This is the oldest living sense, directly descended from Old English hebban “to lift up”.
  • To throw or hurl (something heavy) — “he heaved the sack over the wall”: overlaps with hurl and fling but keeps the implication of weight and effort; a cricket ball is bowled, but a medicine ball is heaved.
  • To rise and fall rhythmically — “the sea heaved”, “her chest heaved with sobs”: a slow, massy, breathing motion of water, earth, or a living body; the noun heave names such a swell or surge (a “heave of the ground”).
  • To utter or expel with effort — “he heaved a sigh”, “he heaved a groan”: breath or sound is pushed out of the body as if it were a heavy load.
  • To retch or vomit (colloquial) — “the smell made her heave”: the abdominal spasm is felt as an upward heave of the gut; informal and bodily, common in British English.

Nautical and technical senses

  • Nautical hauling — “to heave on a rope”, “heave the anchor up”, “heave to” (to bring a ship to a standstill by bracing sails or helm); the hauling cry “heave ho!” coordinates a crew pulling together on a rope or capstan; the archaic strong past hove survives in phrases like “hove to” and “hove in sight”.
  • Geology — a heave is the horizontal component of displacement along a fault line (contrast throw, the vertical component); earth-science jargon since the 19th century.
  • Engineering — frost heave: the upward swelling of soil or a road surface caused by freezing water; a costly problem in civil engineering.
  • Weightlifting — a “heave” is an unclean, whole-body jerk used to get a barbell overhead.

Secondary and idiomatic senses

  • The heave-ho — “to give someone the heave-ho” means to dismiss, reject, or sack them (mid-20th century); the phrase recycles the sailor’s hauling cry as a comic image of being thrown out.
  • Upheaval — the derived noun names a violent upward disruption, literally of earth (an earthquake upheaval) and figuratively of society or institutions (political upheaval).
  • Heave offering — in biblical translation tradition, a “heave offering” (terumah) was an offering physically lifted up toward God; the sense “to raise in worship” is ancient and religious.
  • Obsolete senses — to heave was once also “to exalt, raise in rank or dignity” (Middle English), “to raise from the dead” (religious texts), and “to grow, rise (of plants)”; these died out as the physical sense narrowed onto muscular effort.

Semantic bridge: every sense of heave shares the single image of a heavy mass moved upward against gravity — whether the mass is a stone, an anchor, a wave, a chest, a sigh, or a stomach. The nautical cry “heave ho!” (recorded from the 14th century) fixed the word’s association with coordinated collective effort, and from that association grew the modern idiom of dismissal, while the bodily senses (sigh, retch) treat breath and vomit as heavy loads that must be pushed out. The word remains unmistakably physical: even in metaphor it never loses its muscular weight.
