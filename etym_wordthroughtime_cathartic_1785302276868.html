- [17th century: medical sense] - Entered English medicine to name a purging agent — a laxative — and long served as a strictly physical, clinical term for bodily evacuation.
- [19th century: literary-philosophical sense] - Extended to Aristotle's katharsis in the Poetics, naming the purgation of pity and fear in tragedy; the word now designated emotional cleansing in art and criticism.
- [Early 20th century: psychoanalytic sense] - Freud and the therapeutic tradition seized the term for the discharge of pent-up, repressed emotion; catharsis became a cornerstone of psychotherapy and of modernism's fascination with inner release.
- [Late 20th-21st century: everyday emotive sense] - Diluted into popular self-care vocabulary to mean any satisfying emotional release, from a good cry to a favourite song; the sense drifted from physical purgation toward subjective relief, though it still implies a climactic, almost necessary discharge rather than mere comfort.
