feebly (adverb): In a way that lacks strength or force; weakly; faintly; in a manner that is ineffective, faltering, fragile, or barely sufficient for its purpose. The word is the adverbial form of the adjective feeble, and it inherits the whole shadowed history of that adjective — from the tears of its Latin ancestor flēbilis ("lamentable, that which provokes weeping") through the Old French feble ("weak, frail") into Middle English. When Ruskin writes in the Preface to Modern Painters, "I shall shrink from no labour in the execution of a task which may tend, however feebly, to the advancement of the cause of real art," he uses the word as the instrument of a studied, dignified humility: the grandest possible ambition (the "cause of real art") is advanced by an effort the author himself confesses may be but weak. The adverb is double-edged — it concedes the frailty of the labourer while insisting on the sincerity of the labour, inviting the reader to weigh the work by its intention rather than by its force. Polysemy: the adverb carries several closely related senses, shading into one another along a continuum of weakness:
  • Physical weakness of a force or effect: a light that shines feebly, a pulse that beats feebly, a flame that burns feebly — the oldest and most concrete sense, describing a power too small for its task.
  • Ineffectiveness or inadequacy of an effort: to protest feebly, to argue feebly, to resist feebly — the sense in Ruskin, where the effort is made but with little force, so that its result is doubtful.
  • Faintness or near-imperceptibility of a stimulus: a sound heard feebly, a voice carried feebly on the wind — overlapping with faintly, where the emphasis falls on the threshold of perception rather than on the quality of an agent's action.
  • Moral, intellectual, or spiritual weakness: a feebly reasoned case, a feebly supported claim — the extension of bodily weakness to the powers of the mind, common in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century criticism and theology.
  • Faltering, unsteady motion: to walk feebly, to rise feebly — a body whose strength fails mid-action, suggesting fragility and hesitation rather than laziness or reluctance.
The historical bridge that connects all these senses is the notion of deficient power: whatever is feebly done is done with less force than the task demands. The word's deeper history adds a pathos no synonym quite equals. Latin flēbilis meant primarily "weeping, tearful" and secondarily "that which provokes tears, lamentable, pitiable"; the shift to "weak, infirm" is a classic case of semantic inference — the pitiable are typically the weak, the sick, the old, the helpless — so that an idea of compassion became, over centuries, an idea of powerlessness. In English the adverb is formal and literary rather than colloquial; it appears in elevated prose, in narrative description, and in rhetorical self-deprecation, and it is far rarer in speech than its plain synonym weakly. It also retains a faint aftertaste of its mourning origin, so that "feebly" often carries a residue of pity and tenderness that purely mechanical words like ineffectually lack. Register and boundary notes:
  • Weakly is the neutral default; feebly adds pathos and fragility.
  • Faintly describes the threshold of perception (faint light, faint sound); feebly more often describes the quality of an effort or action.
  • Limply denotes flaccidity, the absence of tension; feebly denotes a deficiency of force while allowing for earnest intention.
  • Ineffectually is result-oriented — the effort fails to achieve its end — whereas feebly is effort-oriented: the effort is weak in itself, whether or not it succeeds.
  • As an adverb of humility, feebly became a favourite of the Victorian "modest champion" — the writer who claims to have done little while doing everything — and it is precisely this rhetorical function that Ruskin exploits in his Preface.
