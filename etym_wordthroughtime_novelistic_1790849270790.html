- 1820s–1850s: Coined during the Romantic and early Victorian era to denote qualities or elements belonging to the form or content of novels, frequently carrying a slightly disparaging undertone associated with romantic melodrama, sentimentality, or fanciful unreality.
- 1860s–1910s: Shifted toward descriptive literary criticism as realism (Dickens, Eliot, James) elevated the novel to a serious high-art form, coming to denote sophisticated psychological delineation, social panorama, and immersive narrative craft.
- 1920s–1960s: Broadened into historical, biographical, and journalistic domains, celebrating non-fiction works that successfully employed scene-setting, pacing, and interior depth without sacrificing factual veracity.
- 1970s–Present: Reinterpreted in critical theory and semiotics (e.g., Barthes's le romanesque) as a pervasive, imaginative, subjective textual mode, alongside widespread contemporary usage praising multi-layered, character-driven storytelling across film, television, and narrative journalism.
