*dek- (Proto-Indo-European, "to take, accept, perceive; to seem") → Greek δέχομαι (dékhomai, "to take, accept, receive," 8th–7th c. BCE attested in Homer; same root also gives Latin docēre "to teach" via a derived sense "to make accept/perceive") → Greek δοκέω (dokéō, "to think, suppose, seem," the o-grade *dok- of the root, 6th–5th c. BCE) → Greek δόγμα (dógma, "that which seems true to one; opinion, belief; later decree, ordinance," formed with the suffix -ma, attested from the 5th–4th c. BCE in philosophy and politics) → Latin dogma ("doctrine, tenet, decree," borrowed from Greek in the 1st c. BCE, used by Cicero) → Church Latin dogma ("doctrine defined by the Church," from the 4th c. CE with the councils) → English DOGMA ("an authoritative tenet," borrowed from Latin in the 1540s, attested in theological contexts). Note on debated points: the exact PIE root shape behind Greek dokéō is sometimes given as *dek- "to take, perceive" with an o-grade, sometimes separated as *deḵ-; the semantic bridge "to take something as so" > "to seem" is standard but the precise derivational route is debated. The Latin form dogma and the English borrowing are undisputed.
