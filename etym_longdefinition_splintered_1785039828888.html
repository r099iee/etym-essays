SPLINTERED is the simple past and past participle of the verb to splinter: to have broken into long, thin, sharp fragments. In the stone-giant passage of The Hobbit, ch. iv ("Over Hill and Under Hill"), the giant-cast rocks, tossed down into the darkness, either "smashed among the trees far below, or splintered into little bits with a bang." The word sits in a carefully built double contrast: first against smashed, which names the heavy, crunching impact on trees, and then against "with a bang," which gives the fragmentation an audible, explosive edge. The past tense marks the act as completed and final — the rock is gone, reduced to little bits — and Tolkien's phrasing makes the giants' "game" an instrument of wholesale destruction: solid boulders are pulverised as an afterthought of play. The tone is violent, almost relishable in its terrible neatness, and it underscores how far beyond human scale the mountain-storm has become: what the party experiences as a catastrophe, the giants treat as casual recreation. Senses of the word (exhaustive)
  • Broken into thin sharp fragments (primary, physical): wood, bone, or glass splintering along its natural lines — a shield splintered by a warhammer, a door splintered by the wind, a mast splintered in a storm.
  • Figurative division (modern, strong): communities, parties, or opinions "splintered" into hostile factions; a "splinter group" or "splinter party" is a fragment that has broken away from a larger body — the noun splinter, meaning "a fragment," used attributively.
  • Noun splinter (the source-word): a thin, sharp, needle-like piece of wood, glass, or bone — as in a splinter under the skin — from which the verb was formed.
  • Medical and technical family (splint): a splint is a thin rigid strip used to immobilise a broken limb; splinting is the act of fitting it; the whole family is the same Germanic splitting-root, and splintered is its adjectival extreme.
  • Quasi-adjectival use: splintered can be used attributively and as a participle adjective — "splintered bones," "a splintered edge," "splintered glass" — describing the finished, fragmented state.
Semantic bridge The noun splinter (14th c.) named the sharp fragment itself, and the verb was formed from it in the 16th century: to splinter is to reduce something to splinters. The figurative sense — a community or party "splintered" into factions — is a 20th-century extension, driven by the language of politics (splinter groups, splinter parties breaking away from larger movements): wholeness shattered into shards along its fault-lines, exactly as wood shatters along the grain. The physical image is always recoverable beneath the political one: a splintered party, like a splintered plank, keeps its sharp edges and does not easily reunite. Register and near-synonyms Splintered is a strong, physical, finished word, equally at home in narrative and in politics. Among kindred words the boundaries are firm: shattered means broken violently into many irregular pieces; smashed stresses the loud, crushing impact; split names a clean division along a line; cracked describes fissuring without full separation; fractured is the clinical or geological word for a break; crumbled implies gradual disintegration into small particles; and broken is the general term. Splintered is distinct in naming the long, thin, sharp fragments — the needle-like pieces along the grain — and in the completeness of the fragmentation: a thing that is splintered is not merely damaged but gone.
