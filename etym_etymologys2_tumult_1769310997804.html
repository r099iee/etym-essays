Single linear derivation chain for TUMULT (Latin/Romance line):
  • PIE *teuh₂- (or *tewh₁-), "to swell, be puffed up, surge, heap up" (c. 4000 BCE) — a root of physical swelling whose Germanic, Hellenic, and Indo-Iranian reflexes (English thumb, Greek tumbos "burial mound," Sanskrit stūpa) confirm the meaning.
  • Proto-Italic *tum-ē- "to swell" (c. 1000 BCE).
  • Latin tumere, "to swell, be swollen, be puffed up, surge" (attested 1st c. BCE; also the base of tumour "a swelling," tumescere "to begin to swell").
  • Latin tumultus, -ūs, "uproar, commotion, disturbance; a sudden swelling uprising" (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Caesar and Cicero) — formed from tumere with the verbal-noun suffix -tus; in Roman military usage a tumultus (as in tumultus Gallicus) was an emergency rising requiring emergency levy.
  • Old French tumulte, "uproar, commotion, disorder" (12th c.), from Latin tumultus.
  • Middle English tumulte, "uproar, violent commotion," borrowed c. 1400–1430 (attested in Chaucer-adjacent and 15th-c. texts); later respelled to modern tumult.
  • Modern English tumult (16th c. onward; noun), our word, with the senses of crowd-noise, physical disorder, and (17th c.) inner emotional agitation.
NUANCE NOTES: (1) The PIE root *teuh₂- is secure in meaning ("to swell") and its Germanic reflexes (English thumb < PGmc *þūmô, "the swollen one") are accepted; the chain into Latin is the well-attested tumēre → tumultus. (2) Latin tumultus already carried the "swollen uprising" nuance — a sudden commotion that swells into emergency — so the modern double sense (noise + disorder) is present from the start. (3) English also has the rare verb "tumult" (17th c., "to raise an uproar"), a back-formation from the noun, and the adjective "tumultuous" (from Latin tumultuosus), a sibling rather than an ancestor of the noun. (4) The Sanskrit form tumula ("noisy, tumultuous") cited in some popular sources is of uncertain connection and is NOT required for the Latin line, which stands independently.
