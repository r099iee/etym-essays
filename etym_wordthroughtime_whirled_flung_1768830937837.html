- Medieval English — "to turn rapidly": from its first appearance in English, whirl means swift circular motion, and it soon breeds the whirlpool, the whirlwind, and the whirligig — devices and disasters that take hold of the body.
- 16th–18th centuries — the sense of being seized: whirl comes to mean not merely spinning but being drawn into the spin, a vertiginous, enveloping force that unwinds a person into its centre.
- 19th–20th centuries — the social metaphor: the whirl of society, of events, of a ballroom — any dizzying, rapid, inescapable activity; yet the physical rotation remains dominant.
- Contemporary — whirl is still the most energetic of the spin-words, carrying great speed, envelopment, and the sense that the spinner is being sucked into the motion.
flung
- Medieval English — "to throw, to cast": fling enters the language meaning to throw with force, and at once gathers the vigour of carelessness.
- 16th–18th centuries — the careless throw: to fling a stone, to fling oneself into a chair, to fling open a door — sudden, violent, unconcerned release, and later the reckless "fling" of an escapade.
- Contemporary — flung is the contemptuous throw of a thing rather than of a person: whip-like, sudden, and indifferent to where the object lands.
