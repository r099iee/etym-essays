“Pinch” has meant to squeeze something between finger and thumb since earliest English — the sharp, small, compressive grip of the hand.
“Pinch” from that squeeze bred a long chain of figurative senses: “a pinch of salt” (a tiny gripped measure), “to pinch pennies” (to be stingy, squeezing every coin), and “in a pinch” — in a tight squeeze, an emergency or critical spot.
“Pinch” in thieves’ slang came to mean to steal — to lift something away with a sharp grasp — and the police sense “a pinch” (an arrest) followed, both 19th-century underworld uses.
“Pinch” today works on all these levels at once: the physical squeeze, the small measure, the tight economic squeeze, the theft, and the crisis — “as fierce as a dragon in a pinch.”
