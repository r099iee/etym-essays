dungaree (noun)

A heavy, coarse, exceptionally durable twill-woven or plain-woven cotton fabric, historically unbleached or dyed with indigo blue; in its plural form (dungarees), sturdy utilitarian work trousers or one-piece protective bib-and-brace overalls worn by industrial laborers, sailors, farmers, and mechanics. Originating on the western coast of the Indian subcontinent as an inexpensive, hard-wearing calico cloth, dungaree played a central role in colonial maritime trade, global labor history, and 20th-century socio-political fashion movements.

The term displays several interconnected technical, sartorial, maritime, and cultural senses:

  • The Textile Material: In textile manufacturing, dungaree refers to a coarse, thick cotton cloth woven from heavy yarn in a 2/1 or 3/1 twill weave (or sometimes plain weave). It is structurally very similar to denim, but historically differed in its yarn composition: whereas classic denim (*serge de Nîmes*) was woven with dyed indigo warp yarns and unbleached white weft yarns, original Indian dungaree was frequently piece-dyed (the entire woven cloth dyed a uniform indigo blue or unbleached natural off-white). It was celebrated for its high tensile strength, resistance to rough surface abrasion, and ability to withstand repeated laundering in harsh saltwater.
  • Bib-and-Brace Overalls (British & Commonwealth English): In contemporary British, Australian, and Commonwealth usage, dungarees (plural) specifically identifies an all-in-one protective garment consisting of durable trousers attached to a chest bib, held up over the shoulders by adjustable crossed straps or braces, and equipped with large utility pockets for tools. Worn over an ordinary shirt, it serves as protective workwear for tradespeople and gardeners, as well as playful everyday casual wear.
  • Naval Working Uniforms (Nautical & American History): In the United States Navy and merchant marine throughout the 20th century, dungarees designated the standard utility working uniform worn by enlisted sailors. The uniform consisted of loose-fitting, wide-legged blue denim or dungaree bell-bottom trousers paired with a light blue chambray work shirt and a dark watch cap. The wide bell bottoms were engineered for rapid emergency removal in the water if a sailor fell overboard and could be inflated with air to serve as improvised flotation devices.
  • Work Trousers & Blue Jeans (Historical US Usage): Before the marketing triumph of the word "jeans" in the 1960s, heavy denim work trousers in the United States were commonly advertised and purchased under the commercial label "dungarees," associated with railroad workers, coal miners, and construction crews.
  • Icon of Labor, Gender Equality, & Counterculture: Culturally, dungarees represent a profound sociopolitical emblem:
    • World War II Home Front: The standard uniform of millions of female civilian defense workers in munitions factories and aircraft plants, immortalized in the iconic "Rosie the Riveter" imagery.
    • Civil Rights Movement: In the early 1960s, student organizers of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) in the American South wore denim dungarees as a deliberate political statement of solidarity with disenfranchised African American sharecroppers.
    • Feminist & Countercultural Anti-Fashion: During the late 1960s and 1970s, the women's liberation movement adopted dungarees as an egalitarian, functional rejection of constrictive, sexualized patriarchal fashion.

The metonymic progression from a rocky hill quarter in Bombay (*Dongri*) to the coarse fabric produced by its weavers, to maritime work trousers, to wartime industrial overalls, and finally to modern egalitarian street fashion marks dungaree as an indelible linguistic monument to the global working class.
