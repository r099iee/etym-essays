Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *pewd- / *peud- (reconstructed root, c. 4500–2500 BCE) "to thrust, strike, push, press". This root underlies the concrete image of physically shoving or kicking something away. Note on debate: the connection of Latin pudēre "to feel shame" to this root is plausible but disputed; de Vaan treats pudēre as of uncertain etymology, and some scholars prefer a link to *pu- "rotten, foul" instead. Stage 2 — Latin pudēre (attested 2nd c. BCE, Plautus) "to cause shame, to feel shame, to be ashamed". The semantic shift from "to thrust/strike" to "to feel shame" is generally explained as "to strike down, humble, cause to bow the head" (shame as a blow or pushing-down), but the precise bridge is debated and the root connection itself is not certain. Stage 3 — Latin repudium (attested 2nd c. BCE, Plautus, Casina) "divorce, rejection, formal dissolution of a betrothal". Formed with the prefix re- "back, away" attached to the base of pudēre, yielding "a pushing-away" or "a casting-off". In Roman family law repudium was the one-sided formal rejection of a betrothed spouse, distinct from divortium, the mutual divorce of a married couple. Stage 4 — Latin repudiāre (attested 1st c. BCE, Cicero) "to reject, cast off, divorce, disown". A regular first-conjugation verb derived from repudium; in Classical Latin it covered rejecting proposals, motions, and accusations as well as divorcing a spouse. Stage 5 — Latin repudiātus (Classical through Late Latin) past participle of repudiāre, "rejected, cast off, divorced". The participial stem repudiāt- is the form that entered English. Stage 6 — Early Modern English repudiate (borrowed c. 1540s) "to cast off, divorce, reject". First recorded in English in the context of divorce and casting off a wife or betrothed; the meaning "to refuse to acknowledge" developed by the 1820s. Stage 7 — Modern English REPUDIATED (past tense and past participle, also participial adjective) "rejected or disowned the validity of". Final modern form of the chain. Debated / nuanced etymology: the older popular explanation that repudium comes from re- + pēs, ped- "foot" (i.e. "to push away with the foot") is a folk etymology and is not accepted by modern scholarship; the standard account derives repudium from pudēre, whose own PIE origin (whether *pewd- "thrust" or *pu- "foul") remains an open question in historical linguistics.
