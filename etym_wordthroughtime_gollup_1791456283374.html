- 18th–19th c. — gollop/gollup is recorded in slang glossaries and regional speech for swallowing greedily; the tone is homely, rustic, and faintly comic, with no literary prestige.
- 19th c. — Scots and northern English dialect writing adopts it for hard-working men, farm animals, and tavern scenes; it reads as vigorous and unpretentious.
- Early 20th c. — Scottish authors use it of a heron gulping a frog and a mule breathing “great gollops of air,” giving it a robust, affectionate, physical force.
- Mid 20th c. — comic novelists such as P. G. Wodehouse use it for genteel characters being uncharacteristically hearty; the word becomes a signal of jovial indulgence.
- Late 20th–21st c. — it retreats to regional speech, dialect literature, and historical fiction; in standard English it now reads as quaint, deliberately rustic, or humorous.
