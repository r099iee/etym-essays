CASHIERED is the past tense and past participle of the verb cashier, meaning to dismiss someone - especially an officer or holder of high office - from their position in disgrace, formally stripping them of rank, authority, and honour. Where an ordinary firing removes a person from a job, to be cashiered is to be forcibly ejected from an entire world of trust and status: the word implies a public, ritual, and punitive act, most familiar from military life, where a disgraced officer might have his sword broken and his insignia torn off before his assembled regiment. In institutional use it extends to any official, magistrate, or functionary removed from authority in a way that marks them as disgraced. The tone of the word is archaic, martial, and severe; it is rarely used of everyday civilian firings, and its very gravity is part of its meaning.

In the supplied literary context - 'After the report was read, the [cashiered] official was forced to return to his home in disgrace' - the word does exactly this work. The official is not simply relieved of duties; a report, presumably of misconduct or failure, has formally condemned him, and his dismissal is presented as an inescapable public shame that drives him home in humiliation. Cashiered here signals the total collapse of a career and a reputation: the man is not demoted or quietly retired but expelled from honour itself, an exile in his own house.

The full range of the word's senses is best surveyed by category.

  • Military sense (primary) - Dismissal from the armed forces with dishonour, typically after a court-martial or a breakdown of discipline; the ceremonial degradation of the officer - the broken sword, the torn epaulettes, the march before the ranks - is part of the concept.
  • Institutional sense - Removal of a civil official, judge, magistrate, or other figure of authority in formal disgrace, without the ceremonial theatre of the military case but with the same stigma.
  • Obsolete / transferred sense - To cast aside, discard, or reject anything - an idea, a plan, a person - as worthless; this broader use, common in the 17th and 18th centuries, survives today only in historical writing.
  • Figurative use - In modern prose the word is used of any catastrophic, irreparable loss of standing, even outside formal institutions, precisely because it carries the memory of ceremonial dishonour into the new context.

A crucial caution: the verb cashier (to dismiss) has nothing to do with the noun cashier (the person who handles money in a shop or bank). The noun reached English from Middle French caisse and Italian cassa, 'cash box', ultimately from Latin capsa, 'box, case'; the verb came from a wholly different source, the French casser 'to break, annul', descended from Latin cassare 'to make void'. The two words are accidental homographs and homophones in modern English, and their similarity is a trap for the etymologically careless - the mnemonic in the supplied card, which pictures a stealing cashier being kicked out, plays on exactly this false but memorable link.

Within its own register, cashiered differs from its near-synonyms in combining three features at once: (1) the dismissal is from a position of authority or military rank, not merely from employment; (2) it is accomplished in formal, often ceremonial, disgrace; and (3) it is final and punitive, stripping the person of honour rather than merely of pay. A worker is sacked or fired; a politician may be ousted; but an officer is cashiered - and the difference is the public, shaming ceremony attached to the word.

Register and survival: cashiered now reads as deliberately historical or literary. In modern armies the formal term is 'dismissed with disgrace' or 'dishonourably discharged', and the ceremony of sword-breaking survives mainly in memory; consequently, when a modern writer says cashiered, they are deliberately invoking the older, harsher world of courts-martial, duels of honour, and scarlet epaulettes. This archaism is an asset: the word still delivers a force that no synonym of contemporary management speak can match, and the supplied sentence exploits that force to turn a routine bureaucratic report into the instrument of a man's moral ruin.
