rhetoric (noun): the art of effective, persuasive, or eloquent speaking and writing — the classical discipline of crafting language to influence an audience — and, in modern everyday use, a word that has split into two registers: the noble art of persuasion and the pejorative label for insincere, bombastic, or empty political language.

1. The primary sense — the classical art of persuasion

In its original and still central meaning, rhetoric is the theory and practice of persuasive discourse, one of the seven liberal arts of the medieval curriculum (the trivium: grammar, logic, rhetoric) and a foundational subject of ancient and Renaissance education. As a discipline it studies how language persuades:
  • The rhetorical appeals — ethos (appeal to the speaker's character and credibility), pathos (appeal to emotion), and logos (appeal to reason and argument), the triad codified by Aristotle.
  • The five canons — invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery (Latin inventio, dispositio, elocutio, memoria, pronuntiatio), the classical scheme that governs how a speech is devised, ordered, styled, remembered, and performed.
  • Figures of speech — metaphor, simile, antithesis, anaphora, hyperbole, the rhetorical question, and the whole catalogue of figures that give language force, memorability, and beauty.
  • The genres of oratory — deliberative (political persuasion in the assembly), forensic (legal argument in the courts), and epideictic (ceremonial praise or blame).

2. Historical and institutional sense

Historically, rhetoric was the crown of the ancient curriculum: the art of the orator and the statesman, taught from Gorgias, Isocrates, and Aristotle through Cicero and Quintilian to the humanist schools of the Renaissance. In the Middle Ages it was one of the three arts of the trivium; in the Renaissance, Cicero's rhetoric became the model of the educated citizen and the courtier — which is why, in the card's context, C. S. Lewis lists Rhetoric among the subjects of a prince's education. Metonymically, the word also names a single piece of persuasive writing or a style of public address: "a fiery piece of rhetoric."

3. Polysemy — the modern pejorative shift

Since the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries a second, critical sense has grown alongside the noble one:
  • Empty or mere rhetoric — language that is persuasive in manner but hollow in substance: "his speech was all rhetoric," "empty rhetoric," "mere rhetoric," "political rhetoric."
  • Bombast and insincerity — exaggerated, high-sounding, emotionally manipulative language used to cloud issues or rouse a crowd rather than to reason honestly.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the very sophistication of the classical art created the suspicion. Once rhetorical technique was understood as a teachable set of devices capable of making the weaker argument appear the stronger (the charge levelled at the ancient Sophists), persuasion could be separated from truth: a speaker could be eloquent yet dishonest. The word therefore came to name not only the honest craft of persuasion but also the manipulative flourish that substitutes effect for substance.

4. Other modern senses

  • Academic subfield — in universities, rhetoric remains a formal discipline (rhetoric and composition, the rhetoric of science, visual rhetoric, rhetorical criticism).
  • Figurative rhetoric — of any persuasive or expressive mode that works through style: "the rhetoric of advertising," "the rhetoric of the manifesto," the "visual rhetoric" of an image or a building.
  • Rhetorical questions and flourishes — the adjective "rhetorical" (a question asked for effect rather than information) and the noun "flourish" keep the ornamental sense alive in everyday idiom.

5. Register and range

Today the word is two-register: in formal and academic contexts it carries prestige (the noble discipline of eloquence), while in political journalism and everyday speech it is often pejorative ("just rhetoric"). The art, however, has never died: advertising, law, politics, diplomacy, and social media are all arenas of rhetoric, whether the word is praised or damned. Understanding rhetoric means understanding how language shapes belief — the very skill a prince in the Narnian curriculum is being taught.
