- Old English — a garment, any garment: scrūd clothes the living with no thought of the grave.
- Late medieval — death takes the word: the burial sheet becomes the shroud, and every other garment finds a plainer name.
- 15th–16th c. — sailors rig it aloft: the standing ropes that clothe the mast are shrouds too, gear in the Scandinavian sense, honest rope with no mourning in it.
- 17th–19th c. — poets veil the world in it: mists, snows, and silences are shrouded, and the verb to shroud learns to hide anything from hills to scandals.
- 20th c. — engineers fit it to machines: fan-shrouds, propeller-shrouds, and parachute shroud-lines carry the old clothing-word into workshops and airfields.
- Today — mist, mast, and mourning share one plural: stray shrouds of weather in novels, taut shrouds of wire aloft, and the solemn singular sheet of the funeral home.
