- 17th to 18th Century - Originates as a regional Occitan toponym and patronymic in southern France, associated with rugged rocky landscapes (*balssa*) and aristocratic estates in Charente (Jean-Louis Guez de Balzac).
- 1830s to 1850s - Explodes across Parisian literary culture as Honoré de Balzac publishes *La Comédie humaine*, celebrated for its colossal imaginative scale and financial realism, yet attacked by traditionalists for vulgar melodrama and commercial haste.
- Late 19th Century - Canonized by Émile Zola, Karl Marx, and Friedrich Engels as the undisputed founding father of literary realism and the supreme sociological anatomist of nineteenth-century European capitalism.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Reinterpreted by modernist figures (Marcel Proust, Charles Baudelaire, Georg Lukács) who illuminated Balzac's visionary, mystical, and alchemical dimensions beyond naive documentary realism.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Functions as an essential eponym ("Balzacian") across comparative literature and economics (e.g., Thomas Piketty), designating panoramic social scope, consuming monomaniacal desires, and the forensic dissection of wealth and social status.
