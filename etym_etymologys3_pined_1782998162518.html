PINED is the past tense and past participle of the verb pine, and its history is a history of pain. The chain begins in Proto-Indo-European with the root *kwey-, "to pay, compensate, avenge," a root built around the ancient legal-religious idea of requital. From it Greek made poine, the penalty or blood-price — in Homer, the compensation a family received for a slain kinsman, and by extension any retribution or punishment; the same root gave Avestan kaena "punishment." The Greek word passed into Latin as poena, "punishment, penalty, pain" (Cicero uses poena for legal penalties), and it is the source of our modern penalty and of the punitive/punish family, as well as of pain itself. From Latin poena the word entered the West Germanic languages early, as a borrowing carried by the Church and by Roman administration: Old English pin meant "pain, torment, punishment," and its relatives Old Saxon pina and Old High German pina show that the loan was widespread before the English themselves were literate in it. From that noun Old English built the weak verb pinian, "to torment, to torture, to afflict," attested around the year 1000:
  • The verb appears constantly in Old English homilies and saints' lives, where it names the torments of hell and of martyrdom — physical agony deliberately inflicted.
  • This is the sense that dominates the word for its first three centuries in English: to pine someone is to torture them.
The meaning begins to turn in Middle English. By the 12th-13th centuries pinen could be either transitive ("to torment") or intransitive ("to suffer torment"), and the central metaphor of the word's later life emerges: a person tormented by grief or desire is described as being consumed by it, wasting away like a body under torture. Around the 14th century the sense crystallizes that the card records — "to waste away with longing, grief, or confinement; to long intensely" — and Chaucer uses the word exactly this way for the lovesick and the exiled, for whom desire is a slow fire that eats the flesh. The verb's history thus shows a striking semantic drift: the pain that was once inflicted from outside (the jailer's torment) becomes the pain that consumes from within (the heart's own torment), and the passive sufferer of torture becomes the active sufferer of longing who nevertheless "wastes away." The sound history is comparatively simple because the verb is a Germanic weak verb:
  • Old English pinian, with its long /i:/, becomes Middle English pinen.
  • The past tense is formed with the dental suffix -ed that English inherits from Proto-Germanic: Old English pin-ede, Middle English pinede, modern pined.
  • The orthographic i has also shifted: Old English wrote the long vowel as i, Middle English as i, and after the Great Vowel Shift of the 15th-17th centuries the vowel became the modern diphthong /a'/ — which is why pine now rhymes with mine and time, and why pined sounds as it does.
It is worth stressing one persistent confusion: the verb pine is etymologically unrelated to the pine tree, whose name descends from Latin pinus. The vivid image of a tree withering and "pining away" is a folk etymology; the verb's wasting-away sense comes instead from the root meaning "to be tormented," the torment of grief shown as a slow consuming of the body. Keeping these two words apart is a small lesson in the dangers of surface similarity — same spelling, entirely separate families. The literary life of the word is rich:
  • In Renaissance verse pine keeps its double force of suffering and longing — Spenser's Faerie Queene and the sonneteers use it of the languishing lover; Shakespeare's plays use pine for grief, sickness, and the wasting of love and despair.
  • By the 18th-19th centuries, in the age of Romantic feeling, pining becomes almost the signature emotion of longing — the "pining" exile, the pining lover, the pining heart of the ballads.
  • Wordsworth's The Prelude gives the exact shade the card records: escaped from "the vast city," the poet says he "long had pined," and the verb holds together both meanings at once — the torment of confinement in the city and the wasting longing for freedom, nature, and a home. The following line, "A discontented sojourner: now free," completes the arc: pining is the ache of the caged, and the freedom that follows is the relief the whole passage celebrates.
The modern verb has settled into two principal constructions:
  • Pine for names intense, painful longing for an absent person, home, or thing ("she pined for her homeland").
  • Pine away names the physical wasting that accompanies grief or despair ("he pined away after her death").
  • The transitive sense "to torment" is long dead, preserved only in archaisms and in the phrase's older history.
The past form pined — the card's word — keeps an archaic, elegiac softness in literary use, exactly the tone of the Wordsworthian passage: a grief-verb whose very etymology (poine, blood-price) connects longing to the deepest registers of human loss and requital. From the PIE root of compensation to the Romantic ache of separation, PINED is the descendant of a word that once meant punishment — and it still carries, in the silent torment of desire, the echo of that ancient price.
