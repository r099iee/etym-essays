- 1620s "the deep open water visible from shore, clear of coastal shallows" - coined in Jacobean naval navigation from the adverb off + noun-forming suffix -ing; an essential seamanship term.
- Late 17th–18th c. "sea room maintained to avoid a lee shore" - used in phrases like keep an offing and make an offing; symbolized safety from shipwreck on rocky coasts.
- Late 18th c. "riding at anchor in deep water outside the harbor" - naval logs described arriving ships as lying in the offing while awaiting pilots or favorable tides.
- Early-to-mid 19th c. "impending, approaching on the near horizon" - generalized into mainstream literary prose as a temporal metaphor for upcoming events, crises, or changes.
- 20th c. "standard idiom for foreseeable events" - universally recognized in journalism and literature; popularized in comedic British fiction (Wodehouse) as a humorous marker of looming trouble.
- Mid-20th c. (American slang split) "a murder or contract hit" - modern criminal slang derived from the transitive verb to off (to kill), distinct from the classical nautical idiom.
