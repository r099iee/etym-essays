The Evolution of "Supped": From Sip to Supper, and Back

The word supped is a meeting point of two long histories: an unbroken native Germanic line of drinking in sips, and a French-imported line of eating the evening meal — two rivers that merged in Middle English and have flowed together ever since. Its story tracks the changing shape of the European meal itself.

1. The PIE root: liquid and the sacred sip. The native line begins with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *seuə- / *sū-, "to take liquid, press out, suck, extract sap." The root belongs to the oldest stratum of sensory vocabulary, and its reflexes cluster around the idea of liquid nourishment: in the Indo-Iranian world it produced sóma, the sacred pressed drink of Vedic ritual, and rasa, "sap, juice, essence"; in Latin it gave succus, "juice, sap" (whence English succulent and suction); in Greek it shaded toward falling liquid and rain. Already the root carries both the act (taking liquid) and the substance (juice), a doubleness that will echo in the meal’s history.

2. Proto-Germanic *sūpaną: sipping as the Germanic way of drinking. In the Germanic family the root produced the strong verb *sūpaną, "to drink in sips, swallow liquid." Its cognates show how durable the form was: Old Norse súpa, Old High German sūfan, Middle Dutch sūpen, and modern German saufen, which by a characteristically German path drifted from "drink" to "drink heavily, guzzle." The Germanic sipping verb was used above all for warm liquid food: broths, pottages, and soups, eaten with a spoon or by soaking bread.

3. Old English sūpan and the Anglo-Saxon evening broth. Old English sūpan, "to sip, drink, taste liquid food," was the everyday verb for taking the evening meal in an Anglo-Saxon household, because that meal typically was a hot broth or pottage. The evening meal itself was not yet called "supper"; one simply "supped" — sipped and dipped one’s way through a bowl of liquid food at day’s end. This is the crucial fact that will make the two histories one: the meal and the sipping were the same act.

4. The Frankish sop crosses the Channel. Meanwhile, the Franks — a Germanic people ruling much of Gaul — carried the same Germanic sipping vocabulary into Romance. Frankish *sopp-, "bread soaked in liquid," gave Old French soupe, "broth with sopped bread," and the verb souper, "to take the evening meal" (12th–13th c.). The logic is elegant: the evening meal was the broth into which one sopped bread, so "to soup" meant "to have that meal." This same word stock produced English soup and supper, and it gave French its word for the evening meal, souper, which is still eaten in France today.

5. Middle English: the two verbs collide. After 1066, Anglo-Norman French poured the word into England: souper entered Middle English as soupen, "to eat the evening meal," while native suppen continued to mean "to sip." The two were near-homophones, spelled and sounded alike within a few generations, and they fused into a single verb sup with a single past tense, supped. Middle English literature uses the merged verb for both sipping and dining; the overlap was not an accident but a re-union of two branches that had never been far apart.

6. Shakespeare and "supped full with horrors." The verb reaches its literary summit in Macbeth’s famous line, "I have supp’d full with horrors" (Macbeth, V.5). The image treats experience as a meal: the tyrant has taken in so much terror that it can no longer affect him. The metaphor works precisely because sup still meant "consume, take in," and the passive saturation — supped FULL — leans on the meal sense. It is the word’s most quoted moment, and it cemented sup in the literary register of English.

7. The proverbial long spoon. The 17th century fixed another cultural monument: "He that sups with the devil must have a long spoon," a warning against dangerous intimacy. The proverb borrows the supper table as a stage for moral caution — sitting down to sup with an evil partner is like dining with the devil himself, and only distance (the long spoon) makes it safe. "To sup with the devil" survives as an idiom for risky collaboration.

8. Register drift: from everyday to literary. As English dining vocabulary reorganized — dinner moving from midday to evening and supper competing with it — sup and supped slowly left the kitchen of everyday speech. By the 19th and 20th centuries they were chiefly literary, historical, or rustic, used by novelists for period flavor or homely warmth. C.S. Lewis uses "supped" in Prince Caspian precisely for this reason: the archaic, cozy, candle-lit flavor of "Here they supped on food they had brought with them" suits the tone of a medieval-style wilderness camp in Narnia.

9. The verb’s two faces today. Modern supped still carries both senses — ate the evening meal, and drank in small sips — though both are now rare in speech. The sipping sense survives in the careful reading of older texts; the dining sense survives in fiction and in the familiar noun supper. The word’s twin etymology is a living record of how the European evening meal was once, quite literally, a bowl of soup to be supped.

Summary of the drift: "to take liquid/sip" (PIE) → Germanic *sūpaną → Old English sūpan → Middle English suppen = "to sip"; and in parallel: Frankish *sopp- "sop" → Old French soupe / souper → Middle English soupen = "to eat the evening meal"; the two merge into one sup whose past is supped. From sacred Vedic drink to Shakespearean metaphor to a Narnian campfire, the word has always been about liquid food — and the meal that was made of it.
