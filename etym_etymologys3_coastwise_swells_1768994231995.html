coastwise is a two-part word whose parts carry very different histories, joined only in the seventeenth century. The noun coast begins with the PIE root *kost- "bone, rib," a root whose reflexes are scattered across the western edge of the Indo-European world: Latin costa "rib, side," and on the Balto-Slavic side Old Church Slavonic kostь, Russian kost', Polish kość, all meaning "bone." The deep image is anatomical — the ribs curving around the trunk, protecting the vitals — and Latin simply extended "rib" to mean "side, wall," and then to the "side" of a body of land. It was in this secondary sense that the word entered Old French as coste and then English in the fourteenth century as coast, at first meaning "rib" or "side" and only gradually settling on "seashore." The maritime specialization (the shore as the side or rib of the land, its defensive edge) is a Renaissance development, and its ghost survives in modern anatomical costal "of the ribs" and in the culinary cutlet, from French côtelette, a "little rib" of meat. The sound changes along the way are regular: PIE *k remained /k/ in Latin and French; the Old French coste passed into Middle English as coste, and the Great Vowel Shift and later shortening produced the modern /kəʊst/. Semantically the drift is one of synecdoche: the part (the rib of a body) becomes the side of the land, and the side of the land becomes the shore — a body's flank imagined as the nation's.

The second part, -wise, descends from PIE *weid- "to see, to know," the same root that gave Latin vidēre "to see," Greek eîdos "form," Sanskrit véda "knowledge," and English wit and wise. In Germanic, *weid- produced *wīsan "manner, way," because a "way" of doing something was conceived as a way of seeing it — a manner as a standpoint or a fashion of looking. Old English wīse "manner, fashion" hardened into the adverbial suffix -wise, so that a word like coastwise literally means "in the manner of, along the course of the coast." The marriage of an Italic anatomical noun and a Germanic verbal suffix is entirely typical of English word-formation, and the resulting word is a quiet miniature of the language's layering: a Latin rib from PIE *kost-, joined to an English suffix from PIE *weid-, both roots having independent careers across half a dozen branches of the family. The word's first recorded life was maritime and administrative — coastwise trade, coastwise navigation — and American cabotage law gave it a lasting technical citizenship that no synonym quite shares.

  • c. 1300 — coast enters English (via Old French) meaning "rib, side," then "seashore."
  • 17th–18th c. — coastwise is coined in nautical usage, "along the coast," as an adverb and adjective.
  • 18th–19th c. — the adjective enters shipping and American law: coastwise trade, coastwise commerce, coastwise shipping, the coastwise laws restricting domestic carriage to U.S. vessels.
  • 20th c. — -wise extends its manner sense into "in respect of" (weather-wise), making coastwise one of a productive family of new-formations.

swells, by contrast, is a word of native Germanic stock whose biography runs from the body's swelling to the sea's heaving. Its root, PIE *swel- "to swell," is one of the oldest Indo-European words for expansion, a root that conceived growth as pressure from within — the same image in Latin sollus? no, in the Germanic strong verb. The sound shape is stable across four millennia: PIE *sw- remained in Germanic, and the vowel settled as *e in the present, *a in the preterite, *u in the participle — a classic ablaut pattern that English inherited in swell, swelled/swollen. Old English swellan meant "to swell, to become swollen," used of wounds, seas, and pride alike; the spiritual sense ("puffed up" with pride, cf. "swollen with anger") is already Old English, and it is the root of the figurative career of the word. In the sixteenth century the noun broke off from the verb — a swell "a rise, a wave" — and by Shakespeare the oceanic sense was fully alive: the swells of the sea, the swelling of the heart. The nineteenth century added two remarkable offshoots. First, groundswell, the deep, slow motion of the sea felt far from shore, became the political figure for a rising tide of opinion, a metaphor so natural it now belongs to the vocabulary of elections. Second, the noun turned social: a swell was a fashionable, dashing person, and swell! an exclamation of approval — the roundness of the rising wave translated into social polish and delight, a sense that American English still keeps in its affectionate, dated flavor. The medical swelling (an abnormal enlargement) belongs to the same root, and so does the homophone's caution: swelter and sultry come from a second PIE *swel- "to burn," a different root that only coincidentally shares the spelling.

  • Old English — swellan "to swell, expand"; the verb is already in the prose of Ælfric.
  • 14th–16th c. — the verb widens to seas, sails, rivers, and feelings; swollen participles appear in Chaucer and Shakespeare.
  • 16th–17th c. — the noun swell breaks off: a rise, an increase, a wave; the oceanic sense "long unbroken wave" is fixed by Shakespeare.
  • 19th c. — groundswell goes political; a swell becomes a fashionable person and swell! an American exclamation of approval.
  • Contemporary — the oceanographic sense (a long, unbreaking deep-water undulation) is the technical core; the slang senses survive as deliberate color.

In the card's sentence, the pairing is perfect: coastwise — the Latinate, charted, directional word of navigation — and swells — the native, deep, elemental word of the sea itself. The two words rehearse the whole history of maritime English: the learned and legal drawn from Latin and French, the physical and oceanic drawn from the Germanic core.
