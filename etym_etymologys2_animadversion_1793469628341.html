PIE Roots (reconstructed, c. 3500 BCE)
*h₂enh₁- (breathe, soul) + *wer- / *wert- (turn)
‘soul, mind’ + ‘to turn, bend’
↓
Early Latin Phrase (c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE)
animum advertere
‘to turn the mind to, pay attention, heed’
↓
Classical Latin Compound Verb (c. 1st c. BCE)
animadvertere
‘to take notice of, perceive; censure, punish’
↓
Classical Latin Noun of Action (c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE)
animadversio (stem animadversion-)
‘investigation, mental notice; censure, reproof’
↓
Middle French (c. 1500–1550)
animadversion
‘observation, censure, reprimand’
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1560–1580)
animadversion
‘judicial punishment; formal critical remark’
↓
Modern English (current)
animadversion
‘censorious comment, adverse criticism, reproof’

Note: The word represents a classic Latin univerbation: the syntactical phrase animum advertere ("to direct the spirit/mind toward") fused into the single verb animadvertere. In Roman jurisprudence, to "turn the mind" toward an infraction came naturally to mean investigating it, passing judgment, and executing chastisement.
