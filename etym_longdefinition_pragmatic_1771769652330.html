When the text describes Gibbon's politics as prudent and pragmatic, the adjective does more than mean "practical." It characterizes a temperament that measures political questions by consequences, stability, and workable order rather than by abstract purity or revolutionary theory. In this literary-biographical frame, pragmatism is aligned with prudence: a preference for what preserves the fabric of society over what satisfies an ideological blueprint. The tone is approving but cool — praising level-headed realism, not romantic zeal. Gibbon appears as someone who would rather support the arrangement that works than the doctrine that merely inspires.

Primary modern sense. Pragmatic (adjective) means dealing with things sensibly and realistically, in a way based on practical rather than theoretical considerations: a pragmatic approach, solution, politician, or compromise. The focus is on consequences and workability — a pragmatic person asks "does it work?" rather than "is it doctrinally pure?"

Full range of senses.

  • The everyday sense (dominant today): practical, results-oriented, sensible in the face of real constraints; standard educated English, frequent in politics, business, management, and daily life.
  • The philosophical sense (capital-P Pragmatism): the American philosophical school (Peirce, James, Dewey, late 19th–early 20th century) holding that the meaning and truth of ideas are to be judged by their practical consequences; the adjective describes a method of judging by outcomes.
  • The political-historical sense: pragmatic politics = realism and the art of the possible; the antonym is ideological or doctrinaire. This is the sense attributed to Gibbon.
  • The older and now-rare sense "officious, meddlesome": 16th–17th century English could call a busybody or an interfering person "pragmatic"; the sense lingers in historical dictionaries and echoes Latin pragmaticus "skilled in business."
  • The legal-constitutional sense: "pragmatic sanction" — a formal decree by a sovereign on a matter of state (e.g., the Pragmatic Sanction of 1713); a technical, near-fossilized usage.
  • The linguistic-philosophical sense (pragmatics): in linguistics, pragmatics is the study of how context contributes to meaning in use (speech acts, implicature); the adjective pragmatic is used of such context-dependent analysis — a technical-jargon extension of the same "of use / of practice" family.
  • The international-relations sense: pragmatic foreign policy = realpolitik, consequence-driven diplomacy rather than crusading ideology.

Semantic bridge. Greek prâgma means "deed, act, affair, matter"; pragmatikós means "fit for business, skilled in affairs, relating to facts." The family runs from "doing" → "having to do with deeds and affairs" → "competent in the practical world" → "guided by practical results." The old "meddlesome" sense arose because being busy with others' affairs (pragmata) could be judged as interference; the modern approving sense selects the competence rather than the intrusion.

Boundaries and register. Practical is the broad everyday word; pragmatic adds a more deliberate philosophy of judging by results. Realistic stresses accurate perception; pragmatic stresses effective action. Utilitarian brings a specific ethical theory; expedient hints at moral compromise. Register is standard educated English; the tone ranges from complimentary (sensible, unillusioned) to mildly critical when it implies the sacrifice of principle.
