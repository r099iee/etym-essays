Note on philological history: Michiel de Vaan notes that Proto-Italic *aikʷo- is the ancestor of Latin aequus, though exact cognates outside Italic remain contested (possible Baltic cognates like Old Prussian ayky "quickly/levelly"). In Classical Latin, aequābilis was famously popularized by Cicero in his philosophical treatises to describe the harmonious, unshakeable Stoic soul (motus aequabilis). The word was borrowed directly into English during the 17th century, where Isaac Newton immortalized its adverbial form aequabiliter in his Principia to describe the unvarying, absolute flow of mathematical time. Over the 18th century, English lexicographers drew a sharp semantic line between equable (temperamentally calm or physically uniform) and equitable (legally fair, just, and impartial).
