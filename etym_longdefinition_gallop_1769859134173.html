gallop: the fastest pace of a horse or other quadruped — a four-beat bounding gait with a moment of total suspension when all four hooves leave the ground — and, as a verb, to move or ride at that pace; figuratively, any rapid, forceful, headlong advance. Primary sense — the equine gait. The gallop is the horse's fastest gait, a four-beat rhythm in which the footfalls follow a diagonal sequence, the animal gathers its legs beneath it, launches into a brief moment of full suspension in mid-air, lands on a hind foot, and springs forward again. It is distinct from the walk (four even beats, no suspension), the trot (a two-beat diagonal gait, springy but never fully airborne), and the canter (a three-beat gait, often called "a slow gallop", with a longer, smoother ride). In a true gallop the horse extends fully — neck stretched, back lengthening — and covers ground in long, sweeping, bounding strides; the rider feels a surging, pounding rhythm rather than the smooth sway of the canter. Verb use. As a verb, to gallop is to move or ride at this pace: "the horse galloped across the field", "she galloped away to fetch help". The verb applies to other four-footed animals (a dog or a deer can gallop) and, loosely, to any fast, bounding, headlong movement of people or things. Figurative extensions. Because the gallop is fast, powerful, and hard to stop, the word extends to anything that advances rapidly and forcefully:
  • "Galloping consumption" (19th c.) — tuberculosis advancing swiftly and fatally; the classic medical metaphor of the age.
  • "Galloping inflation" — inflation rising rapidly and uncontrollably; the standard economic metaphor.
  • "At a gallop" / "in a gallop" — doing something at maximum speed, hurriedly and even recklessly.
  • "Gallop through" — to read, work, or perform something very quickly and superficially.
  • "A galloping heart" — a fast, racing heartbeat, from exertion or emotion.
Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • The fastest four-beat bounding gait of a horse or other quadruped — primary equestrian sense.
  • To move or ride at that gait (verb) — general.
  • Any rapid, headlong, bounding movement — extended literal use.
  • Figuratively, a rapid and forceful advance (of disease, prices, events) — 19th c. onward.
  • To do something very quickly and superficially ("gallop through") — colloquial.
  • Nautical (archaic): "the gallop of the sea" — the rapid, bounding motion of waves.
  • A fast dance (galop): a lively 19th-century ballroom dance in quick 2/4 time, borrowed from the French form of the same word.
Gait differentiation:
  • Gallop vs canter: the canter is a smooth, controlled three-beat gait of moderate speed; the gallop is four-beat, faster, and fully airborne — the canter is essentially a restrained gallop.
  • Gallop vs trot: the trot is a steady two-beat diagonal gait without airborne suspension; the gallop involves bounding leaps with all four feet off the ground.
  • Gallop vs sprint: sprint applies broadly to short bursts of maximum-speed running by humans or animals without specifying gait; gallop names a specific, structured equine gait.
  • Gallop vs stampede: a stampede is a chaotic, panic-driven mass flight; a gallop is controlled, rhythmic, and intentional.
  • Gallop vs lope: a lope is a relaxed, easy, long-stride canter-like pace; a gallop is full-out, effortful maximum speed.
  • Gallop vs race: race implies competition against others or a clock; gallop names the gait itself, regardless of competition.
The context use. "The horse broke into a gallop as they reached the open field" — the verb phrase "break into" captures the sudden release of pent-up energy: the moment the animal abandons the controlled rhythms of walk and trot and surrenders to full, bounding speed. The open field supplies the space; the gallop supplies the freedom, urgency, and exhilaration. The transition is both a physical and an emotional event — a shift in momentum and temperature, from restraint to release.
