slap (noun, verb, and adverb) — as a noun, a blow struck with the open hand, flat against a surface; as a verb, to strike in that way, or to put or throw something down flat and emphatically; as an adverb, directly, exactly, right against, with nothing in between. In this passage the word is used adverbially in the phrase "slap up against the gate," and it means directly, exactly, right against—so close that there is no gap at all between the orchard and the gate. The nuance the word contributes is abrupt, emphatic, almost physical contact: the layout Edmund describes would put the orchard flush against the boundary, with no intervening margin of ground. Edmund uses it to insist how absurd the whole picture is—"we wouldn't have been such fools" as to plant the orchard right against the gate—and the word's brevity and force carry his indignation. The tone is colloquial and slightly testy, the language of a boy who finds the evidence before him insulting to common sense; the word "slap" makes the impossibility vivid by making the contact brutally concrete. It is worth noting that the same word, in this adverbial use, belongs to a distinctly British idiom family (slap-bang, slap in the middle), so it also sets the voice of the book firmly in its English setting. 1. Primary senses
  • Noun: a sharp blow with the open hand or something flat—the classic "slap in the face"; also the sound of such a blow. From this concrete sense come the idioms "a slap in the face" (an insult or humiliation) and "a slap on the wrist" (a mild punishment).
  • Verb: to strike with the open hand; more broadly, to put, place, or throw something down flat and carelessly ("slap it on the table," "slap on some paint"). The verb is recorded from about 1600.
  • Adverb (the sense of this card): directly, exactly, right against—"slap up against the gate," "slap in the middle," "slap into the wall." The adverbial use is recorded from the 1670s and arose because a slap makes immediate, complete, face-to-face contact with its target: the logic of impact was transferred from the blow to position in space, with nothing in between.
2. Secondary and figurative senses
  • Idioms of rebuke and humiliation: "a slap in the face" (a deliberate insult), "a slap on the wrist" (a token or inadequate punishment), "slap down" (to rebuff or crush someone's argument), "slapdash" (hasty and careless, originally "with a slap and a dash"—in one hasty stroke).
  • Compounds: slap-bang (1786, "headlong, exactly," from slap + bang), slapstick (18th century, from the Harlequin's two-lath "slap stick" of the commedia dell'arte, hence the whole comic genre), slap-happy (1936, "dazed from blows," later "cheerfully foolish").
  • Music and modern slang: in jazz and popular music, "slap" names the percussive technique of striking or snapping a string instrument (slap bass, slap guitar), and "slap" as a verb meaning "to sound excellent" is a modern American slang extension ("that track slaps"). On the internet, "slap" is used for a meme of a comedic blow; "slapdash" in design and "slap" in cooking (to slap dough) stay close to the flat-emphasis original.
  • Register: the adverb is distinctly British and colloquial; a modern speaker outside the idiom would say "right" or "straight," so choosing "slap" deliberately keeps the flat, sharp image of the palm.
3. Semantic bridge
  • The historical bridge across all senses is the flat, sudden, complete contact of a blow: because a slap lands instantly and covers its target with nothing in between, the word came to mean any instant, complete, face-to-face contact—first physical (the blow), then spatial (directly against), then evaluative (a complete insult or rebuff). The emphatic compounds (slap-bang, slapdash) and the modern slang senses all preserve this core idea of suddenness and totality.
