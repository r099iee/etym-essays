Tarahumaras is the English plural of Tarahumara, the long-established Spanish exonym for the Rarámuri Indigenous people of the Sierra Madre Occidental in Chihuahua, Mexico, and for their Uto-Aztecan language. In this passage it simply means the Rarámuri bird-catchers: the Tarahumaras strung kernels on tough buried fibres, birds swallowed the kernels, and the people then took and ate the birds. In short, Tarahumaras here means members of the Rarámuri people acting as fowlers.

The name carries a layered identity. The people call themselves Rarámuri, widely glossed as runners on foot, those who run fast or light feet, though linguists debate whether the everyday sense is simply people or humans as opposed to outsiders. Spaniards heard this as Tarahumara, which became the official Mexican ethnonym, language name and Sierra label. English borrowed it in the 1870s and pluralises it either as Tarahumara (zero plural) or Tarahumaras (regular -s), the latter chosen here to count the actors.

Detailed senses and context:

  • 1. Core ethnonymic sense — members of the people: the primary meaning; an Indigenous maize-farming, canyon-dwelling group of roughly seventy thousand, famed for rarajipari foot-throwing ball races, tesgüino feasts, violin music and persistence hunting; connotes endurance, mountain autonomy and communal labour.
  • 2. Linguistic sense — speakers of the language: by extension Tarahumara denotes the Uto-Aztecan language with several dialects; to say the Tarahumaras caught birds implies Rarámuri-speaking households practising traditional snaring, not Spanish settlers.
  • 3. Technological sense in this passage — kernel-snare fowlers: stringing kernels on tough fibres half buried under ground so birds ingest the line and are held for capture; exemplifies passive snaring ingenuity parallel to narcotic fishing and reed-breathing duck drowning in surrounding sentences; the chain kernels to birds to people mirrors food-web capture.
  • 4. Sporting emblem sense — the runners: not active here but inseparable from the name; foot relays where teams kick balls for hours, women driving ringed hoops, children running mountain paths to school; modern ultra-running celebrates Tarahumaras as barefoot philosophers, a romanticisation absent from this spare technical sentence.
  • 5. Administrative and geographic sense: Tarahumara as Sierra Tarahumara region, Tarahumara municipality, mission district; plural Tarahumaras can thus evoke governed subjects as well as kin groups, depending on colonial versus Indigenous frame.
  • 6. Endonym versus exonym politics: contemporary respectful usage prefers Rarámuri for people (plural Rarámuri or Rarámuris) and reserves Tarahumara for language and place; Tarahumaras in older texts is not slur but dated exonymic plural, now often revised in reprints.
  • 7. Register and grammar: proper noun with capital T, pronounced with Spanish-derived stress; takes regular English -s in this card to mark two occurrences as (1) and (2); zero-plural Tarahumara remains equally correct in dictionaries; adjective form Tarahumara music, Tarahumara country.
  • 8. Semantic bridge summary: self as runner-person to Spanish ear as tribal label to English count noun for actors in a snaring scene; the bridge is contact phonetics plus plural morphology, not any change in who is denoted — the same canyon people throughout, seen first as kin, then as mission subjects, then as ethnographic examples.

In the immersion sentence the function is purely actorial: who set the kernel lines and who ate the birds. Recognising Tarahumaras as the Rarámuri people prevents misreading it as a technique name or place, and restores the human reciprocity — kernels offered, birds taken, people fed — that the source counts as primitive ingenuity.
