The evolution of abstruse: from thrusting away, to hiding, to the depths of thought. No English word better preserves its Latin spatial origin while living an entirely mental life. Abstruse began as a participle meaning "thrust away, concealed" and ended as the standard adjective for what resists the understanding.

1. The Latin engine: thrusting and hiding

The chain starts with the Indo-European root *trewd-, "to thrust, push", which produced Latin trūdere ("to thrust"). Latin habitually built verbs of privation and separation with the prefix ab- ("away from"): as ab-ducere is "to lead away", so ab-strūdere is "to thrust away". Its past participle abstrūsus — "thrust away, concealed, hidden" — is first attested in classical prose (Cicero uses abstrūsus both physically, of things hidden away, and already metaphorically, of things kept secret). The spatial core is a vault or a deep recess: what is abstruse is pushed back out of the light.

2. The borrowing: 1520s–1600s

English borrowed the participle directly from Latin in the 1520s, initially as a physical word: "abstruse caves", "abstruse corners" — hidden places. Within a generation the figurative sense took over: Gerard de Malynes (1622) and Robert Burton ("abstruse questions" in The Anatomy of Melancholy, 1621) use it for what lies beyond common comprehension. The transition is seamless because Latin itself had already made it: what is hidden from the eyes becomes, by the same metaphor, hidden from the mind's eye.

3. The philosophical century: 17th–18th centuries

The 17th century made abstruse a working word of English philosophy and natural philosophy. John Locke speaks of "abstruse and new notions"; the mathematicians of the Royal Society applied it to the higher reaches of their science. In the 18th century it became a touchstone of Enlightenment debate: David Hume's famous opening warning in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739) against "the abstruse philosophy" sets the word at the centre of a methodological quarrel — is philosophical depth admirable or a species of vain obscurity? From this point abstruse carries a double charge: reverence for genuine depth, suspicion of pretended depth. That ambiguity persists to this day.

4. The 19th century: literary consolidation

Romantic and Victorian prose kept the word for what requires special training: "abstruse science", "abstruse points of law". Wordsworth's own usage in the source context of this card — "patient thought / Abstruse" — is a deliberate placement of a Latinate, technical term at the rhyme of a colloquial passage, dignifying intellectual labour as a quasi-monastic discipline. In the 19th century the original physical sense died out entirely; every modern use is figurative.

5. Sound and form: a stable borrowing

Unlike most loanwords, abstruse has hardly changed since its arrival: 16th-century /æbˈstruːs/ is essentially modern /əbˈstruːs/. Its formal, four-syllable profile — even in the common pronunciation that stresses the second syllable after a light schwa — marks it immediately as bookish. Derivatives (abstruseness, abstrusity) multiplied in the 17th century but remain rare; the adjective virtually monopolises the family.

6. Today

Modern use is confined to formal and academic registers: "abstruse theoretical physics", "abstruse legal doctrine", "abstruse theology". In speech it is uncommon, and when it appears it often carries the 18th-century suspicion — "needlessly abstruse" — as often as admiration. The word's career is a miniature of Latin lexical borrowing into English: a spatial participle, a philosophical metaphor, a badge of learning, and finally a polished adjective whose core image — truth thrust out of reach — remains visible to anyone who knows Latin.

  • c. 4500–2500 BCE: PIE *trewd- "to thrust".
  • 3rd–2nd c. BCE: Latin trūdere "to thrust".
  • 1st c. BCE: abstrūdere "to thrust away"; participle abstrūsus "hidden" (Cicero).
  • 1520s: borrowed into English, physical sense.
  • late 16th c.: figurative "hard to understand" prevails.
  • 17th–18th c.: the word of philosophy; Hume's "abstruse philosophy" (1739).
  • 19th c.–: purely figurative; formal register only.
