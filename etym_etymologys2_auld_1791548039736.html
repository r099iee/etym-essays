Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*h₂eltós
"grown, nourished, adult"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*aldaz
"grown, mature, of age, old"
↓
Old English (Anglian dialect) (8th c.)
ald
"old, ancient, of great age"
↓
Early Scots / Northumbrian ME (13th c.)
ald
"old, former, aged"
↓
Middle Scots (15th c.)
auld
"old, former; familiar, beloved"
↓
Modern Scots / English Dialect (18th c.–pres.)
auld
"old, ancient, former; familiar"

Note: Anglian 'ald' did not undergo the West Saxon breaking to 'eald' (which produced Southern Middle English 'old' via vowel rounding). Instead, Northern English and Scots retained the vowel, developing the characteristic digraph spelling 'au/aw' representing the historic vocalization of /l/ and distinct northern phonology.
