- 17th century Civil War coinage - coined in England during the 1640s for a supporter of King Charles I against Parliament; the word is born partisan, charged with loyalty and courage, and, in the Parliamentarian view, obstinacy.
- 17th-18th century party transfer - the label migrates to other dynastic struggles, applied to the Jacobite loyalists and, later in the 18th century, to French supporters of the Bourbon monarchy.
- 19th century politics and temperament - still a political label for defenders of monarchy in France and elsewhere, but also loosened into a temperamental word: Carlyle's "royalist volition" names a will that is proud, imperious, and self-possessed, wearing the dignity of a queen without any party programme.
- Contemporary use - a historical-political term for a supporter of monarchy, neutral in scholarship but still capable of carrying a romantic, aristocratic flavour in figurative use.
