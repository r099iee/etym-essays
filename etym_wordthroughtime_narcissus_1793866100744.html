- Pre-Hellenic Antiquity - [Anatolian / Mediterranean Botanical Substrate] - Originated as an indigenous pre-Greek flower name ending in the substrate suffix -issos, associated folk-etymologically with Greek narkē (numbness, torpor) due to the plant's heavy, intoxicating fragrance and toxic alkaloid narcotic properties. - 8th to 1st century BCE - [Greek Mythological Parable of Hubris] - Formulated in Boeotian and Hellenistic myth as a strikingly beautiful youth who spurned all lovers, suffered the curse of Nemesis, and wasted away gazing at his own reflection in a pool on Mount Helicon; perceived as a moral warning against sexual hubris and emotional coldness. - 1st century CE - [Ovidian Psychological Masterpiece] - Codified in Book III of Ovid’s Metamorphoses, transforming the myth into an immortal psychological allegory of tragic self-deception, fatal reflection, and unrequited love alongside the nymph Echo. - 18th century - [Linnaean Botanical Taxonomy] - Adopted by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 as the formal scientific genus name Narcissus for the family Amaryllidaceae, classifying daffodils, jonquils, and paperwhites. - Late 19th to Early 20th century - [Psychoanalytic Terminology] - Adopted by Havelock Ellis, Paul Näcke, and Sigmund Freud to create the foundational psychological concepts of narcissism and the narcissistic neurosis, shifting the word into clinical psychiatry. - Contemporary - [Psychiatric Diagnosis and Cultural Metaphor] - Functioning today as the root of Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD) in the DSM-5, while serving in popular culture as a universal emblem of vanity, self-absorption, and modern digital selfie obsession.
