- 1646 — “circuitous” is coined in English from Latin circuitus “a going round”; initially literal: a path or journey that goes round instead of straight — learned, technical word.
- 17th–18th c. — used in travel, surveying, and natural history for roundabout routes; still rare and bookish, confined to educated prose.
- 19th c. — extended metaphorically to argument and reasoning: “a circuitous argument”; the word now describes indirectness of mind as well as of road — rhetorical register.
- 20th c. — extended to processes, procedures, and speech: “circuitous bureaucracy”, “circuitous wording”; acquires a mildly disapproving tone of waste and inefficiency.
- 21st c. — remains formal and literary; used of routes, arguments, and paths in legal, academic, and bureaucratic contexts; everyday speech prefers “roundabout” or “indirect”.
