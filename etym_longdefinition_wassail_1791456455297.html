Wassail is a word that arrives in English carrying an entire winter ritual inside it. At its core it names a hot, spiced, alcoholic drink — traditionally ale or cider mulled with apples, sugar, cinnamon, nutmeg, ginger and cloves, sometimes enriched with eggs or cream — drunk as a festive toast, above all at Christmas and Twelfth Night. But the drink is only the most concrete layer of a word that has also meant a greeting, a drinking cry, a carol, a night of licensed revelry and, as a verb, the act of going from door to door singing and asking for hospitality.

Primary sense: the drink and the bowl that holds it. The commonest modern use is nominal: "a bowl of wassail", "the wassail cup", "wassail was steaming on the sideboard". The vessel itself is often called a wassail bowl, a large communal cup or bowl passed from hand to hand, sometimes fitted with a lid and floating toast. Recipes vary sharply by region: in the West Country cider and roasted apples dominate, producing the variant known as lambswool; in the north and Midlands ale, sugar and spice are the base; wealthier households used wine, sack or brandy. The constant is not the liquor but the heat, the spice and the sharing.

Secondary sense: the toast, the cry and the occasion. Historically wassail is not first of all the drink but the words spoken over it. Old Norse ves heill and Old English wæs hāl both mean "be in health", a salutation offered as a cup was raised; the expected reply was drinc hail, "drink health". From this ritual the word acquired the sense of "a carousing salutation" and then of the carousal itself. The semantic slide is a textbook one: word spoken → object used → event enacted. Hence a wassail can be the drink, the bowl, the toast, the party, or the round of house-visiting.

  • Drink sense: the spiced ale or cider itself, and by metonymy the bowl that contains it.
  • Greeting sense: "wassail!" as a health-cry, roughly equivalent to "cheers!", "skaal!", "prosit!" or "slàinte!".
  • Occasion sense: the festive gathering at which the bowl is passed, often on Christmas Eve, Twelfth Night or New Year.
  • Custom sense: wassailing, the house-to-house singing visit, from which the carol tradition grew.

Tertiary sense: orchard wassailing. A distinct folk survival is the apple-tree wassail of the cider counties — Sussex, Somerset, Devon, Herefordshire, Kent and parts of the west. On Twelfth Night, or on "Old Twelfth Night" (17 January under the old calendar), villagers carry a bowl or jug of cider to the orchard, pour it on the roots, lay toast soaked in cider in the branches, fire guns or beat pans to wake the trees and drive off evil spirits, and sing a versified appeal for a good crop. The word here means neither drink nor party but a calendar rite of fertility. The custom was documented by nineteenth-century folklorists and has been consciously revived since the 1970s, now often by cider-makers, morris sides and neo-pagan groups.

Verb and participial uses. "To wassail" means to carouse, to drink healths, or to go singing from door to door. "Wassailing" is the activity and "wassailers" the singers; a choir may advertise a "wassail evening" and a pub a "wassail night". The finite verb is now rare and slightly self-conscious, while the gerund is the liveliest part of the paradigm.

Modern and commercial senses. Craft cider makers, heritage brands, Christmas markets and pagan revivalists have turned wassail into a marker of authenticity: it sells cider, candles, spice blends, ceramic bowls and seasonal experiences. The connotation is warm, communal, faintly archaic and alcohol-suffused. It is also available for jokes, as in the present context, where "two-penn'orth of wassail in the smoking-room" means a modest glass of something cheering after exertion — the word's ceremonial weight played against the smallness of the reward.

Register and usage notes. Present-day wassail is marked: literary, seasonal and a little theatrical. It is rare in ordinary speech outside December, and when it appears at other times it is usually ironic or deliberately picturesque. It has no productive derivatives beyond wassailer and wassailing, and it is one of the few English words whose original grammatical meaning ("be healthy!") is still recoverable from its form if one knows the Norse.
