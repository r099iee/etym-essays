courteous: polite, respectful, and considerate in manner toward others; showing thoughtfulness and good breeding. Primary sense — considerate politeness. To be courteous is to treat people with politeness that is more than skin-deep: courteous behaviour anticipates the needs and feelings of others and responds with kindness and respect. A courteous host makes sure everyone is comfortable; a courteous reply is not merely non-rude but genuinely considerate. The word sits slightly above plain "politeness": it implies habitual, active thoughtfulness rather than a one-off observance of etiquette. The courtly heritage. The word's historical core is the royal court. Courteous literally meant "having the manners of the court" — the polished, refined, chivalric behaviour cultivated among the nobles who surrounded a medieval king or lord. At court, speech was regulated, deference was ritualized, and courtesy manuals (the cortesie, the courtesy books) taught children of rank how to eat, bow, and speak. To be courteous was originally to possess this aristocratic polish; the modern word has democratized that ideal, but the "court" image is still visible in the spelling court-eous. Nuance — genuine consideration vs outward form. A key distinction runs through the word's history: courteous can mean either the inner disposition of consideration or the outer behaviour of polish. Austen's sentence plays on both — Sir William Lucas "was all attention to everybody," courteous "by nature," not merely by manner. True courtesy is taken to be inoffensive, friendly, and obliging, whereas mere etiquette can be hollow. Full polysemy map:
  • Polite, respectful, considerate in manner — modern dominant sense.
  • Refined, polished, well-bred (of manners and speech) — historical and still live.
  • Courtly, chivalrous, gallant (of the age of chivalry) — archaic-historical sense.
  • Obliging and attentive to everyone's comfort — the Austen-context sense.
  • Of behaviour: decorous, ceremonious, ceremonially respectful.
  • Distinct but related: courtesy (the noun — an act of politeness, a kindness; also the "courtesy title" of nobility and "courtesy of the road" conventions), courtesan (a court-attendant turned courtly mistress — an unrelated historical offshoot of the same court-word).
The semantic bridge. Latin cohors (enclosed yard) became the medieval court, the enclosed household of a ruler. Because the court was the school of refinement, behaviour "of the court" (curteis) became the standard of good manners, and when the courtly ideal dissolved into general society, courteous became simply a synonym of "polite and considerate" — a striking example of social elevation becoming general social value. Register and comparison:
  • Courteous vs polite: courteous implies genuine consideration; polite implies polished adherence to rules.
  • Courteous vs civil: civil is the minimum of non-rudeness; courteous is actively warm and obliging.
  • Courteous vs gracious: gracious adds warmth and condescension from a position of strength; courteous is more neutral and egalitarian.
  • Collocations: "courteous to everyone," "a courteous reply," "courteous and obliging," "politely courteous."
  • Register: formal but common; slightly more literary and warmer than plain "polite."
