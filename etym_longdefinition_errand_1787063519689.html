Errand (noun) is a word of Germanic stock meaning a short journey undertaken to deliver a message, to fetch or carry something, or to perform some small commission — almost always at the request of, or on behalf of, another person. Its defining semantic core is delegated purpose plus movement: an errand is not an outing for its own sake but a goal-directed round trip performed for someone else's benefit. In the source context from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the Elvenking “knew now the dwarves' errand”, i.e. the secret mission and purpose that had brought the dwarves through Mirkwood; this is the older, weightier sense of the word — the errand as a charge, a purpose, a design — as opposed to the modern colloquial sense of a quick trip to the shops.

Primary senses

  • 1. A short journey for a specific purpose. The everyday, dominant sense. One “runs an errand” or “goes on an errand” — to the post office, the pharmacy, a neighbour's house. The focus is on the physical trip, short in distance and time, with a known task and an expected return.
  • 2. The purpose, commission, or business of such a journey. “He is away on an errand”; “she told him the errand” — here the word names the charge itself rather than the travel. It is this sense — a mission or design — that Tolkien's sentence employs, and it preserves the oldest meaning of the word, “message, tidings, mission”.

Secondary and extended senses

  • A mission of weight or dignity. In formal and literary registers “errand” can denote a significant, even heroic commission: “a knight riding on the king's errand”, “an errand of state”. The delegation is kept, but the scale is raised from the household to the kingdom; the word here approaches “mission” and “quest” without losing its connotation of being sent by a higher authority.
  • Collocations “errand of mercy” and “errand of peace”. Fixed phrases in which the modifier gives the errand a moral or humanitarian colour: a nurse on an errand of mercy; a diplomat on an errand of peace. These preserve a dignified, Victorian-to-Edwardian flavour.
  • “Running errands”. A habitual plural phrase for the small administrative chores of daily life — banking, shopping, school pick-ups. In this collocation the word has been generalised into a category of routine out-of-house tasks.
  • “A fool's errand”. An idiom (first recorded 1656) for a hopeless, self-defeating undertaking — being sent to do something that cannot be done. The errand here is the messenger's task, and the joke is that the sender (or the task itself) is a fool; the phrase is fixed and no longer decomposes semantically.
  • Historical agent nouns. “Errand-boy”, “errand-girl”, “errand-runner” named the low-status servants who ran household and shop errands from the 17th to the early 20th century. These compounds now read as period terms, and their eclipse marks the decline of domestic service and the rise of self-service retail.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge

The development of “errand” is a classic case of narrowing and social de-escalation. In Old English, ærende meant “message, tidings, mission” — the report or charge borne by a messenger, and the word carried real weight in a world of royal dispatches and church missions. Through the Middle Ages the sense broadened to any business one travelled to transact, and the word's tie to travel became complete. In the early modern period, as household service expanded, the errand became the archetypal servant's duty, and the word's register sank from formal to menial. Modern usage resolves the tension by register: the ordinary sense is the humble chore-trip, while the “mission” sense survives in literary, ceremonial, and religious contexts — exactly the use Tolkien gives it, where “errand” preserves its ancient dignity as the purpose of a perilous journey.

Usage notes

  • The typical verb phrase is run an errand (informal-to-neutral) or go on an errand (slightly more formal); “do an errand” is chiefly American.
  • The word is countable: one errand, several errands.
  • It never takes the sense of an internal task done in place; an errand always implies a journey away from and back to a base.
  • Modern IT usage: “errand” is occasionally repurposed as a playful term for a micro-service or a small automated fetch task, a loose technical metaphor built on the same “go and fetch” core.
