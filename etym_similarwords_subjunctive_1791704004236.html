• Indicative: Similar because both represent foundational grammatical moods that govern finite verb forms in Indo-European linguistics; differs because the indicative asserts factual reality, objective observations, and historical occurrences, whereas the subjunctive expresses non-factual, hypothetical, or desired states.
  • Imperative: Similar because both convey directive force, urgency, or volition rather than neutral statements of fact; differs because the imperative issues direct commands or entreaties to a second person ("Listen!"), whereas the subjunctive embeds demands, hypotheses, or wishes within complex subordinate or formal syntactical structures.
  • Conditional: Similar because both frequently collaborate to express hypothetical scenarios and counterfactual speculation (often co-occurring in 'if... then' periods); differs because the conditional typically marks the consequence or apodosis (often utilizing modal auxiliaries like would or could), whereas the subjunctive historically governs the premise, hypothesis, or dependent clause.
  • Optative: Similar because both are grammatical categories dedicated to articulating wishes, hopes, prayers, and aspirations; differs because the optative is an independent morphological mood in Classical Greek and Sanskrit that merged into the subjunctive in Latin and Germanic, surviving in English solely through fossilized formulaic phrases.
  • Irrealis: Similar because both denote actions or events that have not occurred, are contrary to established fact, or exist purely in imagination; differs because irrealis is a broad typological linguistic macro-category covering all non-factual moods, while the subjunctive is a specific Indo-European grammaticalized mood within that continuum.
  • Conjunctive: Similar because the term is used interchangeably with subjunctive in several European traditions (e.g., German Konjunktiv, Italian congiuntivo) and shares the core etymological notion of connecting subordinate thoughts; differs because in English grammar 'conjunctive' specifically denotes parts of speech that link clauses (conjunctions, conjunctive adverbs) rather than the verbal mood itself.
  • Modal Auxiliary: Similar because modern English heavily relies on modal verbs (may, might, should, would) to convey the identical shades of doubt, permission, and contingency once expressed by synthetic subjunctive inflections; differs because modal auxiliaries are separate lexical verbs functioning analytically, whereas the true subjunctive is an inflectional verbal mood.
  • Potential: Similar because both contemplate states that may happen or might be realized; differs because the potential mood explicitly focuses on capability, probability, or inherent possibility, whereas the subjunctive encompasses non-realized mandates, pure counterfactuality, and formal subordination regardless of likelihood.
