At bay is an adverbial phrase meaning held off, checked, and kept from closing in — a threat, enemy, or misfortune confronted and frustrated but not yet defeated. Hounds hold a stag at bay when they surround it and its terrible antlers keep them from the kill; by extension, anything kept at bay is fended off at a distance, still dangerous but denied its prey.

In the source passage Cobbett charges that the yeomanry cavalry drew their swords to keep the reformers ______ while the tax-eaters stripped the country gentlemen of estates and capital. The image is pure hunting: reformers are the cornered beast ringed by barking dogs, and the cavalry are the hounds — yet Cobbett's bitter twist is that the dogs are holding down the wrong quarry, guarding the thieves while menacing the victims. The sheep, he concludes, surrendered the dogs into the wolves hands.

Core meaning in detail: The phrase always describes a standoff with two sides: one side presses forward, the other holds its ground. To hold, keep, or stand at bay is to maintain that distance by active resistance — barking, swords, arguments, sea-walls, medicines. Crucially, at bay promises no victory: the bayed boar may yet gore a hound, the bayed disease may yet break through, the bayed critics may yet return. It is the language of suspense, not of triumph.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Held off, kept from advancing (primary modern sense, figurative from 16th c.): to hold poverty, disease, competitors, or pursuers at bay. The bridge from the hunting field is direct: the barking pack encircling the stag becomes any ring of resistance encircling any threat.
  • A cornered animal facing its pursuers (literal hunting sense, from 14th c.): the stag, boar, or wolf turns with its back to rock or thicket and faces the baying hounds. The bridge needs no metaphor — this is the scene the whole phrase is built on, where bay is the deep barking of the pack.
  • Brought to a standstill, cornered (of persons, 17th c.): an army, a debater, or a criminal held at bay is stopped and forced to face opposition. The bridge reverses the roles: now the human quarry stands like the stag, desperate but still dangerous.
  • Warded off, staved off (of evils and abstractions, 18th–19th c.): to keep ruin, hunger, boredom, or inflation at bay. The bridge generalises the enemy from teeth and swords to any advancing harm; medicine and economics borrow the hunter's vocabulary.
  • At a distance, aloof (weaker social shade): to keep visitors, journalists, or admirers at bay — less a battle than a boundary. The bridge softens fangs into fences: holding off becomes merely holding away, though the phrase keeps a faint growl.
  • The baying itself (root sense of bay): the prolonged deep bark of hounds on the scent, and by extension any clamour of pursuers. The bridge is metonymy: the sound of the confrontation names the confrontation, so that to be at the bay is to be at the centre of the barking ring.

Register and connotation: at bay is vivid standard English, at home in journalism, history, sport, and everyday speech — creditors held at bay, floodwaters held at bay, age held at bay by exercise. It flatters the holder: whoever keeps something at bay is brave, resolute, standing ground against odds. Cobbett exploits exactly this nobility by inversion: the yeomanry's swords have the posture of courage but the function of collusion. The phrase always leaves the ending open, which is why headline writers love it: a danger held at bay today may break through tomorrow.
