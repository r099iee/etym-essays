Hoops are the plural of hoop, a rigid circular band — of metal, wood, plastic, or other stiff material — whose job is to encircle, bind, strengthen, or support. The oldest and defining use is the band that a cooper drives around a barrel: the curved staves of a cask cannot hold together without hoops shrinking around them, so the hoop is literally what binds the whole vessel into one piece. In the source passage the same logic appears in armour-making: a light helm of leather is "strengthened beneath with hoops of steel," circular bands that keep the soft leather cap rigid enough to guard the skull. Wherever a round thing must be gripped, compressed, or reinforced from the outside, a hoop is the tool for the job.

Core senses. Beyond barrels, hoops bind buckets and tubs, brace cart and wagon wheels, tension drum-heads, and in modern engineering give radial strength to concrete pillars, pressure vessels, and storage silos. A hoop is by definition complete — a closed circle, not an arc — and usually rigid, which distinguishes it from a mere cord or flexible band. The word also names objects shaped like such a band: the ring of a basket, the circle of a net, the metal frame of a drum-head, a napkin or curtain holder, the wire ring supporting a garden cloche, and the petticoat hoop of historical fashion.

  • Basketball. Since James Naismith hung the first peach-basket in 1891, the "hoop" has named the rim and net through which the ball must pass; "to shoot hoops" is to play, and in North American slang "hoops" alone now stands for the sport of basketball.
  • Hula hoop and toys. The 1950s "Hula Hoop" craze turned the ancient spinning hoop into a global toy, but children had rolled hoops along streets with sticks for centuries before it.
  • Hooped earrings. Jewellery formed as a complete ring and worn through a pierced ear is called a hoop; large decorative hoops have been a fashion staple in many cultures and eras.
  • Croquet. In British usage the wire arches through which the croquet ball is played are "hoops" (Americans say "wickets").
  • Garden and horticulture. Wire hoops support plant cloches, netting, and row covers over tender crops.
  • Idiom. Circus animals leap through hoops, and to "go through hoops" or "jump through hoops" is to submit to a tiresome series of tests or formalities — the modern bureaucratic idiom drawn from that image.

Grammar and register. As a verb, hoop means to fit or bind with hoops (to hoop a barrel); the participle hooped describes something ringed or banded. In dialect and older usage a hoop could also be a shout or cry (probably a separate word), and the hoopoe bird was once called a "hoop" after its call — both distinct homophones, reminders that a short word may gather several independent lives.

Semantic bridge. All these senses hang on one visual idea: a closed, rigid circle. From barrel to basket to basketball to bureaucratic ordeal, the "hoop" stays recognisably a circle one passes through or around — which is exactly why a single Old English noun has proved so easy to carry forward, unchanged in shape, through a thousand years of technology from cooperage to the NBA.
