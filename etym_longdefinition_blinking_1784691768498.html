Blinking is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb blink, whose core physiological sense is the rapid, usually involuntary closing and reopening of the eyelids and, by extension, the intermittent flashing or flickering of a light source, a candle flame, or a star. In British English, however, the form has developed a second, wholly colloquial life as a mild intensive adjective or adverb: an emphatic, faintly pejorative modifier placed before a noun, equivalent in force to bloody, confounded, or damned, yet considerably gentler than any of them. When William the troll bursts out, "Never a blinking bit of manflesh have we had for long enough", the word is being used in this intensifying sense: it is not the mutton that is blinking but the speaker who is furious. The adjective carries the texture of coarse, working-class, rustic speech — the unpolished idiom of a hungry creature exasperated by a long, monotonous diet of nothing but sheep. It expresses crude frustration and longing without recourse to outright profanity, and in doing so it characterises the trolls as simultaneously menacing and comically earthy: their violence is real, but their vocabulary is that of grumbling labourers rather than epic villains.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The word has two quite separate lives, and a full definition must keep them apart:

  • Literal, of the eye (primary): blinking is the rapid closing and opening of the eyelids — involuntary (the reflex that protects and moistens the eye), deliberate (a wink is a slow, intentional blink), or compulsive; "in the blink of an eye" means in an instant; "without blinking" means without flinching or hesitation.
  • Literal, of light (extended): a light, star, candle, or signal "blinks" when it flashes, flickers, or pulses on and off intermittently — the eye-flicker metaphor transferred to the light-flicker; the "blinking cursor" of computing is the ubiquitous modern application; a lighthouse "blinks" its warning.
  • Colloquial intensive (British, mild oath): "blinking" before a noun acts as a euphemistic intensive — "a blinking nuisance", "this blinking rain", "you blinking fool" — standing in for bloody or damned while staying printable; it belongs to the British family of B-word minced oaths (bloody, bleeding, blooming, blithering, blessed, blinking, ruddy), all sharing the alliterative "b-" and all softening the force of bloody; the intensive is recorded from the 19th century in British dialect and cockney-inflected comic speech.
  • As adverb: intensifying an adjective or verb — "blinking well", "blinking cold", "a blinking good lunch" — the adverbial use reinforces the oath-like function and is characteristic of the same working-class register.
  • Present participle of the verb: "she sat blinking in the sunlight", "the screen was blinking" — the plain participial construction from which the intensive grew and which remains its literal bedrock.

Historical bridge. The route from eye to oath is a classic euphemism. In Victorian and Edwardian Britain, where profanity was heavily policed in print, speakers needing an emphatic swear-word reached for words that merely sounded like — or alliterated with — the forbidden bloody. Blinking, with its "bl-" onset and its innocent literal sense, made a perfect substitute; it also carried a sly self-comment, since a mild oath is exactly the thing that "makes you blink". The intensifier thus keeps a tiny window open onto the physiology it left behind: you might blink in surprise at the word, precisely as the eye blinks at a flash of light.
