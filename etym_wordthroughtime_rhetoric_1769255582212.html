- 14th-century entry - Borrowed via Old French as the classical 'art of the orator'; a scholastic, learned term and one of the three arts of the medieval trivium.
- Renaissance high point - Ciceronian revival makes rhetoric the master political art: oratory is the ideal training of the statesman, and the word carries pure prestige.
- 19th-20th-century split - Beside the noble art, the pejorative 'mere rhetoric' and 'empty rhetoric' take root, branding persuasive language as words without substance.
- Contemporary - A two-register word: it still names the proud classical discipline and the craft of great public speech, yet is just as often dismissed as insincere 'political rhetoric.'
