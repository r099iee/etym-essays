In the passage from the Chinese classic, bethinking is the hinge of the little novice's decision. Finding himself homeless after the fire, and bethinking himself that a post in a yamen was a fine, gentlemanly way of earning a living, he converts desperation into strategy: the participle marks the mental act by which he re-plots his life toward secular comfort and status. To bethink oneself (of something, or that…) is to come to think of something, to call it to mind, or to take thought — often with a sudden or decisive shift of plan. The reflexive construction is essential: the subject is both the thinker and the one addressed by his own thought. The tone is slightly archaic and literary, fitting a narrator who renders opportunistic self-interest in formal English. The full sense inventory is as follows:
  • Primary sense — "to call to mind, to come to think of": the basic meaning, often with of: "he bethought himself of his umbrella." It covers both recalling something already known and lighting upon an idea.
  • Secondary sense — "to take thought, to consider, to resolve": the active deliberative reading, often with a that-clause: "she bethought herself that she had a friend who might help." The thought is not mere recollection but a calculation that changes what one does next.
  • Reflexive construction — "bethink oneself": the historical norm; the subject directs the thinking back upon himself, giving the verb an inward, almost soliloquy-like force.
  • Intransitive absolute use: "bethink thee" / "bethink ye" — biblical and poetic imperatives meaning "reflect, take thought," addressed to oneself or another.
  • Register sense — "archaic, literary, biblical": in modern English the verb survives mainly in historical, biblical, and formal narrative, where it marks an inward turn of calculation with old-fashioned gravity.
  • The present participle — bethinking: the form in the card, used as a participial clause to describe a continuing or simultaneous mental act, exactly as in the passage.
The morphology explains the meaning. Bethink is be- + think, where the prefix be- is not meaningless: it is transitivizing and often intensifying, converting an intransitive "think" into a verb that takes a direct object (to think oneself, to think something) and adding a sense of thoroughness or directedness. With the reflexive pronoun, the object is the thinker himself, so the verb means "to direct one's thinking at oneself" — to take thought, to remember for oneself, to settle the mind on a course. In older English the prefix also gave such forms as bemoan, bestir, bespeak, and beguile, which share the same intensifying or transitivizing function. The semantic boundaries against near-synonyms sharpen the sense. Remembering recovers a past fact already known; bethinking can include recall but usually implies active consideration or a new resolve. Reflecting stresses deliberate, cool meditation. Realizing stresses sudden awareness of a truth and lacks the reflexive construction. Considering is broader and more neutral. Reminding himself is the closest modern paraphrase but is more explicitly self-prompting — it says the thought was prompted from outside or by a cue, whereas bethinking presents the thought as arising within the thinker's own deliberation. Collocations are largely fixed and somewhat archaic:
  • Bethink oneself of + noun: "bethought himself of a duty, a promise, a resource, a hiding-place" — recall of something available or owed.
  • Bethink oneself that + clause: "bethought herself that a friend might help" — the deliberative that-clause pattern seen in the passage.
  • Bethink + object + clause (transitive): less common, older; "he bethought him how he might escape."
  • Bethink thee / ye: the biblical imperative, "reflect, take thought."
In modern usage the word is rare in speech. It survives where a narrator wants the gravity of self-consultation — a character who pauses, weighs his situation, and changes course. The verb thus carries a built-in narrative pause: the reader watches a mind turning in on itself before acting. In the Red Chamber passage this is exactly the effect: the novice's change of plan is presented not as impulse but as the outcome of a considered, self-directed thought, which makes his opportunism seem deliberate rather than merely desperate.
