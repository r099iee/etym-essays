Chittagong (officially renamed Chattogram in 2018, but still widely called Chittagong in English historical and literary texts) is the second-largest city of Bangladesh and its principal seaport, situated on the Karnaphuli River near the Bay of Bengal in the country's southeast. In the source context — the Chittagong Hill tribes of India — the name appears as a regional modifier for the hill peoples of the Chittagong Hill Tracts, the forested upland borderland between Bengal, Tripura, and Burma (Myanmar) inhabited by Chakma, Marma, Tripuri, and other Tibeto-Burman peoples distinct from Bengali lowlanders.

The sentence catalogs societies practicing communal landholding: North American Indians, Andean Peruvians, Chittagong hill peoples, Borneans, and South Sea Islanders. The author's point is comparative ethnology typical of late-19th/early-20th-century histories of property: private exclusive ownership is presented as a late development, while early or tribal societies held and tilled soil in common. Chittagong here thus signals exotic upland tribal communism to a Western reader, not the modern metropolis.

  • Geographic senses: the city proper (the port and industrial center); the wider district and division; and the Chittagong Hill Tracts (Rangamati, Bandarban, Khagrachari districts) — rugged, forested hills whose peoples practice jhum (shifting) cultivation and communal tenure.
  • Historical senses: an ancient entrepôt known to Arab traders from the 9th century, prized successively by the Sultanate of Bengal, the Mughals (who called it Islamabad), Arakanese kings, the Portuguese (who called it Porto Grande — the Great Port), and the British East India Company, which seized it in 1760 and developed it as Bengal's eastern harbor.
  • Colonial-ethnographic sense: in British imperial writing, Chittagong evokes frontier hill tribes under indirect rule — headmen, circle chiefs, communal forests — studied as living examples of primitive communism, exactly as used in the source.
  • Modern senses: Bangladesh's commercial capital and busiest container port; a site of ship-breaking at Sitakunda, steel, textiles, and fisheries; and, since 2017, the region adjoining the Rohingya refugee response in Cox's Bazar to the south.
  • Symbolic sense: in South Asian memory, Chittagong signifies cosmopolitan exchange (Arab, Persian, Portuguese, Mughal, British layers) and anti-colonial resistance — notably the 1930 Chittagong Armoury Raid led by Surya Sen against British rule.

The bridge between city name and tribal modifier is administrative geography under empire: the hills were governed as an appendage of the Chittagong district, so lowland bureaucrats labeled diverse upland peoples collectively as Chittagong hill tribes. Modern scholarship prefers specific ethnonyms (Chakma, Marma, Bawm, etc.) and recognizes that communal tenure was a flexible adaptation to shifting cultivation and forest ecology, not a uniform primitive stage. The older collective label survives mainly in historical texts like the source.

Usage notes: Chittagong is a proper noun, capitalized, pronounced in English approximately /ˈtʃɪtəɡɒŋ/ and in Bengali [ˈtʃɔtːoɡram]. It takes no plural and functions attributively (Chittagong port, Chittagong Hill Tracts). The official Bengali form Chattogram / Chattagram is now preferred by the Bangladeshi government, but Chittagong remains standard in historical discussion. Connotation in the source is distantly ethnographic and now dated; in contemporary Bangladeshi usage it is civic-prideful and commercial.

  • Related terms: Chattogram (modern official name), Chittagong Hill Tracts, Chakma, Marma, Karnaphuli, Porto Grande, Islamabad (Mughal name for the city).
  • Learner tip: when you meet Chittagong in older English books, check whether it means the port city or the upland peoples — the Hill Tracts sense almost always signals a discussion of tribal custom, land tenure, or frontier administration.
