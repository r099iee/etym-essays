TAST (n., archaic spelling) — An early modern and Miltonic spelling of taste: the sense or act of tasting; the experience or savouring of food or drink; or, by figurative extension, any brief experience or sampling of something. In John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667), Book I, the word carries full tragic force: "Of that Forbidden Tree, whose mortal tast / Brought Death into the World, and all our woe" — the single physical act of eating the forbidden fruit becomes the entry of death and sin into humanity. Core senses
  • Gustatory sense (primary, concrete): the sensation of flavour perceived in the mouth when food or drink stimulates the taste buds of the tongue and palate — the five basic qualities of sweet, sour, salt, bitter, and umami. In Milton's verse this is the physical sense: the forbidden fruit is tasted on the tongue, and that one oral act is charged with cosmic consequence.
  • The act of tasting / sampling: the deliberate action of putting a small amount of food or drink on the tongue to test its flavour, temperature, or quality — e.g. a chef tasting a sauce, or a judge of wine sampling a vintage. This is the oldest English sense, inherited from Old French taster, "to touch, try, sample," and it survives in phrases like "to have a taste" (a small portion).
  • A small portion or sample: "a taste of wine," "take a taste" — a quantity just sufficient to perceive flavour; the metonymic sense in which the word names the tiny portion itself rather than the act.
  • Brief experience (experiential sense): "a taste of freedom," "a taste of life abroad," "his first taste of war" — a short, often tantalising exposure to an experience, generalising the "sample" sense from food to experience in general. This is the semantic bridge between the kitchen and the world.
Polysemy & secondary modern meanings — although the spelling tast is archaic, the word taste it represents has become one of the most richly polysemous nouns in English:
  • Aesthetic discernment (abstract): the faculty of perceiving and judging beauty, style, or quality — "she has impeccable taste in art," "a man of taste." This develops in the late 17th–18th centuries: because a physical taste is immediately pleasant or unpleasant, "taste" is metaphorised into a faculty of immediate aesthetic judgement, the "palate of the mind." The historical bridge runs from the tongue's discrimination of flavours to the eye's and ear's discrimination of the beautiful.
  • Personal preference or liking: "that is not to my taste," "a taste for adventure" — individual inclination, the subjective and democratised endpoint of the aesthetic sense; the shift from objective quality to subjective preference mirrors the modern sense that "there is no disputing about tastes" (Latin de gustibus non est disputandum).
  • Judgement of propriety / social decorum: "a joke in bad taste," "tactless, in poor taste" — by the 19th century "taste" extends from artistic judgement to moral and social appropriateness, so that "good taste" means tact, decency, and refinement, and "bad taste" means offence given by vulgarity.
  • An evaluative ideal in culture and design: "tasteful," "tasteless," "tastemaker" — the word spawns a family denoting conformity to (or violation of) recognised standards of elegance; in modern digital culture "taste" is again a high-value concept, applied to curation, aesthetics, and "having good taste" in media.
  • Verbal archaism — to experience, to know: the biblical and early modern verb "to taste" meaning "to experience, to know, to make trial of" ("taste of death," "taste and see that the Lord is good") survives in fixed religious and literary phrases, and it is precisely this sense Milton exploits: to taste the fruit is to experience mortality.
  • Archaic tactile sense — to touch or handle: the original Old French meaning of taster was "to touch, feel, handle" (preserved in modern French tâter, "to feel, grope"), and English evidence of the 14th–15th centuries still shows "to taste" meaning "to touch, to feel, to examine by touch" (e.g. "tasting the wound" in Middle English medical texts). This tactile core is the root of the whole word's career.
Fixed phrases & modern idiom
  • Acquired taste: a flavour or interest one learns to like through repeated exposure.
  • A taste of your own medicine: experiencing the same treatment one has inflicted on others.
  • Leave a bad taste in the mouth: a lingering feeling of disgust or unease.
  • To one's taste / to taste: "add salt to taste" — exactly as preferred; in recipes, a fixed culinary formula.
  • In good/bad taste, tasteless: the moral-aesthetic judgement of propriety.
  • An acquired taste / a taste for: a developed liking for something demanding.
Grammatical and orthographic note — tast is simply the early modern spelling of taste before the final silent -e became standardised; Milton uses the shorter form for metrical and archaic gravitas. As a verb form it would be the base of "to taste"; in Paradise Lost it functions as a noun. The word's semantic richness — physical flavour, the act of sampling, aesthetic judgement, brief experience, moral propriety — all descends from one ancient tactile verb meaning "to touch," and the historical bridge from hand to tongue to mind is the single thread uniting every sense.
