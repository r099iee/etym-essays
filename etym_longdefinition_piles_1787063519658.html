Piles — the plural of pile: (1) long, heavy stakes or posts, usually of timber (now also of concrete or steel), driven deep into the ground or into the bed of a river, lake, or sea to support a structure; (2) heaps or masses of things lying one upon another; (3) the soft raised nap or surface of a carpet, velvet, or plush; (4) in British informal usage, "piles" is the common name for haemorrhoids.

The sense at work in this card's context is the engineer's sense. Lake-town in The Hobbit is "a busy wooden town" built on "huge piles made of forest trees": great timber trunks driven into the bed of the Long Lake so that the town stands above the water, reached by a wooden bridge. This is the classic pile-supported, or stilt, settlement — a foundation technique as old as the lake-villages of the Bronze Age and still used today for piers, jetties, and bridges. A pile works by transferring the load of the building through soft, wet, or unstable upper ground down to firm load-bearing strata, or by spreading the load through friction along its buried shaft.

  • Primary sense — a driven stake or post: a long, tapering, pointed support driven vertically into the earth or the bed of a body of water, used singly or in rows to carry quays, bridges, wharves, houses, and dams. Related engineering vocabulary: "pile-driving", "a pile-driver" (the great hammering engine), "bearing piles", "friction piles", "sheet piles" (interlocking panels), "a pile foundation".
  • Secondary sense — a heap or stack: a "pile" is a mass of things heaped up, whether deliberately ("a pile of books") or untidily ("piles of junk"); the plural can simply mean "a great many". The verb "to pile" means to heap up, to stack, to accumulate ("piles it on", "to pile on"). This word has merged in spelling with the engineer's pile.
  • Tertiary sense — a stack of fuel or a reactor: from the heap sense comes the "pile" of stacked material that burns or reacts: a "funeral pile" (a pyre), a "voltaic pile" (the earliest electric battery, built of stacked metal and felt disks), and the "atomic pile" or "nuclear pile", the early name for a graphite-moderated nuclear reactor, whose core was literally a stack of uranium and graphite.
  • Further sense — the nap of cloth: "pile" names the upright loops or raised surface of velvet, carpet, plush, and corduroy — the soft "fur" of the fabric. This word comes from Latin pilus "hair", a different origin, but it is spelled and pronounced identically, so a "high-pile carpet" is a carpet with a deep raised nap.
  • British informal sense — haemorrhoids: in British English "piles" is the everyday word for haemorrhoids, so called (like the Latin pīlulae, "little balls", whence "pill") from their round shape. It is a staple of informal medicine and a standing source of wordplay, since "a town built on piles" can sound, to a British ear, simultaneously architectural and medical.
  • Card-game and stacking senses: a "pile" in card games is a stack of cards in a particular arrangement (draw pile, discard pile), reinforcing the heap image in everyday parlance.

Semantic bridges: the spelling "pile" is a meeting-place of several distinct words. The engineer's pile (a driven stake) comes from Latin pīla, "pillar, pier", via Old French pile "stake, pier, mole". The heap of things comes from Old French pile, "heap, stack", thought to be the same Latin pīla in the sense of a "pillar" built up of stacked material, or possibly a separate word for a rounded mass. The fabric nap comes from Latin pilus "hair", and the haemorrhoids from the image of little balls. English therefore holds several different words under one roof of letters, and the plural "piles" is one of the language's most playful coincidences. In the context of building on water, however, there is no ambiguity: "huge piles made of forest trees" can only be driven timber supports.
