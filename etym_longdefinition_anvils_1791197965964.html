Anvils is the plural of anvil: heavy, solid blocks, traditionally of wrought iron or steel with a hardened face, upon which hot or cold metal is placed to be hammered, cut, bent, or otherwise shaped by a smith.

Physically, the classic London-pattern anvil familiar today has a flat top face, a conical horn or beak at one end for curving metal, a heel with square hardy hole and round pritchel hole for holding tools, and a massive waist and foot that absorb shock. Antique and primitive anvils were far simpler: a flat-topped stone, a block of bronze, or a short iron stake driven into a tree stump. The defining property is inert mass and hardness: the anvil does not move when struck but returns the hammer's energy into the workpiece.

  • Core tool sense: A smithing block. Example: a blacksmith's anvil, a farrier's anvil, a jeweller's stake anvil. In the source context - shaped stones into hammers, anvils, kettles - it means stone blocks used as passive bases for percussion work in the Stone Age.
  • Meteorological sense (anvil cloud / anvil head): The flat, spreading top of a mature cumulonimbus cloud that hits the tropopause and fans out, visually resembling a blacksmith's anvil. Pilots and forecasters speak of "anvil tops" and "anvil crawlers" (lightning within the anvil).
  • Anatomical sense (incus): The middle of the three ossicles in the mammalian middle ear, called the anvil because of its rough shape, which transmits vibrations from the hammer (malleus) to the stirrup (stapes).
  • Figurative / idiomatic senses: "On the anvil" means under active formation or discussion, as hot metal on an anvil. "Between the hammer and the anvil" means trapped between two opposing forces, the active striker and the passive receiver. A person or institution can be described as an anvil for enduring blows without breaking - "the anvil outlasts the hammer".

In craft domains the word subdivides further. Farriers distinguish shoeing anvils, knife-makers use knifemaker's anvils, and silversmiths use beak and stake anvils. In manufacturing, a power-hammer anvil or anvil block is the lower die. In shoemaking, a cobbler's anvil (last) is a foot-shaped iron for shaping soles. The semantic bridge is always shape-by-resistance: anything that serves as the unyielding base against which shaping force is applied inherits the name.

Literary and emotional nuance is unusually rich for a tool noun. The anvil connotes endurance, honest labour, incorruptibility, and creative violence - creation through beating. It can feel archaic and pastoral (the village forge), industrial and proletarian (the union emblem), or mythic (Hephaestus, Vulcan, Wayland). Unlike hammer, which feels active and aggressive, anvil feels passive, patient, and steadfast. In plural, anvils often evokes a museum row, a forge full of equipment, or a landscape of thunderheads, multiplying that sense of weight and permanence.

For learners, distinguishing anvil types cements the plural: blacksmith, farrier, saw-maker, and silversmith patterns each optimise face hardness, horn radius, and weight for different work, yet all obey the same physics of mass and rebound. A good anvil rings like a bell when struck and returns about 90 percent of hammer energy; a dead cast-iron imitation thuds and tires the arm. This audible test, known for centuries, is why old catalogues list weight, face steel thickness, and ring tone alongside price.
