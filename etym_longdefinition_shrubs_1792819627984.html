Shrubs, the plural of shrub, are in the botanical sense low to medium woody plants — smaller than trees, with several permanent stems branching from or near the ground instead of a single tall trunk — including roses, lilacs, laurels, rhododendrons, box, and heather, whether wild on hillsides or planted in gardens for flower, fruit, foliage, or shelter.

In the source sentence the word appears in festival finery: amid the clearing of pavilions, tables, lanterns, and crumbs appears the flowering shrubs in boxes — potted ornamentals, probably blooming garden shrubs set out in containers to decorate the feast, now packed away with the chairs and the forgotten gloves. The detail is pure celebration: even the greenery was brought in tubs for the occasion.

Core meaning in detail: Botanists draw the working line between tree and shrub at size and stem count: a shrub is woody, perennial, and multi-stemmed, usually under six metres, renewing itself from the base. Gardeners divide them further — deciduous and evergreen, flowering and foliage, fruiting currants and gooseberries — and landscape designers use them as the middle layer between lawn and tree canopy. Ecologists value wild shrubs as the architecture of heath, scrub, and hedgerow, feeding and housing insects, birds, and small mammals.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Woody multi-stemmed plants (primary modern sense, from Old English): roses, azaleas, boxwood, juniper — the backbone planting of gardens and the clothing of wild hillsides alike. The bridge is the thing itself: the Old English scrybb already meant brushwood and low woody growth.
  • Ornamental container plants (as in the source): flowering shrubs in boxes — potted specimens set out for display. The bridge is horticultural practice: shrubs take readily to tubs and planters, so great households and hotels move them about like furniture.
  • Scrub and brushwood collectively (ecological, literary): the hillside was covered in shrubs and thorn. The bridge is number-to-mass: many shrubs read as one thicket, and the plural slides toward a landscape term.
  • Shrub as a drink (historical, 18th–19th c.): a cordial of fruit juice, sugar, and vinegar or spirits — currant shrub, rum shrub. The bridge is the fruit-bush: the drink was named for the currant and berry bushes whose harvest flavoured it, then the plant word became the beverage word.
  • The scrubby underdog of similes (figurative): a stunted person or enterprise is no more than a shrub beside some mighty oak. The bridge is the tree-shrub hierarchy of the forest carried into human comparison — low, bushy, unregarded growth.

Register and connotation: shrubs is plain, wholesome garden English — compost, secateurs, and blossom rather than hothouse exoticism. It suggests modest, hardworking greenery: the trustworthy background that lets the flowers show off. Only the old punch-bowl sense adds a rakish note — sailors and Georgian topers drinking shrub below decks — a reminder that even the quietest garden word once knew its way round a tavern.
