Chignon (pronounced “SHEEN-yon,” from French) is a noun for a knot or coil of hair worn by a woman, typically low on the back of the head at or near the nape of the neck. It is a French loanword in English, borrowed in the 1780s, and it entered the language as a fashion word — chic, Parisian, deliberately dressed. The primary sense is the hairstyle; but the word's history — from a Latin word for “chain” to a word for the neck — makes it etymologically strange, and in the Dream of the Red Chamber context it carries a precise social charge. In David Hawkes's portrait of Wang Xi-feng, “Her chignon was enclosed in a circlet of gold filigree and clustered pearls,” the chignon is the architectural foundation of the coiffure: the anchored mass of hair that the circlet encircles and around which the pearls are disposed. Hawkes's choice of chignon over bun signals costly, disciplined grooming: even the nape of Xi-feng's neck is an ornamented social statement, matching the meticulous management with which she runs the Jia household. The tone is luxurious and controlled — nothing on this woman is unconsidered.

Polysemy and related uses of the word:

  • Core sense — the hairstyle: a coil, twist, or knot of hair worn low at the back of the head; the hair is gathered, twisted, and pinned into a single compact mass. Chignons may be smooth and sculptural or loose and romantic, but the placement at the nape is defining.
  • Fashion and salon jargon: in professional hairdressing the chignon is a specific constructed style distinct from the everyday bun in technique and finish; sub-types include the classic low chignon, the braided chignon, the twisted chignon, and the high ballerina bun, each a named salon procedure with its own pinning method.
  • Anatomical echo (rare, historical): in older French the word meant “nape of the neck” — le chignon du cou — and some older English dictionaries record that sense, now obsolete; the hairstyle sense arises because the knot sits on the nape, so the word has meant both “the neck” and “the hair worn on the neck.”
  • Register: formal, elegant, and slightly exclusive. The word belongs to fashion writing, bridal magazines, and period fiction, and sounds conspicuously more refined than “bun”; a woman does not “put up a chignon” for the gym.
  • Figurative and cultural extensions: the chignon is shorthand for a certain disciplined femininity — the ballerina, the Edwardian lady, the 1940s film actress, the wedding guest. Mentioning a chignon evokes poise, structure, and costume; the hairstyle becomes a synecdoche for the composed woman wearing it.
  • Historical bridge to “chain”: the word descends from Latin catena “chain” through Old French chaignon, “iron neck-collar,” and then, by metonymy, “the nape.” Every modern chignon thus carries a buried history of fetters, slave-collars, and the flesh where a collar sits.
  • Grammatical note: the word is a countable noun (a chignon, two chignons), and it is used attributively in compounds such as “chignon bun” and “chignon pin”; the English plural keeps the French form chignons.

The semantic boundaries of chignon are unusually sharp. A bun is the unmarked everyday coil, indifferent to placement and finish; a knot is any twisted mass without nape-placement or design; an updo is too broad, covering every elevated style; a topknot sits at the crown, the opposite pole; and the French twist, though elegant and formal, is a vertical rolled fold rather than a horizontal coil. The chignon is therefore defined by three combined features that none of its near-synonyms share: it is low, it is coiled, and it is deliberately constructed. In the sentence, those features make it the perfect ornament-ready foundation for the circlet of gold filigree and clustered pearls — a hairstyle engineered to display jewellery, exactly as Xi-feng is engineered to display wealth, rank, and control.
