• Anaphrodisiac: Direct antonym denoting any substance, medication, or psychological factor that suppresses, reduces, or extinguishes sexual desire and erotic libido.
  • Philter (Philtre): Similar because both are potions or concoctions designed to induce erotic passion and romantic infatuation; differs because a philter is specifically an enchanted, magical love potion from folklore, mythology, and medieval romance, whereas an aphrodisiac is grounded in pharmacology, sensory stimuli, or natural gastronomy.
  • Stimulant: Similar because both excite physiological and psychological activity in the body; differs because a stimulant is a broad clinical category that increases alertness, heart rate, and central nervous system activity (such as caffeine or amphetamine) without specifically targeting erotic appetite or sexual desire.
  • Enticement: Similar because both serve to allure, attract, and provoke strong longing; differs because an enticement is a tactical, psychological, or situational lure that tempts someone toward a particular action, whereas an aphrodisiac operates through visceral sensory or biological arousal.
  • Libido-enhancer: Similar because both increase sexual appetite and erotic responsiveness; differs because libido-enhancer is modern, clinical biomedical jargon focused narrowly on physiological mechanisms, lacking the rich aesthetic, historical, and literary prestige of aphrodisiac.
  • Incitement: Similar because both provoke a sudden surge of energetic reaction; differs because incitement typically refers to stirring up political rebellion, public anger, or violent action rather than sensual, erotic, or creative pleasure.
  • Intoxicant: Similar because both alter consciousness and heighten emotional or sensory intensity; differs because an intoxicant specifically causes cognitive impairment and physiological inebriation through chemical toxicity (like alcohol or narcotics), whereas an aphrodisiac excites desire without necessarily disabling mental faculties.
