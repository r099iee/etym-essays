• Hastily: Similar because both mean "with urgent speed"; differs because hastily is neutral and colourless, while hotfoot paints the vivid image of burning feet.
  • Quickly: Similar because both denote speed; differs because quickly is the default, unmarked adverb with no implication of urgency or destination.
  • Speedily: Similar because both mean "fast"; differs because speedily is formal and often bureaucratic ("the matter will be dealt with speedily"), lacking hotfoot's colloquial heat.
  • Swiftly: Similar because both mean "at speed"; differs because swiftly is literary and suggests smooth, graceful motion, not breathless panic.
  • Rapidly: Similar because both mean "fast"; differs because rapidly measures rate abstractly and carries no emotional urgency.
  • Apace: Similar because both mean "at a good speed"; differs because apace is archaic-literary ("ill news travels apace") and impersonal.
  • Posthaste: Similar because both mean "with great urgency"; differs because posthaste is formal and historical (the old mail-courier's "haste, post, haste") and has a musty, formulaic flavour.
  • Pronto: Similar because both mean "quickly"; differs because pronto is Spanish-borrowed slang implying immediate obedience, not travelling speed.
  • PDQ: Similar because both mean "very quickly"; differs because PDQ (pretty damn quick) is vulgar, abrupt slang with no sense of motion.
  • Double-quick: Similar because both mean "at top speed"; differs because double-quick comes from military drill (double time) and implies disciplined haste.
  • Lickety-split: Similar because both mean "extremely fast"; differs because lickety-split is folksy American playfulness, suggesting smooth rapid motion rather than urgent errand-running.
  • In hot haste: Similar because both mean "with great urgency"; differs because in hot haste is an archaic phrase, while hotfoot is a single fused word.
  • At full pelt / full tilt: Similar because both mean "running at maximum speed"; differs because they emphasise maximum physical exertion rather than the urgency of the errand.
  • Hell-for-leather: Similar because both mean "recklessly fast"; differs because hell-for-leather stresses recklessness and risk-taking, not just haste.
  • Expeditiously: Similar because both mean "with speed and efficiency"; differs because expeditiously is formal, impersonal, and about efficiency rather than urgent motion.
