- Late 15th–16th c. – Formed in Early Modern English from the adjective intricate (Latin intricatus) with the noun suffix -cy; originally carried a pejorative connotation of vexatious entanglement, intellectual trickery, and deliberate perplexity in legal disputes and theological quarrels.
- 17th c. – Adopted by natural philosophers, metaphysical poets, and early anatomists; began shifting from negative obstruction toward descriptive admiration of nature's ingenious micro-structures, elaborate mathematical proofs, and clockwork mechanisms.
- 18th c. – Solidified in Enlightenment essays and aesthetic philosophy (e.g., Addison, Locke, Burke); pluralized as intricacies to analyze the subtle interior machinery of human cognition, ethical duties, and artistic ornament, establishing the aesthetic value of harmonious complexity.
- 19th c. – Prominent in Victorian literary criticism, Victorian novelistic plotting, and industrial engineering; used admiringly to describe multi-plot serialized novels, gothic architectural filigree, and complex thermodynamic machinery.
- 20th–21st c. – Pervasive across modern academic, technological, legal, and critical registers (e.g., psychology, geopolitics, quantum physics, software architecture); denotes the indispensable, fascinating, or labyrinthine inner workings of systems, requiring forensic expertise to dissect.
