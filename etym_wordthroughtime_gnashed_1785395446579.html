- [Earliest recorded sense (14th c., biblical register)] - Gnash enters English in Bible translations — "gnashing of teeth" in hell — as the grinding or striking together of teeth in rage and torment, an image of damned, helpless fury.
- [Echoic extension to any violent tooth-contact] - The harsh guttural gn- onset and hissing -sh release could name any convulsive, noisy grinding — chattering or gnashing jaws without a target — fury with nowhere to go.
- [Literary & melodramatic register (18th–19th c.)] - Gnash became a favourite of Romantic and Gothic prose — villains and the doomed gnash their teeth; the word hardened into a theatrical marker of impotent rage.
- [Modern usage] - Today gnash is a literary, dramatic verb: "to gnash one's teeth" signals bitter, helpless fury in elevated prose, its quasi-animal, reflex-like violence set against everyday words like grind or grit.
