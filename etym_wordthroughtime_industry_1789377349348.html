- Late 15th Century - Borrowed into Middle English from Old French *industrie* (derived from Latin *industria*); originally signified cleverness, ingenuity, artifice, or purposeful contrivance.
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Reformation) - Semantic center shifted decisively toward persistent diligence, assiduous effort, and purposeful labor, celebrated by Protestant theologians and humanists as a foundational moral virtue opposing sloth.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment & Adam Smith) - Adam Smith in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) began applying "industry" to the aggregate productive labor of a nation's populace, bridging personal virtue with macroeconomic productivity.
- 19th Century (Industrial Revolution & Victorian Era) - Transformed into the concrete designation for mechanized factory production, smokestacks, and manufacturing sectors; famously satirized by Oscar Wilde in *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895) through Lady Bracknell's aristocratic disdain for hard work.
- 20th Century - Codified in macroeconomic classification systems (Standard Industrial Classification, NAICS); expanded sociologically to designate commercialized cultural domains ("entertainment industry", "heritage industry").
- 21st Century - Dominates global discussions of technological disruption, post-industrial services, and green manufacturing, while continuing to serve as a revered moral term for dedicated human craftsmanship.
