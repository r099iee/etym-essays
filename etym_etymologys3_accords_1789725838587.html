The history of ACCORDS begins inside the chest. Its ultimate ancestor is PIE *kerd- heart, one of the most stable body-part roots, continued in Sanskrit hrd, Greek kardia (whence cardiac, cardiology), Latin cor, cordis, Gothic hairto, and English heart with regular Grimm’s-law shift from k to h. Latin cor meant not only the pumping organ but the seat of feeling, mind, and will — to learn by heart, to take to heart — so compounds with cor naturally mean agreement of wills. Classical Latin already had concors of one heart, agreeing and discors of divided heart, disagreeing, with nouns concordia and discordia that English still uses.

Vulgar Latin coined *accordare from ad- to, toward plus cor heart, literally to bring heart to heart, unattested in Classical texts but demanded by Romance outcomes and universally accepted by OED and Etymonline. Phonetics are regular: Latin ad- assimilates before c, cor keeps hard /k/, stress falls on the ending, giving Old French acorder in the eleventh century with meanings to reconcile, agree, arrange, and to grant. Anglo-Norman carried it across the Channel after 1066 as acorden, accorden, recorded in Middle English by the twelfth century for truces, marriages, and favours. Modern spelling restores double -c- on Latinising model; pronunciation settles in RP as /əˈkɔːd/ with reduced first vowel and long open-o.

  • Reconciliation to agreement: Old French acorder first reconciles persons — lovers, litigants, warring barons — then by extension makes things agree: stories, accounts, tunes. Chaucer uses accorden for both lovers accorded and tales according. The preposition fixes as with for persons and things compared, to for favours granted, a split still taught today.
  • Granting sense: from joined hearts to open hands: to be at one heart with a petitioner is to give what is asked. Medieval charters accord pardons, licences, and thanks. Shakespeare still uses both branches freely: all things accorded and accord thee my protection. The passive was accorded becomes the diplomatic-ceremonial standard for honours bestowed.
  • Of one’s own accord and diplomatic Accords: the adverbial phrase of his own accord voluntarily is fixed by the sixteenth century, preserving the heart’s spontaneity. In the twentieth century the capitalised plural Accords — Oslo Accords, Helsinki Accords, Camp David Accords — makes the noun the headline form, treaties as joined national hearts. The verb retreats to formal prose, where accords with remains the scholar’s seal of corroboration, exactly as in the Baker sentence where forgetfulness and confession are ruled harmonious.

Socio-culturally ACCORDS moves from feudal handshake to filing cabinet: chivalric peacemaking becomes bureaucratic consistency-checking, yet the cardiac metaphor never dies. To say memories accord with admission is still, faintly, to say hearts beat together across decades, outer witnesses and inner voice keeping the same time.
