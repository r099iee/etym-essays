voracity is a noun denoting extreme, greedy, and insatiable eagerness to devour — literally an excessive appetite for food, and figuratively an uncontrollable craving for anything such as knowledge, wealth, power, or experience. It is stronger and more literary than hunger: it implies swallowing quickly, indiscriminately, and without restraint, often with a moral judgment of excess.

In the Durant context — "this indiscriminate voracity" — it means the primitive, undiscriminating omnivorous hunger of early man, who ate whatever could be caught or gathered. Fire and agriculture limited it by making food selective, cooked, and cultivated rather than seized raw.

  • Literal gustatory sense (core): Ravenous hunger; the quality of eating greedily and in large quantities. Example: the voracity of wolves, lions, sharks. Here voracity is physical and observable — bolting food, overeating, inability to be satisfied. It differs from appetite (neutral) and hunger (need) by stressing excess and greed.
  • Figurative intellectual sense: An eager, devouring desire for reading, learning, information. A "voracious reader" reads constantly and rapidly. The semantic bridge is direct: just as the stomach devours food, the mind devours books. This sense is admiring rather than condemnatory.
  • Economic and acquisitive sense: Rapacious greed for money, property, resources, markets. Corporate voracity, the voracity of developers, tax voracity. Bridge: consumption of wealth as eating. Carries strong moral censure — implying exploitation and insatiability.
  • Sexual and sensual sense: Insatiable lust or craving for pleasure. Literary use: sexual voracity. Bridge: appetite as universal metaphor for desire; Latin already linked gula and luxuria.
  • Destructive / consuming-force sense: Applied to non-human devourers: fire, war, disease, time. The voracity of flames, the voracity of cancer. Bridge: Latin vorago (abyss) and vorax applied to whirlpools and fire — anything that swallows things up.
  • Indiscriminate / omnivorous nuance in anthropology: Eating without selection or taboo — raw, rotten, unfamiliar foods. Durant's "indiscriminate" stresses lack of discrimination, a pre-cultural state before cooking and farming taught choice and restraint.
  • Grammatical and collocational notes: Uncountable noun; often with of (voracity of appetite, of demand). Adjective voracious is far more common in everyday English. Register is formal, literary, academic — one writes voracity in essays, not in casual speech (where greed, big appetite would appear).

In short, voracity spans body, mind, and society: from a wolf bolting meat, to a child devouring books, to an empire devouring land. What unites all senses is the image of an open mouth that cannot be filled — rapid intake, large capacity, no selectivity, and persistence after satiety should have set in. Whether praised (intellectual voracity) or condemned (corporate voracity), it always signals intensity beyond the normal.
