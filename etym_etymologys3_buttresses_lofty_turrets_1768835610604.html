BUTTRESSES — The buttress is architecture made out of a blow. The story begins with PIE *bhau-, 'to strike, to beat', a root whose descendants across the Germanic world kept the sense of striking: Proto-Germanic *bautaną, 'to beat, push, strike', gave Old English bēatan, our beat, and its noun bītel, the mallet, our beetle; Old Norse bauta kept the same sense of striking. In the Frankish dialects of the Rhineland the root surfaced as *bōtan, 'to push, to strike', and it passed into Old French as bouter, 'to push, to thrust' — the verb of butting, of shoving, of driving. The sound history is a model of Germanic voiceless obstruents: PIE *bh- became Germanic *b- (Grimm's Law), and the vowel settled variously as au-, ō-, and ou- across the dialects, the Frankish form *bōtan yielding Old French bouter with the characteristic French diphthongisation and loss of the intervocalic stop.

From bouter, French built a whole family: bout, 'the end, the extremity' (the point of a thrust), which gave English the butt of a rifle and the butt of a joke; the verb butt, 'to push with the head', which came into English in the twelfth century; and, in the building trades, bouterez, 'buttresses' — the masonry members, raised against a wall, that 'push' against it. Old French bouterez (a plural form) entered Middle English as butres, later buttress, in the fourteenth century. The name is the function: the buttress is the push-back, the masonry that answers the wall's outward thrust with a thrust of its own, transmitting the vault's weight to the ground. In Gothic architecture the flying buttress became one of the great inventions of structural engineering — a stone arm pushing against the nave wall to hold up the sky of the vault, the invention that made the great cathedrals of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries possible. The word rose with the church-building age: from fortress and castle it spread to every great religious and civic structure, and then to any outward support whatever.

The seventeenth to nineteenth centuries broadened the term further and transferred it to metaphor: to buttress an argument, an institution, or a state is to prop it up, to hold it against collapse, and the word became as much a verb of rhetoric as of masonry. In modern usage the precise structural sense survives in architecture and engineering, while the figurative sense — anything that supports, strengthens, or defends — is alive in everyday language. In Peake's sentence, the time-eaten buttresses of Gormenghast are that ancient push grown old: shoulders of stone that have pushed for centuries and are now being consumed by time, the architecture of the castle still held up by its own buried blows. The word's inner logic — a push, answered by a push — is the perfect figure for a castle that has resisted its enemies and its years alike, and the 'time-eaten' buttress is the blow grown weary, the defender still standing though worn.

Attestations. From Chaucer's age onward the buttress has appeared wherever walls and institutions are upheld — in the building records of the great cathedrals, in the language of fortification and of finance ('the buttress of the realm'), and in the poetry of endurance, where the ruined buttress stands for the strength that outlived its builders. It is the word for strength that works quietly, pushing against the load rather than carrying it from above, and that character — the shoulder against the wall — has made it the natural metaphor for every support that holds up an institution or an argument without ever drawing attention to itself.

LOFTY — The family of lofty is a family of the air. Its root is Proto-Germanic *luftuz, 'air, sky' — a word whose PIE ancestry is unknown (an old suggestion linking it to *leup-, 'to peel, to break off', is too weak to rely on), but whose Germanic career is richly attested: German Luft and Dutch lucht mean 'air' to this day, Gothic luftus preserves the stem in the fourth century, Old English lyft means 'air, atmosphere, sky', and Old Norse lopt means both 'air, sky' and 'upper room'. The Old Norse double sense is the pivot: the upper room of a Norse hall was a loft, a room under the roof, in the air itself, and Middle English took the word as loft, 'upper room, gallery', along with the phrase on lofte, 'in the air', which gave English aloft. From the same stem, Old Norse lypta, 'to raise', gave English lift, and Old English lyft gave the poetic noun lift, 'the sky'. The adjective lofty, formed in the fourteenth century with the suffix -y, means literally 'of the loft, of the upper air' — and its whole semantic career is the vertical metaphor unfolding.

In its first English senses lofty meant simply 'high, towering, in the air', of things that belong to the upper storey of the sky: lofty towers, lofty mountains. But height in space is the easiest of all metaphors for height in value, and by the fifteenth century the word had transferred to character and manner: lofty ideals, a lofty style, a lofty bearing — the exalted, the noble, the sublime. The same vertical metaphor, judged coldly, produced the third sense: a lofty person is one who stands above, who looks down, and lofty disdain, lofty haughtiness, lofty indifference carry the shadow of snobbery that every vertical word in English eventually acquires. The word therefore moves in one smooth arc from the physical (the high turret), through the moral (the lofty ideal), to the social (the lofty sneer), and all three senses coexist in modern usage.

In Peake's sentence the turrets are 'broken and lofty' — ruined yet still high — and the word does a double service: it gives the little towers physical height over the irregular roofs, and it gives the castle a remoteness and elevation that set it above the ruin and the swarm, a dignity that survives its own decay. The lofty turret is both high and untouchable, a thing of the upper air looking down on the crumbling world below. The word's career — from the Viking hall's upper room, to the sky, to the exalted, to the disdainful — is the vertical metaphor in miniature, and Peake exploits its full range in a single phrase.

Attestations and register. The word's dual life is everywhere in English literature: the lofty thoughts of the psalmist, the lofty aims of the reformer, the lofty contempt of the aristocrat, and the lofty towers of the castle have all been described with the same word for five centuries. Milton's lofty rhyme, the lofty ideals of the Romantics, and the lofty disdain of Victorian snobs show the full vertical range of the metaphor. In modern prose lofty is used of anything that deliberately stands above — a lofty ceiling, a lofty ambition, a lofty manner — and its slight archaism is part of its charm: it is the word of the solemn and the high, never of the merely tall. The moral and the social senses depend on the physical one: because a turret is literally high in the air, the same word can lift an ideal into the sky or hold a snob at arm's length, and the vertical metaphor has never once lost its grip on the language.

TURRETS — The turret is a little tower with a long, disputed ancestry. Its story begins not in any Indo-European root but in the Mediterranean substrate: the Greek word týrris, 'tower, fortified stronghold', is generally treated by modern scholarship (notably Robert Beekes) as a Pre-Greek substrate word — a borrowing from the older non-Indo-European languages of the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean, whose precise origin is lost. An older generation of etymologists tried to derive turris from PIE *dwer-, 'door' (the tower as the fortified gate), but that guess is now rejected: the Greek form has no convincing Indo-European etymology, and the honest verdict is that the word is a substrate survival, one of the oldest strata of Mediterranean vocabulary. Greek passed the word to Latin as turris, 'tower', in the centuries of close contact, and Latin's turris proved one of the most successful loanwords in the family: it gave Old French tour, English tower, Italian torre, Spanish torre, German Turm — and, in Old English, torr, 'tower, rock, peak', which survives as the word tor for a rocky summit.

The diminutive tourette, 'little tower', was formed in Old French from tour with the suffix -ette, and Middle English borrowed it as touret in the fourteenth century, later turret. From the beginning the word was a word of fortification: the small, slender towers of castles, set at the angles and projecting from the wall, served as look-out points, flanking positions, and the housings of staircases that wound within their cramped interiors. The diminutive kept its literal force — a turret is a smallness within the tower — and the word carried the defensive accent of the medieval stronghold through the Renaissance. In the nineteenth century the word took its most dramatic extension: the revolving armoured gun-platform of the modern warship was called a turret, transferring the idea of the little watchful tower from the castle wall to the ironclad and the tank, and engineering followed with the turret lathe, whose rotating tool-holder preserves the same image of a small revolving structure.

In Peake's sentence the turrets of Gormenghast are 'broken and lofty' — the little towers of the stronghold, ruined yet still high, rising over the irregular roofs of the hovels below. The word's dual history — the substrate tower of the ancient Mediterranean and the diminutive little tower of the medieval castle — converges in the image of a civilisation's decaying strength: even the castle's smallest watchtowers have outlasted the world around them, and the broken turret, like the time-eaten buttress, stands as the emblem of exhausted endurance. From the lost Pre-Greek language of the Aegean, through Rome and the French castle-builders, to the gun-turrets of modern war and the broken towers of Gormenghast, the little tower has never stopped watching.

Literary attestations. The turret is the favourite little tower of gothic and fantasy writing — the turret room of the imprisoned wife, the turrets of the castle on the hill, the gun-turrets of the warship in modern naval fiction — and in Gormenghast the broken and lofty turrets rise over the hovels as the last, ruined assertion of the stronghold's height, the little watchtowers that still survey the world they have outlived. The word's diminutive keeps its force even in ruin: a turret is small, subsidiary, watchful — the tower at the angle, the lookout at the corner — and Peake's broken turrets are the eyes of the dying stronghold, still open, still looking down over the irregular roofs and the swarming dwellings at their base.
