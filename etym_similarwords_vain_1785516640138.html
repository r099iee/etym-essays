• Futile: Similar because both describe an effort or action that produces no result; differs because futile stresses the inherent hopelessness of the endeavour — a deterministic, doomed quality — while vain also suggests emptiness and worthlessness in the effort itself.
  • Fruitless: Similar because both denote activity that yields nothing; differs because fruitless foregrounds the absence of yield after labour (an agricultural metaphor) and is more concrete, whereas vain carries the philosophical resonance of hollowness and waste.
  • Ineffective: Similar because both mean "not achieving its intended effect"; differs because ineffective is clinical and mechanical, concerned only with failure to produce a result, and lacks vain's moral and existential weight.
  • Ineffectual: Similar because both describe action that fails to achieve its purpose; differs because ineffectual suggests weakness or inadequacy of the agent as well as failure of the act, while vain points to the emptiness of the attempt rather than the weakness of the doer.
  • Useless: Similar because both can describe something that serves no purpose; differs because useless is blunt, functional, and everyday, whereas vain is literary and implies hollowness or vanity rather than mere lack of function.
  • Idle: Similar because both can mean "empty, worthless, without substance" (vain words, idle words); differs because idle stresses inactivity, laziness, or triviality, while vain stresses emptiness and futility.
  • Empty: Similar because both trace to the Latin idea of emptiness (vānus "empty"); differs because empty is concrete and literal, while vain has evolved into abstract futility and self-regard far beyond physical emptiness.
  • Hollow: Similar because both imply absence of substance at the core; differs because hollow is physical and descriptive, while vain is abstract and moral, judging the emptiness of effort or of self.
  • Conceited: Similar because both describe excessive pride in one's appearance or achievements; differs because conceited names self-admiration alone, without the underlying sense of emptiness at vain's etymological core.
  • Narcissistic: Similar because both describe self-absorption or excessive self-love; differs because narcissistic is the clinical, psychological term for pathological self-regard, whereas vain is everyday and carries the extra sense of futility.
  • Self-important: Similar because both describe an inflated sense of one's own worth; differs because self-important suggests pomposity and self-seriousness, while vain centres on appearance and on the hollowness beneath the pride.
  • Boastful: Similar because both can describe a person who displays empty pride; differs because boastful names the act of bragging, while vain describes the inner disposition of hollow self-regard (and leads to the related vainglory, "empty glory").
  • Vainglorious: Similar because both describe pride in one's own worth and achievements; differs because vainglorious is the stronger, more literary word for excessive or empty glory-seeking, while vain is broader and also covers futile effort.
