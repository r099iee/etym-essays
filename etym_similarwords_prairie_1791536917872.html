• Steppe: Similar because both denote vast, semi-arid, temperate grasslands dominated by herbaceous vegetation without dense forest cover; differs because steppes (characteristic of Eurasia and parts of Patagonia) are typically drier, colder, and dominated by short bunchgrasses, whereas North American prairies encompass wetter tallgrass and mixed-grass ecosystems with significantly deeper, richer humus soils.
  • Savanna: Similar because both are open, expansive grassy biomes that support diverse herbivore populations; differs because a savanna is typically a tropical or subtropical ecosystem featuring a continuous grass layer interspersed with widely spaced drought-resistant trees or shrubs, whereas a prairie is a temperate biome that is almost entirely treeless outside riverine gallery corridors.
  • Pampa: Similar because both describe fertile, temperate lowland plains covered in rich perennial grasses ideal for grazing and agriculture; differs because pampa specifically denotes the South American grassland biome of Argentina, Uruguay, and southern Brazil, shaped by a milder oceanic climate with different indigenous floral and faunal assemblages.
  • Meadow: Similar because both refer to tracts of open, grass-covered land traditionally used for pasture or hay; differs because a meadow is typically much smaller in scale, often moist or riparian, and frequently nestled within or bounded by forests or cultivated fields, whereas a prairie refers to an immense, self-sustaining continental biome.
  • Plain: Similar because both describe extensive stretches of flat or gently undulating terrain lacking prominent hills or mountains; differs because plain is a broad geological and topographic term that can describe any flat land (including coastal plains, alluvial plains, or barren deserts), while prairie specifically requires a dominant ecological cover of temperate grassland.
  • Veld: Similar because both represent expansive, open pastoral landscapes supporting wild game and cattle grazing; differs because the veld (or veldt) specifically designates the open scrub and plateau grassland landscapes of Southern Africa, subject to subtropical high-altitude climatic patterns.
  • Moor: Similar because both are open, uncultivated, treeless natural landscapes; differs because a moor (or moorland) is characterized by acidic, peaty, waterlogged soils dominated by low heather, gorse, and mosses in cool, wet temperate climates (such as the British Isles), contrasting sharply with the deep, alkaline, drought-hardy sod of continental prairies.
