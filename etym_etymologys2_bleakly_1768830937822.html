Linear derivation chain for BLEAKLY (single root word): PIE *bʰel- (to shine, gleam, burn; to be bright, white) — reconstructed root, c. 4500–3500 BCE; the great Indo-European root of brightness, whose reflexes include Latin flamma (flame), Greek phalein (to shine), Sanskrit bhrājate (it shines, debated), and the Germanic pale-and-shining family. → Extended PIE base *bʰleik- (*bʰel- with a -k- extension: to shine white, to become pale) — the stage behind the Germanic color words; a minority reconstruction writes the extension as *bʰelg-. → Proto-Germanic *blaikaz (pale, white, shining, bleached) — reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE. → Old Norse bleikr (pale, white, wan) — attested from the 9th century CE; the direct source of English bleak, borrowed in the 16th century. → Middle English bleik / bleke (pale) — 14th–15th century (sporadic, chiefly northern). → Modern English bleak (16th century, originally a colour word: pale, wan, bloodless) — 16th century. → Modern English bleak + -ly → bleakly (adverb) ◄ TARGET. Native twin: Old English blāc (pale, shining) and blǣcan (to make pale) — the native Germanic reflex of the same root, the source of English bleach and the native twin of bleak. Note: The chain in English is a borrowing, not a direct inheritance: bleak entered the language from Old Norse bleikr in the 16th century, while the native English reflex of the same Germanic root survives in blāc / bleach. The pre-Germanic extension (whether *bʰleik- or *bʰelg-) and the Sanskrit relationship are points of scholarly debate; the Germanic stage *blaikaz and its color meaning are secure.
