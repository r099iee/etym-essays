The plural noun laces is the form of lace used whenever more than one cord, strip, or trimming is meant, and it carries almost the whole polysemy of the singular. At its plainest, a lace is a narrow cord or tape, especially one passed through eyelets or hooks to draw two edges of fabric together: the shoelace is the everyday type. But the same word names the much grander ornament that socially defined European dress for centuries — ornamental braids, gold and silver thread-work, and above all the openwork fabric of threads knotted, twisted, and stitched into airy webs. In Will Durant's sentence about the Bedouins, “nothing will remain for the laces and frills”, the word stands for precisely that ornamental surplus — the refined trimmings and embellishments of civilization that separate a leisured culture from a bare struggle for food.

Core senses.

  • Fastening cord (most common today): a braided or twisted cord, tape, or thin strap drawn through eyelets to close a shoe, boot, corset, or bodice; also the cord used to lace a garment tightly. This is the sense of shoelaces, bootlaces, corset laces.
  • Ornamental braid or trimming: a narrow plaited band of silk, gold, silver, or other metal thread sewn onto uniforms, liveries, and formal clothing (military ‘gold lace’, ‘lace on a naval officer’s cuffs’).
  • Openwork fabric: the delicate, patterned textile made of threads that are looped, crossed, and twisted (needle lace, bobbin lace, tatting); used for collars, cuffs, veils, and table linens. Distinct from embroidery because lace is a self-supporting web of threads rather than stitching on a ground fabric.
  • Figurative ‘refinements’: in phrases such as ‘laces and frills’ or ‘the laces of civilization’, the word names the non-essential, ornamental accomplishments and amenities that make life civilized — manners, arts, comforts — imagined as the trimming on the fabric of society.

Secondary and technical senses (polysemy and semantic bridges).

  • Verb ‘to lace’ (from which the plural noun’s fastening sense is inseparable): to fasten or interweave with a lace (‘to lace a shoe’), to intertwine threadlike elements, and by extension to thread a cord, ribbon, or wire through something. The bridge is the physical image of drawing a cord through; from this come
  • ‘laced with’: a drink or food ‘laced with’ an ingredient (usually alcohol, spice, or a drug) has that ingredient threaded through it in small but noticeable amounts — the cord-image becomes the tracing of one substance through another; also used for prose ‘laced with irony’.
  • ‘lace curtain’: in Irish-American social history (19th–20th c.), lace-curtain families displayed lace curtains as a badge of respectability and upward mobility, in contrast to working-class ‘shanty’ households; the word thus encodes a whole social drama of aspiration.
  • Technical jargon: in bookbinding and matt-making, cords are ‘laced’ through boards; in fishing and trapping, a ‘lace’ is a snare-line; ‘lacewing’ names an insect whose delicate veined wings resemble lace; ‘lace cookie’ and ‘lacework’ transfer the web-image to cooking and metalwork (filigree).
  • Old sporting sense: ‘to lace into’ someone is to thrash or berate them vigorously — the lash of a laced whip, a violent offshoot of the binding-cord image that survived into early 20th-century slang.

Etymology of the semantic clusters. Old French laz (snare, noose, cord) from Latin laqueus (noose, snare) gave Middle English lace‘ a cord, thong, or net. When such cords grew decorative, the word grew with them: braided trim, then the magnificent openwork fabric, then metaphors of ornament and refinement. Hence one word spans the utilitarian shoelace and the grande dame’s lace collar, and in the Durantian idiom, the entire ornamental surplus of civilization. ‘Laces’ is simply that history made plural.
