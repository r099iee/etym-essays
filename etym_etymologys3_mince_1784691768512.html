The deep ancestry of mince is the Indo-European root *mei-, meaning “small, less,” one of the most productive diminutive bases in the whole language family. From it Greek built meiōn, “less” — the source, via meiōsis, “lessening,” of the rhetorical term meiosis, the deliberate understatement that makes things small by saying less than the truth. Latin built even more industriously: minuere, “to lessen”; minor and minus; minimus, “smallest”; and the past participle minutus, “made small.” Everything that follows in the word’s history is a making-small: minutus produced minute (“tiny”), minutiae (“trifles”), and the Old French adjective menu, which gave English menu — originally the detailed little bill of dishes, written out in small detail. The branch that matters here grew out of the late Latin verb *minūtiāre, “to make small,” which passed into Old French as mincier: to cut up small. In French the word found its permanent home in the kitchen, where mincer meant to chop meat and herbs into the finest possible pieces with a sharp blade. After the Norman Conquest, Anglo-Norman mincer crossed into Middle English as mincen, first recorded in the late fourteenth century — a fresh, fashionable Gallicism in the vocabulary of cookery, arriving in the wave of French food-words (boil, roast, stew, sauce) that the Norman kitchens brought to English tables. From the outset, however, the “make small” idea proved too suggestive to stay at the chopping board. By the fifteenth century mincing described the small, dainty steps of an affected walker, moving as if afraid to take full strides — the same image of reduction applied to movement. By the sixteenth century it described affectedly delicate speech and manners, the “mincing” voice of the would-be fine lady. In the seventeenth century the modern idiom arrived: to mince one’s words, or not to mince matters, is to shrink the force of what one says — to make it small, delicate, and evasive, the exact opposite of blunt speaking. The whole cluster of figurative senses springs from the same metaphor: what is minced is diminished, whether it is meat, a step, or a sentence. Meanwhile the kitchen sense went from strength to strength. Mince-pies, filled with finely chopped dried fruit, suet, and spice (and, originally, minced meat), had been a festive dish since the Middle Ages, and by the Victorian era the noun mince itself stood, in Britain, for the chopped meat bought from the butcher. The phrase to make mincemeat of — to destroy utterly, as a victor reduces a rival to shreds — became a standard idiom of sport and argument by the nineteenth century. American English, characteristically, went its own way with ground meat and hamburger, but the British mince remains firmly in place. In The Hobbit (1937), Tolkien exploits the word’s double character to brilliant comic effect. When the trolls propose to “mince them fine and boil them,” the mild, domestic, recipe-like word is applied to the dwarves — and its very gentleness is what makes the proposition chilling. The reader knows exactly how mince is made: small pieces, fine cutting, a pot. By using the most innocuous culinary verb for the most brutal suggestion, Tolkien lets the horror arrive sideways, through the calm, methodical enumeration of cooking methods, and the word’s long kitchen history is compressed into a single sinister sentence. The career of mince thus illustrates three large facts about English vocabulary. First, the Norman Conquest made the French kitchen a permanent part of English life, and cooking words travelled with it. Second, a concrete root image (“make small”) can generate an entire family of figurative senses — physical fineness, dainty walking, evasive speech — by the same act of metaphorical transfer. Third, a word’s register can be exploited by a writer for tone: mince carries two centuries of domestic gentility into a scene of trollish violence, and the incongruity is precisely the point.
