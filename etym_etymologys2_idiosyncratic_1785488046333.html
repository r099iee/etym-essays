IDIOSYNCRATIC is a compound built from three separate Indo-European roots. Each root’s chain is given in isolation below. ROOT 1 — PIE *swé “self, oneself” (the reflexive pronoun; the root of one’s own-ness) → Greek *swed-yós → ídios “one’s own, private, personal” (Classical Greek; the stem of the prefix idio-) → Greek idiṓtēs “a private citizen, a layman” → Latin idiota → English idiot, idiocy, idiotic (a semantic decay from “layman” to “uneducated person” to “fool”) → Greek idíōma “a peculiarity of one’s own language” → English idiom, idiomatic → Greek idio- + syncrasia (see roots 2 and 3) → idiosynkrasía “one’s own blending” → English idiosyncrasy → IDIOSYNCRATIC ROOT 2 — PIE *sem- “one, together, with” (the root of unity and conjunction) → Greek sýn “with, together” (the prefix syn-, of synthesis, sympathy, synopsis, syntax, syndrome) → Greek sýn + krâsis → synkrasis “a blending together” (also dyskrasia “bad mixture”, eukrasia “good mixture”) → Greek idiosynkrasía “one’s own blending” → English idiosyncrasy → IDIOSYNCRATIC ROOT 3 — PIE *ḱerh₂- “to mix, stir” (the root of mixing and blending) → Greek keránnumi “to mix, mingle” (the symposium’s act of mixing wine with water in the kratḗr) → Greek krâsis “a blending, a mixture” (in medicine, the body’s mixture of humours; also the temperate climate of a region) → Greek synkrasis “a blending together”; idiosynkrasía “one’s own blending” → English idiosyncrasy (early 17th c., medical) → English idiosyncratic (19th c.) → IDIOSYNCRATIC Debated / nuanced stages: the Greek form is compounded from ídios + sýn + krâsis, with the krasis element traditionally derived from *ḱerh₂-; this root equation (Sanskrit śrīṇāti “he mixes, cooks” is the standard cognate) is secure, though the vowel grade of krâsis is debated. The development of Greek idiṓtēs into English idiot is a well-attested but notorious example of semantic pejoration. The English adjective idiosyncratic (19th c.) is built on the earlier noun idiosyncrasy (early 17th c.), which entered English as a medical term from Greek idiosynkrasía via Latin idiosyncrasia.
