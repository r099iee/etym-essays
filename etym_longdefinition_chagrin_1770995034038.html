chagrin: distress, vexation, or embarrassment at having failed or been humiliated - a sharp, chafing sense of wounded pride. The word is almost exclusively a noun in English, though a rare verb form ("to chagrin", "to be chagrined") exists. Core sense - the smart of wounded pride. Chagrin is not ordinary sadness and not ordinary embarrassment; it is the particular, pricking irritation felt when one's expectations of oneself are dashed and one's failure or humiliation becomes visible. It is the emotion of being "rubbed raw" by a public misstep. In the context sentence, a man who has failed in public feels "all the shame and chagrin" and yet must "face his superiors" - the chagrin is the internal smart of the failure, the ego grinding against reality, while the facing of superiors is the external ordeal that follows. The word's etymology (rough leather that chafes) is still live inside the modern sense: chagrin is what pride feels like when it is chafed. Key distinguishing features. Chagrin differs from mere disappointment in being self-directed and ego-involved: it presupposes that one cares about one's standing and that a standard has been missed. It often carries a slightly ironic or wry colour in narration ("much to his chagrin", "to her chagrin") because the sufferer's dignity is mildly absurd - the reader can see that the blow is to vanity as much as to achievement. It is at once humiliating and faintly comic, which is why novelists and journalists reach for it when describing the small, sharp defeats of self-regard. Full polysemy map:
  • Distress or embarrassment at having failed - modern dominant sense.
  • Vexation or irritation at something that thwarts one - "to their chagrin, the plans fell through."
  • Mortified self-consciousness after a social misstep - the modern colloquial core.
  • Historical (17th-18th c.): melancholy, ill humour, anxiety, gnawing disquiet - a broader distress not necessarily tied to failure.
  • Archaic/material: chagrin (and its doublet shagreen) "rough, granular leather" - the original leather sense from which the emotion is metaphorically derived, now obsolete in English under this spelling but preserved in "shagreen".
  • Rare verbal use: "to chagrin" - to distress or vex someone; "chagrined" (much more common) means embarrassed, disappointed, or mortified.
The semantic bridge. The word entered English in the 1650s from French, where chagrin had two careers that blended together. The material word chagrin named rough, granular leather (horse, ass, or shark skin) - from Turkish sağrı, the rump of a horse - which was used as an abrasive that chafed and ground surfaces. The emotion word chagrin named melancholy and vexation. French usage fused the two: grief was imagined as a rough leather that chafes the heart, an abrasive that grinds the pride. English borrowed the merged word whole, and the leather sense split off under the spelling shagreen, leaving chagrin purely emotional. Register and comparison:
  • Chagrin vs embarrassment: embarrassment is a milder, social awkwardness among others; chagrin involves sharper disappointment and can sting even in private.
  • Chagrin vs mortification: mortification is more intense, suggesting pride "killed" by shame (with a religious undertone); chagrin is the sharper chafing irritation of a blow to self-esteem.
  • Chagrin vs humiliation: humiliation emphasizes a public lowering of dignity imposed by others; chagrin emphasizes the internal, self-directed distress at one's own mistake.
  • Chagrin vs vexation: vexation is irritation at external annoyances without humiliation; chagrin follows a failure or slight to one's pride.
  • Register: formal and slightly literary; "to one's chagrin" is a staple of narrative and journalism.
