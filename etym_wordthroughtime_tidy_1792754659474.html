- [c. 1225–1350 Middle English] — Formed from noun tid ("time, season, tide") + -y, meaning "occurring in good time, seasonable, timely, opportune, or seasonably appropriate" — [perception: neutral to laudatory civic and agricultural virtue denoting good timing, favorable weather, or fitting seasons].
- [c. 1380–1500 Late Middle English] — Shifted from temporal suitability to personal condition: "thriving, healthy, vigorous, well-favored, plump, or capable in practical duties" — [perception: highly favorable social appraisal of personal physical robustness, craftsmanship, and prosperous well-being].
- [c. 1530–1650 Early Modern English] — Transitioned from personal health to domestic environment and deportment: "neat, trim, orderly, keeping one's dress or household well-arranged and free from filth" — [perception: strongly associated with domestic competence, bourgeois thrift, moral cleanliness, and protestant household discipline].
- [c. 1740–1850 Georgian & Victorian Eras] — Acquired the colloquial quantitative sense of "considerable, substantial, satisfactory, fairly large" (especially applied to money, dividends, inheritances, or distances) alongside the emergence of domestic objects called "tidies" (chair-back protective covers or receptacles for knick-knacks) — [perception: widely celebrated in commercial and rustic speech as a mark of comfortable, respectable prosperity and modest wealth].
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary English] — Solidified dual polarity: universally used for clean physical organization (with phrasal verb "tidy up"), modern digital hygiene ("tidy desktop", "tidy codebase"), and retained in casual British, Commonwealth, and regional American idioms for comfortable sums ("a tidy profit") — [perception: everyday, comfortable, positive signifier of psychological clarity, minimalist order, and healthy financial reward].
