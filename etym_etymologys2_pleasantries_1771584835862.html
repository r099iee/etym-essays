[Root core] PIE *plāk- "to be flat, to be level, to spread out" (c. 4000 BC; Pokorny *pelāk-/plāk-) — with the derived sense "smooth, calm, therefore agreeable, pleasing"; the semantic path runs FLAT → SMOOTH → CALM → PLEASING [Stage 2] Proto-Italic *plākē- "to be calm, to be pleasing" (reconstructed) — the Italic reflex of the root [Stage 3] Latin plācēre "to be pleasing, to be acceptable, to give satisfaction" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BC; Plautus) — from "to smooth over, to make calm," the "flat → calm → pleasing" extension [Stage 4] Old French plaisir "to please" (9th–10th c.); plaisant "pleasing, agreeable" (present participle) [Stage 5] Old French plaisanterie "a jest, a witty or pleasant remark" (16th c.) — from plaisanter "to joke, to jest" [Stage 6] English pleasantry "a jest, a witty remark; a polite remark" (1650s) — borrowed from French plaisanterie [Stage 7] English pleasantries (plural, 18th–19th c.) "good-humoured, polite, inconsequential remarks; polite conversation" — this card's word Same-root companions: Latin placidus "calm, peaceful, gentle" → English placid (1620s); Latin plācāre "to soothe, to appease" → English placate (1670s), implacable (15th c.); Latin complacēre "to be very pleasing" → English complacent (1650s); Latin placēre → Old French plaisir → English please (13th c.), pleasant (14th c.), pleasure (14th c.); Italian piacere "to please, to delight; (n.) pleasure." Greek πλάξ pláx "a flat surface, a tablet" and Latin placenta "a flat cake" (hence English placenta, so named for its flat, cake-like shape) preserve the root's purely "flat" sense. Note on the debate: The semantic extension FLAT → SMOOTH → CALM → PLEASING is the standard and well-supported account (Pokorny), and the Latin plācēre is not in dispute. The exact grade of the PIE base (plāk- vs. pelāk-) is a minor point of reconstruction among Indo-Europeanists; both forms are given in the handbooks. No stage from Latin onward is doubtful.
