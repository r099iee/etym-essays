Philippics is the plural of philippic, a noun borrowed in the 1590s from French philippique, from Latin (ōrātiōnēs) Philippicae, from Greek Philippikos “of Philip”. In the source context the reference is to Cicero's fourteen speeches against Mark Antony, delivered in 44–43 BCE and modelled on the Athenian orator Demosthenes' four speeches against Philip II of Macedon. In general English a philippic is a bitter, formal speech of denunciation; in the plural, “philippics” can mean either a set of such speeches or a sustained campaign of invective.

The word therefore has two centres of gravity: a fixed historical corpus and a loose rhetorical genre. The historical sense is capitalised (the Philippics); the generic sense is lower case and countable (a philippic, philippics against the government). Both are current, though the generic sense is literary and somewhat rare.

The classical reference

  • Demosthenes (4th c. BCE): four speeches against Philip II of Macedon, delivered between 351 and 340 BCE; they attack Philip's encroachments on Greek liberty and urge Athens to resist.
  • Cicero (44–43 BCE): fourteen speeches against Mark Antony, delivered between 2 September 44 and 21 April 43 BCE; the Second Philippic was never delivered but circulated as a pamphlet.
  • The title: Latin Philippicae (sc. orationes), “the Philippic speeches”, a deliberate pairing of Cicero with Demosthenes as the two great defenders of constitutional liberty against a would-be monarch.

What Cicero's Philippics were about

After Caesar's assassination on 15 March 44 BCE, Antony seized the initiative, secured Caesar's papers and funds, and pushed through legislation in his own interest. Cicero, who had withdrawn from politics, returned to the Senate in September 44 and attacked Antony in the First Philippic; the Second, a savage mockery of Antony's private life and public conduct, was written as if delivered. The later speeches backed the Senate's war against Antony, praised the consuls Hirtius and Pansa, and cautiously promoted the young Octavian. After Mutina in April 43 BCE the Senate's coalition collapsed, Octavian marched on Rome, and the Second Triumvirate was formed. Antony demanded Cicero's death; he was killed on 7 December 43 BCE, and his head and hands were displayed on the rostra.

The generic sense

  • A speech of denunciation: a long, rhetorically elaborate attack on a named person or policy, delivered or written as if for delivery.
  • A written polemic: by extension, a pamphlet or article in the same vein — “a philippic against the railway company”.
  • A series: in the plural, a coordinated set of attacks, as in “the philippics of the press”.
  • Ironic use: modern journalism often applies the word to a rant or a tedious scolding, with an eyebrow raised at the orator's self-importance.

Register and connotation

Philippic is formal and bookish; it belongs to the vocabulary of classical rhetoric, politics and literary reviewing. It implies a public target, an organised argument and a degree of eloquence, and it usually suggests that the attacker believes himself to be in the right. Unlike rant, it does not imply incoherence; unlike diatribe, it does not necessarily imply length for its own sake. Typical collocations: “deliver a philippic”, “launch into a philippic against”, “a philippic on the state of the language”.

Why it matters in this reading

The passage calls the Philippics the speeches “for which he is best remembered”, and in the history of oratory that is fair. It does, however, compress chronology: the consulship, the suppression of Catiline and the title Father of the Country all belong to 63 BCE, whereas the Philippics were delivered two decades later, in 44–43 BCE, when Cicero was in his sixties. The compression explains the passage's logic: the Philippics are the sequel to the Catilinarian speeches, the same voice and the same republican conviction, now turned against a new threat.

Number, spelling and derivatives

  • Number: philippic (singular, countable), philippics (plural); the Philippics (capitalised) for the two classical corpora.
  • Spelling: double l, single p after the first syllable; ph represents Greek phi.
  • Derivatives: the adjective Philippic is rare; Philippian belongs to the city of Philippi, not to the speeches, and should not be confused with Philippine (of the Philippines).
