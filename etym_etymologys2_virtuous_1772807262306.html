DERIVATION CHAIN FOR VIRTUOUS: 1. Proto-Indo-European *wiHrós / *wi-ro- (noun, 'man, freeman, warrior') [c. 4500-3000 BCE; reconstructed, no direct attestation] 2. Latin vir (noun, 'man, adult male, husband'; also virīlis 'manly' > English virile) [attested from the 3rd c. BCE onward] 3. Latin virtūs, genitive virtūtis (abstract noun built on vir + -tūt-, 'manliness, valor, excellence, moral worth') [attested from the 2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus] 4. Late Latin and Christian Latin virtūs (sense-shift: 'moral goodness, virtue, the opposite of vice'; the cardinal and theological virtues) [Late Antiquity, c. 3rd-6th c. CE] 5. Old French vertud / vertu (noun, 'virtue, moral excellence; power, efficacy'; with the adjective vertuous) [attested 10th-12th c.] 6. Anglo-Norman and Middle English vertuous / virtuous (adjective, 'morally excellent, righteous'; also 'having power or efficacy') [attested 13th-14th c., e.g. in Chaucer] 7. Modern English VIRTUOUS (adjective, 'having or showing high moral standards'; historically also 'chaste, pure') [contemporary English] DEBATED / NUANCED NOTES: The surface sense 'full of moral excellence' rests historically on a root meaning 'man.' Sister Indo-Aryan and Celtic forms preserve the human/hero sense rather than the abstract moral sense (Sanskrit vīrá- 'man, hero', Old Irish fer 'man', Old English wer 'man' as in werewolf), so the moral meaning is a Latin semantic specialization, not an inheritance from PIE. The Proto-Indo-European root reconstruction (*wiHrós versus *wi-ro-) is debated in details but the 'man' meaning is agreed. The sound change of Latin virtūs through French vertu involves regular loss of intervocalic stages and the familiar development of Latin -tūt- into Old French -tū; the adjective is formed in French (vertuous) from the noun. Each step here is attested except the reconstructed PIE form, which is flagged as such.
