Derivation chain for famished (from the verb famish): 1. Latin fames (genitive famis) 'hunger' (attested from the earliest Latin literature; also 'famine, want, dearth'; of uncertain origin - no secure PIE etymon has been established, and modern scholarship (de Vaan) records the etymology as unknown). 2. Late Latin famare 'to make hungry, to cause hunger' (a denominative verb formed from fames, attested indirectly through its derivatives and compounds) > Vulgar Latin affamare 'to make hungry, to starve' (prefixed with ad- 'to, towards', giving the sense 'to bring to hunger'). 3. Old French afamer (also affamer) 'to starve, to make hungry, to die of hunger' (9th-14th c.; the source of the English verb; Modern French affamer). 4. Middle English famen / famisshen 'to starve, to die of hunger; to cause to die of hunger' (c. 1400; the forms in -issh- continue the Old French inchoative stem in -iss-, as in per-issh and nour-issh). 5. Modern English famish (verb, now archaic or literary) and its past participle and participial adjective famished 'reduced to starvation, extremely hungry' (the participial adjective is recorded from the 16th c.; the hyperbolic 'very hungry' sense from the 18th-19th c.). Note on the debate: the ultimate origin of Latin fames is unknown; no credible PIE cognate set has been established, and the word is treated by de Vaan as of unclear etymology. Some authorities present English famish as formed directly on the Latin stem (fames > famish with the -ish suffix) rather than through Old French afamer; the mainstream view is that Middle English famisshen continues the Old French verb afamer with its inchoative -iss- element, but the two routes are closely related and both pass through the Latin fames.
