Negro enters English in the 16th century as a borrowing of Spanish and Portuguese negro (black), itself from Latin niger (black, dark). It has no secure PIE etymon because Latin niger is etymologically isolated: unlike ater (dead black) or albus/ater pairs, niger has no clear cognates outside Latin and no accepted PIE root. Older connection to PIE *nekʷ- (night, as in night) is phonetically tempting but rejected by de Vaan and others for lack of regular correspondence. The word must therefore be treated as Latin-primary.

In Latin niger meant glossy black, dark, gloomy, unlucky (as in niger dies, black day), opposed to candidus (shining white). Romance languages preserve it regularly: Italian nero (with rhotacism), Old French noir (with loss of g), Spanish/Portuguese negro (conservative). In Iberia by the 15th century, negro as noun already meant a Black African, reflecting Portuguese exploration of West Africa and the slave trade: João Fernandes, Venetian accounts, Spanish Casa de Contratación records list negros as slaves and servants. The adjective (black colour) and noun (Black person) are thus distinguished early.

  • English borrowing: First English attestations 1550s-1570s (neger, negoor, Negro) in Hakluyt voyages and trade accounts: A Negar, a Blacke Moore. Spelling stabilises as Negro (capitalised) by the 17th century, pronunciation /ˈniːɡrəʊ/ anglicised from Spanish /ˈneɣro/. It replaces earlier Moor (Muslim/North African, vague) and Blackamoor for sub-Saharan Africans.
  • Slavery era: 17th-18th c. plantation English: Negro is the legal-administrative term (Negro slave, Negro woman) in codes from Barbados to Virginia. It is descriptive in law, dehumanising in context, but not yet a slur in form; the slur develops from dialect pronunciation (nigga/nigger) in the 18th-19th c. as a separate pejorative track.
  • Respectful standard: Late 19th-mid 20th c.: capitalised Negro becomes the polite, progressive term demanded by Black leaders themselves. W.E.B. Du Bois campaigned for capital N (1920s-30s); Negro press (Chicago Defender, Pittsburgh Courier), Negro leagues, Negro spirituals, United Negro College Fund (1944). To say coloured or Negro with capital was respectful; nigger was the insult. The source sentence — The American Negro is making this transition today — belongs to this era (likely mid-20th c. sociology/economics), using Negro as the normal dignified term for Black Americans undergoing urbanisation or economic change.
  • Phonetics and spelling: RP /ˈniːɡrəʊ/ with long ee and final -o; American /ˈniːɡroʊ/. Capital N marks human dignity vs lower-case negro (colour). Plural Negroes.

The fall comes swiftly in the late 1960s-1970s with Black Power: Negro is rejected as white-imposed, accommodationist, associated with Jim Crow (Negro entrance, Negro day) even when once proudly claimed. Black (capitalised Black) replaces it, then African-American (late 1980s), then Black again. By the 1980s style guides label Negro dated, offensive if used by outsiders; the Census drops it after 2010-2020 debate. It survives only in historic proper names (United Negro College Fund, Negro Leagues Museum), legal archives, and quotations.

Socio-cultural irony: The same word travelled from respectful self-assertion (I am a Negro — Du Bois, King: The Negro lives on a lonely island of poverty) to taboo within one lifetime. Its Spanish origin (simply black) shows how colour adjectives become racial nouns through slavery and law, then become unacceptable through struggle. Modern readers encountering The American Negro in older books must read it as period-respectful, not as slur, but must not reuse it today; say Black Americans or African Americans.

Debate note: Latin niger's isolation is well-known; Michiel de Vaan (2008) lists it as of unknown origin, possibly borrowed from a non-IE substrate. Any PIE *neg- black reconstruction is ad hoc. English Negro must not be confused etymologically with nigger, though they share the Spanish/Latin source: nigger comes via French nègre and English dialect alteration, with separate pejoration history. Both are now avoided, but Negro retains historical-dignified contexts that nigger never had.
