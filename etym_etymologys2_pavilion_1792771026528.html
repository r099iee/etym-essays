Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*pāp- / *pel-
"to flutter, fly, tremble, unfold"
[Sound-symbolic base imitating fluttering insect wings; cf. Latin palpalis]
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
papiliō
"butterfly, moth; military tent"
[Genitive papiliōnis; Roman soldiers' humorous metaphor for fluttering tent flaps]
↓
Vulgar Latin (c. 4th–6th c.)
*pavilione / *papiliōne
"large military tent, canopy"
[Phonological lenition of intervocalic -p- to -v- across Western Romance]
↓
Old French (c. 1150)
paveillon / pavillon
"chivalric tent, royal canopy, pavilion"
[Prominent in Chanson de Roland and Arthurian romances of Chrétien de Troyes]
↓
Middle English (c. 1250–1300)
pavilon / pavilioun
"magnificent tent, canopy of state"
[Borrowed through Anglo-Norman nobility into English courtly literature]
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1500–1650)
pavilion
"ornate tent; decorative garden house"
[Orthography standardized; extended to architecture and heraldry]
↓
Modern English (present)
pavilion
"grand festive tent; park or sports building"
[Enclosing Bilbo Baggins's grand feast and the Party Tree at Bag End]
