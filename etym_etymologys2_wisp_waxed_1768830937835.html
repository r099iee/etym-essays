[CHAIN FOR wisp]
1. No secure PIE or non-PIE root can be reconstructed for wisp itself; the word is Germanic but of unknown origin. It is probably native English, from an unrecorded Old English word, cognate with Norwegian visp and Swedish visp, “a wisp, a small bundle” (both attested).
2. Middle English wispe “a handful or bundle of hay, straw, grass, or twigs” (late 13th c.; first recorded c. 1275). The material sense: something to kindle a fire, sweep a floor, or sit upon.
3. English wisp: the sense broadens from the material bundle to any small, thin bunch or streak — of hair, smoke, mist (filmy sense first recorded 1836) — and feeds the compounds wispy (1717, earlier wispen 1580) and will-o’-the-wisp (1590s, “Will with the wisp,” the marsh-fire).

NOTE ON DEBATE: A connection to whisk (from Old Norse visk, “a wisp of hay to sweep with,” Proto-Germanic *wisk-, “to sweep, wipe,” possibly PIE *weis-, “to turn, twist”) is often proposed and as often strenuously denied. Every step beyond the Germanic sweep-verb is speculative, and wisp’s own link to it is the most doubtful link of all. The honest verdict: wisp is a Germanic word of unknown ancestry.

[CHAIN FOR waxed]
1. PIE *h₂ewg- “to increase, grow, strengthen” (reconstructed; attested in Sanskrit ójas, “vital strength, vigour,” and vakṣ-, “to grow, increase”; Greek aúxō, “to increase”; Latin augēre, “to increase”).
2. Proto-Germanic *wahsijaną “to grow, to increase” (s-extension of *h₂ewg-, i.e. *h₂ewks-; attested by Old English weaxan, Old Norse vaxa, Old High German wahsan).
3. Old English weaxan “to grow, to increase, to wax” (attested from the 8th c.; a strong verb of class VI, preterite wēox). Used of crops, fortunes, and the moon.
4. Middle English waxen “to grow, to increase” (12th–15th c.).
5. English waxed “grew, increased” — regularized past tense of wax; the word now survives mainly in the idiom wax and wane, in waxing moons, and in phrases like waxed eloquent (late 18th c.), where it means “grew or became.”

NOTE ON NUANCE: The modern noun wax and its verb “to coat or polish with wax” are unrelated homonyms from PIE *wokso-, and must never be derived from the growth-verb *h₂ewg-. The two are a classic etymological trap.
