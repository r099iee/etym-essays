1. The twisting root. The chain begins with PIE *slenk- ‘to wind, turn, twist’ as reconstructed in Pokorny's Indogermanisches etymologisches Wörterbuch: a base found across the family (Lithuanian slìnkti ‘to crawl, creep’, Slavic snake-words) and in Germanic as *slingwaną. The image is coiling — a vine winding round a tree, a rope twining — and it survives into Old English as the strong verb slingan (class 3i: slingan, slang, slungen, exactly parallel to singan ‘to sing’), meaning ‘to wind, twist, turn round’. Cognates across the older Germanic world agree: Old High German slingan ‘to wind, twist, twine’ (modern German schlingen), Middle Dutch slingeren ‘to swing, sling, whirl’. The whirling motion of a sling-strap — coiled and twirled overhead before release — is precisely the winding of this root, so the weapon's name is the root's noun use.

2. The weapon and its arrival in English. The sling is one of humanity's oldest missile weapons — used by the Sumerians, the Egyptians, the Balearic slingers who fought for Carthage and Rome (so valued that slingers were paid mercenary troops), the Roman legions' auxiliaries, biblical tradition's David confronting Goliath, and medieval and early-modern light infantry. The English noun appears as ME slynge in the 13th century, chiefly in religious and biblical contexts — David's sling, the slings of the Philistine host — and the verb slyngen ‘to hurl, fling’ shortly after. OED judges the verb a loan from Old Norse slyngva (the Viking-Age verb meaning ‘to hurl, fling, especially with a sling’), reinforced and influenced by the native OE slingan; the noun is likely formed from or alongside the verb. The strong verb pattern is preserved in the participle slung, still current (“a bag slung over the shoulder”), a fossil of the old conjugation.

3. The 16th–17th centuries: from weapon to metaphor. Shakespeare's Hamlet (c. 1600) gives the word its most famous figuration — “the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” — where slings are the type of hidden, long-range, indiscriminate attack; the phrase remains a fixed collocation for the misfortunes that strike from outside. The verb broadens in the same period from stone-hurling to any vigorous throwing (“he slung the coat at the bench”), and the noun begins its migration from the battlefield to the workshop: sailors and soldiers sling loads, muskets and hammocks.

4. The 17th–18th centuries: the strap becomes a carrier, then a bandage. Gun slings — straps for carrying muskets and rifles — are military-issue from the 17th century; nautical and industrial slings (ropes, chains, later wire and synthetic lifts cradling cargo, barrels, pipes and guns) are standard rigging vocabulary; and by the 18th century the transfer that produces the everyday modern sense is complete: a sling is any strap by which something is hung from the body — and a cloth loop hung from the neck cradles an injured arm. “His arm in a sling” becomes plain domestic and medical English, and (in battlefield medicine of the Napoleonic and later eras) the triangular bandage sling is formalised in first aid.

5. The 19th century: colloquial throw, forked elastic, and the drink. The verb turns colloquial and combative: “to sling mud” (to defame, hurling filth like missiles) and UK slang “sling one's hook” (to decamp — a hook being slung on board a ship as it departs) date from the 19th century. American English coins slingshot for the forked elastic catapult (19th c.), a schoolboy weapon that keeps the old hurling word alive at children's scale. And a new, unrelated sling appears in American bars around 1807: a mixed drink of spirit, sugar, water and flavouring (the gin sling; the Singapore Sling was invented at Raffles Hotel in 1915). Its origin is obscure — the favourite guess is German schlingen ‘to swallow, gulp’, but the OED records it simply as a word of unknown history.

6. The 20th–21st centuries: the sense-radiation completes. The hanging-strap idea now covers parents (the modern baby sling), surgeons (the mesh sling that supports a prolapsed pelvic organ — fascial slings developed from the early 20th century, synthetic meshes later), and logistics (helicopter underslung “sling loads”, crane slings). The participle slung remains the standard word for anything hung loosely from the body, and the verb for casual forceful throwing (“sling it here”). A single twisting root has therefore produced a weapon, a throw, a gun strap, a baby carrier, a surgical implant and an arm bandage — and the arm bandage, the sense our card teaches, is at once the most everyday and the most directly traceable to the ancient coiling of the sling's leather around a whirling hand.
