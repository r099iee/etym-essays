In the phrase "having held communion with a new mind," the word names far more than casual talk or shared opinion. It describes a deep, reciprocal intimacy of intellect and spirit: the observer feels not merely that a new scene has been beheld, but that an exchange has taken place with another intelligence — almost a sacred fellowship of perception. The tone is elevated and quietly religious even when the context is aesthetic; communion implies mutual participation, never one-way observation. The "new mind" is entered into, shared with, not simply inspected. This is the literary sense that dominates high prose, and it already contains the whole history of the word folded into it: a sharing that is total, inward, and almost sacramental.

At its centre, communion denotes the act of sharing something held in common, together with the resulting state of oneness between the sharers. The word always carries a double motion: something is shared out (distributed among participants) and something is shared in (each participant receives a part). That two-way structure explains why communion suggests intimacy of a special grade: it is not the mere transmission of information from one mind to another, but a mutual possession of the same experience, feeling, or truth. Every one of the word's senses, from the altar to the art gallery, preserves this two-way structure.

Polysemy — the full range of senses.
  • The Eucharist / Holy Communion: In Christian usage, capitalised, the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, the central rite in which bread and wine are shared as the body and blood of Christ. "To receive Communion" or "to go to Communion" means to take the consecrated elements. This is the word's oldest fixed English sense and the one that carries its sacramental weight.
  • Fellowship among believers: The spiritual bond uniting all Christians — "the communion of saints," the shared life of the faithful across time and space, declared in the Apostles' Creed.
  • Communion with nature, with God, with the dead: The sense of deep, wordless union with something greater than oneself; Wordsworth's "communion with nature," religious "communion with God" in prayer.
  • Communion of minds / intellectual communion: The meeting and merging of two intelligences — exactly the sense in the target passage ("having held communion with a new mind").
  • A social/ecclesial body: By metonymy, a whole family of churches — "the Anglican Communion," "the World Communion of Reformed Churches" — so that communion names the community itself, not just the act.
  • Sharing of goods (archaic): "Communion of goods," the apostolic community in Acts holding property in common; the older, concrete sense of joint possession.
  • Close / closed communion (ecclesiology): The disciplinary practice restricting who may receive the sacrament; its opposite is "open communion." A technical dogmatic sense.
  • Intimacy and social intercourse (archaic): "Communion" could formerly mean familiar conversation or social dealings; this now rare sense is the bridge between ordinary communication and spiritual union.
  • Psychological communion (modern jargon): In depth-psychology and popular spiritual writing, the felt merging of two minds, or of a mind with a tradition; shared inner experience, sometimes extended to group or telepathic experience.
  • Aesthetic communion (critical jargon): In art criticism, the viewer's felt participation in the creative mind behind a work — the precise register of the target sentence.

The historical bridge runs: shared goods and duties (Latin commūniō as partnership) → mutual participation → the sacrament of the Eucharist → the bond of the believing community → any profound reciprocal union of feeling or thought. Each later sense is an abstraction of the earlier one; the word never lost its core image of something held together and shared out.

Near-synonyms fall short of this charge. Communication is the broad act of transmitting information and need not be intimate or mutual; conversation implies spoken social exchange without spiritual elevation; fellowship stresses companionship and shared status but can lack the mental fusion of communion; rapport is harmonious understanding, usually psychological and secular, lighter than the almost sacramental weight of communion; sharing and union are general and lose the word's specific grade of inward depth.

Collocations include holy communion, communion with nature, spiritual communion, in communion with, close communion, communion of saints, the Anglican Communion. Register is literary, religious, or high-critical; everyday speech prefers talk, connection, or bond. In aesthetic prose the word still signals the highest grade of shared experience — exactly the grade claimed when a viewer feels a work has put him in touch with another mind.
