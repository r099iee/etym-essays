The Etymological Journey of 'Missionaries'

The word 'missionaries' traces its origins through a fascinating linguistic and historical trajectory, deeply rooted in Latin and evolving through French before entering the English lexicon. To understand 'missionaries,' we must first dissect its singular form, 'missionary,' and then its core component, 'mission.'

From Latin 'Mittere' to 'Missio'

The ultimate etymological ancestor of 'mission' is the Latin verb mittere, meaning 'to send, to let go, to dispatch.' This verb was highly productive in Latin, giving rise to a multitude of derivatives that have permeated English, such as 'admit,' 'commit,' 'permit,' 'submit,' 'transmit,' and 'remit.' The past participle stem of mittere is miss-. From this stem, the Latin noun missio (genitive missionis) was formed, signifying 'a sending, a dispatching; a release, a dismissal.' In classical Latin, missio could refer to the sending of troops, the dispatch of a messenger, or even the dismissal of an audience. It also had a specific legal sense, referring to the discharge of a soldier from service.

The Birth of 'Mission' in English

The term 'mission' entered English in the late 16th century, primarily from the French mission, which itself was derived directly from the Latin missio. Initially, in English, 'mission' retained the general sense of 'a sending' or 'a dispatching.' However, it quickly acquired a specialized religious meaning. This semantic shift was largely influenced by the Counter-Reformation and the activities of the Society of Jesus (Jesuits). The Jesuits, founded in 1540, were particularly known for their global evangelistic efforts, and they frequently used the term missio to refer to the specific task or 'sending' of their members to propagate the Catholic faith in foreign lands. This usage solidified the religious connotation of 'mission' as 'an organized effort to spread a religion' or 'a specific task or duty assigned to a person or group, especially a religious one.'

The Emergence of 'Missionary'

The word 'missionary' itself is a later development, appearing in English around the early 17th century (c. 1600-1610). It was borrowed from the French missionnaire, which in turn was derived from the Late Latin missionarius. The Late Latin term missionarius was an adjective meaning 'of or pertaining to a mission,' but it quickly became substantivized to mean 'one sent on a mission,' particularly a religious one. The suffix -arius in Latin (which became -aire in French and -ary in English) is an adjectival suffix forming nouns denoting a person connected with or engaged in a particular activity or place (e.g., 'secretary' from secretarius, 'librarian' from librarius).

The earliest recorded uses of 'missionary' in English specifically referred to members of the Roman Catholic religious orders, particularly the Jesuits, who were sent to convert non-Christians or to re-establish Catholicism in Protestant areas. For instance, the OED cites a usage from 1609 referring to 'Jesuit missionaries.' The context provided in the prompt, 'Roman __________ converted the Angles and Saxons,' while historically accurate in terms of the event, uses the term 'missionaries' anachronistically for the 6th-7th century, as the word itself only came into common English usage much later. However, it aptly describes the role of figures like St. Augustine of Canterbury.

Semantic and Connotative Evolution

Over the centuries, the meaning of 'missionary' has broadened slightly but has largely retained its core religious sense. While it can occasionally refer to someone sent on a non-religious mission (e.g., a 'cultural missionary'), this usage is secondary and less common. The primary meaning remains 'a person sent by a religious body to a foreign country to carry on evangelism or other activities, such as educational or hospital work.' The plural form, 'missionaries,' simply refers to multiple such individuals.

The connotations associated with 'missionaries' have evolved significantly over time, reflecting changing socio-political landscapes. In the early modern period, especially during the Age of Exploration and subsequent colonial expansion, missionaries were often seen as agents of civilization and salvation, bringing Christianity and Western culture to 'heathen' lands. Their work was often intertwined with colonial endeavors, leading to complex and sometimes controversial legacies. From the 18th century onwards, Protestant missionary movements gained immense momentum, establishing vast networks of schools, hospitals, and churches across the globe, contributing significantly to literacy and healthcare in many regions.

In the post-colonial era, particularly from the mid-20th century, the historical role of missionaries has been subjected to critical re-evaluation. While their humanitarian contributions are acknowledged, their association with colonialism, cultural imposition, and the disruption of indigenous societies has led to more nuanced and sometimes negative perceptions. Modern missionary efforts often emphasize partnership with local communities, cultural sensitivity, and a focus on sustainable development, attempting to distance themselves from past models perceived as paternalistic or imperialistic. Despite these shifts, the fundamental role of 'missionaries' as individuals dedicated to spreading their faith and serving others, often in challenging environments, remains central to the word's meaning.
