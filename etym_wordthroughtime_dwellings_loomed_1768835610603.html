dwellings
- From lingering to staying (Middle English) - The verb 'dwell' shifts from 'to delay, tarry, linger' to 'to remain, abide'; if you linger long enough in one place you are no longer wandering but staying.
- To reside (14th c.) - 'To dwell' comes to mean 'to make one's home'; the noun 'dwelling' is formed for the place where people live and remain.
- The word of law and chronicle (15th-18th c.) - 'Dwelling' becomes the sober, administrative noun for a place of residence, the flat term of statute, survey, and chronicle, without warmth or sentiment.
- Modern functionalism - Remains the deliberately colourless word for a place of residence — not a home, not a cottage, but a unit of habitation, chosen for its cold neutrality.
loomed
- The indistinct shape (1540s) - Appears suddenly in the record meaning 'to appear indistinctly, to take shape darkly and largely'; origin obscure, probably the slow, heavy motion of North Sea coastal speech.
- The fog and the sea (16th-18th c.) - Used of ships and land emerging from mist: whatever looms is huge, vague, and menacing, its size magnified by its indistinctness.
- The threatening rise (19th c.) - Extends to anything that towers over something in an oppressive way; the physical shape in the fog becomes an image of pressure and intimidation.
- Modern abstraction - 'Loom' also turns to time, of impending events (a crisis looms), while the physical sense keeps the great shape in the upper air, huge, vague, and oppressive.
