Bubbling is the present participle and participial adjective of bubble. As a verb-form it describes liquid rising in, or throwing up, bubbles — round globules of air or gas enclosed in a thin film of liquid — or making the soft, gurgling sound that such agitation produces. As an adjective it describes anything that is effervescent, lively, or continuously welling up: bubbling laughter, a bubbling personality, a bubbling spring. The word is at once intensely physical (the audible and visible agitation of boiling, fermenting, or effervescing liquid) and lightly metaphorical (the continuous, cheerful upwelling of sound or emotion).

In the source context — “a sort of __________ like the noise of a large pot galloping on the fire” — the word names the sound made by a big pot of liquid boiling hard over a fire: the rapid, gurgling, liquid churn that fills the kitchen with rhythm. The participle is used as a noun-like gerund naming the sound itself, and the comparison to a pot “galloping” beautifully captures the eager, noisy motion that bubbling describes.

Primary senses

  • Producing or rising in bubbles (liquid): “water bubbling in the pan”, “a pot bubbling on the stove”. The liquid is agitated by heat or gas, throwing up globules of air.
  • Making a bubbling sound: “a bubbling brook”, “the bubbling of the coffee percolator”. The word names the liquid gurgle even when few bubbles are visible.
  • Of gas or vapour escaping through liquid: “mud bubbling at the surface of the spring”, “gas bubbling up through the swamp”.
  • As an adjective — lively, effervescent: “a bubbling personality”, “bubbling enthusiasm”. Continuous, cheerful, irrepressible — like champagne.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Emotional welling up: “bubbling with excitement”, “bubbling over with joy”. The emotion rises continuously and audibly, like a boiling pot; “bubble over” and its participial form appear throughout 19th- and 20th-century writing.
  • Bubbling under (the surface): a modern idiom meaning something developing unnoticed or suppressed, about to break out — “tensions were bubbling under the surface”; also used of an artist “bubbling under” the charts, near the threshold of breakthrough.
  • Sound design and onomatopoeia: in audio description, a bubbling sound is the regular, watery, gurgling noise of gas escaping through liquid — beloved of cartoons, horror films, and nature documentaries (mud pools, geysers).
  • Scientific and culinary register: bubbling is the standard term in chemistry and cooking for the formation and release of gas bubbles during boiling, fermentation, or carbonation.
  • Economic connection: the noun bubble has its own famous economic sense (an overvalued market that bursts); the adjective bubbling is rare in finance, but both rest on the image of a fragile, rising sphere that suddenly breaks.

Semantic boundaries and register

Bubbling differs from boiling (which specifies heating to the point of vapour formation and can be violent), from seething (which implies furious, disordered agitation and anger), and from effervescing (which is technical, describing dissolved gas, usually of champagne or soda). Bubbling is the affectionate middle register: audible, cheerful, continuous, and not quite out of control. It is used with equal ease of a pot on a fire, a stream over stones, a person's high spirits, and a sound in the ear — as in the source sentence, where a “sort of bubbling” is one of the rhythmical noises troubling the listener.

Etymology in brief

Bubble enters English around 1300 as bobel, probably borrowed from Middle Dutch or Middle Low German (bobbel, bubbele), and is itself imitative — the word echoes the blub-blub sound of rising gas.
