- lank — Old English hlanc, “loose, slender, empty, gaunt”, from a root of bending and flexibility: the supple thing stretched thin over its bones.
- Middle English lank, “slender, thin, meager” — thinness without tone, the word attaching itself to hair, limbs, grass, corn.
- 19th–20th c.: a literary and slightly dated word of rural or labouring bodies — the adjective of a frame emptied by work; modern speech prefers skinny, thin, wiry, so lank sounds old-fashioned, physical, mournful.
- Present: still the thinness of something no longer held taut — a sheet left to hang, a stalk that has wilted; thin and slack at once.
- stooped — Old English stūpian “to bow down, bend”; Middle English stoupen names a single forward bending.
- By modern usage the participle has become the chronic posture: “stooped with age”, “stooped with work” — a habitual, involuntary forward fold of shoulders and back that is the body's settled condition, and that a writer never ridicules.
- bolls — the rounded seed-capsule of cotton and flax, from a root meaning “to swell”: the swollen ovary that holds the fibre.
- 18th–19th c.: fixed in American agricultural vocabulary — “cotton bolls”, the boll weevil, bollworm — a regional, agronomic word kept close to the picker's hand.
- Present: survives mainly as a technical or dialectal term of the cotton-field economy; in a literary sentence it authenticates the world with botanical exactness.
