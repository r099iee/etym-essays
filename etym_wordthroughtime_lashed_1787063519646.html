- c. 1300 — lash (v.) from Old French lachier "to bind, fasten with cord" — a practical, neutral verb for lacing and tying, used by sailors, tailors, and builders alike.
- 14th c. — a second verb lash appears, "to strike, beat" (origin uncertain, probably imitative of a whip's crack) — now the same sound has two faces: binding cord and striking thong.
- 16th–17th c. — the striking sense dominates in punishment: to be lashed is to be publicly flogged; lash acquires connotations of brutality, discipline, and colonial-era punishment.
- 17th–19th c. — nautical English multiplies senses: lash to the mast, lash down the cargo — lashed becomes the standard term for securing anything against storms, connoting shipboard expertise and desperate security.
- 19th c. — nature becomes the agent: rain, wind, and waves "lash" the shore — the whip metaphor extends to weather, gaining elemental force.
- 19th–20th c. — figurative extension: "lash out" at critics, "lash out in anger" — the physical strike becomes verbal aggression; "lashings of" (plenty) drifts from cord-layers to abundance.
- 20th–21st c. — self-lashing: to "lash oneself" = to criticise oneself harshly; "on the lash" (UK slang) = on a drinking spree; "whiplash" = sudden reversal — the word's violence softens into self-directed guilt, hedonism, and metaphor.
