PIE *prek- "to ask, to entreat, to pray" (reconstructed root of asking/requesting; widely attested across branches; c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE) → Latin prex, precis "prayer, entreaty, request" (noun; attested from the 3rd c. BCE) → Latin precārī "to pray, to entreat, to beg" (deponent verb from prex; attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE; also the source of the verb forms behind English pray and prayer via Old French) → Latin precārius "obtained by entreaty or prayer; held by favour, at the pleasure of another; uncertain, insecure" (adjectival formation from precārī with -ārius; attested in Classical Latin and in the Roman legal phrase precarium, a grant revocable at the grantor's will) → French précaire "precarious" (learned borrowing from Latin; also the legal term) → English precarious "not securely held; dependent on chance or another's will; dangerously likely to fall or collapse; uncertain" (borrowed in the 1640s, first in the legal sense, then generalizing).

Note on the debate/nuance: The derivation of precārius from precārī is secure and uncontested, as is the Roman legal use of precarium (a tenure or grant held at the grantor's pleasure and revocable at will). English borrowed the word directly from Latin in the mid-17th century (the French précaire is a parallel learned form); the legal sense is the earliest in English, and the general senses — dependent on chance, then physically fragile — developed in the 18th century onward. The Indo-Aryan cognates of *prek- (Sanskrit praś-/pṛcch- "to ask," Hindi pūchnā "to ask") belong to the same reconstructed root but are not part of the English loan path.
