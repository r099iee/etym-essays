- [Old English] - Old English bōg/bōh originally named a human arm or shoulder (a 'limb' of the body) before being transferred to the 'limb' of a tree — the large branch springing from the trunk.
- [Medieval narrowing] - The word settles on the tree: the great boughs of oak and ash, principal limbs bearing foliage, still the everyday English word for a big branch.
- [15th c. spelling split] - The -ough spelling is deliberately introduced to set the tree-word apart from the archer's bow; the pronunciations later diverge too, cementing two separate words.
- [16th-19th c. literary golden age] - The word thrives in poetry, carols, and biblical language ('the boughs of the tree'), acquiring a dignified, almost archaic elevation; the olive bough becomes an emblem of peace.
- [Modern register] - In ordinary speech bough has largely yielded to the neutral branch, surviving only where dignity or tradition is wanted — poetry, folk song, the storybook canopy — which is exactly the register Tolkien exploits.
