Aquaplaning is a bright young 20th-century compound of an ancient water-word and an ancient flat-word: Latin aqua for water joined to plane in its sense of skimming flat, plus gerundial -ing. It names two different skimmings — a summery sport towed behind a boat and a terrifying loss of road grip — united by the physics of a surface planing flat over liquid.

1. Aqua from flowing water. PIE *h₂ekʷeh₂- "water" yields Latin aqua "water, sea, rain", Old English ēa "river" (in place-names), Gothic ahwa "river", Old Norse á "river". Latin aqua spawns Romance eau, acqua, agua and English learned borrowings: aquatic, aquarium, aqueduct, aqueous, Aquarius. In the 19th century aqua- becomes a productive English combining form for water sports and technology (aqualung, aquanaut, aquaplane). The vowel qu /kw/ is stable from PIE labiovelar *kʷ.

  • Phonetics: PIE *h₂e- → Latin a-; labiovelar *kʷ → Latin qu /kw/, preserved in English /ˈækwə-/.
  • Semantics: remarkably stable for three millennia — always "water".

2. Plane from flatness to flight. PIE *pleh₂- "flat, broad" yields Greek platus "broad" (whence plate, plaza, place), Latin planus "flat, level" (whence plain, plan, plane-tree with flat leaves, and tool plana "smoothing plane"). Old French planer meant both "to smooth with a plane" and, of birds, "to hover with flat wings, to glide". English borrowed both: tool plane (14th c.) and soaring plane (17th c., as gulls plane). From gliding birds to gliding boats is a short metaphorical hop: a hull at speed lifts and skims flat instead of ploughing through.

3. 1910s: the aquaplane board is born. On American and French beaches after 1910, thrill-seekers stood or knelt on a broad wooden board — the aquaplane, also called aquaplane board — towed by a fast motorboat, leaning against the rope like later water-skiers. Riviera resorts including Cannes adopted it instantly. The verb to aquaplane (1914) and gerund aquaplaning name the pastime. Wodehouse's Angela "nearly got inhaled by a shark while aquaplaning" at Cannes evokes exactly this Jazz-Age glamour sport: a girl towed flat over blue water, trailing limbs a shark might fancy. Contemporary papers spell it aqua-planing and debate its dangers.

  • Phonetics: stress on first syllable A-qua-planing; -ing from Old English -ung / -ing gerund suffix, fully productive.
  • Semantics: "standing on a towed board skimming water" — recreational, elite, summery.

4. 1950s: the motorist's nightmare. Road engineers noticed that at speed on a flooded road, tyres cease to displace water and ride up onto a wedge of water, steering vanishing. British English named this aquaplaning (American hydroplaning, from Greek hydro-), first widely in motoring journals c.1952–1958. The mechanism is identical — a flat surface planing on liquid — but the register flips from delight to dread, taught in driving tests and tyre advertisements. Aviation extends it to aircraft landing on wet runways.

5. Modern split. Today dictionaries list both senses in order of frequency: (1) loss of tyre grip on a wet surface; (2) historical water-sport on a towed board. Water-skiing, wakeboarding, and inflatables have largely replaced the old aquaplane, so the sporting sense survives mainly in period fiction like Wodehouse, while the motoring sense dominates manuals. Both keep the gerundial -ing for the activity or event: "aquaplaning killed the rally" versus "they went aquaplaning".
