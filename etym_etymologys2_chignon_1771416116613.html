1. PIE *kat- “to link, twist, weave” — the traditional anchor for Latin catena, but handbooks debate it: some scholars prefer a Mediterranean, Etruscan, or otherwise non-Indo-European loan into Latin. The PIE status is therefore uncertain, not established. 2. Latin catena “chain, fetter, bond” — attested from Plautus, c. 200 BCE. 3. Vulgar Latin *cateniōnem “a small chain, a neck-chain; a neck-iron” — accusative of a derived noun *catenio, built on catena. 4. Old French chaignon “iron collar, fetter; (by metonymy) the nape of the neck” — the neck-collar worn on the neck gives its name to the strip of flesh beneath it. 5. Middle French chignon “nape of the neck” — the metonymy complete; attested from the 14th c. 6. French chignon “the nape; (18th c.) the low hair-knot worn at the nape” — the hairstyle sense develops because the knot sits on the nape. 7. English chignon (1780s) “a coil or knot of hair worn at the back of the neck” — borrowed as a French fashion word; English chignon, the root word. Note on the debate: the sole genuinely uncertain link is stage 1. If Latin catena is not from PIE *kat-, the word itself may be a loan from an unidentified substrate; everything from Latin catena onward is securely attested.
