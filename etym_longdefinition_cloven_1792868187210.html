Cloven is the classic past participle of cleave in its splitting sense: divided, split, or cleft through the middle, usually all the way or deeply enough that the two halves read as separate lobes, prongs, toes, or grooves.

In the source sentence the travellers plunge into a deeply cloven track between night trees, and here the word is topographical: the path has been split into deep parallel ruts, as if some great blade had cleft the earth, so the walkers descend between raised banks exactly as a toe descends beside its fellow in a hoof-print. The choice is countryman-precise: a lane worn by cart wheels, rain, and hooves until it runs double, shadow brimming in the furrows while dry leaves rustle overhead, the road itself seeming wounded open.

Core meaning in detail: To call something cloven is to say the split defines it. A plank may be split and still be one plank mended; a cloven thing wears its doubleness as its identity. The paradigm runs cleave, clove or cleft, cloven: the strong-verb music of the word, with its deep vowel shift, already sounds ancient, which is why poets reach for cloven where prose would settle for split. The division is normally binary and longitudinal, following the grain of the thing, whether that grain is wood fibre, rock strata, or the anatomy of a foot.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Split or cleft in two (general, oldest sense): cloven wood, cloven rock, a cloven path. The bridge is the verb itself: whatever a cleaver or a frost or a wheel-rut cleaves is left cloven, and the participle keeps the completed force of the blow.
  • The cloven hoof (zoology and Scripture): the divided foot of even-toed ungulates — cattle, sheep, goats, deer, pigs — where the third and fourth toes form twin digits in a keratin sheath. The bridge is anatomical description hardened into a term of art by the King James Bible, where clean beasts are those that parteth the hoof and are clovenfooted, so that cloven still carries a Levitical echo in farmyards.
  • The Devil's cloven foot (demonology and folklore): the giveaway hoof-print of Satan, who in legend cannot hide his goat's foot however fine his coat. The bridge runs from the pagan god Pan, goat-legged piper of the wilds, whose shaggy thighs the early Church re-drew as the Adversary, so that a barnyard anatomy became the signature of evil and countless tales turn on a glimpse of the cloven shoe.
  • Cloven tongues (Pentecost imagery): the tongues like as of fire that sat upon each disciple, described as parted or distributed flame-tips. The bridge is visual: a flame-tip forks exactly as a hoof forks, and translators chose cloven to render the dividing fire, giving the word its one great moment of holiness to set against its diabolic twin.
  • Botany and geology (cleft leaves, cleft summits): cloven leaves, a cloven ridge, cloven clouds parting at dusk. The bridge is shape-analogy: botanists and landscape writers borrow the hoof's fork for anything deeply notched, so that a beech leaf and a mountain skyline can share one adjective.
  • The cleave paradox (usage note, not a separate sense): English famously owns two verbs spelled cleave, one meaning to split apart and one meaning to cling together, and learners often ask whether cloven can mean clinging. It cannot: cloven, clove, and cleft belong wholly to the splitting verb, while the clinging verb makes cleaved or clave. The bridge worth remembering is negative: the two verbs converged in spelling from wholly different roots, and cloven never crossed the line.

Register and connotation: cloven is literary, biblical, and slightly eerie — farmyards, pulpits, ghost stories, and fantasy novels are its home ground. Nobody at a building site calls timber cloven; they call it split. But where the division matters symbolically, purity against impurity, hoof against shoe, fire against night, no blunter word will do. It sounds what it means: the dark vowel and the softened ending fall like an axe-blow remembered, and even the night track of the source sentence feels older and more fateful for being cloven rather than merely rutted.
