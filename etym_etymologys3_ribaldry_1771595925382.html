The story of ribaldry begins at the fringes of medieval society, in a Germanic verb meaning "to rub." Since "to rub" was a common euphemism for sexual contact, the Proto-West Germanic base *wrīb-/*rib- carried an informal sense "to be wanton," and Old High German riban is glossed precisely so — "be wanton," literally "to rub." Whether this base descends from a still older Proto-Indo-European root — Etymonline proposes *wer- (2), "to turn, bend," which would make ribaldry a distant cousin of Latin vertere, "to turn," and of English wring, wrestle, and writhe — is uncertain and must be labelled speculative. What is certain is that the base entered the Frankish-influenced Old French of the post-Carolingian world as riber, "to be wanton, to sleep around, to dally amorously," and from it, with the depreciative suffix *-ald/*-aut, came the noun ribaut (or ribalt): the rogue, the scoundrel, the lewd lover — in Cotgrave's seventeenth-century English, "a filthie fellow."

The ribald in medieval society

In Old French literature the ribald belongs to the disorderly poor — camp-followers, vagabonds, drunken jesters, mercenaries — and the word is used of them with open contempt. The ribald is one who lives below the standards of honest men: licentious, dissolute, given to lewd jesting and idle abuse. The word is thus born as a class insult, a name for the human refuse of the medieval road, and it carries that social contempt inside it for centuries. This is why ribaldry is never merely "indecent speech": it is the speech of the degraded — language that belongs to the tavern floor and drags its object down to that level. Ruskin's Victorian usage, calling the press's mockery of high art "ribaldry," draws exactly on this residue of class contempt.

The journey into English

English took the noun early: ribaud is attested from the mid-thirteenth century (and as a surname from the late twelfth), meaning "rascal, ruffian," and from the late fourteenth century "one who jests irreverently or uses offensive language." Alongside it came two abstracts built on the French model: ribaudie (late thirteenth century) and ribaudrie (late fourteenth century), which at first mean "debauchery, licentiousness" and only then narrow to "bawdy speech, obscene or coarse language." The crucial semantic shift is from the person to the speech: the word for a worthless scoundrel generates the word for a scoundrel's tongue. The sexual, licentious sense of the base word — "to be wanton" — was already fading by the sixteenth century as the noun settled on mockery and jeering as its core.

Literary career

  • Chaucer: the family appears in the Canterbury Tales — the Miller's ribaudye — where the coarse, japing humour of the lower orders is both enjoyed and socially marked.
  • Shakespeare and Elizabethan drama: the adjective ribald (c. 1500) means "offensively abusive, wantonly irreverent, coarse," and Elizabethan and Jacobean dramatists use it freely of the foul-mouthed clown, the tavern wit, the scurrilous jester.
  • Ruskin (19th c.): in the passage at hand, Ruskin turns the word on the Victorian press: "While the press vents its ribaldry on the most exalted truth, it becomes the duty of all who know what is great to come forward." The word names the gutter mockery directed at Turner and high art, and Ruskin counterposes it to "what is great" and to the duty of the knowing few — an aristocratic, disdainful note that revives the word's old class meaning.
  • Modern retreat: in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, ribaldry withdraws from everyday speech. The gentler adjective ribald survives in affectionate use — "ribald humour," "a ribald song" — meaning earthy, coarse-comic without malice. The noun keeps its older, strongly pejorative force and is now mostly literary or historical.

Sound and form

Formally the word shows the standard French-to-English route: Old French ribauderie → Middle English ribaudrie → modern ribaldry, with the modern spelling settling by the sixteenth century. The -dry ending is the English re-analysis of the French -erie suffix; the doublet ribaudie (from Old French ribaudie, "debauchery") died out, leaving ribaldry as the sole survivor. The adjective ribald preserves the older form of the stem. Across its whole history the word has kept two constant features: a register (low, coarse, gutter-level) and a target (something higher that it mocks). That is what makes it so exact a weapon for a critic defending the sublime against the jeering crowd.
