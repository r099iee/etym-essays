careworn is an adjective that describes a face, look, person, or even an object that visibly bears the marks of prolonged anxiety, sorrow, responsibility, or hard work. A careworn face is lined, tired, strained, or prematurely aged, not from a single night of poor sleep but from months or years of carrying trouble. The word fuses inner state and outer sign: care has worn the person, as friction wears cloth, leaving creases, droop, pallor, and a subdued expression.

The primary and most common use is for human appearance. Novelists write of careworn mothers, careworn clerks, careworn widows, careworn faces, eyes, brows, and smiles. It evokes sympathy rather than distaste: we are meant to read suffering and endurance, not neglect. A careworn woman in Dickens or Hardy has nursed the sick, lost money, buried kin, or worked without rest, and her face keeps the ledger. In this sense it is close to haggard, gaunt, drawn, and weary, but gentler and more dignified, implying moral weight rather than illness or dissipation.

A secondary extended sense applies to manner and voice. A careworn voice sounds tired and burdened; a careworn laugh is brief and effortful; a careworn air suggests someone trying to be composed while weighed down. Here the focus is less on wrinkles than on emotional tone: low energy, muted humour, patience under pressure.

  • Facial / bodily appearance: lined, tired-looking from long worry; e.g. a careworn face, careworn hands. Bridge: worry imagined as friction that abrades and furrows the surface.
  • Emotional / biographical sense: burdened by prolonged responsibility or grief; e.g. a careworn mother of five, a careworn official. Bridge: metonymy from visible mark to life history that caused it.
  • Manner / voice: sounding or seeming weary and preoccupied; e.g. a careworn smile, a careworn tone. Bridge: transfer from permanent face to momentary expression.
  • Figurative for things: worn by care in the sense of use and anxiety; e.g. a careworn coat, a careworn house, a careworn suitcase. Bridge: objects that have been anxiously kept and overused acquire the pathos of their owners, a pathetic fallacy common in 19th-century fiction.
  • Humorous / hyperbolic use (as in the source): even the most careworn, i.e. the most solemnly depressed person imaginable, would be diverted by a popping bath toy. Bridge: deliberate overstatement for comic bathos, treating amusement as medicine for deep sorrow.

Connotation is sympathetic, literary, and slightly old-fashioned. Careworn belongs to narrative and descriptive prose more than to everyday speech, where tired, stressed, or worn out would be used. It carries no blame: to call someone careworn is to honour what they have endured, not to criticize their looks. Antonyms are carefree, fresh-faced, radiant, and unlined. In the source sentence the comedy depends on this gravity: a trivial sponge-and-duck trick is solemnly presented as powerful enough to cheer even a person ground down by life, elevating bath-time foolery to the level of tragedy cured.
