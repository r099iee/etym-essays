English QUAGMIRES — plural of quagmire (count noun), a compound formed in English in the 1570s from two existing English words of different origin: quag (shaking, boggy ground) + mire (mud, swamp). The two elements therefore have separate chains. CHAIN 1 — QUAG ("shaking bog, marshy ground that gives way"): 1) No secure PIE etymon: quag is an expressive / echoic English word of the 16th c., formed within the cluster of "shaking, trembling, yielding" words around quake (Old English cwacian "to shake, tremble"). Its exact derivation is debated — see note below. 2) Old English cwacian "to shake, tremble, quake" (verb, attested from the 8th c.) — the nearest certain ancestor of the sound-sense family; also cwacung "quaking, trembling." 3) Middle English quaken "to shake, tremble, quiver" (verb, attested 13th–15th c.), with related expressive forms such as quabbe "bog, marsh" (a soft, yielding place) — dialectal marsh vocabulary built on the same qu-/cw- sound symbolism. 4) English quag "a marshy, boggy spot; ground that shakes or yields underfoot" (noun, first attested c. 1550s–1570s) — an expressive noun possibly formed from the verb family of quake or as an independent echoic formation for soft, shaking ground; its precise derivation is uncertain. 5) English quagmire "soft, boggy, yielding ground" (compound of quag + mire, first attested 1570s) — literally "quaking mud"; the plural QUAGMIRES is the target root word. CHAIN 2 — MIRE ("mud, swamp, bog"): 1) PIE *mer- / *mei- "to smear, rub, soften, wet" (reconstructed; the wet-mud semantic field is reconstructed variously; cognates include the root behind "marsh" and wet-ground words across the family) — exact reconstruction debated, projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Germanic *miuzijō- / *mir- "bog, swamp, wet mud" (reconstructed; the source of the Old Norse noun) — projected c. 500 BCE–200 CE; cf. Old Norse mýrr "bog, swamp, moor." 3) Old Norse mýrr "bog, swamp, moor, marsh" (feminine noun, attested from the Viking Age) — the direct donor to English. 4) Middle English mire "mud, swamp, deep mud" (noun, borrowed from Old Norse mýrr, attested from c. 1300) — also as verb "to sink in mud, to entangle" (attested 14th c.). 5) English mire "wet mud, bog; a condition of entrapment" (noun and verb, continuing Middle English) — the second element of the compound. 6) English quagmire "soft, boggy, yielding ground" (compound of quag + mire, first attested 1570s); the plural QUAGMIRES is the target root word. Note on debate: The etymology of quag is uncertain. Most dictionaries treat it as an expressive or echoic formation of the 16th century, probably related to quake (OE cwacian) but not necessarily a direct morphological descendant; some scholars group it with dialectal quab/quabbe "bog" of sound-symbolic origin. There is no securely attested intermediate form, and no PIE root is reconstructed for it. The chain for mire is secure from Old Norse mýrr onward (borrowed into Middle English c. 1300); the deeper PIE reconstruction is looser and is written here with the caveat that handbooks differ (some cite *mei-, some *mer-). The compound date 1570s is the standard first-attestation date given by dictionaries.
