- 16th c. — Rhetorical insertion: The noun parenthesis enters English from Greek via Late Latin, strictly denoting an oral rhetorical device where an orator inserts an explanatory clause that temporarily interrupts the sentence’s syntax.
- 17th c. — Typographical glyphs and early adjectival forms: Renaissance printers formalize curved round brackets ( ) to demarcate parenthetical speech, while early adjectives like parenthetic appear to describe clauses contained inside them.
- Late 18th c. — Emergence of parenthetical: British grammarians and literary critics coin and popularize the adjective parenthetical (c. 1775–1785), establishing a formal vocabulary for qualifying clauses and digressive stylistic structures.
- 19th c. — Victorian metaphorical expansion: Essayists like Thomas Carlyle and novelists like Charles Dickens extend the word metaphorically to human existence, describing brief interludes, pauses, and incidental biographical phases as ‘parenthetical chapters’ of life.
- 20th c. — Academic publishing apparatus: The explosion of academic journals and standard citation guides (APA, Harvard, Chicago) institutionalizes ‘parenthetical citations’ and cross-references (such as ‘see Figure X’), transforming the word into an indispensable scholarly tool.
- Today — Scriptwriting jargon and conversational style: Screenwriters use parentheticals as a noun for actor tone instructions; in digital communication, parenthetical remarks describe casual asides dropped into casual chat or social commentary.
