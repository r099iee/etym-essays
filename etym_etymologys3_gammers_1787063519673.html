Gammer is a phonetic contraction of godmother, and its history is the history of an everyday word worn smooth by use. In Old English the sponsor of a child at baptism was the godmodor ("god-mother"), formed from god (used here in the special sense of "sponsor, spiritual kin") and mōdor ("mother"). The corresponding masculine form was godfæder, "godfather". Throughout the Middle Ages these were words of the highest social importance: to be asked to stand as godparent was an honour and a duty, and the spiritual kinship it created was taken with complete seriousness in canon law — a man could not marry his child’s godmother, and godparents were bound to provide for their godchildren’s religious education. It is no surprise that such important, frequently spoken words should develop colloquial shortenings.

The contraction. In the 16th century the compressed forms appear in the records: gaffer for godfather and gammer for godmother. The phonetic process is a natural one — the reduced first syllable gad- or god- and the following consonant of the second element fused, the unaccented middle vowels falling away, giving gaffar and gammar. These forms are recorded from about 1560 onward, and the early spellings show the transition: "godfather" and "godmother" in formal documents, "gaffer" and "gammer" in plays, letters, and jests. Shakespeare and his contemporaries use "gaffer" for old men; "gammer" is less frequent on the stage but appears in the same homely register.

Root 1: god. The element god- is the ancient English word for the deity, Old English god, from Proto-Germanic *gudą, itself from the Indo-European formation *ǵʰutó-, "that which is invoked", built on the root *ǵʰew-, "to call, to invoke". In "godmother" and "godfather", "god" does not mean "deity" but carries the specific sense of spiritual kinship: the person who, in the place of the deity, sponsors the child. The same prefix appears in "gossip", which is a worn-down form of Old English godsibb ("god-relative"), first "godparent", then "a close friend", then, because godparents were expected to exchange confidences, "a person who talks about other people" — and finally the word for the idle talk itself. The family of god- compounds thus shows the same social progression as gammer: spiritual title, then neighbourhood intimacy, then village talk.

Root 2: mother. The element -mer carries the mother-word: Old English mōdor, Proto-Germanic *mōdēr, and the Indo-European *méh₂tēr, "mother" — one of the most stable words in the entire Indo-European family (Latin māter, Greek mḗtēr, Sanskrit mātár). In the compound it is reduced from "mother" to "mmer" — the final "mer" of "gammer". The masculine counterpart, gaffer, similarly preserves the f- of "father".

Semantic drift. The decisive shift happens within the 16th and 17th centuries. Because godparents were typically older, married neighbours — people of standing in the parish — the words gaffer and gammer came to be used simply for "old man" and "old woman", with no godparent relationship implied, in the same way that "granny" outgrew its strict kinship sense. The words also became terms of address — "Gaffer Goody", "Gammer Gurton" — and entered the folklore of village life. "Gammer Gurton’s Needle" (c. 1563) is one of the earliest English comedies, and its title-figure, the sharp-tongued village old woman, fixed "gammer" as the stock comic elder. From the 17th century onward "gaffer and gammer" is a stock pairing for the old folk of the parish, the keepers of memory, remedies, and gossip.

Modern fate. In the 19th century, as industrialisation reshaped English society and the old dialects began to recede, "gammer" gradually left everyday speech and survived chiefly in dialect dictionaries, folk-tale collections, and regional literature. In the 20th century it is a consciously archaic and literary word, chosen by writers for its rural, old-fashioned flavour. Tolkien’s "greybeards and gammers" in The Hobbit is exactly such a deliberate choice: the pairing of "greybeards" (old men) with "gammers" (old women) evokes the folk memory of the town, the elders whose youth-long memories of the dragon are ridiculed by the sceptical young. The word’s whole history — spiritual title, village honour, worn-down colloquialism, folk-memory — is compressed into that single old-fashioned plural.
