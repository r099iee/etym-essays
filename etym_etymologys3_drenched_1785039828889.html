THE BIOGRAPHY OF DRENCHED: FROM "TO CAUSE TO DRINK" TO "SOAKED TO THE BONE" 1. THE PREHISTORY: A GERMANIC WORD OF UNCERTAIN ANCESTRY The story of drenched begins in the prehistoric period of the Germanic languages, with the strong verb that the Germanic peoples used for drinking. The standard reconstruction is Proto-Germanic *drinkaną, "to drink," a strong class III verb whose pattern is visible in all its descendants: Old English drincan (Modern English drink), Old Norse drekka, Gothic drigkan, and Old High German trinkan (Modern German trinken). Its ultimate Indo-European ancestry is genuinely uncertain. A PIE root *dhreg- has been suggested, with a sense "to draw, drag," from which "to draw in liquid" would give "to drink," but the evidence is thin: the root has no certain cognates outside Germanic, and reputable dictionaries treat the word as of unclear origin rather than confidently PIE. This is worth stating plainly, because popular etymology loves to invent tidy chains, and the honest scholarly answer here is that we can reconstruct the Germanic stage with confidence and must leave the deeper stage open. 2. THE CAUSATIVE DOUBLING: *drankijaną What is securely attested is the grammatical doubling that created drench. Indo-European and its Germanic descendant had a regular morphological device: beside a basic strong verb stood a causative twin, formed by vowel mutation, meaning "to make someone do the action." The pattern is everywhere in English — stink/stench, sit/set, fall/fell, lie/lay, rise/raise. Beside *drinkaną "to drink," Germanic built *drankijaną "to cause to drink." The two verbs then had entirely separate careers. While *drinkaną stayed with its straightforward meaning, *drankijaną moved into a whole cluster of meanings: to water livestock, to ply a guest with drink, to make a man drunk, to soak, to steep, to submerge, and finally to drown. The causative's descendants fan out across the Germanic world with remarkable consistency: Old Norse drekkja, Swedish dränka, Danish drukne, Dutch drenken, German tränken, and Gothic dragkjan all mean "to give to drink," and most of them drift naturally toward the darker sense "to drown" — the Drowning of the Vikings' enemies in saga prose is routinely described with this verb family. It is a striking fact of cultural history that "to make someone drink" and "to kill someone by water" were, in these languages, the same act. 3. OLD ENGLISH: THE WHOLE SPECTRUM, FROM HOSPITALITY TO MURDER Old English inherited the causative as drencan, and the word's meanings in the oldest records show the full spectrum at once: "to give drink to," "to make drunk," "to soak," "to saturate," "to submerge," and "to kill by drowning." The Anglo-Saxon poet of Beowulf uses the verb and its relatives in contexts of water and doom; the verb could describe a king drenching his guests with ale or a sea drenching the land. In this period the drinking sense is still fully alive, and the modern reader can feel the original logic working: to drench something is to force it to drink. The passive participle gedrencan (later drenched) already carries the sense "soaked, saturated," especially of things and persons overwhelmed by water — ships, fields, bodies, and souls. 4. MIDDLE ENGLISH: THE WATER-SENSES TAKE OVER When the word entered Middle English as drenchen around 1200, the balance shifted decisively. The drinking senses faded from ordinary use, and the watery senses moved to the front: drenchen meant "to submerge," "to sink," "to drown," and "to soak." In medieval romance and chronicle, men are drenched in rivers and floods; ships are drenched and lost; the verb is a word of drowning more than of drinking. It is at this stage that drenched and drowned became near-synonyms in English — the fourteenth-century poem Cleanness describes the Flood as the moment when the whole world was drenched. The ancient causative logic survives only as a fossil: no medieval English speaker would have connected drench with drink, but the connection was there in the very sound of the word, hidden beneath five centuries of water. 5. EARLY MODERN ENGLISH: THE SHIFT OF 1550 Around 1550 the crucial modern shift occurred. Drench came to mean "to wet thoroughly by throwing liquid over," and the violence of drowning was tamed into the thoroughness of a good soaking. The Tudor chroniclers and playwrights begin using it of rain and of deliberate wetting; the sense "to administer a medicinal liquid to an animal" also established itself in the same period, as veterinary practice became a distinct craft and needed a verb for forcing liquid down an animal's throat. From this point the word has two stable careers: the general literary sense (soaked through by weather or water) and the professional husbandry sense (forced to drink medicine). Both descend directly from the ancient causative "to make drink." 6. MODERN ENGLISH: THE VICTIM OF WETNESS In Modern English, drenched and its past participle drenched occupy a precise emotional and grammatical position. The word describes a passive subject completely penetrated by liquid, and it is this passivity that separates it from soaked. A sponge soaks; a victim drenches. The water is the agent, the person the patient. This is why the word lends itself so readily to metaphor: drenched in sunlight, drenched in sweat, drenched in sorrow — in every case an external medium pours in and fills the subject to capacity, and the subject has no defence. The metaphorical reach is a direct inheritance of the causative grammar: the element acts; you receive it. 7. TOLKIEN AND THE STORM (LITERARY ATTESTATION) In The Hobbit, chapter three, Tolkien writes: "Soon they were getting drenched and their ponies were standing with their heads down and their tails between their legs, and some of them were whinnying with fright." The choice is exact. The progressive "getting drenched" keeps the water as an ongoing agent; the word is passive, continuous, and cumulative, and it rhymes emotionally with the drooping ponies, the tucked tails, and the frightened whinnying. Tolkien, a philologist who knew the Old English verb drencan and its drowning senses, uses the full inherited weight of the word: the travellers are not merely wet — they are being made to drink the storm, submerged in the mountain's weather as if in water. Every stage of the word's history is audible in that single sentence. KEY STAGES AT A GLANCE. - *dhreg- (hypothesized PIE root, "to draw, drink") — debated, no certain cognates outside Germanic. - *drinkaną (PGmc, "to drink") — the base verb; ancestor of drink. - *drankijaną (PGmc causative, "to cause to drink") — the grammatical twin; ancestor of drench. - drencan (OE, 8th c., "to give drink, make drunk, soak, drown") — the full spectrum. - drenchen (ME, c. 1200, "to submerge, drown, soak") — water-senses take over. - drench (EModE, c. 1550, "to wet thoroughly") — drowning tamed into soaking. - drenched (ModE, "soaked, saturated, overwhelmed") — the victim of wetness, literal and figurative.
