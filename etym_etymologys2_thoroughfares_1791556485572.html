[Chain 1: thorough (through)]
PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*terh₂- / *tr̥h₂-
"to cross over, pass through, overcome"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*þurx
"through, from one side to the other"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c.)
þurh / þuruh
"through, completely, from end to end"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th c.)
thurgh / thorow
"through, thorough, unobstructed"

[Chain 2: fare (journey/way)]
PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*per- / *por-
"to go across, journey, traverse"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*farō / *faraną
"passage, track, to travel, to go"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c.)
faru / faran
"journey, passage, course of travel"
↓
Middle English (c. 14th c.)
fare
"a journey, passage, going"

[Synthesis: Compound Formation]
Middle English (c. late 14th c.)
thurgh-fare
"unobstructed passage, road open at both ends"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
thoroughfare
"public street open to through traffic"
↓
English (modern)
thoroughfares
"major public roads open at both ends"

Note: *Thoroughfare* is a native Germanic compound formed by combining *thorough* (the stressed adjectival/prepositional variant of *through*) with *fare* (the deverbal noun from *faran* "to journey, travel"). In Middle English, the noun first denoted the act of uninterrupted passage before shifting metonymically to the physical road itself.
