A. SWIFT — the speed word. Old English swift (adjective) meant moving quickly, in rapid motion, done at high speed, perhaps originally turning quickly. Etymonline derives it from Proto-Germanic *swip- with past-participle suffix -t (as swift is to swifan as gift is to give), from the strong verb Old English swifan "to move in a course, revolve, sweep" (Class I, past swaf, participle swifen), from Proto-Germanic *swif-, *swip- (Old Frisian swiva to be uncertain, Old Norse svifa to rove, ramble, drift), perhaps from PIE *swei- "to turn, bend, move in a sweeping manner", though the dictionary cross-refers to swing for doubts. The semantic path from sweeping curve to straight speed is natural: what sweeps round fast goes fast. By mid-12th century Swift is a surname (a fast runner), by mid-14th century the adjective means prompt or ready to act without delay (swift to anger, swift witness), and by the 1660s the noun names the bird Apus apus for rapid flight (young swiftlets 1892), earlier also small fast lizards (1520s), with folk reputation as ill-omened screamer.

Phonology is straightforward: OE swift /swift/ keeps /sw/, short i, and final ft into modern /swɪft/, spelling unchanged for a millennium. Compounding is prolific: swift-running, swift-footed, swift-handed, swift justice, swift boat, SWIFT code. The bare adjective never leaves speed, but speed's moral colour shifts — swift can praise (swift aid) or threaten (swift punishment), be physical (swift river) or mental (swift reply). In swift-eddying it is purely physical and visual: bulk velocity over pebbles.

  • From curve to haste: if original sense was turning quickly, the narrowing to linear speed hides the circle; Ruskin's compound restores it by re-adding eddying. The hendiadys is thus etymologically redundant and poetically exact: swift (once turning) eddying (turning again), speed remembering its curve.
  • Bird transfer: 1660s swift for Cypselidae shows the adjective's force: the fastest bird in level flight (up to 69 mph) earns speed as name, displacing older deviling, screamer. Country people hear summer screaming parties round towers and know holidays have come. The Tay's swifts wheel above its eddies, closing the loop.

B. EDDY / EDDYING — the back-turning water word. English eddy (noun) is mid-15th-century Scottish ydy, possibly related to Old Norse itha "whirlpool", from Proto-Germanic *ith- "a second time, again", akin to the common Old English prefix ed- "again, backwards; repetition, turning" seen in eddingung reconciliation, edgift restitution, edniwian to renew, edhwierfan to retrace steps, edgeong to become young again, and crucially Old English edwielle "eddy, vortex, whirlpool". The prefix itself is from PIE *eti "above, beyond" (Latin et and, Old High German et-, Gothic ith and, but, however). The analysis is therefore ed- (back, again) + water: water going back on itself. Old Norse itha confirms the sense whirlpool; the Scottish first record fits northern fishing waters where tidal races and spate rivers breed recirculation.

Verb to eddy is later: transitive 1730, intransitive 1810, participle eddying thereafter. Once verbal, the word generalises rapidly: water eddies, then smoke eddies, leaves eddy, crowds eddy, thoughts eddy, debates eddy. Fluid dynamics later formalises eddy as closed recirculation behind obstacle, distinct from vortex (Latin vortex / vertex, whirling) and turbulence, but everyday and Ruskinian use keeps the visible picture: leaves trapped circling while main current passes. Pronunciation /ˈɛdi/ with doubled d and -ying participle is regular; hyphenated swift-eddying /ˌswɪftˈɛdiɪŋ/ marks joint attributive use before or after noun.

  • From Scots pool to world physics: fishermen's ydy for a Tay or Tweed backwater becomes 19th-century novelists' eddying for London crowds (Dickens) and 20th-century engineers' eddy viscosity, eddy currents for electromagnetism and air. The prefix ed- (again) survives only here and in fossils; speakers no longer parse it, but water still turns back.
  • Ruskin's compounding: Victorian prose compounds participles freely — clear-brown, swift-eddying, far-seen — to compress observation. Swift-eddying with comma-dash isolation mimics a child's pointing: first colour and bed (clear-brown over pebbles), then motion-shape (swift-eddying), then judgment (infinite). The hyphen holds speed and circle in one glance, exactly as the Tay holds them in three feet of water.

Jointly the compound shows two Germanic motion-roots converging: *swip- sweep-turn-fast and *ith-/ed- back-again, one becoming haste, the other return, reunited to name water that hastens by returning. Etymology thus glosses hydrology.

  • Compounding mechanics and punctuation: hyphen binds swift (manner) to eddying (action) into a single attributive modifier, preventing misreading as swift water plus separate eddies; semicolon and em-dash in Ruskin (deep; swift-eddying, — an infinite thing) stage the perception in three beats — bed, motion, wonder. Modern editors keep the hyphen before nouns (swift-eddying current) and often open it predicatively (the current was swift eddying), but Ruskin hyphens throughout for density.
  • Child vision and infinite pattern: three or four feet of clear-brown water over pebbles gives a child both bottom and movement: stones fixed, water never fixed, brown peat tint yet transparent, fast downstream yet locally returning. Psychologists call this joint attention to invariant and variant; Ruskin calls it infinite. The compound therefore names a developmental moment — first physics lesson as aesthetic awe — not just hydrology.

Speed plus return is thus both etymology and autobiography: Germanic roots for sweep and back-again meet in a Perth garden, and a four-year-old looks down into time.
