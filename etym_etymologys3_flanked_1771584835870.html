The history of flanked begins in a bend. The Proto-Indo-European root *kleng- meant "to bend, to turn, to be flexible," and it survives securely only in the Germanic branch: the reconstructed Germanic word *hlanka- named "the side, the flank, the loin" — that part of the body which bends — and the flexible, bendable notion radiates through the whole family. Old English hlanc meant "loose and empty, slender, lank, flaccid" and gives English lank ("slender, thin"), the body sense of the flexible side. Old Norse hlykkr means "a bend, loop, noose." Old High German (h)lanca and Middle High German lanke name the "hip, loin, flank," and the German verb lenken preserves the root's pure verbal sense, "to bend, to turn aside, to steer." A variant form, *hlenka-, produced Old Norse hlekkr / hlenkr, "a link of a chain, a loop" — the bending ring — which gives English link, and a verbal offshoot meaning "to bend, draw back" gives English flinch.

Three English words — lank, link, and flank — all glow with the memory of a bend. The word that becomes flank travels through the Franks. The Frankish Germanic form *hlanka — "side, flank, hip" — was borrowed into Old French as flanc, with the regular conversion of Germanic hl- to Romance fl- (the same shift that turns the Germanic "lance" family into French flèche "arrow"). Modern French keeps flanc "side, flank," builds the verb flanquer "to post on the side, to flank," and gives Italian fianco "side, flank." English borrowed the French noun directly, and flanc appears in late Old English and Middle English with its anatomical sense, "the fleshy part of the side." For centuries it remained a body word and a butcher's word.

The sixteenth century transformed it. With the new standing armies and the professional art of war, the military sense emerged: the flank of an army is its side, the most exposed quarter, and to flank is to guard that side or to turn it in attack. The verb and its participle entered English tactical vocabulary — a position "flanked" is one whose sides are covered, or one that has been turned and taken in the side. From the battlefield the word spread in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to the body and to architecture: the fleshy side between ribs and hip (hence flank steak), the side of a building, the projecting wings or towers that stand at the sides of a facade.

  • Sound shifts: PIE *kl- → Germanic *hl- (Grimm's law); Frankish *hlanka → Old French flanc (Germanic hl- → Romance fl-); Old French flanc → English flank (adoption of the fl- cluster intact).
  • Semantic drift: "the bending part of the body" → "the side" → "the side of an army" → (verb) "to guard or stand at the side" → (passive) "situated on each side of," now a neutral descriptive verb of symmetrical placement.
  • Socio-cultural context: the military revolutions of early modern Europe made the "flank" a strategic concept, and the participle carried martial gravity into polite descriptive prose; today the guarding ghost persists only when the flanked object is ceremonially central — a doorway flanked by lions, a throne flanked by guards.

In modern usage flanked has broadened into a neutral descriptive verb: a table flanked by chairs, a street flanked by shops, a president flanked by advisors. The connotation has shifted from guarding to mere symmetrical placement, yet the old temperature remains — the word still organizes its scene into centre and sides, commander and posted guard, and it is exactly this ordered, deliberate balance that the novel exploits when it places the ritual ding between its two attendants.
