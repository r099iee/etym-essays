Chain for the single root word LINEATION (one continuous chain from flax to the modern word; the deepest PIE stage is debated and is stated explicitly as uncertain): - Possibly PIE *līno- "flax, linen": an uncertain reconstruction. Greek linon "flax, thread" may itself be a Mediterranean (possibly non-Indo-European) loanword, and modern handbooks do not treat the PIE status as secure. State: the PIE stage is uncertain and may not be Indo-European at all. - Greek linon "flax, thread, cord, linen", attested from Homer (8th century BCE) and common thereafter (linon as the thread of the loom, the flax plant, and the linen cloth). - Latin līnum "flax, linen, thread", a loan from Greek linon, attested from Plautus (early 2nd century BCE) and standard in classical Latin (līnum "flax", "linen"; also "a thread"). - Latin līnea (feminine of līneus, "made of flax") "flaxen thread, string, cord; a line" — originally the builder's or surveyor's flaxen string, used to mark a straight line; attested 1st century BCE (Varro). The transferred sense "a line" (drawn mark, row, verse) develops directly from "the thread used for drawing straight lines". - Latin līneāre "to draw lines; to rule; to draw in a straight line", a denominative verb from līnea; attested in late Latin. - Latin līneātiō "a drawing of lines, a line-drawing, a marking with lines", formed from the past-participle stem līneāt- plus the abstract suffix -iō; attested in Vitruvius (De architectura, c. 30–15 BCE), where it denotes the ground-plan or outline of a building. - English lineation "the act of drawing lines; the arrangement or marking of lines", a learned borrowing from Latin līneātiō, recorded from the 17th century (1640s); the specialised modern senses (poetic line-breaks, geological fabrics) date from the 19th–20th centuries. - LINEATION
