• Germany: Similar in denoting the broader national and cultural entity within Central Europe; differs because Prussia was a specific historical kingdom and constituent state (officially abolished after WWII in 1947) that spearheaded the nineteenth-century unification of modern Germany, which also encompasses Bavaria, Saxony, the Rhineland, and Swabia.
  • Austria: Similar in being a preeminent German-speaking Great Power and historic rival for Central European hegemony within the Holy Roman Empire and German Confederation; differs because Austria was ruled by the Roman Catholic Habsburg dynasty and governed a vast multi-ethnic Danubian empire, whereas Prussia was predominantly Protestant, Hohenzollern-led, and centered on the North European Plain.
  • Teutonic Order: Similar in establishing the initial Germanic military state, castles, and administration across the Baltic Prussian lands in the thirteenth century; differs because the Teutonic Order was a Roman Catholic monastic military order of Crusader knights, whereas Prussia subsequently secularized in 1525 into a Protestant duchy and sovereign kingdom.
  • Bavaria: Similar in being a historic German kingdom with a proud independent state identity; differs because Bavaria is situated in southern Germany, is historically Catholic, and possessed a distinct cultural and political tradition that frequently resisted Prussian centralism and militarism.
  • Sparta: Similar as an evocative historical archetype of hyper-disciplined militarism, civic obedience, and martial excellence; differs because Sparta was an Ancient Greek city-state in the Peloponnese, whereas Prussia was an early modern European powerhouse celebrated equally for administrative science, universal schooling, and philosophical Enlightenment (Immanuel Kant).
  • Hegemony: Similar to the political and military dominance Prussia exerted over the North German Confederation and the unified German Empire; differs because hegemony is an abstract political science concept describing regional supremacy rather than a specific sovereign state.
  • Hohenzollern: Similar in historical synonymous usage during royal diplomacy; differs because Hohenzollern refers specifically to the sovereign dynastic family that ruled Brandenburg and Prussia, rather than the territorial state itself.
  • Brandenburg: Similar in forming the core electoral territory and capital (Berlin) of the Prussian monarchy; differs because Brandenburg was an ancient Holy Roman Empire margraviate and electorate, which merged dynastically with the Baltic Duchy of Prussia in 1618.
