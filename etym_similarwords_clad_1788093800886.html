• Clothed: Similar because both are past forms of the same verb clothe, meaning dressed; differs because clothed is the neutral everyday form, while clad is literary, slightly archaic, and often suggests full coverage or a specified material.
  • Dressed: Similar because both mean wearing garments; differs because dressed is the ordinary broad word (dressed in a suit) and can imply care or occasion, while clad is more objective and pictorial about the covering itself.
  • Attired: Similar because both mean clothed, often formally; differs because attired is elegant and ceremonial (state attire), while clad can apply equally to armour, snow, ivy and building facades.
  • Garbed: Similar because both mean dressed in distinctive clothing; differs because garbed suggests a particular costume or uniform (ecclesiastical, occupational), while clad has no such restriction.
  • Robed: Similar because both mean covered in a garment; differs because robed is specific to the loose outer robe (judges, monks, monarchs), while clad covers any kind of covering.
  • Arrayed: Similar because both can mean dressed; differs because arrayed implies order, splendour and display (troops drawn up, a lady in her jewels), whereas clad is plain descriptive coverage.
  • Draped: Similar because both mean covered by fabric; differs because draped stresses loose, hanging cloth and arrangement, while clad stresses the fact of wearing or sheathing.
  • Wrapped: Similar because both mean enclosed in a covering; differs because wrapped is general (parcels, blankets, scarves) and is not specifically about garments, while clad usually implies clothing or a fitted covering.
  • Covered: Similar because both mean having an outer layer; differs because covered is entirely general (a lid on a pot), while clad specifies a manufactured or material sheathing — armour, panels, fabric.
  • Sheathed: Similar because both mean enclosed in a protective outer layer; differs because sheathed is mechanical and tactical (a sword in its scabbard, cables in conduit) and implies tight enclosure, while clad may be loose or flowing.
  • Armoured / mail-clad: Similar because both denote military covering; differs because armoured is the modern generic term for any protective plating, while clad (or -clad) is the poetic partner used in compounds like "ironclad", "steel-clad", "mail-clad".
