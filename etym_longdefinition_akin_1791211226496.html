Akin is a predicative adjective meaning related by blood or, far more often today, similar in nature, character, or origin. To say X is akin to Y is to claim family resemblance without identity: shared parentage, shared structure, or shared spirit.

In the Durant sentence, Akin to basketry, perhaps born of it, was the art of pottery, akin means closely related in origin and technique. Pottery is presented as the child or sibling of basketry, sharing the vessel-making function and possibly deriving historically from clay-lined baskets. The capital A reflects sentence-initial position, not a proper name.

Core senses in contemporary use:

  • Related by blood or marriage: they are akin through their mothers, akin by marriage. This literal genealogical sense is now rare and formal, largely replaced by related or kin to, but survives in legal and anthropological prose.
  • Similar in nature or character: the dominant sense: joy akin to ecstasy, a mood akin to despair, languages akin to German. Implies substantive resemblance in essential qualities, not superficial likeness. Akin pity and love share emotional structure.
  • Of common origin or development: pottery akin to basketry, birds akin to dinosaurs meaning evolutionarily or technologically derived from a common source. Used in history, biology, linguistics for phylogenetic or cultural filiation.
  • Sympathetic or congenial: occasionally akin spirits, akin minds meaning kindred, like-minded, on the same wavelength. More often expressed as kindred spirits, but akin conveys the same warmth.

Syntax and collocation are distinctive. Akin is almost always predicative with to: akin to, akin in spirit, closely / distantly / remotely akin. It rarely precedes a noun directly. One does not say an akin idea but an idea akin to mine. It pairs with abstract nouns: feeling, mood, idea, doctrine, language, species, craft. Degree adverbs mark closeness: closely akin, strikingly akin, vaguely akin.

Semantic bridges and nuances:

  • From blood to likeness: the bridge is ancient: Old English cynn meant both family and kind. Members of one stock share traits, so kinship entails similarity. Akin extends this logic to non-blood resemblance treated as if familial.
  • Strength of claim: akin is stronger than like or similar to because it hints at causal or structural kinship, not accidental resemblance. Saying nationalism is akin to religion claims deep functional likeness, not mere comparison.
  • Register: formal-literary and academic, common in history, criticism, science writing. Everyday speech prefers like, similar to, related to. Durant's use is typical elevated exposition.
  • Capitalization: Akin with capital A is only positional. The lemma is lowercase akin except at sentence start.

Understanding akin thus unlocks a whole Germanic family of kin, kind, kindly, kindred, and its Latinate cousins cognate and genus, all rooted in birth.
