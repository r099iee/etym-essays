SOLICITING descends from one of the most interesting little compounds in the Latin lexicon, sollicitus, and tracing its evolution means following a word that travelled from "thoroughly stirred or agitated" to "anxious," then to "stirred into action," and finally to the very modern act of "asking, requesting, or seeking to obtain" — including the street-level sense of a prostitute's public offer of services. The journey passes through two distinct PIE roots, centuries of French mediation, and a remarkable narrowing in which the legal, commercial, and sexual senses all grew out of a single core image of agitation.

The two PIE roots and the Latin compound. The first component goes back to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "whole, sound, healthy," reconstructed as *solh2- (or *solh2-wo-). Its reflexes include Greek holos "whole" (source of English "holo-" and "catholic") and Sanskrit sarva- "all, whole." The exact shape of the reconstruction is debated — some scholars reconstruct *solh2-wo-s, others posit an o-grade *sol-wo-s — and this uncertainty must be acknowledged rather than hidden. Latin preserves the base as sollus, attested chiefly in archaic compounds: solliferreus "all of iron," and the solemn adjective sollistimus "most auspicious," used by the augurs. The second component comes from PIE *kei- / *k(e)ih1- "to set in motion," which produced Latin ciere "to move, rouse, summon," and its participle citus "moved, roused, quick." The same root gave English "cite" and "excite," both of which still carry the "rouse into motion" idea.

When sollus and citus were welded together as sollicitus, the meaning was "stirred in one's whole being" — that is, "thoroughly agitated, disturbed, anxious." Plautus and Cicero use it freely of a worried mind. From this adjective the Romans built the verb sollicitare, "to disturb, agitate, vex, stir up," and by the classical period it already had the transferred sense "to excite someone to action, to tempt someone away from loyalty." One famous political usage is sollicitare militum animos, "to stir up the minds of the soldiers" — that is, to subvert them, to tempt them to desert. The "stirring" sense is thus the root of everything that follows.

French mediation and the shift to "requesting." The verb passed into Old French as solliciter, where it meant "to stir, disturb, to care for, to urge," and it entered Middle English in the 14th century. In medieval English, soliciten could still mean "to disturb or trouble," but it increasingly took on the senses "to press a matter earnestly upon someone," "to petition," and "to entreat." By the 16th century English solicit meant primarily "to petition or entreat for something," and the older "disturb" sense faded, surviving only in poetic and archaizing usage.

The modern expansion: business, politics, law, and sex. From "to entreat" grew a cluster of specialised uses. In commerce, one solicits business, orders, or customers — the salesman's solicit is a direct approach with an offer. In politics, one solicits votes, support, or patronage, and "solicitation" became the standard term for the active asking for money or influence. In law the word took on two lives: the noun "solicitor" came to denote a member of the junior branch of the English legal profession — a lawyer who "solicits" or urges causes on behalf of clients — while "solicitation" became the technical name for the crime of inciting, encouraging, or requesting another person to commit an offence, so that one may be convicted of "solicitation to murder" even when the crime never takes place.

Most striking is the sexual sense. From the 18th century onward, to solicit was applied to prostitutes who accost men in the street for the purpose of offering sexual services, and "soliciting" remains a criminal charge in many jurisdictions. This sense has so dominated the public ear that "soliciting" now often means sexual solicitation first and everything else second, and it has lent the whole word family a faintly disreputable edge — an irony, given that the same root underlies the respectable title "solicitor."

Derivatives and sound change. English also preserves the adjective solicitous "anxious, concerned, eager" and the noun solicitude "care, concern" — direct continuations of the Latin "agitated/anxious" meaning, showing that the older psychological sense never fully died. Note too that English stress shifted in the verb: Latin sollicitare is accented on -ci- (sol-li-CI-ta-re), and English solicit preserves a similar pattern (so-LI-cit). The spelling lost one l in Middle French and early Modern English, so that classical Latin sollicit- appears in English as solicit-.

Socio-cultural conclusion. The life of this word is a miniature history of persuasion. Its earliest layer is bodily agitation — stirring a person up; its middle layer is formal petitioning in court, church, and chancery; its modern layer is the whole apparatus of fundraising, marketing, political canvassing, legal incitement, and street prostitution. In every layer the deep structure is the same: one person intentionally moves another out of passivity and into a response. The reading context — a man "dashing about, pulling strings and soliciting help from every potential backer he could find" — shows the oldest meaning still pulsing under the newest one.
