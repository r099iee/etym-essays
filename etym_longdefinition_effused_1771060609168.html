EFFUSED — "poured out or spread freely; disseminated, shed abroad; diffused over a surface or through a space." In the supplied literary context, sweet dews and balmy winds are "asperged and effused for the enrichment and refreshment of all terrestrial life": the word describes a generous, uncontainable outpouring of benevolent natural forces, a releasing of something precious from a hidden source so that it saturates and enriches the whole landscape. The emphasis is on free, abundant, life-giving dispersal rather than on any mechanical or accidental spilling.
  • Sense 1 — literal outpouring of a liquid: "effused blood," "effused tears" — a fluid poured out, especially over a surface; the core physical sense descended directly from Latin "effundere" (pour out).
  • Sense 2 — dissemination of subtle or gaseous matter: light, scent, heat, vapour, dew "effused" through the air — the sense demanded by the literary context, where the substance is as much atmospheric as liquid.
  • Sense 3 — figurative / emotional outpouring: "he effused confidence," "she effused warmth" — a person freely radiating a quality, emotion, or charm; the metaphorical bridge is the transfer of physical pouring to emotional or personal generosity.
  • Sense 4 — technical (physics / chemistry, chiefly of gases): to effuse is to pass through a small aperture or porous membrane from a region of higher pressure to lower pressure, per Graham's law of effusion; a specialised scientific sense entirely distinct from ordinary pouring.
  • Sense 5 — medical / physiological: "effused" matter is fluid or blood that has escaped from its vessels into surrounding tissue — as in an "effusion" into a joint or the pericardium; the bridge is again the literal idea of pouring out of a containing vessel.
  • Sense 6 — geological / mineralogical: effused (or effusive) rock is igneous rock that has poured out onto the surface as lava; a precise technical register of the same root.
Polysemy and register. Effused is the past tense and past participle of the verb "effuse," and it sits at the meeting point of several registers. In ordinary and literary English it is a graceful, slightly ceremonious synonym for "poured out, shed, spread abroad," preferred when the outpouring is generous, graceful, or life-giving rather than merely wasteful — hence its natural affinity with poetry, sermons, and Romantic nature-writing. In science it has been given rigorous, narrow meanings (gaseous effusion, effused lava, effused blood), all of which preserve the original Latin geometry of a substance moving outward from a container. The noun "effusion" is far more common in both literary ("an effusion of the heart" — an emotional outpouring) and medical registers ("pleural effusion"); the verb "effuse" and its participle are rarer and more literary. Related forms: "effusive" (overflowing, gushing — of praise or greeting), "effusiveness," and the obsolete "effusionist." Historical bridge. The whole family descends from Latin "ex-" (out of, from) + "fundere" (to pour), giving "effundere," "to pour out." The past participle "effusus" — "poured out, spread abroad" — passed through scholarly and literary Latin into English in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, initially in theological writing about the pouring out of grace, light, and the Spirit, a usage that shaped its dignified, beneficent flavour ever after. Modern scientific senses are a later, analytical re-focusing of the same image onto measurable physical processes. Register is formal-to-literary; connotation is generous, gracious, and often beneficent, though in physics the word is emotionally neutral. The spelling keeps the double -f- of the Latin prefix "ef-" (assimilated "ex-" before f). The mnemonic "EF = out, FUSED = poured" is exactly right.
