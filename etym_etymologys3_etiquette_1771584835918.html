The biography of etiquette begins deep in the Indo-European past, with the Proto-Indo-European root *steig-, "to prick, to stick, to be pointed." This was a root of sharp contact, and its two faces — the point that pierces and the point that adheres — shaped every word that descends from it. In the Indo-Iranian branch it became the Sanskrit root tij-, "to be sharp": tejate, "is sharp," and téjas, "sharpness, brilliance, fire," which survives in Hindi as tej, "sharpness, brilliance," and as tīkhā, "pungent, spicy," from the ancient adjective tiktá, "bitter, sharp-tasting." In Greek the root gave stízein, "to prick, tattoo, brand," and its noun stígma, "a mark made by pricking" — the origin of English stigma, the "pointed mark" that society brands upon the outcast. In Latin it produced instīgāre, "to goad, incite" (English instigate), and distinguere, "to mark off, separate" (English distinguish, distinct). Everywhere the root kept its image of a sharp point leaving its mark.

The line that matters for English runs through the Germanic branch, where the root became Proto-Germanic *stikkaną, "to stab, stick, prick." This yielded English stick and stitch, German stechen ("to sting, pierce"), and Dutch steken ("to stab, thrust"). When the Franks, a Germanic people, settled in Gaul after the collapse of Roman rule, their word *stikkan was borrowed into the speech of the Gallo-Romans as Old French estiquer / estichier, "to stick, to fasten, to affix." From this verb French formed a postverbal noun, estiquet, "a label, a small card" — literally "the thing stuck on" — and this word had two children. One was borrowed directly into English in the 1520s as ticket, originally a small card or note stuck up for public information. The other, via the verb estiqueter, "to label," gave the noun étiquette, "a label, a placard."

The crucial semantic shift happened at the French court. In the France of Louis XIV, the étiquette was the card on which the rules of precedence were written — the ticket that told each nobleman, in writing, where he might stand and when he might approach the king. The word then slid from the physical label to the system it encoded: étiquette came to mean "the prescribed order of ceremony," and from that, by about the mid-eighteenth century, "the code of polite behaviour." English borrowed the word in this ceremonial sense around 1740, and modern French still keeps both meanings — an étiquette is both a label on a jar and the rules of manners. The root's ancient double image is thus preserved in the modern word: etiquette is a code that "sticks" to its practitioners and, like a goad, "pricks" them into compliance.

The socio-cultural history of the word is a history of courtly absolutism. The card-and-precedence system it names belongs to Versailles, where rank was written, posted, and enforced, and where the very unknowability of the rules was part of their power: the outsider could never be sure of the etiquette and so could never be sure of her place. In the eighteenth century the word migrated from the court to society at large, carried by conduct books and the new magazine press; by the nineteenth century "etiquette" had become the great Victorian machinery of class — the table, the visiting card, the dinner invitation — prescribing who might sit, who must wait, who speaks first. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries it has been endlessly renewed: "medical etiquette," "legal etiquette," "diplomatic etiquette," "email etiquette," and the coinage "netiquette" all extend the old idea of an unwritten code that certifies membership in a group. In the Jia mansion, where Dai-yu "asked permission to sit, as etiquette prescribed," the word carries the full weight of that history: a rule written down and "stuck up" for all to read, prescribing exactly how far rank and deference may go, and marking — as surely as a stigma — anyone who fails to obey. Etiquette, the thing stuck on, still sticks to everyone it governs, pricking the unpractised into their first, anxious bow.
