Earliest stage (uncertain). The ultimate Indo-European ancestry of the word for "boy" is debated. Most handbooks derive the Germanic word from a base *knēb(h)- / *knap- with the sense "boy, young man, offspring", possibly connected to PIE *ǵenh₁- "to beget, give birth" (the root of Latin genus, Greek génos, English kin, kind) via a theory that the boy is "the begotten one, the offspring". This connection is plausible but not proven; other authorities treat the Germanic form as of obscure or expressive origin. The reconstruction *ǵenh₁- → *knab- is marked as debated.

Derivation chain:

  • Proto-Indo-European *ǵenh₁- "to beget, give birth, produce" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) — proposed, debated source of the Germanic "boy" word; also the root of genus, genesis, kin. Marked uncertain.
  • Proto-Germanic *knab- / *knap- "boy, youth, young man" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) — attested indirectly by Old English cnafa, Old Saxon knabo, Old High German knabo, Old Norse knapi.
  • Old English cnafa, "boy, youth, male child; boy-servant, menial" (attested from c. 9th century CE, in Beowulf-period glosses and later texts).
  • Middle English knave, "boy, lad, serving-boy, menial" (attested from c. 12th–13th centuries), then "base, low-born fellow" and "rogue, rascal" (from the 14th–15th centuries).
  • English knave, "dishonest or base man; scoundrel" (fully established by the 16th century; frequent in Shakespeare), and "jack in a pack of cards" (from the 16th century).
  • KNAVE (present-day English).

Debate note: The two uncertain elements are (1) the pre-Germanic connection to PIE *ǵenh₁- "to beget", which is plausible semantically but phonologically debated, and (2) the exact relationship among the Germanic cognates (some show *knab-, others *knap-); the English form cnafa points to *knab-. From Old English onward the record is clear and unbroken.
