The biography of contest is the story of two ancient roots fused into one legal word. The traditional and still standard etymology, accepted by the OED and by Etymonline, derives Latin testis 'witness' from a Proto-Indo-European compound *tri-stā-, literally 'one who stands as third' — the bystander, the disinterested third person standing by who can testify to what he has seen. The first element is *trei- 'three' (English three, Greek treîs, Sanskrit tráyas, Hindi tīn); the second is *steh₂- 'to stand' (English stand, Sanskrit sthā-, Greek hístēmi, Latin stāre), the same root that produced obstinacy and state. The compound is first attested in the Old Latin form terstis, which preserves the *tris- grade, and although a few modern philologists treat the derivation as probable rather than certain, it has never been displaced. Two cautions are worth registering: the celebrated joke that testis means the testicle because it 'witnesses' virility is ancient folk-etymology (the diminutive testiculus did borrow the word, but the semantic path is playful, not historical), and English test comes from a different source — Old French test, an earthen vessel used to assay metal. Sound shifts. The compound *tri-stā- first became Old Latin terstis, with the *tris- grade preserved, then Classical Latin testis, the cluster simplifying and the vowels settling. The suffixed verb testārī 'to bear witness' formed the base for a whole legal and theological family: testāmentum 'a solemn declaration; a will', testimōnium 'evidence, proof', testificārī 'to bear witness', and the compounds protestārī 'to declare publicly' (whence protest and Protestant), attestārī 'to bear witness to' (whence attest), and dētestārī 'to curse, calling heaven to witness; to loathe' (whence detest). The derived verb contestārī combined con- 'together' with testārī: literally 'to call witnesses together'. The legal ceremony. In Roman law contestārī lītem — 'to join issue in a suit' — named the formal act by which both parties to a litigation solemnly summoned their witnesses and thereby entered into the dispute. It was this procedural act that gave the Latin verb its transferred meaning: from 'to call witnesses together' to 'to enter into a dispute, to struggle'. The noun contestātiō followed, and in Late Latin and the vernacular the word spread through the law-courts of medieval Europe. Old French contester (thirteenth century) meant 'to dispute, to litigate, to oppose', and from it English borrowed the verb contest around 1604 — a purely legal word meaning to challenge a claim in court, to call into question. The same stem travelled into the modern Romance languages: French contester, Italian contestare, Spanish contestar, which in Spanish came to mean 'to answer, reply' as well as 'to dispute'. From the courtroom to the field. The noun arrived late — English contest as 'a struggle, rivalry, dispute' is attested from the 1670s — and quickly outgrew the law. The 18th century made it a dignified word of constitutional prose: the contest between crown and parliament, between Whig and Tory, between nations and causes. Macaulay's usage in the History of England (1848) is squarely in this tradition: 'the contest which the administration of King James the Second brought to a decisive crisis' — a lawful struggle for sovereignty carried on within the shared framework of English institutions, whose turnings and chances ('vicissitudes') he undertakes to narrate. In the 19th century the word moved into sport: a contest became any competitive match or game, from a prize-fight to a regatta, losing much of its legal gravity. The 20th century completed the democratization: beauty contests, song contests, photo contests, and the rhetorical 'no contest' (with its legal twin, the plea of nolo contendere). Yet the older legal flavour survives intact in the verb — to contest a will, to contest an election, a contested divorce — and in the adjective contestable, where the meaning is still precisely 'capable of being called into question'. Today contest straddles the sports page and the law report: an ancient word of witnesses and lawsuits that ended as the modern word of championships, its 'third-stander' origin still legible to anyone who knows that a witness is the bystander who saw the thing.
