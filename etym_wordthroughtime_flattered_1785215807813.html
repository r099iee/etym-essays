- 14th century stroking - enters English from French meaning to stroke, smooth, or caress; the verb is physical first, and its earliest figurative life is soothing a person with pleasing words, like smoothing a ruffled surface.
- 14th-16th century gratifying deception - to flatter is to praise so as to please or beguile, and "to flatter oneself" means to deceive oneself with pleasing hopes; the word already carries suspicion.
- 17th-19th century vanity and insinuation - flattering names the self-interested stroking of another's vanity to win favour; in the Mill biography it appears as the suspected tactic by which a wife might bend her husband to her views.
- Contemporary use - flexible and common: mildly negative when applied to the flatterer's motives, but positive in the receiver's response ("I'm flattered"), and neutral in "flattering dress," which enhances appearance.
