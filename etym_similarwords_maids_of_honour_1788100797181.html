• ladies-in-waiting: Similar because both are female attendants of a queen or princess in the royal household; differs because ladies-in-waiting are adult, often married women of higher rank and seniority, whereas maids of honour are younger, unmarried, and junior.
  • bridesmaids: Similar because both attend a central woman in a ceremonial, court-like setting; differs because bridesmaids are purely wedding attendants with no court or household office, whereas maids of honour historically held a salaried court post.
  • maid of honor (American spelling): Similar because it is the same office; differs only in orthography (no u) and in the wedding-centred sense that dominates US usage.
  • matron of honour: Similar because it is the married counterpart of the principal bridesmaid; differs because a matron is by definition married, whereas the maid is unmarried.
  • attendants: Similar because both words denote persons serving a principal; differs because attendant is generic and gender-neutral, with none of the aristocratic or bridal connotations.
  • chambermaids / serving-maids: Similar because both are female household servants; differs because they are paid domestic workers of low rank, not honourable young gentlewomen of a court.
  • maiden: Similar because both mean an unmarried young woman; differs because maiden is an archaic or poetic noun in its own right, not an office title, and carries a pure/virginal connotation that the court office only partially shares.
  • companions / gentlewomen of the bedchamber: Similar because these are also court offices attached to a queen; differs because such appointments are adult, married or widowed, of greater seniority and domestic intimacy than maids of honour.
