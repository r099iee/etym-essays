- Late 14th c. (c. 1380–1400) — Leather Craft & Heraldic Pigment: Entered Middle English from Anglo-French *tauné* (Old French *tané*, "tanned"), referring directly to the warm brownish-yellow color of leather cured with crushed oak bark (*tannum*), and adopted into European heraldry as the tincture *tenné*.
- Early Modern & Elizabethan Era (c. 1550–1650) — Poetic Pelts & Human Complexion: Expanded dramatically in Renaissance literature, notably in Shakespeare and Spenser, to describe lion coats, autumnal leaves, and the sun-bronzed skin of exotic rulers (as in Antony and Cleopatra: "tawny front").
- 18th & 19th c. — Taxonomic Classification in Natural History: Formalized as an indispensable descriptor in zoology and ornithology, anchoring common names for species exhibiting yellowish-brown or mottling camouflage (such as the *tawny owl*, *tawny eagle*, and *tawny pipit*).
- Late 19th & 20th c. — Oenological Specialization (Tawny Port): Acquired prestigious commercial and culinary status in winemaking to define barrel-aged port wine that oxidizes over decades, losing ruby fruitiness to achieve an amber-brown hue and nutty profile.
- Contemporary Era (20th–21st c.) — Classical Literature & Sanskrit Translation: Widely utilized in classical studies and comparative literature, notably serving as the standard English rendering for Sanskrit *hari* (denoting the golden-brown or yellowish coats of Indian monkeys and lions).
