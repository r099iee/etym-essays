1. The name's composition. The Greek name Περικλῆς (Periklēs, Ionic-Attic) is a personal compound of two inherited elements, literally "far-famed" or "surrounded by glory": περί (peri), "around, about, beyond, exceedingly", and κλέος (kléos), "fame, glory, renown". Both elements descend from secure Indo-European reconstructions. The first, *per(i), "through, across, beyond", is one of the best-attested roots in the family, appearing in Greek περί, Latin per, Sanskrit pári, and English far (via Germanic). The second, *ḱlewos, "fame", is the celebrated root of glory-words across the ancient world: Greek κλέος (the glory of hero and poet in Homer), Latin inclutusī and clueo "to be famous", Sanskrit śrávas, Old Irish clú, and the Germanic family behind Old English hlūd "loud" and the name-element in Ealhhild etc. (Modern English loud).

2. The name in the Greek onomasticon. Names in -κλέης/-κλῆς are among the oldest aristocratic patterns of Greek naming, at home in the world of Homeric epic, where κλέος is the fame the hero wins and the poet preserves. Periklēs is a perfectly transparent instance, and Greeks at all periods heard it as a proud claim. The historical bearer — the son of Xanthippus and Agariste — received a name fit for the part he played: no Greek of the fifth century could hear "Periclēs" without hearing "glory all around".

3. The man (c. 495–429 BCE). Pericles came of the great Alcmaeonid clan through his mother; his father Xanthippus commanded the Athenian fleet at Mycale (479 BCE). Entering politics after the Persian Wars, Pericles rose with the democratic party, and from the death of his rival Cimon (c. 450) until his own death he was, in effect, first citizen of Athens for a generation — Thucydides famously says that in name it was democracy, in fact rule by the first man. His statesmanship: the transformation of the Delian League into the Athenian empire (the treasury moved to Athens c. 454); the public works that turned the wartime tribute into the glory of the Acropolis (the Parthenon begun 447, the Propylaea, the Odeon); the radicalisation of democracy (payment for jurors, the citizenship law of 451); and the policy that led to the Peloponnesian War (431–404) and to the plague of 430–429 that killed him. His power rested, later writers all agree, on eloquence: he was surnamed Olympios for the thunder of his speeches, and his funeral oration over the war dead (in Thucydides' recreation) remains the supreme ancient statement of democratic patriotism.

4. The ancient afterlife. No sooner dead than the name became a symbol to be claimed or scratched. The comic poets (Cratinus, Eupolis, Aristophanes) mocked the "head-onion" Olympian and his "precious" war policy; the historians took him seriously — Thucydides with admiration, Xenophon and Isocrates in part critically; the philosophers of the fourth century (Plato's Gorgias, Aristotle's Constitution of the Athenians) weighed his democracy's merits and defects. Plutarch's Life of Pericles (2nd c. CE), one of the richest of the Parallel Lives, fixed his double image forever: the wise and incorruptible statesman who adorned Athens with works of immortal beauty, alongside the sobering tale of a city led into catastrophic war. Via Plutarch, Pericles entered Renaissance political thought as a stock example of great leadership and of popular leadership's dangers.

5. The Renaissance and "the Age of Pericles". The rediscovery of Greek civilization in the fifteenth-sixteenth centuries made "Athens under Pericles" the standard of artistic and political excellence. In English literature the name appears in the sixteenth century both as history (North's translation of Plutarch's Lives, 1579, was Shakespeare's source) and on the stage: Pericles, Prince of Tyre (published 1609, joint authorship with George Wilkins) turns the name into a romance-hero of storm, loss, and recovery. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the phrase "the age of Pericles" (Germanic philhellenism, Victor Cousin's and Ernest Renan's France, Grote's History of Greece) became the label of the classical golden age — the era to which all others are compared. Matthew Arnold and Walter Pater, the great Victorian Hellenists, made "Periclean" the adjective for the full flower of Greek genius, and it is precisely this ninth-century-critical sense that Pater deploys in the sentence the student studies: the age of Lorenzo, he says, deserves what is said of "the age of Pericles" — an age productive in personalities, many-sided, centralised, complete.

6. The name in the modern world. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries kept the name alive: Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (1863) consciously reincarnated the Funeral Oration's ideal of government of the people; the Greek name Pericles is still given (and the Italian Pericle); scholars, statesmen, and writers from Pericles the Athenian archaeologist of the 20th century to the Spanish politician of the Civil War era have borne it. "Periclean democracy" is a term of political science; the "Periclean building programme" is a tool of cultural history. Few names of antiquity carry such a continuous charge: from Greek glory-claim, to Thucydides' eulogy, to Plutarch's biography, to the Victorian ideal of Greece, to the modern shorthand for democratic greatness — the word Pericles still means the bright height of civilisation.
