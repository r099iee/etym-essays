Arable is, in its core modern sense, an adjective describing land that is fit for growing crops by ploughing and tillage — soil that can be broken open by the share, sown with seed, and harvested in due season, as opposed to permanent pasture, meadow, woodland, down, or waste.

In the source sentence Cobbett marvels at the steepness of Surrey farmland: even the arable fields climb hillsides nearly as steep as the roof of a tiled house, while wooded ground climbs steeper still. The sense is exactly the husbandman's one: fields under the plough, cultivated year by year, defying slopes a modern agronomist would call marginal. Cobbett, the farmer-journalist riding through southern England in the 1820s, uses the word as every countryman did — arable as the worked ground that feeds the parish, set against woods and chalk downs that feed sheep and coppice instead.

Core meaning in detail: Arable land is land under temporary crops — wheat, barley, oats, beans, turnips, clover — moved through rotation with fallow and seed in the old three-field rhythm or the Norfolk four-course of wheat, turnips, barley, and clover. Agronomists define it by capability: deep enough to take the plough, kind enough to crumble into a seedbed, free-draining enough to work after rain, and fertile enough to repay manure and marl. Soil surveyors grade it by slope, stoniness, wetness, and droughtiness, and planners protect the best and most versatile grades from development. In farm accounts arable is the cropping acreage entered field by field in the field book, costed for seed, labour, and team, and balanced against grass, roots, and fallow.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Fit for the plough, under crops (primary, from c. 1400): arable fields, arable farms, arable districts. The sense Cobbett uses and the one every subsidy form still uses.
  • Arable land as a noun (short for arable land): so many hectares of arable — the cropped area of a farm, county, or country, reported in censuses and contrasted with permanent grass. The bridge is simple ellipsis: the adjective standing in for the whole phrase, as when officials speak of losing arable to roads and houses.
  • Capable of being cultivated even if not yet cropped (extended): newly reclaimed arable on drained marsh or cleared scrub. The bridge runs from fitness to potential: land judged ploughable by soil and slope joins the arable account before the first furrow is ever turned.
  • A farming system or way of life (metonymic): an arable parish, an arable county — Norfolk or the Lothians described by their dominant husbandry. The bridge carries the soil word onto the community that works it, so that arable names not just dirt but a calendar of sowing days, harvest homes, and corn markets.
  • Figurative seedbed — minds and talents described as arable (literary): an arable imagination ready to take ideas and bring them to harvest. The bridge is the ancient farming metaphor of thought as sowing: just as the plough opens soil to seed, teaching and reading open the mind, and the unploughed mind lies fallow beside it.

Register and connotation: arable is the plain professional word of farming, surveying, and policy — unlyrical, exact, and honourable. It carries the dignity of bread corn and honest labour, and in Cobbett it carries indignation too: good arable ground misused, taxed, or engrossed becomes a moral charge. Against pastoral, which dreams of shepherds, arable smells of turned earth, dung, and sweat. Statisticians pair it with permanent grass and rough grazing to divide the whole farmed kingdom, and every acre counted as arable is an acre pledged to the plough.
