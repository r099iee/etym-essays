PASSION-FLOWERS — in the source text the plural of passion-flower — are the showy, elaborately structured blossoms of the tropical and subtropical climbing vines of the genus Passiflora (family Passifloraceae). Around five hundred species are recognised, native mainly to the Americas, with a few in Asia and Australasia. The name is a botanical calque with a theological core: seventeenth-century European observers named the flower after the Passion of Christ, seeing in its parts the instruments of the Crucifixion. The word’s seeming romantic “passion” is thus, historically, the Passion — the suffering and death of Christ on the Cross — a fact that modern speakers routinely misread, and this very misreading is part of the word’s living history.

1. Botanical description. Passion-flowers are among the showiest of flowers: a ring of five sepals and five petals (often closely alike, giving the impression of ten petal-like parts), a prominent corona of radiating filaments in purple, blue, white or pink, five anthers, three stigmas, and beneath them the developing fruit — the edible “passion fruit” of P. edulis and its relatives. The vines climb by tendrils; species range from the hardy North American “maypop” (P. incarnata) to giant Amazonian lianas with flowers the size of a hand.

  • Structure: the corona (the filament ring unique to the genus) and the elevated column (androgynophore) bearing stamens and stigmas together make the flower read as a radiant wheel around a central hub — exactly the configuration that invited the seventeenth-century symbolism.
  • Symbolism (17th century): Italian friar-scholar Jacomo Bosio, in his treatise on the Cross (1610), codified the emblematic reading that Spanish missionaries had already found in Mexican species: the ten petals and sepals = the ten faithful apostles (Peter and Judas absent); the corona = the crown of thorns; the three stigmas = the three nails; the five anthers = the five wounds; the climbing tendrils = the whips of the scourging; the round fruit = the world redeemed by the Cross.
  • Generic name: Linnaeus formalised the genus as Passiflora in his Species Plantarum (1753), Latin for “passion-flower”; the English term is recorded from the 1640s (translating the Latin flos passionis).
  • Passion fruit: the edible fruit of P. edulis was long known in Spanish as granadilla (“little pomegranate”); “passion fruit” entered English in the later nineteenth century — named for the flower’s Passion symbolism, not for any gustatory or amorous ardour.

2. Secondary and modern senses. (a) Horticulture: passion-flowers are cultivated worldwide as striking conservatory and garden climbers; “maypop” is the folk name of the hardy American species, and countless hybrid cultivars exist. (b) Herbal medicine: extracts of Passiflora incarnata have been used since the late nineteenth century and popularised in the twentieth as mild sedatives and anxiolytics for anxiety and insomnia; “passionflower” is today the standard label on supplements (regulation has tightened since the early twenty-first century). (c) Popular re-reading: because “passion” today chiefly means intense love or enthusiasm, most speakers hear “passion-flower” as “flower of desire” — a folk re-etymology that the religious original quietly contradicts; the amorous reading is now the dominant public one.

3. Semantic bridge. The root “passion” entered English from Latin passio, “suffering”, itself from patior “to suffer, undergo”; in Christian Latin the word meant specifically the Passion of Christ, the crucifixion narrative. The flower was named for that suffering in the seventeenth century, when Catholic Europe was saturated in Passion devotion; only later, as “passion” widened to mean love and zeal (sixteenth century), did the compound acquire its misleadingly romantic ring. The plural form in the source — “embroidering passion-flowers on a satin gown” — records the Victorian fashion for embroidered botanical ornament, and carries a poignant irony: the poor seamstress stitches the emblem of divine suffering onto a court lady’s gown while her own child lies ill.

4. Register and spelling. “Passion-flower” (hyphenated) remains the standard British spelling; “passionflower” (one word) is common in American botanical and supplement usage. The plural “passion-flowers” is the ordinary count plural, and the word is always pronounced with the plant’s stress pattern, /ˈpæʃ.ən.flaʊ.əz/.
