The story of REMINISCENCES begins in deep Indo-European prehistory with the root *men-, whose core meaning was "to think, remember, or have in mind" — a root that already fused memory with thought. From this single core the separate branches of the Indo-European family produced distinct descendants. In the Indo-Iranian branch, Sanskrit preserved mánas- ("mind, spirit"), which survives in modern Hindi as man, and built the sacred mantrá-, literally "an instrument of thought," the source of English mantra. In Greek the root yielded mnēmē ("memory"), the ancestor of modern amnesia and amnesty, and mnéma ("memorial"), the origin of mnemonic. In the Germanic branch it gave Old English munan and, through it, the modern verb mind, together with Gothic muns and Old Norse muna. Every branch, in short, kept the equation of remembering with thinking, and of thinking with care for what is in mind.

It was in Latin that the immediate ancestor of reminiscences was forged. The stative verb meminī ("I remember," originally a reduplicated perfect meaning "I have in mind") coexisted with the deponent infinitive reminīscī, "to call back to mind," built from the prefix re- ("again") and the inchoative stem minisci. From reminīscī came the noun reminiscentia, "recollection." Latin reminiscentia passed into Late Latin and into the philosophical vocabulary of the medieval schools, where it acquired a precise technical sense: following Aristotle, medieval thinkers distinguished passive memoria, the storage of impressions, from active reminiscentia, the deliberate mental effort of reconstructing what has been stored — a distinction written into the very structure of the word, whose re- prefix marks the repeated, willed return to past experience.

English borrowed the noun in the seventeenth century: reminiscence is attested around 1658. During the eighteenth century the word acquired its everyday sense of "the act of recalling the past," often in a nostalgic key, and by the nineteenth century the plural had become a genre: "reminiscences" named a published book of retrospective portraits and anecdotes, the loose, episodic counterpart of the systematic autobiography.

The Victorian culture of biography and memoir gave the genre its golden age:

  • Carlyle's Reminiscences (1881) — the work behind this card — was assembled and published after the essayist's death, recording his late impressions of leading figures including Harriet Taylor Mill, whom he described with "ambiguous epithets"; the volume is the classic instance of the word's double weight, at once a private act of remembering and a documentary record intended for posterity.
  • The genre's register — elegiac, anecdotal, sometimes querulous — colours the plural noun, which in such titles always suggests portraits of other people and scenes rather than a continuous life-narrative.
  • The modern aftermath — the verb reminisce (a nineteenth-century back-formation) carries the everyday act of recalling, while the noun keeps its literary, published sense; in psychology, "reminiscence therapy" and the "reminiscence bump" formalize the same human habit the word has named for three centuries.

The full history is written into the word itself: the Indo-European root that fused memory with thought, the Roman perfect "I have in mind," the medieval distinction between storage and active recall, the seventeenth-century English borrowing, and the nineteenth-century literary genre — from the Sanskrit mantrá to Carlyle's ambiguous epithets.
