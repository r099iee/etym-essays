Chain for the tree-name pines: PIE *peyH- / *pey- “to be fat, be swollen; juice, sap, fat” (reconstructed root; c. 4500–3500 BCE; reflected in Latin pinguis “fat,” Greek pīya “rain, dew, rich pasture”; the exact form and membership are debated, see note below) → Latin pīnus “a pine tree, a fir; the resinous tree” (classical Latin, attested from the 2nd century BCE onward; the tree is named for its fat, resinous sap, the same sap-word family as pinguis “fat”) → Old English pīn “a pine tree” (borrowed from Latin in the Anglo-Saxon period; attested in the compound pīntrēow “pine-tree” and pīnbēam) → Middle English pine / pyne “a pine tree; pine-wood” (12th–15th centuries CE) → Modern English pine, plural PINES (this card) NOTE on the debate: the deeper derivation of Latin pīnus is usually given from a root meaning “fat, sap, juice” (the tree named for its resin), linking it with Latin pinguis “fat” and Greek pīya; but some specialists treat pīnus as Mediterranean or substrate in origin, and the precise Indo-European pedigree is not universally agreed. Crucially, the verb to pine — “to waste away with longing” — is NOT from this tree-name: it descends from Old English pīnian “to torment, afflict” (through the noun pīn “pain, torment”), ultimately from Latin poena “punishment, pain.” The tree and the verb are homonyms only.
