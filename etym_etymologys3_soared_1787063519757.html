From a Greek breeze to a Latin air. The ancestry of soar begins not in the air of flight but in a gentle Greek breeze. Ancient Greek αὔρα (aúra), attested from the classical period, meant "breeze, breath of fresh air" — a soft, cool wind. Latin borrowed it as aura, "air, breeze, breath", and in Latin the word acquired a subtle spiritual colour: the aura could be a breath of influence, a delicate emanation. It is from this gentle, airy word that the whole soaring family descends — a fitting root for a verb of effortless ascent.

Vulgar Latin and the compound of the air. From aura, spoken Latin formed a prefixed verb, reconstructed as *exaurāre — ex- "out, up" plus aura "air" — with the sense "to expose to the air, to raise into the air". This verb is not directly attested in surviving texts, so its form is inferred from its Romance descendants (the standard scholarly reconstruction), but its meaning is secure: to lift something up into the air, to let it meet the wind.

Old French: the falconer's word. From this Latin compound came Old French essorer (12th century), meaning "to fly up, to rise aloft", and its reflex essoarer became part of the rich vocabulary of hawking and falconry — the technical word for a hawk mounting on the air. When Middle English borrowed the verb in the 14th century as soren (spelt variously sore(n), soar(e)), it arrived carrying this falconry pedigree: to soar was to mount into the air as a hawk does, wheeling upward on a rising breeze without beating the wings. The word immediately took on poetic altitude in English, describing birds and (very quickly) mountains, towers, flames, and the mind.

The 16th–19th centuries: figurative elevation. Within two centuries of its borrowing, soar had split into literal and figurative streams. Literally it described birds, cliffs, spires, and flames rising to great height; figuratively it described ambition, thoughts, hopes, and spirits lifting to a noble or exalted state — "soaring thoughts", "soaring fancy", "soaring ambition". The double life gave the word its characteristic grandeur: soaring is never merely going up, but rising toward something high, noble, or awe-inspiring, and the image of the effortless hawk remained audible beneath every figurative use.

The 20th century: flight and finance. The age of powered flight adopted the word for aircraft climbing and cruising at altitude, reinforcing its literal strength. Then, in the 20th century, the world of money borrowed the hawk's ascent: prices, stocks, shares, and rates began to "soar" in the financial pages, and the metaphor stuck so firmly that soared is now a standard headline verb for steep quantitative increase — "inflation soared", "shares soared", "temperatures soared". The modern word thus runs a triple course: majestic literal flight (eagles, jets, dragons), steep measurable climb (markets, temperatures), and lyrical emotional lift (spirits, melodies, careers).

The inflected form and Tolkien's dragon. Soared is the regular past tense (soar + -ed), in continuous use since the verb entered English; the modern noun soar is a later back-formation, not the source. In the Tolkien sentence the word deploys its oldest and grandest register: the dragon, having issued from the Gate while the waters rose in fierce whistling steam, soared blazing into the air and settled on the mountain-top — one effortless, majestic, fire-fringed ascent. Two and a half millennia after a Greek breeze named the air itself, the descendant word still carries that hawk's wing and that breath of wind, now lifting a dragon above a mountain in a spout of green and scarlet flame.
