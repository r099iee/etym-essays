Primary sense — the bird. The doves are the birds of the family Columbidae commonly called “dove” in English: small to medium-sized, plump-bodied, short-legged, soft-feathered seed-eaters with small heads, slender bills, and a characteristic low, rhythmic, two-note cooing call. In everyday British and American usage the word dove tends to name the smaller, gentler, often whiter birds — the turtle dove, the collared dove, the mourning dove, the rock dove — while pigeon names the larger, grey, city-adapted forms and the domestic breeds; ornithologists, however, use the two words interchangeably, since pigeons and doves belong to the same family and the ubiquitous urban pigeon is simply the domesticated descendant of the wild rock dove (Columba livia). Doves feed on seeds and grains, nest in loose twig platforms, pair in long monogamous bonds, and rear their young on “crop milk”; the soft, endlessly repeated call that gives the verb to coo is their signature. “Doves” is the ordinary regular plural, used of flocks, of species collectively (“the doves of the Old World”), and of the birds seen in pairs or companies. In Wilde’s story “The Happy Prince” the pink-and-white doves of the Temple at Baalbec are the softly cooing witnesses of affectionate peace in the warm East, against which the Prince’s cold, sacrificial winter is measured.

  • Symbol of peace and of the Holy Spirit. The dove is the most freighted bird in Western symbolism. In the ancient Near East the dove was sacred to the love-goddesses Ishtar, Astarte and Aphrodite/Venus; in the Bible the dove returns to Noah with the olive branch (Genesis 8:11), and at Jesus’ baptism the Holy Spirit descends “like a dove” (Matthew 3:16). From these texts the dove became the Christian emblem of the Holy Ghost and, fused with the olive branch, the universal icon of peace. Picasso’s 1949 dove lithograph for the World Peace Congress fixed the “peace dove” for the twentieth century, and white doves are still released at weddings, funerals and openings as living symbols.
  • Political sense — “doves” versus “hawks”. Since 1962 the plural “doves” has named the faction that prefers diplomacy, negotiation and restraint to military force; “hawks” want escalation and war. The pairing was popularised during the Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962) in the article “In Time of Crisis” by Stewart Alsop and Charles Bartlett in the Saturday Evening Post, and became universal vocabulary during the Vietnam War (“the doves in Congress”, “dove senators”). The semantic bridge is folk character: the dove is gentle and peaceable, the hawk fierce and predatory, and the two birds were made to stand for the two temperaments of government.
  • Term of endearment and character type. “Dove” has been an affectionate name for a beloved person since at least the sixteenth century — Shakespeare uses it for sweethearts — and a “dove” is simply a gentle, innocent, harmless person (“she is a dove”, “dove-like meekness”). This sense flows directly from the bird’s softness of voice and manner into human character.
  • Colour, keeping and species names. “Dove grey” is a soft grey with a faint bluish or brownish cast — the colour of the rock dove — long a fashion and interior colour; a dovecote is a house built for doves; “stock dove”, “turtle dove”, “ring dove” and “rock dove” are species names; the once-numberless passenger pigeon, an American “dove” hunted to extinction in 1914, shows how large the family’s flocks could be.
  • A modern homograph. The verb form “dove” (past tense of dive, chiefly American: “she dove into the pool”), pronounced /doʊv/, is a completely different word — an analogical formation on the pattern of drive/drove that happens to share the spelling of the bird. The bird word is /dʌv/; the dive-word is /doʊv/. Plural “doves” belongs only to the bird, but the spelling collision is a staple of English wordplay and a trap for spellers.

Bridging the senses. Every meaning of “dove(s)” hangs on one thread: the bird’s perceived temperament — soft, tender, cooing, harmless, monogamous (turtle doves pair for life, hence their role in love poetry). The Holy Spirit of the Gospels, the lovers’ endearment of Elizabethan verse, the peace-politics of the 1960s and the white wedding release are all the same bird seen through different cultural telescopes; the plural “doves” simply lets a whole flock, a whole species, or a whole peace-party share the word at once.
