Etymology of English haunches (plural of haunch): a Germanic loanword transmitted through French, so the chain begins not at PIE but at the reconstructed Germanic form. The deeper link some handbooks float to PIE *kenk- 'to gird, bend, loop' is debated and not securely established; accuracy requires stopping at Germanic.

1. Proto-Germanic *hankō / *hanhō 'hip, haunch, the bent hind region' [reconstructed; recovered from the Romance loans and from Germanic kin, no written records].
2. Frankish (Old Low Franconian) *hanka 'hip' [reconstructed, 5th-7th c. CE] — the form carried into Gallo-Romance territory.
3. Old French hanche 'hip, haunch; thigh region' [attested, 12th c.] — borrowed from the Frankish source.
4. Anglo-Norman / Middle English haunche, hanch(e) 'hip, haunch, upper thigh' [attested, 14th c.] — enters English after the Norman Conquest via Anglo-Norman.
5. Modern English haunch, plural haunches [this form].

SENSE BRANCHES IN ENGLISH
  • Anatomical: hip + buttock + upper thigh, human or animal — the source of 'on one's haunches'.
  • Culinary: a joint of meat from the hind limb — 'a haunch of venison'.
  • Technical: the haunch of an arch or beam — a thickened, hip-like swelling, extended by shape-resemblance.

ROMANCE COUSINS FROM THE SAME GERMANIC SOURCE
  • French hanche 'hip'; Italian anca 'hip'; Spanish and Portuguese anca 'haunch, hindquarter' — all show the same Frankish/Germanic loan penetrating Romance.

NOT TO BE CONFUSED
  • haunch versus hunch ('hump', 'intuition'): different words and different etymologies.
