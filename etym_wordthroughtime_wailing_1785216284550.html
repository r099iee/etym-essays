- [c. 1300: the word enters English] - Borrowed as waylen meaning 'to cry aloud in sorrow', a verb built on the primal cry of grief; from the start it is bound to mourning, never to ordinary tears.
- [14th century: the biblical register locks in] - 'Wailing and gnashing of teeth' fixes the word as the sound of utter hopelessness; wailing becomes the public, piercing, often wordless cry of despair, sharper and more sustained than mere weeping.
- [Medieval to early modern: mourning ritual] - The word dominates scenes of funeral lament, prophecy, and disaster; it stays firmly elevated and intense, refusing to soften into a neutral synonym of 'crying'.
- [19th-20th century: the cry goes beyond humans] - Wailing is extended to anything that keens and pierces: sirens wail, the wind wails, bagpipes wail; the mournful, high-pitched quality is transferred wholesale to non-human sound.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still the prolonged, high-pitched cry of grief or pain, largely wordless and desperate; it keeps its archaic, eerie, dramatic register, sitting between whine and howl — and is never used lightly.
