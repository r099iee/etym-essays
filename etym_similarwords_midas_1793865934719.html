• Croesus: Similar because both were legendary kings of ancient Anatolia (Midas in Phrygia, Croesus in Lydia) famed throughout history for astonishing, proverbial wealth; differs because 'rich as Croesus' denotes having colossal, actual historical reserves of gold and treasury assets, whereas 'Midas' emphasizes the magical or uncanny faculty of generating gold from anything touched, coupled with moral foolishness, short-sighted greed, and unintended tragic ruin.
  • Plutus: Similar because both represent the manifestation and accumulation of material wealth; differs because Plutus is the personified Greco-Roman god of wealth and agricultural bounty (traditionally depicted as blind, bestowing riches without regard to merit), whereas Midas is a mortal mythological king whose wealth is the product of a foolish, self-inflicted curse.
  • Mammon: Similar because both embody the pursuit of riches; differs because Mammon is a biblical and theological personification of wealth regarded as an evil, corrupting master or false idol that demands spiritual subservience, whereas Midas embodies human cupidity, tragic lack of foresight, and hubristic folly.
  • Dives: Similar because both denote fabulously wealthy men whose riches fail to bring true fulfillment; differs because Dives (the Latin noun for 'rich man' in Luke's gospel parable of Lazarus) is characterized by callous, uncharitable indifference to the destitute at his gates, leading to eternal damnation in the afterlife.
  • Tycoon: Similar because both refer to persons who accumulate colossal commercial fortunes; differs because tycoon (from Japanese taikun, 'great lord') is a modern secular term for a powerful industrial magnate or business executive whose wealth is built through corporate strategy, monopoly, and operational scale, lacking all mythological or magical connotations.
  • Magnate: Similar because both signify figures of immense financial influence and wealth; differs because magnate (from Late Latin magnas, 'great person') designates an influential leader in a specific economic sector (e.g., shipping magnate, oil magnate), without the cautionary moral baggage of the Midas myth.
