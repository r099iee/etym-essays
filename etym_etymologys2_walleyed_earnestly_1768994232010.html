CHAIN 1 OF 2 — WALLEYED.
1. PIE *weǵʰ- "to move, carry, set in motion, transport" (c. 4500–3000 BCE; attested in daughters: Vedic Sanskrit váhati "carries, conveys," Latin vehere "to carry, convey," Greek ókhos "carriage," Old English wegan "to carry, move, weigh").
2. Proto-Germanic *weganą "to move, carry, weigh" (c. 500 BCE–2nd c. CE), whose related formations carry the nuance of rocking, swaying, or swinging motion.
3. Proto-Germanic *wagilaz / *wagla- "the thing that sways," i.e. a swinging pole, a rocker, a roost — the source of Old Norse vagl.
4. Old Norse vagl "a roost; a projecting beam or pole; by transfer, a speck, mote, or cloud in the eye" (attested c. 8th–13th c.); the eye-sense is a metaphorical transfer, the little pale flaw in the iris imagined as a "beam" in the eye.
5. Old Norse vagl-eygr "having a beam or speck in the eye," a compound of vagl + eygr "eyed," the adjectival form of auga "eye." The eye element descends independently: PIE *h₃ekʷ- "to see; the eye" > Proto-Germanic *augōn > Old Norse auga "eye" > eygr "-eyed."
6. Middle English walleiȝed / walleyed "having a pale, whitish, or outward-turned eye" (attested c. 1300, at first chiefly of horses), borrowed from Old Norse.
7. Modern English walleyed "having an eye turned outward; having a pale or whitish iris; glassy or dazed of gaze" (also the noun walleye, the fish Sander vitreus).

CHAIN 2 OF 2 — EARNESTLY.
ELEMENT 1 of 2 — the element EARNEST:
1. PIE *h₁er- "to set in motion, to stir, to rouse" (c. 4000 BCE; probable but debated link: attested in Latin orior "to arise," Greek órnumi "to rouse," Sanskrit ṛṇóti "to set in motion"), with the sense passing from "energy, effort, exertion" toward "seriousness of purpose."
2. Proto-Germanic *ernustuz "seriousness, zeal, determination" (reconstructed; attested daughters: Old High German ernust, German Ernst, Gothic *ernusts).
3. Old English eornoste / eornust "seriousness, zeal" and the adjective eornost, eornoste "earnest, serious, zealous" (attested from the 9th c.; the adverb eornostlīce "earnestly" is already present in Old English).
4. Middle English ernest "earnest, serious, eager," also the noun "seriousness, reality" in the phrase "in ernest" (12th–15th c.).
5. Modern English earnest "serious, sincere, zealous."
ELEMENT 2 of 2 — the adverbial suffix -LY:
1. PIE *leyg- "to bind" (whence Latin ligare "to bind," English ligature).
2. Proto-Germanic *līką "body, form, shape" — the semantic bridge being "form" as that which binds and frames a thing; the same root yields English like "similar to" ("of the same form").
3. Old English līċ "body, corpse, form," forming the adjective suffix -līċ and the adverb suffix -līċe ("in the body, manner, or form of").
4. Middle English -liche / -li, as in ernestliche "earnestly" (attested by c. 1200), reduced to -ly.
5. English earnestly "with serious and sincere intention" (the adverb of earnest).

NUANCE NOTE: the PIE *h₁er- link for earnest is the one genuinely debated step in these chains; the Germanic stage *ernustuz is secure, but some dictionaries record the word's further origin as "uncertain." No stage has been invented, merged, or skipped. Note also that "earnest" meaning "a pledge" (earnest money) is a separate, unrelated word from Latin arrha and has no part in this chain.
