bucking — the present participle of the verb to buck, meaning (of a horse or other animal) to leap into the air with the back arched, head lowered, and legs stiff or kicking, usually in an attempt to dislodge a rider. On this card the word is tested in its equestrian sense: "The horse started bucking as soon as he tried to mount it." The image is violent, sudden, and athletic — the animal's spine convulses into a rigid arch, its forelegs and hind legs leave the ground in rapid succession, and the rider is flung. The word is onomatopoeically suggestive of a short, sharp, explosive movement, and it belongs to the working vocabulary of the American West, rodeo, and horsemanship.

1. Primary sense — the horse's leap (equestrian)

The core sense: to spring up with the back arched, coming down stiff-legged, in an effort to unseat a rider. A horse that "bucks" repeatedly is said to "buck-jump" or "sunfish"; a bronco that cannot be ridden is a "bucking bronco" or "bucking horse." This is a violent, uncontrolled, defiant motion — quite different from a playful prance.

2. Secondary sense — to resist or oppose (metaphor)

From the horse's refusal to be ridden comes the modern figurative sense: to buck the trend, buck the system, buck authority — to resist, defy, or push against something, especially a prevailing tendency or an institution. "Bucking" here keeps the image of an animal throwing off a rider: the trend is the rider, and the bucker refuses to carry it.

3. The noun "buck" — male animal (the etymological root)

The verb derives from the noun buck, a male deer, goat, rabbit, or antelope. Male deer and goats leap and "buck" in courtship and combat displays, arching their backs and springing stiffly; when English speakers saw a horse reproduce exactly that posture, they borrowed the animal's name and turned it into the verb. Thus the horse sense is itself a transfer from deer and goat behavior — a striking case of animal-to-animal metaphor.

4. Other modern senses of the same word family

  • To buck (a burden/thing): to move something upward with a jerk, or (of an engine or vehicle) to move forward in a series of jerks.
  • "Pass the buck" / "the buck stops here": idioms about responsibility, from the practice of passing a marker (originally a buckhorn-handled knife) in poker — an entirely separate etymology from the animal and the verb.
  • Buck as money: "a buck" = a dollar, from buckskin (deerskin) used as a unit of trade on the American frontier — again a separate development from the same animal noun.
  • Historical — buck-washing: to soak or wash clothes in lye; a separate verb of obscure origin, now obsolete.

5. Historical semantic bridge

The chain is: Proto-Germanic *bukkaz "he-goat, male deer" → Old English bucca "he-goat" and buc "male deer" → the noun buck → (19th-century American English) the verb to buck, "to leap like a buck," describing horses → (20th century) the figurative "buck the trend." The bridge between the animal noun and the horse verb is purely behavioral: the arched-back leap of a male deer or goat is the motion of a bucking horse, so the name of the animal became the name of the movement.

6. Register and stylistic note

  • Register: plain, vigorous, American; central to rodeo and cowboy vocabulary.
  • Emotional coloring: the verb implies defiance, wildness, and untamed energy — whether the animal's or the resister's.
  • Related forms: "buck" (verb/noun), "bucking" (participle, adjective: "a bucking horse"), "buck-jump," "bucking bronco," "sunfishing" (a specific bucking style).
  • Common confusions: do not confuse with "buck" (dollar) or "pass the buck" (responsibility), which come from different sources.

In summary, "bucking" on this card is the equestrian verb — a violent, arched-back leap by a horse that refuses its rider — but the word opens onto a wider family: the male deer that gave the verb its name, the figurative "bucking the trend," and the frontier dollar, all descendants of the same ancient word for a male animal.
