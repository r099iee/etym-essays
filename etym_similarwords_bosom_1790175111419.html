• breast: the ordinary modern word for the same part of the body; differs only in register, being neutral where bosom is literary, decorous and faintly erotic.
  • chest: the anatomical and general term; differs in covering the whole rib-cage rather than the front of it, and in being the word a doctor or a tailor would use.
  • bust: an older and now archaic alternative for the chest or the breast; differs in being almost entirely confined to sculpture, fashion and antiquarian writing.
  • heart: the closest figurative substitute, as in the bosom of the heart and in one’s bosom; differs in having lost the physical concreteness that bosom keeps, and in not carrying any suggestion of shelter or enclosure.
  • lap: shares the image of a sheltered hollow and the fixed phrases, as in ‘in the lap of the gods’ and ‘the lap of luxury’; differs in usually meaning the knee and the things resting on it, and in not extending to the figurative sense of one person’s feelings.
  • bosom friend, close friend, confidant: the neutral modern terms for the friendship sense, of which confidant is the closest in adding the idea of shared secrets; confidant differs in being strictly about confidences, whereas a bosom friend is simply the nearest of one’s friends.
  • intimate: the ordinary adjective matching the sense in the quoted sentence; differs in being clinical and slightly cold, and in saying nothing at all about warmth or closeness, which bosom does.
  • dearest and darling: the affectionate equivalents in the attributive use; differ in being warmer and more familiar, where bosom is grander and more formal.
  • embrace: shares the physical source, the holding to the chest; differs in naming the action rather than the place, and in lacking every figurative extension that bosom has accumulated.
  • inmost part, recess, interior, heart: the modern general words for ‘the innermost, hidden part’; differ in being abstract and geometric, while bosom keeps the sense of warmth and protection that the physical chest provides.
  • shelter and refuge: match the ‘in the bosom of’ sense of being enclosed and looked after; differ in omitting the intimacy that the phrase normally carries, and in being institutional rather than personal.
  • hollow, vale, glen, coomb: the topographical substitutes for the valley sense of the phrase the bosom of the earth; hollow is the closest, differing only in lacking the suggestion of shelter.
  • bosom of the sea versus roadstead and anchorage: for the sense of sheltered, landlocked water the technical terms are exact, where bosom is the poetic one.
  • bosom sins and secret sins: the modern equivalents of the puritanical sense, the sins of the inner life; the compound survives mainly as a historical curiosity.
  • the bosom of Abraham: the fixed religious phrase for the sheltered place of the faithful dead; it has no true synonym in English, since the word and the doctrine arrived together.
