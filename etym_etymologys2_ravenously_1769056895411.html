PIE *rep- (also reconstructed *Hrep- by Beekes; c. 4000+ BCE) — "to snatch, seize, tear away"; a root of violent taking, whose Italic reflexes are secure.\n\nSTAGE 2 — Latin rapere (rapiō, rapere, rapuī, raptum) (attested from the 3rd c. BCE) — "to seize, snatch, drag away, carry off, plunder"; the classical verb of violent taking, active in law, war, and love poetry.\n\nSTAGE 3 — Latin rapīna, -ae (1st c. BCE) — "robbery, plunder, booty"; a feminine noun formed from rapere with the *-īna suffix of activity or result.\n\nSTAGE 4 — Old French ravineus / ravineux (12th-13th c.) — "rapacious, greedy, given to seizing"; an adjective formed on ravine (from Latin rapīna), with the regular Gallo-Romance change of Latin -p- to -v- in this position.\n\nSTAGE 5 — Middle English ravenous (14th c.) — "voracious, famished, predatory, greedy for plunder"; borrowed from Old French, first applied to beasts and famished men.\n\nSTAGE 6 — English ravenous + the adverbial suffix -ously → English RAVENOUSLY — "in a ravenous, predatory, extremely hungry manner"; the adverbial formation used on this card.\n\nNOTE ON THE DEBATE: the Greek cognate ἐρέπτομαι (eréptomai), "to feed on, devour" (Homeric), is grouped under *rep- by Beekes but rejected by some handbooks; the Sanskrit root rap-, "to injure," is too thinly attested to rely on. Neither affects the Italic → French → English line, which is secure.\n\nFALSE FRIEND FLAGGED: the bird raven (Old English hræfn, from Proto-Germanic *hrabnaz) is NOT related to ravenous; the similarity is pure coincidence, and the card's mnemonic is a deliberate folk-etymology, not a historical derivation.
