- 15th–16th c. — Falconry & early tennis: in Middle French, volée meant the flight of a bird or striking a ball in flight (à la volée) in the royal sport of jeu de paume.
- 1570s — Gunpowder warfare importation: borrowed into Elizabethan English as military terminology for the simultaneous discharge of muskets or cannon, immediately adopted by Shakespeare.
- 17th c. — Dutch linear tactics & military doctrine: formal volley fire (*countermarch*) becomes the central doctrine of European infantry combat under Maurice of Nassau and Gustavus Adolphus.
- 18th c. — Satire & naval broadsides: Jonathan Swift applies volley to Lilliputian archery in Gulliver's Travels (1726), while maritime narratives deploy it for naval warfare.
- 19th c. — Modern sports revolution: Walter Wingfield codifies the tennis volley (1874), and in 1895 William G. Morgan's new game is christened Volleyball.
- Today — Universal athletic, rhetorical, and military term: universally used in soccer, tennis, military honors (three-volley salute), and political journalism for rapid barrages.
