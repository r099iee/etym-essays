- (c. 1400, borrowed from Latin potens) - A learned, dignified adjective for rulers and the mighty: “potent prince”, “potent king” — formal, exalted register.
- (16th–17th c.) - Broadens to any effective agent: potent arguments, potent medicines, potent liquors, potent charms (Shakespeare’s “potent art”) — the sense shifts from social rank to sheer efficacy.
- (18th–19th c.) - Science and medicine adopt it: potent remedies, potent poisons, potent doses — the word becomes quantitative: it measures strength of effect per unit of substance.
- (19th–20th c.) - Reproductive physiology: “potency” and “impotence” become clinical terms; pharmacology measures drug potency; “potential” develops its own scientific career (potential energy, electric potential).
- (20th c.–today) - “Potent” is used of wine, whisky, coffee, perfume, magnets, arguments, imagery — always meaning concentrated, effective force; journalism adds “a potent mix”, “potent symbolism”. The word has travelled from royal courts to labs, bars, and headlines without losing its core: power that shows itself in effects.
