- 1620s–1650s - Originating as university and legal slang (*quib*, from Latin *quibus*), meaning a clever pun, verbal play, or sarcastic quip used in academic disputations.
- 1670s–1720s - Expanded with the frequentative suffix *-le* into *quibble*, denoting both an evasive verbal trick to dodge an argument and a literary pun or double entendre.
- 1760s–1790s - Prominently featured in 18th-century literary criticism (notably Samuel Johnson) as a frivolous rhetorical indulgence where serious meaning is sacrificed for a clever play on words.
- 19th c. - The literary sense of 'pun' declined, while the argumentative sense of 'raising petty, frivolous objections or hair-splitting distinctions' became the primary standard definition.
- 20th–21st c. - Established universally across legal, political, and colloquial registers as the standard term for evasive semantic pedantry and trivial disputation.
