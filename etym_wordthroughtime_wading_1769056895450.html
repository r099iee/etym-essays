- Old English - 'wade' is already an English verb meaning broadly to go, to advance, and from the first it covers crossing water or pressing through what resists.
- Later Middle English and the early modern centuries - the sense narrows and hardens onto water itself: to walk through water too deep to run in but shallow enough to keep one's feet on the bottom.
- 16th-17th century - figurative use blooms beside the literal: 'wade through' books, business or difficulties, and 'wade in' for plunging energetically into a task, images drawn from pushing into a resistant element.
- 19th-20th century - the participle acquires a technical reach: 'wading birds' (waders) are named for feeding in the shallows, so 'wading' gains an ornithological register alongside the everyday one.
- Contemporary - 'wading' means purposeful immersion with the feet grounded, water around ankles or calves, carrying both the freshness of relief and slow, deliberate effort; its figurative senses persist in 'wading through' paperwork or 'wading into' an argument.
