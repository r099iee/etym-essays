- 1685 — “stand-still” is coined as a noun from the verb phrase “to stand still”; it means simply “a coming to a stop” — neutral, physical.
- 18th c. — extended to machinery and industry: a mill or engine “brought to a standstill”; the word enters the language of production, gaining a mechanical, systemic feel.
- 19th c. — industrial and commercial senses thrive (“business at a standstill”, “traffic at a standstill”); the word becomes standard for paralyzed enterprise and transport.
- 19th–20th c. — abstract extension to politics, talks, and diplomacy: “negotiations are at a standstill”; the word now means failure of progress, not just absence of motion.
- 20th c. — the idiom “grind to a standstill” appears and spreads; “standstill” becomes a journalism staple for strikes, storms, and gridlock — dramatic, negative register.
- 21st c. — used both literally (vehicles, machines) and figuratively (economies, projects, societies); fully naturalised, slightly formal, strongly negative in connotation.
