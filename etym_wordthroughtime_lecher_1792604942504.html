- [11th–13th c. Old French] — Originated from lechier ("to lick"), designating a glutton, gourmand, parasite, or dissolute tavern-haunter who indulged in culinary and physical excess — [perception: low-status social parasite and shameless glutton]
- [Late 14th c. Middle English (Chaucer & Langland)] — Borrowed via Anglo-Norman into Middle English, where medieval theological doctrine linked gluttony directly to carnal lust, narrowing the term specifically to an individual abandoned to fornication and sexual incontinence — [perception: grave moral sinner guilty of the deadly sin of Luxuria]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English & Renaissance Theatre] — Became a prominent theatrical insult and moral archetype in Shakespeare, Jonson, and Webster, applied to corrupt magistrates, lustful old men, and treacherous courtiers — [perception: contemptible, repulsive hypocrite whose secret lust corrodes civil order]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Eras] — Intensified in bourgeois moral censure, serving as a severe term of social ostracism against predatory men who preyed on vulnerable women or breached Victorian codes of domestic respectability — [perception: dangerous moral predator and destroyer of female virtue]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Retained as a potent term of disgust applied to predatory sexual harassers, creepy authority figures who exploit power dynamics for sexual favors, and chronic consumers of vulgar prurience — [perception: deeply pejorative label for an unwholesome, predatory, and boundary-violating sexual pursuer]
