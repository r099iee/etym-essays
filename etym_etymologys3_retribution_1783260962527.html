At the root of retribution stands one of the most interesting puzzles of Indo-European word-building: the Proto-Indo-European root *treb-, "to build, settle, dwell", whose concrete sense is a dwelling or settlement — a place people build and live in. Its footprint across the family is striking and helps to fix the meaning. Celtic preserves it in Old Irish treb "building, farmstead" and the verb trebaid "to dwell, till", and in Welsh tref "town, home", which survives in countless Welsh place-names. Germanic shows Proto-Germanic *þurpa- "village, settlement", giving Old English þorp / þrop (our place-name element -thorp, as in Cleethorpes), Old Norse þorp, German Dorf and Dutch dorp. Baltic has Lithuanian trobà "house, building, farmstead". In Italic the same root yields Latin tribus, "a tribe" — originally a territorial division of the Roman people, the sort of district a settled community forms.

The Roman antiquarian tradition explained tribus differently, as "one of the three parts" (from tri- "three" and a form of the verb "to be", after Romulus' three original tribes), and that folk-etymology is still recorded in dictionaries. Modern comparative scholarship, however, prefers the derivation from *treb- "settlement, district", because the Celtic, Germanic, and Baltic settlement-words point so coherently to a root meaning "a place people build and live in", and because the Roman tribus was, in historical fact, a territorial unit. The debate is genuine and should be acknowledged: the traditional tri- explanation is not philologically dead, but it rests on a folk story rather than on comparative evidence.

From the tribal unit comes the verb of allocation. Latin tribuere means "to assign, allot, apportion, give" — originally to divide property among the tribes. Its derivatives are legion and almost all reach English: tributum "a tax levied on the tribes" gives tribute and tributary; contribuere "to bring together into a common fund" gives contribute; distribuere "to divide among" gives distribute; attribuere "to assign to" gives attribute; and tribunus "tribune, chief of a tribe" gives tribune and tribunal. To this family belongs the crucial verb retribuere — re- "back" + tribuere "to give" — literally "to give back": to repay, recompense, requite, whether with good or evil. The past participle retribūtus "given back" and the abstract noun retribūtiō, retribūtiōnis "repayment, recompense" complete the Latin series.

The crucial semantic history now unfolds. In classical Latin retribuere could still be perfectly neutral — a repayment of money or a return of favour. But in Christian and biblical Latin the word took on its great moral career: God retribuit, God repays every person according to his deeds, and retribūtiō becomes the technical word for divine recompense, whether reward or punishment. It is this theological colouring that Old French retribution (thirteenth century) and Middle English retribucioun (first attested c. 1385, in Chaucer) inherit: the word enters English already freighted with the idea of God's moral bookkeeping.

The shift in English can be tracked across the registers. In Middle English retribucioun means recompense or reward, still honouring the Latin neutrality. Over the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, under the pressure of theology and of the English Bible, retribution increasingly specialises to the punishment side of the ledger — divine retribution, retribution for sin. By the eighteenth century, the word has been secularised into political and historical prose: it names the deserved consequences that follow public wrong, the working-out of a moral balance-sheet in the affairs of nations. It is precisely this secularised, providential sense that Macaulay deploys when he writes that "wrong was followed by just retribution" in the American colonies and Ireland — a Whig conviction, expressed in a single Latinate noun, that history itself is a tribunal and that empires pay their debts.

  • Sibling words from the same root. Tribute, attribute, contribute, distribute, tribunal, tribune, tributary — all descend from Latin tribuere / tribus and thus ultimately from *treb- "settlement".
  • False-friend warning. Do not confuse retribution (re- "back" + tribuere, "to give back") with restitution (re- "back" + statuere, "to set up again", from the *steh₂- root of "standing"); the rhyme is coincidental, the roots are different.
  • Sound change. The form is a loanword, so the Germanic sound laws do not apply; the Latin sequence tribuere → retribūtiō → Old French retribution → Middle English retribucioun shows only the normal Romance and English simplification and the French loss of inflectional endings.
  • Literary attestations. Chaucer (c. 1385); the English Bible and homiletic tradition; Milton; Macaulay's History of England, where "just retribution" sums up his philosophy of history; and the modern legal-philosophical literature of retributivism (Kant's punishment theory, modern criminology).
  • Contemporary note. In modern use retribution is now almost exclusively the punishment side of the ledger, while requital and recompense preserve the neutral "giving back". The theological sense survives in religious prose; the financial sense is extinct. The word remains formal and grave — nothing in English merely receives "a little retribution", the term insists on desert and proportion.
