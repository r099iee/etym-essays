- [c. 1400 Late Middle English] — First recorded as a military term (straglen), denoting soldiers who broke away from their regiment's marching column to scavenge, forage, or plunder — [perception: an undisciplined, dishonorable violation of martial order punishable by death]
- [16th c. Tudor & Elizabethan Era] — Broadened to describe vagrants, masterless men, and gypsies roaming the English countryside outside parish jurisdiction — [perception: a dangerous social nuisance threatening civil peace]
- [17th c. Early Modern English] — Adopted into natural history and agricultural writing to describe untidy, rambling vines, unpruned hedges, and scattered trees — [perception: wild, uncultivated nature needing the pruning shears of human artifice]
- [18th–Early 19th c. Enlightenment to Romanticism] — Transitioned into landscape aesthetics (picturesque rural cottages scattered irregularly along valleys) and poetic imagery; Wordsworth in The Prelude applied it to acoustic diffusion ("straggling sounds") — [perception: charming picturesque irregularity, and later, a poignant emblem of acoustic and energetic decay]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Era] — Standardized across urban planning (straggling suburban sprawl), athletics (straggling runners trailing the marathon pack), and logistics (straggling arrivals) — [perception: a neutral to mildly critical descriptor of disorganized, unpunctual, or loose dispersion]
