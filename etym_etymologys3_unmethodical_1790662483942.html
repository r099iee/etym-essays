Unmethodical is a textbook English negative formation: take the seventeenth-century adjective methodical, add the native prefix un-, and you get not-methodical, first recorded in 1623. Its story is therefore the story of method itself, plus the characteristically English habit of negating Latinate adjectives with a Germanic prefix rather than borrowing in- or a- forms.

The deep root is PIE *med-, to take appropriate measures — a root that branches into measuring, considering, healing, and advising. Greek shows it in medesthai, to take counsel, and in medon, a ruler. From it, by prefixing meta- (after, along) to hodos (way, road, journey — a word of obscure further origin), Greek formed methodos: literally the way after or along which one pursues something, hence a pursuit of knowledge, a mode of inquiry. Plato and Aristotle use methodos for disciplined investigation; medical writers use it for a system of treatment. Latin borrowed it as methodus, chiefly in rhetoric, medicine, and late-antique teaching, keeping the sense of an orderly way of proceeding.

  • 1541–1600: method enters English. Early Tudor writers take methode from Middle French for regular, teachable procedure — in logic, grammar, medicine, and warfare. To do something by method is to follow a set order that others can learn and repeat. The word carries Renaissance prestige: method distinguishes art from knack, science from guesswork.
  • 1611: methodical appears. Once method is established, the adjective methodical follows in the King James era, meaning done according to method, orderly and systematic. It quickly acquires a double edge: praise for thoroughness and blame for plodding rigidity. A methodical person finishes the catalogue; a methodical style can also feel mechanical.
  • 1623: unmethodical is coined. Almost immediately the negative is needed, and English reaches for un- rather than Latin immethodical (which never takes root) or Greek amethodical (a rare learned rival). Early uses describe unsystematic inquiry, disordered arrangement, or rambling procedure — often with moral overtones of carelessness. The form is transparent to any speaker: un + method + ical.
  • 1660–1800: science, devotion, and manners. Restoration scientists contrast methodical experiment with unmethodical guessing. Divines worry about unmethodical prayer or reading. Crucially, the Methodists (John Wesley from the 1730s) take their nickname from methodical devotion, making unmethodical by implication irreligious or undisciplined in popular polemic. Letter-writers apologize for unmethodical correspondence; naturalists apologize for unmethodical lists.
  • 1800–present: the concessive adjective. In modern prose unmethodical is rarer than unsystematic or haphazard, and slightly formal or old-fashioned. It survives where the author wants to concede disorder while excusing it — exactly the source sentence move: the traditional terms, unmethodical though they are, usually work well enough. The concessive frame (X though they are) turns a defect into a forgivable, even endearing limitation. The word now signals mild, gentlemanly criticism rather than fierce condemnation.

Phonetically the development is straightforward anglicization. Greek methodos with aspirated th becomes Latin methodus, French methode with silent h restored in spelling, English method /ˈmɛθəd/. Adding Romance -ical shifts stress to methodical /mɛˈθɒdɪkəl/, and prefixing un- adds a secondary stress: /ˌʌnmɛˈθɒdɪkəl/, the RP form required here. Spelling never wavers once established; the only rivals, immethodical and amethodical, remain dictionary curiosities.

Literary attestation shows the concessive use early: seventeenth-century virtuosi confess unmethodical collections of curiosities; eighteenth-century essayists praise unmethodical conversation over stiff system; nineteenth-century reviewers forgive unmethodical plots if characters live; twentieth-century stylisticians, like the author of the source passage, forgive unmethodical traditional terms — sincere, lucid, terse, turgid — because as introductions they work. The irony is that unmethodical is itself a perfectly methodical formation, built by rule from known parts, even as it names the absence of rule.
