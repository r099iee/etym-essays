In Gibbon's self-description — that "diligence and accuracy are the only merits which an historical writer may ascribe to himself" — the verb performs a precise act of intellectual accounting. It means to assign or credit a quality, cause, origin, or authorship to a person, period, or thing, and here it is used reflexively: the historian writes only two sober merits onto his own name, refusing to "write" genius or brilliance into his self-portrait. The word is deliberately judicial and bookish rather than casual; it turns attribution into a formal, verifiable claim rather than an act of vanity.

Primary modern sense (attribute to a cause or source). To ascribe X to Y is to state that X has its origin, explanation, or responsibility in Y. The construction is transitive and takes a prepositional complement: ascribe X to Y. Typical objects are abstract — success, failure, motives, importance, authorship, date, origin — and the subject may be a person, an action, a text, or a phenomenon.

Secondary sense (authorship and provenance). In scholarship and criticism, to ascribe a work means to assign it to a particular author, school, period, or place: "This poem is usually ascribed to Shakespeare, although some scholars disagree." This is the historical core of the word, the sense closest to its Latin origin in ascrībere "to write in addition / enroll" — the image of writing a name into the record beside a text.

Tertiary sense (motives, responsibility, blame, credit). With persons as object, ascribe handles moral and psychological attribution: ascribe motives to someone, ascribe blame or credit to a policy, ascribe an outcome to luck. Unlike impute, which usually loads blame, ascribe is neutral in charge — it can assign praise, blame, or mere cause with equal ease.

Technical and metaphorical extensions.

  • Statistical and causal attribution: in data science and experimental design, to ascribe variance or an effect to a variable ("the model ascribes the improvement to the new feature") — a modern transfer of the "assign a cause" sense into quantitative jargon.
  • Attribution studies and stylometry: computational tools "ascribe" anonymous texts to authors, reviving the old authorship sense in digital form.
  • Medical and diagnostic metaphor: symptoms are ascribed to conditions; the word is common in clinical notes and case reports.
  • Historiography and biography: dates, monuments, and cultural changes are ascribed to periods, cultures, or rulers — the Gibbonian register par excellence.
  • Legal and notarial usage: documents, opinions, and responsibilities are ascribed to their makers; the word keeps its ancient "written onto the record" flavour.

Historical-semantic bridge. Latin adscrībere (ad- "to, toward" + scrībere "to write") meant literally "to write in addition," then "to add a name to a list, to enroll, to place on an account." The bridge from "write onto" to "attribute" is a bookkeeping metaphor: attribution is entering a credit or a responsibility onto a name in a ledger. That documentary, notarial image survives in the modern word's formal tone.

Register and distribution. Ascribe is formal, academic, and literary; it flourishes in historiography, literary criticism, legal and moral prose, and scientific writing, and is markedly rarer in everyday speech, where attribute, credit, put down to, or chalk up to dominate. Its derivatives — ascription and ascribable — reinforce the same field. The reflexive use in the Gibbon sentence ("ascribe to himself") stresses what one may honestly claim for oneself, an ethical nuance of self-accounting rather than of gossip about others.
