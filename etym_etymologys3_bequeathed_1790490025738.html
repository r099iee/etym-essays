Bequeathed is the regular past of bequeath, Old English becwethan, itself be- plus cwethan to say. The story is therefore twofold: a little prefix that intensifies and redirects, and a great speech-verb that shrank to a legal formula. Old English cwethan (Mercian cweothan, past cwæth, participle cwethen) meant broadly to say, speak, name, call, declare, proclaim — cognate with Old Saxon quethan, Old Norse kvetha, Old High German quedan, Gothic qithan, all from Proto-Germanic *kwethanan. In heroic poetry a king cwæth his doom; in law a man becwæth his goods, literally spoke them to another. The prefix be- (unstressed bi, by) adds direction — to say to, to speak about thoroughly — turning utterance into disposition.

By 1200 the simplex quethan was fading, leaving its past quoth (quoth he) as a fossil quoted speech-tag, while the compound lived on precisely because wills repeated it. Medieval testaments, first in Old English then in Latin-French-English macaronic, formulaically bequethe soul to God, body to burial, goods to kin. The sense say died c. 1300; the sense leave-by-will monopolised the verb. Phonology is conservative: initial be- reduces to /bɪ/, stressed queath keeps /kwiːð/ with voiced th /ð/ from Old English th between vowels, past adds /d/ giving /bɪˈkwiːðd/. Spelling keeps qu for /kw/ and ea for /iː/, a Middle English inheritance.

  • From speech to will (900–1300): charters show becwethan bridging exhort, blame, and bequeath — to speak solemnly to someone is to enjoin and to assign. As written wills replace oral death-bed declarations, the verb's object shifts from words to things: one bequeaths words, then bequeaths goods by words. The OED's remark that it is an old word kept alive in wills captures this institutional life-support.
  • Legal specialisation (1300–1800): common lawyers distinguish bequeath (personalty: money, plate, stock) from devise (realty: land, houses) from Old French deviser to divide. Wills therefore give, devise and bequeath in triplet. Bequeathed as participle labels the property after death: the bequeathed estate, sums bequeathed. Ruskin's debts bequeathed him stretches the category — debts are not property — for moral effect, a stretch Victorian readers would savour.
  • Figurative extension (1800–present): once wills are familiar, journalists and historians borrow the past tense for any intergenerational transfer: the war bequeathed by empire, the collection bequeathed by a collector, the climate bequeathed to children. The past tense suits retrospect: we discover what was bequeathed only after the giver is gone. The verb thus moves from court to commentary without losing solemnity.

The debated PIE root *gwet- to say must be handled honestly. Pokorny listed Sanskrit gad-, Welsh gwed-, Latin vetare as kin, but Boutkan and de Vaan note formal irregularities (voicing, ablaut, semantics of forbid versus say) and prefer no certain IE etymology beyond Germanic. The English chain from 800 CE is, by contrast, fully secure in texts. To teach bequeathed is to teach a Germanic speech-verb preserved by lawyers long after speakers stopped saying quethan for ordinary saying.
