- Old English & Middle English sense - howl is an echoic word for the long, loud, doleful cry of wolves and of the wind; the vowel itself is the sound, an open-mouthed wail without words
- 16th-19th century - the cry of dogs, wolves and gales is matched by the human howl of pain, grief and fury; a howl is the cry that escapes control, carrying suffering rather than threat
- 19th-20th century comic extension - the word stretches to laughter: 'a howl' is an uproariously funny moment, and 'a howler' a grotesque blunder; sirens, storms and amplifiers also howl
- Contemporary sense - the animal and wind cry remains primary, while the human uses span raw distress and helpless laughter; the word is sound-painting, its long wailing vowel doing half the work
