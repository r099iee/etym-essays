- Classical antiquity — Maiandros/Maeander perceived purely as the famously looping river of Caria; name already proverbial for anything crooked or labyrinthine
- Late 16th–early 17th c. English — meander enters as learned noun/verb: ornamental “meander” patterns and rivers that “meander”; still classical flavor
- 18th c. — Geographic and travel prose normalizes the river sense; figurative hints appear for winding paths of travel and argument
- Romantic / 19th c. — Strong positive or pastoral charge: meandering streams, meandering walks, leisurely narrative drift; scientific geomorphology begins codifying meanders as landforms
- Early 20th c. — Dual life solidifies: technical earth-science term vs. literary/colloquial image of digression and unhurried motion
- Late 20th–21st c. — Broad everyday use for winding routes, casual strolls, and tangential talk; “meandering speech” can be affectionate or gently pejorative depending on context; place-name origin mostly opaque to general speakers
