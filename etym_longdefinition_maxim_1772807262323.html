Maxim means a short, pithy statement expressing a general truth or rule of conduct — a condensed principle offered as wisdom or as guidance for action. The word comes, through French, from the Latin maxima (sc. sententia), 'the greatest proposition': a maxim claims the status of a highest, most general truth from which reasoning and behaviour may proceed.

IN THE PASSAGE

In the account of Li Shou-zhong's educational policy, maxim names the 'good old' saying 'a stupid woman is a virtuous one'. Here the word is not a neutral logical term but a culturally entrenched rule of conduct, invoked as authority for withholding serious study from girls and from his own daughter. The tone of the narrative is coolly ironic: what the household treats as a highest truth is exposed as a tool of limitation. The maxim functions as moral shorthand that closes debate — once quoted, it justifies a whole regime of female ignorance framed as virtue. The irony depends on the word's pretensions: a maxim is supposed to be the distilled wisdom of experience, and here it is a slogan that licenses a policy.

PRIMARY SENSES

  • Rule of conduct / moral principle: a short statement of practical wisdom or a guiding precept ('live by the maxim that honesty is the best policy') — the dominant modern sense.
  • General truth: a pithy observation widely held to be true, in the spirit of La Rochefoucauld's Maximes ('a maxim about human nature').
  • Fundamental principle (philosophy / logic): a self-evident starting point of reasoning, the 'greatest proposition' of scholastic logic — an older and now chiefly historical sense (maxima propositio).
  • Legal maxim: an established principle of law stated compactly (e.g., ignorantia juris non excusat), a standing technical sense in jurisprudence.
  • Practical / technical rule: in medicine, engineering, and crafts, a maxim is a compressed rule of good practice derived from experience.

HOW THE SENSES BRIDGE

  • The Latin source maxima ('greatest') named the highest, most general premise of an argument; from 'greatest proposition' the word moved to 'leading principle' and then to 'a concise statement of such a principle'.
  • The moral sense grew out of the logical one: once a principle is compressed into a striking sentence and applied to conduct, it becomes a maxim of behaviour.
  • The legal and technical senses preserve the 'leading principle' idea in specific fields: a maxim of law or of practice is a settled, highest-level rule.

REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS

  • Collocations: live by a maxim, the old maxim that…, a guiding maxim, maxims of prudence / morality / statecraft, as the maxim goes.
  • Register is formal to literary; the word appears in philosophy, etiquette, jurisprudence, and in narrative characterization of people who rule by slogans.

BOUNDARIES WITH NEAR-SYNONYMS

  • An axiom is a self-evident starting point in logic or mathematics; a maxim is a practical, often moral saying.
  • A proverb is a traditional folk saying, anonymous and broadly circulated; a maxim may be more sententious and attributed to a wise source.
  • An aphorism is a terse, often witty observation that need not prescribe conduct; a maxim prescribes or advises.
  • A dictum is an authoritative pronouncement, sometimes judicial; a precept is an explicit rule for action; a motto is a word or phrase adopted as a badge of intention.
