WOBBLE means to move unsteadily from side to side with a quick, rocking, slightly swinging motion, as if pivoting on a loose joint; to sway and quiver because something is not firmly fixed, balanced, or under one's control. The motion it names is small, rapid, and oscillating — a repeated tilting back and forth rather than a single lurch — and it always carries the implication of something loose, ill-fitted, or about to give way: a wheel wobbles on a bent axle, a chair wobbles on an uneven floor, a jelly wobbles on a plate. In the sentence from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the goblins' legs wobble the instant sunlight strikes them: the idiom is one of sudden collapse of bodily control, their limbs rocking loosely beneath them while their heads swim with the same vertigo ("giddy"). The tone is at once comic and physiological — Tolkien renders the goblins' hatred of daylight as a kind of physical poisoning, and wobble makes the effect faintly ridiculous and undignified, an involuntary, twitching unsteadiness rather than a deliberate movement.

The verb is both physical and psychological by long habit. At its literal core it describes unsteady motion of objects and bodies; by a very old figurative extension it describes unstable states of mind, opinion, and affairs — a plan wobbles, a decision wobbles, a government wobbles, a currency wobbles. The bridge between the two senses is the idea of instability itself: what wobbles is what is not firmly fixed, whether a loose wheel or a wavering resolve. The word's register is informal and vivid — it belongs to everyday speech, to mechanics, and increasingly to science and politics — and it is always on the side of the unsteady, the wobbly, the about-to-topple.

  • Sense 1 — physical, of objects: "to rock unsteadily from side to side, to sway on a loose pivot," of wheels, furniture, towers, jelly, and anything poorly balanced or loosely fitted. This is the oldest attested sense (wabble, 1650s) and remains the core.
  • Sense 2 — physical, of bodies: "to move unsteadily, to sway while walking or standing," of a person or creature whose legs give way, who is weak, ill, drunk, frightened, or giddy — the sense Tolkien uses of the goblins' legs in sunlight.
  • Sense 3 — figurative, of persons and minds: "to waver, vacillate, to be uncertain or irresolute," of opinions, loyalties, and decisions; "he wobbled on the issue" means he could not hold a position. The extension is attested from the 18th century.
  • Sense 4 — figurative, of institutions and markets: "to be unstable, to threaten collapse," of governments, economies, prices, and shares. The word applies the unsteadiness of a loose wheel to the instability of public affairs.
  • Sense 5 — scientific and technical jargon: in physics and engineering, wobble describes unstable rotational precession or axial vibration — the Chandler wobble of the Earth's axis (named 1901), a wobbling gyroscope, a wobbling wheel on a test rig. In astronomy, a star's "wobble" is the periodic displacement of its position caused by an orbiting planet, the Doppler wobble used to detect exoplanets.
  • Sense 6 — in computing and electronics: jitter, the small, rapid, irregular variation of a signal's timing or amplitude, is colloquially a "wobble"; the term migrated from the turntable (a "wow and flutter" wobble in sound pitch) into digital signal analysis.
  • Sense 7 — idiom "to throw a wobbly" (British slang, recorded 1968): to lose one's temper, to have a fit or tantrum — the unsteadiness of balance metaphorically transferred to emotional control.
  • Historical bridge for the secondary senses: every figurative use is the same image — the loose wheel that rocks because it is not fixed to its axle. Once "wobble" had named physical instability, its transfer to the mind, the market, and the state was almost automatic, because all of them could be "unfixed," all of them could rock on slack pivots and threaten to come off.

Why wobble in this sentence. Tolkien pairs wobble with giddy to split the goblins' collapse between the body and the head: the legs rock loose on slack joints while the head swims with the same vertigo. The verb's informal, almost comical sound — the wobbly, bouncing phonetics of the word itself — is essential to the effect: it makes the goblins' daytime agony faintly ridiculous, a bodily farce rather than a heroic collapse, and it keeps the reader aware that for all their menace the goblins are creatures of the dark, undone by a light they cannot bear.
