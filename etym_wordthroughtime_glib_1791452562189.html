- 1590s - “smooth and slippery,” a dialectal physical adjective applied to surfaces and substances; the word still carries the tactile idea of something that offers no hold.
- c. 1600 - the metaphor moves from slippery surfaces to slippery speech; “glib” begins to describe a tongue, speaker, or answer that is fluent and too easy.
- 17th century - the moral judgement sharpens: glib language is plausible but unreliable, and the derivative glibbery explicitly means “untrustworthy.”
- 18th–19th centuries - the word settles into its modern critical role in conduct writing, political commentary, and fiction; it becomes a standard accusation against smooth-talking men, lawyers, and politicians.
- 20th century - “glib” is absorbed into journalism, advertising criticism, and media commentary, often attached to slogans, soundbites, and public relations.
- 21st century - the sense extends to algorithmically fluent or corporate language: a glib summary, a glib press release, a glib chatbot reply. The core judgement remains unchanged: fluency without accountability.
