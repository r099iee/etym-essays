- Old English — the sewn edge: hemm names the border of a garment, the folded finish that stops fraying, humble work of every household.
- Middle English — the verb is born: to hemmen is to edge cloth with stitching, and poets borrow it for meadows hemmed with flowers.
- 16th c. — the trap closes: to hem in appears for armies and crowds surrounding victims, sewing turned to siege, still with the domestic shiver.
- 17th–18th c. — manners catch it: to hem and haw spreads for throat-clearing hesitation, a separate echo-word that tangles happily with the sewing word.
- Victorian era — nature hems: novelists love woods, hills, and fogs that hem villages and travellers, landscape as tailor.
- Today — rules and roots: we are hemmed in by traffic, by budgets, by regulations, while Tolkien trees remind us the oldest hemmers are patient boughs closing without a sound.
