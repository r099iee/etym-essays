- [Late 14th–15th c. (Middle English)] — emerged in Anglo-French and Middle English literature (*subduen*) describing military conquest, political subjugation, and the physical vanquishing of rebellions, hostile armies, or beasts — [perception: understood as a forceful, authoritative act of triumph and imperial or feudal mastery]
- [16th–17th c. (Early Modern English)] — extended from external battlefields into moral, spiritual, and psychological literature, denoting the deliberate taming or mortification of rebellious passions, sinful lusts, pride, and unruly temperaments — [perception: celebrated as a virtue of Christian asceticism, moral discipline, and emotional self-restraint]
- [18th c. (Enlightenment & Augustan Aesthetics)] — transferred into sensory, artistic, and rhetorical domains to describe toned-down palettes, softened brushwork, quiet speech, and restrained architectural ornamentation — [perception: admired as a hallmark of neo-classical decorum, aristocratic refinement, good taste, and understated dignity]
- [19th c. (Victorian Literature & Social Demeanor)] — crystallized into a standard character descriptor for quiet, chastened, modest, or sorrowful human behavior, especially in response to bereavement, religious awe, severe illness, or majestic spectacle — [perception: viewed as a mark of emotional depth, quiet gentility, and respectful gravity in the face of solemn reality]
- [20th–21st c. (Modern & Contemporary Discourse)] — broadened into macroeconomic reporting, financial analysis, and design aesthetics, qualifying sluggish market trading, restrained consumer demand, temperate inflation, and minimalist muted interior décor — [perception: universally employed as an objective descriptor for calm, low-key, damped-down, or non-volatile conditions]
