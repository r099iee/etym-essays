Gauze is a noun for a very thin, light, translucent fabric with a loose, open weave, and by extension for anything with that filtering, see-through quality. It is one of the few words in English that passed from luxury to laboratory to poetry, and it now has three living registers: textile, medical, and figurative. In the Dream of the Red Chamber sentence, “Peeping through the gauze panel which served as a window, she could see streets and buildings more rich and elegant and throngs of people more lively and numerous than she had ever seen in her life before,” the gauze is the membrane that both reveals and veils. Dai-yu, newly arrived in the capital, rides hidden inside her palanquin, and the fine translucent silk of the window-panel lets her observe the great city while screening her from it. Hawkes is rendering the traditional Chinese window lattice or palanquin curtain (窗紗, chuāngshā) fitted with fine silk, and “gauze” catches both the material's thinness and its perceptual effect: the world seen through it is softened, hazed, and dreamlike. “Peeping” reinforces the point — she does not look, she peeps — and the gauze is both the reason she can see and the reason she must look through something to do so.

The full polysemy of the word:

  • Textile sense (core): an open-weave, very sheer fabric, originally silk and now usually cotton or a synthetic; used for veils, curtains, dress trimmings, millinery, and as a stiffening interlining. The characteristic property is the loose grid of threads that lets light and sight pass through.
  • Medical sense: absorbent surgical gauze — the loosely woven cotton dressing used to cover wounds, pack cavities, and absorb fluids; “sterile gauze” is a clinical stock item. This sense dates from nineteenth-century antiseptic surgery, above all Lister's carbolic gauze of 1867.
  • Laboratory and industrial sense: wire gauze, the square-meshed metal mesh placed over a Bunsen burner to support a beaker or flask and spread the flame's heat; a standard piece of school-laboratory equipment. Here the word has crossed entirely from cloth to metal while keeping the “open mesh” idea.
  • Figurative / poetic sense: anything thin enough to be seen through dimly, or that obscures like a veil — “a gauze of fog,” “a gauze of memory,” “the gauze of time.” The fabric becomes a metaphor for filtration, distance, and half-revelation; the adjective gauzy (“gauzy light,” “gauzy sentiment”) drives this register.
  • Related fabric terms: cheesecloth is effectively a coarse, unbleached gauze used in cooking and straining; silk gauze, cotton gauze, and gauze bandage name specific products; in theatre, a scrim-like gauze can be lit to produce transparency effects.
  • Historical registers: a sixteenth-century luxury import worn by the rich as veils and dress fabric, the word was democratised by industrial cotton in the nineteenth century and then medicalised; it now keeps both the aristocratic (bridal veil) and the clinical (bandage) associations alive at once.
  • Adjectival family: gauzy, “of or like gauze; diaphanous,” extends the word into atmosphere; gauzily and the noun gauziness complete the set. The figurative use is where the word has grown most in modern English.

Semantically, gauze is defined by three combined properties: thinness, openness of weave, and translucency. Its figurative power comes from the idea that to see through gauze is to see truly but not quite clearly — a perfect image of perception filtered through cloth, of knowledge obtained through a screen. That is precisely the novel's situation: the heroines of the Dream of the Red Chamber live screened from the world yet observe it minutely, and even Dai-yu's first glimpse of the capital is mediated by gauze. The word thus names both a fabric and a mode of vision.
