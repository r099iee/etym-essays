- [Late 14th c. Middle English] — coined by John Wycliffe and early biblical translators as a literal native Germanic compound (sēn + -ere) translating Latin videns and Hebrew ro'eh — [perception: reverently regarded as a sacred, pre-monarchical vessel of direct divine vision and revelation]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English] — canonized in the King James Bible (1 Samuel 9:9) and Renaissance tragedy; clearly demarcated from vulgar fortune-tellers as a venerable, righteous prophet of the ancient world — [perception: venerated as an archaic and holy patriarch imbued with divine truth]
- [18th c. Enlightenment] — subjected to rationalist skepticism, empirical critique, and anti-superstition satire; seers were frequently dismissed by philosophers as religious enthusiasts, charlatans, or victims of optical delusions — [perception: viewed with intellectual condescension and suspicion of fraudulent trickery]
- [19th c. Romantic & Victorian Era] — rehabilitated by Romantic poets (Blake, Wordsworth) and Victorian essayists (Carlyle, Emerson) as the archetype of the spiritual poet-philosopher who pierces mechanical materialism to perceive eternal truths — [perception: celebrated as a noble, intuitive genius in heroic communion with transcendent reality]
- [Late 19th–Early 20th c.] — absorbed into popular occultism, the Spiritualist movement, and the Theosophical Society, referring to crystal-gazers, trance mediums, and practitioners of Scottish second sight — [perception: mingled fascination, sensationalism, and occult curiosity]
- [Late 20th–21st c.] — widely secularized into economic, technological, and political forecasting (market seers, geopolitical seers), often employed with subtle journalistic irony highlighting both the public's thirst for prophecy and the perennial fallibility of predictive models — [perception: admired for uncanny strategic foresight, yet scrutinized for speculative vulnerability]
