- Roman — wandering outside: Latin extra vagari (to roam beyond bounds) fuses into Vulgar Latin *extravagare — the legal and pastoral term for stock and persons outside their limits.
- 12th c. — French strays: Old French estraier means to wander, with its past participle estraié naming the homeless and the lost — the stray as a person with no road.
- c. 1300 — English adopts it: strayen covers wandering beasts, lost travellers, and erring souls together — feet and morals in one verb from the start.
- 14th–17th c. — Scripture fixes the moral sense: the strayed sheep of sermon and parable makes straying the gentle word for sin — lost, not wicked, and always retrievable by the shepherd.
- 18th–19th c. — the poets' verb: straying eyes, straying hands, and straying hearts fill Romantic verse; the technical world borrows it for stray bullets and currents.
- Today — the betraying drift: from Tolkien's sword-hilt to wandering attention spans, strayed still names the movement its owner will not own — the small departure that confesses the large desire.
