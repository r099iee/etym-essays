I. The Germanic name and its PIE questions


The bird-name swallow is native Germanic: Old English swealwe (variants swalewe, swalowe), from Proto-Germanic *swalwōn “swallow (bird)”, attested across the family — Old Saxon swala, Old High German swalawa (modern German Schwalbe), Middle Dutch swalwe (modern Dutch zwaluw), Old Norse svala (modern Swedish svala, Danish svale) — the continental forms agreeing exactly in stem and gender. Beyond Germanic the picture is debated. The traditional view, followed by Pokorny, connects the bird-name to the PIE root *swel- “to drink, swallow, gulp” — the root of the Germanic verb *swelganą “to swallow” (English swallow the verb): the swallow eats on the wing, and its habit of snapping up insects, or the gaping throat pouch it shows when feeding young, would make it “the swallower”. But the shape of the bird-name (*swal-w-) is not the shape of the verb root (*swel-g-), and the OED and modern handbooks mark the connection uncertain — it may be a folk-etymological convergence. A rival suggestion links the name to a root meaning “to whirl, to move swiftly”, after the swallow's darting flight, but no secure etymon is attested. Celtic offers a parallel puzzle: Welsh gwennol, Irish fáinleog, Breton gwennel form a clear Celtic family of their own, but they are not the same word as the Germanic one — the branches coined independent names. In fact a striking linguistic fact: unlike “wolf” or “water”, the swallow's Indo-European names are NOT cognate across branches — Greek khelidṓn, Latin hirundo, Slavic lastovička, Baltic kregždė, and Armenian tsitsernak are all separate words. The Germanic branch named its own bird, and the exact pre-Germanic source of that name is a genuine gap in the record — stated here rather than guessed.


II. Old English to Modern English: form


Old English swealwe (feminine n-stem) yields Middle English swalowe/swalwe (13th century). Early Modern spelling settles as swallow in the 16th century. The pronunciation is regular: Middle English /ˈswaləu/, with the initial /w/ rounding the following short a — the familiar “wash/want/swan” change — to /ɒ/, and the final Middle English diphthong smoothed to modern /əʊ/: /ˈswɒləʊ/. The -ow spelling is shared, coincidentally, with the verb swallow “to gulp” (Old English swelgan, Proto-Germanic *swelganą), with which the bird name is not securely related — a homograph pair of a thousand years' standing, and the oldest pretext in English for the folk-etymological joke that the swallow is the bird that “swallows” insects. The bird's name has been remarkably stable for twelve centuries: same referent, same two-syllable shape, from swealwe to swallow.


III. The bird in culture: proverb, folklore, literature



  • Proverb: “one swallow makes not a summer” — from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (“one swallow does not make a spring”), diffused through Erasmus's Adagia and naturalised into English by the mid-16th century; it still teaches that a single instance proves nothing.

  • Folklore: swallows nesting under the eaves bring luck to the house and protection from storms; harming a swallow brings misfortune; their faithful return to the same nest was read as constancy and homecoming, and in Christian legend the swallow's affectionate nest-building earned it favour — one of the few birds exempt from medieval bad omens.

  • Nautical: from the 19th century, the swallow tattoo marks five thousand nautical miles sailed; the swallow returning to its nest made it the sailor's emblem of safe return — the “homeward-bound swallow”.

  • Literature: Aesop's fable of the swallow and the other birds (the bird that warns of the fowler); Ovid's Philomela story (Metamorphoses VI), retold by Chaucer, in which the swallow is the nightingale's sister, Procne, transformed for revenge; and above all Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888), where the little Swallow — in love with the Reed, staying behind while his friends fly to Egypt — becomes the story's hero and martyr, giving the bird-name its most famous literary afterlife.


IV. Science and the modern name


Linnaeus's Hirundo rustica (1758) fixes the scientific name; where other languages reached for classical names, English kept the folk name as the standard vernacular for the family Hirundinidae, subdividing it into barn swallow, house martin, sand martin, and cliff swallow. In ornithological usage, “swallow” strictly contrasts with swift (Apodidae) — the same aerial niche, different families — and the pair teaches birders a classic lesson in convergent evolution. In the 20th century the word built compounds: swallow-dive (the British swan dive), swallow-tail (the forked tail; the swallow-tailed coat), swallow-wort (the plant, from its seed pods shaped like the bird's tail). Modern conservation makes the word a monitor species: barn-swallow numbers track insect populations and farmland health, so “swallow” is at once one of English's oldest words and one of its newest symbols of environmental anxiety — the little bird that returns, and whose return we now count on.
