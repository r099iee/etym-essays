- 13th–14th Century (Middle English): Entered English from Scandinavian/Low German roots as blotte, specifically referring to an accidental drop or smudge of ink on parchment and an exposed piece in early board games.
- 15th–16th Century: Rapidly expanded in Renaissance theology and drama into a dominant metaphor for sin, moral corruption, and dishonor that "blots" the soul or family honor (frequent in Shakespeare).
- 17th–18th Century: Developed the verbal sense of absorbing wet ink with absorbent material (leading to the development of commercial "blotting paper"), alongside the idiomatic phrase "blot out" for total erasure.
- 19th Century: Emerged as a popular idiom for aesthetic and landscape criticism, giving rise to the famous formulation "a blot on the landscape" in Romantic and Victorian topographical writing.
- 20th–21st Century: Expanded into scientific nomenclature in molecular biology (Southern, Northern, Western blots) and clinical psychology (Rorschach inkblot test), while remaining a vibrant literary metaphor for disgraceful eyesores and scandalous stains.
