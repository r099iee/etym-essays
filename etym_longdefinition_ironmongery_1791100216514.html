Ironmongery is the British term for hardware made of iron and allied metals, and by extension the shop where it is sold and the trade of selling it. An ironmonger deals in nails, screws, hinges, locks, tools, grates, and kitchen metalware; his stock collectively is ironmongery and his premises an ironmonger's shop. The American equivalent is hardware or a hardware store. In the Dickens source it supplies a deliberately flat commercial frame for a metaphysical joke: a coffin-nail judged as ironmongery, as if death could be graded like nails.

The core sense is concrete and commercial. Traditional ironmongery included builder's ironmongery such as door furniture, hinges, and sash fasteners; furnishing ironmongery such as curtain rails and fire irons; and tool ironmongery such as hammers, saws, and files. Catalogues distinguished wrought, cast, and japanned finishes. To speak of a piece of ironmongery is to stress cold manufactured metal as opposed to wood, cloth, or provisions. Hence Dickens can call a coffin-nail ironmongery to underline its lifeless mineral nature.

  • Goods sense: articles of iron or metal hardware; e.g. architectural ironmongery for a restored church.
  • Shop sense: an ironmonger's premises; e.g. he apprenticed at the village ironmongery.
  • Trade sense: the business of dealing in hardware; e.g. she was brought up in the ironmongery trade.
  • Builder's jargon: door knobs, locks, hinges, and closers specified by architects as ironmongery schedules.
  • Figurative coldness: anything clanking, hard, and unfeeling likened to ironmongery, as Dickens does for comic effect.
  • Heritage flavour: surviving shop signs and museum displays where ironmongery connotes Victoriana and craft.

Usage is markedly British and increasingly archaic. Younger speakers say hardware shop or DIY store; ironmongery survives on old fascias, in period novels, and in the specialist phrase architectural ironmongery still used by builders. It is countable in the goods sense (a piece of ironmongery) and uncountable as stock or trade. It collocates with dead, cold, heavy, and clanking, and with verbs like stock, deal in, and hammer out.

Polysemy is small but the semantic bridge matters: from metal to merchant to merchandise to shop. The suffix -ery, as in grocery, bakery, and haberdashery, can name both place and stock, so ironmongery slides naturally between I bought it at the ironmongery and the shelf was full of ironmongery. The American hardware never developed this shop sense in one word, which is why ironmongery feels exotic to Americans and nostalgic to Britons. Dickens exploits exactly that ordinariness: by filing death under ironmongery he makes Marley's deadness both commercial and absolute.

Architectural catalogues still divide ironmongery into knobs, levers, hinges, closers, bolts, and letterboxes, each graded by fire rating and corrosion resistance, a precision that keeps the old word alive among specifiers even as shoppers ask for hardware. Collectors of Victoriana prize stamped ironmongery for its japanning, patent mechanisms, and foundry marks, and restoration joiners match tramp-art hinges to listed doors. The comic force in Dickens depends on this mundane abundance: against shelves of identical cold stock, one nail can be crowned the deadest, a superlative of lifelessness drawn from commerce rather than metaphysics.
