squirm — Modern English verb, first recorded in the 1640s, 'to twist and wriggle the body.' Origin unknown: there are no known Germanic cognates outside English dialect, no Latin or Greek source, and no reconstructible Proto-Indo-European ancestor; the word is probably echoic or sound-symbolic, its s- followed by the kw- cluster and the curling -erm syllable miming a twisting, writhing motion (cf. wriggle, worm; the dialect form squirn hints at a longer unrecorded spoken history). → squirm — 'to move like a worm or eel; to escape by twisting,' senses established from the 17th–18th c. in physical description of eels, snakes, worms, and people in confinement. → squirmed — past tense and past participle, '-ed' pronounced /d/, the form in 'He squirmed through the opening.' NOTE ON DEBATE: the OED records the origin as obscure; the echoic explanation is the accepted working hypothesis, and any earlier reconstruction would be pure invention. The word has no larger family tree — it is a small, self-contained English group (squirm, squirmy, squirmer, squirm), with sound-symbolic neighbours (wriggle, squirt, swirl, worm) that are parallels, not cognates.
