- [16th century: the physical pang] - The word enters English naming a sudden, sharp, piercing pain, above all the intermittent spasms of childbirth — "the pangs of labour" — brief, point-like, and intensely localized.
- [17th century: the moral pang] - The sharp spasm transfers from the body to the mind: "pangs of conscience" appear as the sudden, involuntary stab of guilt or remorse that strikes and passes like a physical twinge.
- [18th-19th century: full emotional reach] - Fixed collocations multiply — pangs of hunger, pangs of love, pangs of loss — so the word crosses freely between the physical and the moral, always retaining its brevity and point-like intensity.
- [Contemporary sense] - The word now carries a slightly literary, old-fashioned flavour, used of brief sharp emotions (a pang of regret, a pang of nostalgia) and still set against the dull, continuous ache it is defined by.
