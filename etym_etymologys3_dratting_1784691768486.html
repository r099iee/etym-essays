The biography of dratting is not primarily a story of sound change but of social history — of the long English effort to swear without actually swearing. It belongs to a phenomenon philologists call the minced oath: the deliberate deformation of a profane or blasphemous formula so that its force survives while its offence is muffled. English has been exceptionally rich in such euphemisms because, from the Renaissance onward, the culture combined a deep taboo on taking the name of God in vain with a stubborn appetite for strong language. The result was an elaborate machinery of softened curses: zounds for 'God's wounds', 'sblood for 'God's blood', egad and i'gad for 'by God', gadzooks for 'God's hooks', gosh for 'God', golly for 'God', blimey for 'God blind me', crumbs, crikey, and cor blimey, darn and dash and drat for 'damn', and, in America, doggone, goldarn, and a whole range of euphemisms for hell and damnation in the nineteenth century. The particular parent of drat is the imprecation 'God rot (him, it, or them)!' — a genuine curse, current in the seventeenth century, that wished upon its object the decay and putrefaction of the grave. It drew on the ancient English verb rot, which descends from Old English rotian 'to decay, putrefy', recorded from the tenth century and built on a Proto-Germanic root *rutēn / *rutjan with cognates in Old Saxon roton, Old Frisian rotia, Middle Dutch roten, and Old Norse rotna — a word family so old and so widespread that its presence in every early Germanic language suggests a root of real antiquity, though its deeper Indo-European connections remain uncertain and cannot be securely reconstructed. The curse 'God rot you' is thus literally a wish that the victim decompose; it belongs to the large class of curses that condemn an object to waste, decay, or dissolution, like 'rot in hell' or the milder 'blast it'. The reduction proceeded by the standard mechanics of minced oaths. The divine name, being the offensive element, was first masked — appearing in the reduced phrase as the neutral 'd, so that 'God rot' became ''d rot', a shape in which the taboo syllable has vanished entirely (some handbooks write the intermediate form od rot, with od standing in for God as in od's bodkins). From ''d rot' the phrase contracted to the monosyllable drat, first recorded about 1815, at the turn of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, in the homely colloquial dialogue of the early nineteenth century. The new word behaved exactly like its parent: it could stand alone as an exclamation ('Drat it!', 'Drat the man!'), or act as a transitive or intransitive verb, 'to drat someone or something', from which the participle dratting descends; and it quickly generated its own adjective, dratted, 'confounded, annoying', recorded from the nineteenth century in phrases like 'that dratted cat' or 'the dratted lock'. The semantic content of the curse deserves attention. Unlike oaths that condemn to hell, to fire, or to the devil, drat condemns its object to rot — to decay, to spoil, to become worthless. This is a rustic, almost agricultural curse, the language of a farmyard rather than a law court, and it carries none of the theological terror of a damnation. By the time Tolkien wrote The Hobbit (1937), the word had been worn smooth by a century of comic and children's usage until almost nothing remained of it except a faint, comical irritation. That is precisely the register the word has kept ever since: a curse with the teeth drawn, anger without blasphemy, the strongest language a period English kitchen or a children's book allows itself. Literary attestations trace this career nicely. Drat appears throughout Victorian comic fiction and the domestic novel; it is a stock property of Dickensian side-characters, of Punch-style humour, of the school story, and of pre-war children's books. Dratted survives rather better than the verb — 'that dratted thing' is the form most modern speakers will have met. The gerundial dratting is the rarest and most literary of the derivatives, occurring almost exclusively in humorous narrative, which makes its appearance in The Hobbit all the more telling: Tolkien places it in the sentence with grumbling precisely to establish the dwarves' comic, domestic scale of protest. As the party blunders through the pitch-dark wood, 'do what they could they made a deal of rustling and crackling and creaking (and a good deal of grumbling and dratting)' — grumbling gives the low, continuous complaint, dratting the sharp, intermittent expletive, each unseen root and puddle calling forth a small explosion of mild profanity. The word humanises the dwarves; it turns thirteen would-be heroes into thirteen flustered travellers cursing quietly at the furniture of the Wild. The wider lesson of drat is historical and social rather than phonological: it shows that a society's swearing is a record of its taboos. Because the taboo in early modern England fell on the divine name rather than on bodily functions or sex, the great corpus of English euphemism was built to defuse God, Christ, and the Virgin — and drat, along with zounds, egad, gosh, and darn, is a fossil of that particular moral economy. In the twentieth century the taboo shifted and weakened, and with it the urgency of the minced oath declined; drat now sounds unmistakably dated, a deliberate period voice, and its continued life in archaising and children's writing is the final stage of its career: the once-blasphemous curse has become an affectionate antique. Milestones in brief: - 10th century: Old English rotian 'to decay, putrefy'. - 17th century: the imprecation 'God rot (him/it/them)!' current. - 17th–18th century: euphemistic reduction ''d rot' / 'od rot'. - c. 1815: drat (interjection and verb) first recorded. - 19th century: dratted (adjective) 'confounded'. - 1937: dratting in Tolkien's The Hobbit. - Today: archaic, comic, and affectionate; the curse whose teeth were drawn two centuries ago.
