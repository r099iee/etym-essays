Engulf means to swallow up, overwhelm, or completely surround something so that it disappears inside a larger mass, force, or feeling — as a wave engulfs a rock, darkness engulfs a valley, or fear engulfs a mind. The word always implies a devouring container: a gulf that opens, receives, and closes over its victim, leaving nothing visible above the surface.

In the source sentence, fear itself is the devourer: Fear seemed to stretch out a vast hand, like a dark cloud rising in the East and looming up to engulf him. Here the verb carries its full physical menace carried into psychology — dread is imagined as a storm-cloud with a hand, swelling on the horizon until it folds over the sufferer entirely. The victim does not merely feel afraid; he is drowned in fear, hidden inside it, cut off from light and rescue exactly as a ship is hidden inside fog.

Core meaning in detail: At its centre engulf combines two pictures: swallowing (taking inward, as a throat takes food) and surrounding (closing round on every side, as water closes over a stone). Prototypical agents are fluids and formless masses — seas, rivers, floods, tides, lava, mudslides, avalanches, fog, smoke, flames, darkness — and prototypical patients are smaller solid things — boats, villages, coastlines, swimmers, travellers. The result state matters as much as the act: after engulfing, the patient is invisible, unreachable, absorbed into the agent. Grammar underlines this completeness: engulf is transitive and totalising, so writers intensify it with adverbs of totality such as completely, entirely, wholly, and almost swallowed.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To swallow up physically, to drown or bury under a mass (primary, from the 1550s): the tide engulfed the causeway; lava engulfed the village; the avalanche engulfed the climbers. The bridge is the noun gulf — a deep chasm or bay of water — plus the prefix en- meaning put into: literally to plunge something into a gulf so the waters close above it.
  • To overwhelm emotionally or mentally (figurative, fully established by the 18th century): engulfed by grief, panic, despair, guilt, homesickness. The bridge is metaphorical drowning: emotions are imagined as seas, and the sufferer as one who cannot swim. Psychology writing and fiction favour this sense above all others, because it makes an inner state visible as weather.
  • To surround and enclose, to envelop on every side (extended physical sense): fog engulfed the airfield; flames engulfed the stairwell; the crowd engulfed the visitors. The bridge weakens the downward-swallowing picture into pure surrounding: the patient may still be standing, but every line of sight and escape is sealed. Fire reporting uses this sense constantly.
  • To absorb into a larger economic or institutional whole (modern business and finance): the small firm was engulfed by the conglomerate; savings engulfed by debt; the project engulfed millions of pounds. The bridge runs from devouring water to devouring money: capital is imagined as a sea in which smaller craft disappear, and the verb supplies a more dramatic synonym for absorb or consume.
  • To plunge into a state or activity, to immerse deeply (literary and journalistic): engulfed in controversy; a region engulfed in civil war; engulfed in preparations. The bridge generalises the surrounding sense from physical masses to conditions and events: war, scandal, and festivity become atmospheres dense enough to drown in.
  • To flow over and cover, especially of light, sound, or vegetation (poetic and nature writing): ivy engulfed the ruin; applause engulfed the hall; golden light engulfed the valley. The bridge softens menace into abundance: here engulfing delights rather than destroys, though the completeness remains — the ruin is all ivy, the hall is all sound.

Register and connotation: Engulf is dramatic and literary without being archaic — at home in novels, journalism, disaster reporting, and sermons. It is almost always grave: things engulfed are lost, threatened, or utterly transformed, so the word carries pity or awe and rarely comedy. Its favourite subjects are the four classical perils — water, fire, darkness, and passion — which keeps its flavour elemental even in boardrooms. Writers choose engulf over plainer cover or surround precisely when they want the reader to feel the closing-over, the moment the last trace disappears beneath the surface.
