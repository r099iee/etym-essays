Norman-French (more precisely Anglo-Norman when referring to England) is the variety of Old French spoken by the Normans and, after 1066, by the French-speaking ruling class of England. It is both a regional dialect of the langue d’oïl family and a historical language of state: for roughly three centuries it was the vehicle of English law, royal administration, aristocratic literature and school instruction, and it left an imprint on English vocabulary, spelling and legal style that is still visible on every page.

Three distinct referents

The term is used in three overlapping ways, and clarity requires keeping them apart:

  • Continental Norman — the northern French dialect of the duchy of Normandy, a langue d’oïl with its own phonology and vocabulary, spoken from the tenth century and still surviving in reduced form in the Cotentin and the Pays de Caux.
  • Anglo-Norman — the variety established in England after 1066, used by the court, the baronage, the law courts, the schools and the church, and cultivated as a literary language from the twelfth to the fourteenth century. This is what English historical writing usually means by “Norman-French.”
  • Insular Norman — the modern dialects of the Channel Islands (Jèrriais, Guernésiais, Sercquiais) and of the Norman mainland, which descend from the medieval dialect but are not the same as the medieval administrative language.

Linguistic character

Norman belongs to the northern group of langue d’oïl dialects and shares features with Picard and Walloon. Its most characteristic developments are:

  • Retention of /k/ before /a/ — Latin cantare gives Norman canter, where central French has chanter; Latin cattus gives cat against French chat. The same conservatism explains the English doublets catch beside chase and cattle beside chattel.
  • /w/ where central French has /g(w)/ — Norman warde, warant, wile against central garde, garant, guile. English preserves both branches as ward/guard, warrant/guarantee and wile/guile.
  • -our for -eur — honour, colour, favour, labour, where modern Parisian spelling has honneur, couleur, faveur, travail.
  • qu- for /k/ — Anglo-Norman scribes wrote qu for the sound /k/, and their habit replaced the Old English cw spellings of queen, quick, quench.
  • Simplified final consonants and a strong stress accent — features that contributed to the distinctive rhythm of spoken Anglo-Norman and, through it, to the stress patterns of borrowed English words.

The language of England, 1066–c. 1400

After the Conquest, England became trilingual. Latin remained the language of the church, scholarship and formal record; Norman-French became the language of the court, the aristocracy, the law and fashionable literature; English remained the speech of the great majority. For two centuries a substantial part of the population was bilingual or trilingual, and French was the marker of status.

  • Law and administration — plea rolls, charters, statutes, Year Books and parliamentary records were kept in French or Latin; the Year Books, our main reports of medieval case law, are in Anglo-Norman.
  • Literature — Wace’s Roman de Brut and Roman de Rou, Gaimar’s Estoire des Engleis, the Lais of Marie de France and a large body of saints’ lives, romances and chronicles were written in Anglo-Norman for an Anglo-Norman audience.
  • School and fashion — French was taught as the language of polite society, and the French of England was increasingly distinguished from the French of Paris.
  • Bilingual interference — English borrowed thousands of words, but also calqued idioms and adopted French word-formation patterns such as -ment, -ance, -able and -ity.

Decline and afterlife

The loss of Normandy in 1204 weakened the continental tie, and the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries saw a gradual retreat of French. The Provisions of Oxford (1258) were issued in Latin, French and English; the Statute of Pleading (1362) complained that French was “much unknown” in the courts and ordered pleas to be heard in English; the Hundred Years’ War made French an enemy tongue. By the early fifteenth century English had recovered its position as the language of administration and literature, and Anglo-Norman survived chiefly as Law French, a professional jargon that persisted in the inns of court until the eighteenth century.

Law French left a permanent mark: tort, trespass, estoppel, laches, voir dire, fee simple, mortgage, demurrer, oyer and terminer, cy-près. It was formally abolished as the language of legal proceedings in 1731.

Impact on English

Norman-French is one of the two great external sources of English vocabulary, the other being Latin. Its contribution includes:

  • Government and law — parliament, council, justice, judgement, crime, felony, assault, battery, prison, estate, property, rent, revenue.
  • War and chivalry — army, battle, siege, castle, tower, dungeon, knight, squire, page, honour, courage.
  • Church and learning — clergy, sermon, prayer, school, study, grammar, logic.
  • Domestic life — dinner, supper, chair, table, blanket, curtain, mirror.
  • Fashion and art — dress, gown, veil, colour, painting, image, beauty.

The doublets are especially revealing: catch/chase, cattle/chattel, castle/château, ward/guard, warrant/guarantee, wile/guile, William/Guillaume. In each pair the Norman form entered first or through the conquerors, and the central French form entered later through Parisian prestige, so that English preserves two snapshots of the same word at different stages and from different regions.

Modern senses and survivals

  • The historical language — the standard academic sense, usually called Anglo-Norman in linguistics.
  • The modern Norman dialects — Jèrriais in Jersey, Guernésiais in Guernsey, Sercquiais in Sark, and mainland varieties such as Cotentinais and Cauchois; all are endangered and the subject of revival efforts.
  • “Norman French” as a cultural label — used loosely for the French-influenced culture of the Anglo-Norman aristocracy, and in tourism and heritage writing for the medieval period.
  • Legal fossil — Law French survives in fixed phrases and in the ceremonial vocabulary of the Inns of Court.

Semantic bridges

The term is a compound of two names, and each name carries a history. Norman identifies the Scandinavian settlers of the Seine; French identifies the Romance speech they adopted. Together they describe a language that was French in structure but Norse in ruling class, and then English in setting. The paradox is the point: Norman-French is the French of people who were descended from Vikings, spoken in a country whose majority spoke English, and remembered chiefly for the words it gave to English.
