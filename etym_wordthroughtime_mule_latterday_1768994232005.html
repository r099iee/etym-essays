mule
- Old English (8th c.) — a plain draft hybrid, the sterile cross of horse and donkey: valued for strength, endurance, and patience, with none of the horse's romance and none of the ass's contempt.
- 16th c. — "mulish" (1570s) transfers the beast's character to humans: obstinacy fused with laboring power, the stubbornness of a worker who cannot be moved, not the foolishness of a dupe.
- 19th-century American frontier — the mule becomes the freight machinery of the West: mule trains, pack mules, mule skinners, the engine of the gold rush and the Texas freighting roads; a plain, serviceable, unromantic civic hero.
- Contemporary — proverbial in "stubborn as a mule" and "mule-headed," extended to hybrid carriers (the smuggling "mule"); the old draft-animal sense recedes while its symbolism of patient hybrid labor endures.

latterday
- Scriptural era — "latter days" names the closing period of the world in apocalyptic and religious speech, carried into the Latter-day Saints (1830) with full eschatological gravity.
- 19th-century journalism — "a latter-day X" becomes an ironic formula: a present figure standing at the close of a longer story, a diminished or comic reincarnation of something greater.
- Contemporary — "latterday" is a small temporal verdict: recent yet terminal, new and already exhausted, the coinage of mock-grandeur and footnotes.
