EARNEST is a native Germanic word, and its biography runs from the battlefield to the pulpit to the law court. At the root core lies the PIE base *h₃er-, 'to set in motion, rouse, stir up', a root of energy and arousal. The Germanic branch developed from it an adjective *arniz, 'efficient, capable, sure, vigorous' — a word for someone who is roused into action and therefore dependable — and from *arniz was formed the abstract *ernustuz, 'seriousness, vigor, zeal', whose oldest Germanic senses were anything but polite: earnestness meant the strength of the struggle, the heat of the fight, the grim energy of battle. Old English eornost still carries exactly this weight: 'zeal, seriousness, and battle'.

From this root the word descended in an unbroken native line. Old English eornost and eornust produced Middle English ernest, the noun of seriousness; and the Old English adjective eornoste, built directly on the noun, gave the Middle English adjective ernest. Both noun and adjective flowed into Early Modern English as the earnest we know — the noun in the fixed phrase in earnest, 'in reality, seriously, not in jest', and the adjective meaning 'sincere, serious, zealous'. Shakespeare already uses the contrast of jest and earnest in Richard III, where feigned prayer is answered 'in earnest' — the two poles of the word's moral universe.

  • Sound shifts: the inherited *e- of PGmc *ernustuz remained a stable front vowel in English; Old English eornost shows the diphthongized on-glide, smoothed to a long mid vowel by Middle English, while the final *-uz was lost, leaving the modern two-syllable ernest.

The word's semantic drift is from physical struggle to moral seriousness. In Old English eornost could still name a battle; by Middle English the martial sense faded and the word settled on zeal, gravity, and sincerity. In the nineteenth century earnest became a badge of Victorian character — earnestness as a doctrine of moral sincerity — and Oscar Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest (1895) made the name Ernest and the virtue of being earnest collapse into one comic pun. The religious register of earnest prayer and earnest faith continues the old sense of fervent struggle.

  • Semantic phases: (1) PIE *h₃er- 'rouse, set in motion'; (2) PGmc *arniz 'capable, vigorous'; (3) PGmc *ernustuz 'vigor, zeal, battle'; (4) OE eornost 'earnestness; battle'; (5) ME ernest 'seriousness' and erneste 'serious'; (6) Modern earnest — sincerity of purpose, opposed to jest and pretence.

A warning is worth keeping distinct: the legal earnest money is a different word — a medieval loan through French from Latin arrha, ultimately from a Semitic root meaning 'pledge' — whose spelling collided with the native word. Today's earnest, the virtue of wholehearted seriousness, is purely Germanic, and its sound still carries the old struggle from which it was born.
