trudge — The biography of trudge is the biography of a sound.

The word has no PIE root, no Germanic ancestor, no Latin uncle; it surfaces in English in the sixteenth century as an expressive creation, and the OED marks its origin unknown, probably imitative. Everything about it performs its meaning: the blunt initial tr- is the phonestheme of effortful contact (compare tread, tramp, trip, trample), and the heavy -udge rime (nudge, drudge, grudge) seals each syllable with a dull impact. A trudge is the walk in which every footstep is a small labor, and the word was built, by the sound system of the language itself, to be that labor's name. It is the onomatopoeia of the weary — a rare case of etymology giving way to pure acoustics.

  • Expressive birth (c. 1540s, Scotland and the north): an imitative coinage for laborious walking; no reconstructed ancestor exists.
  • Sound symbolism: the tr- onset and -udge rime are phonesthemic — the sound performs the meaning of feet planted in mud.
  • The noun (17th c.): "a long trudge" — the weary walk itself, measured in effort rather than miles.
  • The fixed phrase "trudge on": walking that must continue; endurance, not energy.
  • Register: plain, humble, informal — the verb of soldiers, servants, laborers, and pilgrims.
  • The literary transfer: steamboats that "hoot and trudge past" — the weary-walk word lent to machines.

Early attestations are northern and Scottish, and the word belongs from the first to the walking poor — soldiers, servants, laborers, pilgrims, all those for whom travel is measured in effort rather than miles. It spread through plain narrative prose and dialect, acquiring the noun (a long trudge), the agent noun (trudger), and the fixed phrase trudge on, in which the adverb carries the real meaning: trudging is walking that must continue. There is no energy in a trudge, only endurance; the word never suggests enjoyment and almost never speed. Its register stayed humble, and that humility is its strength — it is the verb of every anonymous forced march in the language. In the card's sentence, trudge performs its most daring transfer yet: the steamboats hoot and trudge past. The expressive word of the laboring walker is lent to the machine, and the transfer is exactly what the image needs — the great hulls do not glide or cut, they labor, heavy and laden, their passage through the black water read as a weary plod. Because trudge has no lineage, it arrives in the poem with nothing to hide: it means what it sounds like, and what it sounds like is feet going on in the dark.



alight — The shining alight belongs to the greatest light-root of the Indo-European family.

The PIE root *leuk- meant "to shine, to be bright," and it spread into nearly every branch of the family: Sanskrit rócate "he shines," Greek leukós "white" (whence leukemia, the white blood), Latin lux and lumen (whence lucid, luminous, illuminate, and luna, the moon as "the shining one"), and the Germanic *leuht-, which gave Old English lēoht — the word light itself. Alight is built on that root with a simple prefix: a- "on, at" plus the verb to light, "to kindle," so that a thing alight is literally a thing kindled, lit up, burning — the adjective of ignition, the state of having been set aglow.

  • PIE *leuk- "to shine": one of the family's great roots — Vedic ruc-, Greek leukós, Latin lux/lumen, Germanic *leuht-.
  • Old English lēoht "light": the Germanic reflex; the verb līhtan "to kindle" is the base of the adjective.
  • a- + light → alight: "on" + "to kindle" — literally "set burning, lit up"; the adjective settled in the 16th–17th c.
  • The homonym (dismount): to alight "to descend" is from *legwh- "not heavy," a separate root — a famous false-friend pair.
  • Register: predicative, literary, ceremonial — the poet's alight, not the kitchen's lit.
  • The card: steamboats "all alight like cities" — the radiance of civilization on black water.

English complicates the story with a celebrated pair of homonyms. There are two lights in the language: light "illumination," from *leuk-, and light "not heavy," from a different root *legwh-. Each built its own alight. From *leuk- came alight "ablaze, shining," the word of this card; from *legwh- came the verb to alight "to dismount, descend" — originally to relieve a horse of its load, to make it light — and the adjective alight "unladen." The two words coincide in spelling and pronunciation while descending from unrelated roots, a famous trap for the etymologist and a gift to the poet, who can set a man alight (dismounting) beside a city alight (burning) without the reader knowing which is which.

The shining alight lived on the margins of the language — predicative, literary, never the plain "lit" of the kitchen — until the sentence of this card gave it back its grandeur: the steamboats pass "all alight like cities." Because the root *leuk- had already furnished the vocabulary of civilization (illuminate, Lucifer, luna, luminous), the small adjective alight carries in this line the whole history of artificial day — fire, lamp, and lighthouse — raised to the scale of drifting cities on black water. The word that began as "to shine" ends as the radiance of a floating town, and the shadowed river is held, for a moment, in its glow.



adrift — The story of adrift is the story of how a people became the passive of their own verb.

The PIE root *dhreibh- meant "to drive," and in Germanic it produced the strong verb *drībanan, "to drive, push, urge onward." Old English drīfan gave English drive, a word whose whole career is active: the shepherd drives the flock, the ox-driver drives the plough, the wind drives the clouds, and the modern verb still means "to operate, to impel, to force onward." From drive came drove, the herd being driven, and drover, the man who drives it — the active, controlling end of the family, the language of human force applied to the world.

  • PIE *dhreibh- "to drive": the active root; a proposed Greek reflex (tríbō "to rub") is debated and flagged.
  • Proto-Germanic *drībanan → Old English drīfan: "to drive, hunt, urge" — the active shepherd's verb; English drive.
  • Proto-Germanic *driftiz "a driving": the derived noun of what is driven; Old Norse drif "a snowdrift."
  • Middle English drift: "a driving, a snow-drift" → English drift "a tendency, that which is carried along."
  • a- + drift → adrift (16th–17th c.): "floating at random, without anchor or steering" — the passive of the family.
  • Psychological extension (19th–20th c.): a person adrift in the city — the nautical helplessness becomes the metaphor for a life unmoored.

But the same root also built its passive. The derived noun *driftiz meant "a driving, that which is driven," and in Old Norse it specialized into drif, "a snowdrift" — snow that has been driven into a bank by the wind. Middle English took drift to mean both "a driving" and "the thing driven," and the meaning slid from the act to its result: a drift is what current and wind push along, a tendency that moves without direction. The modern senses follow that slide — the drift of an argument, driftwood, a drifter, the snowdrift — so that the active shepherd of drive becomes, in drift, a person or thing simply carried. To be adrift, a- "in" plus drift, is to live inside that result: floating loose, no anchor, no rudder, driven rather than driving.

The word's career is the completion of the card's three-word journey. First the boats trudge — heavy, driven, still effortful; then they are alight — kindled, radiant, still burning; and in the last word they are adrift — surrendered to the water, floating cities with no hold on the land. The evolution of adrift mirrors the loss of agency it describes: from the active drive of the shepherd to the passive drift of the boat, from force to force-lessness, and finally, in the sentence, to the whole human world made maritime and unmoored on the black waters of the night.
