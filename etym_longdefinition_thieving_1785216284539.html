Thieving is the present participle and gerund of the verb thieve (to steal, to take dishonestly), and also a fully adjectival word meaning "inclined to steal by habit". In The Hobbit the word opens a window onto Gollum's past: "But suddenly Gollum remembered thieving from nests long ago" — a memory of sly, small-scale predation, robbing birds' eggs from riverside nests, recalled with nostalgic relish rather than remorse. The nuance is essential: thieving here is not armed robbery or larceny but stealthy, habitual, furtive pilfering — the creeping vice of a creature of the dark. The gerundial form frames the act as an ingrained behaviour, something Gollum does by nature, and the immediate echo — "Eggses!" — shows how the memory of thieving kindles his appetite for Bilbo.

Core meanings. The word moves across three grammatical faces that shade into each other:

  • Gerund (the activity itself). "Thieving is wrong" — the act of stealing named as a practice or occupation. This is the abstract-noun use, and it often carries a moral judgement: to call an activity "thieving" is to condemn it plainly.
  • Present participle (the action in progress). "He was caught thieving" — the act of stealing as it happens, forming continuous tenses ("was thieving", "is thieving").
  • Adjective (a disposition). "A thieving magpie", "thieving hands", "a thieving nature" — inclined to steal by habit or character. This adjectival use is where the word does its strongest moral work: it describes not an act but a type of person or creature, and it is nearly always condemnatory, though it can also be ruefully affectionate (a thieving cat, a thieving child).

Exhaustive polysemy and register.

  • Plain, everyday register. Thieving is the blunt, homely, everyday English word for the vice, morally condemning yet familiar — the language of the common thief and the scolding parent, not of the courtroom.
  • Legal and technical terms in the same territory. English law distinguishes larceny (technical theft), robbery (open, confrontational seizure with force or threat), burglary (breaking into buildings to steal), embezzlement (misusing entrusted funds), shoplifting (stealing from shops), theft (the general statutory crime), and misappropriation (unlawful diversion of property or funds). Thieving is none of these: it is the non-technical umbrella, and it emphasizes the sneaking, habitual character of the act rather than its legal category.
  • Petty and sly stealing (the closest territory). Thieving overlaps with pilfering and filching (petty, sneaky stealing) but is broader and blunter; with pinching, nicking, swiping (informal slang for stealing small things); and with poaching (illegal taking of game) only in its sneaking quality. It can also cover the casual, opportunistic taking that slang calls lifting.
  • Metaphoric and figurative shifts (modern). The word extends metaphorically to any dishonest appropriation: "thieving" an idea is plagiarism; "time-thieving" describes distractions that steal one's hours; a performer "steals the show". The related "thief" yields "thieves' market", "thief of time", and the idiom "like a thief in the night" — and the participle can be pressed into metaphorical descriptions of anything that takes without consent.
  • Intensified and literary uses. In older and literary English, thieving appears in moral and religious rhetoric as the plain name of one of the capital vices (theft), and in proverbs ("opportunity makes a thief"). Its homely bluntness is exactly what Tolkien exploits: Gollum's "thieving from nests" is a crime that sounds like an animal's natural habit, neither romanticized (unlike a pirate's plunder) nor technical (unlike embezzlement) — the sneaking, egg-stealing theft of a wild creature.

Historical semantic bridge. The word descends from Old English þēof "thief" and its weak verb þēofian "to thieve"; the -ing form has named the activity since late medieval times and has developed the adjectival "disposed to steal" sense alongside. Across its history thieving has always kept the same core: the stealthy, wrongful taking of what belongs to another. What changes is register — it never becomes a courtroom word, and its habitual, sneaking connotation is constant — which is why, today as in Gollum's memory, thieving is what a sly creature does, by nature and with relish.
