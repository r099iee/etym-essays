MUTTERS — the low, closed-mouth sound of speech that refuses to speak At its plainest, MUTTERS is the third-person singular present tense of the verb mutter: to speak in a low, indistinct voice, mumbling words under the breath with nearly closed lips, often from irritation, unease, or solitary thought. The word names a particular kind of speech — or near-speech — that is quiet, muffled, and half-suppressed, produced with the mouth almost shut so that the words are barely audible and often not meant to be clearly heard. It is speech turned inward, whether from grumpiness, secrecy, or simply the habit of talking to oneself. SENSE 1 — to speak low and indistinctly (the core sense). To mutter is to utter words in a low tone with compressed lips: the old man mutters to himself while walking, a student mutters under her breath, an official mutters a reply. The mutterer is present but not fully social — the words are addressed to no one in particular, or to the speaker alone. SENSE 2 — to complain quietly: to grumble. From the same root motion, to mutter about something is to voice discontent in a low, half-suppressed way — the workforce mutters about the cuts, the crowd mutters its disapproval. The sense carries displeasure but not open protest; muttering is the sound of complaint that stops short of confrontation. SENSE 3 — the noun: a mutter; mutterings. The noun names the sound itself — a low murmur — and the plural mutterings (or mutters, as a mass) names the accumulating, half-heard complaints of a group: the mutterings of discontent in the party, the first mutters of rebellion. In political commentary, "mutterings" and "murmurs" are the standard words for widespread but not yet voiced discontent. SENSE 4 — figurative: of the wind, thunder, trees. To mutter is also used of inanimate low sounds: the wind mutters through the cracks, distant thunder mutters on the horizon. The sound is continuous, low, and speech-like but wordless — exactly the sense Tolkien needs in the riddle of the wind, where the wind, having no mouth, nonetheless mutters. SENSE 5 — grammar and form. As the third-person singular present of a regular verb, mutters takes the -s of the simple present: he mutters, she mutters. The form is the one that makes the word a statement of habit or ongoing state — the wind mutters, the old man mutters — rather than a single act. POLYSEMY SUMMARY. Mutters spans a narrow but rich band of low, closed-mouth sound: deliberate low speech, suppressed complaint, the noun naming the sound itself, the plural naming a group's hidden discontent, and the figurative use of wind and thunder. Every sense keeps the root posture of compressed lips and barely audible words — speech that is there but not open, meant to be sensed rather than clearly understood. IN THE HOBBIT. In the riddle of the wind — "Voiceless it cries, Wingless flutters, Toothless bites, Mouthless mutters" — the answer is the wind, and mutters is the riddle's final, quietest verb: the wind has no mouth, yet it produces a muffled, murmuring, half-audible sound as it stirs through cracks and hollows. Tolkien's personification is precise: each verb of the riddle matches an organ the wind lacks (voice, wings, teeth, mouth), and mutters captures the wind's characteristic low, continuous, inarticulate sound — not words, but speech-like noise, as of someone mumbling to themselves in the dark. The tone is eerie and intimate, and subtly comic: a wind that mutters is being treated as a grumbling old creature — a touch of anthropomorphism that suits Gollum's own muttering manner. NEAR-SYNONYM BOUNDARIES (the low-voice field):
  • Murmur: softer, gentler, more continuous, often pleasant or flowing (a murmuring brook) — muttering is more closed-mouthed, more private, often irritated.
  • Mumble: stresses inarticulacy, words swallowed so they cannot be understood — muttering is more intentional, half-private speech.
  • Grumble: adds displeasure and open complaint, a low complaint voiced — muttering is quieter and more self-contained.
  • Whisper: quiet but deliberate, articulating distinct words breathily — muttering is more closed-mouthed, muffled, barely intelligible.
  • Drone: a monotonous, sustained, unvarying hum — muttering has a broken, half-articulate rhythm.
  • Splutter: choked, spitting, broken speech — muttering is continuous and low, not spasmodic.
  • Stammer / stutter: repetition and blockage of sounds in otherwise audible speech — muttering is low and muffled, not blocked.
