wayes (noun, plural, archaic spelling) — the early modern English spelling of ways, plural of way: physical paths or roads; by extension, courses of action, methods, manners, customs, and — in the Miltonic context that preserves the word on this card — the providential methods and dealings of God. In Paradise Lost (1667) the poet promises to "assert th' Eternal Providence, / And justifie the wayes of God to men," where wayes means the designs, methods, and dealings of divine providence. Core senses. (1) A physical path, road, or route ("the way to the city," "a path that was the only way across the marsh"); (2) a course of travel or journey, and hence direction ("this way," "losing one's way"); (3) a manner, method, or course of action ("the way to solve this problem," "in a peculiar way"); (4) a custom, habit, or mode of behaviour ("his strange ways," "the ways of the local community"); (5) means or resources ("ways and means"); (6) in the Miltonic and religious register, the ways of God — the providential methods by which God orders the world. Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Figurative and providential "ways": from the Psalms ("thy way, O God") through the Gospels and the King James Bible to Milton, "the ways of God" are the hidden designs and just dealings of providence; this is the elevated, theological pole of the word, and the exact sense on the card.
  • Manner and method: "a way of doing things," "the American way," "in a way," "no way," "that's the way it goes" — the sense of manner or means is now the dominant modern meaning.
  • Custom and behaviour: "the ways of the world," "mend your ways," "his strange ways" — ways as settled patterns of conduct, at the boundary between personal habit and social custom.
  • Distance sense: "a little way(s)," "a ways off," "the station is a good way down the road" — in some dialects ways (singular in construction) means distance.
  • Nautical and technical "ways": in shipbuilding, "the ways" are the sloping timbers down which a ship is launched; the plural form survives in this technical sense.
  • Fixed phrases and compounds: "by the way," "in the way," "out of the way," "under way," "the other way round," "way of life," "the way of the world"; and the lexicalized compounds always (all + ways), away, wayward, wayfarer, way station, waybill.
  • Archaic plural spelling -es: early modern English often wrote the plural -s as -es or -ies, so Milton's text has wayes, eyes, daies; the -es spelling here is a period orthography, not a different word, and the modern standard is ways.
Historical semantic bridge. The bridge runs from concrete path to abstract method. In Old English, weġ already meant both the physical road and the "course of life" or "manner of doing things"; the figurative extension from road to method is therefore as old as the English word itself. The plural ways generalized from "many routes" to "many manners, customs, dealings," and in religious language "the ways of God" designated the totality of God's providential dealings with humanity — the sense Milton harnesses when he undertakes to justify them. Every modern sense — route, direction, manner, custom, means, distance, shipways — grows from the ancient image of a path travelled along.
