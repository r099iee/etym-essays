Derivation chain for THITHER (single root word; the demonstrative stem and its Germanic descendants are reconstructed at the earlier stages, with the Old English form attested):

1. Proto-Indo-European *to- (reconstructed) — the oblique demonstrative stem "that," partner of *so-; a pure deictic, pointing away from the speaker. Reflexes across the family: Sanskrit tat "that" (and tatra "there"), Avestan tat "that," Greek to (neuter article, from the Homeric demonstrative), Latin iste "that one of yours" (a parallel 2nd-person demonstrative), and Proto-Germanic *þa- "that."

2. Proto-Germanic *þadēr (reconstructed) — "thither, to that place," formed on the demonstrative stem *þa- ("that") with the directional suffix *-dēr; its siblings were *hidēr "hither" (to this place) and *hwidēr "whither" (to which place).

3. Old English þider / þæder — "thither, to that place," attested throughout the Old English period, including in Beowulf (c. 8th–11th centuries); the ordinary allative adverb of the language.

4. Middle English thider → thither — 12th–15th centuries; the final -r is analogical, spread from the paired forms here, there, and where, so that the adverb took the shape it has kept for six hundred years.

5. Modern English thither — "to that place, in that direction," archaic and literary in the modern period; the card's form, THITHER.

Note: the directional suffix *-dēr is the same element seen in the older stages of hither and whither; its precise Indo-European pedigree (sometimes compared with the contrastive suffix *-tero-) is debated and is here left open rather than asserted.
