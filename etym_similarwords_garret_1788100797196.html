• Attic: Similar because both name the space under the roof at the top of a house; differs because an attic is the whole storey — usually a storage void — while a garret is specifically a small habitable room, and the word carries its occupant with it.
  • Loft: Similar because both can mean a room directly under the roof; differs because a loft is traditionally open and unpartitioned (hayloft, organ-loft), and in modern English has been glamorised into the open-plan converted apartment — the very opposite of the cramped, partitioned garret.
  • Cockloft: Similar because it is a small, low space at the very top of a house, often over the attic; differs because a cockloft is a narrow void used only for storage or access, never a room in which anyone lives — it never acquired the garret's romance.
  • Mansarde (garret room under a mansard roof): Similar because French mansarde denotes exactly the top-floor room under a double-pitched mansard roof, the Parisian equivalent of the garret; differs because it is a loanword tied to one roof shape and to French bohemian tradition, and is far rarer in English.
  • Box-room: Similar because a box-room is a small room — often under the roof — for storing boxes and luggage; differs because it has no occupant-sense: a box-room is never someone's dwelling, whereas a garret is.
  • Roof-space / roof room: Similar because both denote the room directly under the rafters; differs because they are modern, neutral, estate-agent terms with none of the garret's literary and bohemian associations.
  • Turret / watchtower: Similar because the garret's earliest English sense was exactly this, a raised place of guard; differs because the military words stayed on fortifications while garret descended into the private house — the pair now points to opposite buildings.
  • Penthouse: Similar because a penthouse is also a top-floor dwelling; differs because it is the luxurious inverse of the garret — modern, serviced, expansive, while the garret is cramped, cold and romantic; the two are antonyms in cultural connotation.
  • Studio: Similar because a studio is a small working room of an artist or writer, the modern inheritor of the garret's function; differs because studios are purpose-lit, professional, often converted spaces, stripped of the garret's poverty and pathos.
