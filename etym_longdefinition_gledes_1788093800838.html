Gledes (plural of glede, also spelled gleed) is an archaic and literary word for a glowing ember — a piece of live, burning coal that still shines and gives heat without flame. It is in the plural that the word now chiefly survives: where we would say "embers", older and more poetic English said "gledes". In the source sentence — the death of Smaug in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) — the dying dragon's convulsive last throes splinter a wooden structure into "sparks and gledes": sparks flying through the air and gledes glowing in the wreckage, the debris of the impact still alight in the dark. Tolkien deliberately reaches for the archaic word to give the narrative the burnished register of old heroic English.

1. Core sense.

  • A live coal / glowing ember: a piece of coal or wood that burns without flame, glowing red or white with heat, as at the heart of a fire or at the end of a dying one. The glede is the ember that still gives light and can rekindle flame; the distinction from ashes and cinders is fundamental — gledes are alive, dead matter is not.
  • Plural dominance: "gledes" — the glowing remains scattered in a hearth, a forge, or after a catastrophe. The word's plural is the form readers meet, in the same way that "throes" is the plural of "throe": the image is naturally plural, a scatter of still-burning fragments. One glede can be blown into a flame; a hearth full of gledes is the banked life of the household.

2. Register and survival. Today glede(s) is obsolete in ordinary speech, but it has never quite died:

  • Dialect survival: northern English dialect (especially Yorkshire) preserves gleed "a live coal" into living memory; the word has a continuous oral life even where the standard language lost it.
  • Literary archaism: 19th-century historical and poetic writers revived glede as a rust-coloured archaism of fire; 20th-century fantasy and heroic fiction (above all Tolkien) adopted it wholesale, because it sounds both ancient and exact. A writer who needs a word for an ember with medieval fire in it reaches for glede.
  • Cognate awareness: the word's kinship with glow is still felt — a glede is, transparently, a glowing thing, and the etymology is visible through the spelling.

3. A caution about the homonym. English has a second, unrelated noun glede: the kite, a bird of prey (from Old English glida, from glīdan "to glide" — the bird that glides). The two words converge on one spelling but have entirely different roots: the ember glows, the bird glides. In the Tolkien sentence there is no ambiguity — "sparks and gledes" is a phrase of fire — but the dictionary reader should note both beasts: the glede of the hearth and the glede of the sky.

4. Grammar and morphology. Countable noun; plural gledes; dialect variants gleed, gleeds. The word was a feminine ō-stem noun in Old English (glēd) and now shows only a fossil derivational family: its noun relative glow (and the compounds glow-worm, afterglow) belongs to the same "shine" root but developed separately, as do the distant kin gold and yellow.

5. Summary. Gledes are glowing embers — live coals still shining without flame — a word that was already retreating from standard southern speech during the 15th–17th centuries, survived in northern dialect, and now glows most brightly in the deliberate archaism of fantasy and heroic prose, where "sparks and gledes" scatters the burning wreck of a dragon's death across the night.
