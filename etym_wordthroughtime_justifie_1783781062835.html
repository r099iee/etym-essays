14th-Century English Borrowing - Justifie enters English in the 14th century from Old French justifier ("to make just"), a legal and moral verb meaning to show something to be just, right, or lawful, in ordinary and religious prose.
Reformation Theological Weight - With the Reformation, justify gains a specialised doctrinal sense — the act by which God accounts a sinner righteous ("justification by faith") — so the word becomes heavy with theology in English discourse.
17th-Century Theodicy - Milton uses the early-modern form justifie at the hinge of Paradise Lost — "And justifie the wayes of God to men" — to demonstrate the justice of divine providence, the highest moral-philosophical reach of the word.
Contemporary Sense - Modern justify keeps the core sense "show to be right or reasonable" and adds a printing sense (justifying text aligns margins), while the -ie spelling survives only in archaic reproduction of early modern texts.
