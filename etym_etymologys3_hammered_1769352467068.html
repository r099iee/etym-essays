The biography of hammered begins at the dawn of human technology, in the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eḱ-, "sharp, pointed, piercing." For the earliest Indo-European speakers the sharpest thing they knew was a stone — a flint edge, a stone axehead, the hard rock that could cut, split, and smash. From this root the family built *h₂eḱ-mōn, "stone," literally "the sharp thing," and the word radiated across the ancient world with astonishing consistency: Vedic Sanskrit aśman named Indra's stone thunderbolt and the stone vault of heaven; Greek akmōn named the cosmic anvil that legend said fell from the sky for nine nights, as well as meteors and pestles; Baltic and Slavic preserved the plain "stone" sense in Lithuanian akmuõ and Russian kamen'. In every branch the same primal equation held: the hardest, sharpest, most heavenly object was a stone, and stones were for striking.

In the Germanic world this "stone" word was built into the tool itself: Proto-Germanic *hamaraz, "hammer." The strongest proof of its stony origin is that it kept the meaning "stone, crag, cliff" as well — Old Norse hamarr means both "hammer" and "steep rock," and Scandinavian place-names preserve the rock sense in hundreds of crags and headlands. The prehistoric hammer was a heavy stone lashed to a handle, used first to split wood and bone, then, with the coming of bronze and iron, to beat metal into shape on the anvil. Norse mythology gives the final glow to this stone-to-tool story: Thor's hammer Mjölnir, whose name itself is built on the word for "crushing, milling," was conceived as a stone-headed weapon that hurled lightning — the sky-stone of Indra's aśman reincarnated as the thunder-god's mallet. When the Anglo-Saxons carried the word to Britain, Old English hamor was already the smith's tool and the god's weapon, and the verb hamerian "to beat, forge" grew from it in the same period.

Middle English inherited both noun and verb — hamer and hameren — and the participle hamered, later hammered, appears from the fifteenth century onward. For the next three hundred years the word stayed close to its forge: it meant beaten, driven, and shaped by percussive blows, the neutral, craft-registered language of smiths, builders, and armourers. From the seventeenth century the verb began its great metaphorical migration. Anything subjected to repeated force was "hammered" — a path hammered hard by feet, a chest hammered by fear, a conscience hammered by guilt. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries added the decisive idioms: to "hammer out" an agreement, like forging metal into a finished shape; to "hammer home" a point, like driving a nail home; to go "under the hammer" at auction, where the gavel seals the sale. Each idiom preserves the blacksmith's action translated into another field.

The twentieth century produced the two senses now most familiar in speech. In finance, markets and shares "get hammered" when they are sold off heavily — the image of a severe, repeated beating delivered by panic selling. In sport, a team "gets hammered" when it is decisively defeated. And in slang, to "get hammered" is to become heavily drunk: the forge's pounding becomes the pounding of drink in head and body, the drunkard beaten flat and senseless like a nail under the hammer. The word's great strength is that all these senses co-exist without confusion — a hammered stock, a hammered team, and a hammered party-goer are all recognisably being struck, and struck repeatedly. The full journey, from the PIE sharp-stone, through Thor's thunderbolt and the smith's anvil, to the Wall Street ticker and the Friday-night bar, makes hammered a living museum of metaphor — one root, one blow, echoed across four thousand years.
