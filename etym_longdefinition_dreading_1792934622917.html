Dreading, the present participle of dread, names fear pointed forward in time: not the startle at what is here, but the cold anticipation of what is coming — hoofs on the road ahead, results day, the dentist's chair, the reckoning no one can quite avoid. To be dreading something is already to be suffering it in rehearsal, and the grammar of the participle keeps the suffering continuous: an unfinished fear for an unfinished event.

In the source sentence the travellers hear what they had all been dreading — hoofs on the road ahead. The past perfect progressive matters: the fear predates the sound, stretches across the whole journey, and is then confirmed in a single beat. Every reader knows the sinking recognition — the feared thing was real all along — and the sentence stages it perfectly: dread first, hoofs second, the participle holding the long before against the sudden now.

Core meaning in detail: Dreading governs a coming evil, real or imagined: dreading the news, dreading Monday, dreading the knock at the door. Psychologically it blends prediction with aversion — the mind simulates the bad outcome and flinches in advance — which is why dread is the most intellectual of fears, requiring imagination where panic requires only nerves. The word also carries a social sense (dreading a lecture, dreading small talk) where the feared thing is tedium or embarrassment rather than danger, but the mechanism is identical: time itself becomes the threat, because it delivers what is awaited.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Fearing beforehand, anticipating with aversion (primary modern sense): dreading the exam, dreading what the doctor will say. The bridge is time plus fear: dread looks forward where fright looks around, and the participle stretches that looking into a state.
  • Awaiting with reluctant resignation (softened everyday sense): dreading the in-laws' visit, dreading the Monday meeting. The bridge scales danger down to nuisance: the same grammar of anticipation, but the feared object is boredom, and the tone is wry rather than cold.
  • Holding in reverent awe (archaic, the old god-fearing sense): the generations that dreaded the Lord — feared with worship, not with flight. The bridge is the word's own past: dread once meant awe before power, and dreading meant living under that awe continuously.
  • Shying from, being reluctant (extended): dreading to ask, dreading to begin. The bridge carries anticipation into action avoided: the infinitive names the deed, and the participle names the long hovering before it.
  • The Rastafari sense by homonym (distinct word, noted for clarity): dreadlocks and their dread come from a different use — awe-full, terrible hair — and do not share this participle's history; the bridge is spelling only, and lexicographers keep the doors shut between them.

Usage, grammar, and register: dreading is standard in every register, from playgrounds to pulpits, and it governs two complements — the noun (dreading the results) and the verb (dreading to tell her, dreading telling her), the gerund slightly more British, the infinitive slightly more formal. Collocations cluster round time: been dreading, had been dreading, dreading all week — the perfect and progressive tenses are its natural home, because dread is fear with duration. Tone ranges from the comic (dreading the school run) to the mortal (dreading the diagnosis), and the single participle spans them without changing shape: only the object tells you whether the cold in the stomach is embarrassment or doom.
