contrition is a noun denoting deep, sincere sorrow for sin, fault, or wrongdoing, accompanied by repentance and the inward resolve to confess, atone, and amend. Stronger than casual apology and more inward than mere confession, it names the crushed, bruised heart that owns its guilt: the penitent does not merely say sorry but feels smitten, broken open, and determined not to repeat the offence. In the Wodehouse source, a few manly words of contrition are imagined as the plain, honourable apology that could have saved love's frail craft from coming a stinker — blunt masculine remorse as emotional salvage.

Theology gives the word its technical edge. In Catholic moral theology, perfect contrition (contritio) is sorrow for sin out of love of God, while attrition (attritio) is imperfect sorrow out of fear of punishment; both dispose toward absolution, but perfect contrition reconciles even before sacramental confession. Protestant and Jewish traditions use the word more broadly for godly sorrow versus worldly regret. The shared entailment is crushing: Latin conterere means to grind, crush, bruise — contrition is the soul ground by its own guilt, like grain under millstones.

Secular usage keeps the depth while dropping the dogma. A public figure's contrition after scandal, a lover's contrition after betrayal, a nation's contrition after atrocity — each is judged by signs of inward bruising: unqualified admission, no excuses, visible humility, offer of restitution, changed conduct. PR apologies that minimise, deflect, or perform are dismissed as lacking contrition. The Wodehousian manly qualifier is telling and dated: it asks for straightforward, unwhining remorse without grovelling — upper-class English manliness that owns up briskly and moves on.

  • Primary religious sense: godly sorrow for sin with purpose of amendment; perfect vs imperfect (attrition) contrition; act of contrition (prayer: O my God, I am heartily sorry...). Bridge: the heart crushed (contrite) like stone ground to powder.
  • General moral sense: profound remorse for wrongs to persons, not only to God — contrition for lying, cheating, cruelty, neglect. Requires acknowledgement of agency: accidents merit regret, wrongs merit contrition.
  • Public / political sense: performed and institutional remorse — apologies, truth commissions, reparations judged as showing (or feigning) contrition. Absence of contrition is an aggravating factor in courts and press.
  • Nuances vs neighbours: regret can be wistful and passive; remorse gnaws and can be self-tormenting; repentance stresses turning and changed life; penitence stresses suffering and penance; compunction is a prick beforehand; contrition uniquely fuses crushing sorrow + humble resolve. A manly words of contrition promises both feeling and fix.
  • Domain usages: liturgy and catechesis (act/examination of contrition); law (remorse and contrition as mitigation; lack of contrition at sentencing); therapy (genuine contrition vs shame in restorative justice); diplomacy (statements of contrition for wartime acts); literature (contrite hearts from the Psalms to Dostoevsky).
  • Collocations and register: formal, elevated, Latinate, often religious-literary. Patterns: deep / sincere / genuine / heartfelt contrition; show / express / feel contrition; words / tears / act of contrition; without a shred of contrition. Adjective contrite (a contrite apology, contrite heart); adverb contritely. More solemn than sorry, apology; less self-punishing than penitence, mortification.

In short, contrition is apology with a bruised heart and a turned will. The frail craft of love in Wodehouse founders for want of it: pride withholds the few plain words, the crush is never owned, and romance — figured as a tiny boat — takes on water. The comedy depends on the theology: even farce knows that crushed hearts float better than proud ones.
