- 15th century — 'mopped' enters English as an ordinary domestic verb: wiping floors or faces with a cloth (the implement moppe, mappe).
- 16th–17th century — everyday use branches out ('mopped his brow' is common narrative idiom); alongside it, an unrelated 'to mop' = to grimace produces the grotesque comic collocation 'mopped and mowed.'
- 18th–19th century — the grimace sense dies; mopped = cleaned or wiped only; the word is plain, humble, useful in kitchen, inn, ship and workshop.
- 20th century — 'mop up' goes to war: dispatches describe 'mopping-up operations' against the last pockets of resistance; then economists and bankers 'mop up' surplus cash and liquidity.
- 21st century — mopped is entirely everyday; it grounds even fantasy scenes (a sweating traveller borrowing a silk handkerchief) in homely, concrete reality.
