A word built to go downhill. The history of PEJORATIVE is the history of an ancient spatial metaphor that hardened into a precise technical label. At the bottom of the family stands a Proto-Indo-European root *ped-, "to go, walk, step" — the root of Latin pēs, pedis "foot," of pedestrian and pedal, and of the English word foot itself. From this everyday root of walking, Indo-European built a comparative form meaning "worse," and the image encoded in that form is striking: the worse person is the one who goes badly, who stumbles and trips on the path, who does not keep his footing. Latin inherited this as peior, "worse," the ordinary comparative of malus, "bad," and built from it a superlative, pessimus, "worst" — the same base in its fullest negative degree. Already in Classical Latin, then, the family carries a physical picture of falling, of losing one's step, that will silently govern everything the modern word does: to call something pejorative is to say that language has knocked it off its feet, pushed it downhill, set it beneath its proper place. From "worse" to "making worse." Classical Latin used peior as a plain comparative — "worse" — but it was Late Latin that turned the adjective into an engine of change. The denominative verb peiōrāre, "to make worse," appears in the Christian Latin of the fourth and fifth centuries, and its past participle peiōrātus, "made worse," supplied the stem on which the Romance adjective was built. The step from "worse" to "making worse" is the crucial transformation of the story: it converts a state into a function, a comparison into an action. Where Classical Latin could only say that one thing was worse than another, the new formation could name the very act of worsening — and it is that act that the modern word captures. A pejorative term is not merely a bad term; it is a term that does the work of making its object worse. Through French. French formed the adjective péjoratif on the Latin participle stem peiōrat- with the suffix -if, and the word is attested from the early sixteenth century, at first as a term of grammar and rhetoric: a péjoratif word or sense was one that depreciated, that pulled its object down. French had long possessed the verb péjorer, "to worsen," and the new adjective gave the language a way to name the tendency itself — the property of a form to carry its meaning downhill. It is this French adjective that English borrowed in 1882, and the borrowing arrived precisely at the moment when English lexicography and historical linguistics were becoming systematic sciences: dictionaries wanted a precise word for marking the sense that had "acquired a pejorative meaning," and péjoratif supplied it. Pejoration: the process named by the adjective. Once the adjective was in place, English built on it the noun pejoration, naming the historical process in which a word's meaning drifts toward the worse. The great textbook examples are as old as English itself: silly, which once meant "blessed, happy, innocent" and sank to "foolish"; vulgar, which once meant simply "common, of the people" and sank to "crude, offensive"; knave, which once meant "boy, servant" and sank to "dishonest rogue"; and awful, which once meant "inspiring awe" and now commonly means "very bad." Pejoration is the downward half of a two-way drift, and its opposite is melioration or amelioration, the upward drift (knight, once "boy, servant," rising to "noble warrior"; steward rising from "sty-ward, pig-keeper" to "manager"). The adjective pejorative names the state, the noun pejoration names the process, and together they give historical linguistics two of its sharpest instruments. Modern senses and the critical register. In modern English pejorative lives at two levels. At the technical level it is a lexicographer's label: a dictionary entry notes that a word "has acquired a pejorative sense," and the label itself is strictly neutral — it classifies, it does not condemn. At the everyday level, seen in the card's sentence, the word describes remarks and language whose function is to belittle: "pejorative remarks," "pejorative language," "used in a pejorative way." The two levels are continuous, because both depend on the same idea of downward evaluation: whether applied to a single suffix or to a critic's whole review, pejorative names language that works to lower the status of its object — to make the novel's themes "look small," in the sentence's phrase. The word thus carries within it the whole arc of its history, from the Proto-Indo-European walker who stumbles on the path, through the Latin comparative "worse," the Late Latin verb "to make worse," the French adjective "depreciatory," to the precise, neutral label that modern criticism uses to name the weaponizing of words.
  • PIE (c. 4500–3000 BCE): *ped- "to go, walk, step" gives rise to the comparative *ped-yōs "worse," literally "stumbling, going badly."
  • Latin (2nd c. BCE): peior "worse," comparative of malus "bad," with superlative pessimus "worst" on the same base.
  • Late Latin (4th–5th c. CE): peiōrāre "to make worse" and peiōrātus "made worse."
  • French (16th c.): péjoratif "depreciatory, tending to make worse," formed on the Latin participle stem.
  • English (1882): pejorative borrowed from French as adjective and noun; pejoration (the process) follows; the word becomes a standard instrument of lexicography, linguistics, and criticism.
