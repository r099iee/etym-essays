Crannies (plural of cranny) are small, narrow openings, chinks, or crevices — slight fractures or gaps in a solid surface such as stone, wood, or rock that are barely wide enough to admit a finger, a sliver of light, or a thread of wind. The word always carries a sense of smallness and inaccessibility: a cranny is not a wide opening but a thin, secretive aperture, often formed by weathering, splitting, or rough masonry, and frequently overlooked by the casual eye.

Core meaning

  • Small fissure or crack: A narrow split or break in a material — a cranny in a wall, a cranny in the rockface.
  • Recess or niche: A tight, often shadowed recess or corner in which things may be lodged, hidden, or forgotten.
  • Figurative hiding-place: Any obscure, tucked-away location — “every cranny of the old house”, “the crannies of memory”.

Semantic character and register

  • Cranny is markedly smaller and more secretive than words like opening or gap; it implies a space that does not invite entry but does invite concealment.
  • It is almost always concrete and physical, though it extends easily into metaphor (a mind’s crannies, the crannies of history).
  • Its most famous partner is nook — the phrase nook and cranny (often every nook and cranny) means “every part or place, however small or hidden”, and has been idiomatic since the 17th century.

Polysemy and modern senses

  • Physical crack in rock or masonry (the sense in Tolkien): In geology and mountaineering, crannies are small fractures; climbers wedge fingers and gear into crannies.
  • Architectural detail: In older buildings, crannies in mortar or between boards are typical of worn or weathered construction.
  • Biological ecology: Small crannies in bark, stone, or coral provide micro-habitats for insects, lichen, and nesting creatures.
  • Figurative and metaphoric: “The crannies of the mind”, “hidden in some cranny of the bureaucracy” — the sense of an obscure, hard-to-reach pocket.
  • In the idiom “nook and cranny”: a fixed phrase meaning every hiding-place and remote corner of a place, used in contexts of searching, cleaning, and exploring.

Historical bridge

  • The word descends from the idea of a “notch” or notched cut (see etymology fields): a cranny was originally a notch, a nick, a small incision-like gap.
  • From “notch” the meaning slid naturally to “crack”, because a crack is precisely the kind of narrow irregular gap that a notch in a surface becomes when it splits deeper.
  • Because such gaps are small and shadowed, the sense of concealment (“a hiding-place”) arose, and from concealment the idiomatic pairing with nook followed.

Grammar

  • Crannies is the regular plural of cranny (the final -y changes to -i- before -es).
  • A countable noun; typically used in the plural when enumerating many small gaps, or after “every”.
  • Verbs commonly collocating: to peer into, crawl into, lodge in, hide in, or wedge into crannies.

Register and tone

  • Standard, everyday vocabulary with a slightly literary or old-fashioned flavour when used alone; very common in travel, gardening, renovation, and descriptive prose.
  • In Tolkien’s usage (“the wind in the crannies of stone”) the word contributes a sense of desolation: thin cracks where only the wind can penetrate — no bird, no sound, only moving air threading through narrow gaps in rock.
