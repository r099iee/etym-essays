Tarrying is the present participle — and by long use the participial adjective — of the verb tarry: to linger, to delay going, to remain for a time where one might have moved on. It describes neither rushing nor settling, but the soft middle state between them: the guest not yet risen from the table, the traveller pausing on the threshold, the Exiles of the source sentence dwelling a while in Middle-earth before they sail. The word’s very sound loiters, with its doubled consonant and hovering -ing, as if reluctant to end.

In the source sentence the exilic sense is at its fullest: ‘we too are now only ______ here a while, ere we return over the Great Sea.’ Here tarrying is not idleness but sojourn — a conscious, sorrowful lingering in a land that is no longer truly home, before a final departure. Tolkien, steeped in the King James Bible where the word occurs again and again, chooses it precisely for that gravity: his Elves do not hang about or kill time; they tarry, as patriarchs and apostles tarried.

Core meaning in detail: To tarry is to spend time somewhere beyond the expected moment of leaving, whether deliberately (waiting for a companion, savouring a place) or helplessly (hindered, delayed, becalmed). As a participle, tarrying modifies persons — the tarrying guest — or stretches of time — a long tarrying — and carries a distinctly gentle, slightly archaic colour. Nobody in modern prose tarries at the bus stop; one tarries in gardens, in foreign cities, in memory.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Lingering in a place, delaying departure (primary, from late 14th c.): tarrying travellers, do not tarry on the road. The bridge from the earliest transitive sense (to hinder, to keep waiting) is a shift of viewpoint: the delay moves from the obstacle to the lingerer, from being delayed to delaying.
  • Staying or sojourning temporarily (literary, Tolkien’s sense): tarrying here a while. The bridge narrows lingering into dwelling-with-an-end-date: a stay understood as provisional, shadowed by a departure already decided.
  • Delaying action, procrastinating (extended): tarrying over a decision. The bridge carries the physical pause into the mental realm: feet that will not move become wills that will not resolve.
  • Waiting expectantly, abiding (biblical): tarry ye in Jerusalem. The bridge sanctifies the wait: lingering becomes vigil, and delay becomes obedience, as the disciples are commanded to remain until they are given power.
  • Being slow in coming, lagging behind (of things, archaic): older writers speak of tarrying help or tarrying justice. The bridge personifies abstractions as travellers on the road, late through no fault of the waiter.

Register and connotation: Tarrying is tender and time-haunted — poetry, liturgy, fantasy, and farewell letters. Against its blunt cousins dawdling (scolding) and loitering (suspicious), it sounds forgiving and even noble: nobody is blamed for tarrying. The cost of that beauty is archaism; used of a delayed train or a late email it would sound affected. It belongs to departures that matter — exiles, lovers, pilgrims — and lends any waiting it touches a gravity its modern synonyms cannot supply.
