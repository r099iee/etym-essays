Devilry is an abstract noun (also spelled deviltry) meaning wicked, cruel, reckless, or mischievous conduct — behaviour worthy of, or attributed to, the devil. Across its history it has spanned the full register from deadly serious demonology (sorcery, black magic, diabolical plots) to playful roguery (a child's impish tricks, a cunning practical joke). It is formed from devil + the suffix -ry (as in "roguery", "knavery", "mystery").

Primary senses

  • Diabolical wickedness: evil deeds attributed to Satan or demonic power — "the devilry of the dark forces". In medieval and early modern contexts this is the sense of devil-worship, possession, and demonic agency.
  • Sorcery and black magic: the practice of magic by which a person is believed to ally with or command devils — the witch's craft. This sense dominated 16th-century usage, when the word appeared in accusations, trials, and religious polemic.
  • Reckless or cruel mischief: wild, destructive, or dangerous behaviour that seems inspired by the devil — "the devilry of bored boys", "sheer devilry" as a label for wanton vandalism or a dangerous stunt.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Cunning tricks and cleverness: "some cunning devilry of his" — a shrewd, deceptive, or elaborate scheme; the word can carry grudging admiration for ingenuity, as when a magician, con artist, or rival outmanoeuvres someone.
  • Playful mischief: in everyday and dialect use, mild naughtiness — teasing, pranks, prankish energy — "the children are up to some devilry". This is now the most common ordinary sense, and it is often affectionate rather than condemnatory.
  • Personification and collective sense: "the devilry" can mean the whole class of devilish beings or activities — "all the devilry of hell" — a collective counterpart to "the devil".
  • Set phrases and special uses: "devilry" in cricket is the lively, unpredictable bounce of a fast bowler's delivery ("there was devilry in that pitch"); in folklore and Gothic fiction it denotes uncanny occurrences. The rarer variant "deviltry" is interchangeable and slightly more literary.

Polysemy: the semantic bridge

The word was coined in Middle English (late 14th century, as develrye) on the pattern of French-derived abstracts in -erie, meaning "the doings of the devil". Its breadth comes from the devil's own double personality in European culture: on one side the Prince of Evil of Christian theology, source of sorcery and damnation; on the other the folkloric trickster and tempter, source of pranks, mischief, and clever roguery. As religious fear receded after the 18th century, the word's centre of gravity slid from horror toward mischief: the same devilry that once burned witches now describes schoolboy pranks or an artful stratagem. Every modern sense preserves the devil as a model of a being whose energy is clever, forceful, and unbound by ordinary rules — admirable or appalling depending on the speaker.

Register and usage

  • Neutral-to-colloquial; more vivid and expressive than plain "wickedness", with a theatrical, storybook flavour.
  • Capable of being ironic or affectionate (a grin and "you devil!" shifts devilry toward charm) or deadly serious, depending entirely on context.
  • The noun is common in fiction and journalism but rare in formal technical prose; the magical sense lives on in fantasy, horror, and religious writing.
