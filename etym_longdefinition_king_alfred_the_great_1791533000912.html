King Alfred the Great is the conventional English name of Alfred, king of the West Saxons from 871 to 899, the ruler who halted the Viking conquest of England, imposed terms on the Danish leader Guthrum, built a network of fortresses and reformed the coinage, the law and the Church. He is also the only English monarch habitually called "the Great", a title that is post-medieval rather than contemporary with him. The name itself is a two-element Old English compound, Ælfrǣd, meaning "elf-counsel", and it is one of the most fully documented personal names of the early Middle Ages.

Primary historical sense

Alfred was born in 849, the youngest son of King Æthelwulf of Wessex. He became king in 871 in the middle of a Viking war, suffered a series of defeats, withdrew to the Somerset marshes, and in 878 rallied his forces to win the battle of Edington. He then imposed the treaty known as the Peace of Wedmore (or the Alfred-Guthrum agreement) on the Danish leader Guthrum, who accepted baptism and withdrew to the Danelaw. In the following decade Alfred rebuilt Wessex as a fortified kingdom and laid the foundations of a unified English monarchy.

  • Military defence — victory at Edington (878), the treaty with Guthrum, and the burh system of fortified towns that ringed Wessex.
  • Naval innovation — the construction of a royal fleet to intercept Viking ships, often cited as the beginning of English sea power.
  • Law and government — his law code, the Domboc, which combined Mosaic, Carolingian and West Saxon traditions and presented the king as a Christian lawgiver.
  • Learning — translations of Gregory's Pastoral Care, Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, Augustine's Soliloquies and Orosius's history, and the commissioning of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.
  • Church reform — patronage of monasteries, recruitment of continental scholars such as Asser, Grimbald and John the Old Saxon, and promotion of pastoral care.

The epithet "the Great"

No contemporary source calls Alfred "the Great". Asser calls him Angulsaxonum rex and rex Anglorum, and the Chronicle calls him simply Alfred cyning. The honorific is a later construction, first recorded in the sixteenth century and established as conventional in the nineteenth, when Victorian historians and artists made Alfred the model of a Christian king and the founder of English institutions.

  • Post-medieval coinage — the epithet appears in Tudor antiquarian writing and becomes standard in Victorian historiography.
  • Criteria — military survival, legal and educational reform, and the creation of a national foundation myth.
  • Comparators — Charlemagne, Cnut, Catherine, Peter and Frederick are all "the Great"; the title is a historiographical award, not a constitutional one.
  • Modern debate — historians argue about how much of the "Alfred legend" is ninth-century reality and how much is later national myth.

The onomastic sense: the name Alfred

As a personal name Alfred is a dithematic Germanic compound of ælf "elf" and rǣd "counsel, advice". Such two-element names were common among the Anglo-Saxons, and they were often chosen for the good qualities the elements invoked: wisdom, protection, victory or nobility. The name therefore means something like "elf-counsel" or "wise counsel", and it belongs to a large family of Ælf- names such as Ælfric, Ælfgifu and Ælfwine.

  • Ælf "elf" — a supernatural being of Germanic folklore, later reduced in English to a small mischievous creature.
  • Rǣd "counsel, advice" — the same element as in rede and German Rat; it also underlies the modern word rede "to advise" and the surname Read.
  • Meaning — "elf-counsel", perhaps "wise counsel" or "counsel of the elves".
  • Variants — Ælfrǣd, Ælfred, Elfredus, Alfred; modern short forms Alf, Alfie and Fred.
  • Not related to Albert — Albert is a different compound, Adalbert "noble-bright", and the similarity is accidental.

Cultural and legendary senses

Beyond the historical king, "Alfred" is a figure of English legend and a stock image of the wise ruler.

  • Alfred and the cakes — the medieval story of the king, hiding from the Danes, who was rebuked by a peasant woman for letting her cakes burn; it first appears in the twelfth century and became a Victorian favourite.
  • Alfred as lawgiver — the constitutional myth of English liberty and trial by jury, much of it anachronistic but deeply influential.
  • Alfred as patron of English — the myth of the founding of English prose and of a national literary language.
  • Alfred in modern media — The Last Kingdom, Vikings and the 1969 film Alfred the Great have kept the legend alive.

Commemoration

  • Winchester — the bronze statue by Hamo Thornycroft, unveiled in 1901 to mark the millenary of Alfred's death.
  • King Alfred's Tower — a folly at Stourhead in Wiltshire, completed in 1772 and later associated with the site of Egbert's Stone, where Alfred is said to have rallied his army.
  • King Alfred's Way — a long-distance cycling route across southern England.
  • Schools, pubs and naval ships — the name is a recurrent emblem of English identity.

Semantic bridge

The phrase joins a personal name, a royal title and an honorific epithet. Alfred names the man, King names his office, and the Great names the judgement of posterity. Each element has its own history: the name is a Germanic compound, the title is a native English word for a ruler, and the epithet is a Latin-derived convention of historiography. King Alfred the Great is thus not a single lexical item but a layered onomastic phrase, and its interest lies in the way those layers have been welded into a national symbol.
