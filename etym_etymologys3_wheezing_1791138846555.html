Wheezing is the present participle of the verb wheeze, whose history is almost entirely imitative: people shaped a word that sounds like the sound it names. Middle English whesen (c. 1300, “to make a whistling noise in breathing”) is borrowed or cognate with Old Norse hvæsa “to hiss (like a snake, a cat, escaping steam).” Old Norse hv- regularly corresponds to English wh- (what / hvat, white / hvitr). Proto-Germanic is reconstructed as *hwēsan or *hwēsōn, and historical linguists connect it to a wider PIE imitative cluster *kwes- / *pes- for panting, puffing and hissing, also seen in Latin quere? and Sanskrit śvas- “to pant, breathe hard.” Because the root is sound-symbolic rather than arbitrary, exact cognate sets are debated, and Etymonline and the OED both flag the origin as “probably imitative, cf. Old Norse.”

Early attestations are sparse because wheezing belonged to barn, sickroom and kitchen rather than chronicle. The verb appears in Middle English medical and devotional texts for asthmatic or dying breath: “he wheseth in his brest.” By the 16th century it had acquired its full modern spelling wheeze after the Great Vowel Shift raised Middle English long e /eː/ to /iː/, and the initial wh- simplified in most English accents from /ʍ/ to /w/. Shakespeare does not use it prominently, but 17th-century physicians (Harvey, Sydenham) use wheezing as a clinical participle for obstructed bronchi, and sailors use it for bellows and leaking pipes that “wheeze.”

Semantic development stayed close to breath while branching by metaphor:

  • Literal respiratory: to breathe with a high-pitched whistling or rattling due to narrowed airways — asthma, bronchitis, croup, cold air, exertion. This is Dickens’s use: frozen Londoners “go wheezing up and down,” their breath audible in fog.
  • Extended to animals and objects: horses with heaves, dogs with kennel cough, old bellows, concertinas, radiators and asthmatic engines that emit a similar whistle. By the 19th century any leaky pneumatic device could wheeze.
  • Figurative for persons / institutions: a “wheezing joke,” a “wheezing excuse,” a “wheezing empire” — something old, laboured, out of breath, kept going with effort. This jocular-political sense booms in Victorian journalism.
  • Noun wheeze: both the sound itself (“a wheeze in his chest”) and, in British slang from the early 20th century, a clever trick or wheeze (“a wheeze to save tax”) — from the idea of something “wheezed out” or whispered.

Cultural context matters for Dickens. Victorian London fog was sulphurous coal-smoke (“pea-souper”), and cold-air bronchospasm made even healthy pedestrians audibly wheeze. To say people went “wheezing up and down” is therefore both realistic reportage and moral atmosphere: the city itself is asthmatic, labouring to breathe. Physicians of the 1840s already linked fog to “wheezing cough” and prescribed flannel, stamping and hand-beating — exactly the self-warming behaviours Dickens lists alongside.

Modern medicine has narrowed wheezing into a precise auscultation term: continuous musical adventitious lung sounds, typically expiratory, indicating bronchoconstriction, versus stridor (inspiratory, upper airway), crackles and rhonchi. The lay sense remains broad — any noisy, whistling breath — and the participial adjective (“a wheezing pensioner,” “a wheezing accordion”) preserves the progressive, ongoing quality: not one wheeze but a habit or condition. Spelling and pronunciation have been stable since 1700: wheeze, wheezes, wheezed, wheezing /wiːz/, /ˈwiːzɪŋ/.

In sum, wheezing travelled from an Old Norse hiss-word for snakes and steam into a Middle English sickroom verb, then into a Victorian urban-atmosphere word and finally a clinical sign, without ever losing its onomatopoeic transparency: say wheeze slowly and you perform the laboured exhalation it denotes.
