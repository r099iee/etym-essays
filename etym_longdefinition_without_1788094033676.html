Primary privative sense (preposition). The core modern meaning of without is the expression of absence, lack, or exclusion: someone or something is not accompanied by, not possessing, or not made use of a given person, thing, condition, or quality. In "he left without money", "she cannot sleep without a fan", or "without your help we are lost", the word marks the negation of accompaniment or possession and governs a noun or noun phrase. This is the overwhelming default sense in contemporary English and the one that dominates all registers, from colloquial speech to legal drafting, scientific prose, and religious language ("faith without works is dead").

Adverbial sense. Without also functions as an adverb, usually governed by a preceding noun or pronoun, meaning "not having or not possessing (what has just been named)": "Do you have a ticket? I'm afraid we'll have to go without"; "if there is no sugar, we shall do without". In this use the preposition's object is ellipted because it is recoverable from context. Related elliptical phrasal verbs — go without, do without, manage without — treat the word as a quasi-adverb meaning "in a state of lacking".

  • Privative core: the dominant sense, "not having or in the absence of", governs nouns, pronouns, gerunds ("without thinking"), and clauses (archaic "without that...").
  • Adverbial/elliptical: "we'll manage without", "make do without" — the object is omitted because it is contextually obvious.
  • Spatial/archaic sense: "outside of, beyond, on the outside of" — preserved in phrases like "within and without", "without the walls", and in architectural or literary register ("the enemy camped without the city").
  • Concessive / conditional nuance: in fixed expressions such as "without further ado", "without delay", "without fail", the word signals the absence of a barrier or condition and shades into an assurance of immediacy or certainty.

Secondary and idiomatic senses. A wide set of set phrases rely on without as their load-bearing negative marker: without a doubt ("certainly"), without prejudice (legal formula: not harming a legal right or claim), without loss of generality (mathematics: a standard assumption that does not restrict the argument), without GMO / without sugar (product labelling), there is no X without Y (proverbial structure of mutual dependence), and count without one's host (to plan without considering limitations). In finance, "without" appears as an adverb meaning "excluding" (a share sold "ex" or "without dividend"). In logic and mathematics the phrase "without loss of generality" (abbreviated WLOG) is a technical idiom meaning that an assumption may be made freely because it does not weaken the proof. In Scottish and legal English, "without" historically also functioned as a conjunction meaning "unless" ("without he repent"), a sense that is now archaic.

Grammatical range. Morphologically without is a fossilised compound and today is a fixed, uninflected form. In Old English it was an adverb-preposition built from two elements; in modern grammar it is treated as a preposition (governing the objective case of pronouns: "without him/her/them"), as an adverb, and — archaically — as a conjunction. It can negate gerund phrases ("without asking"), participle phrases, and even single adjectives in elliptical style. It is the natural negative counterpart to with: whereas with expresses accompaniment, means, or instrument ("walk with a stick", "cut with a knife"), without negates all of these ("walk without a stick", "cut without a knife").

Semantic bridge. The historical core is spatial: without originally meant "on the outside, outside of". The leap to the privative sense arose because what is "outside" one's possession or reach is exactly what one does not have — the spatial exterior became a metaphor for absence and lack. As English developed a need to distinguish "outside" (purely spatial) from "not having" (privative), the spatial job was handed to outside while without increasingly specialised in privation. Understanding this bridge explains the word's apparent polysemy: every sense, from "without the walls" to "without hope", is the spatial "outside" rotated through the metaphor of possession and reach.
