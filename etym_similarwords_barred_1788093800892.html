• Bolted: Similar because securing a door or gate shut; differs because a bolt is a sliding rod working within a lock-mechanism, and bolted never develops the legal-exclusion or striped senses that barred carries.
  • Locked: Similar for “shut and secured”; differs because it implies a key or lock rather than bars, and lacks the imagery of exclusion by rule or of barring the way.
  • Latched: Similar as a small-scale fastening; differs because much lighter (a latch is a catch), and it is never figurative, legal or heraldic.
  • Shut / Closed: Similar for “not open”; differs because they are general and neutral, with no barrier image and no striped or legal senses.
  • Blocked / Obstructed / Impeded: Similar for the “way barred” sense; differs because they are often accidental or neutral (“a road blocked by snow”), while barred implies an active shutting by barrier or authority.
  • Sealed: Similar in making impassable or closed; differs because it implies completeness and often formality (wax, tape, official orders), whereas barred keeps the image of the beam and of exclusion of persons.
  • Excluded: Similar in the exclusion sense; differs because it is fully general (arguments, items, people), while barred retains the barrier and legal resonance.
  • Prohibited / Forbidden: Similar in “not allowed”; differs because they forbid the act, impersonally and usually by decree, whereas barred excludes the person from a place, right or body and keeps the boundary image.
  • Banned: Very close in the modern informal sense (“barred from the pub” ≈ “banned from the pub”); differs because banned is the everyday word for prohibitions of any kind, while barred is specifically exclusion from a place or body, and it alone retains the older physical and striped senses.
  • Debarred: Similar because formally excluded, and etymologically identical material (de- + bar); differs because it is an older legal-flavoured synonym, rare outside statute and parliamentary language.
  • Disbarred: Similar because removed from a professional bar; differs because it is strictly technical, meaning expulsion from the legal profession, and is never used of other kinds of exclusion.
  • Striped / Banded / Striated: Similar for the “marked with bars” sense; differs because they are generic visual terms — barred implies regular transverse bands in the specialist idiom of heraldry, ornithology and ichthyology.
  • Cordoned (off): Similar in sealing an area against entry; differs because it denotes a police or security ring (a cordon) that can be lifted later, not a permanent bar.
  • Fenced: Similar in enclosure; differs because fenced means surrounded by a fence, while barred closes the passage itself with a bar.
  • Ribbed: Similar for lines on a surface; differs because it implies raised ridges, not flat transverse bands, and never occurs in law.
