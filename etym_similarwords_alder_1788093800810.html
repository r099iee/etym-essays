• Birch: Similar because it is the closest botanical kin (same family, Betulaceae) and a similar fast-growing pioneer of disturbed or wet ground; differs because it is a separate genus (Betula) with white peeling bark, different fruit and a drier, more northern ecology.
  • Hazel: Similar because it belongs to the same family and is a common native woodland tree; differs because it is a shrubby underwood producing edible nuts, found in drier woodland rather than wetland.
  • Hornbeam: Similar because it is a birch-family tree with catkins and hard wood; differs because it favours dry heavy soils, has beech-like leaves and ribbed bark, and is not water-loving.
  • Willow: Similar because it is the archetypal companion tree of rivers and wetlands, sharing alder's habitat and its literary pairing; differs because it belongs to a different family (Salicaceae), has long flexible twigs and spring catkins, and its wood is pliant rather than firm.
  • Poplar / Cottonwood: Similar because they are fast-growing trees of wet floodplains with light timber; differs because they belong to the willow family, grow tall and columnar, and do not fix nitrogen.
  • Elm: Similar because it is a large broadleaf of moist lowlands whose timber was also used for waterlogged piles; differs because it belongs to a different family (Ulmaceae), grows far larger, and was decimated by Dutch elm disease.
  • Elder: Similar because it shares the name-shape (both developed an excrescent -d-) and a folklore niche; differs because it is an unrelated hedgerow shrub (Sambucus) with edible flowers and berries, not a waterside timber tree.
  • Winterberry / Black alder (Ilex verticillata): Similar because it carries 'alder' in its common name; differs because it is a holly — a wetland shrub of eastern North America with red berries — not a true alder.
  • Alder buckthorn (Frangula alnus): Similar because both its common and scientific names echo 'alder'; differs because it is a shrub of another family with distinct leaves and bark, best known as a purgative in herbal medicine.
