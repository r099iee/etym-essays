- 4th Century BCE (Classical Antiquity) - Known in ancient Greece as *basilikón phyton* ("royal plant"), borrowed from *basileus* ("king"); revered for its regal scent while simultaneously feared in folklore for alleged connections to scorpions and the deadly mythical basilisk.
- 13th to 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed into English from Old French *basile*, derived from Late Latin *basilicum*; cultivated in monastic herbariums for digestive cordials and wound plasters.
- 15th to 16th Century (Renaissance Era) - Popularized across Tudor England as a culinary herb and strewing plant; featured in Thomas Tusser's gardening verses (1573) and referenced in Boccaccio's famous Italian tale of Isabella's pot of basil.
- 17th to 18th Century - English herbalist Nicholas Culpeper notes the superstitious disputes surrounding the plant, calling it an herb of Mars under Scorpio; native European *Clinopodium vulgare* becomes widely recognized as "wild basil".
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Canonized in Romantic poetry by John Keats's *Isabella, or the Pot of Basil* (1818); celebrated by Oscar Wilde in *The Devoted Friend* as a charming cottage-garden staple.
- 20th to 21st Century - Elevated globally as the undisputed "king of herbs" in modern gastronomy through the international popularity of Italian pizza and Genoese pesto, while holy basil (Tulsi) achieves worldwide fame in holistic wellness and herbal adaptogens.
