- c. 9th-10th c. - Attested in Old English as āwiht / ōwiht, functioning as a vital positive polarity pronoun meaning "any creature, any thing whatsoever" across heroic and theological poetry.
- 12th-14th c. - Contracted phonetically in Middle English into ought / aught, widely used in common speech, legal oaths, and Chaucerian narratives alongside its negative counterpart nought.
- 16th-17th c. - In Early Modern English (Shakespeare, King James Bible), aught became idiomatic in conditional and interrogative formulas ("for aught I know", "if aught befall"), though everyday colloquial conversation increasingly favored anything.
- 18th-19th c. - Receded from spoken vernacular into high literary, poetic, and historical registers (Wordsworth, Tennyson, Walter Scott), used deliberately to evoke solemnity, nobility, or antiquity.
- 20th-21st c. - Preserved in epic fantasy and historical fiction; simultaneously acquired a distinct American colloquial role via metanalysis (rebracketing "a naught" as "an aught") to designate the zero digit in calendar decades ("the Aughts", 2000-2009).
