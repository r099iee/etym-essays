To proffer is to hold something out — usually in one's hands — for another person to take, and to do so with deliberate, ceremonious courtesy. In this scene from Chapter 3 of Dream of the Red Chamber, Dai-yu observes that "another maid proffered a spittoon", and from that single gesture she infers that the tea she has been given is for rinsing her mouth, not for drinking. The word matters because it captures the disciplined, anticipatory service of the Jia household: the maid does not merely hand over an object, she extends it forward, anticipating the guest's needs, so that even the offering of a spittoon becomes a piece of ritualised etiquette. The tone is courtly, dignified, almost weighty — "proffer" elevates a mundane act into choreographed propriety and signals that the servants' movements, like the guests' manners, are governed by an exacting code of conduct. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES "Proffered" is the past participle of "proffer", and although the word is far narrower than its synonym "offer", it still has several distinct uses:
  • Physical sense (the sense in the context): held out or extended in the hand for someone to take — a proffered spittoon, a proffered hand, a proffered gift, a proffered cup. The word always implies the gesture: the object is advanced toward the other person, unsolicited or in response to a need, with courtesy and often formality.
  • Offered for acceptance (of non-physical things): presented for consideration or acceptance — a proffered apology, a proffered deal, a proffered invitation, proffered help. Here the "holding out" becomes abstract: the speaker extends an offer toward the other party, and the word retains its ceremonial, slightly weighty colouring.
  • Legal and technical sense: in law, "proffered" describes evidence or a plea formally offered for the court's consideration — "proffered evidence" is evidence whose admissibility is being tested; the court may sustain or overrule an objection to the proffer. This usage is professional and procedural, a fossil of the word's old formality.
  • Historical register: in Middle and Early Modern English the word was common for any offering of a gift, service, or condition — the medieval "profre" appears constantly in chronicles and romances for a knight offering a gift, a challenge, or a service. The word has steadily retreated from the everyday toward the formal, so that modern "proffered" sounds consciously literary, courteous, or legal.
  • Related forms: "proffer" (verb and noun: "a proffer of friendship"), "proffered" (past participle and adjective), "proffering" (present participle), "profferer" (one who proffers).
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The bridge between the physical and abstract senses is direct and can be read in the word's own Latin construction: "pro-" means "forward" and "ferre" means "to carry", so "proferre" is literally "to carry forward, to bring forth". The concrete sense "to bring forward out of one's hands" came first, and the abstract senses — "to bring forward an offer, an apology, evidence" — are the same gesture translated from the hands to the voice and the will. In every use the word preserves the image of someone stepping forward and extending something, which is why it feels courtly: it is a verb that keeps its body. SYNONYM BOUNDARIES AND REGISTER Beside its synonyms, "proffer" is the most formal and the most physical of the "giving" verbs. "Offer" is the broad, neutral default: you can offer a cup of tea or offer an opinion, and the word carries no ceremony. "Tender" is chiefly legal and commercial (tender payment, tender a resignation). "Present" is broader still — a lecture, an award, a bill can all be "presented". "Submit" implies documents or proposals handed upward for judgement. "Hand" is casual and neutral. "Proffer" alone combines the physical gesture (held out in the open hand) with a ceremonial courtesy, and it is marked for formality: rare in casual speech, where "offer" has displaced it, but alive in literary narrative, etiquette description, and legal writing. In the novel it is exactly right: the word makes a servant's act read as choreography, dignifying the spittoon into an object of ritual and turning household service into theatre.
