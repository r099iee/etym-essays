brassed is the past tense of brass in the British slang phrasal verb brass up, meaning to pay up, hand over money owed, or settle a debt, especially promptly and without quibbling. "He brassed up like an officer and a gentleman" means he paid the money at once, honourably and generously, behaving as a gentleman should. The up marks completeness, as in pay up or cough up; the past form reports a completed act of payment.

The image is copper coinage: brass has meant money, especially small copper cash, since the 16th century, so to brass up is literally to produce the brass. Unlike neutral paid, brassed up is colloquial, vivid, and faintly comic, suggesting cash physically counted out. In Wodehouse it carries an approving nuance — the payer does the decent thing without being chased. Elsewhere it can imply reluctant extraction ("If you don't brass up I'll put you off the bus", 1890), the context deciding between gallant and grudging. The verb governs no direct money object in the intransitive use ("he brassed up") but can be transitive in period slang ("he brassed up the thick 'uns" = paid the sovereigns, 1898).

  • Primary sense — to pay, settle: to hand over what is due. Example: "I presumed Uncle Tom would brass up" (Wodehouse, 1971). The bridge from metal to payment is metonymy: brass coins stand for money in general, and producing them discharges obligation. This is the only live sense in the Wodehouse passage.
  • Secondary / separate senses of brass (not this verb): the noun brass also means the yellow copper-zinc alloy, the brass section of an orchestra, high-ranking officers ("the top brass", from uniform braid, 1899), and impudence ("bold as brass", 1620s). The military verb brass up meaning to bombard with gunfire (from spent brass cartridges) is a homonym, as in "to brass up a position". Context — money versus machine-guns — prevents confusion, but learners must note the split.
  • Coating sense of to brass (distinct): standard to brass means to coat with brass ("brassed fittings"), and brazen means to face impudently ("brazen it out"). These share the metal root but involve covering or cheek, not paying; only up + money context triggers the pay reading.
  • Register and grammar: dated British slang, informal, now less common than fork out or cough up but instantly understood. Intransitive or transitive with sum; past brassed, participle brassing up. Typical subjects are debtors, backers, or losers paying winners; typical occasions are fares, bets, bills, and blackmail. The "officer and a gentleman" comparison elevates it to honourable, willing payment.
  • Connotation in context: Aunt Dahlia uses it admiringly: Uncle Tom did not haggle or moralise but handed over the cheque at once. The comedy lies in the clash between military honour language and a domestic touch for magazine debts — Wodehouse makes a cheque sound like a charge.

In short, brassed up = paid up, with brass coins as the mental image and honour as the overtone here. Learners should anchor on "where there's muck there's brass" (money): to brass up is to convert brass metal into brass money by handing it over.
