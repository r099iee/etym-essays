Pizarro (proper noun) — the surname of Francisco Pizarro (c. 1475–1541), the Spanish conquistador who led the expedition that conquered the Inca Empire of Peru, and, by extension, a byword for ruthless imperial conquest in the Americas. In Macaulay's History of England the name occurs in the celebrated comparative sentence on the growth of the British colonies: "how, in America, the British colonies rapidly became far mightier and wealthier than the realms which Cortes and Pizarro had added to the dominions of Charles the Fifth." Macaulay yokes the two conquistador surnames as twin emblems of the Spanish method of empire — realms seized by the sword, gold drained away, native institutions destroyed, nothing durable built — and sets them in deliberate antithesis to the English colonies, which grew "far mightier and wealthier" through settlement, law, commerce, and the slow accumulation of capital. A single surname therefore carries the moral argument of the whole passage: the Spanish method conquered; the English method created. CORE SENSES AND USES
  • The historical person. Francisco Pizarro, born about 1475 in Trujillo, Extremadura, the illegitimate son of an infantry captain, who began life as a swineherd and never learned to read. As a soldier in the New World he rose to lead one of the most audacious military enterprises ever undertaken: with fewer than two hundred men he captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532, extorted the legendary roomful of gold as ransom, executed his captive in 1533 despite the ransom, seized Cuzco, the Inca capital, and founded Lima in 1535 — only to be assassinated in 1541 by followers of his former partner Diego de Almagro.
  • The surname as metonym. In 18th- and 19th-century historiography (Robertson, Macaulay, Prescott's The Conquest of Peru, 1847) "Pizarro" stands synecdochically for the entire Spanish conquest of Peru, and beyond it for the first plundering phase of European empire in America — conquest as extraction rather than settlement.
  • The archetype. In later and modern usage the name can be genericized to name the type of the ruthless conquistador: audacity without scruple, cruelty, greed for gold, and the destruction of a high civilization. This is the sense Macaulay exploits and the one that has kept the name alive in political and literary discourse.
  • Anthroponymic and related-word uses. As a living family surname (common in Spain and Latin America) Pizarro retains its hereditary value. The Spanish common noun pizarra "slate; blackboard" belongs to the same Basque word-family as the surname but is a different word: the surname is a byname "man of the rocky place," not a derivation from the noun meaning "blackboard."
DISTINCTIONS AND BOUNDARIES
  • Pizarro vs Cortes. Both are conquistador surnames paired by Macaulay; Cortes is the emblem of conquest with strategy — alliances, diplomacy, self-justification in writing — while Pizarro stands for plain rapacity: seizure, extortion, and killing without any constitutional ambition.
  • Pizarro vs conquistador. conquistador is the generic Spanish class noun ("one who conquers") applied to hundreds of individual soldiers and captains of the conquest era; Pizarro names one specific, notorious man whose biography supplies the moral colour.
  • Pizarro vs colonizer / settler. colonizer implies the planting of population, institutions, and lasting rule; Pizarro founded towns (Lima) chiefly as bases for extraction and left behind a ruined native order and civil war among the conquerors.
  • Pizarro vs adventurer. In 16th-century English and Spanish the fitting term was "adventurer," a licensed fortune-hunter acting under a royal contract (capitulación); modern "adventurer" has shed the hard commercial and predatory sense.
  • Pizarro vs explorer. The conquest was not discovery in the scientific sense; there was no cartography, natural philosophy, or curiosity about the Andean world in Pizarro's enterprise, only the search for gold and dominion.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE AND HISTORICAL WEIGHT Because Pizarro is a proper noun, its lexicographic interest is metonymic rather than polysemous in the ordinary sense: it has no plural, no comparative, and no derived verb. Its "meanings" are referential and allusive. The historical bridge that turned a surname into an epitome is the sheer extremity of the Peruvian conquest — the most spectacular, most lucrative, and most destructive of the Spanish enterprises — which fixed its leader's name as the type-case of the destroyer of civilizations. The British imperial historians of the 18th and 19th centuries, writing while Britain built its own empire by trade and settlement, found in Pizarro the perfect negative image: the empire that seized and destroyed, as opposed to the empire that planted and enriched. Macaulay's pairing "Cortes and Pizarro" compresses two centuries of that comparison into two names, and the name Pizarro in English thus carries a dark, diagnostic moral charge — the conquest that added realms to a distant crown and built nothing in return.
