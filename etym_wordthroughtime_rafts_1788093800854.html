- c. 1300 — Middle English “raft”: “a beam, a log, a rafter” — a single piece of timber, from Old Norse raptr — a carpenter's and builder's word
- late 15th c. — the sense shifts from the log to the structure: “raft” = a floating platform of timber — the part names the whole
- 16th–17th c. — rafts carry armies, fugitives and freight across rivers; travellers' accounts make the word familiar in Europe and the Americas
- 18th–19th c. — the American frontier: immense log rafts float lumber down the Mississippi; “rafting” becomes a river industry and a way of life (Huckleberry Finn's raft is its literary monument)
- 1837 — “a raft of” appears in American English: “a great number, a heap” — the quantity sense is born from the piled-logs image
- 20th c. — life rafts and inflatable rafts: the word becomes a life-saving device; whitewater rafting turns it into a sport
- Today — the platform sense (emergency, recreation, transport) and the colloquial quantity sense (“a raft of”) both thrive; “rafter” reminds us the word began as a beam
