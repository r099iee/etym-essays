Rattled belongs to that ancient and large class of English words born not from an inherited Indo-European root but directly from the sound they imitate. The story begins in the prehistoric Germanic speech community, where a stem *hratt- — an onomatopoeic gesture reproducing a rapid, repeated percussion — appears to have been coined independently. Because the word is imitative, its ultimate origin is classified as unknown or "expressive" rather than traceable to PIE: there is no reconstructable Proto-Indo-European etymon, and attempts to derive it from an Indo-European root meaning "to strike" or "to run" have not won general acceptance. The stem is nonetheless shared across the Germanic world, which shows that the noise, and the imitation of it, circulated widely: Old Norse hrata means "to reel, totter, stumble" (a sense of rapid, unsteady motion close to the modern "rattle along"), while on the continent Middle Dutch ratelen, Middle Low German retelen, and Old High German razalōn all mean "to rattle". The very similarity of these forms across mutually unintelligible dialects is the point: people hearing the same rattle made the same sound with their mouths. The word enters English in the thirteenth century. Middle English ratelen is first attested around 1300 in the sense that is still the primary one today — to emit a rapid succession of short, sharp, clattering sounds. The form is frequentative in tendency: the verb names not a single impact but a long, broken series of them, and a related frequentative suffix -el- in the stem reinforces the repeated, hammering character of the noise. English has always been hospitable to such vivid imitative verbs — clatter, clink, jangle, clank, whistle, sizzle — and rattle sits at the head of this family, its own cognates in Dutch and German confirming the shared Germanic imagination of percussion. Sound changes and form. The English form shows the ordinary history of a Germanic loan-native: the initial cluster of the continent, when it appears, is simplified, and the medial vowels reduce in unaccented syllables, so that the verb settles into the two-syllable rat-le pattern with a silent final -e in Middle English spelling. The past tense rattled is a perfectly regular weak formation: the Old English and Middle English weak past suffix -ede/–de attaches to the stem, giving rateled(e), which by the modern period has contracted to -ed, pronounced /d/ after the sonorant. There is nothing irregular or archaic about the form — it is the standard English way of making a past tense, and its very regularity has helped the word ride through seven centuries of change unbroken. Semantic drift: from sound to machine to nerve. The history of the word is a history of the sounds that surrounded English speakers. In the later medieval and early modern period the verb attaches itself to the new mechanical world: carts and chariots rattle over cobbles, looms rattle in the weaving sheds, and the fixed phrase "the death-rattle" (16th c.) names the final, frightening rattle in a dying person's throat — a grim application of the noise to the human body at its most vulnerable. By the eighteenth century the adjective "rattling" means brisk and energetic ("a rattling pace"), and the sound, always fast, has come to stand for speed and vigour. The decisive transfer happens in the nineteenth century: in 1869 "to rattle someone" first means to disconcert or unnerve them. The metaphor is bodily and acoustic — the shaken person's composure is itself rattled, knocked about by a rapid succession of shocks, exactly as a loose object is rattled in a box. From this transfer grows the modern psychological sense, "rattled" = flustered, thrown off balance, which now dominates sports writing, journalism, and everyday speech. "Don't let it rattle you" has become a proverb of composure. Idioms and the social life of the word. The same sound has generated a remarkable family of idioms. "To rattle off" (1823) means to recite rapidly; "to rattle through" means to do something hastily. "Saber-rattling" (1876) imports the sound of clashing blades into international politics, a calque of German Säbelgerassel — Tolkien's own goblins, who rattle their swords, are themselves a kind of sabre-rattling. The noun senses thicken the picture: the baby's rattle, the snake's rattle (the rattlesnake, so named by the eighteenth century, signals danger with exactly this sound), and the mechanical rattle of a machine or alarm. Literary attestations run from medieval alliterative verse, where rattling describes armor and weaponry, through the ballads and the Romantic poets, to Tolkien's deliberate use in The Hobbit, where "swords rattled" belongs to a sentence built wholly of noise. What makes "rattled" endure is that it is onomatopoeia at its purest: the reader who hears the word has already heard the sound, and seven centuries of English have only widened the occasions on which it can be heard.
