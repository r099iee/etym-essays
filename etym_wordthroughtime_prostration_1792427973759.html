- [Late 14th–15th c. (Late Middle English)] — borrowed from Old French and Late Latin (*prostracioun*), primarily denoting the formal religious act of casting one's body prone on the earth before the holy altar, crucifix, or monarch — [perception: revered as the ultimate physical manifestation of spiritual humility, penitence, and absolute religious surrender]
- [16th–17th c. (Tudor & Stuart Eras)] — extended from personal devotional liturgy into political sovereignty, diplomacy, and military conquest, describing the complete subjugation, humiliation, or political ruin of a defeated nation or rebellious city — [perception: viewed as a stark, tragic sign of utter political ruin and sovereign vassalage]
- [18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Medicine)] — appropriated by clinical pathology, neurology, and physiology to describe catastrophic systemic bodily collapse, physical helplessness, and nervous depletion ("nervous prostration", "heat prostration", neurasthenia) — [perception: regarded as a severe, debilitating medical pathology characterized by total loss of vitality and muscular energy]
- [20th–21st c. (Contemporary Era)] — flourishes with dual vitality: in anthropology and comparative religion as the technical term for full-body devotional salutations (e.g., Hindu *daṇḍavat praṇāma*, Islamic *sujūd*, Buddhist prostrations, Catholic ordination rites), and in broader discourse as an evocative metaphor for total psychological burnout, institutional paralysis, or economic collapse — [perception: universally recognized as both a sacred posture of sublime egoless reverence and an acute state of physical and emotional depletion]
