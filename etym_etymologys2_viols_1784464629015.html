Medieval Latin vitula “a bowed stringed instrument, fiddle-type” (attested in Medieval Latin glosses and writers; ultimate origin obscure and debated) → Old Provençal / Occitan viola “bowed string instrument” (attested 11th–12th c.) → Old French viole “viol, stringed instrument” (attested 12th c.) → Middle French viole (same sense) → English viol “fretted bowed consort instrument” (attested c. 1500, in Elizabethan usage) → Modern English VIOLS “fretted consort instruments, plural; Tolkien's form, 1937” (present day). Debated / nuanced note: The chain is secure and attested from the Romance stage onward. What is genuinely uncertain is the birth of the word itself. Medieval Latin vitula has no agreed etymology: older handbooks link it to Latin vitulārī “to exult, celebrate, leap for joy” (with the minor goddess Vitula, Joy, as a folk-etymological anchor) — the festive-music image; others see an onomatopoeic or pre-Romance substrate instrument-name that cannot be reconstructed as a clean Proto-Indo-European root. The honest position, adopted here, is that the chain begins at Medieval Latin with the origin flagged as obscure; no credible PIE root set exists, and none should be invented. A parallel Romance branch, Italian viola and its diminutive violino (“little viola,” i.e., violin), fed the later violin-family names, so English viol, viola, and violin are relatives by name but different instruments by history.
