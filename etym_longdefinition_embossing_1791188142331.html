Embossing is the process, craft, or result of creating raised relief decoration on a pliable surface — most typically paper, leather, metal, textile, or parchment — so that a design stands out in low relief from the background when viewed or touched. In its core modern sense it denotes both the action (the act of embossing) and the product (the raised pattern itself), produced by pressing the material between an engraved male die and a matching female counter-die, by stamping, rolling, or hammering, with or without heat, ink, or foil.

Historically the term grew from medieval metalwork and bookbinding where bosses — knob-like ornaments — were applied to shrines, bindings, armour, and plate. From the 14th to 16th centuries to emboss meant to adorn with bosses, to carve in relief, or to chase protuberances in metal and wood. With the rise of printing, papermaking, and later industrial stationery, the sense narrowed toward paper: embossed letterheads, embossed notepaper, embossed Braille-like raised letters for the blind, embossed postage stamps, and embossed leather bindings. In the context of tactile literacy — as in your source about raised letters on paper — embossing means specifically producing legible raised type that can be read by touch.

Modern technical extensions multiply the core image of raising:

  • Paper, stationery and print finishing: blind embossing (raised image without ink), debossing (the inverse, pressed inward), foil embossing, sculpted or multi-level embossing for wedding invitations, business cards, certificates, book covers, and official seals where a notary or corporate seal is embossed to authenticate a document.
  • Industrial manufacture: embossed metals (coinage, car body panels, kitchen foil with micro-dimples), embossed plastics (textured dashboards, non-slip flooring), embossed textiles (quilted velvet, brocade effects, faux leather grain), and embossed wallpaper where pulp is roller-pressed into anaglypta patterns.
  • Biology, imaging and computing: in microscopy and photography an emboss filter gives a pseudo-three-dimensional relief; in cartography hill-shading is called emboss effect; in dermatology a wheal or plaque may be described as embossed; in data-visualisation embossing denotes raised graphical elements for affordance.
  • Figurative and literary use: poets speak of veins embossed on a leaf, stars embossed on night, or memory embossed on the mind — meaning sharply imprinted, standing out from a flat ground, durable and touchable. To be embossed with an experience is to carry it as a raised scar or ornament.

Polysemy bridges are transparent: all senses share the schema FIGURE RAISED FROM GROUND. From jewellers' bosses (round knobs) came the verb to furnish with bosses, then to raise any ornament in relief, then the gerund for the process and result, then metaphorical imprinting. Deboss is a 19th-century back-formation for the opposite; boss (supervisor) is an unrelated homograph from Dutch baas and must not be confused.

Grammatically embossing functions as (a) present participle — she is embossing the cover, (b) verbal noun / gerund — embossing requires pressure, and (c) concrete countable noun — an embossing, several embossings meaning individual raised designs. Collocations include embossing press, embossing die, embossing folder, embossing powder (for heat-embossed rubber stamping), embossing seal, blind embossing. Register is neutral-technical, common in crafts, design, manufacturing, philately, and accessibility discourse.
