The modal wilt of thou wilt descends in an unbroken line from Old English willan, to wish or want, whose second-person singular wilt served ordinary conversation for a thousand years. It shares the ancient Indo-European family of volition: cognate with German willst, thou willst, and rooted in the reconstructed base meaning to wish or choose. Through Middle English wilt remained completely everyday — Chaucer's pilgrims wilt and would, shall and shalt, without ceremony — and the King James translators of 1611 set it ringing through scripture: thou wilt keep him in perfect peace. The grammar only died when its partner died: as thou surrendered to universal you in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, wilt lost its only subject and froze into liturgy, hymn, and deliberate archaism. Modern fantasy and translated wonder tales, like the Koshchei story of the source sentence, thaw it out whenever a king, witch, or immortal horse needs to sound timeless, pairing it with thee and oft for full period flavour.

The plant verb wilt is a separate creature with a murkier past, first clearly recorded in 1691 for causing greens to droop. Scholarship derives it, with stated hesitation, as an alteration of Middle English welken, to wither or fade, current around 1200, whose own deeper ancestry is obscure — possibly Germanic, possibly touched by Scandinavian forms, but nowhere attested in a chain firm enough to print without qualification. The irregular consonant shift from welk to wilt resists tidy sound-law explanation, suggesting dialect reshaping: gardeners and cooks altering a fading word until it sounded like the collapse it described, the short i itself sagging between consonants.

Eras and extensions:

  • Garden origin: seventeenth-century kitchen gardens gave the verb its first home — herbs and lettuces wilting in noon heat — with the transitive causative (the sun wilts them) following close behind the intransitive droop.
  • Metaphorical bloom: by the 1800s novelists wilted heroines, spirits, and hopes, transferring vegetable limpness to human morale so thoroughly that modern speakers meet the metaphor before the marrow.
  • Scientific adoption: twentieth-century phytopathology fixed wilt as a disease name — Fusarium wilt, Verticillium wilt, bacterial wilt — and soil physics defined the permanent wilting point, the moisture level below which a plant cannot recover, making the old garden word a measured constant.
  • Modal fossilisation: simultaneously, thou-wilt completed its journey from grammar to costume, surviving in wedding vows, the Lord's Prayer echoes, Shakespeare performance, and fantasy translationese as instant antiquity in two syllables.
  • Coexistence without confusion: the two histories never touch, yet speakers navigate them effortlessly: thou or thee nearby selects the king's verb, while sun, soil, and stems select the gardener's.

Attestations mark both tracks: Milton and Bunyan wield thou-wilt as living grammar, while Evelyn's garden writings and later agricultural journals nurse the drooping sense into respectability. That a wonder tale about Koshchei the Deathless still reaches for thou wilt three centuries after its grammatical death proves the fossil's vitality: some words retire from work and begin a second career as atmosphere, and modal wilt is their exemplar, reigning over fable while its twin rules the greenhouse.
