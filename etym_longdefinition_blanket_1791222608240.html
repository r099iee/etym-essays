Most concretely, a blanket is a large, usually rectangular piece of thick, soft, woven material - traditionally wool, now also cotton, acrylic, fleece, or blends - used as a bed covering for warmth, as a wrap for the body, or as a covering for animals. Blankets are distinguished from sheets by thickness and pile: sheets are thin and next to skin, blankets provide insulation above them, often under a duvet or as the top layer. In the money-history passage, all three (two knives plus stockings) equaled a blanket because a sturdy woollen blanket was universally useful on frontiers - bedding, coat, shelter, and trade good at once. Hudson's Bay point blankets, Navajo blankets, and military blankets show how a simple rectangle became currency, art, and survival gear.

By extension, blanket functions as verb and adjective. As verb, to blanket means to cover thickly and uniformly as a blanket covers: snow blankets the fields, fog blankets the airport, news coverage blankets the nation. As adjective or attributive noun, blanket means general, uniform, applying to all without exception: a blanket ban, blanket coverage, blanket amnesty, blanket rule. A blanket ban on smoking prohibits it everywhere; blanket insurance covers all items; blanket consent covers all procedures. This figurative sense derives visually from the physical act of throwing a blanket over something - complete, even, undifferentiated covering - and is now dominant in news, law, and policy.

Technical and idiomatic layers abound. In printing and offset lithography, a blanket is the rubber cylinder cover that transfers ink; in horse care, blankets are rugs and sheets for warmth and fly protection; in fire safety, a fire blanket smothers flames; in slang, wet blanket is a person who spoils fun, from the image of a wet blanket smothering fire. To blanket out in radio is to overpower weaker signals. Babies have security blankets for comfort (Linus blanket), and beaches have blanket as groundsheet. Colour idioms like blanket bog in ecology denote peatland covering slopes like a blanket.

  • Bedding sense: wool, cotton, electric, weighted blankets; thermal properties, tog ratings, weaving (plain, twill, pile); cultural forms from kilims to point blankets.
  • Animal and functional covers: horse blankets, picnic blankets, emergency space blankets (Mylar), fire blankets; all share portable warmth or protection.
  • Verb sense - literal covering: snow, ash, leaves blanket ground; stresses thick, soft, continuous layer.
  • Verb sense - figurative suppression: to blanket criticism, to blanket the media; to smother or saturate.
  • Adjective sense - universal: blanket policy, blanket pardon, blanket approval; implies no exceptions, sometimes criticized as crude or overbroad.
  • Technical senses: offset blanket, gas blanket (inert gas layer over liquid), blanket bog, blanket formation in geology.
  • Idioms: wet blanket (killjoy), security blanket (comfort object), born on the wrong side of the blanket (archaic: illegitimate).

The unifying image is uniform covering for protection or concealment. From commodity money to snow to legislation, a blanket evens out differences: it warms every sleeper alike, hides every contour alike, binds every case alike. That is why economists cite blankets as primitive money (divisible only by barter rates but universally desired) while editors praise or blame blanket statements for sweeping generality. Mastering the word means moving fluidly between cosy noun, covering verb, and sweeping adjective.
