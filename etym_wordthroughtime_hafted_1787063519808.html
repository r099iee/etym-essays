- Old English (8th-11th c.) - "hæft" = "handle" but also "bond, captivity, seizure" — both the concrete grip and the abstract idea of being held, from the same root
- Middle English (13th-14th c.) - "haft" narrows to the tool sense: the handle of a knife, axe, or other blade
- 14th-16th c. - "hafted" appears as the past participle and the verb "to haft" — to fit a blade with its handle; the word settles into craft and martial vocabulary
- 17th-19th c. - stable as a working word of tradesmen and soldiers; appears in dictionaries with the handle sense fixed; the archaic "bond" sense fades from living use
- 19th-20th c. - archaeology adopts "hafted" as the technical term for prehistoric stone tools mounted on handles; museum and fieldwork language
- 21st c. - chiefly technical, archaeological, and literary; everyday speech prefers "with a handle", but craft writing and heroic prose retain "hafted" for its exactness and age
