The Juangs (also spelled Juang, Juango, or Juanga) are an Austroasiatic, Munda-speaking Adivasi (Scheduled Tribe) community concentrated in the Gonasika Hills of Keonjhar (Kendujhar) district and adjoining parts of Dhenkanal and Angul in northern Odisha, eastern India, with a population today in the tens of thousands. The singular Juang denotes one member or the group as a collective ethnonym, while the English plural Juangs — formed with the regular English -s — denotes multiple persons or the community in the aggregate, as in “the Juangs threw the seeds.” Anthropologically they are known as one of the most conservative Juang-Kharian branches of the North Munda peoples, alongside the Santals, Mundas, Hos, and Kharias.

Culturally and economically the Juangs are classically associated in colonial and Indian ethnography with shifting cultivation — chasa, dhara chasa, and the very rudimentary broadcast sowing described in the source: seed scattered or “thrown together into the ground” and left to find its own way up, without ploughing. This hoe-and-digging-stick horticulture, supplemented by forest gathering, hunting, and later wet-rice terraces in valleys, made them a textbook example of “primitive” agriculture in early 20th-century diffusionist writing (Hahn, Werth, and the passage quoted here). Their traditional dormitory system (mandaghar or majang), clan organization (boksa), absence of a plough until recent times, leaf-dress (kapat) formerly worn by women, and Austroasiatic Juang language (ISO 639-3: jun) all reinforced that image.

Linguistically Juang is both an ethnonym and a glossonym: the Juang language belongs to the Munda branch of Austroasiatic, closely related to Kharia, and preserves archaic features of great interest to historical linguists. Religiously the Juangs traditionally venerate village and hill deities (Dharam Devta, Basumati), practise ancestor rites, and have in modern times interacted with Hinduism and Christianity. Since Independence they are listed as a Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Group (PVTG) in Odisha, with protective land rights, reservation benefits, and development programmes, though mining, deforestation, and agrarian change have pressured their habitat.

  • The people / ethnic group (noun, plural, primary): “the Juangs” = members of the Juang tribe. E.g. “The Juangs live in Keonjhar.” This is the sense in the source.
  • A single member (noun, singular Juang): “He is a Juang.” English often back-forms the singular by stripping the -s; in Odia and Hindi the form is Juang for both singular and collective.
  • The language (proper noun, usually Juang): “She speaks Juang.” A North Munda language with northern (Gonasika) and southern (Chakradharpur/Nagam) dialects. Shares the ethnonym by metonymy: people → speech.
  • Cultural type / textbook example (noun, attributive): “Juang agriculture,” “Juang dormitory.” Used in anthropology to exemplify shifting cultivation, youth dormitories, or Munda kinship. Bridge: part-for-whole from group to trait.
  • Administrative category (noun, official): “Juang PVTG,” “Juang household.” Appears in census, tribal welfare, and reservation lists. Meaning narrows from lived identity to bureaucratic classification.
  • Adjectival use (attributive): “Juang village,” “Juang myth.” Formed without inflection. Common in ethnography where the plural Juangs is replaced by the stem.

The word is always capitalized as a proper ethnonym. Pronunciation in English is commonly /ˈdʒuːæŋz/ with a hard j, long u, velar nasal ng, and voiced plural z; in Odia and Juang itself the stem is closer to [dʒuaŋ] or [dʒuʌŋ]. To call a practice “Juang” in older literature sometimes carried a primitivist overtone — “simplest,” “rudest” — which modern anthropology rejects as pejorative evolutionism; today the neutral contemporary use stresses Indigenous knowledge, language endangerment, and land rights. In the source sentence the term is purely denotative — the people who broadcast seed — but the surrounding chapter uses them to illustrate a stage of agricultural evolution from digging-stick to hoe to plough.
