- 7th–13th century (Arabic badawiyyūn) - Identified as the noble, austere desert-dwelling Arab pastoralists whose pure dialect and martial prowess formed the bedrock of classical Arabic poetry and early Islamic conquests.
- 14th century (Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah) - Conceptualized as the sociological archetype of natural bravery, raw hardihood, and powerful social cohesion ('aṣabiyyah) in direct contrast to soft, urbanized civilization.
- 14th–16th century (Late Middle English / Old French adoption) - Entered Western European languages (as Bedoyn) via Crusader chronicles and pilgrim travelogues, perceived as exotic, fierce, and nomadic desert warriors of the Holy Land and Sinai.
- 18th–19th century (Enlightenment to Victorian Romanticism) - Romanticized by European explorers and orientalist scholars (Burckhardt, Doughty, Burton) as aristocratic, uncorrupted 'sons of the desert' possessing legendary chivalry and unmatched hospitality.
- Early 20th century (Arab Revolt & Mandate Era) - Viewed through political and military lenses (e.g., T.E. Lawrence) as crucial irregular cavalry forces whose tribal loyalties were navigating the collapse of the Ottoman Empire and the creation of modern borders.
- Late 20th–21st century (Modern Nation-State Era) - Regarded as indigenous desert communities facing profound challenges of modernization, urban sedentarization, land disputes, and cultural preservation within centralized nation-states.
