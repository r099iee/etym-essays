• Hardships: Similar because both denote difficult, trying conditions; differs because hardships stress suffering and misfortune that may be accidental or external, whereas rigours stress systematic, structural severity and unbending discipline.
  • Severities: Similar because both mark harshness; differs because severities highlights punitive or climatic harshness (of a judge, a winter, a parent) and is less idiomatic as a fixed plural for a way of life.
  • Austerities: Similar because both can describe harsh self-denying living, especially religious; differs because austerities sound chosen and ascetic (fasting, plain living), while rigours are imposed institutional grind that one endures.
  • Strictness: Similar because both name severity of rules; differs because strictness is a quality of conduct or enforcement, lacking the bodily “cold-hardness” metaphor that still lives in rigour.
  • Stringency: Similar because both denote tight, unyielding standards; differs because stringency is chiefly about rules and budgets (stringent regulations), with no plural “rigours-of-life” idiom.
  • Rigidity: Similar because both derive from the same root meaning “stiff”; differs because rigidity is about stiffness of structure, rule, or personality, not about lived ordeal or demanding conditions.
  • Difficulty / trials: Similar because both name hard conditions; differs because difficulty is vague and general, while rigours implies a recognized system or season of harsh demands.
  • Discipline: Similar because both can denote strict training or rule; differs because discipline emphasizes the system of training (and can be welcomed or chosen), whereas rigours emphasize the harshness of the demands themselves.
  • Deprivation: Similar because both can describe hardship; differs because deprivation names the lack of comforts specifically, a component of rigours rather than their whole.
  • Rigor (US spelling): Similar because it is the American spelling of the same word; differs orthographically, and in that rigor also carries the medical sense of a fever-chill (rigor mortis) that the plural rigours does not.
