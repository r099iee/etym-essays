DERIVATION CHAIN — DERISION: PIE ROOT: UNCERTAIN. Latin rīdēre "to laugh" has no secure Proto-Indo-European etymology. A debated connection links it to the root *weyd- "to see" (cf. Latin vidēre "to see"), through a hypothetical development "to see clearly" → "to look at with knowing mockery"; another proposal invokes a root *wrēid- "to be ashamed/embarrassed" (cf. Sanskrit vrīḍā "modesty, shame"). Neither is firmly established, and rīdēre is usually classified as of unknown origin. The attested chain therefore begins with Latin. → Latin rīdēre "to laugh" (attested classical Latin verb) [c. 2nd–1st c. BCE] → Latin dērīdēre "to mock, laugh at, deride" (compound: dē- "down, away, thoroughly" + rīdēre "to laugh"; the literal image is "to laugh down at") [attested 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE] → Latin dērīsiō (stem dērīsiōn-) "mockery, derision" (abstract noun formed from the supine stem dērīsum of dērīdēre) [attested 1st c. BCE – 2nd c. CE] → Old French derision "mockery, scorn, derision" (from Latin accusative dērīsiōnem) [attested 12th c.] → Middle English derisioun / derision "mockery, contemptuous ridicule" [attested 14th c.] → Modern English derision "contemptuous ridicule or mockery" → DERISION NOTE ON THE DEBATE / NUANCE: Only the pre-Latin stage is uncertain. The development within Latin (rīdēre → dērīdēre → dērīsiō) and the Romance-to-English transfer (Old French derision → Middle English derision) are fully attested and uncontroversial. The mnemonic on the card — "DE- (down) + RIDERE (laugh) = laughing down at someone" — accurately captures the classical Latin formation, which is the securely documented core of the word's history.
