Conspirator is a person who takes part in a conspiracy — a secret plan, typically with others, to commit an unlawful, rebellious, or at least clandestine act. In the party speech in The Hobbit, Thorin (or the toasting voice of the company) names Bilbo "our friend and fellow conspirator, this most excellent and audacious hobbit." The word is half-serious elevation: the unexpected party becomes a cabal, the burglar-recruit a sworn plotter, and Bag End a conspirators' hall. Tone: mock-grand, affectionate, slightly dangerous — adventure dressed as secret politics. For a respectable Baggins the label is comic overstatement; for the dwarves' real plan against a dragon it is nearly literal. The word is pure Latin morphology built on breath: spirare "to breathe," con- "together" — a conspirator is etymologically one who "breathes together" with others in a shared hidden design. Core senses of conspirator
  • Agent noun, joint secrecy (primary): a person who takes part in a secret plan with others, especially an unlawful or rebellious one — "the conspirators met at midnight," "he was named as a conspirator in the plot."
  • Criminal law: a party to a conspiracy — the legal offence of two or more persons agreeing to commit an unlawful act; in law, a conspirator is defined by the agreement itself, not by any overt act.
  • Political and historical: a plotter against a ruler, government, or public order — the Gunpowder plotters, the Catiline conspirators — where the word carries dread and treason.
  • Half-ironic / mock-grand use (this card): a playful, affectionate label for partners in mischief or adventure — "fellow conspirators" — the word's dark charge softened into cozy collusion around cake and ale.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and modern register senses
  • Legal doctrine: in criminal law, "conspiracy" and "conspirator" have precise technical senses — the agreement (and often an act in furtherance) is the crime, and conspirators can be liable for each other's foreseeable acts; the word in this register is formal and neutral of emotion.
  • Conspiracy-theory discourse: "conspirator" in the modern age appears in the vocabulary of conspiracy theories — alleged co-plotters of hidden events — where the agent noun is used both literally and dismissively.
  • Figurative breathing together: the etymological image survives in literary and rhetorical use — conspirators as people who "share the same breath of design" — a metaphor available to writers, and exactly what Tolkien's toast plays on.
  • Coconspirator / co-conspirator: the doubled agent noun, emphasizing the jointness (con- twice), common in legal and journalistic English.
Semantic bridges The whole family grows from Latin spirare "to breathe" and the comitative prefix con- "with, together": conspirare "to breathe together," then "to be in harmony, to agree," then — in political Latin, especially in descriptions of plots — "to agree secretly against authority." The noun conspirator is the agent formation on that verb, and English takes it (via French conspirateur) in the Middle English period with the dark political sense already fixed. Because conspiracy is etymologically "breathing together," it is essentially social — never a solitary act in the strict sense of the word's formation. Conspirator in the dwarves' toast is that Latinate legal-political word made cozy — and yet the plot against Smaug is real, so the comic and the literal senses both hold at once.
