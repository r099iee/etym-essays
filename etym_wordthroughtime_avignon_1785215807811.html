- Roman and early medieval town - the English name Avignon first registers the modest southern Gallo-Roman river town of Avennio on the Rhône, long before its fame.
- 14th century City of the Popes - in the English and European imagination the name becomes inseparable from the Avignon Papacy (1309-1377), when seven French popes held their court on the Rhône; the name means papal exile and splendour, the "Babylonian captivity."
- 16th-18th century papal enclave - Avignon remains a papal possession within France, a theocratic curiosity on the map, slowly fading in English awareness to a quiet provincial city.
- 19th century romantic association - for Victorian readers, as in Mill's Autobiography, the name gains a melancholy glamour as the place where Harriet Taylor Mill died and was buried (1858) and where Mill bought a house overlooking her grave.
- Contemporary use - a tourist byword: the famous bridge of the nursery rhyme "Sur le pont d'Avignon," the Palais des Papes, and the annual theatre festival; the name carries a durable mixture of papal grandeur, romance, and song.
