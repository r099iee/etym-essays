The history of elicited is the history of a hunting image that never lost its shape. The word descends from Latin ēlicere, a compound of the prepositional prefix ē-/ex- ("out, forth") and the verb lacere ("to entice, lure, decoy, trap"). Its literal meaning is therefore "to lure out"—to entice a hidden creature from cover, exactly as a hunter draws a wary animal into the open with bait or a trapper entices a bird into a snare. The verb lacere itself is a puzzle for etymologists: it survives chiefly through its prefixed compounds (ēlicere, dēlicere "to allure away," illicere "to entice in") and a family of related nouns (laqueus "noose, snare," illecebra "allurement"), but no securely attested Indo-European root has been established for it. Some scholars propose a connection to a root *lak- "to entice, allure, snare," citing in support the Germanic family of invitation (compare Old English lathian and the modern invite), but the link is speculative and most authorities simply record lacere as a Latin root of unknown deeper ancestry.

What is certain is how Romans used ēlicere. In Classical Latin the verb had a remarkably wide and vivid range. Hydraulic engineers ēlicēbant water when they drew groundwater up to the surface by engineering and pumping. Priests and augurs ēlicēbant omens, responses, or even spirits when they coaxed divine communications out of the heavens or the underworld through ritual—a religious sense preserved in the famous prayer formula of the pontiffs. And lawyers, magistrates, and negotiators ēlicēbant answers, confessions, and secrets when they drew the truth out of reluctant witnesses or adversaries. In every domain the verb's logic was the same: the thing drawn forth was present but concealed or withheld, and the drawing was achieved by art, ritual, or persuasion rather than by force. This was a crucial difference from extrahere ("to drag out"), which implied violence, and it is a difference that survives intact in modern English.

From the participle ēlicitus and the related stem ēlicit-, post-classical Latin built the frequentative and verbal forms that would give modern languages their verbs: Late Latin ēlicitāre, Italian elicitare, Spanish elicitar, and French éliciter. English borrowed the word directly from Latin in the mid-seventeenth century, at the height of the Renaissance vogue for Latin scholarship. The first recorded English uses, around the 1640s, were in philosophical and theological prose, where to elicit meant to draw out a truth or principle that was latent in premises or in the soul—a sense close to "educe" or "deduce." The verb immediately settled into the formal registers of law, medicine, and philosophy, where it described drawing testimony from witnesses and symptoms or reflexes from patients.

The nineteenth century carried elicit into the mainstream of educated speech, and the rise of experimental psychology in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries gave the word a precise technical home: in behaviorist psychology, a stimulus elicits a response, and the stimulus-response pairing became the central model of the field. It is telling that the same logic governed both the Victorian courtroom and the twentieth-century laboratory—in each case a designed, controlled intervention draws forth a predictable response from a subject who would not have produced it spontaneously. This modern scientific sense is simply the classical image of the hunter's bait, formalized.

Key points of the evolution:

  • Composition: The word is built from Latin ē- ("out") + lacere ("to entice"); the compound verb ēlicere "to lure out" is first attested in Classical Latin of the 1st century BCE.
  • Classical polysemy: Already in Latin the verb covered engineering (drawing out water), religion (coaxing out omens and spirits), and law (drawing out testimony), all united by the image of artful luring-forth.
  • Sound and form: English retains the Latin participle stem ēlicit- almost unchanged, giving the regular past form elicited; the prefix was respelled without its macron and the initial long ē was reduced to the English /ɪ/ vowel.
  • Borrowing date: Entered English c. 1640s from Latin, in philosophical and legal prose; spread to general educated usage over the 18th–19th centuries.
  • Psychological modernization: In the 20th century the stimulus–response sense made elicit a standard verb of experimental psychology, while everyday use broadened it to any deliberately drawn reaction—from applause to a smile to a confession.
  • Literary attestation: In the translation of Dream of the Red Chamber, "Yu-cun elicited from him that the visitor was a scholar from the capital" deploys precisely the word's classical nuance: information drawn out by conversational finesse from a source who would not have volunteered it—the modern heir of the Roman advocate coaxing testimony from a reluctant witness.
