Imparting is the gerund (verbal noun) of the verb impart, and it names the act of giving, communicating or passing something on to another. In the source it is used in a polemical phrase: Cicero insists that the teaching of the orator is "not the imparting of tricks" — that is, not the handover of a bag of technical devices, but the slow formation of the whole person. The gerund is doing real work here: it turns the verb into a thing, a method of instruction, and makes that method the object of Cicero's denial.

Core senses of the verb

  • To communicate information, knowledge or news: to impart a secret, impart wisdom, impart the facts to the police. This is the dominant modern sense, and it always implies a recipient, usually marked by to.
  • To bestow or transmit a quality, property or effect: to impart flavour, colour, warmth, momentum, dignity, a sense of occasion. Here the object is abstract or physical but not propositional; what is transferred is a characteristic rather than a message.
  • To grant a share of something material: the older, now archaic or legal sense, "to give a part of, to share out." A testator may impart an estate; a host may impart provisions.
  • To make known by disclosure: a narrower sense, common in confidentiality clauses and in the law of confidence, where "information imparted" is information entrusted to another in circumstances implying an obligation of secrecy.
  • Obsolete intransitive use: "to impart with" or "to impart in" once meant to share or participate in something, a construction that has disappeared from standard English.

The gerund and its grammar

  • The -ing form is both gerund and present participle. As a gerund it behaves like a noun — "the imparting of knowledge" — yet it retains verbal properties and can take a direct object: "imparting knowledge to children."
  • Two constructions compete: the gerund with of ("the imparting of skills") and the gerund with a direct object ("imparting skills"). The first sounds more nominal and formal, the second more verbal and active.
  • The derived noun impartation exists but is rare outside theology and law; imparting is the ordinary nominal form, and it is noticeably more formal than "passing on" or "sharing".
  • Because the verb is transitive and directional, the gerund typically implies an asymmetry: someone who has, gives to someone who lacks. Teacher to pupil, expert to novice, author to reader, God to creature.

How the senses are related

  • The root notion is division into parts: Latin pars "part" underlies partire "to divide, distribute" and, through the compound impertire (reshaped in Late Latin as impartire), "to share out, bestow." To impart is originally to give someone a part of what one holds.
  • Material sharing yields the legal and archaic senses: land, money, goods divided and granted.
  • Immaterial sharing yields the modern senses: knowledge, news and qualities are treated as possessions that can be portioned out. The metaphor is so natural that it is invisible — we "share" information, "give" advice, "pass on" a skill.
  • The direction of transfer explains why impart rarely appears without a beneficiary: the word is about a one-way grant, not a mutual exchange. "Impart to" is the characteristic frame.

Register, collocations and connotation

  • Register: formal and slightly elevated. It belongs to sermons, statutes, dedication addresses, examination boards and academic prose more than to conversation.
  • Typical objects: knowledge, wisdom, information, news, skills, a secret, a sense of, flavour, colour, dignity, momentum, gravity.
  • Connotation: beneficent and deliberate. To impart is to intend the transfer and to expect it to benefit the receiver; it lacks the slyness of "leak" and the neutrality of "supply".
  • Avoidance in speech: colloquial English prefers "give", "tell", "pass on", "share" or "communicate"; impart in casual speech can sound pompous, which is why it is often used ironically.
  • Theological use: "imparted righteousness" (righteousness actually given to the believer) is distinguished from "imputed righteousness" (righteousness reckoned or credited); "impartation" names the conferring of spiritual gifts, especially in Pentecostal and charismatic traditions.

In this passage: the phrase "the imparting of tricks" reduces rhetorical education to a mechanical transfer of devices. The surrounding argument insists on the opposite: oratory is "the gradual bringing to bear of the whole man," not a technique that can be handed over like a tool. The gerund's implication of a neat, one-way delivery is exactly what the sentence denies, which is why the choice of imparting rather than "teaching" or "training" is rhetorically pointed.
