The journey of confounded begins at a vessel being filled. The Latin verb fundere — to pour, to melt metal, to cast — descends from the PIE root *gheu- (to pour, especially the poured libation), the same root that gives Greek khein (to pour) and English kin like geyser, found (to cast metal), and font through Germanic and Latin channels. Prefixed with con- (together), confundere meant to pour together: to mix liquids, to mingle crowds, to jumble words — and by the natural slide of metaphor, to confuse, to disorder, and finally to destroy, since what is thoroughly mixed is thoroughly ruined. A general who pours his enemy's ranks together routs them; a cook who pours everything together spoils the dish. Rome heard no contradiction between mixture and ruin.

Through French into England:

  • Classical Latin range: confundere covered the whole slide — to mingle, to confuse, to disorder, to destroy, to put to shame. Cicero confounds arguments, Livy confounds battle-lines, moralists confound right with wrong. The verb was already both an error of the mind and a weapon of war.
  • Anglo-French bridge (13th c.): confundre crossed with the Conquest, leaning hard on the destructive half — to destroy, to ruin, to overthrow — the sense conquerors reach for first. Legal and chronicle French used it for defeating enemies and annulling claims.
  • Middle English settlement (c. 1300): confounden took root with the full Latin spread intact: to mix up and mistake, to perplex the mind, to overthrow in battle, to abash with shame. The Wycliffite Bible and chronicles alike wield it — tongues confounded at Babel, hosts confounded in the field, sinners confounded before judgment.
  • Early modern theology and drama: Reformation polemic made it a drumbeat — each side's pride, idols, and arguments duly confounded — while Shakespeare uses it across the whole keyboard, from lovers' identities confounded by disguise to curses (confound your faces) that already lean toward damnation.
  • The great bleaching (17th–19th c.): Confound it and confounded + noun settled into the role of the respectable oath — strong enough to vent, weak enough for the parlour. Sheridan's and Dickens's grumblers confound weather, waiters, nephews, and visitors; each comic use washed a little more brimstone out until only cosy irritation remained.
  • Modern split: twentieth-century standard English keeps both heirs without confusion between them: the participial adjective of bewilderment (confounded by the evidence, at home in reports and textbooks) and the fossilised damn (this confounded printer, at home in comedy and period prose). The poured-together root still shows through both — thoughts that will not separate, nuisances one wishes poured down the drain.

Sound shifts and form: Latin confundere lost its infinitive ending and softened through French: the stressed vowel levelled, the -re dropped, and Middle English re-Latinised the spelling while keeping the French pronunciation shape. The past participle confounded is perfectly regular — confound + -ed, spoken in three syllables — and its adjectival use is so old that no speaker feels the verb inside the curse. Few English words wear their etymology so honestly: say confounded slowly and you can still hear liquids glugging together in one doomed vessel.
