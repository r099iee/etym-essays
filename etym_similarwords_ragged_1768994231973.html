• Torn: Similar in being damaged cloth; differs because torn names the result of a single violent act — ripped at a specific moment — while ragged describes the accumulated, worn state of a garment, torn in many places over time.
  • Tattered: Similar in being worn into shreds; differs because tattered emphasizes hanging scraps and flapping pieces of cloth, while ragged emphasizes the uneven, jagged, broken edges and roughness of the surface.
  • Frayed: Similar in being worn at the edges; differs because frayed describes the edge unraveling thread by thread, a slow rubbing away at the boundary, while ragged covers the whole garment, with holes and tatters as well as frayed edges.
  • Threadbare: Similar in being worn by use; differs because threadbare is precise: the warp and weft have worn so thin that the threads show, the fabric transparent with use, while ragged is about broken, torn edges rather than visible weave.
  • Shabby: Similar in being worn and dingy; differs because shabby is the general word for worn, faded, and neglected through long use — it implies poverty and carelessness but not necessarily tears — while ragged implies actual torn and broken cloth.
  • Worn: Similar in being damaged by use; differs because worn is the broadest, mildest word, covering any sign of use or abrasion, while ragged is the extreme — worn past repair into tatters.
  • Tatty: Similar in being shabby and worn; differs because tatty is informal and mild, suggesting untidiness and cheapness rather than destitution, while ragged is stronger, suggesting real poverty and torn clothes.
  • Scruffy: Similar in being dirty and untidy; differs because scruffy is about general neglect — dirt, dishevelment, lack of care — while ragged is specifically about torn and broken cloth.
  • Moth-eaten: Similar in being damaged cloth; differs because moth-eaten names a specific cause — holes eaten by insects — and suggests old clothes stored too long, while ragged is worn and torn by use, not by moths.
  • Frazzled: Similar in being worn at the edges; differs because frazzled describes the edge worn into a fuzzy, straggling mass by rubbing (and, of a person, exhausted), while ragged describes the garment torn into uneven points.
  • Shaggy: Similar in being rough and tufted; differs because shaggy describes long, coarse, unruly hair or fur (and shaggy cloth), while ragged describes torn, broken cloth; the words are related historically — ragged entered English from Old Norse meaning "shaggy" — but now divide hair from tatters.
  • Jagged: Similar in being rough and uneven; differs because jagged describes sharp, pointed, irregular projections in any material — rock, glass, metal — while ragged is primarily a textile word for torn cloth, and only secondarily of broken outlines.
  • Rough: Similar in being uneven and not smooth; differs because rough is the very general word for any surface lacking smoothness, with no implication of tearing or poverty, while ragged implies broken, torn edges and (of clothes) social destitution.
  • Uneven: Similar in being irregular; differs because uneven is the plain, neutral description of irregularity, while ragged adds the image of broken, straggling, frayed edges — an unevenness that looks torn.
  • Unkempt: Similar in being untidy; differs because unkempt applies chiefly to hair or appearance left uncombed and disordered, while ragged applies to torn clothes and broken surfaces.
  • Scraggy: Similar in being rough and irregular; differs because scraggy describes a thin, scrawny, jagged outline (a scraggy neck), often of people or animals, while ragged describes torn cloth and, by extension, broken edges.
  • In tatters / in shreds: Similar in being torn to pieces; differs because these phrases name the end-state of complete destruction — the garment reduced to hanging strips — while ragged can describe a lesser, merely worn-and-torn state.
  • Haggard: Similar in being worn and gaunt; differs because haggard applies to a person's face and appearance — exhausted, gaunt, wild-eyed — while ragged applies to clothing and surfaces; but the two often appear together, as in the pale, thin wearer of a ragged shirt.
