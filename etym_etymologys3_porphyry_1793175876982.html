The voyage of porphyry begins in the sea, with a snail. Ancient Greek porphýra named the murex shellfish and the dramatic purple dye boiled from its glands, the costliest colour of the Bronze Age Mediterranean, worth more than silver weight for weight. The adjective porphýreos, purple, spread from dyed cloaks to anything dark-red and heaving: Homer's porphyreos sea, blood welling purple from wounds, rainbow and rainbow-hued cloth. Colour came first; stone followed the colour.

Rome made the stone imperial. Under Claudius or Tiberius, legionaries discovered the purple-speckled igneous rock at Mons Porphyrites in Egypt's Eastern Desert, and the emperors promptly monopolised it: only Caesar might quarry, carve, or coffin himself in porphyry. Pliny the Elder, who saw it new, called it porphyritēs, the purple stone, and marvelled at obelisks and statues no iron could easily cut, worked only with emery abrasive and endless slave-hours. Diocletian paved his palace at Split with it; Constantine's family slept in it; the porphyry chamber of Constantinople's palace gave the empire the phrase born in the purple, Porphyrogenitus, for legitimate princes.

Stages of the change:

  • Aegean purple (2nd millennium BCE): Minoan and Mycenaean dyers boil murex into purple, and porphýra names snail, dye, and dyed robe together, with no certain deeper etymology, likely a Pre-Greek substrate word.
  • Greek colour-word (8th–5th c. BCE): porphýreos colours seas, blood, and cloth in Homer and the tragedians, while the purple-speckled Egyptian stone inherits the adjective as its proper name.
  • Roman monopoly (1st–4th c. CE): marmor porphyrites becomes law, liturgy, and luxury at once; the quarries run day and night, and porphyry columns rise from Baalbek to Rome.
  • Medieval relic-stone (5th–15th c.): popes, emperors, and Norman kings carve new honours from old Roman porphyry, from Charlemagne's throne-stone to the sarcophagi of Palermo, and Old French porfire carries the word north.
  • Scientific widening (18th–20th c.): geologists abstract the texture from the colour: any rock with large phenocrysts in fine groundmass is porphyritic, and miners discover that copper-porphyry intrusions hold the world's great copper fortunes, from Chuquicamata to Bingham Canyon.

English met the word through French building-accounts and Italian antiquaries: Middle English porfurie already glitters in descriptions of Rome, and the modern spelling with -phyry restores the Greek -phyra ending that French had worn down. The fairy-tale palace of the source sentence, built of porphyry and roofed with gold, is thus the end of a three-thousand-year logic: purple snail to purple stone to purple power, until a Russian storyteller, needing the grandest of three palaces, reaches without hesitation for the emperors' own rock.
