Embattled is an adjective with three great historical senses braided together: (1) furnished with battlements, like a castle wall; (2) drawn up in battle array, ready to fight; and (3) — the dominant modern use — beset, beleaguered, under attack from all sides, whether by armies, critics, lawsuits, or misfortunes.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses the martial middle sense: fireworks rise ‘with a yell like an embattled army’ — an army arrayed for battle, banners flying, shouting its war-cry as it springs. The silver spears of fire are soldiers leaping to the assault, and embattled gives them discipline, fury, and noise at once.

Core meaning in detail: The word is built as en- (‘in, into’) + battle + -ed, literally ‘put into battle’ or ‘furnished with battle(-ments)’. Which of the two nouns — battle (combat) or battlement (parapet) — came first is a genuine chicken-and-egg of English word history, since both senses appear in the 14th–16th centuries and both descend from the same Old French root. Modern use has settled overwhelmingly on sense (3): the embattled prime minister, the embattled company, the embattled currency.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Beset by difficulties or opposition (primary modern sense, 20th c.): ‘the embattled mayor faced fresh allegations’. The bridge runs from the battlefield: a general ringed by enemies becomes any figure ringed by troubles — journalists, creditors, rivals. Journalism made this the default sense.
  • Drawn up in battle order (historical/literary, 16th c., Tolkien's sense): ‘an embattled army’ — troops marshalled rank on rank, ready to engage. The bridge is direct: en- + battle = placed in battle formation.
  • Furnished with battlements (architectural, 14th c.): ‘embattled towers’ — crowned with crenellated parapets, alternating merlons and gaps. The bridge runs through battlement: a wall prepared for battle, its notched crown giving archers shelter to shoot through.
  • Engaged in combat, fighting (archaic): ‘two embattled hosts’. A stronger shade of sense (2): not merely arrayed but already locked in struggle.
  • Heraldic — having a battlement-like edge (technical): in heraldry a line ‘embattled’ is drawn stepped like castle crenellations. The bridge is purely visual: the shield's border imitates the fortress wall.

Register and connotation: embattled is dignified and dramatic — headline English at its most sympathetic. To call someone embattled is usually to take their side faintly: they are the outnumbered defender, holding the walls. The architectural sense survives in guidebooks and poetry (‘embattled towers’), the military sense in historical and fantasy writing, but the ‘beset by troubles’ sense owns nine-tenths of modern usage. Headlines love it because it turns defendants into defenders.
