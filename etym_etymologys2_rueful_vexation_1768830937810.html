Derivation chain for RUEFUL (root word one of two): Stage 1 — PIE *kreu- "to strike, dash against, cause blood to flow" (reconstructed; pre-4th millennium BCE) — the root of physical blows and raw flesh. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hrewwaną "to grieve, distress, feel sorrow; literally to be struck within the heart" (reconstructed; before c. 500 CE) — the physical blow internalised as emotion; noun *hrewwō "grief, regret, sorrow." Stage 3 — Old English hrēow "sorrow, grief, regret, repentance" (noun) and hrēowian "to feel sorrow, to repent" (verb) (attested 8th–11th centuries). Stage 4 — Middle English rewe / reuwen "to regret, be sorry for; sorrow" (12th–15th centuries). Stage 5 — English rue "to regret; (archaic noun) sorrow" + the suffix -ful "full of" → rueful "full of regret or sorrow" (coined 14th century) ◄ TARGET. Note on the debate: The connection of *kreu- to the Germanic "grief" family is broadly accepted, but the intermediate chain showing exactly how a "strike" root produced *hrewwaną is reconstructed rather than attested; some scholars posit contamination with a separate root. No solid Sanskrit cognate for the "strike" sense is agreed upon, so that branch is omitted. The Germanic-to-English chain (stages 2–5) is secure.
Derivation chain for VEXATION (root word two of two): Stage 1 — PIE *weǵh- "to carry, convey, travel in a vehicle" (reconstructed; pre-4th millennium BCE) — one of the widest-spread Indo-European roots of motion. Stage 2 — Latin vehere "to carry, convey" (attested from the 3rd century BCE); its intensive formation vexāre "to shake, jolt, toss about, harass" (attested from Plautus onward, 3rd–2nd centuries BCE) — repeated, violent carrying, the image of a rough cart-ride. Stage 3 — Latin vexātiō, vexātiōnis "a shaking, a jolting, a harassing, an annoyance" (attested Classical Latin) — the abstract noun of vexāre. Stage 4 — Old French vexacion "torment, harassment, annoyance" (12th–13th centuries) — borrowed from the Latin noun. Stage 5 — Middle English vexacioun / vexation "physical torment or affliction, later mental annoyance" (entered 14th century). Stage 6 — Modern English vexation "the state of being annoyed or worried; the cause of such a state" ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain *weǵh- → vehere → vexāre → vexātiō → vexation is fully secure. No genuine Sanskrit or Germanic cognate of the intensive formation vexāre is attested; vexāre is the frequentative/intensive of vehere, and the sense shift from "jolting about" to "harassing" is well documented in Latin texts.
