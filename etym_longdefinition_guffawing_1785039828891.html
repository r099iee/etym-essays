GUFFAWING (present participle of the verb guffaw; also the noun guffaw) CORE SENSE. To guffaw is to laugh suddenly, loudly, and coarsely — an open-mouthed, explosive burst of laughter that erupts from the belly rather than the throat and carries a long way through open air. As a noun, a guffaw is such a laugh. The word combines three features that mark it off from every other English laughter word: suddenness (it bursts out without warning), loudness (it is meant to be heard at a distance), and coarseness (it is unrefined, unselfconscious, and often insensitive). A guffaw is the laugh of a person — or a giant — with no self-restraint and no consideration for others. LITERARY CONTEXT (The Hobbit, ch. 3). Deep in the storm, as the travellers crouch frightened among the cliffs, they can hear the stone-giants guffawing and shouting all over the mountainsides. The word does heavy atmospheric work in the scene. The giants are not merely laughing; they are guffawing, and the sound echoes among the peaks like a second form of thunder. The word's coarseness is essential to its tone: it paints the giants as mindless, elemental brutes, mountain-sized and childish, who treat the devastation of the storm as an uproarious game and are utterly indifferent to the terrified creatures cowering below. Their laughter is loud, unrefined, and unfeeling — the amusement of forces that cannot be reasoned with — and this single verb tells the reader more about the giants' monstrous detachment than any description could. The plural, repeated present participle (guffawing) adds volley after volley: not a single laugh but a sustained, echoing barrage indistinguishable from the thunder. POLYSEMY: THE FULL RANGE OF SENSES. - The laugh (primary sense). A sudden, loud, coarse, open-mouthed burst of laughter. To guffaw is to burst into such laughter. This is the sense in the Hobbit passage. - Figurative use for loud, hollow sound. In literary prose, guffaw can be transferred to anything that makes a loud, coarse, percussive noise resembling the laugh — the guffaw of thunder, of cannon, of breaking machinery. Bridge: onomatopoeic transfer from the human sound to any acoustically similar blast. - As a verb of manner/attitude. To guffaw at someone or something is to laugh at them contemptuously, boisterously, and without sympathy — the laughter of mockery as much as of mirth. Bridge: the coarseness of the laugh makes it natural for derision and bullying. - In humorous register, self-description. People say "I couldn't help guffawing" to describe losing all self-control in amusement — a laugh so violent it escapes the body's restraints. Bridge: the suddenness and physicality of the sound. ON THE SOUND ITSELF. The name is imitative of the sound: a deep, hollow, breathy peal that begins in the chest, bursts through the open lips, and trails off in a long open vowel — gu-ff-aw. English is unusually rich in words for laughter, and each has its own register: chuckle is low, soft, and inward, almost silent; chortle, coined by Lewis Carroll in Through the Looking-Glass (1871) as a blend of chuckle and snort, is a gleeful, half-suppressed, self-satisfied laugh; snicker and snigger are suppressed, derisive, and sneering; cackle is shrill, harsh, and broken, the laugh of a witch or a hen; roar is loud and deep but can be joyful or triumphant; belly-laugh is deep and wholehearted but genial. Guffaw alone combines suddenness, loudness, and coarseness. REGISTER AND CONNOTATION. Guffawing belongs to the vocabulary of the tavern, the farce, and the barracks rather than the drawing room. It marks the laugher as unrefined, hearty, and oblivious — sometimes jovial, more often coarse. In modern usage it often carries a faint negative edge: a guffaw is a laugh that disregards others, that cannot be controlled, and that may be cruel. Its Scots dialect origin (gawf, 16th century) kept it vivid and colloquial when it entered standard English in the 18th–19th centuries, and it has never lost that rough, folk flavour. WORD FAMILY AND DERIVATIVES. guffaw (verb and noun); guffawing (present participle and gerund); guffawed (past tense); guffawer (rare, one who guffaws). The word has no English cognate family and no PIE ancestry — it was coined in Scots from the imitation of the sound — though it is cousin in spirit to the other imitative laughter words cackle, chortle, snort, and haw-haw. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS (summary). - roar — loud and deep, but can be joyful, triumphant, or communal; guffaw — loud AND coarse AND sudden, often solitary and unfeeling. - belly-laugh — deep and wholehearted but genial and warm; guffaw — deep but coarse and unrestrained. - cackle — shrill, harsh, broken, hen-like, often female-coded; guffaw — deep, open, belly-driven. - chortle — gleeful, half-suppressed, self-satisfied; guffaw — open, explosive, unselfconscious. - chuckle — low, soft, inward, almost silent; guffaw — the loudest and most public of laughs. - snicker / snigger — suppressed, derisive, sneering, secretive; guffaw — open, coarse, unashamed. - laugh (neutral) — any vocal expression of amusement; guffaw — the specific loud, coarse, belly-driven subset. IN THE HOBBIT, the word is the giants' signature: the coarse, heartless, thunderous roar of amusement that tells the reader — without a word of description — exactly what the stone-giants are.
