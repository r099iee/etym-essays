Hellenism (noun) — the quality of being Greek, or the devotion to what is Greek: the civilization, culture, language and spirit of the Greeks, and the historical movement by which those things were spread abroad. The word is a chameleon of registers: it can mean the whole of ancient Greek civilization, the Hellenistic world created by Alexander’s conquests, the philologist’s term for Greek idiom, or — in the criticism of Arnold and Pater, the key inherited sense — the intellectual temper that values seeing things as they are, clarity, beauty and the free play of the mind.

Primary sense — Greek culture and civilization. At its widest, Hellenism is the totality of the achievement of the ancient Greeks — in Pater’s words on the same page, “the things of the intellect and the imagination for their own sake”. The word is used in this civilizational sense of the language, the art, the philosophy and the religion of the Hellenes, and of their influence wherever Greek colonists, kings and books carried them.

  • Secondary sense — the Hellenistic world. Since the nineteenth century, and especially since Droysen’s Geschichte des Hellenismus (1836), “Hellenism” has also named the period and the culture that followed Alexander the Great (d. 323 BCE): the fusion of Greek with Egyptian, Syrian, Persian and Indian elements in the kingdoms of the Ptolemies, Seleucids and Antigonids, the world of Alexandria and Antioch, of the Septuagint and of “Hellenistic” art, religion and science. The narrower adjective “Hellenistic” was coined precisely to distinguish this post-classical world from classical “Hellenic” civilization.
  • Tertiary sense — the critical ideal. In Victorian criticism “Hellenism” designates a temper, not a period: Matthew Arnold’s Culture and Anarchy (1869) opposed Hellenism — “spontaneity of consciousness”, the desire to see things as they really are — to Hebraism, the “strictness of conscience”, the discipline of conduct. Pater’s sentence, praising the humanists’ enthusiasm for intellect and imagination “for their own sake”, is squarely in this Arnoldian register: Hellenism is the love of knowledge and beauty as ends, not means.
  • Quarterly sense — religion and language. The Greek noun hellēnismós originally meant “use of the Greek language, Greek idiom” (the opposite of barbarismós), and in later antiquity, especially in the emperor Julian, it came to mean the Greek religion — paganism — as against Christianity. Hellenism in modern religious scholarship therefore also denotes the Hellenization of Judaism (Hellenistic Judaism, the diaspora and the Septuagint) and of the early Christian world.
  • Semantic bridge. The whole family hangs on Hellēn (Ἕλλην), the self-name of the Greeks — mythically the son of Deucalion and ancestor of the Hellenes — with the abstract suffix -ismos forming a noun for “being Greek”. The sense history is a history of widening: dialect; people; culture; religious identity; civilizational ideal. What never changes is the reference to the Greeks’ own name for themselves.

Modern register. In current English the word is thoroughly bookish: one speaks of the Hellenism of the Byzantine court, of Renaissance Hellenism, or of Germano-Hellenism in Goethe’s generation; the everyday synonym is simply “Greek culture”. But the Arnoldian sense survives wherever criticism talks of “the Greek spirit” — clarity, measure, proportion, the free play of intellect and imagination — which is exactly the sense Pater invokes in the context on this card.
