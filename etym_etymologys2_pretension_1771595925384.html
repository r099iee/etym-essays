Chain for PRETENSION: 1. Proto-Indo-European *ten- "to stretch, extend" (5th millennium BCE; attested across the family — Sanskrit tanóti "he stretches," Greek teínein "to stretch," Latin tendere "to stretch," Germanic *þanjan- whence English thin "stretched out, attenuated"). 2. Latin tendere "to stretch, spread out" (classical, attested from the 3rd c. BCE in Plautus). 3. Latin praetendere "to stretch in front, spread before; to hold up as a screen; to put forward as an excuse or allegation" (prae- "before, in front" + tendere; classical, used in forensic senses by Cicero). 4. Latin praetēnsus, past participle of praetendere, "stretched in front; alleged, pretended." 5. Medieval Latin praetēnsiō, praetēnsiōnis "a putting forward, an allegation, a claim" (scholastic Latin). 6. Middle English pretensioun "assertion, allegation, objection, intention, signification" (mid-15th c.). 7. English PRETENSION "a claim, especially one regarded as unearned" (modern senses from c. 1600) — OUR CARD. Notes: - The Old French channel pretrendre "to lay claim" gave English pretend (late 14th c.), pretender "a claimant" (16th c.; the Stuart Pretenders 1688–1745), and pretense/pretence (early 15th c. via Anglo-French); French prétention was re-borrowed into English in the 15th c., and French prétentieux gave English pretentious (1852). - The parallel Latin verb tenēre "to hold" (tenacious, tenant, contain, content) is a sister formation from the same *ten- via the "stretch so as to keep taut" shift; it is not the direct ancestor of pretension. - No major dispute attaches to the *ten- root itself; the interesting historical question is the semantic bridge from "stretch in front" to "allege, claim," which is securely attested in classical Latin usage.
