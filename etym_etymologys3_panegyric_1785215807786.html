Evolution of “panegyric.” The word is a fossilised festival: its history runs from the great panhellenic gatherings of ancient Greece, through the courts of imperial Rome and Renaissance Europe, to the Victorian dedication page that moved a biographer to superlatives. At every stage it means the same thing — public, formal, full-dress praise — but the stage, the audience, and the politics change.

1. The Greek festival. The root is the Greek verb ageírō, “to gather together, to collect” (from the PIE root *h₂ger- “to gather”), which also produced agorá, the assembly and marketplace. Joined to pan- “all,” it gives panēgyris, “the gathering of all the people” — the great national festival assembly, such as those held at Olympia, Delphi, Isthmia, and at the Panathenaea in Athens, where the whole city gathered for games, sacrifice, and speeches. It was at these gatherings that orators practised epideictic (display) oratory: the panēgyrikós lógos, the festival speech, in which the orator celebrated the city, its heroes, its ancestors, and its values before the assembled people.

2. Isocrates and the genre. The genre received its classic name and model from Isocrates, whose famous speech of 380 BCE — urging the Greek states to unite against Persia — was called the Panēgyrikós. From that moment the word named not just any festival speech but a formal composition of high praise, rhetorical, elevated, and public. The rhetorical handbooks (Aristotle's Rhetoric, then Hermogenes and Menander) codified the panegyric as the crown of epideictic oratory, with set topics — noble birth, great deeds, virtue, and benefits to the city — that would survive for two thousand years.

3. Imperial Rome. Rome took the genre into politics. The panegyricus became the speech of praise delivered before the emperor, the highest display of courtly eloquence. Pliny the Younger's Panegyricus of 100 CE, praising the emperor Trajan, is the great surviving model, and the collection known as the Panegyrici Latini (3rd–4th centuries) preserves a dozen such speeches to emperors. Here the festival crowd is replaced by the court, and praise becomes political: the panegyrist praises the ruler's virtues the better to bind him to them — the genre's characteristic double function of compliment and counsel.

4. Renaissance and early modern English. The humanist revival of classical rhetoric restored the panegyric to the courts and academies of Renaissance Europe: kings and cardinals were greeted with grand Latin panegyrics, and the genre passed into English letters in the 17th century, first recorded c. 1603. In the neoclassical 17th–18th centuries the panegyric was a stock form — poems and prose celebrating monarchs, victories, and worthies — though satirists soon mocked its excess: the great age of the panegyric is also the age of its parody.

5. The 19th century and the present context. By the 19th century the word had settled into criticism and biography as a sharp label for formal praise. It is precisely in this register that the present context uses it: the biographer of John Stuart Mill writes that the dedication of On Liberty to Harriet Taylor — hailing her as “the inspirer, and in part the author, of all that is best in my writings” — “contains the most extraordinary panegyric on a woman that any philosopher has ever penned.” The word here carries its full ancient weight: a public, formal, almost ceremonial tribute, intended not for the privacy of affection but for the world.

6. The modern ironic sense. Today the word is rare in speech and kept in literary and critical writing. It contrasts with eulogy (funeral-specific), encomium (literary tribute), and flattery (insincere), retaining the sense of a formal, presumptively sincere encomium — and, in ironic use, a criticism of excess: to call a biography “a panegyric” is often to say it flatters rather than judges.
