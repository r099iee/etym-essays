GOWN denotes a long, flowing garment worn over other clothing. In the source text — a satin gown being embroidered for a court ball — it means the formal woman’s dress of the nineteenth century; more broadly the word covers (1) a woman’s long formal dress (evening gown, wedding gown, ball gown), (2) a ceremonial or professional robe (academic, judicial, clerical), (3) a loose garment for sleeping or indoor wear (nightgown, dressing gown), and (4) the clinical hospital gown. Historically “gown” was simply “a loose outer garment”, worn by men and women alike; the modern meanings are the product of centuries of narrowing and specialisation into public ceremony, feminine formality and institutional utility.

1. Primary modern sense — the woman’s formal dress. From the eighteenth century “gown” became the standard word for a woman’s principal dress: the hooped mantua gown and sack gown of the 1700s, the empire-waisted gowns of Jane Austen’s era, the crinolined and bustled gowns of the Victorians, the beaded gowns of the twentieth century. Today the word attaches to the most formal items of women’s dress:

  • Bridal sense: the wedding gown — dominant in modern commerce and culture; the bridal industry’s central artefact, traditionally white since the nineteenth century (following Queen Victoria’s 1840 wedding), and the subject of a continuing couture and retail trade.
  • Evening gown: a woman’s long formal dress for ceremonial evenings, galas, balls and red carpets; “gowned”, as an adjective, means dressed in such a garment (“the gowned guests”).
  • Ball gown and court gown: the most ceremonial uses, with full skirts and trains; the source text’s “satin gown for the loveliest of the Queen’s maids-of-honour to wear at the next Court-ball” is precisely this dress, and the phrase “Court-ball” anchors the encounter between the poor seamstress and the court’s luxury.

2. The robe of office and profession. Since the medieval period the gown has been the dress of ceremony and institution: the academic gown (graduation and formal university occasions), the judge’s and barrister’s gowns in court, the cleric’s gown in church, the livery and civic gowns of officials and aldermen. The collocation “town and gown” (established at English universities from the eighteenth century) opposes the university — whose members wear gowns — to the surrounding town, and has come to name the whole relationship between academic institutions and their host cities.

  • Academic gown: worn at graduation ceremonies and formal university occasions; distinct shapes at Oxford, Cambridge and other universities (the “commoners’ gown” with open sleeves, the scholars’ gown with wings, the doctors’ gowns trimmed with fur), and now a standard of commencement ceremonies worldwide.
  • Legal gown: black gowns of the judiciary and the bar in common-law jurisdictions — garment as institutional identity, prescribed by court dress conventions and centuries of practice.
  • Clerical gown: the preaching and choral gowns of clergy and choristers, carrying the sense of ministry and office.

3. Household and clinical senses. The nightgown (a loose, usually sleeved garment for bed, from the eighteenth century), the dressing gown (worn over underclothes before dressing), the morning gown of earlier centuries, and the hospital gown (the open-backed garment issued to patients, twentieth century) show the word’s range spanning the most ceremonial and the most mundane clothing. In all these the gown is the loose covering put on over — or instead of — day dress.

4. The semantic bridge. All senses descend from the late-medieval “loose outer garment worn over other clothes”. Wherever that loose outer garment became institutional (robes of the university, bench and pulpit) or feminine-formal (the decorated dress of the eighteenth-century lady) or utilitarian (night, morning and hospital wear), the word followed. The narrowing happened in two movements: in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries the male gown retreated to office and ceremony while the female gown grew ever more decorated and formal; in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the fashion sense attached to the most formal women’s dresses, leaving “dress” for the everyday and “gown” for the ceremonial.

5. Grammar and register. Count noun; plural “gowns” (the source uses the singular with “a satin gown”). Register: formal-to-neutral — “gown” is a dressier word than “dress” and a plainer word than “robe”; it belongs to wedding, graduation and judicial vocabulary as much as to fashion. The word has also produced the verb “to gown” (to dress in a gown) and the derivative “gowned”, used of surgeons, judges and guests alike.
