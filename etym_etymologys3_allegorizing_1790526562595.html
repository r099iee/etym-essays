The evolutionary trajectory of allegorizing (from Greek ἀλληγορίζω to Late Latin allegorizare, Old French allegoriser, and Early Modern English allegorize) spans over two thousand five hundred years of philosophical debate regarding how language conceals, decodes, and reconstructs reality through figurative substitution.

1. Greek Etymological Components (*h₂élyos and *h₂eǵ-)

  • The First Element (*h₂élyos to ἄλλος): The prefixal component derives from the Proto-Indo-European pronominal root *h₂élyos, meaning "other, another, or different." In Ancient Greek, this developed regularly into the adjective ἄλλος (allos, "other, different"), cognate with Latin alius ("other", source of alias, alien, alibi) and Old English elles (source of modern English else).
  • The Second Element (*h₂eǵ- to ἀγορά and ἀγορεύω): The base verbal component derives from the PIE root *h₂eǵ- ("to drive, lead, bring together"), which formed the Greek noun ἀγορά (agora, the public gathering, assembly, or marketplace). From agora arose the verb ἀγορεύω (agoreuein), meaning "to speak publicly in the civic assembly, to harangue, or to proclaim."
  • The Hellenistic Synthesis (ἀλληγορία to ἀλληγορίζω): Compounding these two elements yielded the Greek compound noun ἀλληγορία (allēgoria, literally "speaking otherwise" or "saying one thing publicly while meaning another"). By the 1st century BCE (attested in the rhetorical treatises of Philo of Alexandria, Cicero, Quintilian, and Plutarch), the denominative verb ἀλληγορίζω (allēgorizō, "to interpret as an allegory, to allegorize") was formalized in Hellenistic literary scholarship.

2. Patristic & Medieval Latin Adoption (Allegorizare)

  • Late Latin Naturalization (4th c. CE): As early Christian theologians sought to reconcile the Hebrew scriptures (Old Testament) with Christian theology, they adopted Hellenistic Jewish interpretive techniques pioneered by Philo. Church Fathers such as Origen of Alexandria, Saint Jerome, and Saint Augustine adopted Greek allēgoria directly into Church Latin as allegoria, creating the Late Latin ecclesiastical verb allegorizare ("to explain or interpret scripture allegorically").
  • The Scholastic Hermeneutic System: Throughout the Middle Ages, allegorizare represented the foundational cognitive tool of monastic and scholastic scholarship. It allowed theologians to read historical Old Testament narratives (such as Abraham sacrificing Isaac or the Exodus from Egypt) as prefiguring the New Testament passion of Christ and the salvation of the Church.

3. French Mediation & English Borrowing (14th–16th c.)

  • Old / Middle French allegoriser: In 14th-century France, the Latin verb entered the vernacular as allegoriser, used both in biblical commentary and in vernacular adaptations of secular allegorical masterpieces such as the Roman de la Rose and commentaries on Ovid's Metamorphoses (Ovide Moralisé).
  • English Reception (1530s): The word was imported into Early Modern English during the English Reformation and the Renaissance humanist revival. Attested in the 1530s in the writings of Protestant reformers and humanist scholars (such as William Tyndale and Thomas More), allegorize described both the translation of pagan mythology into Christian moral lessons and the critical debate over the limits of figurative biblical interpretation.
  • Participle & Gerund Formation: The present participle and gerund form allegorizing became standard in Elizabethan and Jacobean prose, appearing in literary prefaces (such as George Puttenham's The Arte of English Poesie, 1589) to describe the continuous rhetorical practice of weaving allegories.

4. Romantic Skepticism & Modern Theoretical Rehabilitation

  • The 19th-Century Romantic Rift: During the late 18th and early 19th centuries, German Idealists (Goethe, Schelling) and English Romantics (Coleridge) drew a sharp, derogatory dichotomy between allegorizing (dismissed as mechanical, cerebral, and arbitrary decoding) and symbolism (celebrated as an organic, living synthesis of particular form and universal essence). This caused allegorizing to acquire a temporary connotation of dry, didactic pedantry.
  • 20th-Century Critical Renaissance: In the 20th century, the term underwent profound philosophical rehabilitation. Walter Benjamin's The Origin of German Tragic Drama (1928) demonstrated that allegory reflects historical melancholy, rupture, and the transient nature of material reality. Later, post-structuralist theorists like Paul de Man and Hayden White demonstrated that all narrative history and literary prose rely fundamentally upon allegorizing—transforming contingent chronological events into structured semantic architectures of meaning.
