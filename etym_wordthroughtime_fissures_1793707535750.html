- Early 17th Century (Anatomy/Geology) - First attested in English, primarily referring to a natural cleft or groove in the body (especially the brain) or a crack in the earth. Connotation: Scientific, descriptive, neutral.
- 18th-19th Century (Expanded Scientific Use) - Usage broadened to include any deep crack or cleavage in rock, ice, or other solid materials. Also used in dentistry. Connotation: Still scientific, precise, objective.
- Late 19th-20th Century (Metaphorical Extension) - Began to be used metaphorically to describe divisions or breaks in abstract concepts like social unity, political parties, or relationships. Connotation: Suggests weakness, instability, potential for rupture, often negative.
- 21st Century (Contemporary Usage) - Continues in all established senses, with metaphorical use becoming increasingly common in journalism and political commentary. Connotation: Retains scientific precision for literal uses; for metaphorical uses, it implies significant, often problematic, underlying divisions.
