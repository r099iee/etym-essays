- Early usage (13th c.): “full of wiles”, crafty, cunning — a cleverness that could be admired (outwitting enemies by stratagem) or condemned (devilish guile) — an ambivalent register.
- Elizabethan era: perception hardens toward the negative — the “wily” figure is above all a schemer and deceiver, though theatrically fascinating (Shakespeare's villains); cleverness becomes suspect.
- 18th–19th c.: settles as a didactic, fable word — “the wily fox” — conveying sly, self-serving cunning that is ultimately punished in story-morals; the register is mildly literary.
- Modern English: remains pejorative but softens into admiring-wariness — “a wily veteran” implies respect for craft — while “wily” keeps its slightly archaic, storyteller flavour in contemporary prose.
