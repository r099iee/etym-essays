The history of fanned begins in the Indo-European root *h₂weh₁-, a verb of moving air meaning simply "to blow". This modest root proved one of the most prolific in the family: its descendants blow their way across the whole Indo-European map. The participial noun *h₂wéh₁n̥t-, "the blowing one", gave the wind its name in every major branch — Sanskrit vāta- and vāyú- (the god Vāyu), Avestan vāta-, Hittite ḫuwant-, Greek ánemos (whence English anemometer and anemone), Latin ventus (whence Italian vento, French vent, Spanish viento, Portuguese vento, Romanian vânt), Lithuanian vėjas, Slavic větъrъ (Russian veter, Polish wiatr, Czech vítr), and Proto-Germanic *windaz, the ancestor of English wind. Persian bād carries the same ancestry eastward. The word for the element is thus, everywhere, literally "the blower" — the wind conceived as the thing that blows. But the specific ancestor of fan is a more specialized derivative of the same root: *h₂weh₁-nó-, "a blowing instrument", which became Latin vannus, the winnowing basket or shovel. The winnower's art was to toss threshed grain into the air so that the breeze — or the sweep of the winnower's own implement — blew the lighter chaff away, leaving the heavy grain to fall back. The word vannus then travelled two roads. Within Romance it survived in Old French van and related forms for the winnowing basket, and Latin ventilāre, "to fan, to winnow, to expose to the wind", lives on in English ventilate and ventilator. More importantly for English, the word was borrowed directly from Latin into Old English as fann, itself meaning a winnowing basket or fan; the Latin v- becoming Old English f- is an ordinary development in such learned borrowings, seen also in pairs like vallum and wall. The shift from winnowing basket to fan was natural: both are flat, curved implements that move air, and a hand-fan is simply a small, decorative winnowing tool. In Middle English the word broadened considerably. The noun fan(ne) named the winnowing shovel in the barn and, by easy extension, any instrument for moving air — the cooling hand-fan of the lady's chamber and, eventually, the powered devices of the industrial age. The verb fannen carried the full agricultural sense, "to winnow", and from it the more general "to blow upon, to ventilate, to cool". The decisive figurative step came in the sixteenth century with the phrase to fan the flames: since a fire fed with air burns more fiercely, to fan anything became, by metaphor, to rouse, inflame, and increase it — fanning anger, fanning hope, fanning controversy. This is the sense that gives the goblins' song its special cruelty: the fiery breeze does not merely dry and singe the feathers, it actively feeds the blaze that destroys the birds. Alongside the fire sense, the shape sense grew. Because the ribs of an open hand-fan radiate in a semicircle, the verb to fan (and the participle fanned) came to describe anything opening into that geometry: a peacock fanned its tail, troops fanned out across a battlefield, a gambler fanned a hand of cards, search parties fanned out from a hill. This geometric meaning is comparatively late — the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries made it productive — but it has become the dominant everyday use in modern English. The two strands, fire and shape, now run side by side, and fanned is able to mean either "blown upon so as to burn" or "opened into a semicircular array", or, as in Tolkien's line, both at once. Socio-cultural factors have shaped the word's history as much as sound change. The fan itself was an instrument of European courts and fashionable society from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, an object of gesture and flirtation, and the vocabulary around it (fan-work, fan-light, fan-vaulting) carried the same radiating geometry into architecture and design. The verb kept pace: fanning oneself became the emblem of patience, boredom, or coquetry. In the twentieth century the word acquired new, non-etymological neighbours — the sports fan and the fan club, from fanatic — which share nothing with this root and must be kept apart, yet whose near-identical sound makes the modern semantic field feel richer and more ambiguous than it really is. Phonetically the word is one of the quietest in the family. There is no significant sound shift from Latin vannus to modern fan beyond the v-to-f correspondence at the borrowing point; the vowels shorten and the spelling simplifies, but the word has remained monosyllabic and uncomplicated for over a thousand years. The meaning, by contrast, has been in constant motion, and the history of fanned is really the history of a single agricultural gesture — the winnower's sweep — being successively borrowed for cooling, for feeding fire, for rousing passion, and finally for the pure geometry of radiating lines. Tolkien's line, in which the feathers are simultaneously a fan spread open and fuel for a breeze that burns, is a perfect summary of the word's double inheritance: the shape of the instrument that scatters chaff, and the fire that the same instrument can feed.
