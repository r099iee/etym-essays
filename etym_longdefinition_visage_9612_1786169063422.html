EXHAUSTIVE LEXICOGRAPHICAL & POLYSEMOUS ANALYSIS: VISAGE

Visage is the word for a face that has been looked at. Where plain 'face' names the anatomical front of the head, visage names the face as an object of sight and regard — the countenance seen, read and remembered. In the Cormac McCarthy passage quoted on this card ('All history present in that visage, the child the father of the man'), the word performs exactly this work: the face is a legible document, every hardship of a life written in its lines. Below is the complete exegesis of its senses.

SECTION 1: THE PRIMARY SENSE — COUNTENANCE AND EXPRESSION

1. The face as seen. A visage is a person's face, features, and expression considered as a whole — the visible surface by which character, mood, age and identity are read.

  • Legibility: unlike 'face', 'visage' implies that the face can be read like a text; it is the look, the bearing, the total appearance — 'a man of stern visage', 'a visage lined with care'.
  • Physical description: in literary description it ranges from the sublime to the monstrous — a 'visage' can be beautiful, terrible, deathly, or angelic; the word adds solemnity and scale to the description.
  • Expression: it often means the cast of the features at a given moment — 'his visage darkened' means his expression became grim — blending the permanent face and the passing look.


2. High-register and archaic colouring. In modern English visage is consciously formal, literary, archaic or mock-heroic. It survives in the registers of epic, romance, Gothic fiction, and high tragedy; everyday speech says 'face'.

  • Shakespeare: the dramatist uses 'visage' for dignity and menace — 'I saw Othello's visage in his mind' (the Moor's face is re-created by love); 'a fair visage' and 'a fell visage' populate the plays.
  • Romantic and Victorian prose: Dickens and the Gothic novelists use 'visage' for faces of horror and pity; Wordsworth's 'The Child is Father of the Man' — echoed in the card's context — treats the face of childhood as containing all future history.
  • Modern use: 'visage' now signals stylistic elevation or irony; a fantasy novel or a solemn headline uses it to raise the tone, and Cormac McCarthy's austere prose uses it to give the face the weight of scripture.


SECTION 2: EXTENDED, TECHNICAL AND METAPHORIC SENSES

1. The face of non-human things. By metaphor, visage can name the front, surface or appearance of anything — the 'visage' of a mountain, a landscape, the sea, a building, or a historical period. The word lends a face, and therefore a personality, to the faceless.

  • Landscape and place: 'the visage of the land' means the land's whole visible character — its countenance.
  • Abstract history: a century can wear a visage; in political rhetoric, a regime's 'visage' is its public face, its front.


2. Heraldic and numismatic usage. In heraldry, a 'visage' (or 'visaged') describes a face shown in a shield or crest; on coins and medals the 'visage' is the portrait of a ruler. Both uses stress the face as official, public representation — the face of authority stamped on metal or blazoned on a shield.

3. Related false friends and compounds. French visage is still the everyday word for 'face'; 'vis-à-vis' ('face to face') preserves the same root as a loan-phrase. The English word is kin to a large family from Latin vidēre 'to see': vision, visible, vista, evidence, provident, survey, revise — all built on the act of seeing that makes a face a 'visage'.

4. The semantic bridge. The whole meaning of the word is contained in its etymology: Latin visus, 'seen, sight, appearance'. A visage is not a face you have; it is a face that is beheld. The word therefore always implies an observer, and it always implies that the face tells a story — which is precisely why McCarthy's sentence works: the boy's visage contains all history, because a visage is by definition a face that has been read.

SECTION 3: THE WORD IN THE CONTEXT

In the passage, 'All history present in that visage, the child the father of the man' — the visage is the child's face as a compendium of every future fate; 'the child the father of the man' inverts the Wordsworthian line so that the face of the boy already contains the man he will become. The word's connotation of readable, dignified, almost fateful appearance is doing all the work: this is not a face, it is a destiny written in features.
