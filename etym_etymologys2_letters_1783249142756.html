The root word letters is the plural of letter, and letter is a Latin culture-word of uncertain, probably non-PIE origin. Two rival theories, neither certain: - Theory 1 (favoured by many scholars): borrowed via Etruscan from Ancient Greek διφθέρα (diphthera) 'skin, hide; writing material' (a leather scroll) → hypothetical Etruscan intermediary *liðra 'writing tablet' (with a d→l shift) → Latin littera 'a letter of the alphabet; a writing'. - Theory 2 (ancient, still cited): from Latin linō, linere 'to smear, anoint' — wax tablets were 'smeared' before being written on; if true, the ultimate root would be PIE *slei- 'slimy, sticky' (cf. English slime). Attested core (secure regardless of origin): Latin littera / litera 'a letter of the alphabet; document, writing' → plural litterae 'writing, records, letters, literature, learning' → litterae humaniores 'the more humane letters' (ancestor of the humanities and of Oxford's Literae Humaniores) → Old French letre / lettre 'alphabetic sign; epistle; learning' → Anglo-French letre → Middle English lettre 'letter' (13th century) → English letter → plural letters 'literary culture, learning' (the institutional plural sense, modelled on Latin litterae and French lettres). Warning: English letter 'one who lets' is a different word, from Old English lǣtan 'to let, allow', unrelated to Latin littera despite identical spelling; the native Old English word for 'alphabetic sign' was bōcstæf ('book-staff'), displaced by letter in the 13th century.
