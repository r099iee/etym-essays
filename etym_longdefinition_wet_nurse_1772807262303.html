In the household arrangements that surround Dai-yu's arrival in Dream of the Red Chamber, one of the two people closest to her is her old wet-nurse, Nannie Wang (the other a young maid, Snowgoose). A wet-nurse is a woman hired or retained to breastfeed and care for another woman's child; in elite Qing households such women were staples of nursery life, and they often formed deep, lifelong bonds with their charges. The novel treats Nannie Wang not as a mere employee but as a quasi-parental figure — trusted companion, emotional anchor, and living link to Dai-yu's earliest years — whose presence softens the girl's transplantation into the vast Jia mansion. The word thus carries intimacy and hierarchical service at once: nourishment, loyalty, and the peculiar family authority of someone who has literally sustained a child's life. The core meaning of wet-nurse is 'a woman who breastfeeds and cares for another's child.' The compound is transparent: wet marks the milk-giving function, nurse the care of a child; the whole names a specific, historically vital household role that long predates the English phrase itself and existed in every stratified society where a wealthy mother might delegate nursing. The word is polysemous, though its senses radiate from a single occupational core:
  • Historical occupational sense: the hired or retained woman who suckles an infant — an institution of premodern elite childcare across Europe and China. The novel's 'old wet-nurse' is such a woman retained long past weaning, into her charge's later childhood.
  • Verb sense (chiefly historical/formal): to wet-nurse, 'to suckle and care for another's child'; also the gerund wet-nursing, naming the practice itself.
  • Contrastive technical sense: wet-nurse is defined against dry-nurse — a woman who minds an infant without breastfeeding; the pair together map the two branches of infant care and are first attested as a contrast in the 17th century.
  • Metaphorical sense (informal, modern): to wet-nurse a project, company, or plan is to nurture it protectively through its early vulnerable stages, as if feeding it; it can also mean to over-protect or micro-manage.
  • Modern replacement terms: in contemporary discourse the literal role is often called a milk donor, surrogate breastfeeder, or milk mother, since wet-nurse now sounds historical or archaic.
The compound is a pure English formation that became necessary when the word nurse, under its own broadening, could no longer guarantee the breastfeeding function. Older English nurse (from Old French norrice, Latin nutrix) had meant precisely a wet-nurse; as nurse spread to medical and general caregiving, English built the explicit compound wet-nurse (17th century) to keep the milk-giving sense available, in strict opposition to dry-nurse. Related roles differ sharply: a nanny need not breastfeed; a nursemaid provides general childcare; a foster-mother implies legal or social fostering; a governess teaches; a milk donor provides expressed milk through medical channels without feeding the child directly. Wet-nurse alone pins the role to milk-giving and to the bodily intimacy that often produced lasting influence over aristocratic children. Register is historical, anthropological, and literary; the hyphenated compound is standard in discussions of premodern elite childcare and still appears in contemporary prose when the specific practice is meant. Collocations: hire a wet-nurse, serve as wet-nurse to, her old wet-nurse, wet-nursing, and the contrastive pair wet-nurse / dry-nurse. It is neither slang nor pejorative, though in modern usage it often reads as period-flavoured or slightly clinical depending on context — exactly the dignified, archaic quality the translator needs for Nannie Wang's role.
