- Late 14th c. — Middle English emergence: The verb peplen emerged from the noun peple (via Anglo-Norman peupler), meaning to populate or furnish a city, kingdom, or region with inhabitants.
- 15th–16th c. — Renaissance expansion: Widely used in Tudor chronicles and biblical translations (such as the Geneva Bible) to describe God populating the earth or rulers establishing subjects across provinces.
- 17th c. — Poetic and imaginative flowering: Adopted by Shakespeare, Milton, and seventeenth-century poets to describe populating the imagination, dreams, or poetic landscapes (e.g. Milton’s angels peopling heaven).
- 18th c. — Enlightenment historiography: Maintained by historians like Hume and Gibbon as the standard, dignified term for prehistoric migrations and demographic expansion across nations.
- 19th c. — Romantic naturalism: Employed by Romantic poets (Keats, Shelley, Wordsworth) to depict the natural world as animated and ‘peopled’ with living creatures, spirits, or memories.
- 20th–21st c. — Literary preservation vs bureaucratic shift: While everyday administrative and demographic prose transitioned to populated, peopled was preserved as an elevated, expressive literary term for creative, historical, and psychological contexts.
