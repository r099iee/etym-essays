Romanovs (plural proper noun) refers to the members, lineage, and historical dynasty of the House of Romanov, the second and final imperial ruling dynasty of Russia, which held monarchical power from 1613 until its overthrow in the Russian Revolution of 1917.

The term encompasses deep political, dynastic, cultural, and historical dimensions:

  • The Imperial Ruling House (1613–1917): The Romanov dynasty was founded when the sixteen-year-old boyar Mikhail Romanov was elected Tsar by the National Assembly (Zemsky Sobor) in 1613, bringing an end to the chaotic and destructive "Time of Troubles". Over three centuries, eighteen Romanov autocrats ruled Russia, transforming a landlocked, medieval Muscovite kingdom into a vast Eurasian empire spanning one-sixth of the globe's land surface. Major monarchs included Peter the Great (who modernized Russia and founded Saint Petersburg), Catherine the Great (who expanded the empire into Crimea and Poland), Alexander I (who defeated Napoleon), Alexander II (the "Tsar Liberator" who emancipated the serfs), and Nicholas II.
  • Genealogical Lineage (Holstein-Gottorp-Romanov): While known universally as the Romanovs, the direct male line ended in 1762 with the death of Empress Elizabeth. The crown passed to the House of Holstein-Gottorp (descended from Peter the Great’s daughter Anna), creating the cadet branch technically known as Holstein-Gottorp-Romanov, which continued to use the Romanov surname until the fall of the monarchy.
  • The Russian Autocratic System: In political and historical discourse, the Romanovs represents the quintessential model of divine-right absolute autocracy (samoderzhavie). Unlike Western European constitutional monarchs, the Romanov tsars wielded unchecked legislative, executive, and judicial authority, closely intertwined with the Russian Orthodox Church, where the Tsar was venerated as the "Father of the Nation" (Tsar-Batyushka) and God's earthly defender of the faith.
  • The "Twilight of the Romanovs" (Historical Era): In cultural and social history, the final decades of Romanov rule (spanning the late 19th century through 1917 under Alexander III and Nicholas II) represent a poignant, tumultuous epoch. It was characterized by extreme social contradictions: dazzling artistic and theatrical blossoming (such as Chekhov's drama, Stanislavski's Method acting at the Moscow Art Theatre, Diaghilev's Ballets Russes, and the Silver Age of Russian poetry), contrasted against rigid state censorship, peasant poverty, revolutionary terrorism, and imperial ossification.
  • Cultural Symbol of Opulence and Tragedy: In popular culture and literature, the name Romanov evokes the pinnacle of aristocratic luxury (the Winter Palace, imperial balls, Fabergé eggs) juxtaposed against the harrowing tragedy of the dynasty's collapse in World War I and the execution of Emperor Nicholas II and his family by Bolshevik revolutionaries at Yekaterinburg in July 1918.

Semantic Bridges and Polysemy Development:

The name Romanov shifted from an ordinary patronymic surname ("descendant of Roman") to an imperial brand, and ultimately into a historical metonym. During the 17th and 18th centuries, the name became inextricably linked with the creation of the modern Russian state. In 19th- and 20th-century Western historiography and literature, "the Romanovs" expanded beyond the individuals themselves to designate the entire socio-political apparatus of Russian Tsardom. When contemporary writers speak of "Russia in the twilight of the Romanovs," the word encapsulates an entire civilization on the precipice of revolutionary destruction—a world of imperial majesty, spiritual searching, and authoritarian control.
