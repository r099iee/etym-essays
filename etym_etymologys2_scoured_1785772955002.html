The history of scour runs through two interlocking streams, and the relationship between them is debated; the major dictionaries describe the origin as 'of uncertain origin; probably a blend'. No single attested chain is beyond dispute, and both candidate paths are given here. The most commonly accepted path for the cleaning verb runs through Romance.

Primary chain (cleaning sense, Romance transmission):
1. Latin cūra 'care, concern, attention, healing' — of uncertain further origin. No securely reconstructed PIE root is agreed (the old link to a root *kʷois- / *kʷeys- 'to heed, notice' is speculative), and de Vaan records no convincing Indo-European etymology; a loan into Latin has been suspected. State explicitly: the deeper ancestry is unknown.
2. Latin cūrāre 'to care for, attend to, treat, heal, manage' (classical).
3. Late Latin / Medieval Latin excūrāre 'to take care of thoroughly, to clean off, to purge' (ex- 'out, thoroughly' + cūrāre), the sense specialized from 'attend carefully' to 'clean'.
4. Old French escurer / escourer 'to clean by rubbing, to scour, to polish' (attested from the 10th–13th c.).
5. (Contact and reinforcement) Middle Dutch schuren / Middle Low German schüren 'to scrub, scour, polish' (13th–15th c.) — see the debate note below.
6. Middle English scouren 'to clean by hard rubbing; to purge' (c. 1300, also spelled scowren, scowre), fusing the Old French and the Middle Dutch / Middle Low German forms.
7. English scour 'to clean or polish by hard rubbing'.
8. English scoured — the regular past tense and past participle (scour + -ed), the exact form on this card.

DEBATE NOTE: The Middle Dutch / Middle Low German cleaning verbs are sometimes treated as loans from Old French escurer (the Romance cleaning word passing back into Germanic), and sometimes as native Germanic formations on a scraping base related to the PIE root *sker- 'to cut, scrape' (the family of shear, scrape, scurf); both directions have been argued, and the English form shows contamination of the two streams. The spatial sense — 'to range over and search exhaustively' (from the late 15th–16th c.) — developed within English by metaphor, 'sweeping' an area clean, and may also have been reinforced by an older English verb scour 'to rush, dash' (14th c.) of uncertain, possibly Scandinavian, origin; that merging, too, is debated.
