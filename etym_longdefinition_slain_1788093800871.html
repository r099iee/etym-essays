SLAIN is the past participle of the strong verb slay — "to kill violently" — and it is the older, heavier word for being put to death. Where "killed" is a neutral police report, "slain" carries the whole weight of the blow: it belongs to epic, scripture, battle-despatches and tragedy. "The Worm of Dread is slain and dead" is its natural habitat — a sentence that needs a word as final as the death it reports.

Primary senses.

  • Killed by violence, especially in battle or by a weapon: "the slain of the field", "the slain hero". The word is used conspicuously of warriors, monsters and victims of slaughter — deaths with a slayer and a blow behind them.
  • As a noun, "the slain": the dead of a battle or massacre, collectively — the usage of dispatches, memorials and scripture ("the number of the slain").
  • Murdered: in older and biblical diction, slaying is any deliberate killing, including murder — "Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him" (KJV).

Secondary and extended senses.

  • Figurative "destroyed": hopes, reputations, arguments and institutions can be "slain" — a literary extension in which violent death stands for total destruction.
  • "Slain in the Spirit": in charismatic Christianity (from the mid-20th century), believers overcome by religious ecstasy fall or lie prostrate, said to be "slain in the Spirit" — the death-word re-imagined as a holy, involuntary collapse.
  • The slang of the stem "slay": while "slain" itself stays archaic, its present-tense stem has drifted twice into slang: "you slay me" (early 20th-century American: "that kills me with laughter") and, from the 1980s–2000s ballroom and drag cultures, "slay" = "to perform dazzlingly" ("she slayed that runway"), now a global pop exclamation ("slay!"). The slang re-energises the oldest meaning — to strike and overcome — redirecting it from victims to audiences.
  • Collocational fossils: "battle-slain", "slain in combat", "slaying dragons", "the slain officer" — the word clusters with warfare and monsters, keeping its epic colour even in modern journalism.

Distribution and idiom. “Slain” is at home in a narrow but vivid set of frames: attributive (“the slain dragon”), collective (“the slain”), scriptural (“the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world”), heroic (“battle-slain”), and solemn-journalistic (“the slain officer”). The derived noun “slayer” keeps the word's heroic cast, and hyperbolic “slaying” in sports headlines (“the champions were slain in the semifinal”) shows the epic verb still flexing metaphorically today.

Grammar and register. Slay is a class VI strong verb (Old English slēan: slōg, slægen), and modern English preserves the archaic-looking alternation slay – slew – slain. "Slain" appears after have/be auxiliaries ("has slain", "is slain"), as an attributive participle ("the slain dragon"), and substantively ("the slain"). Its register is the most elevated of the kill-words: almost never casual, almost never clinical — always imagining an act of violence with a slayer and a blow, a body and a weapon.
