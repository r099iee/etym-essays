- Mid-18th Century (c. 1750–1754) - Emerged suddenly in London as fashionable university and coffeehouse underworld slang; initially defined by contemporaries (such as *The Student*, 1751) as a mysterious new buzzword denoting a practical joke, a cunning hoax, or a trick.
- Late 18th Century (c. 1770–1799) - Transitioned from ephemeral London slang into recognized literary and theatrical English; used by essayists and dramatists to denounce social imposture, medical quackery, and parliamentary hypocrisy.
- Early to Mid-19th Century - Experienced two major cultural expansions: adopted in Northern England as the name for the iconic striped peppermint boiled sweet, and popularized across the English-speaking world by Charles Dickens in *A Christmas Carol* (1843), establishing Scrooge's "Bah! Humbug!" as global folklore.
- Late 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Embraced in America by P.T. Barnum as the art of entertaining showmanship, while British satirists like Oscar Wilde in *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888) used it as the supreme verbal retort to deflate pompous vanity and moral hypocrisy.
- 20th Century - Preserved as a beloved literary and theatrical idiom, forever tied to classic Victorian adaptations, the Wizard of Oz's unmasking, and traditional confectionery culture.
- 21st Century - Continues as an evocative, slightly nostalgic exclamation and cultural archetype, maintaining its unique power to instantly label pretentious posturing, corporate greenwashing, and political bluster.
