The story of communion is the story of how a Latin word for sharing goods became the name of the central Christian rite and then the literary word for the deepest union of minds. It begins with a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European compound built from two very old elements: *ḱom-, the preposition of being with, beside, together, and a derivative of the root *mei- "to change, to exchange." The compound *ḱom-moini- would have meant something like "exchanged among, held in common" — the property of being shared out and shared in. It is a reconstruction, not an attested form, and its exact suffixal detail is debated; but the semantic core is not in doubt: before Latin was Latin, speakers of the ancestor language possessed a word for "that which is held together by exchange."

Within Latin the outcome is certain and richly attested. From very early (the 3rd century BCE) commūnis means "common, public, shared by all," the standing opposite of privātus "private, set apart." The sound changes are regular: the compound *ko-moini- yields com-, and the sequence develops into the familiar -ūn- of commūnis under Latin's rule of vowel weakening in unaccented syllables. In Roman law and political thought commūnis was a weighty word: res commūnēs were things open to all, and the adjective was a pillar of republican language about the public good — communis salus, the common safety; communis patria, the common fatherland. The poets and philosophers used it too: Lucretius speaks of a world common to all, Cicero of the community of human fellowship.

From this adjective Latin created the verbal and nominal machinery that feeds English. The verb commūnicāre, "to make common, to share, to impart" (2nd century BCE), gives communicate and communication. The noun commūniō, commūniōnis, "a sharing, mutual participation, partnership" (classical), described shared walls, shared rights, partnership in property and duty — still close to the concrete life of a community. In this classical layer the word is thoroughly secular: it belongs to the marketplace, the law, and the neighbourhood, not to the altar.

The great semantic turn came with Christianity. The early Christians, writing in Greek, used koinōnia, "fellowship, sharing," for the bond that held believers together and for participation in the Lord's Supper. The Latin translators of the scriptures rendered koinōnia with commūniō, and the word was lifted from the law-court to the sanctuary. By the second to fourth centuries AD, Ecclesiastical Latin commūniō named both the spiritual fellowship of the faithful and, preeminently, participation in the Eucharist — the sharing of the consecrated bread and wine. This is the hinge of the whole history: the word that had meant "sharing goods with one's neighbours" came to mean "sharing the body of Christ with one's fellow believers," and the two senses have coexisted ever since, the sacramental one shading the secular one with its glow.

From Latin the word entered the vernaculars of the Romance-speaking world and crossed the Channel. Old French communion/comunion (12th century) carried both the general sense of fellowship and the specific sense of the sacrament; Middle English communioun (c. 1300) settled the form. In medieval England "communion" was above all a church word: to be "in communion" was to be in the fellowship of the church, and "to receive communion" meant to take the sacrament. Medieval theology made the word do double work — it named the rite and the state of being united by the rite.

The Reformation sharpened and diversified the word. Protestant and Catholic controversies about the nature of the sacrament kept "communion" at the centre of public language, and new technical senses grew from the debates: "close" or "closed" communion (restricting the sacrament to members of a church), "open" communion, "communion in both kinds" (the consecrated bread and wine), and "communion of saints" (from the Latin creed, the fellowship of all the faithful, living and dead). The word also acquired a corporate, institutional sense: the "Anglican Communion" is not an act but a worldwide family of churches — communion as the community itself.

Meanwhile a second, literary life was flowering. From the seventeenth century onward, especially in the language of poetry, devotion, and philosophy, "communion" extended far beyond the altar: "communion with nature" (Wordsworth and the Romantics), "communion with the dead," "communion of minds." The target passage — "having held communion with a new mind" — belongs exactly to this branch: the aesthetic and intellectual sense in which an encounter with a great work, a landscape, or another intelligence becomes a shared experience of the highest grade. Each abstraction preserved the old structure: something held in common, exchanged between sharers, and binding them together.

Sound changes to note: PIE *ḱom- → Latin com- with regular assimilation; the *-moini- element vowel-weakened in unaccented positions to give the -mūn- of commūnis; Old French com- → English com-; and the final -ion from Latin -io, -iōnis via French -ion. The modern word runs a double life: in ordinary speech it has largely retreated to the religious domain (receiving Communion; the Communion service), while in literary and critical prose it remains the word for the deepest grade of shared mental or spiritual experience. Its register is high; everyday talk prefers connection, bond, or talk. Yet even at its most secular, communion keeps the faint sacramental resonance inherited from the Latin church — a word that, after two thousand years, still means that two beings have shared in something together, and that the sharing has joined them.
