- Roman Latin — in promptu, in readiness, at hand: an account kept ready, goods available, a neutral business and household phrase with no hint of performance.
- 17th-c. French — impromptu, the witty verse dashed off on demand: salon poets turn readiness into a parlour feat, and spontaneity becomes fashionable genius.
- 1660s England — borrowing for verse: an impromptu is a short occasional poem, a gentleman's trick of seeming brilliance, admired exactly because it claims no labour.
- 18th–19th c. — broadening to all performance: speeches, toasts, songs, and then Schubert's piano Impromptus, where full composition dresses up as improvisation.
- 20th c. — democratization: impromptu meetings, parties, and press conferences, as informal modern life finds the word for praising organized spontaneity.
- Today — warm approval for the unplanned that works: from corridor meetings to platform picnics, with the classical piano genre preserved as a fossil of the older prestige sense.
