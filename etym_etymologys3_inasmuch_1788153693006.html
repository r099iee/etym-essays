1. Three ancient stems in one small word.Inasmuch is a wholly native English compound, and each of its three parts is older than English itself. The element in goes back to PIE *h₁én "in, into", inherited into every Germanic language. The element as is a reduction of Old English eallswā — literally "all-so", "just so" — which had already worn down to alswā and then to as by Middle English. The element much descends from PIE *meǵh₂- "great", via Proto-Germanic *mikilaz and Old English micel, whose comparative māra gives modern more. The history of the whole phrase is a miniature study in grammaticalisation: ordinary words, arranged in a common pattern, slowly frozen into a single conjunction.

2. Middle English: "in as moche as" (14th c.). The phrase begins life in the 14th century as literally "in so much as" — a measure phrase meaning "in that amount in which", with the proportion fully open. Chaucer and the Wycliffite writers use it with the double value it still has: both "to the extent that" and "for the reason that". The proportions were felt: speakers said "in as much as" much as modern speakers say "in so far as", comparing degrees rather than citing causes.

3. Univerbation (16th–17th c.). As spelling stabilised in the early modern period, the collocation was increasingly written as one word, inasmuch, while retaining the separated particle as: inasmuch as. The 16th and 17th centuries gave it heavy official work. Legal documents, parliamentary papers, and the prose of Hooker and Bacon use it to open measured grounds; the King James Bible and the Book of Common Prayer anchor it in religious prose, where "inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these" links proportion and obligation in a single clause.

  • Religious register: Matthew 25:40 — "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me."
  • Legal register: statutes and deeds use inasmuch as to attach liability "to the extent that" a condition holds.
  • Philosophical register: 18th–19th century logic and ethics prize it for stating the precise scope of an obligation.

4. The modern retreat. The 20th century pressed English toward economy: because and since cover cause, insofar as covers proportion, and American usage especially has let inasmuch as drift to the margins of formal style. It survives — as in Pater’s prose here — as the measured, courtly conjunction of literary and academic English, prized precisely because it refuses to choose between "since" and "to the extent that", and so holds two logical relations in one elegant word.

5. The grammatical life.Inasmuch as belongs to the class of complex subordinators, and its behaviour reveals its origin: because it began as a measure phrase, it still tolerates logical paraphrase ("to the extent that") and still admits the correlative pair "in as much as ... in so much as ..." in older prose. Fowler’s Modern English Usage (1926) files it among formal, legalistic connectives and warns against heaviness; mid-century American guides preferred insofar as for proportion, leaving inasmuch as to formal cause. The distinction between cause and extent remains live in careful writing, and a sensitive reader feels both meanings at once in a clause such as Pater’s — which is exactly why the conjunction has endured where blunter synonyms have not.
