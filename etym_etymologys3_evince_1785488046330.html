Behind “evince” stands a thoroughly martial image. Its ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *weik-, “to conquer, overcome, fight”, a base of real antiquity attested across the western branches of the family. Celtic kept it in Old Irish fichid, “he fights”; Germanic preserved it in Gothic weihan, “to fight, contend”, in the poetic Old English wīgan, “to fight”, beside the noun wīg, “war, battle”, and in the Old Norse víg, “battle, slaying”, still visible today in Scandinavian place-names and compounds. Some scholars also adduce the Indo-Iranian reflex in Sanskrit vijáyati, “he conquers”, and vijaya, “victory” — a celebrated set (the name of the goddess Vijaya, and the root of many royal titles) — though the connection is debated and cannot be treated as certain. But the richest development of the root was Latin, which drew from *weik- the great verb vincere, “to conquer, to defeat”, whose past participle victus generated victor, “conqueror”, and so victory, the goddess Victoria, and the Roman surname Victor. Where Celtic and Germanic kept the root as a word of physical combat, the Italic branch carried the idea of conquest into the intellectual world.
  • The prefix family of vincere: Latin compounded vincere with a series of prefixes, building a family of intellectual as well as military victories. Convincere, “to overcome thoroughly in argument”, gave convince and convict — the mind conquered by evidence. Devincere, “to conquer, bind fast”, gave the rare devince; pervincere, “to win through”, and revincere, “to reconquer, disprove”, left only ghosts in English; and provincia, probably “territory won by pro-conquest”, gave province.
  • Evincere: the direct ancestor of our word is evincere, literally “to conquer out” — ex-, “out”, plus vincere — which meant to conquer utterly, to win outright and carry one’s point. In educated Latin usage evincere then made the decisive migration from the battlefield to the courtroom and the schoolroom: to evince a case was to prove it so completely that no one could doubt it, to conquer, as it were, by demonstration. A parallel legal sense, “to recover property by legal process”, produced the Late Latin noun ēvictiō and, through English law, the separate verb evict.
  • Sound changes: the root *weik- → Latin *weik- → vincere shows the regular Italic shift of *k to /k/ before *i and the raising of the vowel; the English borrowing evince (1609) preserves the Latin infinitive stem ēvinc-, with the prefix reduced to ev- and the cluster /ns/ simplified in pronunciation to /ns/ as spelled.
The English history is a steady retreat from force to inference. The first recorded sense of evince, in 1609, was fully martial: “to conquer, vanquish, overcome”. In the course of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word migrated to the arena of argument — “to convict, refute, disprove” — so that a disputant evinced an opponent’s error by conquering it in debate; the passage’s “opponents despatched in savagely civil footnotes” shows how deeply this combative inheritance still runs through scholarly rhetoric. Then, in the eighteenth century, evince settled into its modern role: to make evident, to show by proof, to demonstrate from the evidence. The older senses survive only as archaisms in legal and historical prose, but they explain the word’s peculiar force: to say that these works evince a deep passion is to say that the passion has won — it has conquered its way out into the open and stands proven before us. In modern usage evince is formal, chiefly British, and governs abstract objects — passion, interest, concern, skill — and it asserts proof beyond reasonable doubt, which is exactly the weight the chapter’s sentence needs. Its literary career runs from seventeenth-century divinity and the philosophical prose of the Royal Society through the great eighteenth-century critics (Johnson, Burke) who evince their proofs, to the modern scholarly essay, where evince remains a connoisseur’s verb, slightly old-fashioned and therefore exactly right for the wryly admiring tone of the passage.
