Nanking is a proper noun naming a major historical and modern metropolis of eastern China, situated on the lower Yangtze River in present-day Jiangsu Province. Its place in the world's vocabulary is double: it is at once a living Chinese city of more than eight million people and a heavily freighted historical toponym that once designated the capital of successive Chinese dynasties. The name is the English romanization of the Chinese 南京 (pinyin Nánjīng), literally "Southern Capital", and it is one of a matched pair — the "Southern Capital" and the "Northern Capital" (北京, Běijīng, "Peking") — whose rivalry structured Chinese political geography for centuries.

Sense 1 — The city itself (denotation). In its primary, literal sense, Nanking denotes the city on the southern bank of the Yangtze, today the capital of Jiangsu Province and one of China's great industrial, educational, and transport centres. It is a walled metropolis with a long urban lineage: founded near the site of earlier settlements, it rose to prominence as the seat of the Eastern Wu kingdom (229–280 CE), then as Jiankang under the Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties (317–589), and again as the Southern Tang capital (937–975). The modern municipality retains this continuous identity, and any reference to "the city of Nanking" points, first and foremost, to this physical, contemporary place.

Sense 2 — Historical capital (the "Southern Capital"). The name 南京 (Nánjīng, "Southern Capital") was deliberately applied when the Ming founder Zhu Yuanzhang established his capital there in 1368; the same word then circulated as the official counterpart of the northern capital. Over its history the city served as capital of roughly ten regimes — the Wu, Eastern Jin, the four Southern Dynasties, Southern Tang, the early Ming, the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom (1853–1864), and the Republic of China (1927–1937 and 1946–1949) — a record that gives the word its air of dynastic gravity. When a text speaks of Nanking as a "capital", it invokes this layered imperial history rather than mere administrative status.

Sense 3 — The romanization vs. the pinyin (Nanking vs. Nanjing). Strictly, "Nanking" is the older Western transliteration (based on historical and dialectal pronunciations, including a final -k retained in older and regional renderings), whereas "Nanjing" is the modern pinyin romanization of the same name. Both denote the same city; the choice is chronological and stylistic. "Nanking" predominates in literature, treaty documents, and history written before the late twentieth century — the Treaty of Nanking (1842), which ended the First Opium War, is the most famous instance — while "Nanjing" is the contemporary form in Chinese official usage and modern scholarship.

Sense 4 — The literary and cultural emblem (contextual sense). In the card's context — "The family's wealth and prestige were reflected in their great and ancient mansions in Nanking" — the word is a cultural emblem rather than a mere address. This sentence belongs to the world of classical Chinese fiction (the milieu of the great novel Dream of the Red Chamber, whose central family is rooted in Nanjing). Here Nanking connotes ancestral grandeur, scholar-gentry culture, southern refinement, silk and garden elegance, and the deep-rooted prestige of a house that measures its lineage in generations. It stands in contrast to the northern capital's ethos of imperial officialdom: Nanking is the south's seat of culture, wealth, and memory.

Sense 5 — Historical-political resonance (tragedy and treaty). In twentieth-century world history the name acquired a solemn register through events attached to it: the Treaty of Nanking (1842), the Nanking National Government of the Republic of China, and most painfully the Nanking Massacre (1937) during the Second Sino-Japanese War. In historical discourse the bare word can thus carry the weight of colonial-era diplomacy, modern Chinese statehood, and wartime trauma — an associative polysemy that no dictionary gloss alone conveys.

Exhaustive summary of senses.

  • City proper: the modern metropolis and provincial capital on the lower Yangtze, Jiangsu, China.
  • Dynastic capital: seat of about ten regimes across two millennia, formally titled the "Southern Capital" (南京) from 1368.
  • Romanization pair: "Nanking" (older transcription, with -k) vs. "Nanjing" (modern pinyin); same referent, different registers.
  • Literary emblem: in classical fiction, a byword for southern aristocratic culture, wealth, ancestral mansions, and refined prestige.
  • Political and historical term: Treaty of Nanking, Nanking Government, Nanking Massacre — a name saturated with modern history.
  • Etymological compound: 南 (nán, "south") + 京 (jīng, "capital"), the "Southern Capital", counterpart of Beijing ("Northern Capital").

Semantic boundaries. Against Beijing/Peking, Nanking is the culturally southern, historically romantic seat of literati and merchant wealth; against generic capital or metropolis, Nanking is specific, proper, and emotionally textured. The word is a proper noun that behaves like a symbol, and its register shifts with context from neutral geography to dynastic nostalgia.
