- 4th–5th c. — the Church coins an adjective for anything belonging to Moses and his law; it is learned, bookish and confined to Christian learned writing.
- 16th c. — English adopts it, at first as “Mosaical”, in sermons and scriptural commentary.
- 17th c. — “Mosaic law”, “Mosaic chronology” and “Mosaic cosmogony” become fixed phrases, and the adjective signals a literal reading of Genesis.
- 18th–19th c. — “Mosaic authorship” turns the word into a battleground as source criticism of the Pentateuch develops.
- 20th c. — “Mosaic covenant” and “Mosaic dispensation” remain standard in theology, while the unrelated art term spreads into figurative and scientific compounds.
- 21st c. — Jan Assmann’s “Mosaic distinction” gives the adjective a new life in cultural theory; meanwhile the art homograph dominates everyday use of the spelling.
