disengage (verb) - to release or detach from engagement: to free a person or thing from a bond, fastening, entanglement, or obligation; in military use, to break off contact with an enemy; in mechanics, to disconnect one part of a machine from another; and, reflexively, to withdraw oneself from involvement. The word is the negation of engage, whose ancestry runs through Old French engagier ("to bind by a pledge") back to the Germanic pledge-word (gage, "security, pawn"), so that the history of engagement is the history of a binding promise - and disengagement is its undoing. In the literary context: Rottcodd, entering the hall, "would disengage himself of his jacket" - a reflexive, faintly archaic construction ("disengage himself of") that gives the everyday act of undressing a ceremonial, almost juridical weight. The verb describes an unlocking: the formal jacket, the outermost layer of the social self, is unbound before the grey overall is drawn over his head. Peake's tone is ritualistic and methodical - disengagement is the first movement of a daily liturgy of dust, the shedding of one uniform before the assumption of another. Polysemy of disengage:
  • Physical release. To free from a fastening, grip, or entanglement: to disengage a rope, a knot, a hand from a grip.
  • Military withdrawal. To break off contact with an enemy and fall back - "disengagement" is the tactical term for an orderly retreat from battle-lines.
  • Mechanical disconnection. To throw a machine part out of engagement so that it no longer transmits motion (a disengaged clutch, disengaged gear teeth).
  • Personal and social withdrawal. To detach oneself from a commitment, a relationship, or a course of action; emotional disengagement is the modern psychological term for deliberate or defensive withdrawal from involvement.
  • Reflexive / archaic use. "To disengage oneself of X" - the pattern Peake uses - is a mildly archaic construction meaning to divest oneself of something, retaining the old pledge-drama of unbinding.
Differentiation: Detach is purely physical and neutral, without pledge-imagery; release implies letting go of something held; unfasten is merely mechanical; disentangle implies freeing from a snarl or complexity; resign concerns duties and offices. Disengage alone carries the historical burden of the broken pledge - it is the negation of engage, a word born of legal bonds, betrothals and battle-lines - and in Peake's sentence it quietly mints that history into a butler's shrug.
overall - two words in one form. As a noun, an overall is a loose-fitting protective garment worn over one's ordinary clothing, covering the whole body; in the plural, work trousers with a bib and shoulder straps. As an adverb and adjective, overall means "taken as a whole, in total": overall length, an overall impression, "overall, the day was quiet." The name performs its own definition: the overall goes over all - it is the final layer, the envelope that covers every other garment and, in its adverbial life, the summary that covers every detail. In the literary context: Rottcodd draws "a long grey overall" over his head, and the garment "descended shapelessly to his ankles." The grey overall is the institutional robe of his custodianship: shapeless, protective, engulfing - the uniform of a man who has no other life than the hall. The word's dullness is part of Peake's effect: the everyday protective smock, the servant's second skin, worn over the ceremonial jacket that was just disengaged. Polysemy of overall:
  • The garment. A one-piece protective work garment (British singular "an overall"); American plural "overalls" = bib-and-brace trousers, the working man's garment since 1759.
  • The adverb / adjective of totality. "Over all" in a summarizing sense: considered as a whole, in total, universally - used of sums, measurements, impressions, and final judgments.
Differentiation: Smock, apron, and coverall name other protective garments, narrower in scope; uniform implies institution and rank, which the overall rejects; wholly and entirely are adverbial near-synonyms but lack the summarizing, final-judgement sense of "overall".
descended (verb; past tense and past participle of descend) - moved or hung downward from a higher to a lower place; came down; and, in genealogy, to be derived by descent from an ancestor or source. The word is the reverse of ascend: Latin descendere is de- ("down") + scandere ("to climb") - climbing performed in reverse, a step-by-step downward movement. In the literary context: Rottcodd's long grey overall "descended shapelessly to his ankles" - the word holds the shape of the fall: not a drop, but a slow, graded, flowing descent, the garment settling down the body in folds, shapelessly, as water finds its level. The verb preserves its etymological dignity even for cloth: it describes the graduated downward progress of a thing, whether a staircase, a hill-path, a family line, or a length of grey material. Polysemy of descend:
  • Movement downward. To come or go down, literally: to descend a staircase, a mountain, a river valley.
  • Hanging down. To extend or hang downward - the sense used of the overall that "descended shapelessly to his ankles."
  • Genealogy. To be descended from: to derive by birth or ancestry from an earlier person or line.
  • Derivation of things. To be inherited or transmitted from a source - customs, properties, titles, languages, and words are said to descend.
  • Attitudinal descent. To lower oneself, or to fall in rank or estimation - a formal, slightly archaic use.
Differentiation: Drop is quick and brief, a fall; fall implies gravity and loss of control; sink implies submersion; slope is a static gradient. Descend alone preserves a graduated, orderly, step-by-step movement - from Latin scandere "to climb", the descent is climbing performed in reverse.
