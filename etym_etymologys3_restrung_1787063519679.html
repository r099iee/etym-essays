From cord to chordophone: the deep past of “string”. The story of restrung begins far back in the Indo-European family with a root reconstructed as *strenk- (“tight, rigid; twisted”), which produced words for cords and ropes across the daughter branches. In the Germanic branch this yielded Proto-Germanic *strangiz (“cord, rope”), the ancestor of Old English streng (“line, cord, thread”) — recorded already in early English prose and glosses — and of cognates such as Old Norse strengr, Dutch streng, and German Strang. The oldest English sense is that of a simple line or cord, often one used for tying, binding, or hanging.

The Middle English shift. In the Middle English period (12th–14th centuries) the form drifted from streng to string, with the vowel shift and the sense widening to include “a row or series of things” (a “string of beads”, a “string of horses”) and, decisively, the strings of musical instruments. It is this musical specialisation that sets the later course of the word: the noun string became the standard name for the tensioned cords of harps, lyres, and fiddles, and the verb to string came to mean “to furnish with a string”, applied first to bows (archers “string” a bow by bending it and hooking the bowstring) and then to instruments.

Coining the prefixed verb. By the 16th century English formed re-string / restring with the productive prefix re- (“again, anew”), and its past participle restrung soon followed. For the first centuries of its life the participle was a workaday word: a bowyer restrung bows, a musician restrung a lute, a seller restrung goods. It carried no special emotional colour — it simply recorded that the cord had been replaced.

The biblical shadow. The poetry of the word comes from Psalm 137, where the exiled psalmist declares, “we hanged our harps upon the willows,” refusing to sing in a foreign land. Whenever a harp is restrung in English verse, the Psalm 137 image hovers behind it: the restored instrument is the emblem of a restored people, of grief ended, of songs resumed. The card’s own context (“His crown shall be upholden, His harp shall be restrung, His halls shall echo golden To songs of yore re-sung”) is squarely in this tradition: the restrung harp guarantees the re-sung songs.

Modern expansion of the participle. The 19th and 20th centuries broadened the practical reach of restrung:

  • Musical instruments — the luthier’s and player’s sense, for the whole violin, harp, and guitar family, and for the modern guitar culture of the 20th century.
  • Jewellery and devotional objects — pearl necklaces, bracelets, and rosaries restrung on new silk or wire when the original cord wears out.
  • Racquets and bows — tennis, badminton, and squash racquets restrung with fresh gut or synthetic string; archery bows restrung after the old bowstring snaps.
  • Figurative language — the participle as a symbol of resilience: a “restrung” person, community, or institution, able to sing again after silence.

Sound and form. The participle inherits the old strong-verb alternation string–strung (restrung), contrasting with the surviving regular form stringed in the phrase “stringed instruments”. The stress falls on the second syllable, re-STRUNG, and the prefix may be hyphenated when the repetition is emphasised. Spelling pressure from the simple verb ensures that restrung (not restringed) is the standard form in all the registers where the word lives today.
