- 1840s–1860s: Introduced into English primarily as a geographical toponym for the Mexican state of Tabasco and as a botanical descriptor for an imported, unusually fiery chili cultivar (Capsicum frutescens). - Perceived as an exotic, blistering Central American agricultural curiosity.
- 1870s–1890s: Commercialized by Edmund McIlhenny on Avery Island, Louisiana, as a bottled proprietary condiment aged in oak barrels. - Rapidly embraced by Gilded Age saloons, oyster houses, and luxury restaurants as an indispensable condiment of sophisticated culinary heat.
- 1900s–1930s: Expanded into widespread English and Anglo-American slang as a vivid metaphor for intense spice, caustic wit, fiery temper, or the authentic article ('the real Tabasco'). - Perceived culturally as the pinnacle of punchy, no-nonsense vigor and lively verve, celebrated in humorous prose by writers like P.G. Wodehouse.
- Mid-20th Century: Achieved universal domestic household ubiquity as the quintessential American hot sauce, distributed globally in military combat rations and commercial pantries. - Perceived as a reliable, classic staple, transitioning from exotic novelty to timeless pantry baseline.
- 21st Century: Maintained its iconic status amid an explosion of global craft hot sauces. - Widely regarded as a benchmark of heritage vinegar-forward pepper sauces, while retaining historical literary recognition as a synonym for sharp, fiery authenticity.
