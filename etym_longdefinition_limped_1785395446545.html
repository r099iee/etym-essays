LIMPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb "limp": to walk with an uneven, halting gait, favouring one leg because the other is stiff, painful, weak or shorter — movement that has lost its rhythm and its ease. In the passage from The Hobbit the company "limped along now as fast as they were able down the gentle slopes of a pine forest": the single verb does the work of the whole sentence's feeling, telling us the travellers are beaten and footsore, no longer marching but hobbling, each step a reminder of their bruises. The gentleness of the slope sharpens the irony — even this easy going, downhill and through pleasant pine forest, is hard for them. To limp is not to fall or to stop: it is persistence through pain, motion that continues but cannot hide its cost, and the word carries both suffering and dogged endurance, the dwarves pressing on toward the lands to the south with sore legs and set jaws.

SENSES AND USES

  • Literal, of a person or animal (core): to walk lamely because of an injured, stiff or deformed leg or foot — "the injured dog limped back home"; "she limped off the field after twisting her ankle".
  • Of a ship, vehicle or machine (19th–20th centuries): to move slowly and with difficulty while still functioning, as if wounded — "the tanker limped into port"; "the plane limped home on one engine".
  • Figurative, of enterprises, seasons and careers (20th century): to proceed with difficulty, barely holding on — "the campaign limped to a close"; "the economy limped through the recession". The metaphor treats an institution as a creature favouring a damaged leg.
  • The noun "limp" (19th century): the uneven gait itself — "a slight limp", "to walk with a limp" — the permanent or temporary mark of lameness.
  • The gerund and participle adjective "limping": "limping soldiers", "the limping economy", "a limping gait".
  • Older transferred sense in dialect and archaic use: "to fall short, to fail" — the bridge sense from which the walking verb grew.

IMPORTANT HOMONYM

The adjective "limp" — "flaccid, soft, without stiffness", as in "a limp handshake" or "a limp lettuce leaf" — is a separate word, first recorded in 1706, probably formed independently from the same ancient idea of hanging slack. Modern English treats the two as a homograph pair, and "limped" belongs only to the walking verb; the adjective makes "limper" and "limpest", not "limped".

CONTRASTS AND BOUNDARIES

  • "Hobbled" suggests clumsy, constrained movement, often from tied feet or stiffness — heavier and more obstructed than a limp.
  • "Staggered" implies losing balance, lurching as if about to fall; a limp is controlled though painful.
  • "Shuffled" means sliding the feet without lifting them — slow, but not painful in the way limping is.
  • "Hopped" is motion on one leg, rhythmic and sometimes energetic, whereas a limp alternates both legs with one bearing the weight.
  • "Lumbered" describes a heavy, clumsy gait of large bodies, with no suggestion of injury.
  • "Tottered" suggests unsteadiness and the threat of falling, often from weakness or age, not a single damaged leg.
  • "Faltered" names the loss of momentum or courage, not a specific gait.
