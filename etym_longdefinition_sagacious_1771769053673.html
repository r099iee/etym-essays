The word SAGACIOUS occurs in one of Gibbon's most sardonic sentences: even the most sagacious divines, he writes, could distinguish, according to the colour of their respective prejudices, which calamity followed which sin — an earthquake for heresy, a deluge for the progress of error. The adjective is applied to religious controversialists with a double edge. On the surface it is the highest compliment to their intellect — keen, acute, far-seeing, penetrating — and Gibbon grants that the divines are indeed sharp. But the compliment is undermined by the very clause it introduces: their sagacity is exercised according to the colour of their respective prejudices. Their acuteness merely rationalizes what their sectarian bias already dictates; the sharper the mind, the more ingenious the superstition it defends. The tone is mock-heroic irony, the signature weapon of Enlightenment satire: sagacious praises the instrument of reason precisely so that the reader sees how shamefully that instrument has been wasted on folly. CORE SENSE AND DEFINITION:
  • Primary sense: having or showing keen mental discernment and good judgment; shrewd, perceptive, penetrating in judgment, with the power of reasoning quickly and well, especially in practical or human affairs.
  • Distinguishing feature: sagacity couples intellectual keenness with an almost instinctual swiftness. The sagacious mind does not merely reason slowly to a conclusion; it scents the truth out, as a hunting dog scents its quarry. The word therefore sits between pure intelligence and pure intuition — the discernment that works with the speed of instinct.
  • Register: elevated, literary, and formal. Sagacious is never a colloquial word; it belongs to the prose of historians, essayists, judges, and moralists, and it retains a dignified, Latinate gravity that plain synonyms like "shrewd" or "smart" lack.
FULL POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • Sensory keenness (earliest Latin sense, ghosting through the English word): from Latin sagāx, 'keen-scented,' sagacious originally described the acute physical senses of a hunting hound — the nose that unerringly follows a trail. This sensory core survives as an undertone: a sagacious reader 'senses' an argument's weakness, a sagacious observer 'smells' a lie.
  • Mental discernment (dominant modern sense): the ability to judge soundly in practical matters, to read people and situations at a glance, to distinguish what is true, valuable, or prudent from what is deceptive. Gibbon's 'sagacious divines' are keen interpreters — they can tell which sin produces which punishment.
  • Applied to animals (zoological/natural-history register): naturalists apply sagacious to animals, especially hounds, elephants, and dolphins, credited with remarkable intelligence and learning; this is the literal heir of the Latin 'keen-scented' sense.
  • Irony and mock-heroic application (rhetorical usage): as in Gibbon, the word can be loaded with sarcasm — a compliment to penetration used to expose the folly that penetration defends. 'Sagacious' + irony = the Enlightenment's favorite tool for praising reason in order to convict it of waste.
  • Archaic/regional intimation of divination: through the Latin relative praesāgīre 'to sense beforehand,' the word family carries a faint prophetic or presageful colour — the sagacious mind as one that half-foresees consequences.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE (how the senses connect): The bridge is a metaphor transferred from nose to mind. Latin sagīre meant 'to perceive keenly by the senses,' the verb of a hound casting about for a scent. Because keen physical scenting was the model of reliable perception, Latin authors extended the verb and its adjective sagāx to intellectual perception: the acute judge, the shrewd observer, the general who reads a battlefield. English inherited both the concrete remnant (applied to animals) and the mental sense (applied to persons), and the two have coexisted since the seventeenth century, with the mental sense overwhelmingly dominant in modern use. COLLOCATION AND SYNTACTIC BEHAVIOUR:
  • Sagacious frequently modifies nouns of judgment and counsel: a sagacious observer, sagacious advice, a sagacious decision, a sagacious policy.
  • It is often followed by 'enough to + infinitive' or a subordinate clause: 'sagacious enough to see through the pretense.'
  • It combines with degree adverbs: 'most sagacious,' 'highly sagacious,' 'extremely sagacious.'
  • The abstract noun is sagacity ('the quality of being sagacious'); the adjectival adverb is sagaciously. The noun appears more frequently in modern prose than the adjective itself and is the more natural, less archaic of the two.
CONTEXTUAL READING (Gibbon's sentence in full critical detail): Gibbon's sentence is built on a precise syllogism of ironic praise. The divines are 'sagacious': they can distinguish, with genuine acuteness, which calamity is the punishment of which sin. The Establishment of heresy, they conclude, tends to produce an earthquake; the progress of sin and error, they conclude, produces a deluge. The whole instrument of their reason is thus devoted to matching catastrophes to offences, and their 'sagacity' is calibrated by 'the colour of their respective prejudices' — Anglican, Catholic, Calvinist, each sect reading the same disaster differently. The compliment is therefore annihilating: these men are not stupid, and that is precisely the problem. Their cleverness is real, their discernment swift, their learning deep; and all of it is wasted in the service of folly. Sagacious, by raising them, makes the waste visible, and the elevation is the insult. The word is thus the perfect hinge of the passage: it names the faculty whose misuse is the chapter's subject. RELATION TO THE REST OF THE WORD FAMILY: Sagacious shares its Latin stock with sagacity, presage ('a foreboding'), and the Romance adjectives Italian sagace, Spanish sagaz, and Portuguese sagaz. The Germanic root *sāg- 'to seek' (behind English seek, sought, beseech and German suchen) is widely held to be the same Indo-European base, and Greek hēgéomai 'to lead' has been compared, though that comparison is disputed by modern handbooks. Within English, the -acious ending (from Latin -āx, genitive -ācis, filtered through the suffix -ous) makes sagacious a member of the family that includes audacious, tenacious, vivacious, perspicacious, and efficacious — all adjectives built on Latin adjective stems with the same 'full of, characterized by' force.
