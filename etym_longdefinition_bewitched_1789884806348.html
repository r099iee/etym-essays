BEWITCHED is the past participle and participial adjective of the verb bewitch, meaning to put under a spell by witchcraft — to cast an enchantment over a person, animal, or thing so that it behaves strangely, suffers misfortune, or obeys a magical will. “That accursed goose must be bewitched” means the goose acts as if some witch has laid a spell on it: it will not cook, will not move rightly, or sticks fast against nature. The core image is malign magical causation, the folk explanation for stubborn uncanniness when ordinary causes fail.

Beyond literal sorcery, bewitched describes being charmed, delighted, or fascinated to the point of helpless captivation. A lover is bewitched by beauty, an audience bewitched by music, a child bewitched by a story. Here the witch is metaphorical: charm works like a spell, robbing judgment and binding attention. The adjective thus spans fear and delight, curse and allure, united by the idea of power that overrides the will, whether to harm or to enrapture.

  • Literal sorcery sense — put under a spell: “The prince was bewitched into a frog.” This is the dictionary centre: affected by witchcraft, enchanted by spoken charms, potions, glances, or pacts. In folk-tales pans stick, geese refuse to roast, and travellers lose their way because someone bewitched them. The semantic frame is pre-modern causation: misfortune as agency.
  • Possessed or ill-fated sense — acting as if spell-bound: “That accursed goose must be bewitched!” The goose is not seen to be spelled; its strange resistance proves the spell. This evidential use diagnoses magic from symptoms — sticking, burning without cooking, uncanny adhesion — and belongs to tale-logic where the marvellous explains the mechanical.
  • Enchanted-delight sense — captivated, enraptured: “Bewitched by her smile,” “bewitched, bothered and bewildered.” Here bewitchment is pleasurable captivation. The bridge from curse to charm is the shared loss of control: fear surrenders to a hag, love surrenders to a face, awe surrenders to music. Literature, especially Shakespeare and romance, made this positive pole central.
  • Fascinated-attention sense — spellbound, riveted: “The children sat bewitched through the whole show.” This shades toward spellbound and entranced: fixed gaze, suspended time, oblivion to surroundings. The magic is attentional rather than ontological; no transformation is claimed, only irresistible holding of the mind.
  • Archaic and dialectal extensions: Older English used bewitch for bewitching with the evil eye, overlooking cattle, curdling milk, or laming horses. Puritan tracts and witch-trial pamphlets speak of bewitched butter, bewitched children in fits, and bewitched pins. Modern sceptical use keeps the word but ironises it: “my computer is bewitched” means perversely malfunctioning as if spiteful.
  • Grammatical profile: As participle, bewitched is passive — someone bewitches, someone is bewitched. As adjective it is gradable in the figurative sense (“utterly bewitched,” “half bewitched”) but absolute in the literal sense (one either is or is not under a spell). It governs by for the agent (“bewitched by a sorceress”) and with for the instrument or charm (“bewitched with elfin music”).

In short, bewitched names magical subjection and, by extension, any fascination so complete it feels magical. From tale-goose to love-song it marks the moment when will yields to wonder, whether in dread or in delight, and preserves in one word the old world where song, glance, and word could bind.
