disembark is the newest word of the five, and its biography is a tale of a Mediterranean boat. The ultimate source is a nautical word of the eastern sea: Egyptian bꜣjr, a seagoing vessel, which plausibly passed into Greek as bâris, the round-hulled Nile barge described by Herodotus. From Greek, or from a parallel western Mediterranean source, came Late Latin barca, "a small boat," which gave every Romance tongue its word for bark or barge (Italian barca, Spanish barca, French barque, whence English barque in the fifteenth century). A sibling form barga produced French barge and English barge around 1300. Around these boat-words, French formed the verb embarquer, "to put aboard a boat," by adding en- "into"; English borrowed embark in the sixteenth century, and then, with the negating prefix dis-, coined disembark in the 1590s, first in naval and logbook registers. The whole family is a loanword chain with NO PIE ancestry — it is routed as non-Indo-European and uncertain, and it would be an error to force it under any PIE "carry" root. Its meaning has barely shifted in four hundred years: to disembark is still to step out of a boat onto land, exactly what the settlers do, and the word's documentary dryness is the fossil of its logbook birth. In the twentieth century it crossed from sea to air — "passengers will disembark through the rear door" — and gained the noun disembarkation, remodelled on French débarquement.

aboard compresses two histories into one small word. The core is board, from Proto-Germanic *burdą "board, plank, table," whose deeper PIE connection (possibly *bʰer-dʰ- "to cut, split") is contested — modern handbooks often call the family an origin-uncertain North-West-European innovation, and it is NOT related to the PIE root *bʰer- "to carry" that underlies English bear. Old English bord was a plank, and by extension a table and, most importantly for our purposes, the "side" and "deck" of a ship — the ship's boards as its bulwark, the wooden platform on which the crew stood. To come "on bord" was to step onto that planked platform, and the compound "on bord" fused into aboard in the fourteenth century, the initial vowel being the weakened form of "on." For four centuries the word belonged to the sea alone; the nineteenth century then generalized it to every conveyance — aboard the train, aboard the plane — and the twentieth extended it to every enterprise, so that "welcome aboard" now means joining a company or team. The sound change is minimal (Old English bord → modern board shows the normal shortening and /ɔː/ rounding; a- from on shows the familiar reduction of unstressed prepositions), and the semantics show a classic movement from the concrete (the ship's planks) to the abstract (membership).

lighter is a worker's noun, named for the work it does. It descends from PIE *h₁lengʷʰ- "light, quick," which in Germanic became *linhtaz, Old English lēoht "light, not heavy," and the derived verb līhtan "to make light, to relieve." To that verb English added the agent suffix -er and produced, by the 1480s, a lighter: the flat-bottomed barge that "lightens" a deep-draft ship of part of its cargo so the big vessel can cross a bar or reach a quay. The whole harbor economy grew around the word — lighterage is the work and its fee, lighterman the worker — and the word's plain physicality (low water, ropes, oars, wet planks) survives in historical prose. The same root ran on a parallel track into Latin as levis "light," giving English lever, levity, alleviate, and elevate, and into Sanskrit as laghú-, the ancestor of Hindi halkā "light." In the early twentieth century a new machine took the name by a different metaphor: the pocket "lighter" makes light, not lightness — a functional pun on the same word — and today this is the commonest sense. The comparative adjective lighter ("more light in weight") is formally identical and semantically distinct, a standing ambiguity the context must resolve.

chattels is the word that connects property law to the barnyard. Its chain runs from PIE *kap-ut- "head" — the same root as Latin caput and English head — through Latin capitalis "of the head, chief, principal," which in Late Latin turned economic: capitale meant the "principal" sum or stock of wealth, the chief goods of a household. Old French palatalized the initial ca- to cha- and produced chatel "property, goods," which English took as chattel in the thirteenth century. But the same Old French word, in its Anglo-Norman form catel (which kept the hard ca-), entered English as cattle — at first meaning "property" and then narrowing to "livestock," because movable wealth was mostly animals. Thus chattel and cattle are doublets, two English forms of one Norman word, one meaning "movable property" and the other "livestock." The legal career of chattel is precise: in the law of property it names movable personal possessions as distinct from real property (land and buildings), fixed forever in the formula "goods and chattels." The darkest extension came with the slave trade: chattel slavery, in which a human being is legally reduced to a movable possession — the same word that once meant a cow driven before its owner now naming the ownership of a person. That double history — property law and human property — is the weight the word carries in the settlers' story, where a family's chattels are the small things that move with them into a new land.

bight is the most native word of the five, a direct inheritance from Old English. Its root is PIE *bʰewgʰ- "to bend, to curve," the same root that produced English bow "to bend," bough "a bent limb or branch," and elbow (Old English elnboga, literally "forearm-bend"). Proto-Germanic *buhtiz turned the verb of bending into a noun "a bend, a curve," and Old English byht meant "a bend, a corner, a bay" — already in the oldest records the word could name the curve of a coast or the corner of a room. Middle English bight carried both senses forward, and modern English keeps two settled lives: in geography a bight is a wide, shallow curve of the coastline (the Great Australian Bight, the Bight of Benin), and in seamanship the bight of a rope is its slack middle loop between the two ends. The two senses are one image — curvature — applied to shore and line alike. The sound history is unbroken and simple: PIE *bʰ → Germanic *b (with aspiration lost), the vowel *e raised through the umlaut-driven forms to Old English y and then to modern ai; the spelling gh is purely ornamental, inherited from the Middle English spelling bight and now silent. A caution belongs here: the root *bʰewg- "to flee" (whence Latin fugere, English fugitive, refuge) is a different root that only coincidentally resembles the bend-root; the two must never be merged. In the card's sentence the bight is the thin curve of sand and scrub pine in the haze — the coastline rendered as a curving line, exactly the ancient native meaning of the word.

  • disembark: Egyptian bꜣjr → Greek bâris → Late Latin barca → French embarquer → English embark (1540s) → disembark (c. 1590). No PIE ancestry.
  • aboard: PIE *bʰer-dʰ-? (debated) → PGmc *burdą → OE bord → on bord → aboard (14th c.).
  • lighter: PIE *h₁lengʷʰ- → PGmc *linhtaz → OE lēoht → līhtan → lighten + -er → lighter (1480s).
  • chattels: PIE *kap-ut- → Latin caput → capitale → Old French chatel → chattels; doublet cattle.
  • bight: PIE *bʰewgʰ- → PGmc *buhtiz → OE byht → bight.
