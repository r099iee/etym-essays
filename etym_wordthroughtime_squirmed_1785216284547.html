- [1640s: the worm] - squirm enters the written record purely physical: 'to twist and wriggle the body,' used of eels, snakes, and worms, and of people struggling out of ropes and tight places; dialect forms such as squirn hint at an older, unrecorded spoken life.
- [17th-18th centuries: escape and restraint] - the word's natural habitat is confinement - wriggling out of a hold, out from under restraint, out of a coat; the motion is ungainly, animal, and undignified.
- [19th century: the moral transfer] - to squirm gains its transferred sense: to writhe with embarrassment, guilt, or discomfort; the body's twisting becomes inward moral unease, so that an awkward question 'makes someone squirm.'
- [19th-20th centuries: the family grows] - squirmy (a restless child; a situation that breeds unease) and the noun squirm (the motion, or a pang of embarrassment) extend the word beyond the verb.
- [Contemporary: two living patterns] - the physical (squirm through an opening, squirm out of a hold) and the figurative (squirm with guilt) remain equally standard; informal and vividly physical, the word's skw- onset and curling -erm syllable enact the very motion it names.
