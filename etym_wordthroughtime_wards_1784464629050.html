“Ward” entered English meaning to guard, watch over, or defend — the verb of protection, and from it the many noun senses of those who guard and the places that keep watch.
“Ward” from the guarding sense built the lockmaker’s technical vocabulary: the internal ridges and projections that guard a lock against wrong keys are “wards,” and the key’s corresponding grooves “ward” past them — the lock’s mechanical password.
“Ward” through the centuries spun off a whole family — a hospital ward (the guarded room), a ward of court (the guarded person), “ward off” (to fend off) — all from the same protection core.
“Wards” today, in the locksmithing sense, still means those intricate internal ridges that only a correctly cut key can pass — a key “with intricate wards” is a small precious engine of guarded access.
