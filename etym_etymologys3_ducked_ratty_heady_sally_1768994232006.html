DUCKED — The biography of "duck" begins in the same root that flutters and shakes. The Proto-Indo-European base *dʰeu- describes violent, scattered motion — the shaking that Sanskrit preserves in dhū-, "to shake, agitate," and in dhūma, "smoke," the thing that shakes and drifts — though the family is not fully secured, and some scholars prefer an imitative (onomatopoeic) origin for the Germanic verb. In Germanic the base hardened into the diving verb *dūkaną / *dūkijaną, "to duck, dive, stoop."

  • The bird-name. Old English built its bird-name on the verb: the dūce, the "diver," the duck that upends itself for food, replacing the older Germanic bird-word (Old English ened, cognate with Latin anas, "duck"). The verb *dūcan, "to duck," is inferred from that bird-name.
  • Cognates. The water sense spread through the Germanic family: Old High German tuhhan → German tauchen, "to dive, submerge"; Middle Dutch dūken → Dutch duiken; Old Norse dýfa, "to dip, immerse" (possibly related, uncertain).
  • Middle English. By 1300 Middle English had douken/duken, the quick lowering of the body, and the word never stopped being physical.
  • Sound changes. The vowel shortened and shifted through Middle English, and the preterite "ducked" shows the regular weak dental suffix of English verbs — a fully native formation.

Semantic drift: from immersion to avoidance. English uniquely broadened "duck" from diving into water to dodging: one ducks a blow, a branch, a missile, or a doorframe. The physical lowering becomes a gesture of avoidance, the origin of "duck and weave," "duck and cover" (the Cold War civil-defense drill), "duck out," and "duck a question." The noun wandered in other directions too: the cricketer's "duck" (a score of nought, short for "duck's egg") and the British term of endearment "duck." Literary English used the verb for bowing and submission; the medieval "ducking stool" punished scolds by immersion.

In McCarthy's passage, "the kid ducked into the canvas tent" is the word at its most physical: the body compressing to enter a mean and cramped space, a single completed act of submission to squalor, carrying with it the whole history of diving, dodging, and the bird that shares its name.

RATTY — "Ratty" is a young word built on a very old animal, and the animal-word itself is famously uncertain. The Germanic *rattaz — Old English ræt, Middle English ratte, modern rat — is of disputed origin, and two main theories compete.

  • Native Indo-European. Some scholars connect the word with Latin rōdere, "to gnaw," under the PIE root *reid-, "to scratch, tear, gnaw," making the rat the "gnawer"; the same root yields English rodent, corrode, and erode.
  • Wanderwort. Others treat it as a substrate or trade word that crossed Europe with the rodent itself — carried along the same routes as the black rat, which reached Europe from Asia in the Middle Ages. The Romance words — French rat, Italian ratto, Spanish rata — are borrowings back from Germanic, evidence of how the animal and its name moved together.

The adjective builds. Upon the English noun, the native adjectival suffix -y (from Old English -ig) built "ratty" in the early nineteenth century, and the adjective developed its meanings in a clear sequence:

  • 1812 — "of or like a rat": the rodent transferred to a quality.
  • 1840s — "infested with rats": the animal becomes an infestation.
  • 1867 — "shabby, worn": the vermin-sense slips to general squalor, gnawed and soiled.
  • 1880s — "angry, irritable" (chiefly British, of a person): the worn thing becomes a snappish temper, as if bitten.

The socio-cultural context matters: "rat" itself carried widening moral freight in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries — the informer who "rats" on his companions, the "rat race," the rat as vermin of cities — and "ratty" absorbed that squalor. McCarthy's "ratty canvas tent" sits exactly in the 1867 sense, but the older meanings — vermin, gnawing, infestation — remain audible in it, so that the tent itself seems to have been chewed by the very poverty it contains. The word is a small capsule of disgust whose animal root is always present.

HEADY — "Heady" is the adjective of the head, and the head's PIE name *kaput- is one of the most productive roots in the whole lexicon. From it Latin made caput, and from caput Europe drew capital, captain, chief, chef, chapter, and the Romance capo and cabeza; Sanskrit made kapāla, the skull, which became Hindi kapāl; Tocharian kept kap; and the Germanic world shifted the root by Grimm's Law into *haubudą, whence Old English hēafod and modern head.

  • Grimm's Law. The shift from PIE *k- to Germanic *h- and from *p- to *b- transformed *kaput- into Proto-Germanic *haubudą: the initial velar became the voiceless fricative, the medial labial became voiced. Old English hēafod carried the word into Middle English as hed or heved, and modern English spells it head with a stable pronunciation /hɛd/. The same Germanic form gives German Haupt, Dutch hoofd, and Gothic haubiþ.
  • False siblings. Greek kephalē, "head," is a different word from a different root (*ghebhel-) — a sister, not a cognate, which is why "head" and "cephalic" are unrelated.

The adjective "heady." Upon "head," English formed "heady" in the fourteenth century with the adjective suffix -y (from Old English -ig). Its senses developed in two waves:

  • 14th century — "headstrong, impetuous, hasty": the quality of acting from the head alone, now archaic, surviving in the noun "headiness."
  • c. 1570 — "apt to affect the head, going to the head, intoxicating": applied to wine, perfume, and aroma — a force that acts on the senses like alcohol or a drug.
  • Modern extension — "heady days," "heady heights," "heady success": exhilarating overload, at once glamorous and dizzying; in journalism and wine-writing the word describes a thrilling, consciousness-filling intensity.

It is the second sense that McCarthy seizes in "heady reek": the smell is so dense it acts on the brain like wine, and the word that normally advertises pleasure here advertises suffocation. The adjective's double life — headstrong or intoxicating — is the whole point of its use in the passage.

SALLY — "Sally" is a leap that traveled from Asia to Europe and from the battlefield to the page. Its PIE root, *sel-, "to spring, jump, leap," survives in Greek hállesthai, "to leap" (with s → h), and in Vedic Sanskrit sarati, "to run, flow" (root sṛ-). Latin carried the root in salīre, "to leap," which proved extraordinarily fertile.

  • assilīre, "to leap upon" → English assail, assault;
  • exsilīre, "to leap out" → exult;
  • resilīre, "to leap back" → resilient;
  • insultāre, "to leap at, mock" → insult;
  • resultāre, "to leap back, rebound" → result;
  • saliēns, "leaping" → salient (1656), "leaping" then "prominent, projecting";
  • saltāre, the frequentative, "to leap, dance" → saltation, Italian saltare, French sauter.

The French detour that made "sally." The exact ancestor of the note's word is Old French saillir, "to leap, rush, gush forth," from Vulgar Latin. Its feminine past participle saillie named the military sortie — the garrison's sudden, deliberate rush out against the besiegers, launched through the sally port, the gate in the fortification. The shift from "gushing forth" (of water or blood) to "rushing out" (of soldiers) is a leap in language itself.

English career. English took the noun in 1540 and the verb in the 1550s, and the word then branched through the centuries:

  • 16th century — the military sortie: the garrison breaking out against the besieger.
  • 17th century — extended to speech: a "sally of wit," a sudden pointed verbal attack.
  • Modern — deliberately archaic and mock-heroic: "sally forth" survives in literary diction for an old-fashioned, purposeful breaking-out, its siege origin still audible. (The female given name Sally is a separate word, from Sarah.)

Because Germanic lost the root *sel-, all the English descendants — sally, assault, exult, resilient, insult, result, salient — are Latin/French loans, a rare case where English borrowed back a whole family through the Romance channel. McCarthy's "sally forth into the downpour" concentrates the whole genealogy: an ancient leap, a medieval sortie, and a modern wit, all breaking out of a besieged and suffocating tent for a mouthful of rain.
