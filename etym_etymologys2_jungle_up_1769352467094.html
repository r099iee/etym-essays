Compound root word: jungle-up (American English phrasal verb, from the noun jungle + the particle up). Because the compound has two root components, each is traced separately. CHAIN FOR "jungle" (the noun): 1. Sanskrit jaṅgala, an adjective-noun meaning "dry, arid, uncultivated land; wasteland, desert, waterless ground" — attested in Sanskrit of the ancient period. NOTE: the deeper origin of jaṅgala itself is uncertain; it is widely suspected to be of non-Indo-Aryan substrate origin (possibly Dravidian or another early Indian language), so no Proto-Indo-European root is reconstructable for it. This is stated explicitly because the word is not Indo-European in origin. 2. Hindi / Urdu jaṅgal, "forest, wild land, wilderness, uncultivated overgrown country" — the sense shifted from "arid, dry waste" to "dense, wild vegetation" as the word was used of the forested regions of the subcontinent. Attested in Hindi and Urdu; via Indo-Aryan transmission to colonial British English. 3. English jungle, "land overgrown with dense, tangled vegetation, a tropical forest or wilderness" — borrowed into English from Hindi through the East India Company and colonial administration, c. 1770s–1780s (OED first attestation 1776); the word entered general English and then figurative use ("the law of the jungle," "concrete jungle") in the 19th c. 4. American English jungle in hobo slang, "a rough encampment of vagrants or tramps, typically in wild overgrown ground near a railway or water" — attested from the late 19th c.; the "hobo jungle" transferred the sense of wild, untended, lawless land to the unofficial camps of the road. 5. American English jungle + the particle up —> jungle-up, "to set up a makeshift camp in the open, to settle for the night as a tramp or hobo" — attested in early 20th-c. American slang. Component node: jungle. CHAIN FOR "up" (the particle): 1. Proto-Indo-European root *upo (also *h₁upo), "under, up from under, below, from below upward" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE; attested in Sanskrit úpa, Greek hypó, Latin sub. 2. Proto-Germanic *upp, "up, upward, from below" — a particle formed on the zero-grade of the same root; attested descendants include Old English up / upp, Old Norse upp, Old High German ūf (modern German auf). 3. Old English up / upp, "up, upward" — attested from the earliest Old English texts. 4. English up, "to a higher position; completely, thoroughly (aspectual)" — the particle used to mark completed action or assembly in phrasal verbs (fix up, build up, rig up). Component node: up. FINAL COMPOUND NODE: jungle + up —> jungle-up, "to set up a makeshift camp in a rough outdoor area" — American hobo and tramp slang of the early 20th c. (period of the Steinbeck-era railroad culture). DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The only genuinely uncertain stage is the origin of Sanskrit jaṅgala itself; the deeper derivation of the Hindi-English word jungle is not Indo-European and cannot be traced to PIE. The formation of the phrasal verb jungle-up is transparently English (noun + aspectual particle) and is not separately reconstructed. No other stage is in dispute.
