Clarinets (plural of clarinet) are single-reed woodwind instruments with a roughly cylindrical bore, a flared bell, and a mouthpiece that holds a single cane reed—capable of a wide range from chalumeau darkness to brilliant upper register, and famous for a clear, singing, flexible tone. They are relative latecomers to European art music (mature form from the early eighteenth century) but thoroughly established by Tolkien's modern English. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, Bifur and Bofur go out and come back with clarinets that they had left among the walking-sticks: woodwinds stored with practical gear, as natural to dwarves as packs and sticks. Tone: slightly anachronistic-comic if one presses real-world instrument history against ‘ancient’ dwarves, but in the book's English the word simply stock-lists a clear-voiced reed instrument beside fiddles, flutes, and drum—texture for the company band.

Anatomy and sound. A clarinet has a single reed clamped to a mouthpiece, a mostly cylindrical tube (which gives it a different acoustics from conical instruments), a key system along the body, and a flared bell. Its lowest register, the chalumeau register, is dark and mellow; the upper registers grow bright and piercing. Because of the cylindrical bore it overblows at the twelfth rather than the octave, a quirk that shaped its key system. Its tone has been praised for a voice-like, flexible quality, which is exactly why jazz later prized it.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. An oboe is double-reed with a narrower conical bore and a more nasal tone. A saxophone is single-reed but metal and conical, a 19th-century invention. A flute (also in the paragraph) is edge-blown without any reed. A chalumeau is an earlier single-reed ancestor without the register key and extended range. A recorder is a fipple-flute, not a reed instrument. Clarinets keeps the single-reed cylindrical woodwind, plural. Context selects instruments retrieved from the hall—music as portable dwarf property, not metaphor.

Polysemy and modern usages. The word's semantic range, past and present, includes the following:

  • The instrument (primary sense, here plural): a single-reed, cylindrical-bore woodwind with a flared bell; the standard soprano member of the clarinet family. The sense fixed by the context.
  • The clarinet family: in musical jargon, ‘clarinet’ also names the whole family—soprano clarinet, alto clarinet, bass clarinet, and the rare contrabass—so that ‘the clarinets’ can mean the section or the instrument type as a class.
  • Acoustics and instrument-building sense: in technical usage, the word labels the cylindrical-bore, single-reed design and its overblowing behaviour (the twelfth), distinguishing it from conical single-reed instruments like the saxophone.
  • Slang: ‘licorice stick’ is the jazz-musicians' nickname for the clarinet, from the black wooden body and the smooth, sweet tone; ‘to squeak on the clarinet’ is a joke at beginners' expense.
  • Metaphoric and figurative use: rare compared with fiddle or drum, but ‘a clarinet-like tone’ and ‘the clarinet voice of the orchestra’ occur when writers want a clear, singing, woody timbre.

Historical bridge. The semantic reasoning runs: the instrument was named in French as a diminutive of clarine, a little bell or high clear-sounding instrument—itself from clair ‘clear, bright’, from Latin clarus—so the clarinet is literally ‘the little clear-toned instrument’. The name travels from the high, clear trumpet-like signals to the new reed woodwind prized for its clear, flexible voice.
