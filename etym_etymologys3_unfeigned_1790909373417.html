The adjective unfeigned is an English morphological compound constructed from the native Germanic privative prefix un- (denoting negation or reversal, tracing back to Proto-Indo-European *n̥-) and the participial adjective feigned (the past participle of the verb feign). The verb feign was borrowed into Middle English in the late 13th century from Old French feindre (earlier feigner), which evolved from the Classical Latin verb fingere, meaning 'to touch, handle, mold, knead clay, fashion, devise, invent, or fabricate a falsehood.' Latin fingere descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeiǵʰ-, meaning 'to knead, form, mold clay, or build a wall of earth,' which also gave rise to English dough, fiction, figment, figure, and paradise (via Old Persian paridaida, an enclosed earthen garden). The conceptual shift from 'shaping clay' to 'inventing stories' and ultimately to 'faking or pretending' occurred in Roman antiquity, where an invented persona or false excuse was described as fictum or fictio.

The historical journey of unfeigned through the English language highlights its role as a bedrock term of ethical purity, spiritual authenticity, and emotional transparency:

  • Middle English Formation and Moral Theology (14th Century): The compound unfeyned first appeared in written English during the second half of the 14th century (notably in the works of John Wycliffe, Geoffrey Chaucer, and John Gower). It was coined specifically to translate Latin theological phrases such as fides non ficta ('unfeigned faith') and caritas non ficta ('unfeigned love'). In Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde and devotional texts of the era, unfeigned immediately stood as the ultimate mark of genuine moral character, contrasting sharply with the hypocrisy and dissimulation condemned in medieval ethics.
  • The Reformation and the King James Bible (16th–17th Centuries): The prominence of unfeigned was indelibly established across the English-speaking world by 16th-century Protestant translators, particularly William Tyndale, the Book of Common Prayer (1549), and the King James Authorized Bible (1611). In passages like 1 Timothy 1:5 ('Now the end of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned') and 2 Corinthians 6:6 ('by love unfeigned'), the word was chosen to translate the Greek ἀνυπόκριτος (anypókritos—composed of a- 'not' + hypokritēs 'stage actor, pretender'). Thus, unfeigned explicitly meant 'without playacting' or 'without theatrical masks.'
  • Enlightenment and Victorian Literature (18th–19th Centuries): During the 18th and 19th centuries, the word transitioned from strictly devotional registers into refined psychological prose. In the novels of Jane Austen, the Brontë sisters, and Charles Dickens, unfeigned was frequently deployed to validate deep emotional truth against the backdrop of polite social artifice. Characters displayed 'unfeigned delight,' 'unfeigned astonishment,' or 'unfeigned repentance,' signaling to the reader that their inner virtue was incorruptible by social vanity.
  • Modern and Theatrical Usage (20th–21st Centuries): In contemporary English, while everyday casual conversation often favors genuine or sincere, unfeigned remains an elevated, highly expressive literary term. It is especially cherished in theatre criticism, acting theory, and serious literature to describe spontaneous, unfiltered human vulnerability—moments where the barrier between artifice and genuine raw human experience completely collapses.
