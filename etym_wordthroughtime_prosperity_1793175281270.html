- Rome — prosperus: according to hope, an adjective for favourable winds, fortunate undertakings, and men whom the gods oblige.
- Late Latin — prosperitas: the abstract noun of the thriving state, used by moralists for the dangerous gift of good fortune, already shadowed by warnings.
- Middle Ages — prosperite: Chaucer's age borrows it through French for worldly success, ever paired with its wheel — prosperity and adversity turn like day and night.
- 1611 — the Bible's blessing: in the day of prosperity be joyful fixes the word as God's smile, broader than money, covering harvest, health, and sons.
- 18th–19th c. — the economist's measure: political economy narrows it toward trade, employment, and output; nations now enjoy prosperity the way fields enjoy rain.
- Today — toast and indicator: prosperity lives double, the warmest word in the wedding speech and the driest in the treasury report.
