Banished is the past tense and past participle of the verb banish, meaning 'to send (someone) away from a place, country or community as a punishment or by authority; to cast out; to expel'. As a participle it also works adjectivally — 'a banished prince', 'the banished gods' — and figuratively, of things driven from the mind or from a situation: 'fear banished', 'banished from memory'.

Primary sense — exile by authority. To banish a person is to sentence them to leave, typically a ruler, court, church or state decreeing that a named person must depart a country or territory, often forever, on pain of death if they return. Historically this was a formal punishment — banishment by the Roman Senate and emperors, English outlawry, the French banissement — and it differs from mere expulsion in being a judicial or sovereign act aimed at a specific person. 'He was banished from the realm'; 'the tyrant banished his rivals' — the construction with from is the norm. The result of the act is a state: the banished person is an exile, cut off from house, kin and rights.

Secondary sense — driving away generally. Outside legal language, banish means to force something out of a place, person or situation: to banish intruders, to banish the cold, to banish darkness. It shades into 'drive off' or 'dismiss' and is common in fantasy and heroic writing about evil — banishing a demon, banishing the shadow. The verb keeps the stamp of authority: something is banished by a greater power or will, and the banishment is meant to be complete.

Tertiary sense — figurative removal from the mind. The commonest modern use is psychological: to banish a thought, a fear, a doubt, a memory — to make it leave one's mind decisively. 'She banished all hesitation.' 'He banished the memory.' The semantic bridge: as a sovereign expels a person from a country, so the will expels an unwelcome content from the mind; the banishment is deliberate, complete, and intended to be permanent — though, like political exiles, such thoughts can always try to return.

Grammatical and collocational notes.

  • Forms: banish / banishing / banished; the participle serves in perfects ('has banished'), passives ('was banished'), and as an adjective ('the banished man').
  • Collocations: 'banished from (the kingdom / the court / society)'; 'banished to (an island, the provinces)'; 'banished for life'; 'banish fear / doubt / gloom'; 'banished from the world / from memory'.
  • Register: formal and literary. In everyday speech 'sent away', 'kicked out', 'deported' or 'expelled' are plainer; 'banished' carries solemn, judicial or tale-like weight — which is precisely why Gandalf uses it of Sauron in Tolkien's The Fellowship of the Ring: 'Yet I wish he were banished from the world!'
  • Related words: banishment (the act or state), ban, banns, bandit, banal, abandon — a far-flung family sprung from a Germanic base meaning 'to proclaim', via French and Italian.

Literary resonance. Banishment is a great theme of myth, drama and fantasy: Adam and Eve banished from Eden, Ovid exiled to the Black Sea, Cordelia banished by Lear, the banished duke in Arden in As You Like It. The participle 'banished' in particular rings in curses and verdicts — Shakespeare's Romeo cries that there is no world without Verona walls, 'but banished' — and in Tolkien it reaches to nothingness itself: to be banished from the world is to be unmade from the story of the world. Across these uses the word conveys authority, finality and pity at once: the banished are condemned, yet the word mourns them.
