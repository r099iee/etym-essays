In the preface to his study of English poetic rhythm, the author explains that he has drawn 'extensively and promiscuously on the poetry of writers with established reputations from Shakespeare to Yeats.' The adverb carries a tone of deliberate, lightly self-deprecating openness: he freely mingles poets from very different periods, canons, and styles, taking examples wherever he found them rather than confining himself to one school, century, or pedigree. The nuance is 'indiscriminately' but emphatically not 'carelessly' — this is a consciously eclectic borrowing, old and new, celebrated and obscure flung together without fussy distinctions of taste, because the aim is to illustrate general points about metre rather than to build a hierarchy of poetry. The word is used here in its original, scholarly sense, before (or alongside) its modern sexual association.

Polysemy: the senses of PROMISCUOUSLY
  • Sense 1 — indiscriminately, without distinction (primary, original): 'In a random, indiscriminate, or unselective manner — drawing freely from many mixed sources without distinction or preference.' This is the sense the adverb has had since it entered English around 1604, and the sense the preface uses: a mixing of many kinds together, with no sorting by taste.
  • Sense 2 — sexual (modern dominant): As the adjective promiscuous came to be applied to persons 'not restricted to one sexual partner,' the adverb acquired the corresponding sense, 'in a sexually promiscuous manner,' now the dominant everyday meaning, carrying strong moral disapproval. The bridge: from 'mixed, common, indiscriminate' to 'mixing without distinction' in the sexual domain.
  • Sense 3 — scientific/technical (modern jargon): In biology and chemistry, promiscuous describes molecules, enzymes, or binding sites that interact with many different partners: a promiscuous enzyme catalyses many substrates; promiscuous DNA or receptors bind widely; in network theory, promiscuous connectivity. The bridge: a return to the root idea of 'mixing with many' in a neutral, technical register.
  • Sense 4 — scholarly reclamation: In academic prose, promiscuously is still used in its original colour, meaning 'drawn freely and indiscriminately from many mixed sources' — as in the preface's 'extensively and promiscuously' — a consciously eclectic, lightly self-deprecating mingling without fussy distinctions of taste.

The semantic boundary: The near-synonyms differ in telling ways. Indiscriminately stresses the mere absence of selection or care; randomly emphasizes pure chance with no governing principle at all; haphazardly implies a disorder bordering on carelessness; arbitrarily suggests choice made on whim or without a rule; casually is the light, informal word for doing something without fuss; unselectively is the neutral, formal term for taking everything; liberally and freely praise abundance and openness without the suggestion of disorder; eclectically is the positive word for choosing from many schools with taste; heterogeneously describes the mixed character of the result rather than the manner of the act. Only promiscuously combines 'mixed' and 'without distinction' in a single word — and, in modern ears, quietly carries the ghost of its sexual sense, which is why scholars use it with a knowing smile.
