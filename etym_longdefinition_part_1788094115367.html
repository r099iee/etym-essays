PART is one of the most heavily used words in English, functioning as noun, verb, adjective, and adverb. Its core meaning as a noun is "a portion, piece, or division of a larger whole"; as a verb it means "to divide or separate", and — the sense required by the reading context from Tolkien's The Hobbit — "to take leave of someone, to go away from them". In the passage ("where it is of little worth, I wish to ___ in friendship from you, and I would take back my words"), Bilbo tells Thorin that he wishes to part in friendship: to separate, to leave his company, while remaining on friendly terms. This verbal sense of part — "to separate from, to leave" — is one of the oldest English verbal uses (via Old French partir "to divide, share out, go away"), and it survives today chiefly in formal and literary registers: to part from (a person), to part with (a possession, i.e., to give it up), the ways part (roads diverge), to part company.

Primary senses of the noun:

  • A portion of a whole: "a part of the cake", "the best part of the day". The most basic, concrete sense: a piece or share considered in relation to the whole.
  • A component or member: "spare parts", "car parts", "the parts of a machine" — a functioning constituent of an assembly.
  • A share, allotment, or role in an undertaking: "to take part in", "to play one's part", "for my part", "part and parcel". Here the sense is participatory: one's portion of responsibility or involvement.
  • A role in a play or film: "to play the part of Hamlet" — a theatrical extension of "share in an undertaking"; the actor's assigned portion of the script.
  • A musical line or voice: "the violin part", "a four-part harmony" — one strand of a polyphonic texture.
  • A side in a dispute or question: "on my part", "on the part of the government" — the portion of a viewpoint or responsibility.
  • A region or area: "in these parts" ("in this region"), "in foreign parts" — an archaic-to-literary plural use meaning "regions, districts".
  • Grammar: part of speech — one of the classes of words (noun, verb, adjective, and so on).
  • The parting of the hair: the line where the hair is divided, called a part in American English (British: a parting).

Primary senses of the verb:

  • To divide or separate (transitive): "to part the curtains", "the police parted the crowd", "to part one's hair" (to divide it with a comb).
  • To separate from, to leave (intransitive): "to part from a friend", "we parted at the station", "to part in friendship" (the Tolkien sense) — to go away from someone, to take leave.
  • To part with: to give up, to relinquish a possession ("I would not part with this book for anything") — the bridge is "to separate oneself from a thing".
  • To part company (with): to stop associating; also, of ships, to lose sight of one another.

Adjective and adverb uses. Part means "partial, in part" in compounds and fixed phrases: "a part payment", "part-time", "part-way", "part and parcel" (an essential component). The adverb sense "partly" appears in such constructions as "part one thing, part another".

Semantic bridges. All senses radiate from the Latin root sense of "a share, a portion" (Latin pars). The verb senses come from the notion of "sharing out, dividing into parts": what is divided separates, and people who divide company separate from one another. The theatrical and musical senses extend "a share" to "an assigned portion of a performance". The phrases for the most part ("mostly"), in part ("partly"), and take part ("participate") all preserve the portioning sense. The phrase part and parcel (from the 15th century) redundantly pairs two words for "portion" to mean "an integral element". The industrial age added spare parts (a component kept in reserve) and part-time (a share of the working week), while American English keeps part for the division of the hair — the line where the hair is "parted", i.e., divided.

Register today. The noun senses are among the most frequent words in English; the verbal sense "to leave, to separate" is now distinctly formal, literary, or archaic — which is precisely why Tolkien uses it in the elevated dialogue of The Hobbit. In everyday speech one would say "leave" or "say goodbye"; part carries a weight of ceremony and finality ("we parted as friends", "they parted on bad terms"), making it ideal for farewell scenes and for the solemn moment when Bilbo takes back his words at the Gate.
