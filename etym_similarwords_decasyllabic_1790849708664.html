• Pentameter: Similar because in English accentual-syllabic verse, iambic pentameter consists of ten syllables; differs because pentameter strictly means a line of five metrical feet (regardless of whether the feet contain two, three, or variable syllables), whereas decasyllabic strictly counts the total number of syllables (ten) regardless of foot structure.
  • Hendecasyllabic: Similar because both are classical and Romance metrical lines of near-identical length; differs because hendecasyllabic refers to an eleven-syllable line (common in Catullus and Italian poetry with a feminine ending), while decasyllabic has exactly ten syllables.
  • Alexandrine: Similar because both are major classical European poetic meters; differs because an Alexandrine is a twelve-syllable line (hexameter), standard in classical French drama (Corneille, Racine), which is longer and more solemn than the ten-syllable decasyllabic line.
  • Octosyllabic: Similar because both are syllabic verse forms defined by syllable counts; differs because octosyllabic lines contain only eight syllables, making them faster, lighter, and more suited to ballads and short romances.
  • Metrical: Similar because both relate to poetic rhythm and formal verse construction; differs because metrical is a broad, overarching term describing any verse governed by regular poetic measure, whereas decasyllabic specifies the exact syllable count of ten.
  • Prosodic: Similar because both pertain to the study and practice of versification; differs because prosodic encompasses the entire field of poetic sound, rhythm, intonation, and stress patterns across all languages, while decasyllabic describes one specific metrical structure.
