- Medieval High German Era: Rooted in Middle High German *beruofen*, meaning to call upon, summon, or cast an evil spell by invoking someone's name or speaking incautiously.
- 16th to 19th Century (German & Yiddish Tradition): Crystallized in Central and Eastern Europe as the apotropaic formula *unberufen* (or Yiddish *um berufn* / *unberufen*), meaning "unsummoned / uncalled-for", spoken immediately after praising a child's health or boasting of prosperity to avoid alerting jealous demons or the evil eye (*ayin hara*).
- Late 19th to Early 20th Century (Anglo-Jewish & British Slang): Borrowed into British society through Jewish communities in London and Anglicized phonetically into slang variations like "unberoofen", "oom beroofen", or "beroofen".
- 1920s to Mid-20th Century (P.G. Wodehouse & Comedic Fiction): Popularized permanently across English literature by P.G. Wodehouse in his *Jeeves and Wooster* and *Blandings Castle* novels, where characters like Aunt Dahlia, Bertie Wooster, and Lord Emsworth regularly utter "oom beroofen" whenever they fear they have jinxed their good fortune.
