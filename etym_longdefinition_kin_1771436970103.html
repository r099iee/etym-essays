Kin denotes one's family and relations collectively — the whole body of persons connected by blood or by marriage. In the passage from Dream of the Red Chamber, the narrator records that a character "married his own kin, the niece of his Uncle Zheng, strengthening the family's ties." The word's force here is anthropological before it is emotional: it marks the marriage as an act of consolidation rather than of romance. The bride is not a stranger entering from outside but kin already — a niece, a blood-relation of the bridegroom's uncle — so that the wedding knits the extended clan more tightly upon itself, folding branch into branch. The register is that of family chronicle and lineage accounting, the novel's abiding fascination with descent, inheritance, and the moral mathematics of the great house. To marry "one's own kin" is a closed-circuit transaction of power and property within the Jia lineage, and the word kin is the narrator's shorthand for that whole dense web of consanguinity.

The word is polysemous, and each sense grows out of the ancient idea that one's "kind" is one's born nature:

  • Sense 1 — blood relations collectively (primary). The whole body of persons connected by blood: parents, children, uncles, aunts, cousins, and the far-flung branches of the lineage. This is the sense on the card, where the bride is already kin by blood before the marriage.
  • Sense 2 — relatives by marriage (affinal kin). In modern and anthropological usage kin embraces in-laws and step-relations as well as blood, so that marriage extends, rather than creates, the kin network.
  • Sense 3 — the legal phrase "next of kin." The nearest blood relative, with rights and duties in inheritance, medical decision-making, and law; the phrase is the word's most conservative and most modern survival.
  • Sense 4 — the archaic categorical sense. In Old English, cynn meant any kind, race, or species — the kin of animals, the kin of things. That categorical sense has migrated almost entirely into the cognate kind, so that modern kin is narrowed to people alone.
  • Sense 5 — metaphorical kinship. Species, spirits, or ideas felt to be related are said to be akin, "kindred spirits," or "of a kind"; anthropologists speak of a kinship system or kin network. The adjective akin has drifted from "of one's blood" to "similar in kind," a quiet metaphor now felt as plain resemblance.
  • Sense 6 — the compound and idiomatic family. Kindred, kinsman/kinswoman, kinfolk/kinsfolk, and the fixed pair kith and kin, where kith adds one's friends and acquaintances — the known ones alongside the blood ones.

Against its neighbours the distinctions are exact. Family is today the narrow, household-level unit — parents and children under one roof — whereas kin is wider and older, embracing the whole bloodline. Relatives is the plain, modern, slightly bureaucratic catch-all for any relation by blood or marriage; kin carries an archaic, formal, even legal dignity that relatives lacks. Clan names a unilineal descent group with a strong territorial or chiefly identity (especially Scottish), and tribe an even larger group usually seen from outside; kin stays intimate, genealogical, and blood-precise. In register kin is formal-to-archaic, most at home in legal language, historical fiction, and scholarly discourse; in everyday speech it survives almost only inside its fixed phrases. Its close cognate kind preserves the deeper idea — "of the same born nature" — which is why a kind person and a kin group are, etymologically, one family.
