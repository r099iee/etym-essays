Rime as a noun means a white coating of frost or ice formed when water vapour or supercooled fog droplets freeze on cold surfaces — on grass, branches, beards, eyebrows — granular, feathery, and glittering; also called hoarfrost in looser use. As a verb it means to cover with such frost.

The prototypical image is Dickensian: a frosty rime on head, eyebrows, and wiry chin. Breath condenses and freezes, fog touches hair and whiten each strand. It is not snowfall (flakes falling) nor glaze (clear ice from rain) nor dew frozen flat, but a rough, opaque, crystalline accretion that grows windward, thickening twigs and rigging.

  • Meteorological rime / hard and soft rime: in modern weather science, soft rime is feathery, easily shaken off; hard rime is dense, wind-driven, hazardous to aircraft, power lines, and masts. Mountaineers dread rimed cornices. This technical precision preserves the old word in aviation manuals and mountain forecasts long after everyday speech prefers frost.
  • Poetic hoarfrost: in literature rime means any beautiful white frost-work: rimed hedges, rimed windows, fields silvered with rime. Keats, Coleridge, and Dickens use it for age, purity, and cold beauty. Hoar (grey-white with age) overlaps: an old man's rimed beard suggests both winter and years.
  • Verb to rime: to frost, to encrust with ice. Example: fog rimed the rigging; his breath rimed his scarf. Past rimed, also spelled rhymed never for frost. Adjective rimy means frosty.
  • Figurative age and coldness of character: a head rimed with years, a heart rimed with indifference. Dickens exploits this: Scrooge's outward rime manifests inward cold. The metaphor works because rime both whitens (age) and chills (unfeeling).
  • Homophone trap — rime vs rhyme: verse-rhyme (matching sounds, Ancient Mariner's Rime as tale) comes from Old French rime from Latin rhythmus, unrelated to frost-rime from Old English hrim. Pronunciation is identical /raɪm/, spelling differs only by h. Learners must keep them apart: frost is rime, poetry is rhyme (though Coleridge archaically used Rime for his poem).

Register is literary-technical. Everyday conversation says frost (frost on the car), farmers and poets say rime/hoarfrost, meteorologists and pilots say rime icing. Using rime signals either poetic elevation or specialist precision. It is countable (a heavy rime) and mass (much rime).

Visual and tactile qualities matter: rime is white, opaque, needle-like, rough to touch, glittering in sun, fragile yet caking. Unlike powder snow it adheres; unlike black ice it shows. To picture rime, imagine eyebrows turned white, twigs feathered, spider webs stiffened into lace.

Everyday contrasts help fix the sense: snow falls and blankets, frost settles on still nights, rime grows into the wind from freezing fog, feathery on the windward side. Gardeners know rime kills buds by desiccation, sailors know it caps rigging and must be beaten off, photographers chase it for black-branch-white-needle contrast. Remembering Dickens helps: Scrooge is rimed because he has stood too long in his own freezing fog, and the coating proves exposure.
