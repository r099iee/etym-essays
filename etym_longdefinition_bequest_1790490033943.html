bequest as a noun names both the act of bequeathing and, more commonly today, that which is bequeathed: a legacy, a gift directed by a will to take effect after death. In the source sentence — He accepted the bequest, and paid them all before he began to lay by anything for himself — Ruskin refers back to the debts bequeathed by his grandfather: the father treats even liabilities as a solemn bequest, accepts rather than renounces, pays in full, and is remembered as an entirely honest merchant. The singular the bequest here is collective, covering the whole burden as one moral item. Victorian readers, for whom probate, executors, and legacies were household realities, would hear both law and character: to accept a bequest is to accept the testator's will as binding.

Modern law and charity keep bequest alive. Solicitors draft specific bequests (a named object: my gold watch), general bequests (a sum: £5,000), demonstrative bequests (a sum from a named fund), residuary bequests (what remains), and charitable bequests (gifts to museums, colleges, hospices). Fundraisers solicit gifts in wills and legacy pledges under the warmer label leave a bequest. E.g. to receive a generous bequest, to contest a bequest, to administer bequests, the Beaumont bequest of paintings, a bequest for scholarships. Museums label rooms Bequest of...; colleges list benefactors by bequest; wills state I make the following bequests.

  • Act sense (oldest, c. 1300): the bequeathing: e.g. by bequest of his father, the bequest of property is restricted by law. Formal, procedural; now often replaced by bequeathing or disposition by will, but surviving in phrases like by bequest, deed of bequest. The bridge to thing-sense is metonymy: the act gives its name to its product.
  • Thing sense (late 15th c.–present, dominant): legacy, inheritance: e.g. a bequest of £20,000, a bequest of books, to accept / decline a bequest. Always post-mortem, always willed; lifetime gifts are donations, grants, or presents, never bequests. Ruskin stretches it to debts, which lawyers would call liabilities of the estate rather than bequests proper, for ethical emphasis.
  • Charitable and cultural sense: endowments that found institutions; e.g. the Ashmolean's Tradescant bequest, Rhodes bequests, a bequest in aid of cancer research. Here the word carries prestige and gratitude: to be a legatee of a great bequest is honour, to make one is immortality. Campaigns translate it as your legacy, your lasting gift.
  • Figurative sense: anything handed down as if willed; e.g. the bequest of empire (railways and borders), the bequest of the sixties (music and attitudes), a bequest of pollution. Journalists favour it forvaluative retrospect: the past's will is read at the present's probate. Like bequeathed, often rueful.
  • Distinctions that matter: bequest (Germanic, personalty, will) versus legacy (Latin legatum, same, but more elegant), devise (realty), inheritance (what the heir gets), bequeathal (rare synonym of act), heirloom (object handed down through generations). To confuse bequest with request is a beginner's error: the t is unetymological (as in behest), not a sign of asking.

To know bequest is thus to know Victorian probate and modern philanthropy in one syllable: a dead hand directing living hands, accepted or contested, spent or stewarded, remembered on brass plaques and granite slabs.
