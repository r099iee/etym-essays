→ PIE expressive base *kēu- / *ū- (a long, open-mouthed cry; to howl, wail), reconstructed as an imitative root built directly on the sound of a prolonged open-throated wail; the same material appears in Latin ululāre (to howl; source of English ululate), Greek ololyzein (to cry aloud in ritual wailing), Sanskrit ululīkāra (a howling, wailing cry, Vedic), and Old English ūle (owl, named from the same hollow ū-sound) → Proto-Germanic *hūlijanan / *hūlōną (to howl, wail), late 2nd–1st millennium BCE; continued by Old High German hūwilōn / hiwilōn (later Middle High German hiuwelen, German heulen), Middle Low German hūlen (Dutch huilen), and Old Norse yla (from *ūl-; Swedish, Norwegian yla, Danish hyle) → Old English hūlian (to howl), attested in Old English records; the probable native basis of the Middle English verb → Middle English houlen (to utter a long, doleful cry — of wolves, dogs, the wind, and grieving or anguished people), first recorded in the 13th century CE → Modern English howl (verb and noun), the noun from the 16th century CE; howler (one who howls; later "a glaring blunder") from the 19th century → HOWL — the card's target form: the verb and noun for a long, loud, doleful cry. NOTE on the debate: the Middle English verb houlen may have been inherited from Old English hūlian or reinforced (or replaced) by Middle Low German hūlen and Scandinavian yla during the Viking and Hanseatic centuries; all three channels existed, and the exact contribution of each is not fully certain. The wider Indo-European items (ululāre, ololyzein, ululīkāra) are expressive parallels and imitative near-cousins, not strict descendants of a single reconstructed root.
