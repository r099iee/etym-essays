=== vellum === PIE *wet- "a year" (the cycle of seasons by which a young animal is measured; reconstructed c. 4500–2500 BCE) → Latin vitulus "a calf, a yearling" (the young beast counted by its first year; attested 1st c. BCE) → Old French veel "a calf" (9th–12th c.) — also the source of English veal, the calf as meat → Old French velin "made of calfskin" (12th–13th c.) → Middle English velym "vellum, fine calfskin writing material" (14th c.) → English vellum (present day) Cognates confirming the root: Sanskrit vatsá- "a calf, a yearling; a young animal or child," passing into Hindi as bachchā "a child; also a calf" (via Prakrit); Greek étos "year" (from *wétos, with the regular loss of initial w-); Latin vetus "old," literally "having years"; Proto-Germanic *weþruz "a yearling ram" → English wether. === battlements === PIE *bhau- "to strike, to beat" (reconstructed; note: the immediate Latin formation is debated) → Latin battuere "to beat, pound" (attested 1st c. BCE; its immediate origin is debated — possibly a borrowing from a Celtic source — though it is traditionally grouped under PIE *bhau-) → Late Latin battalia "fighting exercise, combat" (4th–5th c.) → Old French bataille "battle" (10th–11th c.) → English battle → Old French bataillier "to fight; also to fortify" (12th c.) → Old French bataillement "fortification; a parapet built for fighting" (12th–13th c.) → Middle English batelment "a wall-top fortification" (14th c.) → English battlement (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE/NUANCE: the immediate source of Latin battuere is disputed — it may be a Celtic loan (compare Gaulish forms), though it is traditionally placed under PIE *bhau- "to strike"; the sequence from battalia onward is secure. === cantonment === Pre-Greek kanthós "corner of the eye; edge, rim" (a substrate word with no verified PIE ancestry; attested in Greek from early antiquity) → Latin canthus "iron rim or tyre of a wheel; rim of a vessel" (borrowed from Greek; 1st c. CE) → Italian canto "corner, side, edge" (medieval) → Italian cantone "a large corner; a district" (medieval) → French canton "corner of territory, district" (15th c.) → French cantonnement "the quartering of troops in a district" (mid-17th c.) → English cantonment (mid-18th century; English also borrowed canton "a division of territory," as in the Swiss cantons) NOTE ON DEBATE/NUANCE: the deepest root of canthus is unverified — it is widely treated as a Pre-Greek/Mediterranean substrate word, and the semantic bridge (corner of the eye → rim → corner → district → military quarter) is regular, but each step is a separately attested leap.
