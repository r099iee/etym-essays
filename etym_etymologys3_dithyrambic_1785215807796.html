The adjective DITHYRAMBIC descends, without secure Indo-European ancestry, from the Ancient Greek noun dithyrambos, a cultic word whose oldest attested senses are both the ecstatic choral hymn sung and danced in honour of Dionysus and a title or epithet of that god himself. The origin of dithyrambos is generally held to lie in a Pre-Greek, probably Aegean or Anatolian substrate; the recurrent -nth- and -mb- consonants mark it as non-Indo-European, like labyrinthos and korinthos. Greek tradition connected the word with the winter festivals of wine and rebirth, and legend made Arion of Lesbos the first to give the dithyramb a fixed choral form.

The classical dithyramb. In archaic and classical Greece, the dithyramb was a choral song of fifty performers who danced and sang with unrestrained emotion, elevated diction, and a wildly complex rhythm, often in exchange with the aulos. It was the ritual seed from which Attic tragedy grew — Aristotle in the Poetics derives tragedy from the leaders of the dithyramb — and it was the medium in which the chorus of satyrs was later formalized. The god who presided over it was the god of wine, ecstasy, and possession; to sing a dithyramb was to be swept out of ordinary consciousness. From this concrete ritual base, the adjective dithyrambikos (“pertaining to a dithyramb”) arose in Greek and was borrowed by Latin as dithyrambicus.

The journey into European tongues. Through the Latin adjective the word passed into the vernaculars of Europe — Italian ditirambico, Spanish ditirambico, and French dithyrambique — and entered English in the early seventeenth century as dithyrambick. For two centuries it remained a technical term of classical rhetoric and prosody, describing a lyric of frenzied irregular metre. In the nineteenth century the word underwent a decisive metaphorical extension: critics began to use DITHYRAMBIC of any speech, prose, or praise that was intoxicated in tone, passionately exaggerated, and rhythmically exalted.

Mill and the modern sense. It is precisely this extended, metaphorical sense that Mill's Autobiography exploits. The “dithyrambic praise” of the “almost infallible counsellor” is praise that has taken on the character of a hymn — rhythmic, ecstatic, cult-like — and the word quietly conveys both the writer's adoration and the reader's sense that such worship exceeds the measure of sober prose. The word has retained this flavour into the twentieth and twenty-first centuries: it is a purely literary and critical adjective, still faintly orgiastic and ritual-flavoured, applied to the intoxicated enthusiasm of the crowd, the reviewer, or the lover.

  • Sound and form. The English word keeps the Greek -y- (dithyrambic), reflecting Greek u written as Latin y; the noun dithyramb and the plural dithyrambs accompany the adjective.
  • Register drift. From a genre-name of Greek ritual poetry, the word became a prosodic term, then a general critical word for ecstatic manner — a steady journey from concrete ritual to abstract style.
  • Literary attestations. The dithyramb is attested in Archilochus, Pindar, and the tragic poets; the adjective enters English criticism with the seventeenth-century revival of classical learning and is used throughout nineteenth-century literary biography — culminating in Mill's Autobiography.

The word thus preserves, in a single adjective, the whole history of an ancient ritual: the drunken winter festival of the god, the fifty dancers, the wild rhythm of the aulos, the birth of tragedy — and, in modern dress, the fervour of any enthusiasm too great for its object.
