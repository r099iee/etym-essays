Be so good is a fixed polite formula used to frame a command or request as a compliment on the hearer's kindness, meaning please be kind enough to do something or would you kindly do something. Grammatically it is an imperative (be) + degree adverb (so) + adjective (good), usually completed by as to + infinitive: be so good as to shut the door, be so good as to send me the bill. In practice it functions exactly like please + imperative, but with an older, more formal and distinctly upper-class British flavour. Bertie Wooster's Be so good, Jeeves, as to shove that thing in the cupboard is not a meditation on Jeeves's moral goodness; it is a peremptory order to change a dinner jacket, velveted in courtesy.

The force of the phrase lies in its elegant indirection. A bare imperative (bring my jacket) exposes power; please softens it minimally; be so good as to pretends the action will be voluntary proof of the hearer's good nature. This makes it ideal for master-servant, host-guest and official correspondence where hierarchy must be maintained without open rudeness. The so measures goodness by compliance: you will be good to the extent of doing what I ask. Variants include be so kind, be so obliging, be good enough, and the clipped modern be so good as to — all sharing the logic that obedience equals kindness.

  • Polite imperative to servants and subordinates: Be so good, Jeeves, as to fetch my coat is the classic Wodehousian use — an order from employer to valet that preserves gentlemanly manners while brooking no refusal. The vocative comma (Be so good, Jeeves) marks personal address within the command.
  • Formal written request: in letters and emails, be so good as to confirm receipt or be so good as to forward the papers functions as stiffly courteous please, common in Edwardian business English and still found in legal and bureaucratic prose.
  • Condescending or ironic command: because the form is now dated, modern use often sounds arch, patronising or comic. A teacher's be so good as to sit down to a chatty class, or Bertie's bally orders to Jeeves, carry mock-grandeur: grandeur that knows it is grandeur.
  • Elliptical everyday use: the as to + infinitive can be ellipted when gesture supplies it — Be so good, Jeeves (indicating the jacket) — or reduced to be so good as to with the verb understood from context, showing how fossilised the chunk has become.
  • Flattery-as-leverage sense: literally be good to such a degree, the phrase trades on the polysemy of good as both morally virtuous and socially obliging; to refuse would be, by the phrase's logic, to confess oneself not good.

Register is everything with this phrase. In 1900 it was normal upper-middle-class politeness; today it reads as deliberately old-fashioned, posh, or Wodehousian. Americans rarely use it except in parody of Britishness. Contemporary equivalents — please, kindly, would you mind, could you possibly — lack its particular blend of command and compliment. Wodehouse preserves it as a class marker: Bertie, who cannot command Jeeves by competence, commands him by idiom, and Jeeves, who obeys in fact, is flattered in form.
