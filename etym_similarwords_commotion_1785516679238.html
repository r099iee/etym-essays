• Tumult: Similar because it denotes noisy confusion among a crowd; differs because tumult foregrounds the swelling emotional agitation of the mob (Latin tumultus, from tumēre 'to swell'), while commotion stresses mixed movement and sound rather than mass passion.
  • Uproar: Similar because it describes loud, chaotic noise; differs because uproar (Dutch oproer 'uprising') is primarily acoustic — sheer overwhelming volume — whereas commotion adds kinetic agitation and often alarm.
  • Turmoil: Similar because it names agitated confusion; differs because turmoil suggests a churning, deep, sustained state of disorder, while a commotion is a sudden, localized, noisy outbreak.
  • Pandemonium: Similar because it describes wild, lawless disorder; differs because pandemonium (Milton's coinage, 'all-demons') is commotion raised to infernal frenzy — the extreme upper end of the scale, with hellish overtones.
  • Hubbub: Similar because it denotes a confused noise of many voices; differs because hubbub is domestic and near-cozy — noisy bustle without movement, threat, or gravity — while commotion implies real agitation.
  • Disturbance: Similar because both name an interruption of calm; differs because disturbance is a neutral, administrative term (police, legal use), while commotion is more dramatic, agitated, and noise-filled.
  • Clamour: Similar because both denote a loud collective outcry; differs because clamour is specifically the sound of voices raised together (often pleading or demanding) and carries no implication of movement.
  • Stir: Similar because both denote excited activity; differs because stir is gentler and more positive — excited attention or mild fuss — whereas commotion implies violent, disorderly disturbance.
  • Ruckus: Similar in informal American use for a noisy row; differs because ruckus is colloquial and light, while commotion can be grave, political, or fearful.
  • Disorder: Similar in legal use for a breach of public order; differs because disorder is an abstract condition, whereas commotion is its noisier, more physical and momentary manifestation.
  • Fracas: Similar because both name a noisy disturbance; differs because fracas (Italian fracasso 'uproar, crash') implies an actual brawl or fight among a few people, while commotion need not involve violence and usually involves a larger, looser crowd.
  • Noise: Similar because both involve sound; differs because noise is any sound at all, neutral and without movement or disorder, whereas a commotion is a specific agitated, moving, disturbing event.
