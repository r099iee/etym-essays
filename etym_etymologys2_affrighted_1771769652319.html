Linear derivation chain for the single root word AFFRIGHTED: Stage 1 — Proto-Germanic: *furht- 'fear, dread' (with the adjective *furhtaz 'afraid, fearful' and the nouns *furhti- / *furhtō 'fear'). Reconstructed on the evidence of all the Germanic branches. NOTE: a deeper PIE ancestor *preḱ- 'to fear' has been proposed (cf. Avestan pərəsaiti 'to be afraid, to dread'), but the link is disputed and no PIE stage is safely established; the attested history of the word is entirely Germanic, and this chain therefore begins at Proto-Germanic with the debate stated. Stage 2 — Old English (attested from the 8th c.): fyrhtu / fyrhto (feminine) 'fear, trembling, dread' — the West Germanic noun; and fyrhtan (verb) 'to frighten, terrify,' from the Proto-Germanic causative *furhtijaną 'to make afraid.' The noun descends to Middle English frighte and modern English fright, 'a sudden scare'; the base verb descends to modern English frighten. Stage 3 — Old English: āfyrhtan (verb) 'to terrify, strike with dread' — the base verb fyrhtan strengthened with the intensive/perfective prefix ā-, from Proto-Germanic *uz-, 'out, utterly, completely.' This intensified form is the direct ancestor of affright. Stage 4 — Middle English (attested 14th c.): afrighten / affrighten (verb) 'to terrify, to frighten utterly.' Stage 5 — Early Modern English (attested 16th c.): affright (verb) 'to frighten violently, to strike with sudden and great fear'; also the noun affright 'terror, sudden fear' (16th–19th c.). Stage 6 — Early Modern English (attested 16th c.): affrighted — past participle and participial adjective of affright, 'terrified, filled with dread'; in this form it becomes the standard literary word for complete terror, as in Gibbon's 'their affrighted imagination.' Note on debated stages: the only contested point is Stage 1's deeper ancestry — whether Germanic *furht- reaches back to PIE *preḱ- 'to fear' (Avestan pərəsaiti, and possibly the root of Latin poscere, 'to demand,' and English 'to pray,' in a semantic shift from 'ask' to 'fear/respect'). Modern handbooks treat the connection as plausible but unproven; the OED notes the Germanic base as the secure starting point. Every stage from Proto-Germanic onward is securely attested.
