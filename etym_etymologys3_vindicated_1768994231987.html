Vindicated is a word built from two separate acts, and its biography is the story of their slow fusion. In Latin the phrase vim dicāre meant "to show, proclaim one's force" — the formal, almost legal gesture by which a person asserted an authority or a claim — and from the welding of vim, the accusative of vis, "force" (from PIE *weyh₁-, "to be strong, to pursue with vigor"), with dicāre, "to proclaim, declare" (from PIE *deyḱ-, "to point out, show"), Latin produced vindex, "an avenger, claimant, protector," and the verb vindicāre, "to lay claim to, assert, avenge." The underlying picture is physical and almost theatrical: to vindicate was originally to lay a hand on something — a slave, a piece of property, an accused person — and declare, by that enforced gesture, whose right prevailed. The two roots behind it had already scattered their families across Europe: *weyh₁- gave Latin vincere, "to conquer" (hence victory, convince, invincible, vanquish), and the Germanic win-family — English to win, German gewinnen — words of striving and overcoming; *deyḱ- gave Sanskrit diśati, "he shows," Hindi diśā, "direction," Greek deíknumi, "to point out" (behind English deictic), Latin digitus, "the pointing finger" (hence digit), and the Germanic token-and-teach family, the words by which people show and are shown. Vindicāre concentrated the two ideas — force and proclamation — into a single legal verb.

The legal career of the word was long and rough. In classical Latin vindicāre meant chiefly "to claim as one's own, to assert a right," and also "to liberate" — one could vindicāre a slave to freedom — and "to avenge," since the avenger is the one who asserts the wronged party's claim by force. Vindicta, the revenge that vindication sanctioned, gave English vindictive, "revengeful," a word whose ugly modern sense still preserves the older violence of the family, and the Roman procedure of the vindicta (the rod laid on a slave's head in the ceremony of manumission) shows how concretely legal the gesture was. When Latin flowed into French, the verb was remade as venger, "to avenge," which English borrowed as avenge and revenge — words that kept the violent half of the family while English also borrowed vindicate straight from Latin.

English took vindicate directly from the Latin participle in the 16th century, and its first recorded senses were the proprietary ones: to lay claim to, to assert, and to avenge or punish. In the 17th century the word narrowed onto its modern path: to clear of blame or suspicion, to defend against accusation — the vindicated person walks out of court, the vindicated reputation is restored in the eyes of others. The 17th and 18th centuries extended the word to ideas: a man vindicated a thesis, a cause, a book, a religion, and the age of pamphlets and apologetics is full of titles promising to vindicate everything from poetry to the Christian faith — the word entered the vocabulary of polemic and moral debate, always carrying the note of a rightness asserted against attack. By the 19th century vindicated attached itself to public character and honour, and in the 20th and 21st centuries it relaxed from the courtroom to the everyday: a prediction, a decision, a coach's gamble, or an instinct is "vindicated" by later events, meaning proven right after doubt. The modern sense keeps the old grammar of accusation answered — you can only be vindicated if you were first doubted — and that is exactly the grammar the passage exploits when a man standing over bleeding strangers feels "mankind itself vindicated": the species stood accused, and its outlandish, far-flung, suffering variety has answered the charge.

  • 1st c. BCE: Latin vindicāre "to claim, assert, avenge, liberate"; vindex "avenger, claimant."
  • 16th c.: English vindicate borrowed from Latin vindicātus — senses of claiming, asserting, avenging.
  • 17th c.: narrowing to "clear of blame, defend against accusation"; vindicta → vindictive preserves the older violence.
  • 17th–18th c.: extension to ideas — the vindication of theses, causes, and reputations in polemic.
  • 19th c.: attachment to honour and public character.
  • 20th–21st c.: everyday proof-by-events — a prediction or decision vindicated after doubt; and here, the vindication of the whole human species.
