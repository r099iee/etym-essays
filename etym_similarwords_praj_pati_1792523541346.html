• Brahmā: Similar because both represent the supreme personified creator god in the Hindu pantheon responsible for generating the universe and living beings; differs because Prajāpati was the earlier, late-Vedic sacrificial demiurge of the Brāhmaṇas who created through self-dismemberment and tapas, whereas Brahmā became the formalized creator deity within the classical Purāṇic Trimūrti alongside Viṣṇu and Śiva.
  • Viśvakarman: Similar because both are venerable Vedic creator divinities; differs because Viśvakarman (literally "All-Maker" or "Universal Architect") emphasizes the celestial artisan, divine engineer, and craftsman who builds palaces, weapons, and cosmic geometry, whereas Prajāpati emphasizes paternal biological procreation and the fathering of living lineages (*prajā*).
  • Hiraṇyagarbha: Similar because both denote the primordial source and womb of cosmic existence; differs because Hiraṇyagarbha (the Golden Womb / Golden Embryo) is the primeval cosmological egg or radiant golden seed floating upon the cosmic waters from which the creator emerged, functioning as the cosmic vessel rather than the personal progenitor.
  • Kaśyapa: Similar because Kaśyapa is celebrated as the foremost *Prajāpati* who married the daughters of Dakṣa and fathered all cosmic species; differs because Kaśyapa is an individual mythological sage and patriarchal personage, whereas Prajāpati is the overarching cosmic title, divine office, and primordial Vedic creator deity.
  • Dakṣa: Similar because Dakṣa is one of the premier Prajāpatis appointed by Brahmā to increase the population of the universe through progeny; differs because Dakṣa is a specific Purāṇic personage renowned for his sacrificial conflict with Lord Śiva, whereas Prajāpati is the universal creator archetype.
  • Puruṣa: Similar because both are cosmic sacrificial entities whose bodies are offered in primordial sacrifice (*yajña*) to manifest the manifold cosmos; differs because Puruṣa in the *Puruṣasūkta* is the infinite, unmanifest cosmic person encompassing three-quarters of transcendent reality, whereas Prajāpati in the Brāhmaṇas experiences personal desire (*kāma*) and exhaustion in the act of generation.
  • Demiurge: Similar because in Platonic and Gnostic philosophy the Demiurge (*dēmiourgos*) is the fashioner or architect of the material cosmos; differs because the Demiurge is an artisan fashioning pre-existing matter (and in Gnostic theology often an ignorant or lesser deity), whereas Prajāpati emanates the cosmos directly from his own divine essence through tapas.
  • Dhātṛ: Similar because Dhātṛ is an ancient Vedic and classical epithet meaning "the Establisher, Creator, or Ordainer"; differs because Dhātṛ focuses on the structural establishment, placement, and preservation of cosmic laws and individual destinies, whereas Prajāpati focuses specifically on biological generation and patriarchal fatherhood.
  • Vidhi: Similar because Vidhi is a classical epithet for the Creator god overseeing destiny and order; differs because Vidhi literally means "rule, ordinance, or fate," personified as the cosmic dispenser of predetermined destiny, whereas Prajāpati is an active, generative ancestral father.
  • Janaka: Similar because both are associated with generation and paternal procreation (from roots *jan* and *prajā*); differs because Janaka was a historical and epic philosopher-king of Videha and adoptive father of Sītā, whereas Prajāpati is a transcendent divinity and ancestral fountainhead of all cosmos.
  • Manu: Similar because both are primal ancestral fathers of humanity; differs because Manu is specifically the human progenitor, lawgiver, and flood-hero who survived the deluge to found mortal society, whereas Prajāpati is the divine cosmic creator from whom Manu himself descended.
  • Tvaṣṭṛ: Similar because both are Vedic deities associated with fertility and creation; differs because Tvaṣṭṛ is specifically the divine shaper of physical forms in the womb and the forger of Indra's thunderbolt (*vajra*), whereas Prajāpati is the supreme source of life and consciousness.
