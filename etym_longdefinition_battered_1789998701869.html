Battered is the past-participial adjective from the verb batter (“to strike repeatedly with hard blows”): damaged, dented, worn, or bruised by repeated hitting, harsh treatment, heavy use, or prolonged exposure. Its prototype is physical: a battered door is dented, a battered face is bruised and swollen, a battered car has dents and peeling paint. From this concrete image radiates a family of extended senses, all preserving the idea of cumulative impact over time rather than a single break.

In everyday material use it means worn by age and service: battered luggage, battered books, battered furniture, battered hats. Here the blows may be metaphorical — scuffs, rain, sun, constant handling — and the connotation can be affectionate (a battered teddy bear, a battered guitar with mojo) or pitying (a battered economy, a battered reputation). Nautical and military contexts keep the older literal force: battered fortifications, battered troops, a storm-battered coast. Weather-battered is almost a synonym: Lashed by wind, rain, and waves until scarred but still standing.

A distinct and socially weighty sense concerns persons subjected to sustained violence or hardship: battered child, battered wife/woman, battered husband, battered asylum-seeker. Since the 1960s-1970s these have been clinical and legal terms (battered-child syndrome, battered-woman syndrome) denoting a pattern of abuse, learned helplessness, and trauma. The bridge is direct: bodies and psyches, like walls, break down under repeated blows. Related is the psychological extension to non-physical entities: battered confidence, battered pride, battered markets — worn down by repeated shocks.

  • Core physical sense — struck and dented: e.g. battered helmet, battered door. Semantic anchor: intensive frequentative of beat = many blows.
  • Wear-and-tear sense — shabby from use/weather: e.g. battered suitcase, storm-battered harbour. Bridge: time and elements reimagined as a battering agent; often affectionate patina.
  • Human-victim sense — abused, traumatised: e.g. battered child, battered spouse. Bridge: medical-legal adoption in the 1960s-70s; denotes pattern, not single incident.
  • Abstract-institutional sense — weakened by repeated shocks: e.g. battered economy, battered party, battered reputation. Bridge: metaphor from fortress to institution under siege.
  • Culinary homonym — coated in batter: e.g. battered fish, battered Mars bar. Bridge: FALSE FRIEND — from batter (noun, beaten mixture) + -ed, not from damage; means dipped and fried. Learners must separate this from all above.

Grammar: attributive and predicative adjective (a battered van / the van is battered), gradable (very battered, badly battered), often modified by adverbs of degree or agent (storm-battered, war-battered, time-battered). Do not confuse with beaten (single contest lost) or bruised (only flesh): battered implies prolonged, repeated, often structural damage with visible history. In your space-harness context it signals veteran campaigning — kit that has seen action — dignified wear, not mere shabbiness.
