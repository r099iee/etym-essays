Derivation chain for MEDALLIONS (from Latin medius, "middle"):
  • PIE *medhyo- "middle, in the middle" (c. 4000-3000 BCE) - the root meaning "mid, middle", source of Sanskrit madhya, Greek mesos, and Latin medius.
  • Latin medius "middle, mid" (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) - "being in the middle, central"; with the adjectival suffix -alis it forms medialis "of the middle".
  • Late Latin medalia "a half-denarius coin, a coin of middle value" (Late Antiquity, c. 4th-6th c. CE) - a small coin, literally the "middle" or halved denomination; the exact intermediate form is debated, and some scholars instead derive it from metallum "metal" by folk etymology, but the mainstream account followed by the OED starts from medius.
  • Italian medaglia "coin; medal" (Medieval Italian, c. 12th-13th c.) - from medalia, shifting from "half-coin" to "coin" generally and then to "a struck commemorative piece".
  • Italian medaglione "large medal, medallion" (Italian, c. 16th-17th c.) - formed with the augmentative suffix -one, giving "big medal".
  • French medaillon "large medal, medallion" (French, c. 17th c.) - borrowed from Italian medaglione.
  • English medallion (first recorded 1658, OED) - borrowed from French medaillon; the plural medallions appears by the late 17th century.
  • MEDALLIONS (present card) - the plural form, used in the decorative sense of "round or oval framed motifs".
Debated / nuanced points: the exact intermediate between medius and medalia is uncertain, and medalia may have been influenced by the separate word metallum "metal" in popular etymology; some authorities reconstruct an unattested *medaliu-. The decorative sense "ornamental round or oval motif" is first attested in English in the later 17th century, and the culinary sense "round slice of meat" in the 19th century. A separate and misleading root must not be confused here: Greek metallon "mine, quarry, metal" (probably a pre-Greek substrate word) is the ancestor of English metal, not of medal or medallion; the two words are historically unconnected despite their resemblance.
