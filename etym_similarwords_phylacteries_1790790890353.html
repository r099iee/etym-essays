• Tefillin: Similar because both refer to the exact same traditional Jewish ritual prayer items; differs because tefillin is the authentic Hebrew term used within Judaism, carrying profound devotional dignity and sacred obedience, whereas phylacteries is a Greek-derived translation that originally emphasized outward protection (amulet) and sometimes carries polemical overtones from New Testament passages.
  • Amulets: Similar because both are portable objects worn on the body to provide spiritual, magical, or physical protection against evil or harm; differs because an amulet may be any stone, carved figurine, or gem without containing scriptural texts or requiring a complex daily binding ritual.
  • Talismans: Similar because both are sacred or magical objects believed to exert protective power; differs because a talisman is typically consecrated or astrologically engraved to attract specific supernatural virtues or good fortune, whereas phylacteries are containers of divine biblical law.
  • Mezuzot: Similar because both are traditional Jewish ritual objects containing parchment scrolls inscribed with the *Shema* and biblical verses; differs because a mezuzah is permanently affixed to the doorposts of a home or room, whereas phylacteries (tefillin) are worn directly on the human body during morning prayer.
  • Periapt: Similar because both are charms or items worn suspended around the neck or body for magical protection; differs because periapt is an archaic, general literary word for any hanging charm, devoid of religious liturgical obligations.
  • Reliquaries: Similar because both are sacred protective containers holding venerated holy material; differs because a reliquary houses the physical bodily remains or clothing relics of a saint and is kept in a church or altar, rather than being bound to the body during individual prayer.
  • Scapulars: Similar because both are devotional garments or objects worn on the body as signs of religious dedication and spiritual protection; differs because scapulars belong to Roman Catholic monastic and Marian devotional tradition (two cloth panels connected by cords), whereas phylacteries are cubic leather boxes containing Hebrew Torah scrolls.
  • Banderoles (Phylacteries in Medieval Art): Similar because both use the term phylactery for inscribed scrolls; differs because in art history, a banderole or art-historical phylactery is a painted speech scroll or banner issuing from a figure's mouth in a painting to depict dialogue or prophecy, rather than a wearable prayer capsule.
