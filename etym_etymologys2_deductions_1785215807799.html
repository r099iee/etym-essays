PIE *deuk- "to lead, to draw, to pull along" (reconstructed root, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; securely attested in Latin dūcere, Old English tēon "to pull", Old Irish do·uc "he brings", and Sanskrit duh- "to draw") → Latin dūcere "to lead, draw, pull, conduct" (attested 3rd c. BCE onward) → Latin dēdūcere "to lead down, to draw off, to derive" (classical Latin; prefix dē- "down, away" + dūcere, attested 2nd–1st c. BCE) → Latin dēductiō, genitive dēductiōnis "a leading down, a drawing off, a subtraction, a deduction" (classical and post-classical Latin; also the legal term for leading colonists to a settlement) → Middle French déduction "a deduction, a subtraction, a setting-off" (14th c.) → Middle English deduction "a subtraction from a sum; a logical inference drawn from premises" (15th c., attested by c. 1485) → Modern English deduction, plural DEDUCTIONS "conclusions reached by reasoning from general principles; amounts subtracted from a total" (16th c. onward) Note: the verb deduct is a later back-formation from deduction (17th c.), and the adjective deductive comes via Latin deductivus and French déductif; the logical sense of the word was fixed by the medieval scholastic tradition (Boethius, the thirteenth-century logicians) before the word reached English.
