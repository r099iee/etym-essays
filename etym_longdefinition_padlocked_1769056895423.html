padlocked (adjective / past participle) — secured by a padlock, a portable lock that can be attached to and removed from a chain, hasp, or staple, typically with a hinged iron shackle that snaps shut. The word names both a physical fastening and a finished state of security. In the treasure-chamber passage the chests of Cair Paravel are “strengthened with iron bars and heavily padlocked”: the bars say the chests cannot be forced; the padlocks say they cannot even be tampered with discreetly; together the two defences create a sense of desperate, layered security. The nuance of padlocked here is portable, mercenary fortification: unlike a built-in mortise lock, a padlock can be carried, clapped onto anything, and removed when the treasure is finally reclaimed. Its heavy, mechanical finality — the audible snap of the shackle — is exactly the “doubly secured” feeling the sentence builds, and the homely iron practicality of the word suits a dwarfish treasury far better than a courtly French borrowing would.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Literal sense (state): fastened with a padlock — “the gate was padlocked shut,” “she padlocked her bicycle,” “a padlocked chest”; the participle describes a completed state of closure.
  • Verb sense: “to padlock” — to fasten with a padlock; the act of clapping the lock on; also figurative, “to padlock away,” to seal something out of reach.
  • Figurative sense (shut, sealed, inaccessible): a “padlocked” door, shop, or building is closed and abandoned; the word comes to mean shut down, derelict, or unavailable — a business that closes can be said to be “padlocked,” and “padlocked lips” mean sealed, refusing to speak.
  • Digital and security jargon: the padlock icon in a web browser signals an encrypted (HTTPS) connection; “the little padlock” has become the universal symbol of online security, and “padlock” is used loosely for encryption, data protection, and secure sites.
  • Cultural sense (love locks): “love locks” — padlocks clipped to bridges by couples as a symbol of commitment (most famously the Pont des Arts in Paris); the utilitarian lock is repurposed as an ornament of romance.
  • Compound and noun senses: “a padlock” — the object itself; “padlock law” — a law ordering premises closed and padlocked (e.g., against unlawful activity); the plural “padlocks” as a commodity.
  • Metaphoric sense (memory and secrets): memories or secrets “padlocked away” in the mind — sealed, unopened, deliberately forgotten; the word supplies a concrete image of psychological closure.
The word’s internal history is a compound: “pad-” of obscure origin (perhaps a basket or hamper) joined to the native Germanic “lock” (see etymologyS2). The essence of a padlock is portability — a lock that travels to its work — and every later sense, from the HTTPS padlock to the love lock, keeps that idea: a little clamp of security that can be snapped on anywhere and snapped off again. The shift from “locked” to “padlocked” is a shift from generic to specific, and the shift from “padlocked” to “sealed/shut” is a generalization that modern usage has completed.

Padlocked differs from its near-synonyms in the way security is achieved. Locked is the general word covering every fastening; “padlocked” specifies the external, removable iron lock and is therefore more concrete and more defensive in tone. Bolted describes a sliding metal bar shot home across a door — a fixed, immovable barrier, not a portable clamp — and also means “fled,” a homonymic distraction. Latched is a light fastening, a small pivoting catch that keeps a door closed but offers no real security. Sealed suggests an official or magical closing (wax, a seal, an enchantment) with no mechanical mechanism. Shackled and chained humanize the object, describing something bound like a prisoner. Clamped emphasizes squeezing pressure rather than a lock mechanism. Secured and fastened are general, security-neutral terms. Only padlocked specifies the external, removable iron lock — concrete, defensive, and portable.
