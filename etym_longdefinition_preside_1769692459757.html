preside: to be in the position of authority at a meeting, ceremony, or gathering; to act as chairperson or director; to exercise control or supervision over a proceeding. Core meaning. The verb preside describes holding the seat of authority at a formal or semi-formal occasion — chairing a meeting, officiating at a ceremony, or sitting at the head of a table or court. The word's literal Latin meaning is "to sit in front of": the person who presides sits at the front or head of the assembly, facing the others, and from that seat directs the proceedings. It is most often used with "over" (preside over X) or, more traditionally, "at" (preside at the meeting). In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Miss Bingley is "willing to preside at his table": she is willing to act as hostess, sitting at the head of Bingley's table and directing the household as the mistress would. Primary sense — to chair or direct a proceeding. The most common modern use: "The judge presided over the trial," "She presided at the board meeting," "He was asked to preside over the debate." Here preside names the formal authority of the chair — deciding procedure, recognizing speakers, keeping order. It implies the office of chairperson, judge, or moderator. Extended sense — to supervise or govern a situation. A looser, very frequent modern use: to preside over an event, era, process, or development, meaning to be in charge while it happens — "He presided over the company's expansion," "The minister presided over the reforms." In this sense the "sitting" image fades; what remains is responsibility and authority for what happens "under one's chair." Often the word is neutral or even negative when the presided-over events are unwelcome ("She presided over the scandal"), suggesting that the person was in charge while it unfolded. Sense history and related forms.
  • Literal Latin sense: "to sit in front of, to sit before (a group), to guard, protect" — the Roman praesidere combined prae- "before" + sedere "to sit." A Roman officer "sat before" the troops or gates, guarding them.
  • Governance sense: to exercise the authority of a governor or protector — this underlies the English noun president, from Latin praesidens "the one sitting before" (the present participle), which came to mean "governor, chief magistrate."
  • Hostess sense (Austen): to act as the lady of the house at table — a specifically social use of the "head of the table" image.
  • Modern technical use: "to preside over" a meeting, court, or hearing is standard legal and parliamentary vocabulary.
Register and collocations.
  • "Preside over" (dominant) / "preside at" (traditional, especially of ceremonies and tables).
  • Collocations: preside over a meeting, a trial, a session, a ceremony, an inquiry, a debate; preside at the table.
  • Register: formal, official, procedural; neutral in tone, carrying the weight of legitimate authority.
  • Word family: president, presidential, presidency; the presiding judge/officer (the one currently in the chair).
Polysemy summary (all senses, living and historical).
  • To act as chairperson or director of a meeting or ceremony — modern, dominant.
  • To be in charge of a situation, era, or process — modern, extended.
  • To act as host or hostess at a table or house — historical-social (Austen's use).
  • To sit before, guard, or protect (Latin literal) — historical, the etymological bridge.
  • To hold the office of governor or chief magistrate — via president, still implicit in the family.
The semantic bridge. The whole word rests on the image of the chair. A person seated at the front or head of a group physically embodies authority — the chief sits while others stand or sit below. From "sit in front of" (Latin praesidere, guarding), the word moved to "sit at the head of" (chairing a council), then to "be in charge of" (governing a process). The president of a country is, etymologically, the person who sits before the assembly. Every modern use of preside still carries a faint echo of that Roman chair.
