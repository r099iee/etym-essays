Derivation chain for ENDEAVOUR (morphologically en- + Old French devoir "duty"): 1. Proto-Indo-European *gʰeh₁bʰ- "to grab, take, receive" (Pokorny's *ghebh-) — a root of seizing and grasping, the ultimate ancestor of the Latin verb "to have." Proposed but uncertain cognates are cited in Celtic (Old Irish gaibid "takes") and Indo-Iranian (Sanskrit gabhasti- "hand, arm, ray"); the secure attested chain runs only through Italic. 2. Latin habēre "to have, to hold" (attested from the earliest Latin, 3rd c. BCE onward) — from *gʰeh₁bʰ- with regular development; "to have" conceived as "to grasp, to hold in hand." 3. Latin dēbēre "to owe" — a compound of the preposition dē "away from, down from" + habēre, literally "to have away from oneself": to hold something only as something owing, hence "to be under obligation," "to owe." This is the semantic pivot of the whole chain: having becomes owing. 4. Old French devoir "to owe; duty, that which is owed" (attested from the 9th c. in the Strasbourg Oaths and continuously thereafter) — the regular development of Latin dēbēre in Gallo-Romance (with the inter-vocalic -b- voiced and the loss of the medial syllable). As a noun, devoir means "duty"; as a verb, "to owe, must." 5. Old French phrase (se mettre) en devoir / en devoir "to put oneself to one's duty" — the prepositional phrase in which duty becomes a task one sets oneself to; this phrase, borrowed into English, supplies the modern word's shape, with the Old French en- fused into the English word. 6. Middle English endeven / endeveren "to make it one's duty, to strive" (attested late 14th c.; the spelling endever appears in the 15th c.) — borrowed via Anglo-Norman from the Old French phrase; English "to put oneself in devoir" = to set oneself earnestly to a task. 7. English ENDEAVOUR (noun and verb, 15th c. onward) "a serious, earnest attempt / to strive" — the noun is a back-formation or direct nominal use of the verb; the spelling -our is inherited from the Old French model (devoir, like savour, favour), while American English writes endeavor after Webster's reforms. Note on debated points: - The proposed cognates of *gʰeh₁bʰ- outside Italic are contested: Old Irish gaibid "takes" and Sanskrit gabhasti- ("hand, arm," or by some readings "ray, grasp") are cited in the handbooks but the exact root relation is debated; the derivation of English endeavour is secure only through Latin habēre. - The traditional English folk-analysis that endeavour comes from "in duty" is essentially correct — it descends from the Old French phrase en devoir — but the modern word must not be treated as if it were coined by combining English en- with English devoir; the whole phrase was borrowed and then truncated and reshaped by English usage.
