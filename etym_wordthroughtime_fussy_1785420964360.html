- Early 18th century (c. 1701) - The noun 'fuss' surfaces in colloquial English as 'needless commotion or bustle over trifles,' already mildly pejorative and faintly absurd
- c. 1770s - 'Fussy' is coined as 'given to making a fuss'; of persons it means finicky, hard to please, habitually anxious over small matters, with a warm, exasperated, almost parental tone
- 19th century - Broadens to things and processes: 'over-elaborate, cluttered with unnecessary detail' (a fussy design, fussy ornament, fussy details)
- 19th-20th century - Enters aesthetic and literary criticism: fussy editing or scholarly apparatus 'burdened with minor qualifications,' gently self-deprecating rather than truly condemnatory
- Modern - Settles into an informal-to-neutral register, equally at home of a crying baby, an exacting eater, an over-decorated room, or an over-annotated page; far less formal than 'fastidious' or 'meticulous'
