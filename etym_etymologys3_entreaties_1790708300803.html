Ancient Origins: From Physical Pulling to Mental Handling
The deep ancestry of entreaties traces to the Proto-Indo-European root *trāgh- (or *dʰreǵʰ-), meaning 'to drag, draw, or pull along the ground', which also generated English words like track, drag, trail, and tractor. In Proto-Italic and Classical Latin, this root gave rise to the primary verb trahere ('to pull, drag'). To describe the iterative, frequent, or deliberate handling of an object or topic, Latin developed the frequentative verb tractāre ('to draw violently, handle, manage, conduct, or discuss in speech/writing'). This concept of 'handling' laid the semantic foundation for the English words treat, treatise, treaty, and tract.

The Romance Transformation: From Managing to Negotiating

  • Development in Old French: As Latin transitioned into Old French, tractāre evolved into traitier ('to treat, handle, negotiate'). To intensify the relational dimension of interpersonal engagement, speakers prefixed the prepositional morpheme en- (from Latin in- 'in, into, upon'), forming the compound verb entraiter ('to deal with, treat, negotiate terms with, or address').
  • Middle English Adoption: Following the Norman Conquest, Anglo-French entraiter entered Middle English around the late 14th century as entraten. In its earliest English attestations, it mirrored the broader senses of its French parent, meaning 'to treat or entertain a guest' or 'to discourse upon a literary theme'. However, in legal, courtly, and diplomatic settings, when one 'entreated with' a lord, sovereign, or magistrate, the discourse inevitably took the form of petitioning, bargaining, or soliciting a favor.

Semantic Specialization into Earnest Pleading
During the 15th and early 16th centuries, the verb underwent a decisive semantic shift: the neutral sense of 'handling or discussing' dropped away, and entreat specialized exclusively into 'to plead, beseech, solicit earnestly, or implore'. To match this refined verbal sense, Early Modern English coined the derivative abstract count noun entreaty (plural entreaties) around the mid-16th century, suffixing -y (from Latin -ia / French -ie). The noun became an indispensable element of Elizabethan and Jacobean tragic and dramatic literature, capturing the poignant rhetoric of characters pleading for mercy, justice, or familial restraint.

Literary Context and Modern Preservation
In Daniel Defoe’s 1719 masterpiece Robinson Crusoe, the pairing of 'entreaties and persuasions of my mother and other friends' exemplifies the classic Early Modern pairing of emotional begging (entreaties) with rational argument (persuasions). Throughout the 18th and 19th centuries, entreaties maintained its position as a high-register literary word, favored by novelists such as Jane Austen, Walter Scott, and Charles Dickens. In modern English, while everyday speech favors simpler words like 'pleas' or 'begging', entreaties remains an evocative, dignified term that conveys profound vulnerability, solemn gravity, and deep emotional urgency.
