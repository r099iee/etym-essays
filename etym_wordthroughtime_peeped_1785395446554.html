- [Late 15th c.] - Recorded as an imitative word of quick, furtive looking — to glance through a small opening or from cover; the sense of a brief, partial, cautious look is present from the start.
- [16th-17th c.] - Settles as the verb for secret or playful observation through a keyhole, curtain, or chink; drawn to prepositions of passage (peep through, peep out, peep over), the word leans on the aperture and the concealment.
- [18th-19th c. idioms] - Grows a noun and fixed phrases: peeping Tom (attested 1796, from the Lady Godiva legend), the Victorian peep-show, and the infant game of peekaboo — the look becomes publicly synonymous with spying, curiosity, and small thrills.
- [19th-20th c. transferred senses] - A second imitative strand merges into the same word shape: a chick's peep of sound, and 'not a peep' meaning utter silence, where the noun names a single sound or syllable.
- [Modern register] - Informal and confidential, the word of narrative voice, children's books, and affectionate spy-play; the briefest and most comic act of vision, still tinged with bird-like secrecy.
