In this context. Calling the author “a true philologist” is a compliment of the highest order. A philologist, in the classic nineteenth- and twentieth-century sense, is a scholar who studies language through its written and literary monuments — one who reads texts as evidence of linguistic history while still delighting in them as literature. The sentence makes the word’s value explicit: this author “effortlessly straddles literary values and linguistic technicalities”, convincingly showing that each illuminates the other, drawing analogies with music at one moment and pointing out “basic relationships with ordinary speech” at another. The praise rests on an older, richer meaning than mere “linguist”: the true philologist is a lover of words (Greek philos “loving” + logos “word, speech”), who treats language and literature as one indivisible pursuit. The tone is warm, scholarly, almost idealising — it resurrects a noble and now somewhat unfashionable title.

Core meaning. A philologist is a scholar of philology: the study of language through its historical and literary sources, uniting linguistic analysis with literary scholarship. The classic definitions centre on the love and study of learning and literature, and specifically on the historical study of languages as revealed in their written texts. The word names a person, a practitioner, where philology names the discipline itself.

Polysemy: the full range of senses.

  • Historical linguist (the nineteenth-century sense). In the 1800s “philology” was the umbrella science of language — comparative grammar, sound laws, the reconstruction of language families — and the philologist was the scholar who practised it: the comparativists who worked out Grimm’s law and the Indo-European family tree, tracing Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and the Germanic tongues back to a common ancestor. “Comparative philologist” is the fossil of this sense.
  • Literary-historical scholar (the older and continuing sense). A scholar who reads texts as documents: editing manuscripts, dating them by dialect and sound change, tracing the history of a word or construction through its attested forms, and interpreting literature in the light of its language. This is the sense the sentence praises — the union of literary values with linguistic technicality.
  • Textual critic and editor. In practice the philologist is often the editor of ancient and medieval texts: reconstructing manuscript lineages (stemmatics), establishing readings, and writing commentaries. The sentence’s phrase “a true philologist” in a book-series Foreword sits exactly in this tradition.
  • Lover of words / learned person (the etymological and seventeenth-century sense). From the Greek etymology, a lover of learning, words and argument; in the seventeenth century philologist could simply mean an erudite person, a man of letters, before the word narrowed into a professional title.
  • Modern loose sense. Today the word is often used admiringly (and slightly ironically) of anyone who combines literary taste with technical command of language — a “word person” — which is exactly its force here.

Semantic bridge. The development runs from “lover of words and learning” (classical Greek) through “scholar of classical texts” (Renaissance Europe) to “scientist of language history” (nineteenth century) and back to a warm, generalising compliment (twentieth–twenty-first centuries). Each stage preserves the founding idea of love (philos) for language (logos) — the discipline’s original gesture, which the context deliberately evokes.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. A linguist studies language generally, often descriptively and scientifically, and need not care about literary texts; a grammarian specialises in the rules of grammar and can be narrow or prescriptive; an etymologist focuses on word origins; a philologist uniquely bridges historical linguistics and literary scholarship, reading texts as documents of culture. The term has shifted: in nineteenth-century universities “philology” meant what we now split into historical linguistics and literary criticism, so “philologist” suggests the lost unity of those disciplines — precisely the breadth this author is praised for.
