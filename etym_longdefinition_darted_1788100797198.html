In Wilde's tale the Swallow "darted" through an opening in the statue and came into the room: he moved suddenly and very fast, in one quick straight line, like a dart leaving a hand. The word darted is the past tense and past participle of the verb to dart — to move suddenly and rapidly in a particular direction, or to cause something (a missile, a glance, a ray of light) to move in that swift way. The core image is always the dart itself: a small pointed projectile whose flight is sudden, straight and brief. Every sense of the word is a metaphor of that missile.

Primary verbal senses. (1) Intransitive — to move suddenly and swiftly: of people, animals, fish, insects and birds ("the sparrow darted across the road"), of glances ("his eyes darted about the room"), of light and shadow ("sunbeams darted through the clouds"), and of abstract things ("a thought darted through her mind"). The movement is one quick, direct burst — not sustained running, but a single decisive dash in a straight line. (2) Transitive — to throw or shoot a missile: "he darted a spear at the boar"; in older language, to discharge darts or javelins in warfare or the chase. (3) Transitive — to direct suddenly: "she darted a look of fury at him" — to send out a glance, question or remark quickly and pointedly; this is the most common modern transitive use. (4) Reflexive and older senses: "to dart oneself forward" — to fling oneself in a sudden movement.

Secondary and domain senses. The noun senses that feed the verb deserve their own record:

  • The missile: a dart is a small pointed missile with a light shaft, thrown by hand or blown through a blowpipe; historically a light javelin or arrow-like weapon of hunting and war (the blowgun's poisoned dart; the panther's dart). The pub game of darts (with its dartboard, wire treble rings and steel-tipped projectiles) developed in English inns around the turn of the twentieth century and grew into a professional televised sport.
  • The tailoring dart (19th century): in dressmaking, a dart is the pointed, V-shaped fold or tuck that shapes cloth to the body at the waist, bust or back; a "darted" bodice or waistband is one shaped by such tucks. The name plays on the tuck's arrow-like pointed form — one more migration of the missile image, here into the sewing room.
  • The zoologist's dart: many land snails (families such as the Helicidae) shoot calcareous "love darts" into one another during courtship — small sharp spikes of calcium carbonate fired from a sac near the head; the behaviour is a famous curiosity of malacology, and "darting" is the technical verb for it.
  • Darts of sensation: figurative literary use for sharp, sudden, stabbing pains or emotions — "darts of grief", "darts of sunlight" — the missile image again, pointed and sudden.

Grammar and prosody. Darted is a perfectly regular weak form: dart + the past suffix -ed, pronounced /ˈdɑːtɪd/ (the /t/ of the base and the /ɪd/ of the suffix giving the word its characteristic three-beat shape). It serves as simple past ("he darted through the door") and as past participle in perfect constructions ("the fish had darted away") and passives ("a glance was darted"). Its register is literary and narrative; prose writers from fairy tales to thrillers choose it when a movement must be quick, sudden and vividly seen — the exact effect Wilde needs for the Swallow's flight into the statue's interior.

  • Sense 1 (intransitive): to move suddenly and rapidly — the swallow darted through the opening.
  • Sense 2 (transitive): to throw or shoot a dart or missile; historically to dart a lance.
  • Sense 3 (transitive): to direct suddenly — to dart a glance, a look, a question.
  • Sense 4 (domains): the tailoring dart (a pointed tuck); the snail's love dart; darts of pain; the pub game of darts.
