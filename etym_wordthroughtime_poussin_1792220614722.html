- 12th–16th c. — Established in northern France as an everyday agricultural noun and affectionate nickname (poussin, "chick"), derived from Vulgar Latin *pullicīnus.
- 1624–1665 — Nicolas Poussin establishes his workshop in Rome; his canvases are celebrated among Roman humanists and the French court of Cardinal Richelieu as the height of erudite, Stoic narrative painting.
- Late 17th c. — The French Royal Academy codifies Poussin's doctrines as the national standard of art; the famous *Querelle des Poussinistes et Rubénistes* splits the art establishment between intellect (Poussin) and sensation (Rubens).
- 19th c. — Revered by Neoclassicists like David and Ingres; subsequently adopted as a major source of inspiration by Paul Cézanne, who sought to reconcile Poussin's architectural logic with modern visual perception.
- 20th–21st c. — Cemented in global aesthetic discourse as the quintessential monument of classical clarity, intellectual draftsmanship, and timeless architectural order in painting.
