NEW-HEWN is a compound adjective built from two very old native English elements: new, "recently made or come into being", and hewn, the past participle of hew, "to cut, chop, or shape with a cutting tool". Together the two mean "freshly cut" — most often of stone or timber that has been quarried, felled or dressed so recently that it still shows the marks of the work: the pale face of newly split rock, the white axe-strokes on green oak, the sharp edges of ashlar that no weather has yet softened.

Core sense — freshly quarried stone. In the source passage the Gate is blocked "with a wall of new-hewn stone": stone cut and fitted for the wall at the moment it was needed, in contrast to ancient or weathered masonry. The phrase carries the drama of hasty but skilled construction — work so recent that the wall is no older than the emergency it answers.

  • Of stone and masonry: new-hewn stone is freshly dressed block stone, often still sharp-edged, pale and unweathered. It contrasts with old, lichened or tumbled masonry, and with rubble fill thrown together without dressing.
  • Of timber and woodwork: new-hewn timber is freshly felled and shaped by axe or adze — logs, beams and planks still fragrant with resin, "green" in the builder's sense (unseasoned). The adjective sets the material's newness against the slow seasoning it will still need.
  • Figurative and literary: the compound is almost wholly literary and monumental in register. Its closest relatives — "rough-hewn", "hewn out of the rock" — echo scripture and epic; "new-hewn" adds freshness to the image of things cut from living rock or forest: new-hewn idols, new-hewn tombs, new-hewn ramparts.
  • Grammar and sentiment: "hewn" is the older, preferred participle in attributive position (a hewn beam) and in passive constructions, where it often alternates with the younger "hewed" (was hewed down). "New-hewn" occurs essentially only as an attributive, hyphenated adjective; the unhyphenated "new hewn" is rare. The compound is confined to formal or archaic diction: in practical modern building speech one says "freshly cut" or "newly dressed".

Secondary and adjacent senses of the elements. "New" contributes its full polysemy — freshly made, recently discovered, unfamiliar, renewed, additional (a "new wall" could also mean a second wall) — while "hewn" contributes the craft senses of "hew": to cut down (trees), to strike with a cutting blow (a sword hews), and to shape by cutting (to hew stone square). The compound therefore means, at its narrowest, "just cut to shape", and at its widest "made from virgin material by cutting, and made just now". It is a term of builders, poetic prose and epic description rather than of the street; its value today is precisely its archaic, monumental flavour, which makes stone and timber seem new and raw at once — the wall of a besieged gate, raised between nightfall and dawn.
