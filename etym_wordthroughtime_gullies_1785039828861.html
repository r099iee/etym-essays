- 17th century - 'gully' appears in English (probably via French 'goulet,' a narrow throat or passage) as a small, narrow channel cut in earth by running water; the image is of the land's throat, a narrow gullet swallowed into the slope.
- 18th-19th century - the word passes into colonial and frontier vocabulary; American pioneers and surveyors use it for the steep little water-courses of the new territories, and 'gulch' appears beside it as a New World variant.
- 19th-20th century - geology fixes 'gully' as the mid-scale landform, larger than a groove and smaller than a ravine, carved by runoff rather than by a permanent river; it becomes a standard technical and geographical term.
- Contemporary - 'gullies' is the plain, exact word for water-worn channels, used in science, travel, and literature alike; its signature danger — narrow width hiding appalling depth — makes it a favourite of wilderness and adventure narrative.
