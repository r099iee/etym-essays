The voyage of hulk begins, fittingly, with a voyage: Ancient Greek holkas named the fat-bellied merchantman that could not sail smartly but had to be towed, from helkein, to drag or pull, itself from PIE *selk-, so that the first hulk was defined by its need to be hauled, bulk as burden from the very start.

Late Latin borrowed the hull as hulcus or holcas for the same broad transport, and the early medieval North Sea world inherited it as Old English hulc, glossing a light swift ship in some texts and a roomy trader in others, the contradiction revealing how sailors named vessels by use rather than by plan: whatever carried much and moved slow could be a hulk, and cognates in Middle Low German hulke and Old Norse shipping lists show the word sloshing around the whole northern waterfront.

  • Medieval merchantman: by the 1300s the Middle English hulke is the big-bellied cog’s cousin, praised for stowage and mocked for handling, and port records treat hulks as the container ships of their day, profitable precisely because they are tubs.
  • Reduction to hull: through the 1500s and 1600s naval practice creates the modern sense almost by accident, since worn-out men-of-war and traders are stripped of rigging and kept as sheer hulks for masting, store hulks for provisions, and later prison hulks, so that hulk slides from ship to hull, from vessel to carcass, the name following the timbers as masts, sails, and purpose fall away.
  • Floating gaols: the word’s darkest century is 1776 to 1857, when Britain, cut off from American transportation, housed convicts in the rotting hulks of the Thames, Portsmouth, and Bermuda, and Dickens, opening Great Expectations among the marshes with Magwitch from the hulks, fixes the association of damp, chains, and looming timber for every later reader.
  • Bulk ashore: once a hulk means any stripped shell, the leap ashore is short: Victorian novels describe hulks of men at the docks, early motoring writers mock the hulks of abandoned cars, and foresters call veteran hollow trees hulks, since each is a hull run aground on land, too massive to shift and too useful to burn.
  • Tolkien’s gentling: the passage’s huge hulk of a tree keeps the bulk and the hollowness but washes away the menace, since the crack away from the road becomes a door and the leaves overhead a roof, restoring the oldest hospitality of hollow trees, from Robin Hood’s Major Oak to childhood dens, and proving the word can shelter as well as loom.

Sound and spelling tell the same story of bluntness: the initial guttural h- breathes, the vowel collapses to the short northern u, and the final -lk cluster thuds shut like a hatch, a phonetic bulk to match the semantic one, while the doublet hull, from the same family via different routing, keeps the technical shipbuilding sense and leaves hulk free to lumber through general prose, looming wherever size outgrows grace.
