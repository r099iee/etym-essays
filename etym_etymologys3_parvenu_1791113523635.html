Parvenu literally means arrived in French, yet in both French and English it sneers: one who has arrived socially but is felt not to belong. Its evolution from a neutral verb of motion to a weapon of snobbery traces Roman travel, feudal attainment and modern class anxiety.

The root is PIE *gʷem-, to go, come, step — source of English come, German kommen, Latin venire, Greek bainein, Sanskrit gam-. In Proto-Italic this gave *gʷen-je-, in Latin venīre, ventum, one of the commonest verbs. Prefixed with per-, through, thoroughly, it became pervenīre: to come through difficulties to a goal, to reach, attain, succeed. Cicero uses pervenire ad honores, to attain office; Virgil uses it for reaching shores after voyaging.

  • Late Latin to Old French parvenir. Phonetic change turned pervenire into Old French parvenir, parvenir à: to reach, manage, succeed. The prefix per- weakened to par-, v stayed, infinitive ending levelled. Feudal usage stressed social arrival: parvenir aux honneurs, to rise to honours.
  • French past participle as noun. The past participle parvenu, parvenue, arrived, having succeeded, began in the 15th-17th centuries to be used substantively for a person newly risen in rank or fortune. Molière's age loved to mock les parvenus: financiers, tax-farmers, bourgeois buying offices, aping nobles but betraying low birth by manners, dress or accent. The feminine parvenue was equally cutting.
  • Borrowing into English. English borrowed parvenu in 1802, Napoleonic era, when émigrés and war profiteers made new fortunes conspicuous. Byron, Thackeray and later Edith Wharton wielded it for social climbers. Pronunciation vacillated between French /paʁ.və.ny/ and anglicized /ˈpɑː.və.njuː/. Spelling kept the masculine form generically; purists reserve parvenue for women.
  • Semantic pejoration. What was neutral arrival became presumptuous intrusion. The shift required a stratified society that valued birth over wealth: to have arrived implies you were not there before and are not truly welcome now. Dictionaries mark it derogatory. Synonyms like upstart, nouveau riche, arriviste share the sneer; self-made man is the admiring counterpart.
  • Modern ironic extension. In the 20th-21st centuries parvenu broadened beyond birth class to any arriviste taste: parvenu art, parvenu suburbs, intellectual parvenu. Sociologists use it neutrally for upward mobility, but literary use stays barbed. The present context — that timid parvenu, man — is a striking philosophical twist: humanity itself as a late, timid newcomer among beasts, newly master of earth yet evolutionarily upstart. Durant inverts snobbery into deep time: man the successful intruder.

Phonetically little changed since French; semantically everything changed from motion to status. The path is come → come through → succeed → newly succeeded person → vulgar climber. Each step adds social evaluation to physical movement.

Culturally, parvenu encodes anxiety about authenticity: who belongs, who performs belonging, and who judges. To call man a timid parvenu is to remind readers that dominance is recent, insecure and perhaps ill-mannered — an upstart mammal aping mastery.
