The word pall is an extraordinary lexicographical convergence in the English language, where two historically independent linguistic streams—one nominal and one verbal—evolved in parallel, eventually influencing and mutually reinforcing each other's evocative connotations of fading vitality, darkness, and solemnity.

1. The Verbal Stream: From Pale Pigment to Sensory Satiety

The verb to pall (meaning to lose interest, become tasteless, flat, or tedious) descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *pel- / *pal-, meaning "pale, grey, dark-colored, or dull." This root evolved into an extensive network of color and fading terms across the Indo-European languages:

  • In Proto-Germanic, it produced *falwaz, which entered Old English as fealu (modern English fallow, describing pale yellowish-brown soil or fields).
  • In Ancient Greek, it yielded polios (πολιός, "grey, white-haired").
  • In Latin, it produced the verb pallere ("to be pale, wan, or colorless") and the adjective pallidus ("pale, sickly"), which entered English directly as pale and pallid.

In Old French, the adjective pale formed the derivative verb apalir or apallir, meaning "to make pale, grow pale, lose color, fade, or weaken in vitality." When this Norman-French verb was borrowed into Middle English during the 13th and 14th centuries, it appeared as apallen (meaning to cause to fade, to lose courage, or to become weak, which survives in the modern English verb appall, literally "to make someone turn pale with horror").

Through the common linguistic process of aphesis (the gradual dropping of an unaccented initial short vowel), Middle English speakers shortened apallen to pallen. In 14th-century culinary and enological contexts, pallen specifically described fermented beverages (such as ale, wine, and cider) that had sat too long exposed to air, losing their effervescence, freshness, alcohol vigor, and crisp flavor, turning flat, lifeless, and insipid. By the late 16th century (in the works of Shakespeare and his contemporaries), this physical sense of beer "going flat" shifted into the psychological realm: when entertainments, pleasures, flattery, or fireworks "pall," they lose their sparkling zest and become tiresome.

2. The Nominal Stream: From Classical Cloak to Funeral Shroud

The noun pall (a dark covering cloth or figurative blanket of gloom) follows an entirely separate ancient lineage, descending from the PIE root *pel- (meaning "skin, hide, covering, cloth," cognate with English pelt, pellicle, and film):

  • In Classical Latin, it produced pallium, a large rectangular woollen cloak or mantle worn by Greeks and Roman philosophers, draped over the tunic.
  • In the early Christian Church, the pallium became a consecrated liturgical vestment—a narrow circular band of white lamb's wool adorned with black crosses worn over the shoulders by the Pope and conferred upon metropolitan archbishops as a symbol of pastoral authority.
  • In early Old English (c. 8th–9th century), Latin pallium was borrowed as pæll, designating expensive, fine-woven purple silk or velvet cloth used for royal coronation robes, altar hangings, and noble bed coverings.

3. Specialization into Funeral Pall & The Pallbearer

During the High Middle Ages (c. 13th–14th century), the noun pall underwent a solemn funerary specialization:

  • It became the standard term for the large, heavy black, purple, or white velvet cloth spread over a coffin, bier, or tomb during a Christian funeral mass.
  • The noble attendants or close friends chosen to hold the four corners of this heavy cloth or carry the casket during the funeral procession were designated as pallbearers—a term that remains universally used in the English-speaking world today.

4. Convergence: The 19th-Century Figurative Blanket of Gloom

By the 18th and 19th centuries, the noun pall had generated a pervasive figurative meaning in English literature. Just as a physical funeral pall cast darkness and solemn finality over a casket, any oppressive, dark, suffocating phenomenon—a heavy layer of industrial coal smoke, a sudden wave of grief, an eerie silence, or an ominous cloud—was described as "a pall":

  • Authors such as Charles Dickens, Edgar Allan Poe, and Leo Tolstoy frequently evoked this imagery: "a pall of black smoke hung over the city," "the tragic announcement cast a pall of gloom over the assembly."
  • This figurative noun merged seamlessly in the cultural imagination with the verb to pall: an atmosphere where fireworks and festivities pall (lose their joy) feels as though a heavy pall of boredom and mortality has been draped over the scene.
