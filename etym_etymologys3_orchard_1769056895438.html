Orchard is a biography of the 'enclosed plant.' Its first life begins in Old English as ortgeard, a household compound of two ancient Germanic ingredients: ort, the 'plant,' and geard, the 'enclosure.' The word was not inherited ready-made from Proto-Indo-European; it was coined inside English from materials each several thousand years old. The same conceptual recipe — 'fruit-tree enclosure' — was made independently across the Germanic world: German Baumgarten ('tree-garden'), Dutch boomgaard ('tree-yard'). The English coinage happened to be the one that stuck, and it stuck in the specific sense of 'a fenced place planted with fruit trees,' the very image the Narnia passage exploits when the tangle of apples is called an orchard. Root word 1: ort ('plant'). Ort is the Old English plant-word wyrt, metathesized: the w of the cluster wr was lost before r and the vowel shortened, so wyrt became ort. Wyrt descends from Proto-Germanic *wurtiz, a form of the great PIE root *wréh₂d- 'root', which in Germanic split into two shapes: *wrōts 'root' and *wurtiz 'root, plant.' The wider family is breathtaking: Greek rhízā 'root' (with English rhizome, rhizoid), Latin rādīx (English radical, radish, eradicate), Welsh gwraidd, Gothic waúrts, Old Norse rót (which gave English 'root' as a loan), and German Wurzel. In Old English wyrt was the everyday word for any plant, herb, or root, and it survives today only as the -wort of plant names (St John's wort, liverwort, pennywort) — a fossil of a once-general word, exactly as the orchard itself is a fossil of a once-planned landscape.
  • The metathesis wyrt → ort is a pure sound change: the loss of the initial w before r (wr- → r-), with a corresponding shortening of the vowel, is attested in the written record of late Old English.
  • The narrowing of wyrt from 'any plant or root' to 'a plant' mirrors the way the compound's second element came to dominate the meaning: the 'plant-enclosure' became specifically a 'fruit-tree enclosure'.
  • Because the -wort element is now frozen into plant-names, modern speakers do not recognise that 'orchard' contains the same root as 'liverwort' — a hidden kinship worth noting.
Root word 2: geard ('enclosure'). Geard is from PIE *gʰerdʰ- 'to enclose,' whose family includes Latin hortus (horticulture), Greek khórtos, the Slavic grad 'city' (Novgorod, Belgorod), Lithuanian gardas, Sanskrit gṛhá (Hindi ghar 'house'), and, in Germanic, *gardaz — the ancestor of yard, garden (via the Frankish loan into French), and the -gard of Scandinavian names (Holmgard). In Old English geard was the ordinary word for an enclosed piece of ground — a yard, a court, a garden — and in its compound with ort it contributed the essential idea of boundedness: an orchard is an enclosure.
  • The family of *gʰerdʰ- shows the full semantic range of 'enclosure': from the cattle-yard (yard) to the cultivated plot (garden) to the defended town (the Slavic -grad).
  • The same Germanic element appears in 'gird' (to encircle), 'girth', and in the loan 'garden' that re-entered English from Old North French gardin; the orchard's geard is the same 'yard' spelled a thousand years older.
  • The compound ortgeard thus means 'plant-yard' — the enclosure where plants are grown — and the narrowing from 'plants' to 'fruit trees' is the only real semantic change the compound ever underwent.
The social history. The word was coined in the Anglo-Saxon farmstead, where the enclosed plot beside the hall was the household's larder of herbs and fruit. Through the medieval manor the orchard became a standard feature of the great house, providing cider apples, pears, and plums for the table. In literature the orchard carries the Edenic register: a planted place of ordered plenty, and when abandoned, the emblem of a paradise lost. Chekhov's The Cherry Orchard made the word a symbol of a whole way of life passing away, and the Narnia passage quietly repeats the elegy: the orchard has outlived the kingdom, a living ruin fruitful in apples and useless for fuel. The word has never travelled far in meaning — it has been 'enclosed plant-ground' for a thousand years — but its emotional temperature has cooled from the everyday to the nostalgic.
