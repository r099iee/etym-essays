Introduction. Courtship is a word with a royal pedigree and a romantic career: its first element descends from the Indo-European word for an enclosure, becomes the Latin garden, the Roman cohort, and the medieval royal court, and finally — in the sixteenth century — the English verb of wooing; its second element, the suffix -ship, is one of the oldest abstract-making suffixes of English, descended from a Germanic verb meaning "to shape, to create". The compound means, at bottom, "the state of courting" — and the whole history of the court is inside it. The two halves must be traced separately and then joined.

1. The court: from *ǵʰer- to cohors

The chain opens with a Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰer-, "to enclose, to surround", whose derivative *ǵʰor-tó- meant "an enclosed place": from it came Greek khórtos ("an enclosed yard, a pen; fodder grown in it"), Latin hortus ("garden"), and the Germanic *gardaz ("enclosure, yard") that gives English yard, garden, and garth, and Gothic gards ("house, enclosure"). Latin put its prefix for "together", com-, in front of hortus to make cohors (accusative cohortem): the word meant, at first, "an enclosed courtyard", and then — by a decisive and lovely transfer — "the company gathered in the yard": the farmyard throng of men and beasts, then the soldiers mustered in an enclosed space. In the classical army a cohors was a tenth of a legion, and English cohort is a direct loan of that military sense. The crucial development came when the rulers of Late Antiquity and the Middle Ages attached their following to their residence: the yard, the court, became the ruler's household — curtis, "the court", meaning both the palace enclosure and the retinue that filled it.

2. From cohors to court: Old French and Middle English

Popular Latin reduced cohortem to *cōrtem (the form behind curtis in medieval documents), and Old French inherited it as cort (also curt, later cour), attested from the tenth to twelfth centuries with three interlocking senses: the courtyard itself; the royal court — the ruler's residence, household, and entourage; and the tribunal, the court of law, because justice was dispensed in the ruler's court. Middle English took the word in the twelfth century (through Anglo-Norman curte) with all three senses: Chaucer's court of Theseus, the shire court, the castle courtyard, the households of courtly romance — and from the court came the great family of courtly vocabulary: courtier, courtesy (from Old French cortoisie, "courtliness"), courtesan, and finally the verb to court.

3. The verb "to court": paying court to princes, then to lovers

The English verb to court, recorded from the sixteenth century, first meant "to attend or reside at court" and "to pay court to" — to seek the favour of a prince or powerful patron, as in the etiquette manuals of Renaissance courts (Castiglione's Book of the Courtier, 1528, the classic of the art of pleasing at court). By the 1570s the verb had made the transfer to love: to court a woman was to seek her favour with the full ceremonial vocabulary of courtship-of-the-court — attendance, service, presents, addresses, and devotion — exactly as the French idiom faire la cour, "to pay court", was used of wooing. The bridge is precise and social: the beloved was treated as a sovereign, the lover as her courtier; the whole Renaissance psychology of love — service, patience, merit, and the hope of grace — is a translation of court practice into romance. It is this world, poeticised by the sonneteers and dramatised by Shakespeare's comedies, that made the English of love permanently court-flavoured.

4. The suffix -ship: from *skep- to -scipe

The second element of the compound is the suffix -ship, one of the oldest of English abstract-makers. It descends from Proto-Germanic *-skapiz, the suffix of state, condition, office, and dignity — the same suffix as German -schaft (Freundschaft, "friendship"), Dutch -schap, and Old Norse -skapr — and it is built on the Germanic verb *skapjaną, "to make, shape, create" (Old English scieppan, whose agent noun scyppend, "the Creator", was the ordinary Old English name of God; and the noun shape itself, from the same root). The suffix is usually traced further back to an Indo-European root *skep-, "to cut, hew, shape" — often compared with Greek skáptō, "to dig" — though the wider Indo-European connections are uncertain, and handbooks mark them as such. The semantic logic is transparent: a derived noun in -ship names the state or condition that has been "made" or "shaped" — friendship is the state of being a friend, lordship the office of a lord, worship (worth-ship) the condition of being worthy. Old English used the suffix richly (frēondscipe "friendship", hlāfordscipe "lordship", weorðscipe "worship"), and it has stayed a productive modern suffix: professorship, craftsmanship, membership, readership.

5. The compound and its career (1580s to the present)

English joined the two halves in the 1580s: courtship, first recorded in the sense "the behaviour or art of a courtier, courtly manners", and then, by the 1590s, in the sense that has since ruled the word — "the wooing of a person, the process of seeking a person's love with a view to marriage". The Elizabethan court, the forcing-house of both senses, gave the word its double life: the same word served the intrigues of courtiers and the ardours of lovers, and the romantic sense won entirely. From the seventeenth century, "courtship" is the standard formal word for the approach to marriage: conduct books regulate it, the novel's central plot machinery runs on it, and tender prose — "This was his courtship, and it lasted all through the summer" (Wilde, The Nightingale and the Rose) — uses it as the plain name of the whole hopeful season. In the twentieth century the word's social ground shifted: "dating" took over the everyday function, "courtship" retired into formal, historical, and literary registers, and biology gave it a brilliant second career — Darwin's Descent of Man (1871) and the ethologists who followed made "courtship" the technical word for animal mating behaviour, so that a peacock's display is now a "courtship display" and a bowerbird's bower a courtship structure. Meanwhile politics and business revived the oldest sense of the verb: candidates "court" the voters, companies "court" investors, exactly as courtiers once courted princes. The word has thus come full circle — from the enclosure, through the cohort and the court, to the courting of lovers, animals, and voters — and in every sense something is being sought, and someone is being won, with ceremony and intent.
