diastole is pure Greek made universal by medicine. The verb stellein meant to put, arrange, send or draw, from PIE *stel- to put or stand, source also of English stalk, stall and apostle as one sent forth. Prefixed with dia- meaning apart, through or across, it yields diastellein to draw apart, separate or set at interval, whose noun of action diastole means a drawing apart, separation, interval or expansion. Its twin systole joins syn- together with the same base to mean a drawing together or contraction. Aristotle uses both for general physics of expansion and contraction, rhetoricians for dilation versus compression of speech, and metricians for lengthening versus shortening of syllables.

Medicine seized the pair. Hippocratic writers described arteries pulsating, but Galen of Pergamum in the 2nd century CE fixed diastole as the dilation of heart and arteries when pneuma and blood flow in and systole as their contraction when expelled. Transliterated unchanged into Late Latin as diastole, the terms travelled through Byzantine Oribasius, Arabic Avicenna as inbisat and inqibad, and Salernitan Latin into medieval universities. Middle English medicine knew systole and diastole by the 14th century in translations of Galen, though humoral theory reversed their active-passive values.

  • rhetorical diastole: Quintilian dilatio, dwelling expansively on a point.
  • metrical diastole: grammarians' licence to make a short long, marked by diastole dots.
  • Galenic pulse: diastole as active expansion drawing pneuma, systole as active contraction.
  • Harveyan reversal: 1628 De motu cordis proves systole active ejection, diastole passive filling.

William Harvey's 1628 demonstration of circulation redefined diastole as we know it: ventricular systole ejects, diastole relaxes and fills. Laennec's stethoscope, Marey's sphygmograph and Riva-Rocci's cuff made diastolic pressure and diastolic murmurs audible and numeric, the lower number of 120/80 marking the heart at rest. Modern cardiology subdivides diastole into isovolumic relaxation, rapid filling, diastasis and atrial systole, and mourns diastolic heart failure when stiffness prevents opening. Phonetically English /daɪˈæstəliː/ keeps Greek diphthong dia- and Latinate -e, while French /djastɔl/ and Italian diastole show parallel learned borrowings. Orthographically the -stole from stellein links it invisibly to stole, apostle and epistle as things sent.

The source sentence exploits the full etymology: reading as cardiac cycle, linear systole contracting onto sequence and imagistic diastole expanding apart onto vision. From oratory to cardiology to criticism, diastole has always named the generous interval when things draw apart to let life in.

For learners the contrast dia- apart versus syn- together unlocks the whole family: diastole draws apart, systole draws together, stole is a garment sent around shoulders, apostle one sent forth, epistle a message sent to another. Once stellein is felt as to put or send, medical diastole becomes transparent as the putting-apart of heart walls. Modern cardiology deepened rather than replaced Galen: echocardiography divides diastole into isovolumic relaxation, rapid filling, diastasis and atrial kick, each a finer drawing-apart within the larger pause.

  • isovolumic relaxation: valves closed, pressure falling, preserving sealed interval.
  • rapid filling and diastasis: blood rushing then pausing, preserving receptive openness.
  • atrial kick: final atrial push completing fill, preserving preparation for systole.
