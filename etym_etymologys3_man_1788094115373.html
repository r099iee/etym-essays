MAN is one of the oldest and most fought-over words in English, and its history is a mirror of social history. The noun descends from Proto-Germanic *mann- 'person, human being', attested across all the old Germanic languages (Gothic manna, Old Norse maðr, Old High German man, Old English mann). The traditional etymology connects *mann- with the PIE root *men- 'to think', making a human being literally 'the one who thinks' — the same root that gives Latin mens 'mind', English mind, and Sanskrit manu- 'human being' (Manu, the lawgiver of Indian tradition, is 'the thinking one'). This is the etymology given by Pokorny and most handbooks, but it is not universally accepted: some scholars note that a derivation from 'to think' is plausible but unprovable, and treat *mann- as a Germanic word of uncertain Indo-European ancestry. The word is a pan-Germanic isolate in this respect — every Germanic language has it, and no non-Germanic relative is certain.

  • Old English (8th–11th c.): mann (also mon/n, with the usual umlaut alternation) means 'a human being of either sex', 'a person', 'mankind', and secondarily 'a servant, a vassal' — the social hierarchy of Anglo-Saxon England makes 'man' also mean 'one who owes service'. The gender-neutral breadth is visible in compounds: wīfmann 'woman' (literally 'female person'), and in the indefinite pronoun man 'one' (like German man, French on): OE man cwæð 'one said'.
  • Middle English (12th–15th c.): the spelling settles as man. The decisive semantic narrowing occurs: by the 13th–14th centuries man primarily means an adult male, while the generic 'human being' sense retreats to elevated or compound use (mankind, manhood, manslaughter 'killing of a human being'). The gender asymmetry becomes structural: woman (from wīfmann) keeps the -man ending that no longer means 'male'.
  • c. 1450 — birth of the verb: 'to man' is first recorded meaning 'to furnish with men', above all of ships: 'man the fleet', 'man the boat' — the nautical sense is the original one, because a ship is useless without its crew. From ships the verb spreads to fortifications (to man the walls, the guns), and in the 19th–20th centuries to any post needing personnel: man the pumps (a famous Titanic-era cry), man the barricades (revolutionary rhetoric), man the phones (office life).
  • Early Modern (16th–17th c.): the generic sense peaks in elevated prose — 'What is man, that thou art mindful of him?' (Psalm 8, AV 1611); 'man is a rational animal' becomes the stock philosophical formula. Meanwhile the verb acquires the reflexive 'man oneself' = 'summon courage' (16th c.), and 'the Man' is attested in 16th-century slang for an authority or master.
  • 18th–19th c.: the Enlightenment and the revolutionary era give the word its modern political charge: 'the rights of man' (Paine, 1791), 'man is born free' (Rousseau), 'the proper study of mankind is man' (Pope). In the same era the verb gains its emergency-operational register ('man the lifeboats', 'all hands to man the pumps').
  • 20th–21st c. — the generic sense under revision: from the 1970s onward, feminist linguistics and style guides argue that 'man' as a generic is misleading and sexist; 'chairman'→'chair', 'man-hours'→'person-hours', 'mankind'→'humankind' in careful usage. The 1960s counterculture adds 'the Man' = the oppressive establishment; 'man up' (first recorded c. 1900 in 'man up to one's responsibilities', revived as a toughness-imperative around 2000) is itself now contested. The verb, however, is completely unmarked: 'man the desk', 'man the phones' are ordinary, and the verb even appears gender-neutrally of mixed or female staff.

Phonetically the word is a near-perfect fossil: OE mann → ME man → ModE man /mæn/, the main changes being the shortening of the doubled nasal and the regular development of Old English short a in the standard dialect. Its orthography is unchanged since Middle English. Literary anchors: Beowulf's mann-compounds; the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle; Chaucer's 'man'; the Authorised Version's generic man; Shakespeare's 'What a piece of work is a man!' (Hamlet, c. 1600); Paine's Rights of Man (1791); Kipling's 'man the pumps' world; and the style-guide debates of the 1970s–2020s. Few English words offer so complete a record of how a society's gender categories and social hierarchies are written into a single syllable.
