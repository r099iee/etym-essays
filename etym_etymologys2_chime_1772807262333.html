Derivation chain — single root (with a debated early stage): - PIE *(s)geub- / *gembh- "to bend, to curve, to hollow out" (the root commonly cited for Greek kymbē "bowl"; its status as a genuine IE root is debated, and Greek kymbē may be a Pre-Greek substrate word), reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE — but see the debated-points note below. - → Greek κύμβη (kymbē) "bowl, cup, hollow vessel, hollow of a ship," attested from Homer (8th c. BCE). - → Greek κύμβαλον (kymbalon) "cymbal," attested 5th c. BCE (in lyric and drama; the name literally means "bowl-instrument," from the hollow shape). - → Latin cymbalum "cymbal," attested 1st c. BCE (Cicero, Virgil, and later Vulgate usage). - → Old French chimbe / chymbe "cymbal," attested 12th c. - → Middle English chime / chymbe / chimbe "cymbal, bell, set of bells, ringing of bells," attested 13th c. (c. 1230, in the Ancrene Wisse and later ME texts). - → CHIME "a bell or set of bells; the ringing sound they make; an instrument of metal bowls or tubes that rings when struck," modern English. Debated / nuanced points: - The early chain is genuinely disputed. OED notes that it is doubtful whether English chime is a direct descendant of Latin cymbalum or a parallel formation: Old French chymbe may have been remodeled on cymbalum or formed independently, and the sense development "cymbal → bell → set of bells → ringing sound" crosses a broad semantic gap that is not fully documented. - The Greek word κύμβη (kymbē) "bowl, cup" is assigned by Beekes and others to a Pre-Greek substrate rather than a securely reconstructable PIE root; the root *(s)geub- / *gembh- "hollow, curved" is a plausible but debated reconstruction. - Alternative folk derivations (e.g. from a supposed Germanic root meaning "to resound") have no historical support and are rejected by modern dictionaries.
