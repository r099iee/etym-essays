Jewel-encrusted describes an object whose surface is densely covered, set, or studded with jewels — literally “encrusted”, as if the gems formed a hard, glittering crust over the base material. In the description of Bao-yu's coronet in The Story of the Stone — “a small jewel-encrusted gold coronet on the top of his head” — the compound does two things at once. First, it names the material fact of the headdress: the gold is only the ground, and the true value lies in the crust of jewels that covers it. Second, it delivers a verdict of rank: jewel-encrusting is the decoration of imperial and princely luxury, and to describe a boy's everyday headdress in these terms is to mark him at once as the family's “precious” centrepiece — a walking treasury. The tone of the passage is one of astonished, appraising opulence: Dai-yu, who has just been told that her cousin is a “graceless creature”, sees instead a young gentleman whose brow is crowned with hardened, sparkling wealth. The senses of the compound and its parts.
  • Jewel-encrusted (adjective) — covered or studded with jewels so densely that they form a continuous, crust-like layer; the card's sense, typically of crowns, regalia, reliquaries, swords, and luxury objects.
  • Encrusted (generally) — covered with a hard crust or coating, in science, nature, and daily life: a barnacle-encrusted hull, an ice-encrusted window, a rust-encrusted pipe, salt-encrusted bread. Here the word is literal and geological.
  • Metaphoric encrusted — burdened or overlaid with layers of age, tradition, or convention: a ritual “encrusted with centuries of custom”, a bureaucracy “encrusted with rules”.
  • Encrust (verb) / encrustation (noun) — to cover with or form a crust; the act or result of forming a crust, as of minerals, salt, or (in pathology) a coating on a membrane.
  • Jewel (noun) — a precious stone; also, figuratively, anything or anyone greatly valued: “a jewel of a girl”, “the crown jewels”, “the jewel in the crown”.
  • Jeweller / jewellery (British) or jewelry (American) — the craftsman of gems and precious metals; personal ornaments of gold, silver, and stones.
  • Compound family — diamond-encrusted, pearl-encrusted, gold-encrusted, gem-encrusted; also the older participle bejewelled, and the poetic beset (“a crown beset with rubies”).
The semantic force of “encrusted”. The choice of encrusted rather than bejewelled or set is precise. To bejewel is to adorn with jewels placed here and there as ornament; to set is to seat a stone in a metal mount, one or a few at a time, as a ring is “set with rubies”. To encrust is far stronger: a crust is something that forms over a surface and adheres to it, so a jewel-encrusted object is one in which the gems have been packed so thickly that they cover the base almost entirely, like frost on a window or salt on a coastal rock. The word borrows the force of geology — minerals building up a layer — and applies it to the jeweller's art. A jewel-encrusted coronet is therefore not merely decorated: its surface has been converted into a continuous field of gems, and the gold beneath functions only as the ground that holds the crust. It is the vocabulary of the treasure-house, of reliquaries and royal regalia, where the point is not a tasteful stone here and there but the transformation of an entire surface into hardened, glittering wealth. Registers and uses. The compound belongs to three registers. In history and art, jewel-encrusted is the standard description of crowns, orbs, sceptres, reliquaries, icons, and illuminated book-covers — the objects of medieval and early modern treasuries in which gems were set without visible gold, the surfaces reading as solid crusts of stone. In fiction and journalism it is a favourite word for orientalist and fantastical opulence: jewel-encrusted thrones, idols, harnesses, and hilt-fittings. In parody and hyperbole it marks the far end of luxury — the phrase “a jewel-encrusted phone” or “a jewel-encrusted football” signals absurd excess. The single word thus carries both awe and its satire: to call something jewel-encrusted is to say that its maker has pushed ornament to the point where the base material disappears, and that is either the summit of splendour or the height of ostentation, depending on the speaker's tone. Historical bridge. The compound joins two distinct histories. Jewel, entering English in the thirteenth century from Old French jouel, descends through Latin iocus “jest, joke, sport” — a jewel is etymologically a “plaything”, the rich man's toy, the word gliding from jest to treasure. Encrusted, entering English in the sixteenth century from Old French encroster, descends from Latin incrustāre, “to cover with a crust”, built on crusta, “a hard outer surface, shell, rind”, from the Indo-European root *kreus-, “to freeze, congeal, form a crust” — the same root that gives English crust, and, in the Germanic languages, the words for ice and rime. The compound therefore fuses the language of play and delight (the jest that became a treasure) with the language of cold and hardening (the freeze that became a crust): a jewel-encrusted object is treasure that has been frozen into a covering layer — light made hard, wealth turned to rind. It is a word whose two halves carry, in miniature, two very different histories of European culture: the courtly luxury of the jewel and the geological patience of the crust.
