Skids is the inflected third-person singular and plural noun form of skid, a word whose documented history begins not in Latin but in the Germanic timber yard. The ultimate root is PIE *skei- to cut or split, which yields a vast family including shed, schism, ski and shin. In Proto-Germanic the extended stem *skīd- denotes a split piece of wood. Old Norse attests skið as stick, billet of wood, snowshoe and the long runner that becomes English ski. English skid appears in the 1650s meaning a beam or plank laid under a heavy object to let it slide, or a wedge to brake a cartwheel. The borrowing is usually explained as from Old Norse skið via northern dialects, with Middle English cognates such as shide for a plank supporting the connection.

Phonetically the development is regular. PIE *sk- remains sk- in Germanic because the cluster escaped Grimm's Law spirantisation that affected single stops. The diphthongal root *skei- monophthongises to long -i- in Germanic *skīd-, shortened in English to /skɪd/ with the Great Vowel Shift not affecting short i. Final inflection -s for third-person singular continues Old English -es, pronounced /z/ after a voiced consonant, giving RP /skɪdz/. The noun plural skids is identical in form. No French or Latin intermediary reshapes the word; spelling skid is stable since the seventeenth century.

Semantic drift moves from timber to traction to moral decline:

  • Timber support 1650s-1700s: a skid is a log or beam for rolling casks, launching ships or braking wheels. The bridge to sliding is functional: objects slide on skids.
  • Brake-shoe and drag 18th-19th c.: to skid or to put on the skid means to apply a friction brake, then to lock wheels so they slide.
  • Uncontrolled slide 1830s-1900s: wheels skid, cars skid, in the new motoring and cycling age. The meaning inverts from deliberate sliding aid to accidental loss of grip. Anti-skid tyres and skid marks belong here.
  • Aeronautical and social slide 20th c.: planes skid in turns, prices skid, careers go on the skids or hit the skids, meaning to decline toward ruin. The bridge is loss of control reinterpreted as moral or economic slippage.

Socio-cultural context is industrial and automotive. The verb explodes with bicycles, trams and motor cars, where skidding on wet cobbles or tarmac became a daily danger. Highway codes, accident reports and comic writing all adopt skid. Wodehouse's image of a brain that skids after whizzing along draws directly on 1920s motoring slang familiar to every reader: too much speed, failed steering, ditch. Earlier timber and nautical uses survive only in specialised phrases like skid row, originally skid road, the track down which logs slid to the mill, later reinterpreted as a district of decline.

Literary attestations show this clearly. Seventeenth-century naval accounts mention skids for guns and boats. Nineteenth-century railway and cycling manuals warn against skidding. Twentieth-century fiction from Wodehouse to Chandler uses skid for comic and hard-boiled loss of control. The modern word is fully established as both noun and verb, with skids as its ordinary inflected form, carrying a vivid kinetic image of momentum betrayed by surface.
