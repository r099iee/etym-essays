Flunkeys are liveried male servants — footmen in uniform — and the word is used here in the plural. The specific literary context is David Hawkes's translation of the early chapters of The Story of the Stone (Cao Xueqin's Dream of the Red Chamber): "In front of the gateway ten or so splendidly dressed flunkeys sat in a row." The word here does more than label household staff. Ten liveried servants seated in a row before the great gate form a public tableau of institutional power: access to the mansion is mediated, announced, and controlled by a regimented hierarchy of attendants, and the sheer number of them advertises the family's wealth as clearly as any carved rafter. The tone is social-realist with a satirical edge — the translation deliberately chooses flunkeys, a mildly dismissive British word, so that the imposing spectacle is simultaneously a display of servility; the servants are impressive and faintly ridiculous at once, sitting like ornaments on the very threshold of power.

The word is heavily loaded and richly polysemous, and a full definition must separate the concrete and the figurative senses:

  • Concrete sense (household service): a liveried manservant, especially a footman — a uniformed attendant whose duties are ceremonial and domestic, stationed at gates and doors, attending carriages and tables, and displaying the household's wealth simply by standing there in regimented order.
  • Figurative sense (the toady): by extension, any servile, obsequious follower or hanger-on who ingratiates himself with a superior — a subordinate who trades dignity for favour; in this sense the word needs no livery and no house, only a patron and a willingness to please.
  • Political sense ("a political flunky"): in American English especially, "flunky" designates a minor official or party loyalist appointed or employed to do another's bidding — a placeman, a hack, a tool of an administration; the plural "flunkeys/flunkies" is routinely used of the whole body of compliant assistants around a powerful figure.
  • Derived formations: "flunkeyism" (a noun of the nineteenth century) names the practice or culture of servility, obsequious deference to rank; "flunkeyish" (adjective) describes behaviour that is fawning or overdressed in a servile way; the word can also be used as a half-verb in informal idiom, as in "flunkeying around" (behaving like a flunkey).
  • Spelling variation: the plural appears as both "flunkeys" and "flunkies" (the -ies form following the ordinary plural of nouns in -y), and the singular as both "flunkey" and "flunky"; "flunky" is the more common spelling in American English, "flunkey" the more literary British one. Both are pronounced identically.

Boundary comparisons sharpen the sense. A footman is the neutral name for a liveried servant who attends carriages and doors; flunkey adds an unmistakable note of condescension and suggests ceremonial display rather than real work. A lackey (from French laquais) is the nearest synonym — equally pejorative — but it emphasises running at the master's side, where flunkey emphasises the livery and the station at the gate. A valet is a personal body-servant who attends one gentleman intimately (dressing, shaving, tending his clothes); flunkeys are public, undifferentiated, and purely decorative. A butler stands at the head of the male staff, dignified and in charge of the cellar and dining room; flunkeys sit near the bottom of the ladder, interchangeable in their uniforms. A menial stresses low rank and manual toil; flunkey stresses costume and post — the menial works, the flunkey stands and looks the part. A minion is a favourite subordinate of a powerful person, valued and protected; a flunkey is anonymous, replaceable, and despised.
