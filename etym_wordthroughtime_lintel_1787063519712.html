- 14th c.: English borrows lintel for the horizontal timber or stone over a door — a plain builder’s word, strictly practical.
- 15th–17th c.: stays firmly architectural across medieval and Tudor building, naming the load-bearing top member of a doorway or window.
- 18th c.: the classical revival links the lintel to the Greek epistyle and architrave; it gains antiquarian prestige as the emblem of post-and-lintel (trabeated) construction.
- 19th c.: archaeology popularises “post-and-lintel” as the basic system of ancient stone architecture (Stonehenge, Egypt, Greece), lifting lintel from builder’s jargon into educated general vocabulary.
- 20th–21st c.: a precise professional term for the spanning member over openings, still slightly bookish in everyday speech, quietly fundamental whenever doorways in stone are described.
