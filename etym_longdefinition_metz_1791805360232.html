Metz is a city in north-eastern France, built where the Seille joins the Moselle, historic capital of the Moselle département and, until 2015, of the administrative region of Lorraine. In this reading it is the place at which Louis XV, hastening from Flanders to the relief of Alsace in 1744, “was arrested at Metz by a malady which threatened to cut short his days”. The verb arrested is used in its older sense of “stopped, brought to a halt”: the king's progress was interrupted at Metz by a severe illness, and the crisis of that illness gave him the surname le Bien-Aimé.

Metz is one of the oldest cities of France and one of the most continuously strategic. Its name is not French in origin and not even Latin in its first form: it descends from the Gaulish tribe of the Mediomatrici, whose capital was the town the Romans called Divodurum and later Mettis. The modern name is thus a double inheritance — a Celtic tribal name and a Latin city name — filtered through Gallo-Romance phonology.

The 1744 episode

  • Context. In the War of the Austrian Succession the French had been campaigning successfully in the Austrian Netherlands when an Austro-Saxon army under Prince Charles of Lorraine threatened Alsace; Louis XV left Flanders to join the army on the Rhine.
  • Illness. At Metz, in August 1744, the king fell seriously ill; his condition was soon reported as dangerous, and the churches of France offered prayers for his recovery.
  • Aftermath. His recovery was greeted with public rejoicing, and the title le Bien-Aimé — “the Well-Beloved” — became attached to him; Carlyle and Hénault discuss it precisely as one of the royal surnames whose origin is hard to date.

Historical outline

  • Gaulish and Roman. Divodurum was the capital of the Mediomatrici, a people of the middle Moselle; under the empire it became one of the largest cities of Gaul, with an amphitheatre, baths and aqueducts, and its name appears in the Roman itineraries.
  • Late empire and Franks. In the fourth century the city is recorded as Mettis; it was sacked by Attila's Huns in 451 and later became a Merovingian royal residence and a centre of Austrasia.
  • Imperial city. From the twelfth century Metz was a free imperial city, the “République messine”, governed by its own magistrates and prince-bishops and famous for its Roman walls and its dragon legend, the Graoully.
  • French frontier. Henry II occupied Metz in 1552 and Charles V failed to retake it in the siege of 1552–53; the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 confirmed French possession, and Vauban later strengthened the fortifications.
  • Modern wars. In the Franco-Prussian War the army of Bazaine capitulated at Metz on 27 October 1870, a disaster that sealed the fall of the Second Empire; the city was German from 1871 to 1918, annexed again in 1940 and liberated after the Battle of Metz in late 1944.

The city today

  • Architecture. The cathedral of Saint-Étienne, with its vast stained-glass windows, and the churches of the old town make Metz a noted centre of medieval and modern stained glass.
  • Culture. The Centre Pompidou-Metz, opened in 2010, has given the city a national profile in contemporary art; the Arsenal and the opera continue a long musical tradition.
  • Language. The traditional speech of the region is Lorraine Franconian, a Germanic dialect, alongside French; the demonym is Messin, feminine Messine.
  • Geography. Metz lies at a crossroads of the Moselle valley, midway between the Rhine and the Paris basin, which explains its repeated military importance.

Status of the word

“Metz” is a pure proper noun. English uses it unchanged, without an article and without translation; the spelling is identical in French and German, though the pronunciation differs — French /mɛs/, with a silent final t and z, English /mɛts/. There is no common noun “metz”, no derived verb and no figurative sense, although the name carries strong associations: siege, capitulation, annexation, and the stubborn survival of a border city.

Register and connotation

In French, Metz is a byword for a certain austere eastern respectability, a garrison and cathedral city; in English it appears mainly in military and diplomatic history. Carlyle's sentence uses it with no colour at all: Metz is simply the point on the road where the king's body stops the campaign. The interest for the reader is that this neutral place-name is the hinge of the whole episode, since the illness at Metz produced the epithet that Hénault and Carlyle go on to discuss.
