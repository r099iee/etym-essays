1. Indo-European Roots and Temporal Segmentation. The morphological pedigree of the English word tidy begins in the deep phonological stratum of Proto-Indo-European with the reconstructed verbal root *dā- (alternatively represented in modern laryngeal notation as *deh₂- or with an i-extension as *dāi- / *deh₂y-), carrying the fundamental concrete meaning "to divide, apportion, share, cut up, or distribute into portions." This primordial conceptualization of division gave birth across Indo-European daughter languages to words defining both physical apportionments and temporal segments. In Greek, it produced daiesthai (δαίεσθαι, "to divide, distribute") and dēmos (δῆμος, "division of people, district, populace"); in Sanskrit, it yielded dāti (दाति, "cuts, divides") and dayate ("allots, shares"). In Proto-Germanic, this root formed the feminine abstract noun *tīdiz ("a division of time, a distinct period, hour, season"), formed by appending the common Indo-European abstract dental suffix *-tis (Germanic *-diz) to the verbal base. Time was thus conceived not as an abstract metaphysical continuum, but as a sequence of apportioned, cut-off intervals—seasons for sowing, periods for harvesting, and daily shifts in the light.

2. Germanic Development and the Old English Temporal Matrix. In the Germanic branches, *tīdiz gave rise to Old Norse tíð ("time, season, hour, event"), Old Saxon tīd, Old High German zīt (Modern German Zeit, "time"), and Old Frisian tīd. In Anglo-Saxon England, Old English inherited the word as tīd (feminine, strong i-stem), which served as the primary, ubiquitous term for time, season, period, hour, festival, or anniversary (as preserved in religious calendar terms like Christmastide, Whitsuntide, and Eastertide). The modern marine phenomenon of the oceanic tide was originally the identical word, referring to the predictable, recurring "time" of the water's surge and ebb. Crucially, in Old English, tīd was intrinsically bound to the rhythm of natural and liturgical cycles: doing something in its tīd meant doing it in its ordained, God-given season.

3. Middle English Derivation and the Emergence of *Tīdi*. Around the early thirteenth century (first attested in texts such as the Ancrene Wisse and the early Middle English homilies, c. 1225), speakers suffixed the noun tid with the productive adjectival marker -iġ (Middle English -i / -y), producing the derived adjective tīdi (recorded in regional manuscripts as tidy, tydy, tyddy). At its moment of genesis, tidy possessed a strictly temporal and circumstantial signification: it meant "seasonable, timely, opportune, happening at a favorable moment, or appropriate to the hour." Just as an agricultural crop was *tidy* if it sprouted in accordance with the calendar, an action was *tidy* if performed at the ripe, advantageous juncture.

4. Late Medieval Semantic Drift: From Seasonable to Thriving. Throughout the fourteenth century, a subtle but profound semantic shift unfolded across Middle English vernacular literature. An individual, animal, or harvest that arrived "in good season" was naturally robust, thriving, and well-developed. Consequently, by the time of William Langland's Piers Plowman (c. 1380) and contemporary alliterative romances (such as William of Palerne), tidy frequently glossed as "brave, valiant, healthy, shapely, plump, well-favored, or capable":

  • Langland speaks of "a tidy man" to indicate an able, capable, upright, and competent fellow who discharges his obligations with vigor.
  • In veterinary and rural contexts, a "tidy beast" or "tidy heifer" denoted cattle that were well-fed, glossy-coated, and in prime physical condition.

5. Early Modern Shift to Orderliness, Method, and Domesticity. During the sixteenth century, as early capitalism, Protestant work ethics, and urban domestic culture flourished in Tudor England, the semantic center of gravity pivoted decisively. The notion of being "capable, thriving, and well-conditioned" became tightly fused with the spatial discipline of the household. A well-run household required systematic storage, cleanliness, and the timely execution of chores. By the mid-1500s, tidy was increasingly contrasted with *slovenly*, *untidy*, and *slatternly*. Shakespeare and his contemporaries used the term to denote neatness in apparel and domestic order (e.g., in Henry IV, Part 2, Falstaff remarks upon a "tidy little Bartholomew boar-pig", hovering delightfully between the older sense of "plump/well-conditioned" and the newer sense of "neat/trim"). By 1700, the verb to tidy ("to set in order") had arisen from the adjective, cementing its status as an active principle of physical tidiness.

6. Eighteenth-Century Commercial Expansion and Quantitative Understatement. In the mid-eighteenth century, British commercial expansion, banking, and rural estate leasing generated a secondary, highly productive colloquial usage: *a tidy sum*, *a tidy fortune*, or *a tidy bit of land*. This sense was an offshoot of the older "well-conditioned, plump, satisfactory" meaning. A merchant receiving a "tidy" dividend was expressing polite British understatement: the sum was neither inadequate nor ostentatiously vulgar, but solid, comfortable, and thoroughly pleasing. This colloquial idiom rapidly spread across dialectal British speech (as captured in Tolkien’s rustic Westfarthing dialogue, where hobbits discuss "a tidy bit of money tucked away") and remains a fixture of standard English idiom today.
