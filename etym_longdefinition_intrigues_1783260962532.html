intrigues (n., plural of intrigue) — In Macaulay's History of England (1848–61), the word anchors a deliberate antithesis: he declares that he would "very imperfectly execute the task" he has undertaken if he "were merely to treat of battles and sieges, of the rise and fall of administrations, of intrigues in the palace, and of debates in the parliament." The plural gathers an entire opaque texture of courtly life — whisper, private audience, intercepted letter, backstairs conference — into a single noun that stands for all covert manipulation conducted within and around a court. Core sense. An intrigue is a secret scheme, a plot hatched behind closed doors and advanced through oblique means: flattery, rumour, misinformation, influence, bribery, and whispered alliance. Its defining features are secrecy, indirection, and the pursuit of power or advantage by non-transparent means. The plural intrigues, as Macaulay uses it, denotes not a single conspiracy but the whole habitual background hum of factional manoeuvring — the normal, taken-for-granted machinery of palace politics. All primary senses.
  • (1) Secret scheme / plot (count noun). The oldest English sense (from c. 1611): a secret and often crafty or underhanded scheme, especially a political one. "Palace intrigues," "court intrigues," "the intrigues of the cabinet."
  • (2) Secret love affair (count noun). From the late 17th century, intrigue also meant a clandestine amorous liaison — an affair conducted discreetly. "A man of gallantry, full of amorous intrigues." This is the sense behind the French intrigue amoureuse.
  • (3) Uncountable: the general texture of scheming. As a mass noun, intrigue denotes plotting and scheming in general — "a novel full of intrigue," "the boardroom is rife with intrigue."
  • (4) Uncountable: intense curiosity or fascination (modern sense). By the mid-20th century the noun acquired a psychological sense: a state of aroused, fascinated interest. "The mystery held great intrigue for her." This is a re-coining on the model of the modern verb.
  • (5) Verb: to excite curiosity (modern). "What intrigues me is how it happened." Today the verb almost always means "to fascinate, to capture attention," a sense that grew out of the older "to puzzle, perplex."
  • (6) Verb, archaic: to entangle, perplex, complicate. The earliest verb sense (c. 1609), directly from Latin intricare "to entangle": to ensnare, to puzzle. Now obsolete except in deliberate archaism.
  • (7) Derived nouns. Intriguer (one who schemes), intrigante (a scheming woman), intrigant (a schemer).
Modern jargon and metaphoric shifts.
  • Political journalism. "Intrigue" is stock vocabulary for reporting on shadowy power struggles: "intrigues of the Kremlin," "internal party intrigue." It frames normal politics as covert theatre.
  • Espionage and thrillers. The espionage genre trades in "spy intrigue"; a plot thick with mystery and double-crossing is "full of intrigue."
  • Literary and dramatic criticism. In French-derived critical vocabulary, intrigue is used for the plot or storyline of a dramatic work — the "intrigue" of a play — a survival of the Restoration "intrigue plot."
  • Psychological and consumer registers. The noun-as-fascination is applied to books, films, puzzles, and products ("an object of great intrigue").
  • Romance fiction. The old amorous sense survives in historical romance and costume drama ("a tale of passion and intrigue").
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. All the modern senses trace back to one ancient image. Latin intricare meant literally "to tangle up" — to tie something into knots (from in- "into" + tricae "entanglements, tricks"). Italian intrigare then did double duty: it kept "to tangle" and added "to meddle, to embroil oneself," whence "to plot." When the word reached English through French in the early 1600s it carried both strands: a scheme is a "tangle" of plots, and a person caught up in one is "entangled" in affairs. The modern psychological sense grew later: a story that "intrigues" us is one that winds round the mind like a thread, entangling our attention. The semantic thread from physical twisting to mental captivation is unbroken. Usage notes.
  • Usually pejorative or at least morally suspicious: intrigues are rarely honest dealings.
  • Register: formal-to-literary; slightly dated as a live political word, but very much alive in journalism, fiction, and marketing language.
  • Distinguish from plot (a concrete plan) and conspiracy (an unlawful group enterprise) — see similarWords.
  • Collocations: "court intrigue," "palace intrigue," "political intrigue," "romantic intrigue," "a web of intrigue," "rife with intrigue."
