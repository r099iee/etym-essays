MANIFEST (adjective, noun, and verb) and VATS (plural noun) are two words of almost opposite semantic worlds, yoked together in Jack Vance's prose into a single uncanny image: a creature of manifest imperfection that is nevertheless the most successful product of Turjan's vats. The first word belongs to the vocabulary of perception and evidence — what is plainly, undeniably before the eye; the second belongs to the vocabulary of manufacture — the great industrial containers in which raw matter is mixed, brewed, and transformed into something new. To master this card one must trace each word's literal core and then follow its long chain of figurative, legal, technical, and even ideological extensions. SECTION 1 — MANIFEST AS AN ADJECTIVE The primary, and historically oldest, function of manifest in English is as an adjective meaning "clearly visible to the eye or obvious to the mind; plainly recognizable; impossible to doubt or dispute." Something manifest does not require argument, inference, or testimony to be believed — it announces itself. A manifest error is an error visible on its face; manifest danger is danger that no honest person can deny; a manifest injustice is a wrong apparent without any investigation. In the Vance sentence, the adjective performs exactly this forensic work: the creature's imperfection is not a matter of subtle judgment but of open display — anyone who looked could see it.
  • Etymological core: manifest descends from Latin manifestus (earlier manufestus), literally "seized by the hand" — manus "hand" + -festus, an element of debated origin connected with the idea of grasping or striking. What is manifest is so clear that one could seize it, as it were, with the bare hand: palpable, tangible, caught red-handed. The root idea is physical touch converted into intellectual certainty.
  • Legal register: in law, manifest appears in fixed phrases — manifest error, manifest injustice, manifest intent, manifest necessity — meaning an error or intent so obvious that no reasonable observer could dispute it. The word retains its ancient courtroom charge of evidence "seized in the act."
  • Manifest as a verb: to manifest means to show plainly, to make evident or apparent, to display or reveal a quality that was present but hidden. "The illness manifested itself in a fever." From the 16th century the verb extends the adjective's idea of open display into the act of displaying.
  • Manifest as a noun: a manifest is the document listing the cargo or passengers carried by a ship, aircraft, or vehicle — a public statement of what lies open to inspection. This noun sense (17th century, via Italian manifesto) transfers "open display" from perception to paperwork: the manifest makes the vessel's contents plain to port authorities.
  • Manifest Destiny: in 1845 the phrase Manifest Destiny was coined to describe the doctrine that American westward expansion was a plainly apparent, self-justifying national purpose — the root sense of "self-evident" applied to a whole ideology of empire and conquest.
  • Modern self-help sense: in contemporary New Age and motivational culture, manifest has developed a reflexive verb use — "to manifest your dreams" — meaning to bring something into visible reality through will and visualization. This is a striking semantic shift: from "seized by the hand" (passive certainty) to "summoned into existence by intention" (active creation). The bridge is the idea that the hidden becomes open — first by perception, now by projection.
SECTION 2 — VATS AS A NOUN The plural noun vats (singular vat) means large open-topped containers, usually of wood, metal, or stone, used to hold or process liquids or moist substances: brewing vats, dyeing vats, tanning vats, fermentation vats. In the Vance passage the word carries a specifically sinister, quasi-industrial charge — Turjan's vats are the tanks in which he mixes the raw material of artificial life. The word is ancient, Germanic, and working-class in origin, and it has never left the workshop.
  • Etymology: vat descends from Old English fæt, "a vessel, container, jar, cup," a word of Germanic ancestry (Old Norse fat, Middle Dutch vat, Old High German faz, all "vessel"), with no secure Indo-European derivation — its ultimate source is unknown. The spelling with initial v- is the product of a southern English sound change by which initial f- became v- in certain dialectal speech (as in vane from fane, vixen from fixen).
  • Industrial and domestic senses: vats have held beer, wine, dyes, hides, and soap; the vat is the container of transformation, the place where raw material steeps, ferments, and becomes product. A vat-man was historically the papermaker who scooped pulp in a vat; vat-dyeing immerses cloth in great dyestuff vats.
  • Metaphoric sense: from the physical container, vat extends by metaphor to mean a large quantity of something — "a vat of coffee," "vat-grown tissue" — and in modern biotechnology "vat-grown" or "vat-meat" refers to cultured meat raised in bioreactor tanks. The image of the vat as the vessel of mass production persists into the twenty-first century.
  • Compound and idiomatic uses: vat appears in compounds such as vat-dye, vat-house, vat-lined; the idiom "a vat of" simply means a huge, undifferentiated quantity, carrying a faint flavor of industrial anonymity.
SECTION 3 — THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE BETWEEN THE TWO WORDS The pairing on this card is not accidental. Manifest imperfection describes what the eye sees — an obvious, undeniable flaw — while vats names where that flawed thing was made. Perception and production are joined: the manifest is what lies open to sight; the vat is the closed vessel in which the visible thing was generated. Together the two words give the Vance scene its double movement of revelation and concealment — the imperfection is fully manifest, but the machinery that produced it remains hidden in the vats. The card therefore teaches not just two words but a dialectic: the obvious on display, and the obscure industry behind it.
