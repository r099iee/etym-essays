In the sentence on Gibbon, “pessimism” does not describe a private mood but a collective intellectual posture — a growing, almost fashionable conviction that Western civilization is heading toward terminal decay. The phrase “increasingly fashionable” is decisive and faintly ironic: the essayist observes that gloom about the future of Western society has itself become a vogue among readers and thinkers, and that, precisely because this cultural pessimism now prevails, Gibbon’s great narrative of Roman decline has been turned into “a handy guide to the sources of decay in other empires and civilizations.” The word therefore carries a tone of detached, almost anthropological amusement: pessimism is presented as a cultural weather-system rather than a personal affliction, and the Decline and Fall is recast as a diagnostic manual for societies that believe themselves to be rotting. The nuance is broad and systemic — the noun names a shared assumption about historical decline, not an individual’s low spirits — and it is this systemic sense that makes the sentence work: pessimism has become a lens through which the whole course of history is read. As a dictionary word, “pessimism” is a noun with a layered family of meanings. In its original, philosophical sense it is a doctrine: the belief that this world is the worst possible of all worlds, or more broadly that evil outweighs good and that things are fundamentally getting worse — the position classically associated with Arthur Schopenhauer and with the metaphysical pessimism of the nineteenth century, argued as the formal antonym of Leibniz’s optimism. In its everyday, psychological sense it is a temperament: a tendency to see the worst aspect of things and to believe that the worst will happen — the habitual disposition of a person who looks for the failure in every hope. In its social and cultural senses it names a collective mood — the conviction that a nation, an era, or a civilization is in decline — and, in economics and finance, the expectation that markets and conditions will worsen. All these senses share one root idea: the worst is judged most likely, and the glass is judged half-empty. Polysemy — the full range of senses:
  • Philosophical doctrine: the metaphysical claim (Schopenhauer, Hartmann) that existence is suffering and that the world is the worst possible; the formal antonym of optimism in the Leibnizian “best of all possible worlds” controversy.
  • Psychological temperament: the personal disposition to expect the worst, to dwell on the negative, and to interpret events in their least favorable light — “her pessimism about the exam.”
  • Cultural and civilizational mood: the collective conviction that a society, empire, or era is in irreversible decline — the sense used in the Gibbon passage, where pessimism about Western civilization turns history into a handbook of decay.
  • Economic and financial jargon: the expectation that prices, markets, or conditions will fall or worsen — “investor pessimism,” “the market’s pessimism” — a technical mood of the trading floor.
  • Clinical and psychological register: persistent hopelessness and negative expectation associated with depression and anxiety disorders; and the related concept of “defensive pessimism,” a strategy of lowering expectations to manage anxiety.
  • Extended and colloquial uses: loosely, any gloomy outlook or readiness to anticipate failure — “stop the pessimism” — often applied half-jokingly to individuals or groups.
The boundary words sharpen the profile:
  • “Cynicism”: Similar because both expect the worst; differs because cynicism doubts the motives and sincerity of people and is chiefly about human selfishness, not about the course of history — a cynic may expect the worst while staying wryly amused.
  • “Fatalism”: Similar because both hold a grim view of what is coming; differs because fatalism is the belief that events are predetermined and unavoidable, counselling passive submission and making no negative judgment, whereas pessimism actively judges the worst to be the most probable.
  • “Defeatism”: Similar because both are negative outlooks; differs because defeatism is pessimism applied to action — the premature surrender before the battle — while pessimism is the general belief that the worst will happen.
  • “Despondency”: Similar because both involve low expectation; differs because despondency is an emotional state of dejection and loss of hope, while pessimism is a cognitive tendency — a way of judging likelihood — that may be held calmly and even cheerfully.
  • “Gloom”: Similar because both are dark outlooks; differs because gloom is an atmospheric mood of darkness and depression, while pessimism is an intellectual expectation about outcomes.
  • “Negativism”: Similar because both involve a negative orientation; differs because negativism is a general tendency to deny, oppose, or refuse — a stance of refusal — while pessimism is specifically the expectation of the worst.
  • “Nihilism”: Similar because both deny value or hope; differs because nihilism is a philosophical rejection of meaning, value, and truth altogether, while pessimism judges that the worst will happen within a world that still has meaning.
  • “Melancholy”: Similar because both are associated with sadness; differs because melancholy is a temperament or mood of sorrow, often with a gentle beauty, while pessimism is a judgment about what the future holds.
The word’s morphology is revealing: pessimism is built on Latin pessimus, “worst,” the superlative of malus, “bad” — the etymological opposite of optimismus, from optimus, “best.” To be a pessimist is thus, etymologically, to believe in the worst; to be an optimist, to believe in the best. The pair were coined together in the philosophical debates of the eighteenth century, and their Greek-style suffix -ism marks both as systematic doctrines — world-views, not merely moods. In the Gibbon passage the word keeps all this weight: the essayist treats pessimism as a doctrine that has become fashionable, a philosophy of history turned into a reading habit, and the sentence depends on our hearing both the intellectual gravity of the -ism and the ironic lightness of calling such gravity fashionable.
