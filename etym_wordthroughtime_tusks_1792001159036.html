- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) — Attested as tūsc or tux, designating the razor-sharp canine teeth of wild boars, wolves, and primeval beasts, evoking visceral animal terror and martial danger in Anglo-Saxon heroic poetry such as Beowulf.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) — Spelled tusk, tosk, or tousk; expanded beyond native wild boars to denote the imported curiosities of foreign fauna (elephants, walruses, and narwhals) featured in bestiaries, heraldry, and chivalric hunting lore.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — Adopted into architectural joinery as "tusk-tenon" to describe interlocking timber joints; employed poetically by Shakespeare and Milton as vivid emblems of rapacious tearing and predatory destruction.
- 18th–19th c. (Colonial & Industrial era) — Codified in zoological taxonomy and global imperial commerce, where tusks became both celebrated natural history specimens and a tragically lucrative commodity driving the international ivory trade across Africa and Asia.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains primary zoological and figurative meanings while emerging as a central focal point in global wildlife conservation treaties (CITES), anti-poaching forensics, and Pleistocene paleontology.
