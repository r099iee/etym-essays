• Waterfall: Similar because both name a steep descent of a river over rock; differs because waterfall is the generic singular noun and falls is the plural geographic form — "the falls" names a place, "a waterfall" names a phenomenon.
  • Cataract: Similar because both are waterfalls; differs because cataract denotes a large, powerful, often thundering fall (from Greek kataraktēs "down-rushing"), and doubles as a medical word for blindness — two unrelated senses in one form.
  • Cascade: Similar because both are falling water; differs because a cascade is smaller and broken into steps or stages, picturesque rather than sublime, from Italian cascata "waterfall".
  • Rapids: Similar because both are fast descending river water; differs because rapids are shallow, turbulent reaches over a rocky bed without a free drop, while falls plunge vertically; rapids can be run in boats, falls cannot.
  • Chute: Similar because both are steep channels of water; differs because a chute is a narrow shoot or flume (and, by extension, any slide), emphasizing the channel rather than the curtain of falling water.
  • Spout: Similar because both describe water issuing with force; differs because a spout is a jet or stream (a whale's spout, a waterspout), not a broad cascade over a ledge.
  • Torrent: Similar because both evoke rushing, violent water; differs because a torrent is a violent rushing stream or flow, horizontal rather than vertical, with no drop implied.
  • Weir: Similar because both are places where water falls over an obstruction; differs because a weir is a man-made low dam built to control or measure water, small and engineered, while falls are natural.
  • Dam: Similar because both hold and drop water; differs because a dam is entirely artificial storage, not a natural descent; the water falls over it, but the structure is the opposite of a geological cascade.
  • Flume: Similar because both are channels of running water; differs because a flume is an artificial chute or trough for conveying water (logging, milling), with no natural grandeur and no roar.
  • Fall (singular, "the fall of the river"): Similar because both are the same word; differs because the singular is the technical way of naming the gradient or a single drop, while the plural "falls" is the everyday name of the place — the two forms divide the labour of hydrology and common speech.
