- 1580s — the word appears as a bookish term for a work containing the substance of a larger one; scholarly and neutral.
- 17th c. — a favoured word in the titles of handbooks of theology, law, medicine and rhetoric; it carries the prestige of orderly completeness.
- 18th–19th c. — extends from books to collections of things and facts (“a compendium of anecdotes”); slightly antiquarian flavour.
- 20th c. — the British “compendium of games” makes the word domestic, while journalism uses it for compact surveys (“a compendium of errors”).
- 21st c. — digital compendiums of data and content; the word now signals curation as much as condensation, and is sometimes used ironically for a mere list.
