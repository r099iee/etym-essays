Two root elements must be traced separately before they combine in the name Prokópios. CHAIN 1 — element pro- "forward, before": 1. PIE particle/root *pro "before, in front, forward" (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE; cognate Sanskrit prá, Latin prō, Greek pró; sometimes analyzed as the e-grade form of *per- "forward, through"). 2. Ancient Greek pró (πρό) "before, forward, in front of" (attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE) — as a verbal prefix in pro- compounds. 3. Greek prokóptein "to cut forward, to advance, to make progress" = pró + kóptein (attested in Classical prose, 5th–4th c. BCE, e.g., Xenophon). CHAIN 2 — element kopē "a cutting, a blow": 1. The ultimate origin of Greek kóptō "to cut, strike, beat, knock" is uncertain; no secure Indo-European etymon is established (Beekes treats it without a clear PIE derivation; possibly Pre-Greek). Stated explicitly: this stage cannot be traced further with certainty. 2. Ancient Greek kóptō "to cut, strike, beat, knock" (attested from Homer onward, 8th c. BCE). 3. Ancient Greek kopḗ (κοπή) "a cutting, a blow, a stroke" (attested in Classical Greek, 5th c. BCE), from kóptō. MERGE: 4. Ancient Greek prokopḗ (προκοπή) "progress, advancement, success" = pro- "forward" + kopḗ "a cutting," literally "a cutting forward" (attested in Classical and Hellenistic Greek, 5th–3rd c. BCE). 5. Ancient Greek Prokópios (Προκόπιος) "he who progresses, the thriving one" — a personal name (also an epithet), borne by the historian of Caesarea (c. 490–507 CE), by the 4th-c. usurper, by Procopius of Gaza, and by several saints. 6. Latin Procopius — the Latinized (transliterated) form used in Latin historiography and church tradition from late antiquity. 7. PROCOPIUS — English form, used from the 16th c. onward in translations and histories (e.g., Gibbon's Decline and Fall). ◄ TARGET Debated/nuanced note: the compound analysis pro- + kopē is secure and the name's sense "progressive, thriving" is well established; however, the etymology of the simplex kóptō/kopē is unknown or uncertain (possibly Pre-Greek), so the chain for element 2 cannot be extended below the attested Greek verb without guesswork.
