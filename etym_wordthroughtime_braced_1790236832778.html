- c. 1380–1500 - Clasping and mechanical fastening: In Middle English, braced denoted the physical act of clasping in one's arms, strapping armor tightly to the limbs, or binding items with ropes and thongs.
- c. 1500–1650 - Structural framing and archery tension: Expanded during the Tudor and Elizabethan eras into architecture, carpentry, and weaponry, describing timber walls reinforced with diagonal struts and longbows strung under heavy tension.
- c. 1650–1800 - Nautical maneuvering and bodily posture: Emerged in maritime speech for yards squared against the wind and in military drilling for planting feet and locking muscles against recoil or cavalry charges.
- c. 1800–1900 - Psychological fortitude and climatic vigor: Shifted metaphorically during the 19th century into emotional steadfastness ("bracing oneself for bad news") and the invigorating effect of cold sea air ("bracing weather").
- c. 1910–1940 - Edwardian and Interwar British slang: Popularized by P.G. Wodehouse and upper-class youth as an adjective for feeling cheerful, encouraged, heartened, or mentally game ("not feeling any too braced").
- c. 1950–Present - Engineering, medicine, and aviation safety: Dominated modern usage by orthopedic supports, structural earthquake engineering, computing/syntax grouping, and the universal aviation "brace position" for emergency landings.
