EVOLUTION OF FAMISHED: FROM FAMINE TO THE COMIC CRY OF A MISSED DINNER Famished is a word whose modern lightness sits on top of an ancient severity. Everything in its history begins with the Latin noun fames, 'hunger' - the word that also lies behind English famine and, in the Romance languages, the words for hunger and starvation. Fames itself is a word of unknown origin: modern scholarship, including de Vaan's etymological dictionary of Latin, records no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon, and no credible set of cognates in the other Indo-European languages has been established. The word simply appears in the earliest Latin as the plain name of hunger, the most physical and universal of human needs, and its lack of a reconstructable root is not unusual for the most basic vocabulary of a language. What matters for English is that from fames Latin built a family of verbs and adjectives, including the denominative famare, 'to make hungry', and its compound affamare (with the prefix ad-), 'to bring to hunger, to starve'. It is this compound that travelled into the Romance languages and gave Old French afamer, 'to starve'. The entry into English (c. 1400) Middle English took the word up in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries in two related forms: famen and famisshen. The -issh- forms continue the Old French inchoative stem in -iss-, the same element that appears in perish and nourish, and they give the English verb its distinctive shape: famish. In its first English life the verb was literal and deadly. It meant to die of hunger and to cause to die of hunger - the language of famine, siege, and mass want - and it appears in the medieval chronicles with no softness about it. To famish was to starve, and to be famished was to be reduced to starvation, the state of a body emptied and wasted by want. The hardening of the participle (16th-17th centuries) From the sixteenth century the past participle famished hardened into an independent adjective meaning 'reduced to starvation'. This is the sense in which the word entered the great literary tradition of the early modern period: it is the word of romance and epic, where hunger is a force of nature that hollows a man out, and where famished heroes and famished armies stagger through wildernesses and besieged cities. The register was grave, formal, and dramatic - the language of high seriousness - and the word carried the full severity of its Latin ancestor fames. It was in this form that the word reached the Romantic and Victorian writers of adventure, who kept it for moments of genuine extremity. The relaxation into hyperbole (18th-19th centuries) From the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word began to cool toward exaggeration. Used of a missed dinner rather than a real famine, famished became a lively domestic hyperbole: 'I'm famished!' became the ordinary exclamation of a hungry person before a meal, and the word entered the comic and affectionate register of everyday speech. The verb itself retreated into archaism - modern English no longer says 'he famished' to mean 'he starved to death' - but the participle lived on, running on two poles at once: genuinely extreme in serious literary prose, and jokey before dinner in everyday speech. The modern double life In modern English, famished keeps both lives. The comic sense dominates ordinary conversation, where 'I'm absolutely famished' is the standard cry of a hungry person, lightly and affectionately exaggerated. But the archaic gravity never quite dies: the word remains available for genuine extremity, and a writer can sound either pole, or both at once, with the same participle. This is precisely what Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit. When Gandalf tells the Eagles 'we are famished with hunger', the word is fully serious - the company has had next to nothing to eat for three dreadful days, and the archaic formality of famished dignifies their plea. When Bilbo adds 'I am nearly dead of it' in a weak little voice that nobody hears, the extremity becomes pathos. The word that began as the plain Latin name of hunger, passed through the famine-language of medieval chronicles and the high seriousness of epic, and finally relaxed into a comic cry, is here returned to its oldest and most literal strength - the hunger of bodies that are genuinely near the end of what they can bear.
