- 98 CE — Tacitus lists the Anglii among the tribes of the lower Elbe; to a Roman reader they are a remote, minor people associated with the cult of Nerthus.
- 2nd c. CE — Ptolemy’s Angloi place them in the same north-German region; the name is still a geographical and ethnographic label, not a political identity.
- 5th–6th c. — Angles, Saxons and Jutes settle Britain; the Angles give their name to the resulting kingdoms and peoples, though the process is gradual and mixed.
- 7th–9th c. — Old English Engle and Englisc become the common self-description; Bede’s gens Anglorum gives the name a providential, Christian meaning.
- 10th–11th c. — Englaland and rex Anglorum turn the tribal name into a kingdom; after 1066 the Norman chancery keeps Angli for the conquered English.
- 16th–19th c. — Anglo-Saxon is revived as a historical and then racial category; “Angles” becomes a stock item in narratives of English origins and Teutonic liberty.
- 20th–21st c. — Historians and archaeologists treat the “Angles” as a mixed, shifting label; the word survives mainly in academic writing and in the fixed triad “Angles, Saxons and Jutes.”
