- Late 13th c. – legal and physical constraint: “distress” names seizure, siege, and hardship; the perception is of external compulsion rather than inner feeling.
- 14th–15th c. – inward turn: affliction of mind and spirit; religious writers make it a condition of the soul.
- 16th–17th c. – charity and law split: “the distressed” become objects of alms, while “distress” becomes a property remedy.
- 18th–19th c. – sentimental philanthropy: “distressed gentlefolk” softens poverty into pity, giving the word a genteel, slightly patronising warmth.
- Early 20th c. – clinical and financial: distress becomes measurable in psychology and tradeable in finance.
- Late 20th–21st c. – fashion inversion: “distressed” denim and furniture turn wear into desirable authenticity.
- Contemporary – omnibus adjective: from mental health to markets to jeans, unified by the image of pressure.
