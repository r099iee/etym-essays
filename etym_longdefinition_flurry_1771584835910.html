Flurry denotes a sudden, brief, and confused rush — of wind, of snow, of motion, or of activity. In the scene in which it appears, "a flurry of footsteps could be heard outside," the word does precise sensory work: it names a sound that is not a single set of steps but a scattered, overlapping patter of several people hurrying at once. The word converts footsteps into weather — a squall, a gust made of small movements — and the phrase "flurry of footsteps" tells the reader that the household has suddenly become agitated, servants bustling to announce the arrival of Bao-yu. The contrast with the preceding calm conversation of the grandmother and Dai-yu is the whole point: the elders' talk is measured and quiet, and the flurry is its opposite — a brief storm of motion that breaks over the quiet room and announces the disruptive vitality of the family's favourite child. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES Flurry is a polysemous word whose senses all radiate from the shared idea of "a short, restless, many-parted burst". The principal senses are:
  • Meteorological sense (fixed in American English by the early 19th century): a sudden, brief squall of wind, or a brief fall of snow driven by such a gust — "a flurry of snow" is a small, passing snowfall, not a blizzard; it comes and goes within minutes. In weather reporting, "flurries" are distinguished from snow showers and blizzards by their brevity and light accumulation.
  • Human-social sense (the sense in the card's context): a sudden, brief period of excitement, commotion, or agitated activity — "a flurry of footsteps", "a flurry of activity", "a flurry of excitement". Here the word compresses a crowd of small actions into a single storm-image: many people or many small events happening at once and then subsiding.
  • Extended figurative senses (productive in journalism and business): any short, intense burst of a countable or measurable phenomenon — "a flurry of emails", "a flurry of phone calls", "a flurry of deals", "a flurry of activity on the stock exchange". The word foregrounds multiplicity (many items in a short time) and volatility (the burst is brief and then stops), and it is used constantly of markets, diplomacy, and news cycles.
  • Verbal and participial senses: "to flurry" means to move in a flurried, hurried, confused way, or to agitate someone (chiefly passive: "to be flurried"); the participle "flurried" means agitated, hurried, scattered in manner. The verb is rarer than the noun but shares the same core image of brief, disordered motion.
  • Historical and dialectal senses: in English dialect the family around "flurr" meant "to scatter, sprinkle, throw with the hand" (flurrying grain, flurrying dust); the noun also once meant "a short burst of anger or a sudden fright" (17th–18th century), a sense now obsolete.
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The historical bridge between the senses is straightforward and well attested. The word seems to have begun in expressive, dialectal roots meaning "to fling or scatter" (English dialect "flurr"), so the earliest noun sense, attested in 1620, is "a great hurry, a commotion" — a burst of many scattered, hurried movements. From "a commotion of people" the word extended outward along two channels at once: toward weather (a sudden gust that scatters snowflakes and leaves, fixed in American usage by 1828) and toward emotion (a sudden fluster, a fit of confusion or anger). Because all the senses share the image of many small things driven briefly into restless motion — snowflakes, footsteps, phone calls, hearts — the word has been able to move freely between meteorology and human affairs ever since. SYNONYM BOUNDARIES AND REGISTER The synonym field around "flurry" is tight, and the boundaries matter. "Commotion" is a general noisy disturbance, usually longer-lasting and without the lightness or brevity of a flurry. "Bustle" suggests purposeful, busy activity and is usually sustained. "Squall" and "gust" are strictly meteorological and name a single rush of wind, not many small particles. "Frenzy" is far more intense, uncontrolled, and often violent. "Flutter" names the tremulous motion of a single light thing, whereas a flurry is many things at once. Register-wise, "flurry" is standard, fully current English, at home in weather forecasts, newspapers, novels, and casual speech; it is neutral in tone, neither formal nor slangy, and its liveliness comes from its weather-image, which gives a human commotion the flickering, transient quality of driven snow. In the passage from Dream of the Red Chamber the word therefore does two jobs at once: it describes the sound objectively, and it translates the household's sudden agitation into a small, beautiful storm.
