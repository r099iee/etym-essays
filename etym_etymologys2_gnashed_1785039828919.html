Earliest stage: PIE sound-symbolic base *ghen- / *ghn- "to rub, scrape, grind" (reconstructed; source of the gn- word-family: cf. Old English gnidan "to rub, bruise, pound, break to pieces," Old Norse gnīða "to rub, grind, wear away," Greek khnīein "to scratch, scrape") → Germanic imitative extension *gnast- / *gnest- "sharp grinding contact of teeth" → Old Norse gnasta / gnista "to gnash the teeth; to gnash, groan" [probable donor to English] → Middle English gnasten "to grind the teeth together in rage, sorrow, or menace" (early 14th c.) → Early Modern English gnash (variant form that displaces gnasten) (early 15th c.) → Modern English gnash "to grind or clash the teeth in fury" → GNASHED (past tense and past participle) Debated / nuanced note: The deeper PIE ancestry is reconstructed as sound-symbolic rather than as a normal lexical root: the gn- cluster itself is imitative of grinding. The Old Norse origin is probable rather than certain — the verb may have been reinforced by native Old English material, and the earliest English attestations are from northern dialects, which is consistent with Scandinavian influence. Danish knaske "to crush with the teeth" and German knistern "to crackle" belong to the same imitative family.
