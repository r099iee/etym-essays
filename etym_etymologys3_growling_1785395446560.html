Growl belongs to a tight Germanic cluster of sound-words built on the imitative base *gr-, which captures a low, rolling, throaty rumble; the same base produced the whole English family of groan, grunt, grumble, and growl, and on the continent the German grollen. None of these can be traced with confidence to a Proto-Indo-European ancestor: they are, as the OED observes, words 'formed naturally to express a deep guttural sound', and linguists treat the family as beginning the moment a speaker tried to transcribe a noise with the very organs that make it. This is why the etymology is so short and why every member of the family sounds like every other: the sounds themselves were the model, and the words were reinvented, in closely similar shapes, across the Germanic dialects without any single written 'parent' word being required. THE FAMILY SPLITS: GROAN AND GRUNT Old English already shows the pattern splitting into two voices. The long, mournful form grānian produced English groan, the deep sound of pain and lamentation; its Middle Low German and Middle Dutch siblings, grēnen and greinen, meant to weep, to grumble, and to complain, so the whole North Sea branch heard the base as grief. The short, percussive form grunnian produced English grunt, the abrupt, closed-throat expulsion of pigs and of straining men; its cognates run through the whole Germanic world — German grunzen, Middle High German grunzen, Danish grynte, Swedish grymta, Norwegian grynte. From the very start, then, the family had two voices: the long mournful groan and the short percussive grunt, both built on the same guttural base, and both still alive in modern English. THE MIDDLE STRIDES: GRUMBLE AND GROLLEN Middle Dutch grommelen, 'to rumble, to murmur, to mutter', passed into English as grumble, a word of discontent and complaint that survives in a grumbling stomach and a grumbling appendix; its sister grommen remains the ordinary Dutch verb for an animal's growl, and Dutch grom is the growl itself. On the continent German grollen developed in a striking double direction: it means both 'to rumble' (of thunder, of the cannon) and 'to bear anger, to harbour resentment', and the noun Groll means a grudge or rancour — for the German ear the deep sound and the deep feeling were the same thing, the voice of the earth and the voice of the offended heart fused in one word. This continental 'heart-word' is the clearest evidence of how a noise-word could grow an emotional register without leaving the sound behind. GROWL ITSELF ARRIVES IN ENGLISH English growl appears in the 15th century as groulen, and its first recorded uses are not animal at all: it is recorded of thunder and of the belly before it is recorded of dogs, and only in the 16th century does it attach to the threatening throat-sound of dogs and wolves. From that point the word narrowed toward the menacing canine growl that dominates modern use, and it bred a small family of its own — growler (a grumbling person, a four-wheeled cab, a jug of beer, a small iceberg), growly, growling, and the derived noun growl. The narrowing is a textbook case of a generic rumble-word specialising to one dramatic, biologically salient animal sound, and then, remarkably, re-broadening outward again in the modern period to engines, amplifiers, and sub-bass. THE PARTICIPLE AND ITS LITERARY LIFE The participial form growling, which Tolkien chose in The Hobbit, is the continuous present of the verb, formed with the Germanic -ing suffix, and it converts the transient noise into a sustained condition — not a growl but growling, unbroken, occupying time like weather. That is exactly what the passage needs: the Wargs' growling is present throughout the Company's ordeal, the constant low threat beneath the sharper yelps, and Gandalf, 'wizard though he was', reads in that continuity a menace that posture and bark would not convey. The literary history of the word runs from 15th-century descriptions of thunder and rumbling bellies, through 16th-century dog-lore, to the romantic and modern soundscapes of engines and distorted guitars; at every stage the meaning has been the same deep, continuous, barely contained vibration, and the word has remained, as it began, a sound that the throat understands before the dictionary does. KEY ATTESTATIONS AND TURNING POINTS
  • c. 1420 — earliest English record of groulen, of thunder and of a rumbling belly.
  • 16th century — the word attaches to the threatening throat-sound of dogs and wolves; the modern core sense is born.
  • 17th–19th century — figurative human use: 'to growl an answer', the surly hostile voice; the animal colouring colours the human one.
  • 20th century — extension to engines, machinery, and low music; the growl becomes the signature of contained power in industrial and electronic sound.
  • Modern — the participle growling is used adjectivally of anything that sustains the menace (a growling dog, a growling stomach, growling storm-clouds).
