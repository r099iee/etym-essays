The history of surmises tracks a radical change of register: from the formal accusations of medieval law courts to the delicate, ironic guesswork of the nineteenth-century novel.

Stage one — the PIE root of sending

The word's backbone is the Proto-Indo-European root *meit- (with variants), meaning "to exchange, send, cast". It is the source of Latin mittere "to send, let go, cast, put" — one of the most productive verbs in the classical lexicon. English inherits from mittere a vast family: mission (a sending), missive, missile (a thing sent), transmit, submit, commit, permit, promise (a sending forth of words), premise, demise, and dismiss. The root's semantic core — something cast or sent from one place or person to another — will prove essential to the word's meaning.

Stage two — Latin mittere and supermittere

In classical Latin, the prefix super- ("above, over, upon") could be attached to mittere to form supermittere "to send over, lay upon". The image is of casting a thing downward onto a surface — a garment thrown over a couch, a charge thrown upon a person.

Stage three — Old French surmettre and the legal sense

In Gallo-Romance and Old French, mittere evolved into mettre "to put, place" (the familiar modern French verb of placing), and sur- (from super) combined with it to form surmettre "to lay upon, accuse, allege". Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman legal usage carried the verb and its noun into English administration. The feminine past participle surmise became a substantive noun meaning "a charge, accusation, or allegation formally laid upon a defendant" — an unproven claim submitted to the court before witness testimony. In fourteenth-century Middle English, surmise retained exactly this judicial weight; Chaucer and contemporary legal records use it of accusations and suspicions.

Stage four — the softening from accusation to guess

During the Elizabethan period and the seventeenth century, as English evidence law tightened its definitions, the courtroom sense of surmise receded from common speech. The word was left with its "laying upon" image but no longer bound to criminal process: a surmise became anything mentally "laid upon" a matter without proof — a suspicion, a conjecture, a hunch. Eighteenth-century essayists such as Joseph Addison and Richard Steele popularized the gentler sense in their periodicals, and by the nineteenth century surmise was a standard, slightly elegant word for cautious speculation.

Stage five — Austen and the literary register

Jane Austen uses surmises with characteristic irony in Pride and Prejudice (1813): the Bennet women press Mr. Bennet with "barefaced questions, ingenious suppositions, and distant surmises". The collocation "distant surmises" is perfect Austen — the guesses are timidly held at arm's length, offered without the boldness of questions or the ingenuity of suppositions, and all three are evaded by the father's playful skill. The word's old legal charge has evaporated entirely; what remains is polite, gendered, ironic conjecture.

Modern state

  • Today surmise is literary and slightly dated, preferred in prose for its precision and tonal refinement over the blunter "guess".
  • In technical and scientific prose it has largely been displaced by "conjecture" (mathematics) and "hypothesis" (science), though "surmise" survives as a synonym for low-confidence inference.
  • The plural surmises and the third-person verb surmises are identical forms, reflecting the noun–verb pair created in Middle English (the verb back-formed from the noun).

Debate note

The PIE root behind Latin mittere is traditionally reconstructed as *meit- "to exchange, send", but the etymology is not fully certain; the word's core Italic meaning "to send, let go" is nonetheless firmly attested from the earliest Latin texts.
