• Stiff: Similar because both describe an unnatural lack of ease in movement, manner, or style; differs because stiff can be neutral (a stiff back, stiff cardboard) and can mean simply "rigid," while stilted always implies affected, artificial elevation — walking on stilts rather than mere stiffness.
  • Formal: Similar because both describe a controlled, ceremonious, non-casual register; differs because formal can be a positive, appropriate choice (a formal letter, formal dress), while stilted is a criticism of formality carried to stiffness and affectation.
  • Wooden: Similar because both describe stiff, expressionless, unanimated behaviour or writing; differs because wooden stresses lifelessness and absence of expression, while stilted stresses over-elevation and pompous formality.
  • Pompous: Similar because both describe grand, inflated, self-important language; differs because pompous adds an element of arrogant self-importance and show, while stilted focuses on the mechanical stiffness of the delivery.
  • Bombastic: Similar because both describe inflated, high-flown language; differs because bombastic is loudly pretentious and empty, whereas stilted need not be loud — it can be quiet but awkwardly stiff.
  • Artificial: Similar because both describe something not natural or genuine; differs because artificial is very broad (fake flowers, artificial light), while stilted is specific to a raised, wooden, self-conscious manner.
  • Affected: Similar because both describe behaviour put on for show rather than natural; differs because affected implies deliberate pretence and studied mannerism, while stilted may be unintentional — the stiffness may come from nervousness, not vanity.
  • Awkward: Similar because both describe a lack of ease and grace; differs because awkward is clumsy or embarrassing in any situation, while stilted is specifically the stiffness of something raised and propped up, especially in language.
  • Ungainly: Similar because both describe clumsy, ungraceful movement; differs because ungainly is purely physical and describes lack of grace in the body, while stilted applies chiefly to style, speech, and manner, with physical clumsiness as one of its uses.
  • Laboured: Similar because both describe speech or style that lacks ease and flows with effort; differs because laboured stresses the visible effort and heaviness of the production, while stilted stresses its unnatural elevation and stiffness.
  • Self-conscious: Similar because both describe behaviour that is awkwardly aware of itself; differs because self-conscious names the psychological state of nervous self-awareness, while stilted names the resulting stiff, elevated manner or style.
  • High-flown: Similar because both describe language raised artificially above ordinary speech; differs because high-flown emphasises grandiose pretension in the words themselves, while stilted emphasises the stiff, wooden delivery of even simple ideas.
  • Starchy: Similar because both describe excessively stiff and formal behaviour; differs because starchy is a homely, colloquial image of rigid propriety, while stilted keeps the image of walking on artificial poles and can apply to writing, not just behaviour.
  • Grandiloquent: Similar because both describe pompously elevated language; differs because grandiloquent is florid and wordy in a showy way, while stilted is more often simply stiff, plain-but-awkward, and lacking natural flow.
