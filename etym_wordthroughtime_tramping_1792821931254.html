- 14th c. — the heavy tread: trampen first means to tread or stamp heavily, the sound of work boots on earth made into a verb.
- 15th–16th c. — the road life: tramping men walk the country seeking labour, and the verb absorbs vagrancy, pedlary, and the whole economy of the foot-traveller.
- 17th–18th c. — armies and mails: soldiers tramp the roads of every European war while carriers and postmen tramp their rounds; the participle becomes the sound of duty done on foot.
- 19th c. — tramps and steamers: the noun tramp hardens into the homeless wanderer of industrial England, while tramp steamers carry the wandering sense to sea, sailing wherever cargo calls.
- Early 20th c. — leisure discovers the boot: rambling clubs and scouts reclaim tramping as healthy pleasure, and Tolkien's generation walks for joy with packs on their backs.
- Late 20th c. — New Zealand canonises it: tramping becomes the official national word for hiking, with clubs, huts, and Great Walks, wholly respectable and beloved.
- Today — boots versus screens: tramping survives as the muddy, companionable opposite of the sedentary life — Frodo's daylight rambles reborn every weekend on every continent.
