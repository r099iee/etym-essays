The word banquet tells a Cinderella story in miniature: it began as a humble "little bench" in medieval Italy and rose, over four centuries, to name the grandest feasts of courts and states — and in the process made one of the most dramatic semantic climbs in the history of English. Its journey runs from the carpenter's bench, through a moneylender's counter, past a between-courses snack, to the chandeliered banquet hall of the modern hotel.

The Germanic bench

At the base of the family lies a Germanic word for a bench or raised platform: Proto-Germanic *bankiz (the source of English bench and bank in the sense "raised mound"), whose own Indo-European origin is uncertain and possibly connected with a root meaning "to bend", for a bent slab of wood. The Franks carried the word south: their *bank, "bench", passed into Old French and Old Italian, where it became banca/banco, "bench, table". In thirteenth-century Italian cities the moneylender's bench (banco) was so central that it gave us banca "bank" — the financial institution — and bancarotta "bankruptcy", the "broken bench" of the failed money-changer. The bench, in short, became the bank; and the same bench, through the banquet, became the feast.

The little bench and the snack

Italian formed the diminutive banchetto, "little bench". In fourteenth- and fifteenth-century Italy — the world of the humanist courts and the developing culinary arts — diners at grand meals were in the habit of taking light refreshments on benches (banchetti) between, or apart from, the main courses. The word therefore came to mean "a light meal taken at a bench". From this modest meaning, the climb began: a small banquet (as the lighter course was called in French too) gradually became a course, then a rich repast, and finally the feast itself. Semantic ascension of this kind — a word meaning a small thing, reused flatteringly for ever grander things — is a familiar social process; what is striking here is that it took only a few generations in late-medieval Italy and France.

Into French and across the Channel

Middle French borrowed banchetto as banquet, and by the mid-fifteenth century the French word had settled firmly on the meaning "a grand feast, a sumptuous dinner". It reached England through the Anglo-French of the court and appears in English from around 1470 (as banket or banquet), first meaning the most lavish kind of feast. In Tudor England the word carried instant continental prestige: a banquet was the feast of princes, served with the newest French and Italian refinements of cookery. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries saw the "banquet" (or "banqueting") used at court for both the great feast and the grand dessert course, and the building of banqueting houses — detached pavilions for festive entertainment, of which Inigo Jones's Whitehall Banqueting House (1622) is the great survivor, where Charles I walked to his execution in 1649.

The nineteenth-century civic banquet

The nineteenth century democratised the banquet without making it any less formal. The civic banquet became the great instrument of Victorian public life: mayoral banquets, guild banquets, political banquets (the banquet campaign of 1847 helped bring down the July Monarchy in France), charitable banquets, and the ritual of the after-dinner speech. Banqueting was how institutions did business in public, and the word gained its modern institutional weight — a banquet is now as much a political and ceremonial occasion as a meal. Dickens, a great describer of Victorian feasts, uses the word constantly for both genuine splendour and comic pretension.

Modern English

  • Twentieth- and twenty-first-century English keeps the core sense and adds the commercial one: banquet halls and banqueting suites are hired for weddings, conferences and celebrations; a "banquet" menu is a set-menu large-group dinner.
  • State banquets remain a fixed ritual of diplomacy — the seating, menu, and toasts governed by protocol.
  • The word has gone global, applied to the great celebratory dinners of many cultures (Chinese, Indian, Middle Eastern), where it serves as the ready-made English label for any large formal feast.
  • Register is unchanged: formal, celebratory, abundant — never humble. No modern speaker feels the "little bench" beneath the chandeliers.

The history of banquets is thus a perfect example of semantic elevation: a word borrowed from the workaday world of benches and snacks, carried upward by courts and cities until it named the most solemn and splendid meals of state, and finally settled — still grand, still formal — in the banquet halls of the modern world.
