PIE *pet- "to fly, to rush, to fall, to go toward" (reconstructed; cf. Sanskrit pátati "he flies, falls," Greek pétomai "I fly") → Latin petere "to make for, to seek, to aim at, to attack, to request" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE) → Latin impetere (in- "into, against" + petere) "to attack, to rush upon, to assail" (1st c. BCE; cf. Cicero) → Latin impetus "an attack, an onset, a shock; a violent impulse; momentum" (1st c. BCE; Caesar uses it of a charge) → Latin impetuōsus "violent, impetuous, full of impetus" (imperial Latin, 1st c. AD) → Old French impetueux "violent, impetuous" (12th c.) → Middle English impetuous "violent, rushing" (late 14th c.; first recorded c. 1400) → Modern English IMPETUOUS (acting or done quickly without thought; passionate, rushing).

Note: the prefix in- here is the Latin prepositional prefix "into, against," not the negative in- "not"; the stages from petere onward are all attested. The PIE reconstruction *pet- is standard; its precise laryngeal shape is debated (sometimes written *peth₁-), but the root's membership in the "fly/rush" family is not in doubt. No letter labels are used; every stage is a real attested or explicitly reconstructed form.
