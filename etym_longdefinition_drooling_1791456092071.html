Drooling is the present participle and gerund of drool. Its literal sense is the involuntary flow of saliva from the mouth, especially in sleep, infancy, illness or neurological disorder. Its figurative senses are equally important: drooling can mean talking foolishly or incoherently, and drooling over something can mean showing greedy or adoring desire. In the present passage the word is used as a noun meaning "drivel, senseless utterance": the broken words would have seemed mere drooling to most men.

1. The literal sense

  • Saliva escaping the mouth. Drooling happens when saliva accumulates faster than it is swallowed, or when lip and tongue control is reduced. It is normal in infancy and in deep sleep.
  • Physical and involuntary. The word emphasises lack of control: the drooler is not choosing to let saliva escape. That is why the image is so readily used for mental or emotional loss of control.
  • Everyday register. The baby was drooling, he drooled in his sleep, the dog drooled at the smell of meat. The word is informal but not vulgar.

2. The clinical sense

  • Sialorrhea. Excessive salivation or drooling is called sialorrhea in medical writing. It is associated with cerebral palsy, Parkinson's disease, stroke, motor neurone disease, intellectual disability and dental or oral problems.
  • Dysphagia. Difficulty swallowing can cause drooling because saliva is not cleared from the mouth efficiently.
  • Assessment and care. Clinical scales grade drooling severity and frequency, and speech and language therapists advise on posture, swallowing and oral care.
  • Neonatal and paediatric. Drooling is a normal developmental stage in infants and a common concern in paediatric neurology.

3. The figurative sense: foolish speech

From the early nineteenth century drool has meant to talk nonsense or to speak in a feeble, rambling way. The metaphor is direct: words that run out of the mouth uncontrolled are like saliva that runs out uncontrolled. Mere drooling is therefore a strong phrase: it reduces speech to bodily leakage, denying it the status of language. In the passage, the narrator imagines that most men would have dismissed the broken utterances as exactly that.

4. The figurative sense: desire

  • Drooling over a person. To drool over someone is to admire them with obvious, often sexual, appetite.
  • Drooling over a thing. To drool over a car, a cake or a new gadget is to desire it openly and greedily.
  • Drooling at the mouth. The phrase intensifies the image of uncontrollable appetite, sometimes used of anger or madness as well as desire.
  • Drool-worthy. Modern informal English uses this compound for anything that provokes delighted desire, especially food and design.

5. Derivatives and compounds

Drool is both verb and noun. The noun means saliva, or figuratively nonsense. Compounds and colloquial phrases include drool cup, drool bucket (insulting), drool-worthy, drool-proof and drool over. The participle drooling is often attributive: a drooling idiot, a drooling fan. These uses are aggressive or mocking, and they borrow the clinical image to insult someone's intelligence or self-control.

6. Register and collocations

The literal sense is neutral and can be used of animals, babies and patients. The sense "talk nonsense" is informal and usually contemptuous. The sense "desire" is colloquial and often humorous. Common collocations: drooling in his sleep, drooling over the photographs, left drooling, reduced to drooling, mere drooling. The word is not usually used in formal medical prose, where sialorrhea is preferred, but it is common in care notes and patient descriptions.

7. Semantic neighbours

  • Slobbering: similar because it describes saliva running from the mouth; differs because slobbering is wetter, messier and more emphatically disgusted.
  • Slavering: similar and close in meaning; differs because it is more literary and can also mean fawning or flattering.
  • Salivating: similar because it names the production of saliva; differs because salivation can be invisible and is the neutral physiological term.
  • Dribbling: similar because saliva or liquid escapes in drops; differs because dribbling is a lighter, thinner flow and is common in British English.
  • Drivelling: similar because it means talking nonsense; differs because drivel is the older and more literary word for feeble talk.
  • Babbling: similar because it describes incoherent speech; differs because babbling is rapid and continuous, not necessarily saliva-related.
  • Raving: similar because it means wild, senseless talk; differs because raving suggests fever or madness rather than weakness.
  • Gushing: similar because it implies uncontrolled expression; differs because gushing is enthusiastic and often sentimental, not saliva-like.
  • Fawning: similar because it can imply abject admiration; differs because fawning is flattery, not appetite.
  • Lusting: similar because it names strong desire; differs because lusting is explicit and sexual, while drooling is a bodily metaphor for desire.

8. The semantic bridge

The whole family of senses rests on one image: a liquid that leaves the body without control. Saliva is the most literal version. Speech that is not governed by reason becomes "drool" because it seems to spill out in the same way. Desire becomes "drooling" because appetite, like saliva, is imagined as a bodily excess that cannot be hidden. The word is thus a small anatomy of loss of control, moving from the mouth to the mind to the appetite.
