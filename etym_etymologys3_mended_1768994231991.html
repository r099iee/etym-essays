The biography of mended begins, paradoxically, not with repair but with defect. The Proto-Indo-European root *mend- means "physical defect, fault, blemish," and its reflexes are scattered across the family with striking consistency of meaning: Vedic Sanskrit mandá- (मन्द) means "weak, defective, slow, dull," Latin mendum and menda mean "fault, error, blemish," and Old Irish mend means "stammering" — a speech defect. The root's gift to its descendants is not a verb of fixing but a noun of brokenness: the ancient world said "fault" long before it said "to repair." At this early stage the semantic field is already fixed — *mend- is about the flaw in a thing or a person, the crack that the later verb will be built to close. The word enters history, in other words, as a negative, and everything that follows is an attempt to move out of that negative.

Latin builds a verb out of the flaw

Latin performed the crucial transformation. From menda, "fault," came ēmendāre, "to free from fault, to correct," a compound of ex- ("out of") and menda — literally "to bring out of defect." The verb's first territory was texts and conduct: to emend is to correct an error in a manuscript; to amend is to improve a law or one's behaviour. Around the same noun Latin also grew a family of satellites: mendāx, "given to faults," hence "lying, deceitful," gives English mendacious and mendacity; mendīcus, "having a fault," hence "needy, beggarly," gives English mendicant and the mendicant friars, with Italian mendicante "beggar" and French mendiant "beggar" in the same line — an entire vocabulary of defect, deception, and need, all growing from the same root that would eventually yield the humble verb to mend.

Two routes into English

English received the Latin verb twice. The first route was learned and direct: emend (15th century) entered from Latin ēmendāre as the word of textual criticism — to emend a text is to correct its faults — and survives in emendation. The second route was popular and indirect: Old French amender carried the verb through the Romance sound system, and Middle English borrowed it in the 12th–13th centuries as amenden, covering repair, improvement, correction, and cure. Then, in the 13th–14th centuries, English did something characteristically energetic: it clipped the initial vowel. The aphetic form mend dropped the a- of amend and became an independent verb, and it is this clipped, no-fuss form that has carried the domestic sense ever since. The pair amend/mend is a doublet — two English verbs from one Latin ancestor, the longer one formal and moral (amend a law, amend one's ways), the shorter one homely and material (mend a sleeve, mend a fence).

Mending the body, mending the soul

From its first centuries in Middle English, mend was equally at home with things, bodies, and conduct. The moral sense glowed early: "mend one's ways" is to reform one's life, and the word moves freely between pulpit, lawbook, and workshop — to mend is to cure the sick, correct an error, repair a roof, and improve a situation all at once. The intransitive use, "to mend," of a patient, is established by the 16th–17th centuries: a person mends, climbs back to usefulness, recovers — and "on the mend" (19th century) fixes the idiom in everyday speech. The participle mended carries the same double life: a mended sock and a mended man are both things restored to working order.

Domestic labour and folk wisdom

In the 18th and 19th centuries mending became the quiet domestic labour of needle and thread — the darned sleeve, the patched pot, the hem turned at the candle — and the word gathered a folk-wisdom gravity: proverbs like "never too late to mend" turned the verb into a moral maxim. In Victorian writing, mending is repeatedly the emblem of frugal, patient love, the repair that poverty performs rather than replacement. The word's modest register is a deliberate choice: a mended thing is not a new thing, and a mended person is not a healthy person — both are merely good enough to go on, which is the most that ordinary life, as opposed to heroic narrative, usually offers.

Modern drift

In 20th–21st-century speech, casual usage handed most repair work to fix, while repair keeps the formal and mechanical domain; mend has become a deliberately modest, tender word, kept alive in idioms (on the mend, mend fences, mend one's ways), in craft and textile language, and in the literary register. That is precisely why the passage on this card chooses it: "By the time he is mended he has no money to pay her and he leaves in the night" uses a word that treats a gunshot wound as a household repair and a child's recovery as a job of stitching, then undercuts the restoration in the same sentence. The ancient root of "fault" is still audible in the modern participle: you can only be mended if you were first, in some sense, broken — and the word never lets us forget it.
