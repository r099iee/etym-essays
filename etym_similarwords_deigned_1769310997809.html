• Condescended: Similar because both mean doing something considered beneath one's dignity; differs because condescended can be patronizingly gentle or benevolent, while deigned emphasizes proud reluctance and visible self-importance.
  • Vouchsafed: Similar because both mean granting a favour from on high; differs because vouchsafed is formal, often religious or legal bestowal (as of a right, an answer, or a guarantee), while deigned stresses the effort of lowering oneself to perform the act.
  • Stooped: Similar because both mean lowering oneself to do something; differs because stooped suggests descending morally or ethically (stooping to lies), while deigned is about perceived social worth, not necessarily morality.
  • Patronized: Similar because both imply an attitude of superiority; differs because patronizing is the ongoing manner of talking down to someone, while deigning is a single act of reluctantly doing something beneath one's dignity.
  • Slummed it: Similar because both describe a superior mixing with those below; differs because slumming is voluntary, casual, even amused contact with poorer or lower-status company, while deigning implies reluctance and self-importance.
  • Lowered himself / herself: Similar because both involve a descent from dignity; differs because "lower oneself" usually carries a moral or ethical judgement, while deign is social and performative.
  • Humbled himself / herself: Similar because both describe a descent from rank; differs because humbling oneself is voluntary and virtuous (a powerful person serving the lowly), while deigning keeps and displays the rank.
  • Graciously granted: Similar because both describe superior bestowal; differs because "graciously granted" emphasizes generosity and warmth, while deigned emphasizes the reluctance and the superiority.
  • Unbent: Similar because both mean relaxing one's formality or altitude; differs because unbend is lighter and less judgemental, while deign carries the full weight of dignity and condescension.
  • Deigned to favour (archaic): the same word in its older, gracious sense; differs from modern use in lacking the ironic condemnation — the two are historical stages of one verb.
