- [Old Norse inheritance & earliest English sense (13th–14th c.)] - From Old Norse skamt, the neuter of skammr "short, brief," scant entered English meaning "short of measure" — a neutral word for anything falling short of the full amount, whether in space, time, or quantity.
- [Adjectival shift to insufficiency (16th–17th c.)] - Scant hardened from "short" to "not enough": a scant measure, scant supplies. It acquired the flavour of a shortfall that is noticed and regretted — barely sufficient rather than simply absent.
- [Adverb and verb extension (17th–18th c.)] - The word proved strikingly flexible, working as an adverb ("scant two years," barely) and a verb (to scant someone, to give short measure) — the sense of stinting or withholding took over from mere shortness.
- [Modern register] - Today scant is the slightly formal, bookish word for a portion deliberately kept short: scant attention, scant regard. It has shifted from a neutral description of small size to a measured, almost courteous insufficiency — an amount consciously rationed rather than accidentally small.
