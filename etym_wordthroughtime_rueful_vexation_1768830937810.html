- 14th century: "rueful" is coined from rue + -ful, meaning full of grief, sorrow, or repentance — a plain, often penitential regret coloured by the religious register of the age.
- 16th-18th centuries: the heavy grief lightens into a drier, self-aware regret; the word slides toward "expressing regret with a touch of humour or self-deprecation."
- 19th century to present: the wry twist becomes the whole point — a rueful smile is the corners of the mouth turned up at one's own folly, sorrow tempered by understanding.
- 14th century: "vexation" enters English via Old French from Latin vexātiō, a "shaking, jolting, harassing" — a physical agitation or affliction that disturbs the body or spirit.
- 16th-18th centuries: the sense centres on mental disturbance — vexation becomes a settled state of being annoyed, troubled, or worried, the mind rattled rather than merely pricked.
- Modern: vexation means an irritation that disturbs composure, and by extension the very thing that causes it; the old jolt of Latin vexāre still echoes in the feeling of being shaken out of one's calm.
