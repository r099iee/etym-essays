An honest admission of ignorance Raving is a word that leads the etymologist to an honest admission of ignorance. The verb to rave enters English in the early fourteenth century in the form raven, meaning “to show signs of madness or delirium, to rage in speech”, and it is a borrowing from Old French. There the picture is raver, a variant of resver, a verb that meant “to dream; to wander here and there, to prowl; to behave madly, to be crazy”. The crucial and unusual fact is that Old French resver is itself of unknown origin: the great dictionaries decline to reconstruct any ancestor for it, there is no secure Latin etymon, and consequently no Proto-Indo-European root can be proposed for rave. The word sits, in effect, at the head of its own family with no known parent. This is exactly the situation in which the etymologist must resist the lure of a false derivation: rave looks so much like Latin rabere, “to be mad” — the source of rabies and rage — that older writers confidently linked them, but the phonetic route from rabere into Old French raver cannot be made to work, and the OED gives the Latin connection no support. The honest tree therefore marks rave as a root core of unknown or isolate origin, showing its French attestations and its English descendants without inventing a deeper Indo-European past. The French family The French family, though opaque at its base, is rich in its branches. From resver, French derived resverie, “wild conduct, raving, delirium”, which Middle English took up in the fourteenth century as reuerye; the modern English reverie, “a state of dreamy abstraction, a daydream”, is a re-borrowing from French in the 1650s, by which time the word had softened from raving madness to quiet musing — a remarkable semantic slide from the wild conduct of delirium to the gentle wandering of the mind in meditation. Modern French keeps the same verb as rêver, “to dream”, so that the French word which in Old French meant “to behave madly, to prowl in delirium” has become in modern French the ordinary, gentle verb for dreaming — while English, which borrowed the violent early sense, kept rave firmly in the register of madness and excess. The English career In English, rave begins in the fourteenth century as a verb of madness and rage, and its participle raving quickly becomes a fixed adjective. Two great developments then shape the word's modern life. The first is the intensifier. From the sixteenth century, raving mad and raving lunatic are set phrases: the word stops merely describing the manner of madness and starts grading it, meaning “utterly, spectacularly”. The intensifying power is the source of the word's later polarity. In the nineteenth century the same excessive energy is turned to admiration — a raving beauty is a person whose beauty is so dazzling it borders on the unbelievable — and the twentieth century adds rave reviews, the ecstatic press notices of a hit show or film. The word can thus swing between the two poles of excess: fearful madness on one side, ecstatic praise on the other. The second development is the great modern slang rebirth. In the 1980s, the British acid-house scene produced the noun rave, an all-night party with electronic dance music, and the agent noun raver, a party-goer — often claimed to be a fresh coinage from rave “to be wildly enthusiastic”, the same old word repurposed by a new subculture. To rave, at a rave, is to dance with the ecstatic abandon that the verb has always promised, and the participle raving has been pulled along into the vocabulary of club culture. Sound and form
  • Old French resver / raver → Middle English raven → modern rave: the initial consonant is stable; the spelling settled as rave by the sixteenth century.
  • The participle raving is the regular -ing form, used both as a present participle and as a fully adjectival and adverbial form (raving mad).
  • The homophonous noun raven “a big black bird” is a different word, from Old English hræfn, and must not be confused with the early verb spelling raven.
Literary life English literature has made rich use of the word. Shakespeare's King Lear, driven mad on the heath, delivers the raving speeches that define the word's tragic register; the Jacobean dramatists used raving for the extremes of passion and jealousy; and the eighteenth-century novel made “ranting and raving” the stock phrase for a tempest of empty anger — precisely the phrase Hawkes uses in The Story of the Stone, where the warning to Dai-yu about Bao-yu turns on the contrast between honey-sweet words and ranting and raving. In the nineteenth century the “raving beauty” and the “raving success” domesticated the word's excess into hyperbole, while modern journalism speaks of rave reviews and the modern club scene of raving. Seven centuries after its first English appearance, raving still means what it meant in the fourteenth century — speech or behaviour at the extreme of ungovernable energy — only now the extreme may be madness, fury, beauty, praise, or an all-night dance. Social context The word's career tracks the changing attitudes of English society to excess. In the Middle Ages, raving belonged to the vocabulary of sin and madness — the raving man was one possessed, one who had lost the ordering of reason that medieval culture prized. In the Renaissance, raving became theatrical, the language of the mad king and the passionate lover on the stage. In the eighteenth century, with the rise of politeness and the cult of restraint, raving became comic and condemnatory — the empty fury that the novel could hold up to ridicule. In the nineteenth century, romanticism and the cult of enthusiasm allowed the word to turn positive: one could rave about what one loved. And in our own century, youth culture has reclaimed the word as its own — a rave is the celebration of exactly the ungovernable, collective energy that the old word always named. Raving, in short, is excess itself: a word for everything too loud, too fast, and too full for the world to contain.
