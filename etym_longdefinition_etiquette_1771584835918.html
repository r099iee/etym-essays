Etiquette denotes the customary code of polite behaviour observed in a society, a class, or a profession. In the passage from Cao Xueqin's The Story of the Stone, Dai-yu has just entered the Jia mansion as an orphaned guest, and the narrative must establish that she has been correctly raised: "Only then did she ask permission to sit, as etiquette prescribed." The word's power lies in its impersonality. Dai-yu does not sit when she wishes, or at the host's bare word, but according to a rule — a code that exists independently of any single person and that she is expected to know in advance. "Prescribed" reinforces this: etiquette is not improvised in the moment but fixed beforehand, a scripted order of gestures that certifies one's rank and breeding. For Dai-yu, meticulous performance of etiquette is a matter of survival: it signals that the provincial girl is not "outlandish," that she can be admitted to the inner chambers of the great house. The nuance, then, is of conduct as a measurable, examinable system — behaviour that is "correct" or "incorrect" in the way a sum is right or wrong — and of the individual as an examinee under the silent, appraising gaze of the household.

The word's senses fan out across several domains:
  • Social manners: the code of polite behaviour in general society — table manners, greeting conventions, dress codes, the order in which things are done ("social etiquette," "the etiquette of the dinner table").
  • Professional and institutional codes: the customary rules binding members of a profession or institution, not written into law but enforced by the group — "medical etiquette," "legal etiquette," "parliamentary etiquette," "diplomatic etiquette."
  • Court and ceremonial usage: the prescribed order of ceremony and precedence at court, the historic root sense — "court etiquette," the rules of who stands where and who bows first.
  • Modern digital and everyday extensions: informal codes of conduct in new environments — "email etiquette," "netiquette" (network etiquette) — showing the word's continuing power to name unwritten rules.
  • Figurative use: the "etiquette" of any closed activity — the etiquette of mourning, of the duelling field, of the kitchen — the implied rulebook of a way of life.

Etiquette is one member of a precise family of words. Manners are the actual, habitual behaviours of a person — the way one eats, greets, or holds a cup — and they may be individual and variable ("his manners are rough"); etiquette is the codified system that manners are supposed to follow, a social law rather than a personal habit. Protocol is stricter and more official: it governs diplomatic ceremony, court procedure, and formal institutions ("protocol demanded that the ambassador be seated first"), whereas etiquette governs everyday social polish. Decorum is the quality of seemliness and fittingness — of conduct appropriate to an occasion — and it is more aesthetic and ethical than etiquette; one observes etiquette, but one shows decorum. Propriety is conformity to what is proper or morally acceptable, often with a prim or prudish tone, while etiquette is a set of conventional rules that may be perfectly trivial (which fork, who bows first). Mores (from Latin mos, "custom") is the anthropological term for the deeply held customs of an entire society, of which etiquette is only the polite, polished surface. The constant boundary is codification: etiquette is the rulebook; manners are its performance; protocol is its official form.

Collocationally, etiquette is anchored in formal contexts: "strict etiquette," "court etiquette," "proper etiquette," "a breach of etiquette," "etiquette prescribed, required, or demanded," "the etiquette of the dinner table," "etiquette books and guides," "dining, professional, and social etiquette." Register-wise the word is distinctly bookish and formal, and it often carries a faint note of social anxiety — one is "unsure of the etiquette," one commits "a breach," one fears giving offence through ignorance of the rules. Its French provenance is audible: English borrowed the word around 1740 precisely to name the ceremonial rules of continental courts, and it has kept an aristocratic whiff ever since. In the novel, etiquette is a perfect fit for a household where rank is everything: it is the "label" that tells every person at a glance who may sit and who must wait, and the phrase "as etiquette prescribed" converts the whole apparatus of Jia-family ceremony into a single impersonal clause. Behaviour, in this world, is not chosen but prescribed.
