- [5th–4th c. BCE: Classical Athens] - emerged in ancient Greece as exēgētḗs (plural exēgētaí), designating official public interpreters and religious magistrates appointed by the state or Delphi to explain unwritten sacred laws, expiatory purification rites, and ancestral religious customs - [perception: revered as sacred guardians of civic piety, ancestral ritual propriety, and divine omens]
- [3rd c. BCE–2nd c. CE: Hellenistic Alexandria & Early Jewish/Christian Thought] - shifted from oral ritual interpretation to textual scriptural analysis, adopted by Jewish scholars like Philo of Alexandria and Christian patristic writers like Origen to denote scholarly interpreters of biblical allegories - [perception: viewed as elite philosophical masters capable of unlocking the hidden spiritual mysteries of holy scripture]
- [7th–15th c.: Islamic Golden Age & Medieval Scholasticism] - parallel flourishing across civilizations: in Islam, encyclopedic mufassirūn (Quranic exegetes) compiled monumental tafsīr traditions; in Western Christendom, scholastic monks and masters developed the fourfold sense of scripture (literal, allegorical, tropological, anagogical) - [perception: celebrated as the intellectual pillars of civilization who reconciled divine revelation with human reason]
- [16th–17th c.: Renaissance & Protestant Reformation] - entered Early Modern English as a learned humanist borrowing; Protestant reformers (Luther, Calvin) championed "grammatical-historical" exegesis over medieval allegory, insisting on the perspicuity of the literal sense - [perception: regarded as frontline theological soldiers battling over the precise grammatical meaning of biblical Greek and Hebrew texts]
- [18th–19th c.: Enlightenment & Legal Exegetical Schools] - expanded beyond theology into secular jurisprudence (the 19th-century French École de l'Exégèse deducing legislative intent from the Napoleonic Code) and German higher criticism, where exegetes treated ancient texts with secular historical-critical tools - [perception: viewed as rigorous, objective scientific analysts applying philological scalpels to sacred and legal canons]
- [20th–21st c.: Modern Academic & Constitutional Discourse] - established across three distinct domains: biblical studies (historical-grammatical criticism), constitutional jurisprudence (originalist and textualist interpreters), and academic literary theory (close reading of complex modernist texts) - [perception: respected as authoritative scholarly specialists who prevent cultural amnesia by rigorously unpacking foundational texts]
