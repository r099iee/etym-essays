Rightabouts is a rare fused compound naming in its sharpest sense a disciplined half-circle turn toward the right, the movement by which a soldier, rider, horse-team, waggon, or marching column that faced one way pivots on the right and ends facing the opposite way, right shoulder sweeping outward and left shoulder cutting inward until front has become rear.

In the source sentence the word is shouted as practical horseman’s business — ‘we’ll send this rider to the ______!’ — with a waggon to hide in, blankets for cover, a farmer at hand, and a rider to despatch somewhere off to the right of the road or camp. Here it carries the looser frontier sense of ground and going: the country off to the right, the right-hand quarter where a scout can ride, watch, fetch help, or draw pursuit away from the hidden waggon.

Core meaning in detail: Drill manuals from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries teach the right about as one of the elementary facings and wheelings, spoken as two beats — Right — About! — and performed as a smart 180-degree pivot to the right. The fused spelling rightabout names one such turn, and the plural rightabouts names repeated or general turning of that kind, much as whereabouts generalises place. Frontier and colloquial speech then softened the parade-ground snap into geography: the rightabouts as the parts lying to the right, the right-hand surroundings, parallel to thereabouts and hereabouts meaning the neighbouring district.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Drill turn to the right rear (primary historical sense): the ordered 180-degree face or wheel toward the right, executed by infantry, cavalry, or horse-teams on command. This is the parade-ground heart from which every other use grows.
  • Repeated or general right-about turning (plural proper): drill in which recruits practise rightabouts until the pivot is exact, or horsemanship in which a rider puts a green horse through its paces. The bridge is simple number: one rightabout performed many times over.
  • The district off to the right (frontier and dialectal, as in the source): the ground, farms, cover, or road lying on the right-hand side of a party’s line of march. The bridge runs through the adverbial -abouts family — thereabouts, whereabouts — where abouts already means surrounding parts, so rightabouts becomes the surrounding parts to the right.
  • A scouting ride or errand into that quarter (extended, contextual): to send a rider to the rightabouts is to send him out rightward to reconnoitre, carry word, or fetch help. The bridge is purpose for place: the district named, then the mission into it.
  • Figurative reversal toward the conservative or conventional side (rare, modern): a political or moral rightabout, turning rightward in opinion as sharply as a squad on parade. The bridge carries the drill pivot into ideology, helped by right already naming the conservative quarter.
  • Nautical and waggon-handling use (technical, dated): swinging a vehicle’s or boat’s head round to starboard so as to reverse course. The bridge is domain transfer: the same pivot taught on the barrack square, applied to teams, tongues, and tillers.

Register and connotation: rightabouts smells of dust, leather, and shouted orders — plain, masculine, practical speech, half drill-sergeant and half plainsman. In uniform it is crisp and exact; on the frontier it loosens into hearty vagueness, the confident gesture of a man who knows his right hand from his left and expects his rider to do the same. Modern readers meet it chiefly in westerns and historical novels, where it colours a scene instantly: waggons, blankets, farmers, riders, and a captain’s voice dividing danger into left and right.
