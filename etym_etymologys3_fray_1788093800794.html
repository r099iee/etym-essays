Two English words share the modern form "fray", and a full history of the form must follow both lineages — the noun meaning "battle" (the sense in the Tolkien context) and the verb meaning "to wear thin". They converged by accident and now colour each other.

1. The noun: from Germanic "peace" to English "battle"

Era one — Indo-European and Germanic. The chain begins with the PIE root *prey-, "to love, to spare, to be well-disposed". In the Germanic branch this produced a family of words for love, friendship, and peace: the noun *friþuz meant "peace, truce, safety, protection" — the peace that protected kin, guests, and suppliants (compare Old English frīþ "peace, refuge, asylum", Old High German fridu, later German Friede). In early Germanic law "peace" was a real legal force: one could be "under the peace" of a king or clan, and the worst penalty was to be placed outside it.

Era two — the Frankish peace enters Romance. In the early medieval period, when Salian and Ripuarian Franks ruled northern Gaul, the Frankish word for peace crossed into the speech of the Gallo-Roman population. Latin already had its own words for peace (pax) and war, but the Germanic term was adopted into Vulgar Latin with a new prefix: *exfridāre, literally "to take out of peace", hence "to disturb, frighten, put in fear". This is one of the few Germanic legal words embedded in early Romance, and it shows how seriously the Frankish "peace" was taken: the worst thing one person could do to another was to remove them from it.

Era three — Old French and the birth of "alarm". Old French inherited esfreier "to frighten, disturb" and the noun esfrei, effrei "fright, terror, commotion" (compare modern French effroi "dread, terror"). Notice the semantics: at this stage the word is entirely about the emotion of fear, not about fighting.

Era four — Anglo-French and English: fear turns into the fight itself. After the Norman Conquest (1066), Anglo-French carried afray/affrai into England, where the noun affray broadened: it meant an assault, an attack, a violent public disturbance. In medieval English legal usage an "affray" was a breach of the peace — a fight in public. The shift from "fright" to "fight" is a classic metonymy: the thing that frightens — a violent outbreak — becomes the name of the outbreak. The Middle English Dictionary records affray from the early 1300s, and 14th-century texts use it for armed attacks and assaults.

Era five — aphesis: affray becomes fray. By c. 1400 English speakers began dropping the unstressed initial vowel: affray → fray. Aphesis (loss of initial a-) was a common medieval process (compare "mend" from amend, "squire" from esquire). The short form took over the "fight, battle, skirmish" senses, while the full form "affray" was reserved for legal language. The 16th century made "fray" the ordinary literary word for a sharp, disorderly fight; it appears throughout early-modern prose and verse for duels, skirmishes, and riots.

Era six — metaphor and idiom. From the 18th century the word climbed out of the battlefield into every competitive arena: political controversies became "the fray"; combatants "entered the fray" and the prudent stayed "above the fray". 20th-century journalistic usage fixed these phrases, so that today a corporate takeover bid, a tennis rally, or a parliamentary debate is routinely a "fray".

Tolkien's usage (1937, in the very sentence on this card) shows the final layer: a consciously archaic, heroic register that ordinary modern English no longer possesses.

2. The verb: from Latin "rub" to "frayed nerves"

A separate strand of Latin produced fricāre "to rub" (the source of friction, and, through a related adjective, friable "crumbly"). Late Latin passed it to Old French as fraier/freier "to rub, scrape, bruise", and English borrowed it c. 1400 as fraien "to wear away by rubbing". The semantic record is easy to trace: rope wears into a "frayed" end; cloth "frays" at the hem; then the past participle goes metaphorical — "frayed nerves", "frayed tempers" became established idioms in the 19th and 20th centuries, describing people rubbed raw by stress, exactly as cloth is rubbed raw by friction. The homonymy with the battle-noun is pure accident, but the language now exploits it: a person "in the fray" has very plausibly "frayed nerves".

3. Phonetic notes

  • Vulgar Latin *exfridāre → Old French esfreier: the initial /ks/ cluster softened, and the ex- prefix was progressively worn down in everyday speech.
  • Anglo-French and English dropped the entire unstressed first syllable in aphesis by c. 1400.
  • Modern British /freɪ/ is the regular outcome of Middle English /frai/ under the vowel changes of the 15th–17th centuries.

4. Key attestations and contexts

  • Vulgar Latin *exfridare: reconstructed from its Romance descendants (Old French esfreier, Old Italian sfreggiare) — no written text survives, but the spread is secure.
  • Old French esfrei: 12th-century literary texts (chansons de geste) use it for terror and battle-alarm.
  • Middle English affray: legal records and chronicles from c. 1300 onward; "affray of the peace" appears in medieval statute language.
  • fray: c. 1400 onward; the aphetic spelling is standard by the 16th century.
  • Modern law: "affray" remains an offence under the Public Order Act 1986 (England and Wales) — a fight in public causing terror.

Summary: the noun rose from a Germanic legal word for peace, crossed into Romance as "to frighten", and in England turned the fright into the fight; the verb rose separately from Latin "to rub" and now lends the noun its modern "worn-down" flavour.
