Witless is a native English compound whose two elements each have deep, well-attested histories, and whose meaning has shifted along with the dramatic career of the word wit itself. The first element goes back to one of the best-attested roots of the whole Indo-European family, the root *weid- 'to see'. That root gave Greek eîdon 'I saw', Latin vidēre 'to see', Sanskrit véda 'I know', and English wit, wise, witness, as well as, via Latin, vision and idea (via Greek). The crucial semantic move is an ancient one: in the Indo-European perfect system, 'I have seen' came to mean 'I know' — seeing is the root metaphor for knowing, and the perfect stem *woid-/wid- hardened into a verb of knowing. This is the PIE stage that produced the Germanic verb 'to know': Proto-Germanic *witaną, a preterite-present verb whose singular is literally 'I have seen, I know', and from it the noun *witją 'understanding, intelligence'.

Old English inherited both: the verb witan 'to know' and the noun wit 'mind, understanding, intellect, sense' (in Beowulf the wit is the thinking mind, and the poem praises a ruler for sound witt). Old English also already had the compound wītlēas 'witless, foolish, senseless', built with the privative suffix -lēas. That suffix descends from Proto-Germanic *lausaz 'loose, free, devoid of', ultimately from the PIE root *lewH- 'to loosen, release, set free' (the same root as Greek lū́ein 'to loosen' and Latin solvere 'to set free'); in Old English the adjective lēas meant 'loose' and also 'devoid of', and in compounds it produced the sense 'without X' — hūslēas 'shelterless' — a pattern that became the modern suffix -less. Middle English witles continued the compound unchanged from the thirteenth century.

The drift of wit — and what it did to witless. The real drama of the story is the career of the base word:

  • Old English – Middle English: wit means the mind and its powers — intelligence, understanding, good sense. Witless = without that; foolish, senseless.
  • Renaissance (16th c.): wit rises in prestige to mean quickness of intellect, ingenuity, imaginative power — the quality prized at court; witless remains the plain negation.
  • Restoration (late 17th c.): wit narrows further to mean clever, sparkling verbal humour (the wit of the coffee-house and the stage), and the family splits: a wit is a clever joker, a witticism a clever remark. Witless did not follow this narrowing — it stayed anchored to the older, broader 'mental faculties' sense, which is why it still means foolish or dazed rather than simply unfunny.
  • 18th–19th c.: witless appears in serious and semi-legal contexts for the mentally incapable (found witless), and in gothic and sentimental fiction for victims of shock and ruin — a register that fits Tolkien's Thráin perfectly.
  • 20th–21st c.: the fixed intensifier constructions scared witless, bored witless, worried witless make the word common in everyday speech, while the adjective proper remains literary and rather old-fashioned — which is precisely why Tolkien's narrator uses it for a broken dwarf king rather than for comic effect.

Sound, morphology, and the privative suffix. The initial w is the ordinary Germanic reflex of PIE *u/w- (cf. wise, worship from 'worth-ship'); the compound has never been borrowed or remade, and the modern form witless differs from Old English wītlēas only by regular vowel reduction and the levelling of the suffix. Culturally, the word is a monument to the 'see = know' metaphor that runs through all Indo-European, and to the way a base word's prestige (the Renaissance rise of wit) can leave its negation behind on an older semantic level. When Gandalf says Thráin 'was witless and wandering, and had forgotten almost everything except the map and the key', he uses the oldest, most physical sense of the word — a mind emptied of its wits — and the compound's thousand-year-old history of meaning 'without the mind's power' gives the sentence its tragic, almost clinical gravity.
