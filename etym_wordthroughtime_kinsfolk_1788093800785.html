- 'The folk of one's kin' — Middle English kynnesfolk (late 14th c.) is the ordinary, warm word for the extended family in everyday and biblical prose.
- Dignified standard — 16th–17th centuries: King-James-era prose and chivalric narrative use 'kinsfolk' as the formal collective for relatives; slightly old-fashioned even then.
- Sentimental survival — 18th–19th centuries: rural, patriotic and sentimental registers (Scott, Dickens, American frontier prose and dialect) keep it alive with warmth and solidarity.
- Retreat from speech — by the mid-20th century 'family' and 'relatives' rule the everyday; 'kinsfolk' (and American 'kinfolk') survive as dialect, historical and solemn literary usage.
- Rallying-cry register — in fantasy (Tolkien's 'O my kinsfolk!') the word is a conscious archaism: a king's call to his people, carrying blood-loyalty and an old-world dignity that 'family' cannot hold.
