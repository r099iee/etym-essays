ASSEMBLIES is the plural of ASSEMBLY, recorded around 1300 as assemblee for a gathering of persons for a purpose, borrowed from Old French asemblee and assemblee, the feminine past participle of assembler used as a noun for a gathering, union and even marriage. Assembler in the eleventh century meant to come together, join and unite, from Latin assimulare, in Classical Latin to make like, liken, copy and feign, later in Late Latin to gather together. Assimulare joins ad, to, with simulare, to make like, from the stem of similis, like and of the same kind, from Old Latin semol, together, from PIE sem, one and as one. The paradox of likeness becoming gathering is bridged by the idea that similar things belong together.

Sound changes are French-led and regular:

  • PIE sem to Latin simul and similis: vowel weakening and suffixation give adverbs and adjectives of togetherness and likeness; semol to simul shows routine Latin vowel reduction.
  • Similis to simulare to assimulare: denominative verb formation with -are, then prefix assimilation ad plus s giving ass-; classical pronunciation with double s simplifies in speech to single s in French.
  • Latin assimulare to Old French assembler: loss of final syllables, reduction of u and lenition; Anglo-Norman spelling asemblee reflects insular simplification.
  • Old French assemblee to Middle English assemblee: direct borrowing with English plural -s giving assemblies; stress shifts to second syllable and final -y stabilises after 1500.

Meaning expansion follows social institutions:

  • Medieval gathering for purpose: from 1300 a company collected in one place, often for deliberation; by the mid fourteenth century a gathering for debate, hence colonial legislatures from the 1680s.
  • Euphemistic union: in Middle English and Old French also a euphemism for sexual coupling and marriage, now obsolete, showing how joining bodies and joining groups shared language.
  • Polite dancing balls: in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries assemblies meant subscription dancing balls among polite persons of both sexes, the exact sense in the prompt where Elizabeth expects to meet a gentleman at the assemblies; Austen uses the Meryton assembly as a marriage market and manners test.
  • School and worship gatherings: by 1932 the school assembly of all pupils for presentation; earlier chapel and meeting-house assemblies for worship and instruction.
  • Military signal: assembly as a trumpet or bugle call for troops to gather, a specialised imperative use.
  • Industrial fitting together: from 1852 to assemble parts, and from 1914 assembly for the act and the fitted collection itself, as in assembly line; later computing assembly language translation.

Culturally the word tracks how societies formalise togetherness. Feudal musters become parliamentary assemblies; provincial politeness invents the subscription assembly ball with masters of ceremony, ticketed entry and strict etiquette; mass schooling invents morning assembly; Fordist industry invents the moving assembly line described in 1917 factory literature. Literary attestations include chronicles, parliamentary rolls, Austen Pride and Prejudice, where the assembly frames first impressions, and modern school and factory writing. OED lists fourteen noun senses, three obsolete, confirming a genuinely broad but historically continuous career from likeness to gathering to manufactured unit.
