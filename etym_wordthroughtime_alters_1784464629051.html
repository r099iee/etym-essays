“Alter” entered English from Latin in the late medieval period meaning to make different — a change in character, condition, or form, without necessarily destroying what was changed.
“Alter” through the 16th–18th centuries was a word of deliberate adjustment — altering a garment, altering a plan — more formal and Latinate than plain “change,” often carrying a sense of considered revision.
“Alter” in modern English broadened to cover everything from the mildest tweak to consequential redirection, and “alteration” became the business word for modifications of plans and clothes alike.
“Alters” today, in its third-person present form, states a current general effect — news that alters things for the better, a shift that changes the course of hope — deliberate making-different, mild to momentous.
