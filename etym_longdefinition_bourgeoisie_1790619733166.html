Bourgeoisie is a French loanword denoting the middle class — originally townspeople, later the property-owning capitalist class between aristocracy and proletariat — and, by extension and often pejoratively, the mindset of respectable, conventional, materialistic complacency that avant-gardes from Mallarmé to Dada claimed to pique and provoke, as in the present sentence where pranks seek to pique more than just this class.

In Marxist sociology, the classic and most technical sense, the bourgeoisie are owners of the means of production — merchants, manufacturers, financiers — who overthrew feudal aristocracy and are in turn opposed by the proletariat; The Communist Manifesto (1848) fixes this binary for all later theory. In broader French and English usage, however, it simply means the urban middle classes: professionals, shopkeepers, officials, their families and habits.

  • 1. Historical-sociological: town dwellers (medieval): bourgeois as inhabitant of a bourg (walled town) with charter rights; neutral-legal, privileged relative to peasants.
  • 2. Marxist-technical: capitalist owners: employers of wage-labour, accumulators of capital; in Marx neutral-analytic but in polemic exploitative, doomed to be overthrown; plural almost always the bourgeoisie as collective singular.
  • 3. Cultural-pejorative (the sense active here): philistine respectability — dull taste, moral prudery, love of comfort, deference to reason, grammar and good print; Flaubert's bourgeois, Baudelaire's fury, Dada's épater le bourgeois; differs from sense 2 because attitude not income — a poor student can be bourgeois in mind, a rich artist anti-bourgeois.
  • 4. Art-world shorthand: the audience to be shocked: subscribers, critics, gallery-goers, newspaper readers; the Futurist slap, Dada prank and Surrealist scandal all require a bourgeoisie to scandalize; semantic bridge is sociological (actual ticket-buyers) sliding into aesthetic (conventional expectations).
  • 5. Contemporary neutral: middle classes / bourgeois lifestyle: demographers and marketers use bourgeois-bohemian (bobo), petite bourgeoisie (lower middle), grande bourgeoisie (upper middle); pejoration weakens, irony remains.
  • 6. Grammatical note: collective feminine singular in French (la bourgeoisie), borrowed whole; English keeps French pronunciation /ˌbʊəʒwɑːˈziː/ and resists plural (*bourgeoisies rare); related forms bourgeois (person/adjective), bourgeoisify, bourgeoisification.

Domain nuances: historians distinguish haute, moyenne, petite bourgeoisie by wealth and function; sociologists contrast old (industrial) vs new (managerial-professional) bourgeoisie; cultural critics contrast economic definition (relation to production) vs moral definition (conformism). The present book leans cultural: it was more than sociological persons pranks sought to pique, it was conceptual reason built on alphabetic notation — bourgeoisie as embodiment of linear, rule-bound rationality.

Connotation history is essential: admiration (18th-century industrious virtue vs idle aristocracy) → revolutionary enemy (19th-century Marx, 1871 Commune) → artistic butt (late-19th-century Decadents, 1910s avant-gardes) → sociological neutral (post-war stratification theory) → ironic self-label (contemporary bobo). To call someone bourgeois today can still sting precisely because it accuses both privilege and dullness.
