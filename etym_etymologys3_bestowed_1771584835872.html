To stand firm, to place, to give: the biography of bestowed. At the head of the family stands a Proto-Indo-European root of great dignity: *steh₂-, "to stand, to be firm, to set up, to place." It is one of the most fecund roots the language family knows. In its zero-grade and o-grade forms (*sth₂-, *stoh₂-) it generated verbs and nouns of standing across the entire Indo-European world. Sanskrit sthā- "to stand" produced tiṣṭhati "he stands," sthita "fixed," sthiti "stability," and a nest of concrete nouns — sthala "firm ground, a place," sthira "firm, still," sthāna "a station, a place" — words that survive in modern Hindi (sthal, sthir, sthān) and Odia (sthala, sthira) as everyday vocabulary. Greek set up its own statues: histēmi "I place," stasis "a standing," statos "fixed," stoa "a colonnade," from which English takes static, stasis, system, and apostasy. Latin made it the most productive root in the whole administrative vocabulary of Rome: stare "to stand," status "a position, a state," statuere "to set up," statio "a posting," statua "a statue" — and through compounds, instare, obstare, restare, substare, the ancestors of instant, obstacle, rest, and substance. Slavic also stood tall: Russian stat' "to stand, to become" and stan "a camp, a station."

The Germanic branch chose the o-grade. Proto-Germanic *stō- "to stand, to place" underlies *standan- (English stand, German stehen), *stadi- "a place" (Gothic staþs, English stead, German Stätte and Stadt), and — most importantly for our word — *stōwō-, "a place, a placing." This last word was inherited by the West Germanic dialects and in Old English appears as stōw, "a place," a noun so hardy that it survives fossilised in English place-names such as Felixstowe and Padstow, "the place of Felix" and "the place of St Petroc." From the noun English built the verb stōwian, "to place, to stow, to lay up," and from that verb, with the intensive prefix be-, the compound be-stōwian, "to place, to dispose."

The semantics are transparent and physical. To bestow is to "be-place" — to put something in its place, to lay it up safely, to stow it away. In 14th-century English a man bestows his goods in a chest, bestows his arms, bestows his guest in a lodging; the word is a plain verb of placement. It is in the 14th–16th centuries that the social and moral sense grows out of the physical one: what is "placed" on someone becomes what is given to them, and the verb rises in register to mean conferring a gift, honour, or favour. Shakespeare works both senses freely, and the archaic "to give lodging to" still carries the physical placing of a person.

  • Sound shifts: PIE *steh₂- → Germanic *stō- (regular o-grade); *stōwō- preserves the *-w- element; Old English stōw, stōwian → Middle English bestowen → modern bestow (loss of final inflections, raising and settling of the vowel).
  • Semantic drift: "to stand" → "a place" → "to place, to put" → "to place upon someone" → "to confer graciously." The gift-sense is a late, elevated development of a plain verb of putting.
  • Socio-cultural context: the rise of the word's dignity tracks the courtly and religious vocabularies of giving — the king bestows titles, patrons bestow favours, God bestows blessings; in modern usage the word is deliberately lofty, and the participle is chosen precisely when the giving is to be felt as weighty and hieratic.
  • Literary attestations: from Wycliffe's Bible and Chaucer through Shakespeare and the Authorised Version ("He that spared not his own Son... how shall he not with him also freely give us all things" and the psalmist's "bestow" of mercy), the verb has anchored the language of royal grace and divine providence.

The participle bestowed thus carries a full historical freight: beneath the modern gift-word lies the ancient root of standing, the place-word stōw fossilised in coastal place-names, and the plain medieval verb of putting a thing safely away. When Nature "bestows" a sickly constitution on Dai-yu's delicate frame, the word sets the illness upon her as a conferred destiny — a placement so firm it stands.
