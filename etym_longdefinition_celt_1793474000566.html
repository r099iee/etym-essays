Celtæ (frequently rendered in modern orthography as Celtae, and anglicized as Celts or Kelts) is an archaic and formal Latin plural noun designating the Celts—the diverse Indo-European tribal peoples of Iron Age Europe who spoke Celtic languages and inhabited extensive territories spanning from the British Isles and the Iberian Peninsula across Central Europe to the Black Sea and Anatolia. Preserved here in its classical Latin nominative plural form with the historical typographic ligature æ, the term reflects the scholarly orthographic conventions of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historiography, where classical Latin proper nouns were transcribed verbatim into formal English prose.

In the opening sentence of David Hume’s History of England, the author writes: ‘All ancient writers agree in representing the first inhabitants of Britain as a tribe of the Gauls or Celtæ, who peopled that island from the neighboring continent.’ Hume’s deliberate pairing of ‘the Gauls or Celtæ’ directly references the famous opening declaration of Julius Caesar’s De Bello Gallico: ‘Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres, quarum unam incolunt Belgae, aliam Aquitani, tertiam qui ipsorum lingua Celtae, nostra Galli appellantur’ (‘All Gaul is divided into three parts, one of which the Belgae inhabit, another the Aquitani, and the third those who in their own language are called Celts, but in ours Gauls’). By using both terms side by side, Hume pays homage to the classical historical canon, acknowledging that the continental immigrants who originally crossed the Channel into Britain were known interchangeably to Greek and Roman geographers under these two celebrated ethnonyms.

Orthographic and Typographical History: The diphthong æ represents an ancient Latin combination that originally represented the diphthong /aɪ/ in Old Latin, monophthongized to open /ɛ/ or long /eː/ in Late Latin, and was historically printed as a connected ligature (æ) in early modern publishing to signal scholarly fidelity to classical Latin texts. In modern English linguistic and anthropological writing, the ligature has been replaced by the anglicized plural Celts (or occasionally Kelts to emphasize the hard velar stop /k/), while Celtæ survives in historical citations, legal antiquities, and classic eighteenth-century literature.

Exhaustive Polysemy, Historical Dimensions, and Domain Usages:

  • Classical Continental Ethnonym (Primary Historical Sense): The specific tribal peoples of central Gaul who identified themselves as Celts, as documented by Caesar, Herodotus, and Strabo. The semantic bridge is strictly historical and ethnographic: it identifies the original native speakers of Continental Celtic whose material culture corresponds to the archaeological Hallstatt and La Tène cultures.
  • The Pan-European Celtic Linguistic and Cultural Family: Broadened in modern comparative philology (beginning with George Buchanan in the sixteenth century and Edward Lhuyd in 1707) to encompass all peoples speaking languages belonging to the Celtic branch of the Indo-European family, including Insular Celts (Goidelic: Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx; Brythonic: Welsh, Cornish, Breton). The bridge connects the ancient ethnonym of a central European group to a modern genealogical language family.
  • Archaic Historiographical Orthography: Functioning in seventeenth-, eighteenth-, and early nineteenth-century English historical scholarship as an elevated Latinate loanword, deliberately selected by authors such as Hume, Gibbon, and Camden to evoke the unvarnished authority of classical Latin sources.
  • Archaeological Horizon Designation: Used historically in early antiquarian and archaeological reports to designate the builders of European hillforts, tumuli, and torc-bearing elites prior to Roman subjugation.
  • Romantic and Revivalist Mythos: Invoking the idealized ancestral spirit of pre-Roman European freedom, nature worship, Druidism, and poetic bardic traditions, which catalyzed the eighteenth-century Celtic Revival (sparked by James Macpherson’s Ossian poems) and subsequent nineteenth-century nationalist movements in Ireland, Scotland, Wales, and Brittany.

Pronunciation and Phonetic Nuance: The pronunciation of Celtæ reflects an intriguing historical conflict. In traditional English Latin (and British Received Pronunciation), Latin initial C- before a front vowel (such as e or æ) was universally soft, yielding /ˈsɛl.tiː/. However, with the nineteenth-century classical phonetic reform that restored the hard Classical Latin velar stop /k/ and the classical diphthong /aɪ/, academic classicists increasingly read the word as /ˈkɛl.taɪ/. In general anglicized English, both the /s/ and /k/ pronunciations coexisted for centuries, though modern standard usage overwhelmingly favors the hard /k/ for Celt (/kɛlt/) while classical purists may still pronounce Celtæ with either soft or hard onsets depending on the academic context.
