In Edward Gibbon's opening account of the Roman Empire, the plural noun dominions is the object of an entire strategic argument. The republic, he says, achieved the principal conquests; the emperors "for the most part, were satisfied with preserving those dominions which had been acquired by the policy of the senate, the active emulation of the consuls, and the martial enthusiasm of the people." The word is chosen precisely because it is a possession-word: dominions are lands and peoples held under mastery — a portfolio of acquired regions — not an abstract power, and not a single seamless country. The plural form matters: it points to a composite of many regions, provinces, kingdoms, and peoples accumulated piecemeal by a century of competitive aristocratic war, then simply conserved by the emperors. Core definition. A dominion is a territory, or set of territories, under the rule, ownership, or sovereignty of a single government or ruler. The governing idea is dominium — the right of the master, the lord, or the sovereign — applied to land. Polysemy and modern senses. The word's current range is wide, and every sense descends from the Latin idea of lordship:
  • Historical-political (the Gibbon sense): a ruler's territories considered as holdings — "the king's dominions", "the overseas dominions of the empire". Often plural, emphasizing composite extent.
  • British imperial constitutional sense: a Dominion (capitalized) was a self-governing member of the British Empire, equal in status to Britain — the Dominion of Canada (1867), Australia (1901), New Zealand (1907), the Union of South Africa (1910). The term marked semi-independent realms until the Commonwealth of Nations developed; Canada legally renamed "Dominion Day" as "Canada Day" in 1982.
  • Legal sense: "dominion" as the right of ownership and control over property — in Roman law dominium, the fullest proprietary right; in English property law "dominion" or "dominium" is complete ownership. "Eminent domain" is the state's ultimate ownership or right of compulsory purchase.
  • Theology and angelology: a Dominion (plural Dominions) is one of the ranks of the celestial hierarchy — the angels of the second order of the second sphere, who carry out God's rule; from Latin dominationes.
  • Biblical and religious usage: "dominion" as the authority granted to humanity over the earth and its creatures (Genesis 1:26, KJV: "have dominion over the fish of the sea..."). Here the word means stewardship and rule, not territory.
  • Game and title usage: Dominion is the title of a popular deck-building card game, and "dominions" is a standard fantasy-geopolitical word for realms in games and fiction.
  • Historical geography: in early modern English "dominions" could also mean simply "regions, districts" under someone's rule; Gibbon's usage sits in this register.
  • Medieval feudal usage: "dominion" and its sibling "demesne" named the lands directly under a lord's control as opposed to lands held by tenants.
Historical bridge between senses. The bridge is the career of the Latin root domus "house". A dominus was first the master of a household; his power over people and property was dominium. In Roman law dominium hardened into the technical word for absolute ownership. When political writers applied ownership-talk to states, dominium became "lordship over lands", and the English plural dominions named those lands. The constitutional "Dominion" of the British Empire was the late, institutionalized return of the same word: a realm that owned itself while acknowledging the crown. Even the angelic "Dominions" share the root: beings whose very nature is the exercise of lordship. Register and collocation. The word is formal, historical, and slightly archaic-grand in modern English. Typical collocations: "vast dominions", "preserve / defend / extend one's dominions", "the king's dominions", "overseas dominions", "Dominion status", "Dominion of Canada". It is far less common than "territory" in everyday speech and carries a legal-mastery charge that "territory" lacks. (Full synonym boundary analysis appears in similarWords.)
