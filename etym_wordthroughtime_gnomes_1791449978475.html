- 16th century: Paracelsus coins New Latin gnomus for the earth elementals, small spirits that move through soil as humans move through air — learned, cosmological, and slightly sinister.
- 17th century: English adopts the word; gnomes enter Rosicrucian and poetic machinery as subterranean spirits, associated with hidden knowledge and buried treasure.
- 18th century: the creature becomes a familiar figure of European folklore and children’s tales; the “earth elemental” theory fades into general fairy-lore.
- 19th century: German garden gnomes appear and spread; the word acquires a whimsical, domestic, ornamental sense, and gnomes become guardians of lawns rather than mines.
- 20th century: Disney, illustrated storybooks, and fantasy fiction standardize the cute bearded gnome; “gnomes of Zürich” gives the word a satirical financier sense; Tolkien briefly uses “gnomes” for his Noldor.
- 21st century: gnomes are primarily kitsch, fantasy, and gaming figures; the computing desktop GNOME (an acronym) creates a new homograph, while folklore studies keep the older elemental sense alive.
