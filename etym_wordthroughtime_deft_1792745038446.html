- [Old English (8th–11th c.)] — As *(ge)dæfte*, signified "mild, gentle, meek, decorous, accommodating, fitting" — [perception: high Christian and communal virtue of quiet gentleness, humble meekness, and uncontentious suitability]
- [Middle English (12th–14th c.)] — Shifted toward "becoming, neat, proper, suitable, well-ordered" (*defte*, *dafte*), before splitting into two divergent phonetic and semantic paths — [perception: transition from moral meekness to physical and social appropriateness and neatness]
- [Late Middle to Early Modern English (15th–16th c.)] — The historic bifurcation: northern/dialect *dafte* degenerated into "simple, foolish, crazy, mad", while southern *defte* was specialized into "nimble, dexterous, neat in action" — [perception: celebrated as a sparkling compliment for physical agility and artisanal neatness, shedding all connotations of simplicity]
- [17th–18th c. Classical & Literary English] — Embraced by Shakespeare, Milton, and 18th-century stylists to characterize graceful movement, light-footed dance, and elegant poetic phrasing — [perception: aesthetic mark of effortless refinement, artistic polish, and aristocratic grace]
- [19th–21st c. Modern Global English] — Broadened to describe intellectual diplomacy, linguistic wit, surgical precision, and computational efficiency — [perception: universally positive descriptor for light, effortless competence, manual dexterity, and tactful mastery]
