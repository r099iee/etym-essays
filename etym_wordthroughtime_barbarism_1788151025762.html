- [Ancient Greek 'bárbaros': the speech of non-Greeks, a mimetic word for unintelligible foreign talk] - [Perception: chiefly a linguistic category — 'the one who does not speak Greek'; not yet a moral verdict]
- [Classical Athens: 'barbaros' stretches from foreigner to inferior outsider] - [Perception: the word picks up Greek cultural self-importance; Aristotle can argue that barbarians are natural slaves]
- [Hellenistic and Roman periods: 'barbarismos' enters grammar as a technical term for faulty, foreign-flavored speech] - [Perception: double life begins — a precise rhetorical fault on one side, a civilizational slur on the other]
- [Christian Europe: 'barbarian' is applied to pagans, heretics, and unlettered peoples] - [Perception: the dividing line becomes religious as much as linguistic]
- [18th-century Enlightenment: 'barbarism' becomes the systematic opposite of 'civilization' in the new philosophy of progress; Voltaire asks how men 'passed from barbarism to civilization'] - [Perception: history is now a universal ladder; every nation is climbing (or falling) between the two poles]
- [19th-century anthropology and empire: non-Western peoples are classified as savage or barbarous and assigned a place on the ladder] - [Perception: the word is weaponized in colonial science; 'barbarism' helps legitimize conquest and the 'civilizing mission']
- [20th-century Europe: 'barbarism' is applied to the world wars, the Holocaust, and cultural destruction — 'civilization generates its own barbarism'] - [Perception: the ladder collapses; thinkers use the word to indict the West itself]
- [Today: analytical use is hedged and historicized, while moral rhetoric still reaches for it to name atrocity and cultural erasure] - [Perception: necessary and suspect at once — speakers either put it in scare quotes or let it carry full moral weight]
