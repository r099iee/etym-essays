skewer (n. and v.) — a long, thin, pointed pin of wood or metal used in cookery to hold meat together while it cooks, to fasten a joint to a roasting spit, or to thread pieces of meat and vegetable for grilling; by extension, any sharp pointed implement for piercing; and, as a verb, to pierce or fasten with such a pin, or — figuratively in modern usage — to attack or demolish someone or something with a sharp, pointed criticism.

1. Core sense — the cooking implement. In its primary, concrete meaning a skewer is the smaller, lighter cousin of the spit: whereas a spit is long, heavy, and designed to carry a whole carcass over the fire, a skewer is a short pin that pins individual cuts, closes slits in a joint, keeps a stuffing in place, or laces slices together so that they cook as one piece. Skewers are also used to thread small pieces of meat and vegetables for grilling — the ancestor of the modern kebab and satay stick. Wooden skewers cut from green twigs are among the most ancient of cooking implements, and metal (iron, then steel) skewers joined them as metalworking improved.

2. Secondary concrete sense — any sharp pointed pin. By extension the word names any long, slender, pointed object used to pierce or hold something, especially in the butcher's and the cook's trades. In the troll scene from The Hobbit, Bert picks up a skewer as an improvised weapon: the long roasting-pin lying to hand beside the fire becomes a stabbing implement. This shows the semantic readiness of the word to move from kitchen tool to sharp pointed implement, a step that prepared the ground for its modern figurative use.

3. Verb sense — to pierce or fasten. As a transitive verb, to skewer means to pierce and thread with such a pin — "to skewer a joint," "skewered prawns." The verb follows directly from the noun and is recorded from the seventeenth century.

4. Figurative and modern jargon sense — to impale with an attack. The most vigorous modern use is figurative: a critic "skewers" a bad book, a satirist "skewers" a politician, a columnist delivers a "skewering" review. The image is of piercing a target through with a single sharp thrust, so that it is pinned, exposed, and left helpless. This sense has become standard in journalism and commentary and is now, for many speakers, the more energetic use of the word.

5. Historical bridge — why the word means what it does. The word's origin is etymologically obscure but semantically transparent. It entered recorded English in the fifteenth century as skevere / skewer, naming the butcher's wooden pin. Most scholars connect it to the Germanic family of words for "slice, splinter, split piece" — the same family that gives English shive (a slice, a splinter), shide (a piece of split wood), and skiver (a split twig or leather split) — via a Middle Dutch or Scandinavian form (Middle Dutch schiver, "splinter, slice"; Old Norse skífa, "a slice"). The name would thus originally have meant "a sliver of wood" — a small split-off piece used to pin meat. The spelling and pronunciation were later assimilated to the unrelated verb skew ("to turn aside, to be askew"), which is not its source.

6. Register, style, and usage today.

  • Concrete kitchen sense: fully current — kebab skewers, satay sticks, trussing skewers; the word is universal in cookery writing.
  • Figurative sense: fully current in journalism — "the column skewers the government's plan"; a favourite of reviewers and satirists.
  • Military and industrial extensions: slender pointed rods of various kinds are called skewers, and the verb is used for piercing, pinning, or threading in technical contexts.
  • Derived forms: skewering (gerund), skewered (past participle, often of food), and the rare noun skewerage; the adjective skewer-like occurs in technical description.
  • Collocations: wooden skewer, metal skewer, kebab skewer, to skewer a review; figurative objects include reputations, arguments, policies, and performances.

7. Distinctions from near-synonyms. A spit is long and heavy, built to carry a whole carcass; a broach is an archaic French-derived word for a large roasting rod; a pin or peg is a general pointed fastener; a needle belongs to sewing and injection; and a sword or spike implies a much larger weapon. The modern figurative sense — a piercing verbal attack — belongs essentially to skewer alone, which is what makes it such a useful word.
