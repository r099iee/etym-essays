upshot — In the passage from Prince Caspian, Nikabrik closes the Dwarves' debate over the captive Caspian with the flat announcement: “I suppose the upshot is that we have to keep it a prisoner for life.” Here the word performs its classic rhetorical function: it sweeps up the whole moral tangle — murder versus mercy, principle versus practicality — and reduces it to a single, unavoidable administrative verdict. Nikabrik is not hoping, proposing, or wishing; he is presenting what he takes to be the reading of the inevitable, and the summing-up cadence “the upshot is that…” makes his line sound less like a suggestion than like the last word of a case already closed. The word is chosen precisely because it is both ordinary and faintly formal: it belongs to daily journalism and plain speech, yet it still carries a ghost of the old archery image — the “up-shot,” the final arrow that closes a match — and that residue of finality gives the sentence its cold, decisive ring.

Primary sense. The core meaning of upshot is the final result, outcome, or conclusion of a sequence of events, decisions, or discussions — the practical bottom line that a process lands on once all the talk, argument, or calculation is over. It differs from a mere “result” in that it implies a completed process that has been followed right to its end: an upshot is the state of affairs that obtains after everything has been weighed, and it is nearly always introduced with a definite article and a form of the verb “to be.”

Polysemy & secondary senses. Though “upshot” is today a fairly narrow noun, careful use reveals several distinct shades:

  • The practical verdict (primary modern sense): the real-world consequence that settles a matter — “the upshot of the negotiations was a modest compromise,” “the upshot is that nobody gets paid.” It answers “what actually happened in the end?” rather than “what was meant?”
  • The summative / discourse use: a spoken or written signpost that announces a conclusion — “the upshot of it all,” “the upshot being that…” — functioning almost like a connective that flags “here is where all of this lands.”
  • The historical archery sense (now obsolete): the final, deciding arrow shot in an archery contest; the “up-shot” was the last shot of a match, the one that settled the winner. This literal sense (attested from c. 1530) died out as the abstract sense (attested from the 1570s) took over.
  • Administrative and journalistic jargon: in news, law, business, and bureaucratic summary, “upshot” names the operative conclusion of a report, meeting, hearing, or investigation — the single takeaway the reader is meant to remember.
  • Ironic or deflationary use (colloquial): the word can be dryly comic when grand events reduce to trivial results — “after three hours of arguing, the upshot was that we ordered pizza.” The contrast between the lofty build-up and the flat ending is exactly what makes this use funny.

Collocations and grammar. “Upshot” is almost exclusively singular and definite: the upshot is that…, the upshot of the meeting / the whole affair / the investigation, the upshot being that…, as the upshot of…. It rarely takes an indefinite article (“an upshot”) and is seldom pluralised (“upshots” is possible but rare). It prefers the verb “to be” and appears most often as the subject of a defining clause, which is exactly the syntactic slot Lewis gives it in the prisoner line.

Register. The word straddles formal and informal prose. It is at home in journalism and administrative summary, where it lends a note of practised authority; and in plain conversation it can carry a dry, mildly archaic flavour that suggests a speaker who has seen the whole game to its end — the exact rhetorical position Lewis assigns to Nikabrik, whose grim forecast lands with the weight of a verdict.

Historical bridge. The semantic bridge is an archery metaphor. In Tudor England the “up-shot” named the final arrow that rose and closed a match, deciding the winner; within forty years (by the 1570s) the image generalised, so that any long sequence of events could be thought of as a round of archery and its decisive last step as the “upshot.” The word thus transferred from the physical trajectory of a single missile to the abstract verdict of a whole process — a textbook case of an English compound growing a figurative sense and letting its literal one expire.
