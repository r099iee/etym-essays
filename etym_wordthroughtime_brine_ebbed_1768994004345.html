- brine — Old English brīne, 'salt water,' always named the sharp saline liquor of the sea and of pickling; its Old Norse cousin brá-mi was even a poet's word for the sea itself, dignified and epic.
- — In medieval and early modern use it is the everyday pickling bath and the bitter wash of the shore — water so salted that nothing lives in it, sharp and sterile.
- — Contemporary: brine is the industrial and kitchen preservative, the saturated solution, and — in poetic transfer — the dead, bitter dregs of a sea that has died, harsh, ancient, and abandoned.
- ebbed — Old English ebba, 'low tide,' with ebbian, 'to flow back': from the earliest texts ebb and flood name the two halves of the tidal cycle.
- — Figurative extension came early and stuck: the ebbing of strength, of hope, of life — any withdrawal from fullness toward emptiness, slow and inexorable.
- — Contemporary: chiefly figurative (the ebb and flow of fortune), and in geological or elegiac writing given its full weight — whole seas that have ebbed to desert pools, water that went out and never came back.
