• Palace of Versailles: Similar because it denotes the same building and is the standard descriptive English name; differs because the full phrase specifies the palace as a building, while Versailles alone can mean the town, the court, the regime or the treaty.
  • Château de Versailles: Similar because it is the French official designation of the same complex; differs because the French phrase is architectural and administrative, whereas the bare English Versailles carries a wider political and historical charge.
  • Trianon: Similar because it names the royal estate within the park of Versailles and shares the same royal history; differs because the Trianon was the private retreat — first the Grand Trianon, then the Petit Trianon — while Versailles is the public palace and seat of government.
  • Fontainebleau: Similar because it is another great royal château near Paris, used by the same dynasty; differs because Fontainebleau was primarily a hunting residence and never the permanent seat of government, so it lacks Versailles’ political symbolism.
  • Saint-Germain-en-Laye: Similar because it was a royal residence before Versailles and lies in the same region; differs because it was the earlier, medieval and Renaissance seat, superseded by Versailles, and it does not stand for absolutism.
  • the Louvre: Similar because it is a former royal palace in the Paris region and now a museum; differs because the Louvre is urban, ceased to be the court’s residence when the king moved to Versailles in 1682, and now symbolises art and the Republic rather than the court.
  • the Tuileries: Similar because it was the Paris palace to which Louis XVI was brought in 1789; differs because it belongs to the revolutionary and imperial episodes, not to the classical monarchy, and it was burned in 1871.
  • the ancien régime: Similar because Versailles is its most famous symbol and the two are often used interchangeably in political writing; differs because the ancien régime is an abstract system of institutions, while Versailles is a place that stands for it by metonymy.
  • the Treaty of Versailles: Similar because it takes its name from the place and is often shortened to “Versailles” in headlines; differs because it denotes the 1919 peace settlement, not the building or the monarchy, and the context alone tells the reader which is meant.
