Linear derivation chain for FLATTERED (single root, via Germanic into French and English): 1. PIE *plet-/*plat- ("flat, broad; to spread out") — the Indo-European root of flatness and spreading, attested across the family (Greek platys "broad, flat," Sanskrit prathati "spreads out, extends," Germanic *flata- "flat"). 2. Proto-Germanic *flata- ("flat, level") — the Germanic development of *plat-, attested in Old Norse flatr, Old High German flaz, Middle Dutch vlat, and giving Old English flet ("floor, ground") and Modern English flat ("level, even"). 3. Frankish (West Germanic) *flatjan ("to make flat, to smooth") — a derived verb "to flatten, make smooth," reconstructed from the attested Romance outcome; the form is inferred, not directly recorded. 4. Old French flater ("to smooth, stroke, caress, pat; hence, to coax, flatter") — a Germanic loanword in Gallo-Romance, attested from the 12th century; the physical sense of stroking and smoothing is primary, the figurative sense of coaxing with words secondary. 5. Anglo-Norman / Middle English flateren ("to caress with words, coax, praise insincerely") — borrowed from Old French in the 13th–14th century; Chaucer uses the related flaterye ("flattery"). 6. Modern English flatter ("to praise insincerely to gratify vanity") — the established verb. 7. FLATTERED — the past tense and past participle (also adjective, "pleased, gratified"), the form preserved on this card. Note: the verb flater is NOT a native Latin verb but a Germanic loan into Old French, so the Indo-European ancestry runs through the Germanic line of *plat-, not through Latin. The Frankish form *flatjan is reconstructed (attested indirectly through the Old French loan), and some scholars prefer a simpler Frankish *flatōn; the "smooth/stroke" semantics are secure. The past-tense form flattered is regular, adding the English dental suffix -ed.
