- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - hōf (plural hōfas) denoted the horny foot-casing of beasts of burden, vital to Saxon farming and cavalry warfare.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - hof / hoof developed variable plural forms (hoves, hooues, hoofs) as spelling shifted following the Great Vowel Shift.
- Early Modern English (c. 16th–17th c.) - The King James Bible and Elizabethan writers established hoofs in high literary prose (e.g., Exodus 10:26: "there shall not an hoof be left behind").
- 18th to 19th Century - Hoofs and hooves coexisted as dual standard plurals in veterinary treatises, Romantic poetry, and classic Victorian novels.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains dual plural status; deeply embedded in equestrian sports, veterinary podiatry, and cultural idioms (cloven hoofs, hoofing it).
