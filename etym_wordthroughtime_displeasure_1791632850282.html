- Late 14th c. (Middle English) — displesir / displeasour: borrowed from Anglo-French desplaisir; denoted personal grief, physical affliction, sorrow, or a grievance.
- 15th–16th c. (Tudor Statecraft & Chancery) — Acquired severe monarchical authority: "incurring the sovereign's displeasure" became a technical political formula signifying loss of favor, imprisonment, or treasonous disgrace.
- 17th–18th c. (Enlightenment Moral Philosophy) — Adopted by empiricist philosophers (Locke, Hume, Bentham) as the hedonic psychological opposite of pleasure, analyzed as the primary uneasiness driving human volition.
- 19th c. (Victorian Etiquette) — Elevated into the supreme rhetorical instrument of polite understatement, allowing aristocrats and officials to express devastating reproach without vulgar emotional display.
- 20th–21st c. (Contemporary Administrative & Diplomatic Standard) — Retained as an elevated formal noun used in diplomatic communiqués, corporate governance, and academic critique to express measured censure.
