Etymology of EMBROIDERED — a Germanic root, via Old French, with a proposed (and debated) Indo-European base: Stage 1 (PIE, proposed and debated): *bhreus- / *bhrus- (PIE) — "to swell, sprout, burst forth" — the root family proposed for "bristle" and "brush", and by extension for anything that projects or sprouts outward, including threads standing out from a surface. (Note: this PIE stage is a reconstruction; some scholars instead treat the Frankish word as an isolated formation, so the pre-Germanic stage is debated and not universally accepted.) Stage 2 (Proto-Germanic, reconstructed): *bruzdōną — "to braid, embroider, stitch" — built on the same projecting-points/threads family; not directly attested in any surviving Germanic manuscript. Stage 3 (Frankish, reconstructed): *brozdōn — "to braid, embroider" — the Frankish (Old Low Franconian) form carried into the speech of Gaul and into Gallo-Romance. (Inferred from the Romance borrowings, not attested in writing.) Stage 4 (Old French, c. 9th–12th c.): broder / brouder / brosder — "to embroider, to braid". Stage 5 (Old French, c. 12th–13th c.): embroder / embrouder — "to embroider, ornament with needlework" — formed with the prefix en- ("in, into"): to work thread in(to) a fabric. Stage 6 (Middle English, late 14th c.): embrouderen / embroderen (c. 1375, Chaucer and contemporary texts) — "to ornament fabric with needlework". Stage 7 (Early Modern English, 15th–17th c.): embroider (verb) — "to decorate fabric with stitched patterns"; past participle embroidered (also spelled embrouded, enbrodered in early texts). Stage 8 (modern, present day): EMBROIDERED — "decorated with patterns of thread"; also, figuratively, of a tale embellished with invented detail.
