SLAB is one of the most instructive words in English for the way sound symbolism can defeat etymology. Unlike words that descend through centuries of documented change from a Proto-Indo-European ancestor, slab appears in the record with no pedigree at all: the dictionaries honestly confess that its origin is unknown, and the best reconstruction is a Germanic expressive base *slabb- meaning "wet, slippery, slimy stuff". Its history is thus the history of a shape — how a sound-impression of wet, spreading matter became the name of a heavy, flat, monumental piece of stone. THE SOUND-SYMBOLIC BASE The cluster sl- is one of the most consistent sound-symbolic patterns in the Germanic languages. It is the skeleton of slop, slush, slurry, slime, slick, slither, sleet, and slobber — a whole family of words for wet, soft, slippery, spreading substances. The s- onset suggests the friction of a wet surface, and the following liquid l- suggests sliding and flowing. A base *slabb-, with its doubled stop marking a broad, flat, spreading contact, fits this family perfectly: it is the shape of something wet that flattens out and spreads. In the Scandinavian and Low German dialects this base appears with exactly these meanings — Norwegian slabbe "slush, mud, a splash of wet stuff", Swedish dialect slabbe "slippery, slimy", Middle Low German slabben "to dabble, soil", Middle Dutch slabben "to soil, splash" — and English slabber and slobber "to wet with spittle" are borrowed from the same stock. The word is therefore, at its root, not about stone at all, but about the feel and spread of mud. THE BRIDGE FROM WET TO FLAT The intellectual problem of slab's history is how a word for mud became a word for a flat stone. The bridge is the idea of a broad, flat, spreading mass. Mud and slush spread out flat; a thick piece of timber, sawn or split with broad parallel faces, is the same shape; and a stone slab is that shape in stone. The first English record, around 1300, is already of the timber sense: slabbe, "a large flat piece of timber, especially the rough outer slice of a log left when it is squared". The old wetness survives only as a ghost — in the dialectal adjective slab "thick, viscous, gluey" (16th century), and in the stubborn association of the word with the heavy, the inert, and the unshaped. The semantic change is a pure case of shape-transference: the same flat, spreading form was recognized in mud, in timber, and then in stone. FROM TIMBER TO STONE By the 14th century the word had transferred to building: a slab was a thick flat piece of stone, used for paving, for altars, for tomb covers. The connotation from the first was monumental and mortuary — a slab is the stone that covers a grave, the great flat mass of an altar, the heavy pavement underfoot. This is the sense that carries the word through the centuries of church, castle, and cathedral building, and it is the sense that makes slab so effective in Tolkien's landslide, where great plate-like fragments of rock tumble down upon the company. In the Middle Ages the word also travelled socially: the flagstone and the tombstone kept the monumental weight, while the builder's slab stayed a plain term of the trade. THE WIDENING OF THE WORD In the 16th and 17th centuries the word widened from stone to any thick flat piece of solid material — wood, metal, slate, cakes of soap or chocolate. By the 19th century the transferred senses were fully established: a slab of bread, a slab of chocolate, a thick slab of meat, generous square-cut portions on the plate; and the mortuary sense was fixed in the phrase "the cold slab", the mortuary table on which a corpse is laid. The 20th century added the concrete slab of modern construction, and geology took the word into the depths: a tectonic slab is a unit of crustal rock, especially the subducting plate that descends into the mantle. From the slop of the marsh to the depths of the earth is a long journey, and slab made it entirely through the single idea of a broad, flat, heavy mass. CONNOTATION AND REGISTER Throughout its history the word has kept a core of heaviness and impersonality. A slab is unworked, raw, plate-like — the material itself rather than the finished object. It is not precious, not shaped, not delicate. This is why it works so well in the passage: the mountain is shedding itself in huge, flat, broken pieces of raw rock, and the word gives the reader the weight of the catastrophe. The connotation of the modern word runs from the hefty-but-familiar (a slab of cake on a plate) to the solemn and mortuary (the cold slab, a tombstone slab), always anchored by the original image of a broad, flat, spreading mass. Even the kitchen use keeps the blockiness: a slab of chocolate is a thick, flat, weighty piece, closer to masonry than to pastry. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In The Hobbit, slabs appears in the thick of the landslide: "a fearful confusion of slipping, rattling, cracking slabs and stones." The word is chosen for its mineral heaviness and its flatness. These are not pebbles; they are great plate-like fragments of the mountain itself, and the reader feels their weight in the very shape of the word — broad, flat, heavy, and inert. Cracking slabs: the participle splits the flat stone as the slope founders, and the whole sentence is a sound-picture of enormous impersonal mass in motion. Slab, the obscure orphan of the Germanic sl- family, here carries the full weight of the mountain it describes — a weight it has been accumulating since it first meant a spread of mud on the floor of a Scandinavian marsh.
