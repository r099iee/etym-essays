- 1380s–1500s (Middle English Medical Roots) - Base adjective acute entered the language via Old French and Latin as a technical medical term (contrasted with chronic) describing fevers, pestilence, and diseases that came on suddenly with severe, life-threatening crises.
- 1590s–1650s (Elizabethan & Jacobean Adverbial Genesis) - The adverbial form acutely emerged in English literature and philosophical essays, describing physical pain that pierced like a sharp needle, as well as razor-sharp wit and penetrating intellectual discernment in debate.
- 1700s–1850s (Enlightenment Philosophy & Sensory Science) - Flourished in treatises on psychology, moral philosophy, and acoustics; used to describe heightened nervous sensibility, delicate sensory perception, and acute acoustic pitches, alongside mathematical descriptions of acute angles.
- 1900s–Present - Ubiquitous across contemporary English in academic prose, psychology, politics, and literature, especially in established collocations such as "acutely aware," "acutely felt," and "acutely sensitive."
