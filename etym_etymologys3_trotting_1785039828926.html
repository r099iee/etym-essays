**TROTTING: a gait that travelled from Germanic stepping to the Norman road** The present participle trotting carries within it a small history of medieval contact, for although its ultimate root is Indo-European, the immediate ancestor of the English word is a Frankish term that travelled through Old French before crossing the Channel. The word is at once a piece of horseman's technical vocabulary, a loanword of the Norman aristocracy, and — in Tolkien's hands — the exact word for a hobbit's industrious flight. **The deepest source: the stepping root** The deepest source of trot is the reconstructed PIE root *der- (1), which Watkins treats as the base of roots meaning "to run, walk, step." From it came the Proto-Germanic strong verb *tred-, "to step, tread, trample," which produced Old English tredan and modern English tread, with the strong-verb forms trod and trodden. Out of that stepping base Germanic formed a derived stem *trott- / *trod-, denoting a regular stepping motion; the Old High German verb trottōn, "to tread, stamp," shows the stepping stem at work on the continent. The Germanic word for the brisk stepping pace was thus in place long before it ever met a horse. **The Frankish loan into French** In the early Middle Ages the Franks carried the stepping stem into Gallo-Romance as *trottōn, "to go briskly, to trot," and Old French turned it into the verb troter (12th century), used of horses and men alike, with the noun trot naming the four-beat equine gait that falls between a walk and a run. The transfer is a natural one: the regular diagonal stepping of the horse's trot visibly matches the rhythmic stepping the Germanic stem had always named. From Old French the word then split in two directions. Southward, the Romance languages re-absorbed the Germanic stem, giving Italian trottare and Spanish trotar. Northward, English took the noun around 1300 — first for horses, in the vocabulary of the Norman and aristocratic stables — and the verb trotten in the late fourteenth century, by which time it already applied to people moving with rapid short steps as well as to quadrupeds. The word had completed a remarkable circuit: from a Germanic stepping root, through Frankish and Old French, and back into a Germanic language as a loan. **The extension from horse to human** Once in English, trot spread quickly from the stable to the street. A person trots when moving with short, quick, steady steps, and the transfer of the four-beat equine gait to human legs is so natural that the word never felt foreign. By the late medieval and early modern periods the human senses were fully established, and trot broadened into a whole cluster of idiom and slang: bog-trotter (1670s), the disparaging English term for the "wild Irish" who trotted across the bogs; the trots (1808), slang for diarrhoea, the loose bowels imagined as a trotting gait; trot out (1838, figurative 1845), originally to lead a horse out to show its paces and then to produce an idea, excuse, or piece of information for display; and fox-trot, first a slow trotting horse pace (1872) and then, from 1914, the ballroom dance whose syncopated quick-quick-slow rhythm mimics a trotting gait. **The participle in *The Hobbit*** In *The Hobbit*, Bilbo finds himself "trotting along again, as fast as he could trot, at the end of the line" during the escape from the goblin-hall. The participle keeps the motion continuous and open-ended — the trotting could go on indefinitely, tunnel after tunnel, at the tail of the line — and it carries the whole history of the word with it: the brisk four-beat gait of the horse, transferred to the short legs of a hobbit who must trot merely to keep pace with the long-striding dwarves. The tone is peril mixed with pluck: Bilbo is not sprinting in blind panic but doggedly churning his little legs, matching the rhythm of flight down dark passages while the yells of the goblin-hall grow fainter behind him. The word's equine ancestry works quietly here — Bilbo is brought down to the pace of a small, hard-working animal, industriously keeping up at the end of the line. **The word's register today** Trotting today remains an everyday, concrete word of brisk short-stepped movement, at home in horsemanship, in descriptions of people and animals in motion, and in the idioms on the trot, trot out, the trots, and fox-trot. It connotes efficient, steady progress under pressure rather than elegant or heroic motion, and it preserves, after nine centuries of travel from the Germanic stepping root through the French stables and back into English, the exact rhythmic image it was born with: short, quick, regular steps, kept up doggedly, half-walk and half-run.
