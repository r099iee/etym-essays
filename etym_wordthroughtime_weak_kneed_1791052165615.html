- Pliant, bending - Old Norse veikr and early Middle English weik: neutral to mildly negative physical pliancy; willow, thin ale, frail body.
- Lacking force - 14th-17th c.: weak extends to arguments, eyesight, defences; general feebleness without strong moral charge.
- Knees as courage seat - Early Modern idiom: stiff knees (stubborn), bending knees (submission), knees knock with fear; body metaphor ready.
- Compound insult coined - 1850s-1860s Victorian empire and muscular Christianity: weak-kneed as unmanly, sentimental, unfit for rule; strongly masculinist, imperial connotation.
- Political cliché - 20th c.: weak-kneed response, government, reform; standard journalistic scold from left and right; loses literal knee image, becomes pure irresolution.
- Modern self-aware use - Present: still common but macho edge noticed; often used knowingly or quoted; literal weak at the knees splits off for love/awe (positive swoon) vs weak-kneed (negative collapse).
