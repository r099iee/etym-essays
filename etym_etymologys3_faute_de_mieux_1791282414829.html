The history of faute de mieux must be told as three separate French words that fused in French and then emigrated as a frozen phrase into English. Each has a deep Latin ancestry, and their combination is relatively modern French idiom that English borrowed whole in the 18th–19th centuries.

1. faute — fault, failure, lack. Latin fallere meant "to deceive, disappoint, escape notice, fail." From its past participle falsus English gets false, fault, fail, but French took a different path. Vulgar Latin created a feminine noun *fallita "a failing, a miss, a fault," first "a lapse" then "blame for the lapse." Old French faute (12th c.) meant both moral fault and physical lack: faute de "for lack of." The semantic bridge is transparent: a failure to provide becomes an absence. Phonologically Latin fallere lost intervocalic -l- lenition and final syllables in Gallo-Romance: fallita > falte > faute, with /al/ vocalising to /au/ before a consonant, a regular Old French change seen also in cheval/chevaux. By the 17th century faute de + noun was a fixed prepositional locution meaning "in default of."

2. de — of, from. Latin preposition de "down from, concerning, of" is one of the most stable words in Romance. It survives almost unchanged in form from Classical Latin through Vulgar Latin into Old French de and Modern French de, though its range widened enormously as Latin case endings collapsed and prepositions took over grammatical work. Its unstressed vowel reduced but never disappeared. In faute de mieux it is the ordinary partitive/link: "lack of better."

3. mieux — better, best. Latin adverb/adjective melius "better," neuter of melior, itself of uncertain deeper origin, possibly from a PIE root meaning "strong, great." In Gallo-Romance melius > miels > mieux (Old French mielz, miex, 9th–12th c.), with palatalisation of -l- and -us loss. Old French distinguished meillor (adjective) and mieuz (adverb), later levelled to modern meilleur/mieux. Mieux as a noun — "the better (thing)" — is a substantive use of the adverb: le mieux "the best available."

  • Gallo-Romance sound shifts: loss of final -m and -s, vocalisation of /l/ to /u/, palatalisation of /k,g/ before front vowels, and reduction of unstressed syllables shaped all three words without obscuring their Latin source.
  • Idiom formation in French: faute de + noun/infinitive was productive by the 16th century (faute d'argent, faute de temps). Faute de mieux is first widely attested in 18th-century French correspondence and dictionaries as the abstract, absolute form: lacking not any specific thing but "better" itself.
  • Borrowing into English: 18th–19th-century educated English absorbed untranslated French phrases (au fait, ennui, savoir-faire) as marks of cultivation. Faute de mieux appears in English letters, novels, and parliamentary debate from the mid-1700s, always italicised at first, signalling a knowing shrug. Wodehouse's Jeeves-era use is typical: a servant's polished, apologetic French to soften a flawed suggestion.
  • Modern status: in French it remains a living everyday locution; in English it remains a deliberate foreignism, never fully nativised, retaining French spelling and approximate French pronunciation, used for ironic, self-aware resignation.

Together the phrase illustrates how English does not always borrow words but borrows attitudes: a compact French excuse for imperfection that English keeps French precisely because the foreignness makes the apology sound more graceful and less abject.
