The verb gutter is the late, small descendant of one of the oldest words for pouring, and its story runs from the sacrificial fires of ancient India to the wavering candle-flames of Gormenghast.

Stage one — the root of pouring: The PIE root *gheu- meant “to pour” — the fundamental act of making liquid flow — and the pouring of water, of molten metal, and of sacrificial libations were all its children. In the Indo-Iranian world it became the central verb of ritual: Sanskrit juhóti, “he pours out, he offers”, the pouring of clarified butter into the sacred fire, from which Hindi havan, “fire-sacrifice”, still descends. In Greek the root gave khéō, “I pour” — the verb of libations, rivers, and molten bronze — and its nominal form khulós, “juice”, yielded English chyle and chyme, the “poured” fluids of digestion. The root thus associated pouring with worship and with life-giving substance from its earliest attested life: to pour was at once a physical and a sacred act, and the vocabulary of libation grew directly from it.

Stage two — two extensions and the Latin drop: In Italic, an extended form *gheu-d- produced Latin fundō, “I pour, melt, cast”, ancestor of an entire English family of pouring words: foundry (where metal is poured), fuse and diffusion, infusion, confuse (“pour together”), and refund. But the word we are tracing descends from a second extension, *gheu-t-, the formation behind Latin gutta, “a drop” — the smallest unit of pouring, the single bead of liquid. From gutta, Romance built a vocabulary of dripping: Old French goutte, “drop”, gave English gout, the disease once blamed on a “drop” of corrupt humour settling in the joints. The humoral medicine of the Middle Ages imagined disease as a drop of corrupted fluid, and the drop-word carried that medical theory into English.

Stage three — from drop to channel: And from goutte came the Old French goutiere (gouttière), “a water-channel”, the groove that gathers falling water; the derivation runs from the single drop to the trough that collects a stream of drops. English borrowed the channel-word in the thirteenth century as goter, later gutter — the groove along a roof-edge or street that collects falling water and carries it away. For centuries the noun ruled: roof gutters, street gutters, and then, in the nineteenth century, the moralised “gutter” of the slum — “gutter press”, “gutter politics”, to live in the gutter — the word for the lowest channel society can pour through, a social metaphor made possible because the physical gutter is literally the lowest trough a street has, the drain into which everything runs down and is lost.

Stage four — the verb and the candle: The verb appeared in the eighteenth century among chandlers and housekeepers, and it is a perfect re-application of the drainage sense. A badly made candle, or one breathed on by a draught, melts unevenly: the wax runs down one side in a little channel — a gutter, exactly — and the flame, now swimming in its own fuel, flickers, darkens, and burns low. To gutter is thus for the candle to pour itself into its own drain. The verb quickly spread from the chandler's shop into poetry and fiction, where the guttering candle, guttering lamp, or guttering hope became a stock image of wavering life approaching extinction: Dickens uses guttering flames, and the metaphor of a life “guttering out” — like a candle wasting in its own melted wax — is a standard figure of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In Titus Groan Peake deploys it as an instrument of characterisation by negatives: the candles were “never allowed to fail or even to gutter”, where “fail” is total extinction and “guttering” is the subtler betrayal, the first tremor of a flame already losing its discipline — the beginning of catastrophe rather than catastrophe itself, the first wobble of the order that Rottcodd exists to maintain.

Stage five — the modern split: In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, electric light made the candle-sense rare and literary, and the word's life has split across registers: the physical channels of roof and street (and the bowling-lane gutter, the typographic gutter between printed columns and the inner margin of a book), the moral metaphor of degradation (the gutter press, gutter politics), and the preserved candle-sense that now lives almost entirely in period prose, poetry, and the historical novel. The verb's evolution — from PIE “pour”, to Latin “drop”, to French “channel”, to English “roof-groove”, to the candle that pours itself away — is a complete demonstration of how a word's meaning walks, over millennia, from ritual fire to the first wobble of an English flame, and how the smallest domestic object can preserve, in its name, the oldest acts of worship and pouring in the Indo-European world.
