Core meaning. Rekindled is the past tense and past participle of the verb rekindle — literally "to kindle again", i.e. to light a fire once more after it has gone out or died down. From this physical origin the word has grown a vast figurative life: to rekindle is to revive, restore, or reawaken anything that has faded, cooled, or been extinguished — feelings, enthusiasm, interest, hope, conflicts, debate, love, friendship, memory.

Primary sense — literal fire. In the concrete, original sense, rekindled describes a fire that has been relit after dying, a flame stirred back to life, an ember fanned into a blaze again. Key features:

  • It presupposes an earlier fire that had burned and then been reduced (to embers, ash, or darkness).
  • The action can be caused by a person (adding fuel, striking a match) or by conditions (a breeze, dry wood).
  • Even here the emotional overtone is already present: rekindling a fire suggests warmth, home, and survival returning to a cold hearth.

Secondary sense — reviving feelings and passions. The most frequent modern use is metaphorical, built on the ancient poetic equation of fire with passion, love, anger, zeal, and spirit. To say that a feeling has been rekindled implies that it once burned strongly, then cooled or lapsed, and has now been brought back:

  • Romance: rekindled love, a rekindled romance — a relationship renewed after separation or staleness.
  • Enthusiasm and interest: a rekindled interest in history — a hobby or passion revived after years of neglect.
  • Hope and optimism: rekindled hope — optimism restored after disappointment.
  • Anger and conflict: rekindled rivalry, rekindled hostilities — a quarrel or war that flares up again; the metaphor is morally neutral, usable for warm or destructive passions alike.

Tertiary senses and extensions.

  • Intellectual and public life: a debate, controversy, or investigation can be rekindled — a dormant issue thrust back onto the public stage.
  • Memory: a scent, song, or photograph can rekindle old memories — the past "lighting up" again in the mind.
  • Religious and spiritual: devotional writing speaks of rekindling faith, zeal, or charity — re-igniting a spiritual flame that had guttered.
  • Technical and spatial (rare): a furnace, engine, or heating fire can be rekindled; science writing uses the metaphor of "rekindling" a rocket engine or a star's nuclear fire.

Grammar and behaviour. Rekindled is the regular past form of a weak verb (rekindle — rekindled — rekindled). It appears as (1) the past tense and past participle (the reunion rekindled their friendship; their friendship was rekindled); and (2) a passive or participial adjective (a rekindled flame, rekindled hope). It takes a direct object of either concrete fire or abstract feeling and is frequently seen in the collocations rekindled hope, rekindled romance, rekindled interest.

Register and connotation. Rekindled is standard across formal, journalistic, and literary English and is mildly warm and vivid, always carrying a residual image of flame. It is more evocative than bare renewed or restored, and slightly less intense or formal than resurrected. In informal speech it is common enough in set phrases but retains its dignified, image-rich feel.

Semantic history in brief. The word is composed of the Latin prefix re- "again, back" and the English verb kindle "to light, set fire to" (from Old Norse kynda). It entered English in the mid-16th century as a literal fire word and expanded into figurative use across the 17th–19th centuries, becoming a stock term of Romantic and journalistic prose for the revival of anything from love to war.
