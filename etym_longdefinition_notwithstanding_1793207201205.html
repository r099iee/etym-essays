Notwithstanding is the formal English word for defiance of circumstance: as a preposition it means in spite of, despite (notwithstanding the rain, notwithstanding the expense); as a conjunction it means although (notwithstanding that the road was foul); and as a postpositive adverb it means nevertheless, however (the objections notwithstanding, the mud notwithstanding). In the source sentence Cobbett wields the prepositional sense with a sneer: all Middlesex is ugly, notwithstanding the millions upon millions which it is continually sucking up from the rest of the kingdom — all that stolen wealth, and the county cannot buy itself beauty. The word carries the whole weight of his sarcasm: despite everything London pours into Middlesex, the place remains irredeemably ugly.

Core meaning in detail: Grammatically notwithstanding is three tools in one coat. Before its noun it is a preposition governing the objective case: notwithstanding the storm, notwithstanding his age, notwithstanding anything to the contrary. After its noun it is an adverbial survivor of an absolute construction: the rain notwithstanding (= the rain not standing in the way), the cost notwithstanding — a postpositive use almost no other English preposition preserves, and the reason lawyers love it. With that it turns conjunction: notwithstanding that the evidence was thin, the jury convicted — though although and though do this work more plainly in ordinary prose. All three share one logic: X stands against the action, and the action proceeds anyway. The word never hurries, never chats, never shortens; it is the black coat and stiff collar of English connectives, and putting it in a sentence is like stamping a document.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Despite; in spite of (prepositional sense, primary since the 14th c.): notwithstanding the millions — the obstacle named and overridden. The bridge is the original absolute phrase: not withstanding = nothing standing against, no impediment prevailing — Latin non obstante (nothing standing in the way) Englished word for word by medieval translators of law and chronicle.
  • Although; albeit (conjunctional sense, with that): notwithstanding that Middlesex drinks the kingdom's wealth, it stays ugly. The bridge is the preposition grown a clause: what once governed a noun phrase (the millions) now governs a whole proposition (that-clause), the obstacle promoted from thing to fact.
  • Nevertheless; however (adverbial, postpositive sense): the mud, the expense, the objections notwithstanding — the noun first, the defiance after. The bridge is word order preserving history: the noun stands where the absolute construction's subject stood six centuries ago, and notwithstanding still does the participle's old work of brushing it aside.
  • Notwithstanding anything to the contrary (legal boilerplate, modern formula): the clause that bulldozes every other clause — this provision prevails whatever the rest of the document says. The bridge is the preposition armed for combat: draftsmen pick the heaviest word in the drawer precisely because its weight signals that normal interpretation stops here.
  • And yet, formally (literary transition): novelists and historians use a sentence-initial notwithstanding to pivot against everything just established — Notwithstanding all this, the regiment marched at dawn. The bridge is rhetorical mass: where however would whisper and but would jab, notwithstanding delivers the contradiction with judicial gravity.

Register and connotation: Notwithstanding is formal, literary, and legal — the register of judges, statutes, conveyances, and historians, and of polemicists like Cobbett borrowing judicial gravity to pass sentence on a county. In conversation it sounds starched or jocularly pompous; plain-English campaigners have spent a century urging writers to use despite instead. But the word survives every cull because nothing else does its triple duty: despite cannot stand after its noun, although cannot govern one, and nevertheless cannot take an object. Cobbett's use shows its polemical edge at full sharpness — the long Latinate word set down like a seal upon his verdict: ugly, the money notwithstanding.
