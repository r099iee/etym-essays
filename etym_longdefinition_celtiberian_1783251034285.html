In the sentence on this card, Celtiberian names the extinct Celtic language of ancient Spain — the tongue of the peoples of the central and north-eastern Iberian Peninsula, especially the Ebro valley and the Meseta — and it stands in deliberate parallel with Gaulish: "the people of France spoke Gaulish, and the people of Spain spoke Celtiberian — both vibrant, complex Celtic languages." The word is chosen to correct the modern assumption that Spain was always Romance-speaking, and to stress the diversity of the pre-Roman Celtic world: Gaulish in France, Celtiberian in Spain, each a distinct, fully-developed language. The tone is educational and mildly celebratory, presenting Celtiberian as evidence of a rich indigenous civilization — a "vibrant, complex" language of warriors, cities, and inscriptions that Roman conquest, beginning in earnest in the second century BC, gradually silenced.

Sense 1 — The language (the sense used on this card). In historical linguistics, Celtiberian is the name of the Continental Celtic language spoken on the central and north-eastern highlands of the Iberian Peninsula — above all on the Meseta and in the valleys of the Ebro and its upper tributaries (the modern Spanish provinces of Soria, Guadalajara, Zaragoza, Teruel and Cuenca) — in the last centuries before the Roman conquest, roughly the 3rd to 1st centuries BC. It is the best-attested member of the Hispano-Celtic family, the family of the Celtic languages of ancient Spain, and it survives for us only in about two hundred short inscriptions written in the indigenous Iberian script, a semi-syllabary derived ultimately from a Greek-based alphabet and adapted by the native Iberians to write a non-Indo-European language first. The most celebrated texts are the four Botorrita bronzes (found near Zaragoza), the Luzaga Bronze, the bronze of Cortes de Navarra, and the great religious inscription of Peñalba de Villastar, which invokes the god Lugus. Most of the inscriptions are brief — personal names, town and tribal names, divine epithets, formulas of dedication — and much of the grammar remains imperfectly understood, a frontier of research for Indo-Europeanists and epigraphers.

Polysemy — Sense 2: The people. The noun Celtiberians (Latin Celtiberi, Greek Keltibēres) names the ancient people formed in the peninsula's interior by the fusion of Celtic-speaking warriors and settlers with the older, non-Indo-European Iberian population. The Romans met these highland peoples as redoubtable enemies from the middle of the 3rd century BC, and they are remembered above all for the siege of Numantia, a Celtiberian town on the upper Duero which held out against Rome until Scipio Aemilianus starved it into surrender in 133 BC. Roman ethnography describes the Celtiberians as fierce, frugal, warlike and loyal — the archetype of the hardy Spanish mountaineer.

Sense 3: The adjective. As an adjective, Celtiberian qualifies anything that pertains to this people, this language, or this fusion culture: Celtiberian inscriptions, Celtiberian weapons (the curved falcata sword, the small caetra shield), Celtiberian religion, Celtiberian pottery, the Celtiberian wars.

  • Polysemy summary. One word, three interlocking senses — a language, a people, a culture — because the three grew historically from a single label: the name of a tribe became the name of its speech and then of its whole material and spiritual world. In every register the meaning is the same fusion: Celtic + Iberian.
  • Morphology and spelling. A transparent compound of Celt- (the Celtic ethnonym: Greek Keltoí, Latin Celtae) and Iber- (the ancient regional name of the Ebro and of the Iberian peoples), joined by the Latinate adjective suffix -ian (Latin -iānus, 'belonging to'). English spellings fluctuate between Celtiberian, Celt-Iberian and the rarer Celtiberic; the hyphenated form makes the two-element compound visible.
  • Careful boundaries. Celtiberian must not be confused with Iberian proper, the non-Indo-European language isolate of the eastern and southern coasts, written in its own syllabic script and wholly unrelated to Celtic; with Hispano-Celtic, the modern umbrella term for all the Celtic tongues of the peninsula, of which Celtiberian is the best-attested member; with Lusitanian, the western language whose Celticity is debated; with Galatian, the Continental Celtic tongue of Anatolia; or with Gaulish, the Celtic language of France invoked as Celtiberian's structural twin in this very sentence.
  • Q-Celtic character. Celtiberian is strikingly conservative, preserving the Indo-European labiovelar *kʷ as ku (e.g. ku-n-te-kum 'together'), a 'Q-Celtic' trait shared with Irish rather than with Gaulish and Welsh — a fact that makes it precious to linguists reconstructing the family tree of the Celtic languages.
  • Modern extensions and the historical bridge. Today the word is standard equipment for linguists, epigraphers, archaeologists and historians of religion; since the late 20th century it has also been reclaimed by regional movements of central and north-eastern Spain as a badge of a rich pre-Roman heritage. Every newly discovered inscription is reported as 'Celtiberian', so the single label keeps working across all three domains at once — tongue, tribe, and civilization.

In short, Celtiberian on this card means the extinct, richly attested Celtic language of ancient central-northern Spain, but the word itself always points beyond the grammar to the fused people who spoke it and the lost world their inscriptions preserve.
