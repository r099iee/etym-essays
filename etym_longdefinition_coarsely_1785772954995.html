COARSELY — adverb meaning "in a rough, harsh, unrefined, or vulgar manner; in large pieces, with a rough texture, or without fineness of detail." It is the adverbial form of the adjective coarse, and it carries two connected families of meaning: a physical/technical sense (about grain, texture, and material finish) and a social/moral sense (about manners, speech, and refinement). Core physical sense: rough in grain or texture. The primary, everyday sense is about material quality: done or made so that the result is rough-grained, thick, large-particled, or lacking fine division. It describes how something is ground, chopped, cut, milled, sifted, or woven — coarsely ground pepper or grain, coarsely chopped vegetables, coarsely woven cloth, coarsely milled flour. In the card's sentence — "The grain was __________ milled at the local watermill, leaving noticeable husk fragments in the bread" — the sense is purely physical and technical: the milling was done with a coarse setting, so the flour retained large, rough fragments. This is the neutral, value-free sense used in cooking, materials science, and manufacturing. Core social sense: vulgar, unrefined, or rude in manner. The second major sense is about human behavior and expression: done or said in a rough, harsh, indelicate, or vulgar way. Coarsely can modify speech, laughter, jokes, behavior, or treatment — speaking coarsely, laughing coarsely, joking coarsely — meaning with a lack of refinement, tact, or decency, often offensively so. This is the moral and social sense, and it is usually pejorative: coarse behavior is behavior that violates standards of polish and propriety. Polysemy and usage spread. The adverb spreads across several connected uses:
  • Culinary: coarsely ground, chopped, grated, or milled — a precise technical instruction meaning "in fairly large pieces," the standard opposite of finely; value-neutral and ubiquitous in recipes.
  • Materials and manufacturing: coarsely woven, coarsely textured, coarsely cut — describing roughness of finish, grain, or weave in cloth, sand, paper, stone, and metal work.
  • Sound and voice: coarsely spoken, a coarsely made sound — harsh, grating, lacking smoothness.
  • Manners and morals: coarsely behaved, coarsely jesting, coarsely laughing — rude, indelicate, vulgar, violating refinement; the pejorative social sense.
  • Figurative crudeness: coarsely drawn, coarsely done — executed without subtlety or delicacy, applied to art, imitation, or craftsmanship.
The two poles — physical roughness and moral roughness — are linked by the same underlying idea: the absence of fineness, whether in grain or in conduct. English, like many languages, maps the physical scale (fine vs. coarse) onto the social scale (refined vs. vulgar), and coarsely sits at the bottom of both scales. Register and stylistic nuance. Standard English, comfortable in technical, culinary, literary, and conversational use. In its physical sense it is neutral and precise (the recipe instructs "add coarsely chopped onions"); in its social sense it is condemnatory and literary ("he laughed coarsely"). It is slightly softer than crudely (which stresses primitiveness or offensiveness), similar to roughly (but roughly also means "approximately," a sense coarsely lacks), and more physical than vulgarly (which is nearly always about manners and taboo). Word boundaries and near-synonyms.
  • Roughly — both describe rough, unpolished action; roughly also means "approximately" (roughly ten) and need not imply vulgarity, while coarsely specifically opposes finely in grain and texture.
  • Crudely — both mean lacking refinement; crudely stresses primitiveness, unfinished quality, or blunt offensiveness (a crudely drawn map), while coarsely is more about texture and grain and can be quite neutral in cooking.
  • Harshly — both describe rough treatment or sound; harshly emphasizes severity and grating quality (harshly criticized), while coarsely emphasizes lack of fineness and, in manners, lack of polish.
  • Vulgarly — both describe behavior lacking refinement; vulgarly stresses commonness, low taste, and often taboo-breaking, while coarsely can be purely physical (coarsely ground) with no moral sense at all.
  • Indelicately — both describe tactless, rough behavior; indelicately is more about tact and sensitivity, while coarsely is more about refinement and physical texture.
  • Finely — the direct antonym: finely ground vs. coarsely ground; where coarsely means in large pieces, finely means in very small pieces, and in manners finely means with refinement and delicacy.
Grammar and derivatives. Adverb coarsely (comparative more coarsely, superlative most coarsely); adjective coarse; noun coarseness; verb coarsen ("to make or become coarse"). The adverb is formed from coarse + -ly. Note the spelling: coarsely is an adverb and must not be confused with the noun course (a route, a curriculum) or with the phrase "of course"; the two words course and coarse are historical twins, split in spelling in the sixteenth century after their senses diverged.
