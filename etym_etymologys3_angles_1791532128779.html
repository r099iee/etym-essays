Angles is a tribal name recorded at the edge of the Roman world and then, unexpectedly, at the centre of a national story. Its history combines a difficult etymology, a handful of ancient testimonies and a long later career as the eponym of England.

First attestations

The earliest certain mention is in Tacitus’s Germania, written in 98 CE. In chapter 40, after describing the Suebian tribes, Tacitus lists the Reudigni, Aviones, Anglii, Varini, Eudoses, Suarines and Nuithones, who share the worship of Nerthus, the earth mother. The passage is ethnographic and brief: the Anglii are one of several small peoples of the lower Elbe, not a great power. Ptolemy’s Geography, written in the second century, places Angloi in approximately the same region, though the coordinate tradition is confused.

Whether Tacitus’s Anglii are identical with the Angles who later settled Britain is not certain. The name may have covered a cluster of related groups, and the migration of the fifth century may have drawn on a wider North Sea population than the three names in Bede suggest. What is clear is that the name was established in the north-German coastal zone well before the Anglo-Saxon migration.

The etymology of *Angulōz

The usual derivation is from Proto-Germanic *angulaz “hook, angle, fishhook,” from the Indo-European root *h₂enk- “to bend, curve.” The same Germanic word gives Old English angul “fishhook” and angel “hook,” Old Norse ǡngull, and modern English angle “to fish.” The tribal name would then mean something like “the people of the hook-shaped land,” a reference to the curved coastline of Angeln (Latin Anglia) between the Schlei and the Flensburg fjord.

A rival explanation connects the name with the root meaning “narrow” — Latin angustus, angere “to choke,” German eng — and refers to the narrow Schlei inlet. The two roots are close in form and the semantic fit is good in both cases; the question remains open, and responsible dictionaries present the “hook” derivation as the more common but not the only possibility.

It is worth noting that the geometric angle is a different word: it comes from Latin angulus “corner,” which is itself from the same Indo-European root *h₂enk-. The two English words are therefore distant cousins, not borrowings of one another.

Migration and the adventus

The fifth-century migration is documented by Gildas, Bede and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and increasingly by archaeology. Cremation urns, cruciform brooches, grubenhäuser and distinctive pottery show a substantial movement of people from the North Sea coast into eastern and southern Britain. Bede’s three names — Angles, Saxons and Jutes — are a later simplification, but they correspond roughly to different material cultures and settlement zones. The Angles are associated with the east and north: East Anglia, the Midlands and Northumbria.

Name and nation

Old English Engle is the people; Englisc is their language and later their identity; Englaland is their land. The name’s rise was not automatic. For centuries Latin writers preferred Saxones, and the Celtic Britons called the newcomers Saeson. The triumph of “English” reflects the political and cultural prestige of the Anglian kingdoms and, later, of Alfred’s Wessex, which claimed to speak for all Angelcynn. By the tenth century charters style the king rex Anglorum, and the territorial name England is established.

Medieval and early modern

Medieval Latin historiography used Anglia and Angli for the kingdom and its people, and the Norman and Angevin chanceries kept the terms. The Reformation gave the name a new confessional charge: the Church of England became “Anglican,” a word coined in the sixteenth century from Anglicanus. Renaissance antiquaries, reading Tacitus and Bede together, made the Angles part of a origin story in which English liberty was traced to the Germanic forests.

Nineteenth-century racial reading

The nineteenth century turned this origin story into a racial theory. “Anglo-Saxon” became a category of supposed Teutonic vigour, self-government and imperial destiny, opposed to Celtic, Latin and “inferior” peoples. The Angles were recruited into a narrative of English exceptionalism, and “Angles” appeared in schoolbooks as the sturdy ancestors of the English. This reading is now rejected by historians, but its vocabulary survives in “Anglo-Saxon” and in the older sense of “Anglo-” as a cultural label.

Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Modern scholarship treats the “Angles” as a fluid label. Bede’s triad is convenient but schematic; the migrants were a mixture of North Sea peoples, and “Anglian” describes a material culture and dialect as much as a tribe. Ancient-DNA studies since the 2010s have shown both substantial continental ancestry and significant British continuity, complicating older models of replacement. The word “Angles” now appears mainly in academic writing, in the fixed triad “Angles, Saxons and Jutes,” and in the etymologies of “England” and “English.”

Literary attestations

Bede’s Historia is the foundational text, and its account of the three peoples shaped every later history. Victorian historians such as J. R. Green and E. A. Freeman popularised the triad for a mass readership, and the Edwardian schoolroom made “the Angles, Saxons and Jutes” a familiar phrase. Modern writers usually keep the names while qualifying the narrative, a sign of how a national origin story can survive the loss of its literal truth.

Nuances and disputed points

  • Tacitus’s Anglii may be a different or broader group than the later Angles.
  • The etymology of Angeln — “hook-shaped” versus “narrow” — is disputed.
  • The Angles did not call themselves “Anglo-Saxons”; that compound is a later, largely continental and modern label.
  • “Anglesey” is unrelated: it is Old Norse “hook island.”
