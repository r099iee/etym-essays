descent joins a Latin prefix for down to a Latin verb for climb, and then walks downhill through French into every English domain where things go down — bodies, blood, prices and morals. The verb scandere (to climb, mount, scan verses as climbing steps) continues PIE *(s)kand-, to leap or climb, seen in Sanskrit skandati (leaps up), Greek skandalon (trap, stumbling-block, what makes you fall), and English scan, ascend, transcend, condescend. Prefix de- (down from, away, from PIE *de-) reverses the climb: descendere is to climb down, come down, sink, stoop, derive. Classical authors use it for men descending stairs, rivers descending to the sea, estates descending to heirs, and rhetoric descending to particulars — already the full modern range in embryo. Phonologically de- + scandere keeps its consonants intact; Late Latin voices -sc- before -e- toward /ʃ/ in Gallo-Romance.

Old French descendre (verb) and descente (noun, the feminine past participle used substantively: a descended act) carry three medieval senses together: to come down physically, to be descended genealogically, and to make a hostile descent (raid) on a coast — all feudal commonplaces. The Conquest brings verb and noun to England: Middle English descenden (14th century, Rolle, Chaucer) and descent, dissent (lineage, slope, attack). Spelling wavers between -sc- and -s- until Johnson fixes descent for the noun and descend for the verb, with dissent reserved for disagreement. Pronunciation settles to /dɪˈsɛnt/ with stress on the second syllable, the vowel reduced in the first, exactly as French descente /desɑ̃t/ anglicised.

  • Feudal-law fixation (13th-17th c.): common law makes descent a term of art: land descends to the heir-at-law by blood, as opposed to being purchased or devised by will. Treatises distinguish lineal vs collateral descent, descent cast. Genealogy borrows the diagram: a descent is one step down a pedigree, a line of descent the whole staircase. Ruskin’s royalty of descent invokes exactly this heraldic-law sense only to deny it.
  • Moral-theological deepening: Christian Latin descensus Christi ad inferos (Descent into Hell) and descensus Spiritus Sancti (descent of the Dove) fix capital-letter uses; preachers moralise every fall as a descent. By the 18th century critics write of descents from the sublime (bathos), and by the 19th of descents into poverty, drunkenness, slang. Up remains good, down remains bad, and descent names the slide.
  • Darwinian revolution (1859-1871): Origin of Species proposes descent with modification and Descent of Man (1871) makes man descended from apes. The genealogical staircase now holds all life, and descent becomes biology’s master noun (common descent, lines of descent). The same decade gives aviation and alpinism quantified descents (rate, angle), completing the modern set: blood, mountain, aeroplane and soul all descend.
