- [Late Middle English] - Formed in English from un- + befitting, entering use to describe conduct or dress that does not become a person's rank. From its earliest recorded senses it already carries social-moral judgement: a breach of decorum and seemliness.
- [Early modern to Victorian era] - Clusters with the language of court and etiquette — what is unbefitting a gentleman, a lady, a station — and its censure is strongly hierarchical and class-coded, tied to honour, dignity and reputation policed by glance and implication.
- [Modern usage] - Rare in casual speech, preserved in formal, literary and mildly ironic registers; meaning has narrowed toward 'inappropriate to a person's position or dignity' while unbecoming and unseemly have taken over most everyday ground. The connotation stays quietly judgemental but now sounds arch or slightly archaic.
