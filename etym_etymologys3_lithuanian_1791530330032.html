Lithuanian is a word whose meaning shifted from a dynastic territory to a nation and then to a language. Its form is simple — Lietuva plus the English suffix -ian — but its history is one of the most dramatic in European onomastics, because the Grand Duchy of Lithuania was for centuries a multi-ethnic state in which the Lithuanian language was not the language of government.

The first attestation

The name first appears in written Latin as Litua in the Quedlinburg Annals under the year 1009, in a brief notice that St Bruno of Querfurt was killed on the border of Lithuania and Prussia. The form is Latin, but it clearly renders a local Baltic name. Later medieval Latin uses Lituania, German Littowen and Litauen, Polish Litwa, and Ruthenian Litva.

The etymological debate

The origin of Lietuva is genuinely unknown, and the competing explanations should be labelled as hypotheses:

  • The river theory. The name is derived from the small river Lietava or Lietauka near Kernavė, one of the earliest political centres of the Lithuanians. River-derived tribal names are common in the Baltic area, which makes this attractive.
  • The “free man” theory. A connection is proposed with leitis, a word for a free or privileged warrior, which would make “Lithuanian” a social as much as a territorial term.
  • The “rain” folk etymology. Lithuanian lietus “rain” has tempted amateur etymologists to read the name as “rainy land.” The phonology does not support it, but the image has proved memorable.
  • The substrate theory. Some scholars treat the name as pre-Baltic or substrate in origin, which would explain why it has no clear Baltic morphology.

No consensus exists. What matters historically is that the name was fixed early and applied to a territory far larger than the modern language area.

The Grand Duchy

In the thirteenth century the Lithuanians, united under grand dukes such as Mindaugas, resisted the Teutonic Order and expanded eastwards into the fragmented lands of the former Kievan Rus’. By the fifteenth century the Grand Duchy stretched from the Baltic to the Black Sea. Its elite adopted Polish language and culture after the Union of Lublin in 1569, and the resulting Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth was a multilingual federation in which “Lithuanian” meant a citizen of the Grand Duchy, not necessarily a speaker of Lithuanian. This is why English sources of the period can call a Polish-speaking nobleman a “Lithuanian.”

Partitions and the language

The Commonwealth was partitioned in 1772, 1793 and 1795, and most Lithuanian lands passed to the Russian Empire. The Lithuanian language survived mainly as a rural vernacular, while Polish remained the language of the gentry and the Church. In the nineteenth century, under the influence of Romantic nationalism, a revival began: Aušra (“Dawn”) appeared in 1883, and after the Russian authorities banned Latin-script Lithuanian printing in 1864, books were smuggled from Prussia by the knygnešiai, or book carriers. The ban lasted until 1904 and turned the language into a symbol of resistance.

1918 and the modern state

On 16 February 1918 the Council of Lithuania declared independence, and the word became the name of a sovereign republic. Vilnius was disputed with Poland between the wars, and Kaunas served as the provisional capital. In 1940 the Soviet Union annexed Lithuania under the Molotov–Ribbentrop pact; Nazi Germany occupied it in 1941–1944, and the Soviet return brought deportations, collectivisation and an armed resistance movement, the “forest brothers,” which lasted into the 1950s.

Independence and the language today

The reform movement Sąjūdis led to the declaration of independence on 11 March 1990, and Lithuania became the first Soviet republic to break away. EU and NATO membership followed in 2004. Lithuanian is now the state language, used in education, administration and the internet, and its archaic features — the dual, the seven cases, the pitch accent — are a source of national pride and a magnet for Indo-Europeanists.

The Litvaks

A distinct and tragic strand of the word’s history is Jewish. The Grand Duchy was home to one of the great Yiddish-speaking communities of Europe, and “Litvak” came to denote a Jew of that tradition, known for rabbinic scholarship and for the Vilna Gaon. The Holocaust destroyed most of this world; “Lithuanian” in Jewish memory therefore carries a double charge, of cultural achievement and of local collaboration in the massacres.

Literature and culture

Kristijonas Donelaitis’s Metai (“The Seasons,” written 1765–1775) is the founding work of Lithuanian literature, a hexameter poem of peasant life. In the twentieth century, poets such as Jonas Aistis and Tomas Venclova, and the composer Mikalojus Konstantinas Čiurlionis, gave the word a modernist cultural profile. Basketball, amber and the baroque old town of Vilnius now do the same work for tourism.

From a river name of unknown origin to a Grand Duchy, a banned print language, a Soviet republic and an EU member state: Lithuanian has carried almost every kind of political meaning a European name can carry.
