pusillanimous is a formal adjective meaning "cowardly, faint-hearted, mean-spirited". It describes a person, a policy, an action or an institution that lacks courage, and it does so with open contempt. Unlike timid, which can be sympathetic, or cautious, which can be prudent, pusillanimous is always a judgment: it says that the fear is unworthy and that the person should be ashamed of it. In the source passage — "a pusillanimous deserter from good causes" — the adjective tells us what kind of deserter Cicero was: not a man defeated by superior force, but a man who ran because his spirit was too small for the fight.

The etymology inside the meaning. The word is a Latin compound, pusillus "very small, tiny" plus animus "mind, spirit, courage". To be pusillanimous is literally to be small-souled. Latin had the exact opposite adjective too: magnanimus, "great-souled", from magnus "great" plus animus, which gives English magnanimous. The pair is one of the neatest morphological oppositions in the language, and it repays attention: where the magnanimous man has a spirit large enough to absorb injury and risk, the pusillanimous man has a spirit too small to hold any resolution at all.

Senses and applications

  • Cowardice: the dominant modern sense — "a pusillanimous retreat", "pusillanimous conduct in the face of aggression", "too pusillanimous to protest". The fear is moral as much as physical: fear of unpopularity, of expense, of consequences.
  • Petty meanness: the older, more literal sense, in which the smallness of the spirit shows as smallness of conduct — "a pusillanimous policy", "pusillanimous evasions", "pusillanimous spite". Here the word means narrow, mean and ungenerous rather than simply frightened.
  • Of institutions and states: "a pusillanimous government", "the pusillanimous response of the universities", "pusillanimous diplomacy". The word is a favourite of leader writers because it converts a political disagreement into a charge of moral failure.
  • Of actions and proposals: "a pusillanimous compromise", "a pusillanimous half-measure". The implication is that the measure was chosen not from judgment but from the wish to avoid trouble.
  • Related forms: pusillanimity (the quality), pusillanimously (the adverb), and the rare pusillanimousness. The noun is almost as common as the adjective in formal prose.

Grammar and collocation. The adjective applies to agents and to their acts, and it is normally attributive or predicative: "a pusillanimous leader", "the response was pusillanimous". It does not take a complement — one is not pusillanimous about something in the way one is nervous about it; the word describes the whole disposition. Natural collocates include cowardly, craven, timid, weak, abject, and nouns such as leadership, surrender, appeasement, compromise, evasion and conduct.

Register and force. Pusillanimous is bookish, latinate and deliberately heavy. It belongs to the register of the leading article, the parliamentary speech and the sermon, not to conversation, where cowardly or spineless would do the work. Its weight is part of its rhetoric: because the word is long and its parts are opaque to most speakers, it sounds like a considered verdict rather than a shouted insult, and it therefore wounds more precisely. It is also faintly archaic, which gives it a certain Victorian moral confidence.

Why "small-souled" is an insult. The Roman moral imagination measured a person by the capacity of the animus — the spirit that houses courage, purpose and generosity. Great deeds required a great spirit; petty ones betrayed a petty spirit. The Latin tag for the mean, grudging, frightened man was therefore not primarily about the body but about the soul's dimensions. English inherited the metaphor intact, and it is why pusillanimous can mean both "cowardly" and "petty": both are failures of magnitude.

In this reading. The phrase "a pusillanimous deserter from good causes" is the sharpest item in the catalogue, because it accuses Cicero not of changing his mind but of abandoning causes that deserved loyalty. The charge has a factual basis: he left Rome during the civil war, attached himself to Pompey with little enthusiasm, accepted Caesar's pardon, approved the assassination of Caesar, and then tried to steer between Antony and the Senate. Whether these were acts of cowardice or the desperate manoeuvres of a man trying to preserve the Republic and his own life is exactly what his defenders dispute. The word deserter settles the question in advance: it treats prudence as betrayal and fear as the only possible explanation.

Modern currency. The adjective is uncommon but not obsolete, and it has enjoyed a modest revival in political journalism, where it is used of leaders who avoid confronting aggressors or of institutions that decline to defend their own principles. It has no technical senses in law, medicine or science. Its nearest everyday equivalent is cowardly; its nearest literary equivalent is craven.
