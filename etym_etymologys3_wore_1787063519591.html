Proto-Indo-European Roots of Vestment
The historical evolution of wore (and the base verb wear) begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *wes- (or *wes-teh₂-), meaning "to clothe, dress, or cover oneself with garments."

  • This fundamental root of human clothing produced cognates across virtually all major branches of Indo-European:
    • In the Hellenic branch, it developed into Ancient Greek ἕννυμι (hénnymi, "to clothe") and ἐσθής (esthḗs, "garment, clothing").
    • In the Italic branch, it gave rise to Classical Latin vestis ("garment, clothing"), which entered Modern English as vest, vestment, divest, and investiture.
    • In Sanskrit, it produced váste ("he puts on clothes") and vásanam ("clothing, garment").
    • In the Celtic branch, it appeared in Welsh gwisg ("garment, dress").
    • In the Germanic branch, it yielded Proto-Germanic *wazjaną ("to clothe, put on clothing"), which also generated Gothic wasjan ("to clothe") and Old Norse verja ("to clothe, invest").

Old English Morphological Form and the Original Weak Conjugation
In Anglo-Saxon England, the Proto-Germanic verb evolved into Old English werian:

  • Original Conjugation Class: In Old English, werian was a Class 1 weak verb. Its past tense was formed with a dental suffix: werede (parallel to Modern German wehrte or regular English wear / weared).
  • Dual Semantic Strands in Old English: Old English possessed two phonetically identical verbs: werian (to clothe oneself) and werian (to defend, protect, dam up water, source of Modern English weir). The protective function of clothing (cloaks and leather armor guarding against harsh weather and blades) formed a natural psychological bridge between dressing and defense.
  • The Semantic Bridge to Frictional Erosion: During the Old English period, the intensive prefix for- was attached to the past participle (forwered, meaning "worn out, destroyed by long use"). The observation that wearing clothing over months and years causes the fabric to fray, thin, and erode under friction gave birth to the secondary meaning of wear as physical attrition.

Middle English and the Extraordinary Shift to Strong Conjugation
During the Middle English period (1150–1450 CE), the verb underwent one of the most remarkable morphological transformations in the history of the English language:

  • Analogical Levelling: Although historically a weak dental-past verb (werede), weren was rhymingly influenced by the Class 4 strong ablaut verbs: bear / bore / born, tear / tore / torn, and shear / shore / shorn.
  • Birth of the Strong Preterite: By the 14th century, Middle English speakers analogically coined the strong ablaut preterite wore and past participle worn, gradually abandoning the historical weak form wered. By the time of Geoffrey Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, both forms coexisted, with wore emerging as the dominant poetic and literary past tense.

The Temporal Semantic Shift: Time Wearing to Night
In the late 14th and early 15th centuries, the frictional sense of wear ("to rub away gradually through continuous motion") was transferred metaphorically to the dimension of time:

  • Just as water wears down stone or a rider's boots wear down through a long journey, the steady, friction-like progression of the day was conceptualized as "wearing away" the available light.
  • Writers began using phrases such as "the day wore to an end" and "as the night wore on," capturing the palpable sense of human fatigue and the steady, unstoppable passage of hours.

Early Modern English to Contemporary Usage
In Early Modern English (16th–17th centuries), the verb stabilized in the Authorized King James Bible and William Shakespeare's plays:

  • Shakespeare deployed wore across its full semantic breadth: from kings who "wore the crown" to warriors who "wore out the enemy" through relentless siege, to lovers enduring as the "hours wore away."
  • In 18th-century nautical terminology, wore became the preterite of wear ship (from an entirely separate nautical etymology related to veer, yet phonologically assimilated into the verb paradigm).
  • Across five millennia—from a Proto-Indo-European root for cloaking the body, through medieval analogical restructuring into a strong verb, to romantic literary metaphors of time passing—wore remains one of the most versatile and evocative words in English literature.
