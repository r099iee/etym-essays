- [Old French & Middle English collision sense (12th–14th c.)] - From Old French hurter "to knock against," hurtle named the act of colliding — two things crashing together with impact and noise.
- [Frequentative drift: collision becomes motion] - As a frequentative, hurtle moved from the single crash to the repeated violent motion that leads to it — rushing and clashing at speed; to hurtle came to mean to charge or move with reckless, unstoppable force.
- [Divergence from hurl (17th–18th c.)] - Hurl (transitive, to throw) and hurtle (intransitive, to be thrown / to move at speed) split into distinct verbs; their shared Middle English frequentative origin makes the confusion a standing usage trap.
- [Modern register] - Today hurtling denotes enormous speed with mass, noise, and the constant threat of impact — a thing in violent flight toward catastrophe, as in Tolkien's "hurtling down like a thunderbolt."
