Origins: The Cutting Root

The word skirts begins in the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *(s)ker-, "to cut, clip, shear, sever." This is one of the most prolific roots in the Indo-European family: it lies behind Latin carō "flesh" (the meat cut from a carcass — source of carnage and carnivore), Latin curtus "shortened" (source of curt and curtail), Old Church Slavonic kraj "an edge or border" (a line cut into the land), and Vedic Sanskrit kṛntati "he cuts." English native words from the same root include shear, share (a cut-off portion), short, and shard. The core image is severance: anything "skirted" or "shorn" is something cut.

Proto-Germanic: From "Cut" to "Short" to "Garment"

  • In Proto-Germanic the root formed the adjective *skurtaz, "short, cut short, clipped."
  • From it, Germanic speakers made the noun *skurtijōn — literally "a short-cut piece of cloth" — a basic body garment cut from a length of fabric. The garment was named for having been cut to fit, just as a modern skirt is cut from cloth.
  • The same Germanic base, through different channels, gives German Schürze "apron," and the English twins shirt and skirt.

The Great Doublet: shirt vs. skirt

  • In Old English, the Anglo-Frisian sound change palatalized the initial sk- cluster to sh-, so the inherited word became scyrte — the ancestor of modern shirt, the upper-body garment.
  • In Old Norse, by contrast, the hard sk- pronunciation was preserved: skyrta meant "shirt, tunic."
  • When Vikings settled northern England during the Danelaw (9th–11th centuries), Norse skyrta was borrowed into northern Middle English as skirt.
  • English thus kept two descendants of the same Germanic word, splitting them by function: native shirt = upper garment; Norse-loaned skirt = lower, hanging garment or hem. This is one of the clearest examples of an etymological doublet produced by Viking settlement.

Middle English: The Hanging Garment

  • In the 13th–14th centuries skirt named the lower part of a tunic, coat, or robe — the part that hangs from the waist.
  • It applied to both men's and women's clothing; the modern sense of a woman's outer garment became dominant only later.
  • The word's root meaning "cut short" faded entirely; what remained was the visible, hanging border of cloth.

The Spatial Metaphor: The Skirts of the Land

  • By the late 16th century, Elizabethan and Stuart writers transferred the garment image to geography: the "skirts" of a forest, town, hill, or shore are its outer borders, exactly as a hem hangs around the body of a garment.
  • Shakespeare uses the spatial sense; it was standard poetic usage for centuries: "the skirts of the wood," "the skirts of the city."
  • The source sentence — "camping on the skirts of the forest" — is a direct heir of this 400-year-old metaphorical tradition.

The Verb: Skirting the Edge

  • In the 17th–18th centuries the noun produced a verb: to skirt a place is to move along its edge, keeping to the border rather than crossing the middle.
  • By the 19th century the verb took its figurative form: to skirt a subject or an issue is to move around its edge, avoiding its heart — to deal with something indirectly, half-engaging without committing.
  • The modern idioms "skirt the issue," "skirting around the truth," and "skirt the law" all descend from this edge-traveling image.

Sound Changes

  • PIE *sk- remained a hard sk- cluster in Norse and in the English loan, giving /sk/ (unlike native shirt, whose sk- palatalized to /ʃ/).
  • The Norse vowel -yr- (a rounded front vowel, written y) was nativized to English short /ɪ/ (as in "skirt" /skɜːt/ in RP after the later vowel shifts).
  • The plural -s is the regular English plural ending; the final cluster /-ts/ is standard.

Modern Usage and Register

  • Today the garment sense dominates; the spatial sense survives mainly in literary prose and in the compound outskirts.
  • "The skirts of the forest/town" is now poetic or dated, but remains vivid and perfectly comprehensible.
  • Skirt steak (a long, thin beef cut) and skirting boards (baseboards) preserve the "hanging strip" image in technical vocabulary.

Summary

skirts travels from an Indo-European root for cutting, through a Germanic adjective for "short," a Norse word for a cut garment, and a Danelaw loan that split from native shirt, before its hanging-hem image was transferred to the borders of forests and towns and then to the evasive verb "to skirt an issue."
