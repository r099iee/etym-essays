• Anglii: The Latin name used by Tacitus; it is the same people as seen through Roman eyes, but the form is Latin and the reference may be to an earlier, broader group.
  • Engle: The Old English name for the Angles; it is the direct descendant of the tribal name and the source of English.
  • Anglian: The adjective for the Angles or their dialects; it differs in being descriptive rather than naming the people.
  • Anglo-Saxons: The conventional later name for the mixed Germanic peoples of England; it is broader, includes Saxons and Jutes, and is a modern analytical label rather than a self-designation.
  • Saxons: The neighbouring Germanic people who settled southern and eastern Britain; they were partners in the migration but supplied a different name, which survives in Sussex, Essex and Wessex.
  • Jutes: The third people named by Bede, from Jutland; they settled Kent and the Isle of Wight, and are far fewer in the record.
  • Frisians: A North Sea Germanic people often associated with the migration; they are linguistically close but are not the Angles.
  • English: The modern nation and language descended from the Angles’ name; it is the collective successor term, not the tribe itself.
  • Germanic tribes: The wider category to which the Angles belong; it is a linguistic and cultural classification, not a political unit.
  • Teutons: An older synonym for Germanic peoples, now archaic and associated with nineteenth-century racial writing; it is much broader than Angles.
  • Angeln: The district in Schleswig from which the Angles are said to come; it is a place name, not the people.
  • Anglia: The Latin name for England and for East Anglia; it is territorial and ecclesiastical, whereas Angles names the people.
  • Anglophone: A modern term for English-speakers; it derives from the same root but describes language use, not descent.
