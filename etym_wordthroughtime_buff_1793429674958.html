- 16th c. — The Warrior's Armor: Borrowed from French buffle (‘wild ox’); designates thick, oil-dressed leather prized for military jerkins and coats capable of turning sword cuts and arrows.
- 17th c. — The Color of War and the Naked Body: The pale yellowish-tan shade of the leather becomes an established color adjective; simultaneously, the resemblance between smooth tanned hide and human flesh births the idiom in the buff for total nudity.
- 18th c. — Regimental Identity and Politics: Becomes the signature uniform facing of elite regiments (the British ‘Buffs’ and American Continental Army uniforms of blue and buff); symbolizes Whig political allegiance in Britain.
- 19th c. — Fire Engines and the Devoted Follower: Volunteer firefighters in New York City wear buff-colored gear; enthusiastic citizens who chase the pumpers are dubbed fire buffs, planting the seed for the modern enthusiast sense.
- Early to Mid-20th c. — Industrial Polishing and Mass Culture: The mechanical buffing wheel becomes ubiquitous in automotive and metal finishing; journalism generalizes buff from fire chasers to enthusiasts of all stripes (movie buff, jazz buff).
- Late 20th c. to Present — Bodybuilding and Digital Gaming: Slang re-appropriates buff to mean muscularly chiseled and athletic; simultaneously, online multiplayer games establish to buff as the universal term for enhancing a character's power or attributes.
