MULE — single linear chain (originally a non-Indo-European loan; the earliest stage is unknown and is stated explicitly).
1. Stage unknown / not reconstructable: a pre-Roman Mediterranean or Aegean substrate source (no attested form). No PIE etymon can be reconstructed; de Vaan treats Latin mūlus as a probable non-IE borrowing. A link with Greek mýklos (μύκλος), "lewd beast; mule," is sometimes proposed but is uncertain.
2. Latin mūlus (masc.) "mule, hinny" / mūla (fem.) "she-mule" — attested from Plautus, early 2nd c. BCE.
3. Old English mūl "mule" — 8th c., an early Latin loan (the native Germanic line).
4. Middle English mule — c. 1300, converging with the Anglo-Norman re-borrowing of Old French mul / mule (from the same Latin word).
5. Modern English mule — present day.
Note on nuance: two streams (direct Latin → Old English, and Latin → Old French → Anglo-Norman) converge in Middle English, so the attested record shows a single modern form fed by both channels.

LATTERDAY — a compound of latter + day; each element has an independent chain.

LATTER chain:
1. PIE *lē- (also cited as *lēd-; Pokorny) "to let go, slacken, grow weary" — cf. Greek ληδεῖν (lēdeîn) "to be weary" (glossed in Hesychius), Latin lassus "weary" (→ lassitude), Lithuanian lėtas "slow, calm".
2. Proto-Germanic *lataz "slow, lazy, late" — the "slackening" reinterpreted as lateness; cf. Gothic lats "lazy", Old Norse latr "lazy, sluggish", Old High German laz "slow, weak".
3. Old English læt "slow, slack, late".
4. Old English lætra "later, the second of two" (comparative of læt; cf. Gothic laþiza).
5. Middle English lattere.
6. English latter.
7. latter + day → latterday / latter-day (adjective, 19th c.; fixed in Latter-day Saints, 1830).

DAY chain:
1. PIE *dʰegʷʰ- "to burn, be hot" — traditional source of Germanic "day" as "the burning time (of the sun)"; the equation is standard but not beyond dispute (OED notes the uncertainty). Cf. Vedic Sanskrit dáhati "burns", dāha "burning heat" (→ Hindi/Odia dāh), Lithuanian dègti "to burn", Old Irish daig "fire".
2. Proto-Germanic *dagaz "day".
3. Old English dæġ "day".
4. Middle English day.
5. English day.
6. latter + day → latterday / latter-day (adjective, 19th c.; the general sense "modern, recent" from the 1840s).
Note: Latin diēs "day" descends from a different root (*dyew-, "sky"), so the Romance day-words are not cognates of English "day" in this chain.
