TRUDGE enters the written record in the 1540s, first in Scots and the northern dialects of English as trudgen, 'to walk with laborious, heavy effort'. Its ancestry is opaque in a way that delights and frustrates etymologists: unlike most verbs of motion, it cannot be carried back to any Proto-Indo-European root, and the OED classifies it as an expressive or echoic formation — a word, that is, whose sound enacts its meaning. The sequence of sounds in trudge reproduces the rhythm of a heavy footfall with remarkable fidelity: the voiceless dental onset /tr/, the jolting voiced affricate /dʒ/ at the heart of the syllable, and the same affricate closing the word, each articulation a small muscular effort that lands like a boot on hard ground, while the short, dull vowel has none of the brightness of skip or the length of stride — it is the sound of a step that has no spring in it. Such echoic words are common coin across the world's languages, but they are individually coined, not inherited, and that is why trudge's nearest relatives are its Scandinavian look-alikes — Norwegian truga and trudja, Swedish trudaska, 'to tramp or walk heavily' — which are parallel imitative creations, not descendants of a common ancestor. No attested parent links them, and none is needed: heavy walking sounds heavy in every language. A more seductive connection has long been drawn with tread, Old English tredan, 'to step, trample', and the kinship of sound is undeniable — trudge, tread, trade, trod. But tread descends regularly from the Proto-Indo-European root *dreh₂-, 'to run, step', the ancestor also of German treten, Dutch treden, Old Norse troða, and the English cluster trade, treadmill, treadle, and trod; and trudge shows none of the sound-changes that would make it that root's regular reflex. The tr- and the -dʒ simply do not correspond by rule; the resemblance is expressive coincidence, at most a borrowed or blended flavour. Etymology, at its best, refuses the seduction: it records that trudge lacks the regular developments of the *dreh₂- lineage and leaves the connection as a resemblance, not a pedigree. The word's life in English, once recorded, is a steady expansion from northern dialect to standard literary vocabulary:
  • 1540s — trudgen appears in Scots and northern English meaning 'to walk with laborious effort'; a coarse, everyday verb of the common traveller.
  • 16th century — trudge spreads into literary English; Spenser uses it, and it settles into the sense of travelling laboriously on foot, often through mud or over long distance.
  • 17th-18th century — the verb is common in the plain prose of travel, war, and labour; 'a trudge' (the noun) joins it in the early 19th century for a wearisome walk.
  • 19th-20th century — the word deepens its note of grim perseverance and begins its figurative extension: trudging through chores, paperwork, and dull days, the effort always out of proportion to the ground covered.
  • Contemporary — TRUDGE is still informal and vivid, at once physical and moral: a slow, heavy, effortful gait carrying an undertone of unbroken will, exactly the 'tighten our belts and trudge on' of Tolkien's dwarves.
Semantically the word has barely drifted in nearly five centuries; what has changed is the weight of the connotation. The sixteenth-century trudger was merely walking badly; the modern trudger is enduring, and the word has absorbed the grim, unbroken resolve of the refugee, the exile, and the famished company. In The Hobbit, Gandalf's trudge-on is that same old word in full working order: the jolting consonants hammer out the heavy steps of dwarves and one hobbit refusing, mile by mile, to become supper, and the verb's very soundlessness of pedigree — an echoic coin with no grand inheritance — suits a scene stripped of magic, where survival is a matter of putting one boot after another.
