- Old High German fleisk (8th–11th c.) — a concrete mass noun for living flesh, the body and meat, with no sharp division between anatomy and food.
- Middle High German vleisch (11th–14th c.) — courtly and mystical writers pair it with blood in the formula vleisch und bluot, while urban markets use it for butchered meat.
- Early New High German and Luther (16th c.) — das Wort ward Fleisch gives the word its highest theological charge, even as the food sense becomes the everyday default.
- 18th–19th c. — lexicography and industrial butchery classify Fleisch as edible soft tissue; compounds multiply and the butcher's trade becomes a civic institution.
- 20th c. — rationing, meatless days and postwar mass consumption turn Fleisch into an object of prosperity, scarcity and political management.
- 21st c. — Fleischersatz, Fleischsteuer and Laborfleisch make the word a marker of ethical and environmental debate, while its neutral culinary core remains.
