- Medieval English - "amble" came into English around 1300 as a horseman's term from Old French "ambler": the smooth lateral four-beat gait in which both legs on the same side move together, carrying the rider without the jolting of a trot — the gait of comfort for medieval travellers, prized on the roads of Chaucer's England.
- 16th century - the word broadened from the stable to the street: to amble was to walk or move at leisure, the comfort of the ride coming to overshadow its speed.
- Modern English - "ambling" is simply walking or riding at a slow, relaxed, easy pace with no destination pressing, and it still keeps a trace of that smooth, steady, rolling motion of the horse.
