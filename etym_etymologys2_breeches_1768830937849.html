PIE *bhreg- "to break, to split" (c. 4000-3000 BCE) — the break-root; in Italic it produced Latin frangere "to break" (whence fragment, fracture, fragile, frail, infringe, refract), and in Germanic it produced Proto-Germanic *brekaną "to break" (whence English break, breach) ↓ Proto-Germanic *brekaną "to break" (c. 500 BCE - 200 CE) — the Germanic verbal family ↓ Proto-Germanic *brōkiz "breeches — the split garment" (c. 500 BCE - 200 CE) — the specialized noun naming the garment that covers the fork where the body divides, built on the break/split root; the Germanic family includes Old Norse brók, Dutch broek (still "trousers"), German Bruch ↓ Old English brēc "breeches" (7th-11th c.), with plural brēċ "the two leg-coverings" — the plural was already the natural form for a garment of two legs ↓ Middle English brech / breches (12th-15th c.) — the old plural brēċ no longer recognized as a plural, so Middle English re-pluralized it with -es, producing the double plural breches ↓ breeches (Modern English) — the double plural became the standard form of the garment word; the reanalyzed singular breech went its own technical way into anatomy (breech-birth) and firearms (breech-loading), and the dialectal variant britches (18th c.) survives colloquially Note on the debated Celtic parallel: the Gauls had their own garment word, Gaulish *bracā, which Latin borrowed as bracae "trousers" (Roman writers treated the Gallic trouser as a barbarian garment), and which produced Italian brache and Old French braies. Whether the Germanic *brōkiz and the Celtic *bracā are independent creations from the same PIE "break/split" root or the result of early borrowing between the two families is debated; the forms are close, and most authorities treat the two lines as parallel rather than directly related to the English word, whose descent is Germanic.
