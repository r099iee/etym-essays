Tydides (also spelled Tydides, Tudeides) is the Homeric patronymic meaning son of Tydeus, used as a standing epithet for Diomedes, king of Argos, one of the principal Achaean heroes of the Iliad. When Pope, Chapman, or Ruskin write Tydides, they mean Diomedes himself, invoked by his lineage rather than his personal name. The form emphasises inherited valour: he is the son worthy of his ferocious father Tydeus, one of the Seven Against Thebes.

In the Iliad Diomedes called Tydides is second only to Achilles and Ajax in martial prowess. His aristeia fills Book 5, where, strengthened by Athena, he wounds the gods Aphrodite and Ares, routs the Trojans, and duels Glaucus before exchanging armour in guest-friendship. In Book 6 he meets Glaucus; in Book 10 he and Odysseus conduct the night-spying Doloneia; in Book 23 he wins the chariot race at Patroclus's funeral games. Throughout, Homer alternates Diomedes with Tydides for metrical and honorific variety. The source sentence recalls exactly this dominance: in a child's memory Tydides or Idomeneus always killed twenty Trojans to other people's one — a boy's статистика of heroic disproportion, not a literal count, but a true impression that the chiefs monopolise killing.

  • Core literary sense: Diomedes son of Tydeus: the Argive king, son of Tydeus and Deipyle, husband of Aegialeia, leader of eighty ships, famed for courage, piety, and counsel. E.g. valiant Tydides in Pope. This is the only dictionary head sense; the word has no common-noun meaning.
  • Patronymic convention sense: Tydides exemplifies the Homeric system where heroes are routinely named by filiation — Atreides for Agamemnon/Menelaus, Peleides for Achilles, Laertiades for Odysseus. To know Tydides is to know how Homeric naming encodes legitimacy, memory, and metre. Teachers still cite it alongside Atreides as the clearest example.
  • Ruskinian childhood-hero sense: in John Ruskin's Praeterita (the source of this card), Tydides stands for the boy's ideal of kingship: kings do harder work than anybody else, kill more, spear more salmon (via Scott's Redgauntlet), and take less. Ruskin explicitly says Homer and Scott taught him strange, now obsolete ideas about kings as hardest workers. Tydides is thus evidence for Victorian reception — how Pope's Homer shaped a child's moral imagination.
  • No modern extended or metaphoric senses: unlike Trojan (horse, malware, condom) or Ulysses (wanderer), Tydides has never broadened into jargon, slang, or product name. Dictionaries mark it poet. and hist. It appears only in translations, commentaries, classical dictionaries, and discussions of Ruskin. The semantic bridge stops at allusion: to call someone a Tydides would mean a second Diomedes, a valiant young son worthy of his father, but this is vanishingly rare and self-consciously learned.
  • Orthographic and pronunciation notes: capitalised always; pronounced in RP traditionally /ti-DAY-deez/. The plural is not used; for sons collectively one would say Tydidae only in pedantic Greek contexts. Confusion with Tydeus (father) versus Tydides (son) is the common learner error the card guards against.

In sum, to master Tydides is to master one node of Homeric genealogy: Tydeus begat Diomedes, and English poetry remembers the son by the father's name.
