In the 1982 preface to A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language, Randolph Quirk writes that the volume 'permits of no such convenient premonitory paradigm.' The word carries the whole point of the sentence. A paradigm is a model or pattern; a premonitory paradigm would be a model that gives advance notice — a template that could foretell its own ideal reader before that reader ever opened the book. Quirk is saying, with scholarly self-deprecation, that no such tidy, self-advertising formula exists: this grammar cannot conveniently announce in advance whom it is for. Here the adjective is used in its oldest, most neutral sense, 'serving to warn or indicate beforehand,' with no implication that the warning is evil. The tone is dignified, courteous, and faintly playful, perfectly pitched for a scholar conceding the limits of his own preface.

Polysemy: the senses of PREMONITORY
  • Sense 1 — advance notice (primary, neutral): 'Serving as an advance warning; giving notice or signs beforehand of something to come.' This is the sense the word has had since it entered English in the 1580s: any outward sign, symptom, or signal that speaks before the event. A premonitory tremor, a premonitory symptom, a premonitory hint. Whether what is foretold is welcome or unwelcome is left open; the word only promises that notice is given.
  • Sense 2 — ominous foreshadowing (modern default): Under the pull of the noun 'premonition,' the adjective darkened from the later 19th century until, in ordinary modern use, it now usually implies something unwelcome or even catastrophic — a premonitory chill, a premonitory sense of doom, a premonitory darkness before disaster. The historical bridge is association: a premonition is most often felt as dread, so the adjective that once merely meant 'forewarning' picked up the dread too.
  • Sense 3 — medical/clinical (technical): In medicine, 'premonitory' is a precise technical word for signs or symptoms that precede and herald an attack or a disease: a premonitory symptom of migraine, a premonitory aura before an epileptic seizure, premonitory labour pains. In the clinic the word is value-neutral — it is a physiological leading indicator, not a prophecy.
  • Sense 4 — abstract/metaphoric 'leading indicator': By further extension the word applies to any index, datum, or pattern that functions as a leading indicator of a later state: premonitory economic signals, a premonitory silence before a collapse, a premonitory paradigm. The bridge is one of structure — anything that stands before and points forward.

The semantic boundary: Premonitory sits among a cluster of prophecy-words but is set apart from each. Prophetic implies supernatural or divinely inspired revelation; premonitory needs no divinity, only a sign. Ominous and portentous both foresee something evil, and portentous adds a pompous, self-important flavour that premonitory entirely lacks. Foreboding belongs to inner feeling rather than outer signs — a foreboding is felt, not read. Prognostic is the clinical word for a doctor's forecast, chiefly medical. Augural and oracular belong to formal augury and oracle speech. Precursory and anticipatory name the mere fact of preceding or coming before, without the 'warning' element that is the heart of premonitory. Only premonitory combines 'beforehand' and 'warning' into a single adjective that can be used neutrally in scholarly prose — as Quirk uses it — while still, in everyday speech, carrying its modern tinge of approaching trouble.
