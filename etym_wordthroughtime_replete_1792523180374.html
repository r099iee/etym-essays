- [Late 14th c. Late Middle English] — Borrowed from Anglo-Norman and Old French replet, primarily denoting physical bodily satiation, gluttonous overeating, or corpulence resulting from indulgence in feasting - [perception: associated directly with digestion, dietary fullness, and physical sluggishness]
- [15th–16th c. Renaissance & Tudor Era] — Expanded into medical and scholastic treatises to describe the human body when congested or overloaded with bodily humors (plethora), while beginning to acquire positive metaphorical senses of moral or spiritual abundance - [perception: viewed ambiguously as both dangerous physiological engorgement and generous, divine plenitude]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Neoclassical English] — Adopted extensively in literary, historical, and rhetorical prose to denote an intellectual work, library, or landscape richly furnished with goods, ideas, or artistic ornaments; solidified the prepositional collocation "replete with" - [perception: highly esteemed as an elegant, Latinate signifier of scholarly thoroughness and cultural richness]
- [19th c. Victorian Literature] — Contrasted deliberately against hunger, deprivation, and asceticism in industrial fiction (e.g., Dickens, Thackeray), depicting domestic comfort and postprandial contentment, as well as descriptive flourishes of sensory richness - [perception: evocative of bourgeois comfort, hospitable abundance, and leisurely dining]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Technical English] — Specialized in biology and entomology to designate honey-storing ants ("repletes") and in medical physiology to denote rehydration/electrolyte balance ("fluid repletion"), while retaining high-register journalistic currency to indicate comprehensive coverage or saturation - [perception: refined, educated adjective denoting complete material or intellectual fullness without vulgarity]
