- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed via Anglo-Norman from Old French *essorer* (derived from Vulgar Latin *exaurāre*, from *aura* 'breeze'), originally a specialized falconry term meaning to expose a hunting hawk to the wind or allow a bird to rise high on air currents.
- 15th to 16th Century (Renaissance & Elizabethan Era) - Liberated from technical falconry into general poetic discourse (Geoffrey Chaucer, Edmund Spenser, William Shakespeare), signifying majestic flight, lofty political ambition, and transcendent poetic invention.
- 17th to 18th Century (Augustan & Classical Eras) - Solidified in philosophical, religious, and architectural prose (John Milton, Alexander Pope) to represent the flight of the soul in divine contemplation and the vertical majesty of cathedral domes and spires.
- 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Cherished by Romantic poets (Percy Bysshe Shelley, William Wordsworth) to symbolize sublime freedom, ethereal transcendence, and pyrotechnic wonder (as in Oscar Wilde's innocent Fireballoon), while beginning to acquire economic connotations of rising prices.
- 20th to 21st Century (Modern Aviation, Finance, & Media) - Universalized in aeronautics (unpowered glider soaring, thermal soaring) and global financial journalism (soaring markets, soaring inflation, soaring temperatures).
