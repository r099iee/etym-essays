The chain for wobble is given below. The Indo-European link is tentative: the earliest stage may instead be an independent Germanic expressive formation whose wobbly phonetics imitate the motion it names.

  • PIE *webh- "to weave, to move to and fro" (reconstructed; the semantic primitive of threads interlacing and moving, from which the idea of quick oscillating motion could be abstracted). This attribution (as proposed in Watkins) is plausible but not certain. Stage-count note: the attested record begins later; the PIE stage is a reconstruction, not an attested form.
  • Proto-Germanic *wab- "to move back and forth" (reconstructed frequentative base; from *webh- with the oscillation sense, or possibly independently expressive). From this base the family splits into two branches: Old Norse vafla "to hover about, totter" and its parallel vafra "to move unsteadily, waver" (which gives English waver), and the Continental Low German form that gives wobble.
  • Low German wabbeln "to wobble, to move unsteadily from side to side" (a frequentative verb in -eln, expressing repeated small actions; attested in the Low German dialect area in the early modern period).
  • Early Modern English wabble "to move unsteadily from side to side" (recorded 1650s, borrowed from Low German wabbeln; the spelling with a).
  • Modern English wobble "to rock unsteadily on a loose pivot" (the -o- spelling prevailing by 1851, under the influence of the noun and of cognate forms; the noun wobble "an unsteady rocking motion" is recorded from 1690, and wobbly the adjective from the 1850s).
  • Modern English WOBBLE (verb; and the base of the participle/gerund wobbling). The Tolkien sentence uses the plain verb form: "it makes their legs wobble".

Debate / nuance note: (1) The PIE root *webh- is accepted by some authorities (Watkins) but disputed; many etymologists treat wobble as an independent Germanic expressive creation whose repeated, wobbly phonetics imitate the motion, with no secure Indo-European ancestry. (2) The exact route into English — direct from Low German, or via Dutch or a Scandinavian form — is not fully pinned down; the family is shared across North Sea Germanic (English wabble, Dutch wabbelen, German wabbeln). (3) The sibling form waver descends from Old Norse vafra and shows the same root's oscillation sense; it is a cousin, not a stage, of wobble.
