- 13th to 14th Century (Late Middle Ages) - Arabic *qazz* (raw silk) and the Palestinian manufacturing city of Gaza (*Gazza*) give rise to Old French *gaze*, designating an exquisite, transparent silk veil imported through Mediterranean maritime trade.
- Late 16th Century (c. 1580–1600) - Borrowed into Elizabethan English as *gaze* / *gauze*, describing sheer silk ribbons, headscarves, and delicate decorative neckwear worn by court ladies.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - Woven gauze manufacturing spreads across Britain (notably Paisley, Scotland); naturalists adopt the word to describe the transparent, veined wings of dragonflies, damselflies, and moths.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Joseph Lister’s antiseptic surgical revolution adopts carbolized open-weave cotton *gauze* as the global standard for surgical wound dressings; Oscar Wilde employs it to describe shimmering dragon-fly wings.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains dual dominance: ubiquitous surgical and medical dressing in hospitals worldwide, and evocative descriptor in fashion, stage lighting (*theatrical gauze*), and atmospheric literature.
