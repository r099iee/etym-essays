commendation: formal praise, approval, or a recommendation of someone or something; an official expression of esteem. Primary sense — formal praise and approval. A commendation is an explicit, usually formal statement that someone or something is worthy of praise: "she received a letter of commendation," "his bravery earned him a commendation from the chief." Unlike casual praise, a commendation has institutional weight — it is the kind of approval that is spoken publicly, written into a letter, or issued by an authority. In Austen's passage, the sisters' pronouncement of Jane as "a sweet girl" is the commendation that "authorizes" Bingley to think of her as he chooses: their public endorsement carries social force. The entrusting root (historical sense). The word's Latin ancestor commendare meant first "to entrust, commit to someone's care," and only later "to speak well of." This older sense survives in the verb commend in such uses as "commend my soul to God" (entrust) and in the phrase "commend to your care." The modern "praise" sense grew directly out of this: to entrust a friend to another's protection required vouching for their character — entrustment generated recommendation, and recommendation generated praise. Secondary sense — a formal award or citation. In modern institutional usage, especially military and governmental, a commendation is an official citation for meritorious action: a "letter of commendation," a "commendation medal," a "commendation for bravery." This is the dominant formal sense today, carrying the weight of an honour that is recorded and official rather than merely warm words. Full polysemy map:
  • Formal praise or approval; a recommendation — general modern sense.
  • An official award or citation (military, government, academic) — institutional sense.
  • Historical: an act of entrusting or committing to care — Latin-root sense, now chiefly in the verb commend.
  • A letter or document of introduction or recommendation — historical-technical sense.
  • Ecclesiastical: the "Commendation" of a soul to God in the burial liturgy — religious-technical sense.
The semantic bridge. PIE "hand" + "give" → Latin mandare "put in someone's hand, entrust" → commendare "entrust completely." Because entrusting a person to another's care demanded vouching for them, the word's centre of gravity slid from "handing over" to "speaking well of" — a classic shift from action to speech, from custody to praise. The modern commendation is the fossil of that Roman gesture: to praise is still, etymologically, to put someone into good hands. Register and collocation:
  • "Letter of commendation" — formal, institutional.
  • "Earn/receive a commendation" — award context.
  • "With commendation" / "worthy of commendation" — formal register.
  • Related verbs: commend (praise; entrust), recommend (the com- prefix collapsed to re- in Old French), mandate, command, demand (same root mandare).
  • Nuance: a commendation is more official than praise, more specific than approval, and typically uttered or written by someone with standing to judge.
