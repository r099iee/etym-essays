“Squeak” entered English as an echoic word for the short, thin, high-pitched sound — the cry of a mouse, the noise of a hinge, the sharp note of something small under pressure.
“Squeak” through the 18th–19th centuries extended from objects to voices: to squeak is to speak in a small, strained, undignified tone, and “a squeak” became a close call or narrow escape.
“Squeak” in the 20th century broadened into modern idioms — “the squeaky wheel gets the grease,” “not a squeak” (not a single sound) — keeping the small, sharp, high note in everyday speech.
“Squeaked” today is the past form of that brief, high, thin cry — a mouse squeaking, a nervous voice squeaking out a protest — comic and slightly humiliating on a human speaker, exactly the sound of a hobbit’s objections coming out mouse-high.
