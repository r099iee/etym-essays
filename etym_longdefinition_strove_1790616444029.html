Strove is the simple past tense, and historically also a past participle, of the verb to strive, meaning to make great efforts, to struggle vigorously, to contend with difficulty in order to achieve something, or to fight against an opponent or obstacle. To say she strove for excellence, they strove against oppression, or it strove for voice and rhythm, as in the source sentence, is to attribute earnest, sustained, almost strenuous exertion, not casual trying but labouring with the whole will against resistance. The word carries effortful dignity, suggesting serious purpose, obstacle, and moral energy rather than mere attempt.

Grammatically strove belongs to the strong-verb alternation strive, strove, striven, modelled on drive, drove, driven and thrive, throve, thriven, though modern usage increasingly accepts strived as regular past for the non-literal senses. Dictionaries list strove as preferred past tense in literary style and striven as preferred participle, as in she has striven for years, while strove as participle, though Shakespearean, is now marked archaic. The form is intransitive with prepositional complement, to strive for a prize, to strive against fate, to strive toward perfection, to strive with conscience, and rarely transitive in strive battle. Tense nuance matters, strove places effort in completed past time with narrative weight, whereas was striving stresses duration and has striven stresses accumulated result.

Beyond literal exertion, strove has layered literary, moral, and competitive senses. In older English to strive could mean to quarrel, to vie, or to contend in battle, as in knights strove together, winds strove with waves, where effort is interpersonal struggle rather than solitary aspiration. That agonistic sense survives in to strive with or against, and in the noun strife. In modern self-help, educational, and sporting discourse strove conveys aspirational striving, striving for clarity, for justice, for voice, a positive, almost reverent effort toward an ideal. In theology and philosophy it carries existential weight, humans strive, and in striving fail and strive again, with connotations of noble incompleteness.

  • Earnest-effort sense: laboured hard toward a goal, e.g. she strove to finish on time, it strove for voice. Polysemy bridge: from physical contention with an enemy to inward contention with difficulty, transferring battlefield struggle to moral and artistic labour while retaining resistance.
  • Contentious-quarrel sense, archaic: fought or disputed, e.g. brothers strove over inheritance, winds strove. Polysemy bridge: original Frankish sense of quarrelling preserved in Middle English, later narrowed so that quarrel sense moved to the noun strife while verb kept effort sense.
  • Competitive-vying sense: contended for superiority, e.g. athletes strove for gold, firms strove for mastery. Polysemy bridge: via extension from fighting an enemy to competing for a prize, where opponent is rival rather than foe and victory is excellence rather than survival.
  • Aspirational-philosophical sense: yearned and laboured toward ideal, e.g. the mind strove for meaning, prose strove for rhythm. Polysemy bridge: via metaphor from muscular exertion to spiritual and aesthetic aspiration, where resistance is not a person but silence, wordlessness, or formlessness, as in the silent world striving for voice.

Stylistically strove is markedly literary and elevated compared with tried, attempted, or worked hard. Journalists write the team tried, novelists write she strove, and the choice signals seriousness, duration, and dignity. It collocates with for, against, toward and towards, after, and with infinitive to, and it favours subjects capable of will, people, communities, minds, and by personification texts, media, and silent worlds that strive as if alive. To say in a silent world it strove for voice and rhythm is thus to grant writing itself a will, a body straining toward utterance, making typography an act of moral effort rather than mechanical transcription.
