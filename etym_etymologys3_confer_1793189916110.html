The biography of confer is the biography of one of Latin's hardest-working verbs, imported into English with its whole toolkit intact. Latin conferre — com- (‘together’) fused with ferre (‘to bear, carry’) — meant, in order of business: to carry things to one place, to bring them together for comparison, to contribute one's share to a common stock, to consult by bringing counsels together, and to bestow by bringing a gift together with its recipient. English borrowed the verb in the 16th century and kept the two branches that mattered most — consulting and bestowing — while scholars preserved the comparing-sense in the immortal footnote abbreviation cf.

The root that carries the world: PIE *bher- (‘to bear, carry’) is among the most prolific roots in the language, the ancestor of bear, birth, bier, burden, bring (by a different grade), Latin ferre, Greek pherein, Sanskrit bharati — half the carrying-words of Eurasia. Latin ferre is famously irregular ( fero, ferre, tuli, latum — three stems in one verb), and its compounds inherit the irregularity: conferre suppletes its perfect from contuli and its participle from collatum. English felt none of this pain — it borrowed the infinitive stem only — but the relatives betray the family everywhere: transfer, prefer, defer, refer, infer, offer, suffer are all ferre-compounds wearing different prefixes, a whole parliament of carrying.

Prefix and sense-branching: the prefix com- (from PIE *kom, ‘beside, with, together’) does the semantic work. To carry together is to compare (things laid side by side), to consult (counsels laid side by side), to contribute (shares laid in the common stock), and to bestow (gift laid together with recipient). Cicero uses conferre for comparing texts and conferring benefits in the same oration; Livy uses it of consulting the senate; the Vulgate uses it of Mary's pondering (‘conferens in corde suo’ — bringing things together in her heart). Each Latin use licensed an English one, and the 16th-century borrowers — statesmen, divines, and scholars reading all three authors — took the full set.

English career and the cf. fossil: once naturalised, confer divided its labour by preposition: confer with for mutual counsel, confer on/upon for bestowal. The universities made the second branch ceremonial — degrees solemnly conferred in Latin, with caps, gowns, and laid-on hands — while sickrooms, courts, and cabinets kept the first. The comparing-sense retreated into scholarship but never died: every cf. in every footnote is the living Latin imperative confer (‘compare!’), ordering the reader to bring another passage alongside. Few verbs keep a two-thousand-year-old imperative alive in daily print.

Key stages and attestations:

  • PIE *bher- (reconstructed): ‘to bear, carry’ — one of the best-attested roots in the family, with reflexes from Ireland to India.
  • Latin ferre (classical): ‘to bear, carry, bring, endure’ — irregular suppletive verb (fero, tuli, latum); base of the whole -fer compound army.
  • Latin conferre (classical): ‘to bring together; compare; consult; contribute; bestow’ — Cicero, Livy, and the Vulgate attest the full branching.
  • Middle French conférer (14th–15th c.): the borrowing conduit — ‘to consult together; to grant’ — passing the verb to Tudor England.
  • Early Modern English confer (16th c.): both great branches plus the scholarly comparing-sense; divines confer, princes confer benefits, scholars confer texts.
  • 17th–18th c.: institutional capture — universities, crowns, and courts make confer the ceremonial verb of degrees, honours, and rights.
  • Modern: doctors confer, chancellors confer, footnotes command cf. — counsel, ceremony, and comparison in one unbroken tradition.

Sound shifts: the Latin stress accent fell on the second syllable (con-FER-re), and English keeps it (con-FER), which is why the vowel never reduced the way first-syllable verbs reduce. The spelling is conservative Latin via French: con- assimilated before f stays written confer (compare collate from the participle stem, where assimilation shows). The initial f of ferre continues PIE *bh- by the regular Italic devoicing — the same correspondence that pairs English bear (b-) with Latin ferre (f-) across the whole family.
