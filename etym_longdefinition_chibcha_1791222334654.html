Chibcha (also called Muisca or Mosca) denotes an Indigenous people of the high valleys around modern Bogotá and Tunja in Colombia, and by extension their extinct language and the wider Chibchan language family. In the source sentence the Chibcha Indians export rock salt from their barren highlands and import cereals, capturing their classic role as highland salt-miners and traders in a vertical Andean economy.

As an ethnonym, Chibcha refers to the pre-conquest Muisca Confederation — over 500,000 intensive farmers organized into two major states under the Zipa of Bacatá and the Zaque of Hunza plus lesser chiefs, with hereditary rulers, priests, weekly markets, pottery, cotton cloth, and renowned goldwork. They are famed for the El Dorado rite in which a gilded chief rafted on Lake Guatavita. Politically crushed in the 1530s, linguistically extinct by the 18th century, they were assimilated yet remain central to Colombian national identity.

  • People sense: A member of that highland people (plural Chibcha or Chibchas). Example: a Chibcha trader carrying salt cakes to exchange for maize. Britannica and Collins define this as the primary sense. Modern descendants identify as Muisca and campaign for recognition.
  • Language sense: The extinct Chibchan language of the Muisca (Muysccubun), banned in 1770, documented in 17th-century grammars, now revived in schools. Example: Chibcha numerals recorded by Humboldt. ISO 639-3 code chb, Glottolog chib1270, status extinct.
  • Language-family sense: Chibchan (or Chibcha) as a stock of ~20 languages from eastern Honduras through Panama, Costa Rica, Colombia and Venezuela: U'wa, Bari-Motilón, Kogi, Guaymí, Talamanca, etc. Linguists say Chibchan-speaking peoples to include all groups sharing descent, not just the Muisca.
  • Cultural / colloquial sense: In Colombia chibcha can mean native, homegrown, or quintessentially Colombian (music, food, speech), sometimes jocular. This is a semantic broadening from tribe to nation, parallel to Aztec → Mexican.

Domain notes: archaeology stresses intensive raised-field agriculture, causeways, and salt-boiling; ethnohistory stresses centralized tribute and alliance consolidation cut short by the Spanish; linguistics stresses SOV order, agglutination, and a vigesimal number system. The salt-for-cereals trade in the prompt is the textbook example of complementarity between cold highlands (salt, potatoes, cotton cloth) and warm lowlands (maize, coca, gold, feathers).

Polysemy bridges are transparent: people → their speech → all related speeches → national symbol. The bridge from tribe to language family reflects 19th-century comparative philology projecting one well-documented name onto newly discovered sisters. Capitalization distinguishes the proper ethnonym from rare lower-case generic uses. No unrelated homonym exists; even the deity Chibchacum (god of agriculture condemned to bear the earth) contains the same stem.
