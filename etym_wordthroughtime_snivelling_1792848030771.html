- Imitative roots — the nose speaks: Germanic sn- words mimic sniffing, snorting, and sneezing, and a verb for nasal dripping forms among them.
- Middle English — the cold and the cry: snivelen means to run at the nose and to weep with sniffles, equally of winter catarrh and nursery tears.
- 16th–17th c. — candles snivel too: chandlers say a guttering wick snivels as it weeps wax, and moralists find the hypocrite snivelling through sermons.
- 18th–19th c. — the schoolroom sneer: snivelling sneak becomes the stock insult for tell-tales and cowards, contempt fully baked into the participle.
- Dickensian — pathos and satire: snivelling clerks, orphans, and converts crowd Victorian pages, sometimes pitied, more often mocked.
- Today — colds and complaints: doctors still note a snivelling cold neutrally, while everyone else hears self-pity, and Gollum remains literature's great sniveller, dripping excuses between snarls.
