“Pork-pie” emerged in the 18th century as a sturdy market-town food: chopped seasoned pork and jellied stock sealed in a hand-raised hot-water crust, eaten cold and on the move.
“Pork-pie” through the 19th century became a democratic English staple — pub counters, picnics, railway platforms, and Boxing-Day buffets — the meat pie of the ordinary man rather than of the gentry table.
“Pork-pie” gained fame beyond the plate in the 20th century: the flat-crowned “pork-pie hat” of the 1930s and Cockney rhyming slang “porkies” (pork pies = lies) made the pastry’s name part of everyday idiom.
“Pork-pie” today has a protected regional hero in the Melton Mowbray pork pie, and the word still carries its homely, robustly English, thoroughly un-Elvish flavour.
