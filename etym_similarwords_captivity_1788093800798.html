• Imprisonment: Similar because both mean being confined against one's will; differs because imprisonment is specifically a judicially imposed sentence in a prison, while captivity is broader — it also covers prisoners of war, hostages, kidnap victims, and animals, and requires no sentence.
  • Incarceration: Similar because both denote formal confinement; differs because incarceration is the bureaucratic, statistical synonym of imprisonment — always institutional and legal — while captivity spans dungeons, camps, cages, and spiritual metaphors.
  • Confinement: Similar because both mean restriction of movement; differs because confinement is generic — a patient in confinement, historical "lying-in" at childbirth — and lacks the hostile "captured" overtone of captivity.
  • Detention: Similar because both mean being held against one's will; differs because detention is temporary and procedural (pre-trial, immigration, school), while captivity suggests prolonged or indefinite holding.
  • Internment: Similar because both are state-imposed confinement; differs because internment applies to groups (enemy aliens, wartime populations) under security policy, legally distinct from the individual capture of captivity.
  • Bondage: Similar because both mean being bound or enslaved; differs because bondage is archaic/literary and implies physical fetters or slavery proper, while captivity may be merely enclosed (a cage, a camp) without chains.
  • Servitude: Similar because both involve subjection to another's control; differs because servitude is about enforced labour and legal service status (indentured servitude), not necessarily enclosure.
  • Slavery: Similar because both mean being held against one's will; differs because slavery is the total condition of being owned as property — permanent and absolute — while captivity can be temporary and need not involve ownership.
  • Duress: Similar because both involve constraint against the will; differs because duress is an abstract legal concept of coercion (signing under duress), momentary and situational, not a state of being held.
  • Custody: Similar because both mean being held by authority; differs because custody is protective or procedural (police custody, child custody) and often temporary, without the capture-and-hold drama of captivity.
  • Restraint: Similar because both prevent free movement; differs because restraint is mechanical and limited (straps, ropes, self-restraint), describing the means rather than a state of life.
  • Thralldom: Similar because both mean bondage; differs because thralldom is archaic, literary, and strongly emotional ("held in thralldom") — rare in modern speech, where captivity is fully current.
  • Durance: Similar because both mean imprisonment; differs because durance is archaic poetic diction ("in durance vile") and nearly obsolete, while captivity is ordinary contemporary English.
  • House arrest: Similar because both confine a person to a place; differs because house arrest is a judicial sentence served at home — comparatively mild — while captivity usually involves being taken and held elsewhere against one's will.
