ROOT: indulgēre (Latin, attested from Plautus, early 2nd century BCE) "to be kind, complaisant; to concede, grant, yield" — ETYMOLOGY UNKNOWN: no accepted PIE etymon. The OED marks the origin obscure, Ernout–Meillet declare the etymology unknown, and de Vaan gives none. Structurally analysable as in- "on, in, towards" + *dulgē-, but that base has NO attested simplex in Latin; the root appears only in this compound. There are consequently no Sanskrit, Hindi, Germanic, or Slavic cognates — the word is Latin-only and was BORROWED into other languages (loans, not kin).

CHAIN (from the earliest attested form to our word):
1. Latin indulgēre "to be kind, complaisant; to concede, grant, yield" (attested in Plautus, c. 200 BCE, and throughout Classical Latin) — origin unknown, see note below.
2. Latin indulgēns, indulgent- (present participle) "kind, forbearing, complaisant" (Classical Latin).
3. Latin indulgēntia "kindness, forbearance; leniency" (Classical Latin); in Church Latin (2nd–5th centuries) extended to "remission of punishment for sin" — the source of the English noun indulgence and of the 1517 Reformation controversy.
4. Old French indulgence "forbearance, pardon" (11th–13th centuries) — borrowed into Middle English as indulgence (14th century).
5. French indulgent (adjective, 12th century) — borrowed into English as indulgent (c. 1600) < Latin indulgēns.
6. English indulgent + adverbial suffix -ly → indulgently (adverb, 17th century) — formed natively in English, not borrowed. ◄ OUR WORD. (Sister formations in English: indulge (v.) 1630s < Latin indulgēre; self-indulgence 18th century; self-indulgent, over-indulge.)

NOTE (debated origin): historic proposals, ALL unproven and rejected by modern dictionaries — (a) to PIE *dʰerǵʰ- "to make firm" (Sanskrit dṛṃhati दृंहति "makes firm", drḍha दृढ़ "firm"), phonologically problematic; (b) to Greek θέλγω thélgō "to charm, bewitch"; (c) to a root related to Latin longus "long". Because the verb has no known etymon, the honest record states the origin as unknown rather than forcing a chain. Modern Hindi/Sanskrit show only semantic parallels (e.g. kṣamā क्षमा "forgiveness, forbearance"), not cognates.
