Choked is the past tense and past participle of the verb choke, and also a fully adjectival form. In its core physical sense it means having the airway blocked so that breathing is cut off or difficult; from that root spring the mechanical sense (a passage stopped up), the emotional sense (speech or feeling suppressed), and the modern performance sense (failing under pressure).

Primary senses

  • Physically unable to breathe: of a person or animal whose windpipe is blocked by food, liquid, an object, or a narrowing — "he choked on a fish bone", "the child was choking". The adjective "choked" describes the person: gasping, face reddening, unable to draw breath.
  • Strangled or throttled: breathing cut off by external compression of the throat — "he was choked to death". This overlaps with strangulation but stresses the blockage of air rather than the instrument.
  • Blocked, stopped up, congested: of pipes, drains, channels, filters, or machinery — "a choked drain", "the engine was choked with soot", "a channel choked with vegetation". Here the breath metaphor is transferred to any narrow passage.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Overcome with emotion: "choked up", "choked with tears / anger / emotion / joy" — a voice cut off by strong feeling so that the words will not come. Physical blockage becomes a metaphor for emotional blockage.
  • Failed under pressure (colloquial, chiefly North American): in sport and exams, a person who "choked" was capable but froze and performed badly at the decisive moment — "he choked on the final putt", "she choked in the interview". A 20th-century extension of the sense of being constricted and unable to act.
  • Informal and intensified uses: "choked" (British informal) can mean bitterly disappointed or upset ("I was absolutely choked when we lost"); "choked off" (British) means annoyed, cut short, or silenced; "choke hold" is a wrestling and police neck restraint.
  • Technical senses: the noun "choke" names the valve that reduces the air supply to a petrol engine's carburettor ("pull the choke"), an inductor that blocks high-frequency current in electronics (an RF choke), and any constriction controlling flow in a system.

Polysemy: the semantic reasoning

The whole family is one continuous metaphor from the throat outward. The Old English verb ācēocian was tied to the jaw and throat (cēoce, "cheek, jaw"): choking was literally something "going to the jaw", caught in the mouth and windpipe. From the body, the notion of being blocked transferred to drains, pipes, channels, and engines in the early modern period; from a stopped engine it transferred to the valve that stops the air (the choke); and from physical blockage it transferred to the emotions (a choked voice, choked with feeling) and then, in the 20th century, to performance — a person frozen and unable to function at the crucial moment. Every modern sense is a migration of the same image: a passage that should be open has been closed.

Register and usage notes

  • "Choked" is common in everyday speech, journalism, and fiction; the stronger clinical synonym "asphyxiated" belongs to medicine and law.
  • The emotional sense is more frequent in British English as a synonym for "upset"; the performance sense is more frequent in North American sport and business.
  • As an adjective it is usually predicative ("I was choked") but occurs attributively too ("a choked sob", "a choked whisper").
