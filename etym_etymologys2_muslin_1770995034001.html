Non-PIE origin: muslin is a toponymic word — it is named after a city — so no Proto-Indo-European root chain exists. The linear chain runs from the Semitic Arabic root through the city name into the European trade vocabulary: - Arabic root w-ṣ-l (و-ص-ل) 'to connect, join, arrive' (Semitic, Afro-Asiatic; attested in Classical Arabic from the 7th c. CE). - Arabic verb waṣala 'to connect, join, arrive' (attested 7th c. CE). - Arabic mawṣil 'place of connection, junction, linking point' — the name of the city al-Mawṣil (Mosul) on the Tigris, a historic crossing-point and caravan hub (the city is attested from the 6th–7th c. CE; its name reflects its position linking the trade routes between Persia, India, and the Mediterranean). - Old Italian mussolo / mussolina 'fine cotton fabric brought through Mosul' (attested in Italian trade usage, 14th c.). - Italian mussola 'muslin' (modern Italian; mussolina is also used). - French mousseline 'muslin' (attested 16th c.). - English muslin 'lightweight cotton cloth' (borrowed from French mousseline, first attested in English 1603). Debate/nuance note: the root w-ṣ-l is Semitic, so the standard 'PIE to modern English' template does not apply to this word. There is also a historical irony to record: Mosul was the great transit market for these fabrics, not the main place of manufacture — the finest muslins were woven in Bengal (locally mulmul, 'fine cotton') — yet European commerce permanently attached the name of the market city to the cloth.
