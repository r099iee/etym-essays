Bated is the clipped or aphetic form of abated ("lessened, beaten down"), and at its root lies the Indo-European base *bhau- ("to strike, beat") — a reconstruction whose exact shape handbooks debate, but whose offspring ring across the ancient world. The primitive sense was purely physical: the striking of one thing against another, the blow, the beating of grain or flesh. In the Indo-Iranian branch this hardened into the Sanskrit root badh- ("to press, force, oppress, keep back"), which produced badha ("pressure, obstruction") and, in modern Hindi, badha ("obstacle") — already a shift from striking to holding back, the suppression of movement that will ultimately govern the English idiom.

The Italic branch supplies the direct ancestor of the English word. Latin had battuere / batuere ("to beat, strike"), a vigorous verb glossed by the grammarian Festus around the second century CE and sometimes suspected of being a Gaulish or Etruscan loan — an honest uncertainty that handbooks continue to record. In Vulgar Latin a prefixed form *abbattuere ("to beat down") gave Old French abatre / abattre, the ordinary word for felling a tree or knocking a thing down. Anglo-French carried this into England as abater, and Middle English abaten (thirteenth century) took the crucial figurative step: to abate came to mean not only to knock down but to lessen or diminish — a storm abates, enthusiasm abates, force abates. The physical blow had been translated into a reduction of intensity, and that reduction is the semantic engine of everything that follows.

It was from this well-attested abate that the clipped bate was cut, in the same aphetic fashion that gave English mend from amend and sport from disport: the unstressed initial syllable simply dropped in connected speech. The aphesis is the single most important sound-change in the word's biography, and its consequences shaped the modern form:

  • Aphesis (16th c.): the initial a- of abate was lost, leaving bate, a shorter verb and noun meaning the same thing — "to lessen, hold in, reduce"; "bate" was in ordinary use in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, often in the idiom of haggling ("bate me something of the price").
  • The fossilising phrase (1596): in The Merchant of Venice, Shylock says "with bated breath and whispering humbleness," and the phrase lodged in English as the fixed image of suspense — breath literally beaten down and held. The exact Shakespearean date is the anchor of the modern word's identity: before 1596 there is no "bated breath"; after it, the phrase never quite died.
  • The isolation of the participle: because the parent verb bate itself fell out of ordinary use, the participle bated was stranded; with its only companion gone, it attached itself to the single noun "breath," so that today the word survives almost exclusively in that one collocation.
  • Spelling and misanalysis: the very fossilisation produced the chronic misspelling "baited breath" — writers who no longer feel the link to abate hear the homophone bait and write a word that means nothing at all; the correct spelling preserves the etymology (the breath is beaten down, not baited).
  • The siblings of the root: the same root that gave bated also gave batter (Old French batre, "to beat repeatedly"), battle (Latin battuālia, "fencing practice"), and, on the native Germanic side, beat (Old English bēatan, from Proto-Germanic *bautan); the whole family is bound together by the ancient image of the blow.

The word's semantic arc is thus a perfect miniature of how language fossilises meaning. A PIE verb of striking passes into Latin, is beaten down into Old French abattre, is translated into English as the abstract verb abate ("to lessen"), is clipped to bate, is frozen by Shakespeare into a single poetic collocation, and finally survives — six millennia after the root's first blow — as an adjective whose every use still carries, invisibly, the image of breath struck down and held. That is precisely the image Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone needs: a household moving watchfully with its breath beaten down, subdued by the discipline of the great house.
