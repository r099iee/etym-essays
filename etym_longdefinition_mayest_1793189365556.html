Mayest is the archaic second-person singular present of the modal verb may: thou mayest means you may, addressed to a single familiar thou in the grammar of the King James Bible, Shakespeare, and the wonder tale. Like all modals, it never names an action of its own; it colours the action of the verb that follows — with permission, with possibility, with wish, or with purpose — and the -est ending dates that colouring to Early Modern English.

In the source sentence the magic horse lays down the law of the marvellous task: Thou mayest sow a measure of wheat, thou mayest wait till it is grown, harvest and thresh it, grind the grain, bake five ovens of bread — and only then shall pursuit succeed. Here mayest is the modal of prescribed condition, half permission and half requirement: thou art allowed to attempt the labour, and thou must perform it first. Doubled across the two clauses, the form gives the impossible errand the steady music of ordinance.

Core meaning in detail: Modern may covers three great modal territories — permission (you may go), possibility (it may rain), and purpose or wish (so that you may live, may you prosper) — and mayest covers exactly the same three wherever a thou subject governs it. What the ending adds is not meaning but world: solemnity, antiquity, and the intimacy-or-authority of thou address. A master may command with it, a horse may instruct with it, a liturgy may bless with it, and each time the grammar itself performs the oldness of the tale.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Permission — thou art allowed to (primary sense): thou mayest sow grants leave to act. The bridge from the root sense of power is social: what the strong can do, the authorized are allowed to do, so ability-words become leave-words.
  • Possibility — it may be that thou shalt (epistemic sense): thou mayest yet succeed admits the outcome as possible. The bridge runs from permission to contingency: what is permitted by circumstance is what may come to pass.
  • Purpose, result, or wish (subjunctive and optative senses): labour, that thou mayest eat — so that eating follows; mayest thou prosper — a blessing. The bridge is the old subjunctive life of the modal, surviving in prayers, proverbs, and tale-conditions.
  • Concession — thou mayest well object (rhetorical sense): granting the point before answering it. The bridge is dialectical politeness carried by the gentlest of modals: may concedes where must would confront.
  • Ability in older layers (archaic root sense): in the oldest English magan meant to be physically able — I can, in effect. The bridge is the original root itself: before may lent permission, it owned power, and traces survive wherever mayest sounds like canst.
  • Second-person archaic address (grammatical sense of the -est): mayest marks its subject as thou and its era as Early Modern English. The bridge is morphology: as hast is to have, so mayest is to may.

Register and connotation: Mayest is gentle grandeur. Of all the -est forms it is the least scolding and the most enabling: masters permit with it, horses instruct with it, blessings fall with it. Where shalt commands and must compels, mayest opens a door and points down the road. Modern ears hear scripture and story in it at once, which is why the wonder-tale horse — himself a donor-figure, a helper — speaks in exactly this modal: permission wrapped in prophecy.
