1. An Indo-European tree-name. ALDER belongs to one of the few ancient families of Indo-European tree-names that can be carried back to the proto-language. The base is usually reconstructed as PIE *h₂él- or *el-, glossed either directly as a tree-name or through the sense 'red, brown' — the colour of the alder's sap and wood. The reflexes are widespread and coherent: Latin alnus 'alder' (with an -s- extension), Slavic *olxa (Russian ol'kha, Czech olše), Baltic alksnis (Lithuanian), and Germanic *aluz-/*alizō — Old Norse ǫlr (and elrir), Dutch els, Old High German elira (modern German Erle), Old English alor. The precise laryngeal shape and the original colour-referent remain matters of debate in comparative linguistics; one school links the same base to the elk as 'the red-brown animal' (Etymonline and others reconstruct a PIE *el- 'red, brown' behind both names). The family is, in any case, genuinely proto-Indo-European — a tree-name older than any of its attested languages.

2. Germanic and Old English. The Germanic forms point to a feminine *alizō with n-stem and u-stem variants (*aluz-); Old English alor (alongside aler, eler) is attested from the earliest glosses, and the word entered English toponymy and onomastics early — alder place-names and the personal name use of the tree-word are both Anglo-Saxon. Old English used the one word for tree and timber alike: the alder of the marsh, the alder boards in a byre, the alder stake in stream-bank work. Vergil had already given the tree its most famous literary moment a thousand years earlier when the Georgics (I.136) says rivers 'first felt hollowed alders' — tunc alnos primum fluvii sensere cavatas — the first boats dug from alder trunks, a notice that fixed the tree as the shipwright's and boat-builder's wood in the classical tradition.

3. The intrusive -d- of Middle English. The modern spelling with medial -d- first appears in late Middle English: beside aller and alre, scribes begin to write alder in the fourteenth century. The -d- is excrescent — a purely phonetic flourish with no etymological warrant — and its exact cause is still discussed: plausible triggers are a transitional stop after the liquid in clusters, influence of genitive or oblique forms, or contamination from words of similar shape. Whatever the cause, the -d- became standard by the sixteenth century. The same process independently gave the berry-shrub elder its -d- (from Old English ellern, eller), which is why the two names — alder and elder — have been tangled in folk spelling and folk etymology ever since: both existed as aller/eller forms in the southern dialects.

4. A thousand years of waterlogged labour. In medieval and early modern England 'alder' was an unglamorous utility word. Its wood made clogs and clog-soles (the classic material of French sabots and of English clog-making), broom handles, bowls, boxes and barrels; its durable waterlogging made it the pile-wood of choice for bridges, wharves and waterlogged city foundations — the celebrated Venetian tradition of alder-and-elm piles is the most famous case, and the practice is recorded across Northern Europe. From the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries the charcoal trade prized alder, willow and hazel charcoals for gunpowder; dyers and tanners used the bark, and herbalists the astringent decoctions. In this long utilitarian phase the word simply meant 'wet-ground wood'; it appears in surveys, bailiffs' accounts and timber lists without any elevation.

5. Folklore, science and the modern rebrand. Nineteenth-century natural history placed the alder precisely within the birch family and mapped its wetland ecology; the twentieth century discovered the Frankia root-nodule nitrogen fixation (1920s–1960s), transforming the tree's image from 'common waterside weed-tree' into a tool of ecological engineering. Conservationists now restore alder carrs; river-buffer and climate-adaptive planting schemes specify alders deliberately. In the same decades, folklore scholarship recovered the tree's older glamour — the ogam letter fearn glossed 'alder,' the Irish 'blood' of the felled tree, the shield-wood of chiefs — and fantasy literature (the '_____ and Willow!' exclamation of the source card) fed the alder's romantic return as the signature tree of enchanted riversides. The word ALDER has never changed what it denotes in 1,400 years of English, but it has travelled from plain timber-term, to down-at-heel fen-tree, to sacred wood of folklore and ecological icon — one of the quietest, slowest reputation shifts in the lexicon.
