Single linear derivation chain for LITTER (one inherited root; the "trash", "brood", "cat litter" and "stretcher" senses are later developments of the same chain):
1. PIE *legh- — "to lie down, recline" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; also the root of English lie and lay, and of Greek lekhetron "couch, bed").
2. Latin lectus — "bed, couch" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE; from *legh-tos, the guttural cluster *-gʰt- hardening to Latin -ct-).
3. Late / Vulgar Latin *lectāria (also reconstructed as *lectuāria) — "bedding, that which is spread on a bed; straw for animals" (reconstructed; the base of the Romance descendants).
4. Old French litiere — "bed, bedding, straw; a portable couch or stretcher" (attested 12th–13th c.; from Latin *lectāria).
5. Middle English litere — "a portable couch; straw bedding" (attested c. 1300, borrowed from Anglo-Norman / Old French).
6. Modern English litter — "bedding; a group of young animals born at one birth (15th c.); scattered refuse or trash (18th–19th c.); absorbent cat-box material (20th c.); a stretcher or carrying-couch" (from c. 1300 onward; the current dominant sense is scattered public waste).
Notes on debated / nuanced stages: (a) The exact Latin etymon of Old French litiere is disputed between *lectāria and *lectuāria; Etymonline cites Medieval Latin lectaria "litter, bedding." (b) The "brood" sense arose in English by metonymy from the bedding on which sows, bitches, and she-cats give birth (late 15th c.), and the "trash" sense from the idea of straw and refuse scattered over a surface (18th–19th c.); both are English semantic developments, not separate etymologies.
