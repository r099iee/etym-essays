THE ROCK-CELERY OF THE GREEKS Parsley enters European history as a Greek compound: petroselinon, "rock-celery." The second element, sélinon, was the ancient Greek name for the parsley-like umbellifers — the plant we know as celery and its close relatives — already present in Homer, where the meadows of Calypso's island grow sélinon, the celery that Odysseus sees among the violets. The first element, pétros or pétrā, is "rock, stone": the wild ancestors of parsley and celery grow on rocky, stony ground, and the Greeks named the plant for its habitat, "the celery of the rocks." Both halves of the word carry scholarly debate. Sélinon is formed with a suffix (-in-) typical of Mediterranean plant names that are not securely Indo-European, and the standard older derivation from a root meaning "to press, to extract juice" is questioned by modern scholars such as Beekes, who suspect a Pre-Greek substrate word for the plant. Pétros likewise has been claimed for the Indo-European root *per-, "to strike through" (a rock as "that which is cleft or struck"), but a Pre-Greek origin has also been argued. The honest statement is that the compound is Greek, its habitat-reference certain, its deepest roots uncertain. FROM ATHENS TO ROME AND THE MONASTIC GARDEN The Greek compound travelled with Roman horticulture and medicine: Latin petroselinum is attested in Pliny, and the plant appears in Dioscorides' great herbal, where parsley is recommended as a diuretic and a stomach tonic — a medical reputation it carried for nearly two millennia. In late Latin the word was syncopated to petrosilium, the form behind the Romance words (French persil, Italian prezzemolo, Spanish perejil). It reached England early: Old English had petersilie already around the year 1000, in Ælfric's glossary, where it appears among the garden herbs the Anglo-Saxon clergy cultivated in their monastic gardens — the very route by which Mediterranean herbs spread across northern Europe. In Middle English the word was compressed and re-shaped by syncope and metathesis: percil, persely, parsely (recorded in the fourteenth century), until the modern spelling parsley settled in the sixteenth century. THE HERB OF THE KITCHEN GARDEN AND OF FOLK MEDICINE By the early modern period parsley had become a fixed inhabitant of the English kitchen garden, planted alongside lettuces, radishes, and beans — exactly the garden Beatrix Potter would give to Mr. McGregor. Its culinary value was doubled by its medicinal one: parsley was the carminative and digestive of the cottage pharmacy, chewed for the breath, brewed as parsley tea for the stomach, and applied to soothe aches. It is this folk register that explains Peter's instinct in the tale: "feeling rather sick, he went to look for some parsley." The herb is not a garnish there but a cure, the quiet antidote to a greedy rabbit's excess — a detail that only works because of the herb's centuries of digestive lore. SUPERSTITION AND SYMBOL Alongside its uses, parsley accumulated a striking folklore. Its notoriously slow and uncertain germination produced the belief that "parsley seed goes to the devil and back nine times" before it will sprout; transplanting the plant was held unlucky; and a withering parsley bed was an omen of death in a house. Such beliefs gave the mild herb a strange, half-sacred edge in the folk imagination, and they linger in gardening lore that parsley must be sown, not transplanted, and that it "belongs to the devil." THE MODERN GARNISH In the nineteenth century the rise of restaurant cuisine converted parsley into the emblematic garnish — the green sprig that finishes a plate — while the twentieth-century food trade split the herb into flat-leaf (Italian) parsley, prized by cooks for flavour, and curly parsley, preferred for decoration. The word itself, however, has never left its kitchen-garden home: parsley remains the plain name of a common herb, and in Potter's tale it still does what it has always done in the folk tradition — soothe a stomach and restore order after excess.
