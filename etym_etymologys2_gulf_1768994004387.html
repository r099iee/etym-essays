GULF (five-stage chain from Greek to English; the PIE stage is DEBATED — see note): 1. PIE *kʷelp- "to turn, to vault, to be hollow" — a proposed but not universally accepted reconstruction behind Greek kólpos "bosom, fold, hollow" (alternative: a Pre-Greek substrate word; see the debate note). 2. Greek κόλπος (kólpos) "bosom, lap, fold of a garment; hollow; a gulf, bay" (attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE; the geometric sense "gulf of the sea" is well established by the Classical period — Herodotus and later historians use it of the Persian Gulf). 3. Latin colpus / colphus "a gulf, a bay of the sea" (borrowed from Greek kólpos in the Imperial period; the spelling colphus preserves the Greek -ph-; Vulgar Latin colphus is the recorded form feeding the Romance words). 4. Old Italian golfo "a gulf" (from Vulgar Latin colphus) and French golfe "a gulf, bay" (12th c., also from the Latin form) — English took the word through this Romance channel, chiefly via Old French and Italian. 5. English gulf "a deep hollow or chasm; a deep inlet of the sea; an abyss" (first attested 14th c., Chaucer and Middle English texts; the spelling gulf, with English loss of the -o-, is established by the 15th c.) — the target word gulf. The figurative sense "a wide, unbridgeable separation between persons or things" develops within English in the 16th–18th centuries. DEBATED STAGE (stated explicitly): the ultimate origin of Greek kólpos is uncertain. The most commonly cited Indo-European connection is to the root *kʷelp- (or *kelp-) "to turn, to bend, to vault," which would make the "bosom, fold, hollow" sense primary ("the fold of the lap, the hollow of the chest"); but the word has also been analyzed as a Pre-Greek substrate loanword with no secure IE etymology. Both possibilities are defended in the literature, and the reconstruction *kʷelp- is far from certain; the attested record is secure from Greek kólpos onward, and it is from that attested Greek form that the chain below runs without gaps.
