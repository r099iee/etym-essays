- 18th c. - Established in British English as a compound noun denoting an accidental, messy spill or drop of writing ink upon a page or document; regarded as a mark of carelessness, poor penmanship, or bad schooling.
- Mid-19th c. - Transformed into an artistic and literary parlor game in Romantic Germany and Victorian Britain (Klecksographie), where folding paper over wet ink blottings inspired whimsical poetry and grotesque fantasy illustrations.
- Early 20th c. - Adopted into experimental psychology and psychoanalysis through Hermann Rorschach's standardized plates, acquiring an elevated scientific, psychological connotation as a projective diagnostic stimulus.
- Mid-to-Late 20th c. - Solidified in popular consciousness as the universal emblem of Freudian psychiatry, therapy, and psychiatric testing; widely lampooned in cartoons and films as a test where 'everything looks like a bat or a butterfly.'
- 21st c. to Present - Maintained in both clinical psychology and everyday colloquial speech as a metaphor for any ambiguous, shapeless situation that invites subjective interpretation and reveals the mindset of the viewer.
