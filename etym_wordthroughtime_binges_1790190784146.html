- 18th to Early 19th Century - Originated in English Midlands dialect (Northamptonshire, Lincolnshire) as a practical verb meaning to soak a wooden cask, barrel, or tub in water so its wooden staves would swell and become watertight. - Perceived as ordinary rural craftsmanship and household vernacular with no connection to alcohol or human behavior.
- Mid to Late 19th Century (c. 1854) - Borrowed into British university slang (Oxford and Cambridge) and naval jargon as a vivid metaphor for drinking heavily—"soaking one's own vessel" with liquor. - Regarded as lively, collegiate, and slightly disreputable slang for a heavy drinking bout or tavern spree.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Popularized across British and American literature (prominently by P. G. Wodehouse and F. Scott Fitzgerald) to denote fun-loving social sprees, high-society parties, wild nights out, or shopping extravaganzas. - Viewed as a boisterous, lighthearted, and roguish term for youthful revelry and social extravagance.
- Late 20th Century - Adopted by medicine and psychiatry to formulate formal clinical diagnostic categories for compulsive, dysregulated eating and substance abuse (binge eating disorder, binge drinking). - Acquired a clinical, serious, and pathologized connotation of behavioral loss of control.
- 21st Century to Present - Exploded in digital and media culture to designate marathon sessions of viewing streaming television series (binge-watching or media binges). - Perceived simultaneously as a ubiquitous, socially accepted domestic leisure pastime and a serious clinical term for compulsive behavior.
