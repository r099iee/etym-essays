1. A word born almost in print. Unlike the great Latin and Greek inheritances of English, whim is a word of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, born in the tavern talk and jest-books of Tudor England and admitted to the dictionary only gradually. Its ancestor, the reduplicative whim-wham (1530s), named a fanciful trifle — a gewgaw, a dangling ornament, a thing of pointless prettiness — with the very sound of the word miming its subject: a flickering, flapping, aimless thing. The formation is the same comic doubling that gave English higgledy-piggledy and dilly-dally: language making a sound that looks like a shrug in the mouth.

2. From bauble to mental event: the clipping. By the 1690s the clipped form whim had transferred the bauble's name to the mind's sudden fancies — a move typical of the period's psychology, which loved to describe thoughts as motions, turns, and flights. The transfer is significant: a whim is not a considered wish but a glitter of the attention, ornamental and purposeless, exactly what the whimsical object was. The eighteenth century, the great age of reason, reacted predictably. In the moralists and satirists — Swift's circle among them — "whim" became the standing antonym of judgment: the tyrant's whim, the coquette's whim, the arbitrary ruling that no argument can reach. Dr. Johnson's dictionary work captured the word with clinical distaste, marking the shift of connotation from curiosity to defect.

3. The Romantic softening and Victorian whimsy. The nineteenth century completed a strange rehabilitation. Romanticism valued spontaneity, originality, and the unpremeditated flash of genius, and "whim" and especially "whimsical" began to glow. Laurence Sterne's Tristram Shandy (1759-67), written against the rule of method, had already turned whimsy into a literary method in itself — digression as genius — and the Victorians institutionalised the charm: Edward Lear's nonsense, Lewis Carroll's Alice, and a whole aesthetic of the eccentric made whimsy a beloved English virtue. The word split: a whim in power was still tyranny; a whim in art and personal life was now delightful.

4. Caprice, psychology, and the machine age. The twentieth century harvested the word's ambiguity. Psychology absorbed the whim into the scientific study of impulse and caprice — the arbitrary choice became data for theories of the unconscious and of decision. Middle-class culture domesticated the word further ("every whim of the child must be indulged" became the anti-type of good parenting), while political theory kept "at the whim of" as a precise description of arbitrary rule, now extended to bureaucracies, markets, and algorithms. The old opposition of the eighteenth century survives in miniature: we still say "on a whim" with affection for ourselves and with alarm for others.

  • Form: reduplicative whim-wham → clipped whim; adjectival whimsical and abstract whimsy (17th c.) spin off the same stock.
  • Sense: fanciful object (1530s) → sudden fancy (1690s) → arbitrary power and charming eccentricity (18th-19th c.) → impulse studied by psychology, exercised by algorithms (20th-21st c.).
  • Connotation: from curiosity, to defect, to charm, to mild danger — the word's moral temperature follows the age's attitude to unreason.
