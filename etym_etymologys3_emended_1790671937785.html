Emended descends without break from Latin emendare, itself a transparent compound of privative e-/ex-, out of or free from, plus menda or mendum, a fault, blemish or scribal blunder. Roman authors already use it in both moral and textual registers: Cicero urges friends to emend their speeches and their lives, Quintilian instructs the orator to emend his draft as a farmer prunes vines, and Suetonius shows emperors having libels emended — corrected and, ominously, censored. The past participle emendatus stamps approved books: Pliny boasts of volumes emendatissimi, most carefully corrected, an ancient blurb.

Transmission to English is bookish and late-medieval. While amend arrives early via Old French amender and naturalises in law, religion and conduct — amend thy ways, amend a writ — emend is re-borrowed directly from Latin by fifteenth-century clerks and humanists who need a word for what they do to manuscripts. Caxton, Malory's editors and early printers advertise texts as emended; the distinction hardens in the age of print, where errata lists, corrigenda and second editions emended after copy create a commercial language of correction. By 1700 Johnson-style lexicography can define emend as chiefly to correct an author, amend as to reform a thing.

The great age of emendation follows:

  • Humanist daring (1500–1700): Erasmus on the New Testament, Scaliger on chronology, Bentley on Horace and Paradise Lost emend by conjecture with sublime confidence, often without manuscript warrant. Emendation is heroic, combative, fame-making; opponents accuse emenders of corrupting under colour of correcting.
  • Lachmannian science (1800s): Karl Lachmann stemmatics turns emendation from guesswork into method — collate witnesses, build a family tree, strip scribal accretion, admit conjecture only where all witnesses fail. Housman's ruthless rigour makes emend Juvenal, Manilius with epigrammatic severity.
  • Bibliographical refinement (1900s): Greg, Bowers and McKerrow distinguish substantive accidentals, copy-text and authorial revision, narrowing legitimate emendation and obliging editors to record every adopted emendation in apparatus. Emended ceases to boast and starts to confess.
  • Digital present: critical editions, genetic criticism and version control preserve every state, so to emend is now to fork, annotate and justify in public. Outside the academy the verb thins: journalists correct, programmers patch, legislators amend. Emended survives where philological honour matters — acknowledgements, title-pages, textual notes — precisely the use in the prompt, where students are thanked for doing both kinds of mending: improving thought and purifying letter.

Phonology is uneventful: Latin /eːmenˈdaːre/ anglicises to /ɪˈmɛnd/, with regular -ed /ɪˈmɛndɪd/. Spelling wobbles between emend/imend in the sixteenth century before settling. Semantically the word narrows rather than drifts: from general freeing-from-fault to almost exclusively textual correction, then modestly re-metaphorises as scholars speak of emending the record, the canon, memory itself — always with the book in view. To have emended is thus to have acted as scribe in reverse, un-writing error to let the author speak.
