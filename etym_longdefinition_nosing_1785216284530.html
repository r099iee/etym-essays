Nosing is the present participle of nose used as a verb: to sniff, scent, pry, or search about inquisitively, as an animal probes with its snout or a busybody thrusts himself into what does not concern him. In the literary context of this note, from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the "original owners" of the ancient caves are "still there in odd corners, slinking and nosing about," and the word's nuance is deliberately zoological: the creatures do not investigate the way men do — they nose, heads low, following scent and touch through the dark, blind almost, probing the corners of a realm they never surrendered. The tone matches the scene: hushed, primitive, faintly repulsive, the curiosity of things that have gone feral in the underground.

Primary senses. The verb nose has two closely related literal strands:
  • Scenting: to press the nose or snout against something and smell it — a dog noses a trail, noses at a bowl, noses the ground; pure olfaction put into motion.
  • Probing/feeling one's way: to move the head (as if the nose were leading) into or against something — a boat noses forward into a harbour, an aircraft noses down; the snout-as-front-of-the-body image extended to any slow, head-first advance.

Secondary senses and uses.
  • Prying and meddling (figurative): to nose into another's affairs is to pry — the transferred, faintly contemptuous sense that makes "a nosy person" one who noses into business that is not his own; the historical bridge is the image of the probing nose as the organ of curiosity, thrusting itself where it is not wanted.
  • To nose out: to discover by persistent scenting or investigation (the hound nosed out the scent; the reporter nosed out the scandal) — the detective's and the dog's verb alike.
  • Register: the word is vivid, informal, and sensory; in its prying sense it is mildly pejorative, in its animal sense purely descriptive, and in nautical/aerial use ("to nose forward") entirely neutral and technical.

Formation. The verb is denominal — built directly from the noun nose (the body part) in the 16th century, first meaning "to smell or scent," then extended in the 17th century to "to pry, search about." The present participle nosing keeps the noun's concrete image alive even in the most transferred uses: wherever the word appears, a nose is leading, sniffing, probing, intruding.

Nuance in the present sentence. The sentence's pairing is deliberate: the creatures are not merely investigating, they are nosing about — the phrase signals aimless, persistent, small-scale searching rather than systematic enquiry. "About" is essential to the effect: it converts the verb into the idle, roving quartering of a hound working a patch of ground, and it leaves the reader with no sense of a goal, only of endless, low, head-first curiosity. Tolkien also gives the word a faint repulsiveness by withholding sight — these original owners "lie up in the dark," and their nosing is done by smell and touch alone, which makes their probing more alien and more unsettling than a man's would be. Finally, nosing contributes the note of tenacity: to keep nosing is to refuse to be discouraged, and these ancient beings, unlike the goblins who merely widened the caves, have never stopped searching the corners of the realm they never surrendered.
