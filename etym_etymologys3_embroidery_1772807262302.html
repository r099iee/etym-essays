The biography of embroidery is a story of a craft passing from Germanic hands into Romance speech and back into English. The deepest reconstructible stage is a Germanic verb, Proto-Germanic *bruzdjan or *bruzdajan, meaning 'to embroider, to ornament cloth with thread.' The root is essentially western Germanic — the reconstructed form is inferred from the Frankish *brozdon that transmitted it into Gallo-Romance — and no secure Indo-European root is usually cited for it, so a careful account begins at Proto-Germanic and refuses to force the word into a PIE family. What matters about the root is what it meant: ornamental working of thread, decoration applied to cloth, the craft of the needle artist rather than of the seamstress. From Frankish, the verb entered the Romance of northern Gaul as Old French broisder, broder, 'to embroider,' attested from the twelfth century. In French aristocratic culture embroidery was a courtly and ecclesiastical luxury — the rich figured vestments of the church, the embroidered sleeves and bodices of noble dress — and the verb travelled with the prestige of that world. Modern French broder and broderie continue the line. The craft-word was evidently valued precisely because it was tied to ornamental, not utilitarian, sewing: to broder was to add beauty to cloth that was already made. English took the word from Anglo-French, the French of the English court after the Norman Conquest. Anglo-French formed enbrouder, embrouder, with the prefix en- 'in, into,' and from this verb Middle English developed embrouden and, by the late fourteenth century, the noun embrouderie, 'the art of embroidering.' The late fourteenth century is the golden age of the word's arrival: it appears in the courtly poetry of Chaucer's circle, where richly embroidered garments ('embrouded' cloth) are part of the vocabulary of aristocratic splendour, and in the period's descriptions of ecclesiastical and ceremonial dress. The craft entered English as a marker of refinement, the same association the word still carries. The spelling is a small philological drama. English embroider, from the 1500s onward, came to be written with the sequence -oi-, embroider/embroidery, under the influence of the native Middle English word broiden, the irregular past participle of braid, which meant ornamented or braided work. Speakers felt that embroidery was a kind of braiding, and the written word was reshaped to look like it — a false but productive association that fixed the modern spelling. The same reshaped word is the one on this card. Semantically the noun has always run in a double track. From the start embrouderie named the art or practice — the process of embroidering — and from the sixteenth century it also named the finished work, the embroidered textile. That double reference is essential to the Red Chamber setting, where young women both 'do embroidery' as daily discipline and produce pieces that display skill, patience, and status. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word also opened a figurative track: to embroider a story became to ornament it with invented detail, a metaphor that has remained alive in English ever since, and one that shows how deeply the word is associated with the addition of beauty rather than substance. Finally, the nineteenth century industrialised the craft: machine embroidery, and later computerised embroidery, mechanised what had been a signature of the hand, and the word acquired its modern technical reach even as its older meanings remained. From a Frankish craft-verb to a marker of Qing aristocratic feminine culture in translation, embroidery has always meant the same thing: beauty added to cloth by the patient work of the needle.
