Wit belongs to one of the most famous word-families in Indo-European, the family built on the root *weid- "to see". In PIE, the perfect of "to see" — literally "I have seen" — acquired the habitual, stative meaning "I know", because what one has seen one knows. This equation of sight with knowledge is ancient and pervasive: it lies behind Latin vidēre "to see" (whence vision, video, evident), Greek eîdos "form, the seen thing" (whence idea), and Sanskrit véda "knowledge, the sacred lore" (whence the Veda and Hindi ved). English did not inherit a direct descendant of *weid- as its main verb for "see" or "know" — those jobs went to other roots — but it kept the family alive in a dense cluster of abstract words: wise, wisdom, wit, witness, the obsolete verb wot "I know", and (through the Frankish verb for "to show the way") the word guide.

In Germanic the root *weid- appears as *wīt- (the PIE diphthong *ei regularly giving Germanic *ī). The verb *witaną "to know" is attested across the whole branch — Gothic witan, Old Norse vita, Old Saxon witan, Old High German wizzan (Modern German wissen), Old English witan. From this verb Germanic built the neuter ja-stem noun *witją, "the faculty of knowing", which in Old English appears as witt or gewitt: "understanding, mind, intellect, consciousness". The prefixed gewitt (with collective ge-) shows the word covering the whole assembled apparatus of awareness.

In Old English witt is already productive and polysemous. It is the mind as an organ of perception and reason; it appears in compounds such as gewitnes "testimony, witness", witol "knowing, certain", and wittig "wise, witty". The plural, wittas, is frequent and already begins the move toward "faculties, senses": Old English glosses render the five external senses and the inner faculties as the "wits". This medieval psychology of "wits" — the five outward senses plus the inner faculties of imagination, estimation, fantasy, and memory — is the key that unlocks the idiom "to have one's wits about one" and the fear of "losing one's wits".

In Middle English the forms converge: wyt, wit, wytte, plural wittes or wyttes. Chaucer uses the singular for "mind, reason" and the plural for "senses, faculties" — the Knight's Tale and the Nun's Priest's Tale are full of "the five wittes". To be "in one's wyt" is to be sane; to be "out of thy wyt" is madness. The gerundial phrase "to wit" (from the infinitive with the old dative ending) becomes the standard formula for "that is to say", surviving into modern legal English. Already in the 14th–15th centuries the tension is set: the word means both the general knowing faculty and the sharper, quicker edge of that faculty.

It is the early modern period that pushes wit toward verbal brilliance. The 16th century inherits "mother wit" (native common sense) and "the five wits", but also begins to use wit for readiness of mind; the Oxford English Dictionary places the settling of the sense "the faculty of saying brilliant or sparkling things in an amusing way" in the later 16th and 17th centuries. The Restoration and Augustan age makes the word a battleground. John Locke in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690) defines wit as the quick assemblage of ideas into surprising congruities; Joseph Addison and Richard Steele in The Spectator (1711–12) contrast true wit (grounded in judgment) with false wit (mere wordplay); and the "wits" become a social class — the coffee-house intelligentsia who traded epigrams. Dryden, Pope, and Swift use the word with a frequency and self-consciousness without parallel today; this is the golden age in which "a wit" names a person, not just an ability.

The plural, meanwhile, preserves the old faculty sense. "To keep one's wits about one", "to be at one's wits' end", "to be frightened out of one's wits" all descend from the medieval plural meaning the collected faculties. "At their wits' end" appears in the Bible (Psalm 107, in Coverdale's and the King James renderings) for the sailors' despair at sea — an attestation that fixed the idiom in English for four centuries. By the 19th century "to have one's wits about one" is standard in fiction for alertness and presence of mind.

The result is a split modern word. Singular wit is almost always verbal cleverness and comic intelligence: "a dry wit", "a witty remark", "repartee". Plural wits is composure and cognitive grip: "collect your wits", "scared out of his wits", "keep your wits about you". The old bridge — the knowing faculty — survives only in fossil phrases like "to wit" and in the etymology. It is exactly the plural, faculty sense that Tolkien activates when Bilbo says he must "sit down for a minute and collect my wits": the speaker does not want a joke; he wants his scattered mental faculties gathered back. The PIE equation of seeing with knowing — the root that made wit a word about knowledge — is the deep history behind a hobbit's homely request for composure.
