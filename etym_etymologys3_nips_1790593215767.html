The verb nip belongs to the dense thicket of short Germanic words in -ip that denote small sharp actions: sip, tip, dip, clip, snip, grip, pip. Its history is one of staying short while multiplying senses, moving from fingers to feet, from pinching skin to pinching distance.

The earliest recoverable stage is a Germanic root for pressing or pinching. Old Norse has hnippa to prod or press, Middle Dutch and Middle Low German have nippen, nipen to pinch or to sip, and Middle English by the 14th century has nippen to pinch, bite, or cut with cold. Whether English borrowed directly from Norse settlers in the Danelaw or inherited a native cognate reinforced by contact is debated, because the initial h- in hnippa was lost early and the word is so short that independent creation by sound-symbolism cannot be ruled out. What is clear is that by Chaucer time, nip means to squeeze sharply between two surfaces, whether fingers, teeth, shears, or frost.

From this pinching core, medieval and early modern English spins out a family:

  • Horticultural nipping: gardeners nip off buds, nip back shoots, meaning to remove with a fingernail pinch. This gives the idiom to nip in the bud, recorded in the 16th century, meaning to destroy at inception, a vivid image of thumb and forefinger crushing a tender shoot.
  • Atmospheric nipping: frost, wind, and cold are said to nip, to nip the vines, to nip ones fingers, personifying weather as a biting animal. This survives in nippy weather, nip in the air.
  • Convivial nipping: drinkers take a nip, a small pinched quantity of spirits. Eighteenth-century tavern slang speaks of nipping gin, nipping a dram, the smallness of the pinch justifying the smallness of the dose, though habitual nippers soon belie moderation.
  • Dishonest nipping: thieves nip purses, nip watches, cutpurses pinching goods. Elizabethan rogue literature lists nippers as pickpockets, one who nips by sleight of hand.

The motion sense, to nip along, nip out, nip ahead, does not appear prominently until the 19th century, and its emergence illustrates internal semantic drift rather than new borrowing. The bridge is the idea of a movement as abrupt and neatly bounded as a pinch. To nip off is to detach oneself as cleanly as a nipped bud detaches from a stem. To nip round the corner is to round it with the same economy as a pinch. Dickens, Thackeray, and later Wodehouse seize on this handy verb for urban bustle: servants nip, hansoms nip, small boats nip. The particle system of English phrasal verbs does the rest, allowing nip to combine with out, off, along, over, through, past, and on to specify direction while retaining its core brevity.

Phonetically, nip is almost immutable: short high front vowel /ɪ/ plus voiceless bilabial stop /p/, a syllable that itself feels nipped off. Plural or third-person nips adds regular /s/, preserving abruptness. No Great Vowel Shift drama touches it, no Latin suffix ennobles it. Its stability lets speakers feel all senses at once: when a motor-boat nips on ahead, we half-hear the pinch, the sip, the frost-bite, and the light-fingered theft, all conspiring to make the dash seem cheeky and quick. Modern British informality keeps the motion sense alive in Ill just nip to the shops, while American use favours the dram and frost senses. Together they show how a single-syllable pinch can stretch to cover any sudden smallness, whether of pain, drink, theft, or travel.
