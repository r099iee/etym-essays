The word DELUGE closes Gibbon's sardonic sentence about the divines: they could distinguish, he writes, that the establishment of heresy tended to produce an earthquake, or that a deluge was the inevitable consequence of the progress of sin and error. The deluge is first of all the biblical Flood, the great catastrophe of Genesis, and Gibbon invokes it to expose the superstition of his age's religious thinkers, who read earthquakes and floods as divine punishments meted out for moral failings. In this context the word carries a specifically theological resonance: a deluge is not an ordinary flood but a catastrophic, providential inundation that sweeps away a sinful world. Its tone is ironic and deflating — Gibbon sets the absurd causal machinery (sin and error producing a deluge) against the word's solemn grandeur. The deluge is overwhelming, unstoppable, total: it does not merely wet the ground but washes away everything in its path, literally and figuratively, leaving the world remade — or destroyed. CORE SENSE AND DEFINITION:
  • Primary sense: a severe flood, an overwhelming overflow of water that inundates land; specifically, in capitalized use, the Deluge — the great Flood of Noah in Genesis.
  • Secondary figurative sense: a great quantity or flood of anything arriving at the same time and overwhelming those it strikes — 'a deluge of mail,' 'a deluge of complaints,' 'a deluge of information.'
  • Verbal use (deluge, verb): to flood, to overwhelm, to inundate — 'deluged with letters,' 'deluged by rain.'
  • Register: formal and literary; deluge carries a scale and gravity that 'flood' does not, and it retains a biblical echo even in its most modern figurative uses.
FULL POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • Biblical and theological sense (earliest English sense, 14th c.): 'the Deluge' is the Flood of Noah, the providential catastrophe that drowned the corrupt world. This is the word's original and, for centuries, its dominant English sense, and it permanently stamped the word with associations of divine judgment, totality, and world-remaking catastrophe.
  • General hydrologic sense (17th c. onward): any devastating flood or inundation of water — the sea-burst, the bursting dam, the river in spate. Here deluge names not a mere overflow but a catastrophic, overwhelming body of water that destroys what it covers.
  • Figurative sense of overwhelming quantity (17th-18th c. onward): a deluge of words, of petitions, of letters, of complaints, of data, of information — a great quantity of anything arriving at the same time and overwhelming the recipient. This is now the most common modern use and preserves the core notion of being swept over and drowned by sheer volume.
  • Geological/scientific sense (19th c.): diluvium and diluvial/diluvian, antediluvian — the scientific Latin vocabulary of the 'Flood' (diluvium) applied to deposits of gravel and drift once thought to be the remains of Noah's Flood; antediluvian retains the literal 'before the Flood' sense and has spread metaphorically to mean 'hopelessly old-fashioned.'
  • Verbal and participial uses: 'to deluge' someone with something — to overwhelm with quantity; 'deluged' as a participial adjective meaning flooded, swamped, overwhelmed.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE (how the senses connect): The bridge is a movement from scripture to general catastrophe to metaphor. In the fourteenth century the word entered English as the proper name of the biblical Flood. Because the Flood was the archetype of a total, world-covering inundation, the word's scale made it natural to extend it to any devastating flood (17th century), and because the Flood overwhelmed everything without exception, the word then extended naturally to any great quantity that overwhelms — a flood of words or requests (17th-18th century onward). The biblical undertone of judgment has never entirely faded: even a 'deluge of emails' faintly suggests being punished, or at least submerged, by sheer volume. CONTEXTUAL READING (Gibbon's sentence in full critical detail): Gibbon's sentence is the second half of the ironic couplet about the divines. Having established that the sagacious divines could distinguish which calamity followed which sin, he gives the two conclusions: that the establishment of heresy 'tended to produce an earthquake,' and that a deluge 'was the inevitable consequence of the progress of sin and error.' The pairing is deliberate — earthquake and deluge, the two great elemental catastrophes, matched respectively to heresy and to 'the progress of sin and error.' The word deluge carries the full weight of its biblical history into the sentence: the divines, reasoning like prophets, read the world's waters as the instrument of God's displeasure, exactly as their forefathers read the Flood. Gibbon's irony works by letting the solemn grandeur of the word stand while the causal machinery it is attached to is absurd. A deluge is an overwhelming, unstoppable, total catastrophe; that a doctrine ('the progress of sin and error') should produce one is a category error that the historian exposes simply by stating it in the dignified language of the believers themselves. The deluge in the sentence is thus both the real object of superstitious fear — the water that might sweep away the world — and the emblem of how far the religious imagination will go to find moral causes for natural events. COLLOCATION AND SYNTACTIC BEHAVIOUR:
  • Countable noun with the indefinite article: 'a deluge of rain,' 'a deluge of questions.'
  • Common in the pattern 'a deluge of + [mass/collective noun]': a deluge of letters, mail, complaints, information, data, applications, abuse.
  • The capitalized absolute 'the Deluge' remains the name of the biblical Flood and appears in idioms such as 'after me, the deluge' (French après moi, le déluge, attributed to Louis XV or Mme de Pompadour) — used to dismiss concern for what will happen after one's own time.
  • As a verb: 'to deluge' (transitive) 'to flood or overwhelm,' usually with 'with': 'deluged with applications'; also the participial 'deluged.'
RELATION TO THE REST OF THE WORD FAMILY: Deluge comes from Latin dīlūvium, 'a flood,' formed on diluere, 'to wash away, dissolve' (dis-, 'apart, away' + luere, 'to wash'), which belongs to the great PIE root *lewH-, 'to wash.' The same family has given English dilute, dilution, ablution, launder, laundry, lavatory, lotion, latrine, lava, and the learned geological pair diluvium/diluvial. The French form deluge reached English in the fourteenth century; the modern pronunciation with stress on the first syllable (DEL-yooj) and the older stress-on-second-syllable variant (de-LOOJ) both occur, with the first dominant. The word's core semantic idea — a great washing-away — is thus literally inscribed in its form: the deluge is etymologically 'the washing-apart of everything.'
