- Ancient Greek (8th c. BCE–4th c. CE) — τῆλε (tēle): spatial adverb and poetic prefix meaning “far off, afar, at a distance,” appearing in Homeric compounds like tēlemachos (“fighting from afar”) and tēleskopos (“far-seeing”).
- 17th c. — Early Modern scientific neologisms: adopted into Neo-Latin and European scientific registers following Galileo's invention; Kepler and his circle standardize telescopium (1611) to name the astronomical instrument, establishing tele- as the standard European morpheme for distance-conquering optics.
- Late 18th–19th c. — The Industrial Revolution and electronic revolution: French and British inventors coin télégraphe (Claude Chappe, 1792) and telephone (Wheatstone, Bell, 1876), cementing tele- as the quintessential prefix of the modern communications age.
- Early to Mid-20th c. — The broadcast age & colloquial clipping: the invention of television (1900) leads to massive worldwide broadcasting; by the 1930s–1950s, British and Australian English clip television into the affectionate household noun the telly / tele.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Digital era & remote work: explosive renaissance of tele- as an operational prefix denoting remote decentralized services: telecommuting, teleconferencing, telemedicine, and telehealth, while *tele* remains standard corporate shorthand for the trillion-dollar telecommunications industry.
