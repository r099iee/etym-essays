• Commanded / Ordered: Similar because the leading sense of “bade” is “gave a command”. Differs because “commanded/ordered” are the plain modern words, while “bade” is archaic and literary.
  • Directed: Similar because both mean “gave instructions to do something”. Differs because “direct” is neutral, administrative and current, while “bade” carries authority with old-world gravity.
  • Instructed: Similar because both convey that someone was told to do something. Differs because “instruct” suggests teaching or briefing, while “bade” is a bare command or invitation without pedagogy.
  • Charged: Similar because both mean “laid a duty or command upon someone”. Differs because “charged” implies a solemn commission, while “bade” can be as light as an invitation.
  • Enjoined: Similar because both mean “urged or commanded with authority”. Differs because “enjoin” is legalistic, formal and rare, while “bade” is poetic and antiquated.
  • Told: Similar because both mean “said to (someone to do something)”. Differs because “told” is the flat, colourless modern verb, while “bade” never loses its ceremonial edge.
  • Invited: Similar because “bade” in Middle English commonly meant “invited, summoned to a feast”. Differs because “invite” is friendly and voluntary, while “bade” leans toward command even when courteous.
  • Entreated / Begged: Similar because in older usage “bad(e)” could express earnest request. Differs because “entreat/beg” come from below, pleading upward, while “bade” usually comes from above, as a command or gracious summons.
  • Summoned: Similar because “bade them come” ≈ “summoned them”. Differs because “summon” implies an official call to appear, while “bade” colours the command with the speaker’s own dignity and diction.
  • Bid (modern form): Similar because “bade” IS the past tense of “bid”. Differs because “bid” now suggests auctions and offers as much as commands, while “bade” survives only in the archaic command/invite/farewell senses.
