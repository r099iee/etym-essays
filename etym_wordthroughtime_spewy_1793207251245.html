- Old English — the verb is born: spīwan means to spew and vomit, a farmyard word of mouths and stomachs, not yet of fields.
- Middle Ages — the verb widens: spewen comes to mean any violent emitting — springs spew, chimneys spew, dragons spew fire — while the land itself has no adjective.
- Early modern — the -y habit spreads: English mints hundreds of character adjectives (runny, weepy, oozy), and provincial speakers apply the pattern to wet soils.
- 1820s — Cobbett fixes it in print: spewy gravel in Rural Rides gives the dialect word its one great literary attestation, a farmer's curse on Middlesex drift.
- Victorian — the glossarists collect it: provincial glossaries and the English Dialect Dictionary record spewy for oozing ground and weeping sores, marking it rustic and southern.
- Today — a fossil word: kept alive by Cobbett's sentence and the OED, unknown to modern speakers, the very picture of a Tier 5 obscure provincialism.
