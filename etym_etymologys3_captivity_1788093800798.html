Captivity is one of the oldest words of power in the language: behind it stands the great Latin verb of seizing, and behind that a PIE root for grasping that also gave English "have" and "heave". Its record runs from Roman triumph processions to hotel Wi-Fi portals.

1. Indo-European stock: *kap- "to grasp". The chain starts with the PIE root *kap- (also reconstructed *keh₂p-), "to grasp, seize, take hold of". It produced one of the most successful word-families in history: Latin capere "to take, seize, capture" (with hundreds of derivatives — capture, capable, accept, receive, concept, prince) and, through Germanic *habjaną and *hafjaną, English have ("to grasp, possess") and heave ("to seize and lift"). To "have" is etymologically to keep a grip on something; the captive is what the grip holds.

2. Rome: capere, captus, captivus — the prisoner of war. Latin capere (attested from the 3rd century BCE) formed the perfect participle captus "taken, seized" and, from it, captivus "taken captive, prisoner". Roman warfare institutionalized the state: captivi were the living booty of triumphal processions — paraded, ransomed, or sold into slavery. The language even noticed the oddity of the condition: Plautus's comedy Captivi ("The Captives", c. 200 BCE) is built entirely on the inversion of masters and slaves taken in war. From the start, therefore, captivitas ("captivity") was a legal-military status with a strong social shadow: the free man reduced, for a time, to the condition of property.

3. Late Latin and Christianity: captivity becomes spiritual. Christian Latin took captivitas and widened it radically. The Vulgate (late 4th century) uses captivitas for the Babylonian exile — "the Captivity" — and, in the Pauline and patristic tradition, for the bondage of the soul to sin and death ("leading captivity captive", Ephesians 4:8, echoing Psalm 68). The word now spanned two registers: the political-exilic and the spiritual-moral. Medieval Latin and preaching kept both alive, and "the Captivity" passed into every European vernacular as the standard name for the exile of Israel.

4. Old French and Middle English: captivité, captivite. Old French inherited Latin captivitas as captivité/captiveté (12th century) and captif "captive; miserable" — note that Old French captif also developed the sense "wretched, hapless", whence Middle English caitiff (a wretch), a separate borrowing that shows how the word's emotional colour was already darkening: the captive is pitiable. English took captivity itself in the 14th century (Middle English captivite), in legal, biblical, and devotional texts, with the double sense already in place: war-prisoner status and spiritual bondage.

5. Early modern: liberty's shadow. The 16th–17th centuries sharpened the word's moral weight as European political thought made "liberty" the master-value: captivity became its negation — political (captive nations), religious (captive consciences), and personal. The King James Bible (1611) fixed "captivity" in English scripture (Psalm 126: "the LORD turned again the captivity of Zion"). The Barbary pirates and the transatlantic slave trade kept the literal sense violently current: captivity narratives such as Mary Rowlandson's (1682) made the word the title of a genre, and antislavery writing used captivity and bondage almost interchangeably.

6. The 19th century: animals and audiences. Two quiet extensions changed everyday use. First, menageries and then zoos made "in captivity" a standard biological phrase — animals in captivity, contrasted with the wild. Second, the idioms "captive audience" and "captive market" (attested from the 19th century) metaphorized the word into commerce and media: people who cannot leave, customers who cannot choose. The bridge is exact: the captive cannot escape.

7. The 20th–21st centuries: camps, conservation, and cyber. The century of total war gave the word its darkest instalment — prisoner-of-war camps, concentration camps, "captivity" in war memoir after war memoir — while conservation biology built a careful positive register: captive breeding, captive assurance populations, reintroduction (condor, oryx, scimitar-horned oryx — species whose survival hinged on captivity). In the 21st century, technology borrowed the word again: a "captive portal" is the login screen that holds your Wi-Fi connection hostage until you agree to terms — an everyday, slightly comic revival of the ancient seizing. "Captive insurance", "captive markets", and "captive product pricing" keep the economic sense productive.

8. Phonetic record. Latin captīvitās → Late Latin/spoken forms with /v/ → Old French captivité (loss of final syllables, stress on -tiv-) → Middle English captivite → modern /kæpˈtɪvɪti/. The English stress pattern (kæp-TIV-i-ty) preserves the Romance stress; the initial /kæp-/ still sounds the family's original "grip".

Summary. From a PIE word for grasping, through Roman war-booty law, Christian theology of the exiled soul, medieval French and English piety, early-modern liberty discourse, 19th-century zoos and commerce, 20th-century camps, and 21st-century Wi-Fi portals — captivity has spent three thousand years meaning exactly "seized and held", and each era has merely chosen its own jailer.
