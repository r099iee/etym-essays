PIE *wal- 'to be strong, to rule' (reconstructed, unattested; the linking of the Latin and Germanic branches under one *wal- is standard, but the exact root shape is debated — some scholars prefer *h₂welh₁- for the Italic line and treat *wal- as a separate or parallel root) → Latin valeō, valēre, valuī, valitum 'to be strong, be well, be worth, prevail' (attested from the 3rd century BCE; the imperative valē! 'be well, farewell' was everyday Roman idiom; derivatives give valid, value, prevail, convalesce, valediction) → Late Latin valōrem (accusative of valor) 'worth, value, strength' (abstract noun built on valēre; attested in Late Latin, 4th–5th century CE) → Old French valour, valor 'worth, value, courage' (attested 11th–12th century; the semantic shift from 'worth/strength' to 'martial courage' happened chiefly in Old French chivalric culture) → Middle English valour 'worth, valour, courage' (attested c. 1300) → English valour 'great courage in the face of danger, especially in battle' (in use from the 13th century; British spelling fixed with -our, American valor; the fully martial sense established by the 16th–17th centuries alongside disciplined professional armies). Note on debate: the PIE root *wal- is sometimes subsumed under *h₂welh₁- 'strong, powerful' as the source of Latin valēre, but the reconstruction is not settled; the Indo-Aryan comparison with Sanskrit bála- 'strength' is suggestive and widely cited but not universally accepted. The French-mediated shift of valour from 'worth' to 'battlefield courage' is the decisive, well-documented semantic development.
