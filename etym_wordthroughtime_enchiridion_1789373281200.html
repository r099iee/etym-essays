- [c. 2nd c. CE] - Coined in Hellenistic Greek from en ("in") + cheir ("hand") + diminutive suffix -idion to denote a small dagger or handheld tool, and famously appropriated by Arrian as the title for Epictetus's pocket summary of Stoic ethics (Encheiridion) - [connotation: philosophical pocket armor, practical weapon of virtue, handheld spiritual guide]
- [c. 5th c. CE] - Adopted into Late Latin by Saint Augustine in his Enchiridion ad Laurentium, Christianizing the genre as a concise handbook summarizing Christian doctrine through the triad of Faith, Hope, and Charity - [connotation: authoritative doctrinal summary, indispensable manual of salvation]
- [16th c.–Early Modern] - Popularized across Renaissance Europe by Desiderius Erasmus with his celebrated Enchiridion Militis Christiani (1503), deliberately exploiting the ancient pun between "handbook" and "spiritual dagger"; adopted into English as an erudite term for any compact guide to piety, law, or science - [connotation: humanist elegance, moral instruction, essential intellectual compass]
- [19th c.–Present] - Retained in literary criticism, academic publishing, and bibliographical tradition as an elevated title for definitive reference guides, ethical manuals, or foundational compendia - [connotation: scholarly authority, classical pedigree, timeless handbook of wisdom]
