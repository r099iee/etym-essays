Cham is a western medieval spelling of a central Asian title, and its history is really the history of Europe’s image of the Mongol empire — followed by a second, purely English history, in which the word survives because of a single literary joke.

The steppe title

The word behind Cham is the Mongol and Turkic title qaɣan (also written khagan), the supreme ruler over a confederation of khans, alongside the simpler qan or khan. The title is first attested among the Ruan-ruan confederation of the fourth and fifth centuries CE; it was taken up by the Türks in the sixth century, by the Uyghurs in the eighth, and finally by the Mongols, whose leader Temüjin was proclaimed Chinggis Qa’an in 1206. Its deeper origin is unknown, and the reconstruction is made harder by the fact that the word spread across several unrelated language families as a loan.

  • Qan — a ruler, a khan.
  • Qaɣan — the great khan, sovereign over other khans; the form that European writers rendered with a superlative.
  • The title was not a name, so European accounts could speak of “the Great Khan” without identifying any particular emperor — a fact essential to the later English metaphor.

The westward journey

Persian and Arabic, the languages of the Mongol chancery and of the caravan trade, transmitted the title west as khān. The thirteenth-century friars who travelled to the Mongol court — Carpini in 1245–47, William of Rubruck in the 1250s — wrote it in Latin as chanis or canis, and Marco Polo’s book made “the Great Can” a household phrase in Romance Europe. English received the word through French and through Latin geography, and by the sixteenth century the form Cham is established in travel writing and compendia of cosmography, where “the Great Cham of Tartary” denotes the emperor of inner Asia.

The troublesome -m

The final consonant is the word’s one genuine mystery. Turkic khān and Mongol qan end in a nasal -n, not -m; the western forms (French cham, English Cham, Italian cham) show -m. Various explanations have been offered — a dialectal Turkic pronunciation, the influence of Latin or Italian spellings of the title, or simple confusion with the biblical name Cham (Ham), which was familiar from Genesis — but none is demonstrable, and the question is best left open. What matters for English is that Cham is a western spelling of the title, not an eastern word in its own right.

The English literary afterlife

By the eighteenth century Cham was already an archaic, dictionary word, kept alive by geographies and by the memory of the Mongol empire. Its extraordinary second life began with a joke. Tobias Smollett, novelist and editor, is traditionally said to have called Samuel Johnson “the Great Cham of literature” in 1759 — the year of Rasselas, when Johnson’s authority over English letters was at its height. The image worked because every reader recognised the reference: the Great Cham was the remote despot whose will no one dared contest.

  • It flattered Johnson by comparing his authority to an emperor’s.
  • It teased him by making an English moralist into an oriental autocrat.
  • It implied a criticism of literary dictatorship at a moment when reviewers and lexicographers were acquiring unprecedented power.

From title to sobriquet

The phrase was repeated by Johnson’s friends and enemies, canonised by Boswell’s Life of Johnson (1791), and thereafter attached to him permanently. Nineteenth-century historians of literature used it as a convenient label; twentieth-century biographers used it as a title. In the process the word underwent a narrow but real semantic shift: from a title denoting a Mongol emperor, to a byword for arbitrary power, to a fixed English sobriquet for one particular critic. Outside that sobriquet, Cham in this sense is effectively dead in modern English, replaced everywhere by Khan.

Modern usage

Today the word appears in two places only: histories of the Mongol empire, where it is a variant spelling, and Johnsonian criticism, where “the Great Cham of literature” is a learned allusion signalling that the writer knows eighteenth-century letters. Its tone is mock-heroic and affectionate, and its force depends on the reader catching the reference to Tartary — which is precisely why the present passage can use the bare word Cham and expect it to be understood.
