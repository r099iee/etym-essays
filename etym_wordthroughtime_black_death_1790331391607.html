- Mid-14th Century (Contemporary Outbreak) - Contemporaries in Latin and vernacular Europe recorded the disaster as *magna mortalitas* ('the Great Mortality') or *pestis ingens* ('the Great Pestilence'), viewing it as an apocalyptic visitation of divine wrath and universal grief.

- 16th to 17th Century (Scandinavian Chronicles) - First coined as a descriptive compound in Swedish (*swarta dödhen*) and Danish (*den sorte død*) chronicles to characterize the darkness, terrifying mortality, and black necrotic tissue of the epidemic.

- 18th to Early 19th Century (Historiographical Formulation) - Introduced into German historical medicine (*Der schwarze Tod* by J. F. C. Hecker in 1832) and translated into English, rapidly establishing 'the Black Death' as the universal scientific and historiographical name for the 1346–1353 pandemic.

- Late 19th to Mid-20th Century - Analyzed by economic and social historians (e.g., Marc Bloch, Henri Pirenne) as the pivotal catalyst that destroyed feudal serfdom, elevated wages, and laid the foundations of modern European labor structures and market economies.

- Late 20th to 21st Century - Validated through modern paleogenomics and ancient DNA sequencing (*Yersinia pestis*), functioning today both as a precise historical-epidemiological landmark and as the premier civilizational metaphor for catastrophic external shock and systemic restructuring.
