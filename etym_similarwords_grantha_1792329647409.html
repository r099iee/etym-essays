• Śāradā: Similar because both are prestigious regional Brahmic scripts that evolved during the medieval era specifically to preserve and transmit sacred Sanskrit literature; differs because Śāradā developed in the far north (Kashmir) on birch-bark media with angular lines and top-hanging bars, whereas Grantha developed in the deep south (Tamil Nadu and Kerala) on dried palm leaves with circular, flowing curves designed to avoid tearing the delicate leaf fibers.
  • Devanagari: Similar because both scripts possess the complete classical Sanskrit phonological inventory (including aspirated stops, voiced consonants, and retroflex sibilants); differs because Devanagari emerged from northern Nagari traditions featuring continuous horizontal head-bars and became the pan-Indian standard script for Sanskrit, while Grantha remained primarily a southern scribal script used by Tamil and Malayalam Brahmins.
  • Tigalari: Similar because both are closely related Southern Brahmic scripts used extensively for transcribing Vedic chanting, Tantric texts, and Sanskrit treatises on palm-leaf manuscripts; differs because Tigalari (or Tulu script) developed specifically in coastal southwestern Karnataka (Tulu Nadu) and northern Kerala among Havyaka and Shivalli Brahmins with distinct blocky, square-proportioned flourishes, whereas Grantha is centered in the Tamil plains and central South India.
  • Vatteluttu: Similar because both were prominent historical scripts used concurrently in ancient Tamil Nadu and Kerala; differs because Vatteluttu ("round script") was used for writing the native Tamil language in administration, secular poetry, and royal inscriptions and lacked characters for Sanskrit voiced or aspirated consonants, whereas Grantha was deliberately engineered to represent Sanskrit phonetics.
  • Pallava script: Similar because Pallava is the direct ancestral stage of Grantha, often termed "Pallava Grantha," which was utilized across southern stone inscriptions; differs because archaic Pallava script refers specifically to the epigraphic script of the 5th–7th centuries CE that spread to Southeast Asia, while classical Grantha designates the fully matured medieval and manuscript script of the 8th–19th centuries.
  • Malayalam script: Similar because modern Malayalam script is a direct daughter script of Grantha, inheriting nearly all of its consonantal and conjunct shapes; differs because modern Malayalam evolved into a distinct regional orthography to write the Malayalam Dravidian language, whereas Grantha was retained primarily for liturgical Sanskrit.
  • Scripture / Treatise: Similar because in classical Sanskrit literature and modern North Indian vernaculars, grantha is a general common noun denoting a sacred book, philosophical treatise, or literary composition; differs because scripture is an abstract thematic term for holy writ, whereas Grantha carries the specific epigraphic meaning of a distinct historical writing system as well as a physical codicological unit of bound folios.
  • Guru Granth Sahib: Similar because both share the exact same Sanskrit etymological root (*granth-*, "to tie, bind, string together into a book"); differs because the Guru Granth Sahib is the sovereign spiritual scripture of Sikhism written in Gurmukhi script, while Grantha in this context is the South Indian script utilized for Sanskrit epigraphy and manuscriptology.
