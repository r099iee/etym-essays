English receptacle is a Latin learned loan whose deepest verbal idea is grasping and holding. Proto-Indo-European *kap- "to grasp, hold, seize" underlies Latin capere, a high-frequency verb meaning to take, capture, or contain. From capere English ultimately inherits a huge family—capture, captive, capacity, conceive, except, participate, accept, receive—all reflecting different prefixations and semantic specializations of "taking." The Germanic have complex is often linked to the same broad Indo-European holding root, giving the family real sister-branch depth beyond Romance alone, though the exact cognacy of have remains debated among specialists. Prefixing re- ("back, again, in return") produces recipere, "to take back, receive, accept, take in." That verb is the parent of English receive, receipt, reception, and recipe (originally a formula "take these ingredients"): the same stem recept- appears in reception and receptive. From the stem of the perfect participle receptus, Latin formed receptaculum with the instrumental/locative suffix -culum: a place or device for receiving, a container. The noun is attested in classical Latin—Vitruvius uses it for receiving basins and channels—so the "receiving place" idea is old, and the suffix gives the word its concrete, architectural feel: a receptacle is not just any box but a thing made for receiving. Middle French réceptacle carried the noun into English by the fifteenth century, first in learned and technical registers. The word arrived through the same channels as many Latinized nouns of the medieval and early modern periods—translation of religious, medical, and scientific texts—and settled into English as a dignified alternative to the native box or vessel. The semantic history then fans outward. The core remains "that which receives and holds": bins and basins, anatomical cavities, and vessels of all kinds. In botany, the floral receptacle names the part of the flower axis that bears the floral organs—a precise technical extension of "that which bears or receives." In electrical engineering and building codes, a receptacle is the socket that receives a plug, the fixed contact device installed at an outlet; here the metaphor of receiving is entirely literal again, as the socket takes in the male prong. In anatomy and physiology, cavities and structures are spoken of as receptacles of fluids or contents. Figurative use—mind as receptacle of ideas, heart as receptacle of feeling, art as receptacle of powers—was always available from the Latin sense of receiving, and the Platonic and philosophical tradition had long used the image of the receptacle as that which receives form (the Greek hypodoche is a famous analogue). Pater exploits exactly that figurative dignity: aesthetic objects are not empty shells but receptacles of forces and virtues, analogous to natural products that possess qualities. The plural receptacles on this card is thus the end point of a long chain from Proto-Indo-European grasping, through Latin taking and receiving, through a medieval and Renaissance French loan, to a Victorian metaphor of art as a vessel of active impression. The word's career illustrates how a humble container-word can be elevated by philosophical context. In the laboratory and the codebook, receptacle is precise and dry; in Pater's sentence it glows, because the reader hears the old image of the hand taking and holding transferred to the mind taking in impressions and to the artwork holding its virtue. Sound history is light—receptaculum passed into French and English with only expected reductions—but semantic history is rich: the same noun that names a waste bin and an electrical outlet can also name the vessel through which beauty is received. The plural form completes the move from a single physical object to a class of cultural objects, each a repository and transmitter of the powers that make aesthetic criticism possible.
