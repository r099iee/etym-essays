Chain for root word 'glade' (with an explicit note of uncertainty at the origin): - Origin uncertain; the word is probably of Scandinavian origin. Proto-Germanic *gladaz 'smooth, shining, bright, glad' (reconstructed; the root idea is smoothness and sheen; source of Old English glæd 'bright, glad, shining' and modern 'glad'); 1st millennium BCE. - Old Norse glaðr 'bright, glad, shining' (attested; applied to a clear, bright sky — e.g. a 'bright' or 'clear' heaven — and it is this luminous sense that underlies the wood-opening meaning); 8th-13th c. CE. - English glade 'a bright or open space in a wood' (first attested 16th c.; the derivation from the Norse and English brightness word 'glad' is the standard account — a glade is 'a bright place' — and the word may have entered through northern and Scots usage); 16th c. CE onward. - Modern English glade 'an open grassy space or clearing in a wood' (sense consolidated in landscape writing); 17th-21st c. CE; in America also 'a treeless wet opening,' as in the Everglades. - Final node, bold: glade. Debate note: the origin of 'glade' is genuinely disputed. The Oxford English Dictionary marks it 'origin obscure,' and the usual derivation links it to Old Norse glaðr 'bright' (hence 'a bright place in the wood'), which would trace it to Proto-Germanic *gladaz and, speculatively, to PIE *gʰel- 'to shine, glow'; but some scholars prefer an independent Germanic formation meaning 'a cleared space,' and a direct descent from English 'glad' is doubted by many authorities even though speakers feel the link. The PIE stage, if any, is not securely attested: treat the Proto-Germanic and Old Norse chain as the confident core, and the deeper Indo-European link as uncertain.
