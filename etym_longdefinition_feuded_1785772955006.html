To have feuded is to have carried on a prolonged, bitter, and reciprocal hostility with another person, family, clan, faction, or institution — not a single quarrel but a sustained state of enmity in which each side repeatedly injures the other and refuses reconciliation. The word's gravitational center is the historical blood feud: a customary institution, found in many societies, in which an injury to one member of a kin group obliges the whole group to avenge it, and the vengeance in turn provokes counter-vengeance, so that hostility becomes hereditary and is governed by codes of honor rather than by courts. When someone says that two families "feuded for generations," the predicate encodes all of that weight: duration, mutuality, bitterness, memory, and the failure or impossibility of forgiveness.

As the past tense and past participle of the verb to feud, the form feuded usually summarizes an entire era of bad blood in a single finished clause — "they feuded over the boundary for twenty years" — and it can describe any of the modern domains into which the institutional model has been exported: dynastic rivalry, factional politics, corporate warfare, sports antipathies, and celebrity conflict conducted through the press and social media. In all of these the verb keeps its defining features: the enmity is long-term, it is mutual (each party attacks the other), it is emotionally charged with resentment and pride, and it typically involves a continuing series of incidents rather than one confrontation.

  • Core sense — prolonged mutual enmity: The plain meaning: to engage in a long-running, bitter hostility. The past form is frequently used with time expressions: feuded for decades, feuded for years, feuded since childhood.
  • The institutional blood-feud sense: In anthropology and medieval history, feud names a regulated system of kin-based vengeance (with its customary alternatives such as compensation payments, and its honor codes); "they feuded" reports participation in that system. The semantic bridge from anger to institution is that a feud is hostility that outlives the original injury and is transmitted to descendants.
  • The political and factional sense: Political machines, parliamentary blocs, and party leaders feud when they systematically undermine one another — over succession, policy, or patronage — across years. Bridge: factions behave like kin groups, treating defeat as dishonor requiring retaliation.
  • The celebrity and media sense: From the late twentieth century, entertainment journalism reports that stars, influencers, and public figures "feuded" via public statements, interviews, and social-media exchanges. Bridge: the modern idiom borrows the blood-feud model of serial public attack; "feud" is now frequently a headline-stable noun ("a long-running feud").
  • The commercial and corporate sense: Companies, executives, and industry rivals are described as having feuded over markets, patents, or standards. Bridge: competition becomes feud when it is personalized, repeated, and publicly destructive rather than purely economic.
  • The sports sense: Athletes, coaches, and franchises feud across seasons and matches; the word suggests that competitiveness has curdled into lasting personal antagonism.
  • Metonymic and evaluative extensions: Because the prototype is the blood feud, the verb often implies honor, pride, and unreason — so "they feuded" can silently judge the parties as petty, primitive, or obsessive. Conversely, in historical or anthropological contexts the word can be neutral or even respectful of the parties' sense of duty.
  • False-friend boundary — feud, not feudal: The noun-verb feud (enmity) is historically unrelated to feudal/fief (land tenure), which descends from Medieval Latin feudum. The near-identical spelling is a historical accident, not shared ancestry.

Syntactically, feuded is typically intransitive and often takes a prepositional complement: feuded with (a person or group), feuded over (a cause, inheritance, credit, or boundary), and occasionally feuded against. Register ranges from formal historical and legal prose through journalism to informal narrative; the word is never slangy in the way bickered can be, and it is far heavier than quarreled. It contrasts with reconciled, allied, cooperated, united. The present participle feuding and the gerund are common; the noun feud (a prolonged quarrel) is the base from which the verb derives.
