1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and the Concept of Following. The deep etymological ancestor of ensued is the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *sekʷ-, meaning "to follow, accompany, attend, or perceive". This root is one of the most productive and culturally significant verbs in the entire Indo-European lexicon. In Ancient Greek, it yielded hépomai (ἕπομαι, "I follow, accompany") and hoplon ("armor, tool"); in Sanskrit, it gave sacate ("accompanies, associates with"); in Avestan, hachatē ("follows"); in Old Irish, sechithir ("follows"); in Lithuanian, sekti ("to follow, trace"); and in Germanic, it produced Proto-Germanic *sehwanan ("to see", literally "to follow with the eyes", source of modern English see) and Old English secg ("a follower, retainer, warrior").

2. Classical Latin Morphology: From sequī to the Compound insequī. In the Italic branch, the PIE root developed into the Proto-Italic base *sekwōr, which produced the Classical Latin deponent verb sequī ("to follow, come after, pursue, attend"). In Latin word formation, sequī generated an extraordinarily fertile family of prefixed verbs that entered English through Romance, including consequī ("to follow with", source of consequence), persequī ("to follow thoroughly", source of persecute), prosequī ("to follow forward", source of prosecute), and exsequī ("to follow out", source of execute).

The direct Latin ancestor of ensue was the compound verb insequī, formed by attaching the prefix in- ("in, upon, after, against") to sequī. In Classical Latin (attested extensively in Cicero, Caesar, Virgil, and Livy), insequī meant "to follow close upon, press hard, pursue an enemy, follow in chronological order, or succeed in time". Caesar frequently wrote in his De Bello Gallico of cavalry forces that hostes insequuntur ("pursue the enemy hard upon their heels"), capturing the original physical urgency of chasing quarry.

3. Vulgar Latin Restructuring: The Deponent Transition. During Late Antiquity and the transition from Classical Latin to early Gallo-Romance (5th–8th centuries CE), the spoken language (Vulgar Latin) underwent a systematic structural overhaul. The complex Latin deponent verb system—verbs passive in form but active in meaning—was completely dismantled by speakers. Consequently, the deponent infinitive insequī was regularized into an active 3rd-conjugation verb: *insequere ("to follow after, chase").

4. Old French and Anglo-Norman Transmission: The Medieval Tripartite Rivalry. In Old French, *insequere evolved phonetically into ensuir or ensuivre (with Anglo-Norman dialectal variant ensuer), whose past participle was ensui or ensuivi. The initial Latin prefix in- shifted regularly to French en-, while the intervocalic -sequ- underwent lenition and vocalization into -suiv- or -su-.

When the Norman French vocabulary crossed into Middle English during the 14th century (c. 1380), a fascinating linguistic specialization took place. The single Latin root sequī had entered Middle English in three distinct borrowings:

  • Pursue (from Anglo-Norman pursuer / Old French poursuivre, Latin prosequī): Specialized into physical chasing of game, hunting criminals, and chasing personal ambitions.
  • Sue (from Anglo-Norman suer / Old French sievre, Latin sequī): Specialized almost exclusively into the legal sphere, meaning to institute legal proceedings or follow a lawsuit in court.
  • Ensue (from Anglo-Norman ensuer / Old French ensuir, Latin insequī): Adopted into Middle English as ensuen or insewen (past tense ensued), specializing into narrative and temporal sequence, logical consequence, and moral pursuit.

Early attestations of ensuen appear in Geoffrey Chaucer's translation of Boethius (Boece, c. 1380) and in John Trevisa's works. Chaucer used ensue to describe both logical consequences that follow from philosophical premises and the temporal succession of events.

5. Early Modern English and the Biblical Fossilization of Active "Ensue". During the 16th century, William Tyndale, Miles Coverdale, and the translators of the King James Bible of 1611 made a historic lexical decision. In translating the Greek New Testament passage 1 Peter 3:11 (zētēsatō eirēnēn kaì diōxatō autēn), they rendered the clause as: "Let him seek peace, and ensue it." By pairing seek with ensue, the translators preserved the archaic transitive French sense of "running after" or "actively pursuing". Because the King James Bible and the Anglican Book of Common Prayer were read aloud every week across the English-speaking world, this active sense became deeply fossilized in the liturgical consciousness, even as secular spoken English abandoned the transitive use entirely in favor of the intransitive sense ("to happen as a consequence").

6. Romanticism and the Rhetoric of Wordsworth's "Prelude". By the late 18th and early 19th centuries, the past tense ensued had become an indispensable element of English historical and narrative prose (prominently used by Edward Gibbon and David Hume). In William Wordsworth's The Prelude (Book I, line 108), the poet deploys the word with supreme elegance: "I spare to tell of what ensued, the life / In common things". Wordsworth uses ensued to evoke the rich, continuous, organic succession of rural life that greeted him at Grasmere. Rather than treating time as a series of disconnected shocks, ensued conveys the harmonious, seamless unfolding of natural experience. Over its two-thousand-year evolution from a Proto-Indo-European root of accompanying (*sekʷ-), through Roman military pursuit (insequī), chivalric Anglo-Norman court language, and biblical imperatives, ensued matured into one of the most evocative markers of narrative flow and consequence in the English language.
