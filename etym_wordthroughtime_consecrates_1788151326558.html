- [14th–15th c.] - 'consecrate' enters English through religious translation and liturgy: consecrating churches, bishops, the consecrated host - [perception: formal, ecclesiastical, technical]
- [16th–17th c.] - Widens to solemn secular dedication: kings, swords, flags, and lives are 'consecrated' to causes and persons; Milton consecrates poetry and liberty - [perception: elevated, devotional, idealistic]
- [18th–19th c.] - Romanticism turns the word inward: Wordsworth's 'Prelude' imagines a 'gift that consecrates the joy' — art and nature hallowing experience itself - [perception: spiritual but not necessarily religious]
- [19th c.] - Memorial and burial language: 'consecrated ground' for churchyards, war-memorial rhetoric, the consecration of flags and cemeteries - [perception: official, solemn, permanent]
- [20th c.] - Everyday speech narrows it back: mostly bishops, churches, the Eucharist; figurative use sounds deliberately grave or archaic - [perception: ritual, institutional]
- [today] - 'consecrates' survives mainly in liturgical, legal, and high-register prose, its resonance of 'set apart forever' still coloring every figurative use - [perception: hallowed, weighty]
