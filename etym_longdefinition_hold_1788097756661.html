In the context of the note — the vessel and the sailors hauling big chests out of the hold with ropes — a hold is the interior space of a ship, below the main deck, in which cargo is stowed. It is the ship’s belly: a large, dark, roughly finished cavity running from the forepeak toward the stern, entered through hatchways in the deck and worked by winches, tackles, and ropes. The word is an old nautical term recorded in English from the later sixteenth century.

Historically the word descends — through Dutch — from the same root that produced the native English word hole: the “hollow” of the hull. Its spelling with -d, however, is not etymological but analogical: the Dutch word hol (“hollow, cavity”) was taken over by English seamen, and the written form soon acquired a -d under the influence of the unrelated common verb hold (“to hold, keep”), so that “holle” became “hould” and finally “hold”. The modern noun is thus a striking case of folk-etymology spelling reshaping a borrowed word.

  • Cargo hold (core sense): the freight space of a merchant vessel, between the lowest deck and the keel or tank top; full of crates, chests, bales, and barrels, it is loaded and discharged through the hatches. This is the sense of the context sentence.
  • Parts of the hold: the forehold and afterhold (the forward and after cargo spaces), the main hold (the principal one amidships), and the ’tween-decks (intermediate spaces between decks), separated by bulkheads.
  • Aviation sense: by extension, the luggage and freight compartments of an aircraft are the “baggage hold” or “cargo hold”; the word travelled from ships to planes in the twentieth century with its meaning of “interior stowage space” intact.
  • Verb-derived noun senses (a different word with the same spelling): a “hold” is also a grasp or grip (“take hold of the rope”), a controlling influence (“she has a hold over him”), a wrestling or martial-arts hold (a grip used to control an opponent), a delay or pause (a telephone call “on hold”, a merger “on hold”), and in finance a “hold” recommendation — keep the shares rather than buy or sell. In all of these, “hold” is a noun formed from the native verb healdan “to keep, grasp”, not from the Dutch word; the two holds are homonyms that happen to share a spelling.
  • Figurative compounds: “no holds barred” (from wrestling, “without restrictions on grips”), “to get hold of”, “to lay hold on”, “foothold”, “handhold”, “stronghold” — all built on the grasping noun of the verb.

Semantic bridge. The nautical hold is simply the hollow of the hull — the cavity that “holds” the cargo in the colloquial sense, which is why the borrowed Dutch hol felt so natural to English-speakers and why the spelling absorbed the -d of the verb. The enduring image is of darkness and containment: the hold as the hidden, stinking, dangerous interior of the ship, in vivid contrast to the open deck above. In the slave trade the hold became notorious as the packed space of the human cargo — a meaning the word carries in historical and abolitionist literature and that still darkens its textual record.
