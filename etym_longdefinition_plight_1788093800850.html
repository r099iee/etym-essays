Core sense. Plight is a noun meaning "a condition or state, especially an unfortunate, difficult, or dangerous one" — a bad situation that a person or group is caught in: the plight of refugees, the plight of the poor, the plight of the flood victims. In the source context the townsfolk of Lake-town, ruined by the dragon, are "cheered... in their plight" by the thought of the dragon's hoard. The word is almost always negative today; a "plight" is something you suffer, endure, or are rescued from.

1. Primary senses.

  • Bad condition, predicament (dominant sense): a state of suffering, danger, hardship, or difficulty from which escape is hard. Collocations: in a sorry plight, the plight of ..., in their plight, to ease/relieve/address the plight of .... It is the standard humanitarian and journalistic word for the suffering of groups — refugees, the homeless, endangered peoples, farmers, workers.
  • Neutral "condition, state" (older, now rare): the original French-derived sense, "the way things stand", with no good-or-bad colouring — 16th-century English could speak of being "in a good plight" or "in an evil plight". This neutral sense survives only in archaic and literary use; modern speakers hear only the bad one.
  • The homograph "plight" (pledge): a separate word, identical in spelling and pronunciation, meaning "a pledge, promise, engagement" — and the verb "to plight" = "to pledge", famous from the marriage vow "I plight thee my troth". This is a different word entirely (from Old English pliht), but it is part of the modern word's story, because its spelling and its "danger" sense contaminated the French word in the 16th century.

2. Semantic bridge. The modern word descends from Old French plit/pleit, the past participle of pleier "to fold" (Latin plicāre), used as a noun meaning "a fold" and then "a condition, a state" — the state of a thing being the way it is folded or arranged, like the way cloth lies in folds. In Middle English (c. 1200) "plit" meant simply "condition, state". But English also had a native word pliht (Old English) meaning "danger, peril, pledge". In the 16th century the two words collided: the French-derived "condition" word took the spelling plight, and the old word's sense of "danger, peril" bled into it. The result: "plight" was fixed as the word for a bad condition — a state that is also a peril. The bridge is a spelling accident with semantic consequences.

3. Idioms and fixed phrases. "In a sorry plight" (a miserable condition), "in dire plight", "to relieve/address the plight of ..." (the standard humanitarian formula), "a tale of woe and plight". The word is nearly always singular and nearly always preceded by "the" when used of a group: "the plight of the refugees". You would not normally say "their plights" for separate misfortunes; the word names a shared condition. It pairs freely with adjectives of misery — "wretched plight", "sad plight" — a collocation pattern that further locked in its negative polarity.

4. Register and usage. Formal-to-neutral; heavily used in journalism, humanitarian appeals, and history writing; common patterns: the plight of refugees/the poor/workers, in a sorry plight, in dire plight, to highlight/address/ignore the plight of .... It is not slang and not light: you would not speak of a "plight" over a parking ticket. Synonyms by register: formal "predicament, straits", informal "bind, jam, fix, pickle". The pledge-sense homograph survives only in fixed archaic phrases ("plight one's troth") and in legal-historical "plight" (a pledge of land).
