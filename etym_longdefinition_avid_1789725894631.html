AVID is an adjective meaning keenly desirous, eagerly enthusiastic, and wholeheartedly devoted: an avid reader, an avid gardener, an avid supporter, avid interest, avid curiosity. It profiles active hunger for an object — books, birds, football, knowledge — pursued not from duty but from appetite, with sustained attention and pleasure. The register is approving and mildly formal; teachers, obituarists, and reviewers praise avid readers and avid collectors where everyday talk might say keen, mad about, really into. To be avid is to be more than willing: it is to crave and to consume, as Samuel’s reader literally sates himself.

In the immediate context, AVID redeems idleness: Don Samuel goes as far as to describe him as talented, though bone idle, but notes that he was an ______ reader, and literally, to quote Samuel, ‘sated himself in books’. The concessive arc is talented though idle but avid: refusal of set schoolwork is reinterpreted as selective gluttony for self-chosen books. Avid here is not polite diligence but devouring appetite, glossed by sated — he gorged until full, like a starved animal at a carcass. The schoolmaster’s paradox resolves: the boy who would do nothing did everything, on his own terms.

  • Primary sense: eagerly enthusiastic, keenly devoted. An avid reader, an avid birdwatcher, avid fans, to follow with avid eyes. This is the dominant modern complimentary sense, gradable (very avid, most avid) and collocating with hobbies, audiences, and learners. Semantic bridge from Latin greed is softening by object: hunger for food condemned becomes hunger for books praised, the appetite frame kept while moral judgement flips. The bridge explains why avid still feels stronger than keen — it retains bodily craving.
  • Secondary sense: greedy, covetous, grasping (formal, often with for, of). Avid for power, avid for praise, avid ambitions, with avid gaze. Older and still current in literary prose, this keeps Latin avidus greedy undisguised. Bridge to the complimentary sense is лишь evaluation of the object: avid for money censures, avid for knowledge commends, but the psychology — insatiable wanting — is identical. Dictionaries list greedy first historically, enthusiastic first by frequency; both live side by side, disambiguated by complement.
  • Tertiary and technical nuances: biology, commerce, and intensity. Naturalists write of avid predators, avid feeders for organisms that consume voraciously; marketeers of avid consumers, avid demand for insatiable buying; journalists of avid attention, avid curiosity almost as an intensifier meaning rapt. The adverb avidly (to read avidly, to watch avidly) and noun avidity formalise the state. Bridge in all cases is hunger metaphor extended from stomach to eyes, ears, and wallets. Antonyms split: for enthusiastic indifferent, unenthusiastic, reluctant; for greedy generous, abstemious, indifferent.
  • Collocation and tone: avid reader is the flagship collocation, so fixed that it almost defines the word for learners; next come avid supporter, fan, collector, gardener, traveller. The construction avid for/of signals the older greedy sense, bare attributive avid + noun of hobby the newer praising sense. To upgrade keen reader to avid reader is to add devouring bulk — keen likes, avid sates.

Grammatically AVID is a gradable descriptive adjective, readily predicative (he was avid for books) or attributive (an avid reader). It implies sustained habit, not momentary eagerness: an avid reader reads always, not once. In Samuel’s sentence it does biographical work, converting school failure into autodidact legend — bone idleness at lessons proved avid hunger at books.
