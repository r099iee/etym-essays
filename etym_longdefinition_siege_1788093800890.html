Siege (noun) is the military operation in which an army surrounds and blockades a town, fortress, castle or other defended position, cutting off supplies, relief and escape and pressing the place by bombardment, assault, mining or starvation until it surrenders, is relieved or is stormed. The word’s core image is one of prolonged immobilisation: the besieging force “sits down” before the defended place and stays — the noun preserves, in its very form, the Latin root sedēre “to sit”. A siege differs from a battle or an attack in duration, in completeness (enforced encirclement) and in object (the fall of a place rather than the defeat of an army in the field).

1. The military sense (primary). Professional usage gives the siege a recognisable anatomy: (i) the encirclement of the place to sever communication and supply; (ii) approaches, trenches, batteries, counter-mining and siege-works pushed forward against the walls; (iii) repeated assaults or the slower siege of starvation, ending in capitulation, relief or negotiated surrender. The great historical sieges — Troy, Jerusalem (70 CE), Constantinople (1453), Malta (1565), Sebastopol (1854–55), Vicksburg (1863), Leningrad (1941–44) — define the word for the common imagination. The standard idioms are to lay siege to (a place), synonymous with to besiege; under siege describes the defenders’ state and is now the universal metaphor for any person or institution under heavy, sustained pressure (a government under siege, a reputation under siege). Modern warfare has largely replaced formal sieges with encirclements, blockades and prolonged urban operations, but the word is still applied to recent battles for cities: the siege of Sarajevo (1992–96, the longest European siege since 1945) and the siege of Mariupol (2022).

2. The original senses: seat, throne, rank. Before any military use, the English word (Middle English sege) meant “a seat, chair, throne; the seat of honour or authority”, inherited from Old French sege/siège “seat, throne”. In medieval romance a knight is “set in his siege of honour”; the word also meant “rank, station, position” (a man “of no great siege” = of no high rank); and in Malory the famous Siege Perilous is literally the Perilous Seat at the Round Table. This cluster survives today only archaically and in fixed literary phrases, but it is the semantic key to everything else: the military sense grew out of the image of an army “sitting” (taking its seat) before a town. The bridge is documented in Old French well before 1100 and had become the dominant sense by the time English borrowed the word.

3. Extended and figurative senses.

  • Prolonged affliction or pressure: a “siege of illness”, “the siege of grief”, a “siege of doubts” — a period of sustained assault by hardship, attested in English since the 17th century.
  • Romantic pursuit: to “lay siege to someone’s heart” (a cliché of the 18th–19th-century novel) casts courtship as a military investment — the beloved is a fortress, the suitor a patient besieger.
  • Legal–political: state of siege (French état de siège, a declared legal state of emergency in which military authority overrides civil government, formalised by French legislation of 1791 and 1849) entered English in the 19th century and denotes martial-law regimes.
  • Political psychology: siege mentality (20th century) names the defensive conviction of a group or leader that they are surrounded by enemies and must resist at all costs; originally a term of social psychology, now applied to nations, corporations and public figures.
  • Economics: siege economy (20th–21st century) describes a national economy organised for permanent scarcity under blockade or sanctions.

4. Grammatical and formal notes. The verb siege (“to besiege”) existed in English from the 16th to the 19th century but is now obsolete, its function taken by besiege. Under siege functions as an adjective; besiegement and siege-works are technical variants. The older “seat” sense survives inside the word memory: French siège still means both “seat” and “siege”, and English speakers who know the romance or Malory meet the ancient sense in the phrase Siege Perilous. Pronunciation is /siːdʒ/, preserving the Old French palatal -g-.
