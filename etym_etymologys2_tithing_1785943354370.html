Single chain for TITHING (a noun built on the ordinal "tenth"):
  • Reconstructed Proto-Indo-European *déḱm̥ — "ten" (c. 4500–3000 BCE); one of the best-attested numeral roots of the family.
  • Proto-Germanic *tehun — "ten" (c. 500 BCE–200 CE); PIE *ḱ regularly becomes Germanic *h, and final *-m̥ becomes *-n.
  • Proto-Germanic *tehundô — "the tenth, a tenth part" (ordinal formation with *-dô; c. 200–500 CE).
  • Old English tēoþa — "tenth" (attested 8th c.); the Germanic ordinal was reduced by loss of final syllables, giving the þ-stem form.
  • Old English tēoþung / tēoþing — "a tenth part; the taking of a tenth; a district of ten hides or ten households" (attested 9th–11th c.); the noun in -ung/-ing built directly on tēoþa.
  • Middle English tithe, tithing — "a tenth; the tenth paid as a church tax; a tithing district" (12th–14th c.).
  • Modern English TITHING — the practice or institution of paying a tenth, and the historic ten-household unit (bold target).
Notes on nuance and debate: the exact Old English noun form varies between tēoþung and tēoþing across texts. The ten-household administrative sense was systematized under the Norman frankpledge system of the 12th century, but the word and the grouping predate it in Anglo-Saxon law, and scholars debate how far the "district of ten hides" and the "ten-man group" senses were ever fully distinct. The sibling forms from the same PIE root include Latin decem "ten", Greek déka, Sanskrit dáśa, and the native English ten itself.
