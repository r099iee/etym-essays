forking in this context is the present participle of fork in the phrasal verb fork out (for something), an informal British idiom meaning to pay, hand over, or disburse money, especially unwillingly, under pressure, or in a large amount. "He must have got used to forking out" means he must by now be accustomed to regularly handing over cash to cover the magazine's costs. The out is an obligatory particle signalling outward movement of money from the payer; American English often uses fork over with the same sense.

The core pragmatic colour is reluctance. Unlike neutral pay, fork out dramatises the pain of parting with cash: the sums are felt as large, the occasion as imposed, the payer as grumbling. A parent forks out for school fees, a motorist forks out for repairs, a government forks out for a bailout. The verb is transitive on the money (fork out £200) or takes for for the purchase (fork out for a new roof), and appears in continuous and perfect aspects to stress repetition or duration ("keep forking out", "have forked out"). It belongs to conversational and journalistic registers, comic rather than formal, and would be replaced by "disburse" or "pay" in contracts.

  • Primary sense — to pay reluctantly: to hand over money when one would rather not. Example: "I forked out ten quid for the ticket." The historical bridge is manual: either pitching money outward with a hayfork, or producing it with forked fingers. Both images stress physical extraction, hence the feeling of compulsion that survives today.
  • Extended sense — to hand over anything, not just money: recorded from 1835 ("fork it out — to produce anything by the hand"), as in "fork out the documents" or "come on, fork over the keys". The sense generalises from cash to any reluctantly surrendered object, including information or favours, though money remains prototypical.
  • Literal and branching senses of fork (distinct): the same verb fork also means to divide into branches ("the road forks"), to pierce or lift with a fork ("fork the hay"), to insert a forked stake, and in chess to attack two pieces at once. The present participle forking in gardening ("forking over the bed") or traffic ("forking left") has nothing to do with money; only the particle out/over plus a money object triggers the pay reading.
  • Taboo-avoidance homophone note: modern vulgar slang uses "forking" as a minced oath for the f-word ("what the forking hell"), especially in American sitcoms. This is a separate euphemistic development by consonant substitution and must not be confused with Wodehouse's innocent 1930s "forking out". Context — out + money versus exclamation — disambiguates completely.
  • Register, grammar, and collocation: informal, transitive phrasal, separable ("fork the money out / fork out the money"). Typical subjects are individuals, firms, or the state; typical objects are sums, fees, damages, bail. Synonyms grade by formality: shell out and cough up are equally slangy and reluctant; disburse and remit are formal and neutral; pay is neutral and unmarked.

In short, forking out profiles habitual, slightly pained payment. Wodehouse chooses it to make Uncle Tom's generosity sound like a habit formed by repeated extraction — affectionate mockery of the rich backer who keeps pitching cash like hay. Learners should anchor on fingers like fork tines gripping coins: to fork out is to prise those fingers open.
