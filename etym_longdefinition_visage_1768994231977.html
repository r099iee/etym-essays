visage (noun). The face, with a special emphasis on its form, proportions, and expressiveness as a whole — the word looks at the face as a composed object rather than a collection of features. It is the face regarded from outside, as a single visible surface: countenance, appearance, aspect. Etymologically it is “the seen thing” — the look a person presents to the eye. In the passage, “All history present in that visage, the child the father of the man,” the word does rare and heavy work. The sentence reaches back to Wordsworth's “My Heart Leaps Up” (“the Child is father of the Man”) and is spoken of a boy who carries the whole weight of his ancestors in his face: heredity made visible, “all history” compressed into a single gaze.

Tone and register. Visage is an elevated, literary word. It does not say “face”; it says countenance-as-monument, the face as a public record carved in living flesh. Where a plainer word would describe what the child looked like, visage declares that his face is a document — an archive of the blood that cannot be falsified by a failed schoolmaster's pretensions or erased by a drunkard's collapse. The word also carries a faint archaic dignity appropriate to the novel's biblical cadence, and in context it renders the boy's features almost sculptural: a marble visage in which the past is literally present.

Polysemy and secondary senses. (1) Primary: the human face, with reference to its form and features. (2) Extended: the face of an animal, or — by personification — the front or face of a thing: the visage of a building, of the earth, of a mountain; the surface presented outward. (3) Metonymic: the look, aspect, or expression — “a grim visage,” “a stern visage” — where the word drifts from the physical face toward the mood it betrays. (4) Archaic and elevated compounds: grim-visaged, well-visaged; visage is the classic word of epic and romance for the face as the emblem of character. (5) Modern register: today the word is rare and deliberate; it survives in poetic, heraldic, and mock-archaic use, and to use it is to view a face as a document or monument rather than a collection of features. The semantic bridge is continuous: because a visage is “the seen thing,” it has always been read as evidence — the face as the visible proof of what a person is, which is precisely the sense the passage exploits.

  • Primary sense: the human face, viewed as a whole with its form and proportions.
  • Secondary sense: the face or front surface of anything, especially by personification — the visage of a building, of the land.
  • Tertiary sense: expression, look, aspect — the outward appearance betraying mood or character (a stern visage).
  • Archaic compounds: grim-visaged, well-visaged; the epic and heraldic register.
  • Historical bridge: Latin visus “the seen thing” → Old French vis “face” → visage “the whole seen countenance”; the face has always been the visible evidence of the person, so “all history present in that visage” is the word's oldest promise fulfilled.
