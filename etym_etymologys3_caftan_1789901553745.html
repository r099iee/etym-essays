CAFTAN enters English in the 1590s as caftan, also spelled kaftan, denoting a long tunic worn by men in Turkey, Egypt and neighbouring lands. The Oxford English Dictionary treats it as a borrowing from Turkish qaftan, itself from Persian khaftan, a word already denoting a long vest, robe or coat of mail tied at the waist with long sleeves. Etymonline dates the first English sense to 1590s travellers accounts of the Ottoman world, with the Western fashion sense of a similar loose dress only from about 1955. There is no Proto-Indo-European root in this chain; the trail stops at Persian, whose deeper origin is obscure, and scholarly practice requires stating that limit rather than inventing a PIE ancestor.

The Persian starting point matters because khaftan already combined utility, status and ceremony. In medieval Persian usage it could mean an ordinary long coat and also a quilted coat worn under armour, which explains why early European glossaries sometimes define caftan as a vest and sometimes as a coat of mail. Ottoman Turkish qaftan narrowed and elevated the garment into a central institution: ankle-length, long-sleeved robes of cotton, silk, velvet or brocade, often sashed, whose fabric, colour and fur trimming signalled rank. The sultan bestowed hilat robes as honours upon viziers, generals, clergy and foreign ambassadors, so that to receive a caftan was to be publicly enrolled in favour. European merchants, diplomats and travel writers from the sixteenth century onward described these presentations in detail, and their reports carried the Turkish word into French caftan, Italian caffettano and English caftan with the gloss long Turkish vest.

  • Persian khaftan: everyday long coat and military quilted coat, the semantic source of both protection and dress.
  • Ottoman qaftan: courtly system of honour robes, graded by cloth, lining and fur, presented ceremonially.
  • Early modern European borrowing: travel narrative word for Turkish dress, 1590s in English, parallel Russian kaftan for the native long coat.
  • Modern fashion caftan from c.1955: loose women's dress or beach cover, inspired by Middle Eastern and North African cuts.

Phonetic change along this path is minimal, which is typical of cultural borrowings. Persian kh- with a velar fricative becomes Turkish q-, a voiceless uvular or velar stop often transliterated q or k, then European c- or k-. Hence the modern doublet caftan and kaftan records the same Turkish form through French-influenced c- and more phonetic k- spellings, both pronounced with initial k. Stress in Received Pronunciation is usually on the first syllable, with a reduced second vowel, while some speakers stress the second syllable under French influence. No Germanic sound shift or Latin palatalisation is involved; the word was simply fitted to English spelling.

Russian history gives an important parallel that often confuses learners. Russian kaftan, attested from the medieval period, is the same Turkish loan applied to the long-skirted coat worn by peasants, merchants, coachmen and Old Believers, closed with hooks or a sash, exactly the wretched gray garment in the present context contrasting with merchants in boots and fur coats. English did not borrow the word through Russian; English and Russian borrowed independently from Turkish. That explains why the English fashion word feels exotic and the Russian word feels native and peasant-like, though their forms converge.

Literary attestations track widening familiarity. Sixteenth and seventeenth century Hakluyt-type voyages use caftan for Turkish ceremony. Nineteenth century translations of Russian literature, including Gogol, Tolstoy and folk tales, use kaftan or caftan for the peasant coat, often with adjectives plain, gray or wretched to mark poverty against fur-trimmed wealth. Twentieth century Vogue and department-store catalogues from the mid-1950s reintroduce caftan as glamorous leisure wear, popularised by designers and worn by film stars on holiday, which then extends to any loose ankle-length dress even with no Middle Eastern construction. OED therefore records both the historical and the fashion senses as one continuous borrowing with shifted application, not two different words.
