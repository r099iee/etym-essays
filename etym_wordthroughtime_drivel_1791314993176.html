- c. 1000 Old English physiological origin - recorded as dreflian, strictly meaning "to slaver, drool, or run at the nose"; an earthy, physical, medical verb without intellectual connotations.
- 14th c. Middle English metaphorical awakening - recorded as drivelen / drevelen; begins to be applied to the slobbering and foolish babble of teething infants and senile dotards in satirical and devotional literature (e.g. William Langland's Piers Plowman).
- 16th–17th c. Early Modern verbal consolidation - the metaphorical sense ("to speak foolishly, babble like a dotard") eclipses the literal physical sense in literature; Shakespeare and his contemporaries use driveller as a stinging insult for an idiot.
- 18th–19th c. Nominalization and critical weapon - the noun drivel emerges and becomes a favorite weapon of Victorian essayists, reviewers, and satirists to condemn bad poetry, sentimental novels, and vacuous journalism.
- 20th c. Comic British dialogue - immortalized by P. G. Wodehouse as the quintessential upper-class reprimand ("Must you drivel, Bertie?"), capturing affectionate exasperation in drawing-room comedy.
- 21st c. Modern media criticism - widely utilized in political commentary, book reviews, and internet discourse to dismiss sensationalist, unscientific, or vapid content; the literal salivary meaning is now archaic.
