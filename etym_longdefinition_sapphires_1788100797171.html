SAPPHIRES are the plural of sapphire, one of the two precious varieties of the mineral corundum (crystalline aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃), the other being the ruby. Sapphires are prized above all for a deep, velvety blue — the celebrated "cornflower" and "royal" blues of Kashmir, Ceylon and Burma — though "fancy" sapphires occur in pink, yellow, green, orange, violet and the rare pink-orange padparadscha. On the Mohs hardness scale sapphire scores 9, second only to diamond, which is why it has been used for two millennia both as a gemstone and, in modern industry, as a nearly indestructible material.

Primary sense — the blue gemstone. A transparent to translucent corundum crystal of gem quality, cut as a faceted stone or polished as a cabochon. The blue colour arises from traces of iron and titanium in the crystal lattice. A cabochon rich in needle-like inclusions of rutile displays a six-rayed star of light — the asterism of the "star sapphire". Dealers grade stones by colour saturation, origin and treatment, and the trade vocabulary is precise: "cornflower blue", "royal blue", "velvet blue", "Ceylon", "Burmese", heat-treated, lattice-diffused, synthetic (Verneuil).

  • Mineralogical and gemmological jargon. In the trade, unqualified "sapphire" means blue; any other colour must be specified ("pink sapphire", "yellow sapphire"). Since 1902, when Auguste Verneuil perfected flame-fusion growth, "sapphire" has also named a technical material: synthetic corundum is grown for watch crystals, smartphone screens, LED substrates and laser optics, and a titanium-doped sapphire crystal is the gain medium of the tunable Ti-sapphire laser.
  • Historical sense — the stone the ancients meant. In antiquity the Greek sappheiros and Hebrew sappîr named a deep-blue stone that was almost certainly lapis lazuli from the mines of Badakhshan (Afghanistan), not the hard corundum gem of today. The Septuagint, the Vulgate and the Book of Revelation (21:19, where sapphire is the second foundation stone of the New Jerusalem) therefore speak of "sapphire" where a mineralogist would say lapis lazuli; the name transferred to blue corundum only in the Middle Ages, when Ceylonese corundum inherited the ancient name of the blue stone.
  • Religious, heraldic and regal symbolism. In Christian tradition the sapphire stood for heaven, chastity, truth and the contemplative life; bishops wore it in their rings, and it was set in crowns, crosses and pontifical regalia as the emblem of heavenly wisdom. In heraldry "sapphire" denotes the tincture azure. The royal halo persists: sapphires are the September birthstone, the 45th wedding anniversary is the "sapphire wedding", and Queen Elizabeth II's sixty-fifth year on the throne (2017) was called her Sapphire Jubilee.
  • Figurative and colour sense — "sapphire blue". The word has long done duty as a colour name: poets and novelists speak of "sapphire skies", "sapphire seas" and — the path Wilde's sentence takes — "sapphire eyes". The plural in the text ("for eyes he had two bright sapphires") exploits exactly this ocular metaphor: the gems stand for brilliance, worth and, as they are given away one by one, for compassion.
  • Commemorative and trade senses. Beyond the jeweller's shop, "sapphire" appears in the names of awards, of places (the Sapphire Coast in New South Wales, where the gem is mined) and of anniversary markers, all radiating from the same core value: rare, hard, blue, precious.

Semantic bridge. Every secondary sense descends from a single core — a hard, blue, precious stone: the same object moved from Near-Eastern devotional material, through Greek name and biblical translation, to medieval ecclesiastical gem, Renaissance mineral specimen, Victorian luxury commodity, and finally industrial material and colour metaphor. The name travelled with the stone, while the stone's identity sharpened historically from "any blue stone" to "precisely corundum" — so that today the word names both the September birthstone and the crystal in a smartphone screen, with the full history of the blue stone folded inside.

In context. In Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888) the statue of the Prince is "gilded all over with thin leaves of fine gold", has "two bright sapphires" for eyes and "a large red ruby" glowing on his sword-hilt. The sentence inventories the statue's treasure; the Prince's later self-stripping — sending first one sapphire to a poor seamstress, then the other to a match-girl — measures the cost of compassion against the value the word "sapphires" carries. The plural form refers to the paired gems; its connotations of rarity, brilliance and worth make the Prince's sacrifice visible, and its heavenly colour symbolism quietly frames the story's religious allegory.
