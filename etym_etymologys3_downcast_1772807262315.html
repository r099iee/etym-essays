Section 1 — the history of “down” (first element) The first half of downcast is one of the most instructive accidents of English lexical history, because the ordinary adverb down, which now simply names direction toward the ground, begins as a noun meaning “hill.” Old English dūn meant a hill or an expanse of elevated, open ground—a sense still alive in proper names and in words like the Downs of southern England. The deep ancestry of dūn is debated. The most widely accepted account treats it as a loan from Celtic: Gaulish place-names in -dūnum (Lugdūnum, Camulodūnum, Augustodūnum) and Old Irish dún, “a fortress or fortified hill,” point to a Proto-Celtic *dūno- meaning roughly “fortified place,” and the Brittonic languages that English met in Britain (Welsh din, Cornish din) supplied the hill-word that became Old English dūn. On this view the English word preserves the hill-fort terminology of the Celts, transferred from “fortified height” to “height” in general. The ultimate PIE ancestry of *dūno- is not securely established; it is sometimes connected with a root meaning “to enclose” or “to settle,” but the connection is disputed, and the safest scholarly statement is that the Celtic form has no agreed Indo-European etymon. From the noun came a prepositional phrase that changed the language. Old English of dūne literally meant “off the hill,” hence “from high ground down to low ground,” “downward.” The phrase and its one-word relative adūne wore down in Middle English to doun, the plain adverb meaning “to or at a lower place.” Notice the semantic economy: an entire journey down a hillside is compressed into a single preposition meaning merely “down.” The noun sense receded except in fixed contexts; the adverb became one of the commonest words in the language, proliferating into phrasal verbs (come down, sit down, fall down, settle down), prepositional uses (down the street), adjectival uses (down payment, down-and-out), and noun uses (the ups and downs, a down). Each of these developments is a separate small semantic event, but all share the single underlying image of downward motion first carved out on the hillside. The relation between the English hill-word and the dune-words of the continental coast—Dutch duin, German Düne, and the English loanword dune—complicates the picture. These “elevated sand-hill” words are probably related to the Celtic *dūno- family as well, either as loans from Celtic or as cognates from a shared north-west European source; scholars differ on whether English dūn itself should be classed as a Celtic loanword or as part of a wider regional word for high ground. What matters for downcast is that the adverbial branch is entirely secure: hill → from the hill → downward. Socio-culturally, the shift from “hill” to “down” is a story of ordinary usage. The word was never a learned or bookish import; it was the everyday speech of farmers and townsfolk, and that is why it became a grammatical workhorse. By the time Shakespeare wrote his “down” rhythms, down had been an adverb for three centuries, and its visual economy made it the natural partner for verbs of throwing and placing. The first element of downcast, then, is not a rare or archaic word but the commonest possible spatial word, whose hidden etymology still means, at bottom, “down from the hill.” Section 2 — the history of “cast” (second element) The second half of downcast is a Scandinavian gift. Old Norse kasta, “to throw, fling, hurl,” is first recorded in the Viking Age, and its ultimate origin is unknown: no secure Proto-Indo-European ancestor has been reconstructed for it, and most handbooks treat it as a word of expressive or obscure formation. What is certain is its entry into English. When Scandinavian settlers and their descendants dominated the Danelaw in the ninth and tenth centuries, Norse everyday verbs streamed into northern English dialects, and kasta, “to throw,” established itself beside the native throw and fling. Middle English casten is attested from about 1200 and quickly proved extraordinarily productive. The semantic expansion of cast is one of the great lexical careers of the language, and every branch grows from the core idea of throwing. From “to hurl” the verb extended to “to throw one’s gaze”—cast an eye or a look, “to direct one’s glance at something.” From throwing metal came “to mould, to shape in a mould” (cast iron, cast a statue). From casting accounts, figuratively, came “to calculate, to reckon” (cast a sum). From the idea of assigning came “to cast a part in a play” and “to cast a vote” (to throw a vote into the urn). The noun followed the verb: a cast of characters, a plaster cast, a fishing cast, the cast of one’s features, a cast in the eye. The rapid multiplication of senses made cast one of the most flexible verbs of English, a position it still holds today. Within that expansion the phrase cast down deserves special notice, because it is the direct ancestor of downcast. To cast something down is to throw it to the ground, to overthrow it; to cast down the eyes is to lower the gaze—the universal body-language of shame, grief, modesty, and defeat; to be cast down in spirit is to be disheartened, abased, discouraged. The King James Bible abounds in the phrase: “Why art thou cast down, O my soul?” (Psalm 42), where cast down hovers between physical fall and spiritual despair. The participial adjective downcast compacted the whole phrase: first “thrown down, overthrown” of things and fortunes (14th–15th centuries), then “with the eyes lowered” (16th century), then “dejected, despondent” of the whole person (17th century onward). Culturally, the word’s rise tracks the prestige of biblical language and of body-language itself. The metaphoric path—throw → throw down → throw the eyes down → throw the spirits down—runs through devotional prose, Elizabethan drama, and the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century novel, and it is exactly the path activated in the Ying-lian episode, where the wife insists there is “no cause to be downcast” and the girl’s brief return of cheer is a momentary raising of what had been cast down. The compound has never lost the physical image: to this day downcast eyes are the eyes cast toward the ground, and a downcast man is one whose inner self has been thrown down as surely as a wall is overthrown. Section 3 — the fusion The compound fuses the two histories: a word of hill-descent (down) and a Norse verb of throwing (cast), joined as early as the fourteenth century. The first meaning was literal—things and fortunes thrown down, overthrown. The gradual shift of weight from the physical to the emotional, completed in the seventeenth century, is a perfect specimen of how ordinary English builds abstract vocabulary from concrete spatial metaphor, and it remains audible in every use of the adjective. Downcast is thus a small museum of English historical layers: Celtic hill-forts, Viking ships, biblical lamentation, and the body-language of grief all compressed into two syllables.
