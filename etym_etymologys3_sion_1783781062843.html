SION is a toponym of Semitic origin with no Proto-Indo-European ancestry, and its biography is the biography of a sacred hill that outgrew its geography. The Hebrew proper name Ṣiyyōn first attaches to the narrow southeastern ridge of Jerusalem, held by the Jebusites, a pre-Israelite Canaanite people, before David's conquest. 2 Samuel 5:7 records the moment: "David took the strong hold of Zion: the same is the city of David." The name itself is almost universally connected by Semitic lexicographers with the root that denotes dryness and parched ground — Hebrew ṣiyyā, "drought, dry land," and Arabic ṣiyy, "waterless ground" — so that Ṣiyyōn plausibly began as plain topography: "the dry height," the bare limestone ridge well suited to a fortress. The Ugaritic archive of Ras Shamra (14th century BCE) preserves the related verb ṣyw, "to be dry," confirming the antiquity of the root within the family. Because the ridge was the ancient summit of a pre-Israelite settlement, some scholars have proposed a Hurrian or Anatolian substratum origin; this is a minority position, unverifiable on present evidence, and the standard account continues to favor the Semitic root. It was the hill's religious career that fixed its shape as a word. With David's conquest and the translation of the Ark to the city (2 Samuel 6), Ṣiyyōn became the cult-center of the monarchy; with Solomon's Temple on the adjacent Moriah heights (1 Kings 8), the whole area became the seat of the divine presence. The name then began a steady semantic climb from "citadel-hill" to "sanctuary of God" and finally to "the holy city itself." The Psalmists make the expansion explicit: "Out of Zion, the perfection of beauty, God hath shined" (Psalm 50:2); "the Lord hath chosen Zion; he hath desired it for his habitation" (Psalm 132:13). By synecdoche the whole of Jerusalem could be addressed as Zion — "the daughter of Zion," "the gates of Zion" — and the Prophets made the name the pivot of promise and judgment alike: comfort is promised "to her that is in Zion," and the Law is to go forth "out of Zion" (Micah 4:2). The exile and return gave the name its national and emotional load. The singers of Psalm 137 hang their harps on the willows because they cannot sing "the songs of Zion" in a foreign land; the return from Babylon is a return to Zion; and in post-exilic prophecy Zion becomes a synonym for the people of God who are to be redeemed, restored, and glorified. The word is thus already a name for the community, not just the place, before the New Testament took it up. The New Testament transfers the name to the spiritual realm. Hebrews 12:22 writes, "ye are come unto mount Sion, and unto the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" — an explicit declaration that the earthly hill has become the heavenly city and the Church its citizens. 1 Peter 2:6 quotes Isaiah 28:16 of the "corner stone in Sion." In Revelation, the Lamb stands "on the mount Sion" (14:1) with the redeemed, and the New Jerusalem descends from heaven; Christian hymnody from the early Church to the present has sung of Zion as the Church militant and the Church triumphant, the heavenly home. The English forms of the name preserve a split in the transmission. The Septuagint transliterated the Hebrew as Σιών (Siōn), and Jerome's Vulgate kept the form Sion, which therefore reached English through the Latin Bible, the liturgy, and the older translations — Tyndale, the Geneva Bible, the King James Version (1611) all use "Sion" in many places, and the Book of Common Prayer retained it. The alternative "Zion," reflecting a more literal rendering of Hebrew Ṣiyyōn, gained ground in later Protestant and Jewish usage. Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) makes the older spelling theologically precise: "Sion Hill" is set beside "Siloa's Brook" as the twin source of true, biblical inspiration, deliberately opposed to the "Aonian Mount" of the pagan Muses — the hill of revelation outdoes the mountain of fable. In the modern period the two forms have drifted apart in register. "Zion" carries the full load of nineteenth- and twentieth-century symbolism: the national restoration of the Jewish people, the land of the future, the theme of Zionism. "Sion" remains archaic — the property of the King James Bible, the Prayer Book, the hymns, and the poetry of the seventeenth century. The same sacred hill, the same root meaning "dry height," thus now moves through scripture, theology, literature, and politics in two spellings that still point to one city. Sound and orthographic notes:
  • Hebrew ṣ- (צ), an emphatic sibilant, was rendered z- in Greek (Σιών) and Latin (Sion) in the older tradition, but s- or z- in later transliterations; English therefore shows both Sion and Zion.
  • The form Sion preserves the Latin vowel sequence; Milton scans it as two syllables, "Sī-on," fitting his blank-verse line.
  • The -on / -ōn ending reflects the Greek nominative and passed unchanged through Latin into English.
