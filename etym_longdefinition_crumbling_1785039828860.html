In Tolkien's first view of the mountain's lower slopes, crumbling is applied to rock that has been so long exposed to frost, wind, and drought that it has begun to break down into loose fragments and grit. The adjective does more than describe decay: it gives the whole landscape a texture and a history. The land is 'the colour of heather' — the browns and faded purples of dried vegetation — and the crumbling rock extends that dryness into the mineral world, suggesting stone so weathered and friable that it seems to be dissolving back into the earth. The passage notes 'patches and slashes of grass-green and moss-green showing where water might be', and crumbling is the negative image of that promise: it is what treeless country looks like when it is dying of thirst. The tone is therefore one of stark, ancient aridity — a slope without running water, without soil, without rest — and the present participle imparts a process still in motion: not stone already destroyed but stone in the act of falling apart, as though the entire mountainside were slowly disintegrating beneath the travellers' feet.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The verb crumble and its present participle crumbling cover a compact but significant range of meanings:

  • Concrete sense (mineral): to break into small fragments, of rock, earth, mortar, or plaster, through weathering or age — the sense here.
  • Concrete sense (food): to break bread, cake, or pastry into crumbs, either deliberately (crumbling cheese onto a salad) or accidentally (the biscuit crumbled in his hand); this is the oldest recorded use, close to the noun 'crumb'.
  • Intransitive process sense: to fall apart piece by piece under decay or pressure, of buildings, cliffs, ruins, or other structures — 'the old castle wall was crumbling'.
  • Figurative sense (systems, institutions, hopes): to collapse gradually from within — 'the empire crumbled', 'their confidence crumbled'; the bridge is the metaphor of a structure breaking into pieces no longer held together, exactly as masonry falls apart when its binding fails.
  • Figurative sense (resolve, health): to give way or weaken, of a person's resistance or condition — 'she crumbled under the pressure'.
  • Technical/domain uses: in geomorphology, 'crumbly' and 'crumbling' are standard for friable rock and soil; in cooking, 'to crumble' is a standard verb and the noun 'crumble' names a British baked dessert of stewed fruit with a crumbly topping (20th century); in agriculture, 'soil crumbling' describes the breakdown of clods into tilth.
  • Colloquial idiom: 'that's the way the cookie crumbles' — resignation to an uncontrollable outcome; a modern Americanism built on the food sense.

Nuance against near-synonyms. The nuance sharpens against its near-synonyms. Decaying and decomposing imply organic rot, the work of bacteria and moisture — the very opposite of this parched mineral collapse. Disintegrating is technical and neutral, stressing loss of cohesion into constituent parts, and lacks the gritty, crumb-like texture. Weathered names the agentless process of long exposure without any sense of breakage, while eroding presupposes an external force — water, wind, ice — carrying material away from without. Crumbling, by contrast, is internal failure: the rock breaks from within, collapsing under its own brittleness into a scatter of small fragments, exactly like a stale crust crushed in the hand. Placed where it is, the word makes the very mountainside seem as exhausted and fragile as an old ruin, a land whose stone is dying of thirst along with everything else.
