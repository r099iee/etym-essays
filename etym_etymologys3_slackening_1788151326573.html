1. A Germanic adjective for what hangs limp (pre-writing to Old English). The family of slackening begins with a word for slackness itself. Proto-Germanic *slakaz “slack, loose” lies behind Old Norse slakr and Old English slæc, one of those physical-moral adjectives the Germanic languages loved: it described at once a rope that had not been hauled taut, a mouth hanging open, and a man remiss in his duty. The OED records Old English slæc with senses “loose, lax, remiss, negligent,” so the double personality — physics and morals fused — was present from the first attestations. The deeper Indo-European connection, tentatively to a root meaning “to be slack or weak” cognate with Latin laxus and Greek λήγω “to cease,” is plausible but not beyond debate: for once the mundane word “slack” is simpler and better attested than its reconstructed ancestor.

2. The verb: slacken and the productive -en (16th c.). Middle English inherited the adjective slak(ke), still meaning both “loose” and “remiss.” The decisive development for this card’s word form came in the sixteenth century, when English used its native causative suffix -en (from Old English -nian/-n) to build new verbs from adjectives — harden, soften, quicken, gladden, and, from slack, slacken, attested in the middle of the century. The suffix did what the Old English verbal system had done: it made an adjective into an event. Slacken meant “to make slack” (transitive) and “to become slack” (intransitive) from the outset, and its participles and gerund — the form used on this card — followed immediately: the literature of Elizabethan seamanship and Elizabethan ethics alike could speak of slackening sheets and slackening diligence within a few decades of the verb’s birth.

3. The nautical and moral centuries (16th–18th c.). The verb’s first great habitat was the sea. In an age of sail, the difference between a taut and a slack halyard, sheet, or anchor cable was a matter of life and death, and nautical English adopted slacken for giving out line and letting sails ease. Crusoe, Cook, and the whole literature of voyage used it as a working word. On land, meanwhile, the moral sense thickened: slacken applied to pace, pursuit, watchfulness, and industry, almost always with the older note of reproof — to slacken in service, study, or war was to loosen a discipline that ought to hold. The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries fixed the idioms that still organise the word: slacken one’s pace, slacken effort, slacken vigilance, and the noun phrase slackening of + an abstract virtue or activity.

4. The statistical age: slackening becomes an economic and meteorological term (19th–21st c.). The nineteenth century’s industrial journalism found in slackening the exact image it needed for the behaviour of markets: trade, demand, and growth, like sails and muscles, could tauten and ease, and a “slackening of trade” described a downturn with perfect physical transparency. Meteorology and navigation gave the noun a second technical home (“the slackening of the wind”), and twentieth-century economics made “slackening demand” a fixed collocation of official analysis. The modern word is thus a rare thing: a native, homely verb that climbed into the highest registers of formal analysis without losing its rope-and-sail picture. In contemporary English, a central banker’s “slackening growth” and a sailor’s “slackening of the tide” are the same word doing the same job — naming the moment when the strain on the line lets go.
