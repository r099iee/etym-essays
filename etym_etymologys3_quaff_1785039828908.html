QUAFF makes its entrance into English in the early sixteenth century, and it arrives fully formed: the agent noun quaffer, "one who drinks deep," is attested by 1510, and the verb by the 1510s. Its origin, however, has resisted every attempt at a secure etymology, and no Proto-Indo-European root has been convincingly proposed. Three hypotheses compete, and honest dictionaries present all of them. The first is onomatopoeia: quaff may simply imitate the heavy, glugging, gurgling sound of rapid swallowing — the mouthful-by-mouthful noise of someone drinking deep from a tankard, a sound-meaning match of the most primitive kind. The second, favored by many authorities, derives the word from Low German quassen, "to overindulge in food and drink," on the theory that its double -ss- was misread or reshaped as -ff-, producing quaff; the Low German word itself belonged to the earthy vocabulary of feasting and excess. The third, older and now largely discredited, connected the verb to Old Irish cuäch, "cup, goblet, bowl," the source of the Scots quaich, a shallow two-handled drinking bowl, on the theory that to quaff originally meant to drain a quaich; the spelling and meaning make this the least likely of the three.

Whatever its source, the word's meaning has been strikingly stable across five centuries. From its first attestation quaff signified drinking copiously, in large draughts, and with gusto — a sense that never wavered. The noun followed in the 1570s, meaning "a deep draught" or the act of drinking so described, and the verb became a staple of festive and poetic diction. The age of Elizabeth and Shakespeare embraced it naturally: revellers in plays and ballads quaffed wine and ale from great goblets, and the word carried a hearty, convivial tone appropriate to tavern and feast. It suited the mock-heroic register of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, appearing in the verse of Milton and in the ballads and Gothic verse that kept such robust drinking words alive.

As everyday speech changed, quaff drifted toward the archaic and the deliberately jolly. Where once it was an ordinary word for generous drinking, it became the consciously old-fashioned verb of the stage and the historical novel — the robust toast of an Elizabethan costume drama rather than an item of everyday speech. This archaism is itself a kind of semantic change: the word did not shift its denotation but its register, moving from common use to the literary, the ceremonial, and eventually the comic. By the twentieth century a speaker who "quaffed" a pint was doing so in jest, playfully invoking the hearty, boisterous spirit the word had always carried. That spirit is why the word survives at all: it offers a flavor of abundance and relish that no ordinary drinking word quite matches.

The modern era added a new, productive descendant: quaffable, a twentieth-century coinage used chiefly in wine and beer writing to mean "pleasantly easy to drink in quantity" — a wine that invites a second glass without ceremony. Here the word's ancient association with copious, relishful drinking has been commercialized and domesticated, turned into a quality label for agreeable everyday beverages.

Phonetically the word is expressive in its own right: the initial qu- (originally pronounced /kw/, in casual speech often simplified), the short open vowel, and the brisk final -ff give a shape that is almost gulping — a rare case in which a word of disputed origin still feels as though the sound were the meaning.

In Tolkien's goblin song, While Goblins quaff, and Goblins laugh, the word's accumulated history does its work. The captors drink deep and gladly, in great draughts, with the coarse, triumphant relish of a feast in the dark; the old festive heartiness of the word is twisted into gluttony and menace, and the prisoners below hear the noise of their laughter rising round and round far underground. QUAFF thus carries into the modern sentence five centuries of robust conviviality — and in that sentence the conviviality is monstrous.
