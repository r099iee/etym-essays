- Classical Latin — obicere / obiectus: to throw before, to cast against, used of barriers, accusations, and things presented to the senses; physical and rhetorical at once.
- Medieval schools — obiectio: the formal move of the disputation, the difficulty cast against a thesis; objection becomes the engine of scholastic reasoning.
- 15th c. England — to object enters English as a learned verb of reasoning: clerks and councillors object difficulties against proposals in parliament and synod.
- 17th–19th c. — the courtroom adopts it: Objection! becomes the ritual cry of adversarial justice, and polite society makes objecting the civilised form of dissent.
- Today — the everyday verb of demurral, from boardrooms to living rooms: he objected signals a reasoned counter-voice, as in the source sentence, where one difficulty quietly stalls doing both.
