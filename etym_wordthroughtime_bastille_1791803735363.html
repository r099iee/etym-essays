- 14th c. — bastille enters English as a common noun for a fortified tower or small fortress, especially a gatehouse; a neutral term of military architecture.
- 17th c. — the Paris Bastille becomes a state prison, and the name begins to connote arbitrary royal power and the lettre de cachet.
- 1789 — the storming turns “the Bastille” into a European byword for despotism overthrown; the building is demolished and its stones are sold as relics.
- 1790s–19th c. — absorbed into revolutionary iconography; Carlyle (1837) and Dickens (1859) fix the scene in the literary imagination, and “a Bastille” becomes a general name for a prison of tyranny.
- 1880 — 14 July is established as the French national holiday, anchoring the name in the civic calendar as Bastille Day.
- 1989 — the bicentenary and the opening of the Opéra Bastille make the name denote a Paris district and a opera house as well as a fortress.
- Contemporary — “storming the Bastille” serves as a metaphor for any symbolic attack on an entrenched institution.
