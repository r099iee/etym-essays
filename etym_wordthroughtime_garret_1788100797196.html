- c. 1300: a plain military word — the watchtower or turret of a town wall (garite, from Old French), a place of guard and lookout; no emotion attached.
- 14th–15th c.: the word climbs down off the ramparts onto the private house — a turret chamber built on the roof; still architectural and neutral.
- 16th–18th c.: the room under the eaves — the humble top-floor chamber where servants sleep and the poor live; crowded, cold, cheap; a word for the lowest rung of housing.
- 19th c.: the romantic inversion — the "garret-artist": Bohemian myth rebrands the garret as the sacred workshop of the starving genius (Murger, La Bohème, Wilde's playwright); poverty under the eaves becomes a badge of authenticity.
- 19th c. (slang): the garret doubles as the head — being "wrong in the garret" means mad, giving the word a jokey second life.
- Today: a literary, affectionate word for a top-floor room or flat; estate agents prefer "loft conversion" and the rich prefer penthouses — the garret survives as nostalgia for artistic poverty.
