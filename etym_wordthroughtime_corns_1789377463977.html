- Late 13th to 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed into English from Old French *corne* (derived from Latin *cornū* 'horn'), originally signifying a horny callosity or hard excrescence on the human foot or toe.
- 15th to 16th Century (Early Modern English / Tudor Era) - Firmly established in vernacular domestic life and medical herbals; widely recognized in folk leechcraft and weather lore (aching corns foretelling rainfall or storms).
- 17th to 18th Century (Restoration & Augustan Eras) - Spawned vivid figurative idioms of social friction, specifically "to tread upon someone's corns," meaning to trespass upon personal sensitivities or wound someone's vanity.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Ubiquitous in domestic humor, comic theater, and literary satire (as in Oscar Wilde's sparkling dialogue in *The Remarkable Rocket*), serving as an emblem of irritable vulnerability and petty misanthropy.
- 20th to 21st Century (Modern Podiatry & Contemporary Usage) - Clinically classified into distinct dermatological pathologies (*heloma durum* and *heloma molle*), treated with targeted orthotics, keratolytic agents, and modern chiropody while surviving in popular idiom.`
