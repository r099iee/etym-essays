A larch is a tall coniferous tree of the genus Larix, remarkable among conifers for being deciduous: each autumn its soft, short needles turn golden and fall, carpeting the forest floor, and in spring the bare twigs green again. In The Hobbit, Fili and Kili climb 'to the top of a tall larch like an enormous Christmas tree' — the simile fixes the tree's familiar conical silhouette, a tapering stack of regular branches, while the larch adds extreme height and alpine hardiness. The tone is playful and slightly incongruous: a larch in the cold season would be nearly bare, its needles shed, so the 'enormous Christmas tree' joke rests on shape rather than foliage, quietly signalling the dwarves' hopeful, boyish scrambling and the storybook logic in which a bare tree can still be decked as a refuge. The larch's most remarkable feature — that a conifer can lose its needles — has made it a standing example in botany of a 'deciduous conifer', a paradox naturalists have singled out since antiquity: Pliny the Elder already noted in the 1st century AD that the Alpine larch sheds its foliage like a broadleaf, and the observation has never lost its power to startle.

Senses and compounds (polysemy). The word and its compounds cover several related denotations:

  • The tree itself — the genus Larix, with its distinctive soft, short needles, small upright cones, and deciduous habit; the sense in the Tolkien context.
  • Larch timber / larch wood — the durable, rot-resistant wood of the tree, historically prized for shipbuilding, railway sleepers, pit props, and fencing; larch boards and larch battens are still forestry terms today.
  • Larch resin / larch turpentine — the oleoresin of the tree, historically marketed as Venice turpentine and used in varnishes, artists' materials, and traditional medicine.
  • Larch bark — rich in tannins and used in tanning leather.
  • Larch bolete / larch mushroom — fungi such as Suillus grevillei that grow in association with larch roots, named from the tree.
  • Tamarack — the Algonquian name for the North American larch (Larix laricina), and Siberian larch for the Asian species whose timber is famous for rot-resistance.
  • Botanical terms — the adjective laricine 'of the larch', and the Linnaean genus name Larix (1753), which preserves the ancient word itself.

Register and imagery. In literature generally the larch symbolizes mountain resilience, autumn gold, and the hardy beauty of high altitudes, and Tolkien's choosing it for the climb gives the scene a sweep of alpine wildness that a homely orchard tree could not have provided. In forestry and natural history the word is neutral and technical; in poetry it names golden autumn woods and the northern frontier of tree growth. The distinction from its evergreen relatives is the whole point: pine, fir, and spruce keep their needles year-round — fir needles are soft and flat, pine needles long and clustered in pairs or bundles, spruce needles short, stiff, and four-sided — while the larch alone sheds its needles, which turn bright yellow before falling, and bears small upright cones; in winter its bare twigs distinguish it sharply from every other conifer of a European wood, and in spring it appears to re-green like a broadleaf. In Britain the larch was widely planted on highland estates from the 18th century for timber, and the name travelled into English through Old French and Latin — a long journey, via a lost Alpine language, for the name of a tree that had always known the mountains.
