The biography of shrewd is one of the strangest in the language — a story that runs from a tiny, venomous-looking mammal to a compliment for financial and literary judgment. It begins with the Old English noun scrēawa, the “shrew-mouse,” a creature of the family Soricidae: a tiny, ferocious, voracious insectivore that eats almost constantly and attacks animals far larger than itself. The animal name has no secure attested Indo-European ancestor; it is probably expressive or echoic, perhaps reflecting the creature’s shrill squeak or its frantic gnawing, and older scholarship’s attempts to attach it to a Germanic root *skreu-, “to cut, to shred,” rest on no firm evidence. The honest verdict is that the word’s deeper origin is unknown, and no PIE root can be responsibly claimed for it. What is certain is the folk-belief that surrounded the animal: the shrew was held to have a venomous bite, and to meet one was unlucky, so that to “shrew” a person or a beast was to bewitch or harm it.

From the animal the word leapt onto people. By the late thirteenth century, Middle English shrewe meant “a wicked, malicious, evil person,” and by the sixteenth century it had specialised as the standard term for a scolding, bad-tempered woman — the sense immortalised by Shakespeare in The Taming of the Shrew (c. 1590), where the fierce Katherina is the shrew who must be tamed. This figurative transfer is entirely natural: the tiny, snapping, aggressive shrew-mouse was the very image of a person who bites. From this noun English formed the verb shrewen, “to be wicked, to curse,” and from the past participle of that verb came the adjective schrewed — later shrewd — recorded from about 1300 in the sense “wicked, evil, malicious, depraved.” To be shrewd, at first, was to be cursed, venomous, dangerous.

Then, around the middle of the sixteenth century, the word executed its great semantic somersault. A person “shrewd” in the new sense was cunning, artful, knowing, sharp — and what had been a term of abuse became, gradually, a term of grudging respect. The engine of the change was admiration for worldly cleverness in an age of court politics, commerce, and religious manoeuvre: the same revaluation that made crafty, subtle, and sly words of admiration at their peaks. By the seventeenth century shrewd meant “keen, acute, sharp-witted, astute,” and by the eighteenth century it had become a plain compliment — the word Gibbon’s biographers use of Horace Walpole, the sharpest-eyed observer of eighteenth-century letters. Walpole’s own definition of the man of taste, his celebrated “clearness of sight,” is shrewdness by another name.

The word’s modern profile is quietly double-edged. On the surface it is wholly positive — astute, far-seeing, hard to deceive, the adjective of the good judge of men, markets, and manuscripts. But a faint undertone of the dark past survives: a shrewd observer is not merely clever but slightly sharp-edged, unsentimental, self-possessed, quick to exploit what he sees. The compliment therefore carries a residue of biting danger — the shadow of the venomous shrew — and it is precisely that hard edge that makes the praise feel so valuable: an endorsement from a shrewd observer is worth more because shrewdness is not easily won or easily fooled.

• Sound change: the shift from Old English scrēawa to modern shrewd involves regular Middle English developments (initial scr- → shr-, long ē to ū in the early modern Great Vowel Shift) plus the re-analysis of the past-participial -ed as an adjective ending; no stage, however, can be traced beyond Old English.
• Literary attestations: Chaucer uses shrewe of wicked persons; Shakespeare fixes the noun in the Taming of the Shrew; the adjective in its positive sense is everywhere in the essayists and novelists of the eighteenth century; and the modern idiom “a shrewd bargain,” “a shrewd guess,” “a shrewd investment” shows how completely the word has settled into the vocabulary of practical life.
• Socio-cultural factor: the word’s reversal from “cursed” to “smart” tracks the Renaissance and Enlightenment revaluation of worldly prudence over mere piety — the same cultural turn that refashioned the merchant as a hero and made “knowingness” a virtue.
