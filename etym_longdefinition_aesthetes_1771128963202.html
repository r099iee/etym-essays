Aesthetes is the plural of 'aesthete': people who have, or affect, a special and intense appreciation of art and beauty, and who make the pursuit of the beautiful a central principle of life. The word describes an identity more than a hobby. An aesthete does not merely like pictures or music; he or she treats refined sensibility as a discipline, a vocation, even a creed, holding that the experience of beauty is among the highest goods and that life should be arranged around it. In the reading context supplied with this card — 'He was well known among the ______ of the capital for his exquisite taste and vast collection of ancient scrolls' — the word names a recognisable social type: the connoisseur of the city, the cultivated collector whose taste is his passport into a circle of similarly refined spirits. The context captures both halves of the word's character — genuine, deep appreciation on one side, and a certain self-conscious performance of it on the other. The tone of 'aesthete' has always been double-edged, and the word carries its history with it. It was coined, in the 1880s, as a label for the devotees of the Aesthetic Movement in Britain — the champions of 'art for art's sake' who rejected the Victorian belief that art must instruct or improve. For its admirers the aesthete was a liberator, freeing beauty from morality and utility. For its satirists the aesthete was a figure of fun: precious, languid, affected, more devoted to peacock feathers and blue china than to anything real. That tension — between admiration for exquisite sensitivity and mockery of precious affectation — is built into the word and remains its defining quality. To call someone an aesthete can be a compliment or a sneer, or both at once. The full polysemy of the word can be set out exhaustively:
  • Sense 1 — a person devoted to art and beauty: the neutral, descriptive sense: anyone who has a deep, specialised appreciation of the beautiful, especially in the visual arts, literature, and music, and who lets that appreciation shape how they live.
  • Sense 2 — the Aesthetic Movement devotee (historical, 1880s): the original coinage — a follower or sympathiser of the Aesthetic Movement in late-Victorian England, of the circle of Wilde, Whistler, Rossetti, and Pater, holding the doctrine of 'art for art's sake.'
  • Sense 3 — the affected or precious aesthete (pejorative): the satirical sense fixed by the 1880s caricatures (Gilbert and Sullivan's operetta Patience, the cartoons of Punch): a person who puts on a show of refined taste, who is precious, languid, and disconnected from ordinary reality.
  • Sense 4 — the philosopher's aesthete: in aesthetics and existential thought, one who makes the aesthetic attitude the governing principle of life (as in Kierkegaard's 'aesthetic stage of existence'), valuing experience and beauty over duty and faith.
  • Sense 5 — root sense, 'one who perceives' (etymological): from Greek aisthētēs, 'one who perceives,' the literal ancestor of the modern word, though the modern meaning was a 19th-century invention, not an inheritance from antiquity.
The bridge between these senses is a century of cultural history. The Greek root of the word meant nothing more than perception — the raw intake of the senses — and German philosopher Alexander Baumgarten, in the mid-18th century, had coined 'aesthetics' as the science of sensory cognition, which is how the word came to mean the philosophy of beauty. When the British writers of the 1880s borrowed 'aesthete' from the Greek, they compressed all of this into a single social label: the person who lives for the perception of beauty. The word has been caught ever since between the seriousness of that philosophical inheritance and the absurdity of the caricatures that accompanied it — which is exactly the double life the card's sentence, with its connoisseur of 'exquisite taste' known throughout 'the capital,' so neatly captures.
