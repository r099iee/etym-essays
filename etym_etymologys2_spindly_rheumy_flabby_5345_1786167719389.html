Linear derivation chain for SPINDLY (root word one of three): R — PIE *(s)pen- (to draw, stretch, spin) — the root of drawing out, stretching, and spinning thread. → E — Proto-Germanic *spinnaną (to draw out and twist thread, to spin); → *spinilō / *spinil- (the spindle, the instrument of spinning). → D — Old English spinnan (to spin) and spinel (the spindle, the tapering rod for holding thread). → C — Middle English spinnan > spinne (verb) and spindel / spindle (noun: the spindle). → B — English spindle (noun) + the adjectival suffix -y. → A — spindly (coined 17th century: long and thin like a spindle, weak and fragile) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain *(s)pen- → *spinnaną → spinel → spindle → spindly is secure. The related Latin root pendēre ("to hang, be stretched out") shares the same PIE source and appears in English as pendant, pendulum, depend, append, and suspend, but is a separate (Latin) branch, not part of the English spindle lineage.
Linear derivation chain for RHEUMY (root word two of three): R — PIE *sreu- (to flow, stream) — the root of flowing water and streaming motion. → E — Ancient Greek rhein (to flow); → rheuma (a flow, a stream, a bodily discharge or fluxion). → D — Latin rheuma (a flow, a catarrhal discharge) — a direct Greek loan in the medical vocabulary. → C — Old French reume / rheume (a cold, catarrh, watery discharge) — borrowed from Latin. → B — Middle English reume / rheum (14th century: a watery discharge of the eyes or nose; catarrh) + the suffix -y. → A — rheumy (modern English: watery, discharging thin moisture, esp. of the eyes) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain is secure. The same PIE root *sreu- produced Sanskrit srávati ("flows," whence Hindi srot, "stream") and Proto-Germanic *straumaz ("stream"), both being independent collateral branches of the same ancient root.
Linear derivation chain for FLABBY (root word three of three): R — No PIE root: FLABBY is a native English formation of imitative / sound-symbolic origin, built on the expressive base *fl- which mimics loose, soft, swinging, flapping movement. → E — (Imitative base) Middle English flappe ("a blow, a slap," "to flap, to beat") — the ancestor of flap, expressing loose striking or swinging motion. → D — Modern English dialectal flab ("soft, loose flesh") — a back-formation from flabby, itself from the same imitative family. → C — English flab + the adjectival suffix -y. → B — flabby (first attested 1690s: soft, yielding, lacking firmness) ◄ TARGET (this is the final node). → A — (unused slot; the formation is complete at B, since the word is coined in its modern form). Note on the debate: Because flabby is imitative, no PIE ancestry exists and none should be assumed. Latin flaccus ("flabby, hanging down") is an independent, unrelated formation; the Latin-derived English word flaccid (18th century) resembles flabby in sense but is etymologically separate, being borrowed from Latin flaccidus.
