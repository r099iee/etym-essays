Striatum is a Latin word pressed into service by anatomy, and its biography is the story of how seventeenth-century scientists found, in a Roman mason's term, the perfect name for a striped brain region. The adjective behind it, Latin striatus, "furrowed, fluted, striped," derives from the noun stria, "a furrow, groove, or flute of a column" — the vertical channel cut into the shaft of a classical column. Both words are connected, in the standard scholarly account, to a Proto-Indo-European root *streig- (or *strig-), "to stroke, rub, or graze," the verb of drawing a line or groove across a surface, although the pre-Italic reconstruction of stria itself is not entirely certain. That root had a wide family: Latin stringere, "to touch, graze, draw tight," lies behind English strict and constrict; Latin strigilis was the bronze scraper Roman bathers drew across their skin to remove oil and dirt; and in the Germanic branch the same root produced a rich set of "stroke" verbs — Old English strican (whence our strike), Old English strica, "a stroke or line" (our streak), Old Norse strjuka, "to stroke, wipe," and Old High German strhhan, the ancestor of German streichen, "to stroke, spread, paint." The Roman stria itself, however, is the immediate ancestor that matters here, and it is worth pausing on the image it carries: a column is not random stone but shaped stone, and the stria is the groove that gives its shaft rhythm and light. When anatomists wanted a name for a brain region that looked channeled and banded, this was the word they reached for.

The crossing from architecture into the body happened in the Renaissance. When sixteenth- and seventeenth-century anatomists — working in the tradition of Vesalius — began slicing the human brain, they noticed that the deep subcortical mass now called the basal ganglia presented, on section, alternating pale and dark bands: bundles of white myelinated fibres passing through the grey cell bodies of the caudate nucleus and the putamen. Because the pattern recalled the fluting of a column or the stripes of striped cloth, they named the whole structure the corpus striatum, "the striped body." The name is a pure act of descriptive anatomy, carried in the international scientific Latin of the day, and it has survived intact for three centuries because the striping is real, striking, and diagnostic. German anatomists of the nineteenth century coined the native-language echo Streifenhuegel, "stripe-hill," for a related region, and the image proved so natural that it kept resurfacing across the map of the brain; the striate cortex, the visual area of the occipital lobe, is called striped for the same banded appearance.

In the twentieth century the full phrase contracted to the bare neuter noun striatum, and the word became a fixture of neuroscience. Its meaning also deepened with function. As researchers traced the circuits of movement, the striatum was revealed to be the brain's great convergence point: it receives input from nearly the entire cortex, and through the basal ganglia it gates the control of action, so that lesions there produce the movement disorders — the tremor and rigidity of Parkinson's disease, the chorea of Huntington's disease — that made the structure clinically famous. Then came the neurochemical revolution. The discovery that dopamine, produced by neurons of the midbrain's substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area, is delivered to the striatum recast the structure in a new light: not merely a movement centre, but a reward-processing hub. The ventral striatum, especially the nucleus accumbens, became the focus of addiction research, the structure hijacked by drugs and by the cues that predict rewards; the dorsal striatum became the site where repeated behaviour is engraved as habit. Finally, in the computational neuroscience of the 1990s and 2000s, the striatum was given its most precise modern role: it is the site of reward-prediction error. When an expected outcome arrives, the striatum's dopamine signal spikes; when it fails to arrive, the signal dips. The passage's "micro-dose of dopamine" and its "Good job, your prediction of reality was accurate!" is precisely this signal rendered in ordinary words.

The word has thus travelled from a Roman workshop to the pages of popular neuroscience without ever changing its literal meaning: a striped body. Each age found in the old name a fresh justification — first the visible bands, then the motor disorders they helped explain, then the dopamine circuit that made the stripes famous, then the computational signal that made them central to learning. What began as a purely descriptive label is now one of the best-understood and most-mentioned structures in the brain, and its name still carries the mason's groove at its root: stria, the channel cut into the column, has become the channel through which reward flows.
