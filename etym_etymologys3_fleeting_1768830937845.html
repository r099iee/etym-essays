fleeting — The biography of this word is the biography of water. Its earliest known ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *plew-, "to flow, to float, to swim," a water word through and through. The root spread across the Indo-European world with remarkable consistency of meaning: Sanskrit plavate means "he floats, he sails"; Greek pleo means "I sail"; Lithuanian plaukti means "to swim"; and in the Germanic branch it produced Proto-Germanic *fleutaną, "to flow, to float," whose daughters survive to this day — Dutch vlieten "to flow," German fließen "to flow," and, through a closely related route, the English family of fleet, fleeting, and the fleet of ships (a "fleet" being, etymologically, a collection of things that float). The decisive moment in the English line is Old English flēotan, "to float, to swim, to drift," a strong verb used of ships at sea, of swimmers, of anything carried along on a current. In the early medieval English mind the word was firmly aquatic: to flēotan was to be borne on water, moving not under one's own power but at the will of the stream. The Middle English continuation, fleten, kept that sense — "to float, to drift, to flow" — and the great semantic shift occurred when the image of the floating thing was transferred from water to time. Around the fourteenth century the present participle fleeting, literally "drifting," began to be used of things that pass quickly, exactly as things float past a stationary observer on the bank: a fleeting moment, a fleeting life, fleeting pleasure. The transfer was natural because a thing that floats does not abide — it is in motion, carried past us, and gone before we can grasp it — so the spatial meaning "drifting past" became the temporal meaning "passing quickly away." By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word was firmly established as a word of temporality, glossed in dictionaries as "fading, vanishing, transitory," and it had already acquired the elegiac colour it still wears: what is fleeting is usually what is loved, and loss is implicit in every sentence that uses it. The word's poetic career was confirmed by the Romantics, who found in fleeting the perfect name for the impermanence they both celebrated and mourned — the fleeting moment of beauty or inspiration that poetry exists to hold still. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the adjective spread into scientific and technical registers, always with the same core: a fleeting image in optics, fleeting thoughts in psychology, fleeting references in rhetoric. In our own time the word has entered computing as a name for deliberate impermanence — fleeting or ephemeral messages that delete themselves after being read — a strikingly literal modernization of the ancient image: information that drifts past and is gone. Key points of the word's evolution:
  • Sound shift: Old English flēotan became fleet as the long vowel simplified; the -ing participle was added to form the adjective, which then took on a life of its own independent of the now-archaic verb.
  • Semantic drift: "floating or drifting on water" → "moving past" → "passing quickly in time" → "transitory, ephemeral, momentary."
  • Connotation: From a neutral description of motion, the word gained a distinctly elegiac, melancholy tone — fleeting now implies something precious that cannot be kept.
  • Literary attestations: The word is characteristic of Romantic and Victorian lyric poetry; its best-known uses are of "fleeting beauty," "fleeting glance," and "fleeting moment," and of life itself described as fleeting.
  • Cognate family: fleet (swift), fleet-footed, fleetingness, fleetingly, and — through the same PIE root — the noun fleet (a group of ships), showing that the water image never fully receded.
The word's career is thus a perfect demonstration of how a concrete spatial image can be transferred to abstract time: the deepest fact about a fleeting thing is not that it is short, but that it is in motion away from us, carried on a current we cannot command — and that is precisely the sense the passage on this card deploys when it calls the colors of the flowers "fleeting functions of the sky."
