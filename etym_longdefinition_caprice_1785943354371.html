CAPRICE: A sudden, unpredictable change of mood, mind, or behaviour; an unaccountable whim, impulse, or freak of fancy; a disposition to act on momentary inclination rather than on reason, rule, or steadiness of purpose. PRIMARY SENSE: The core of the word is abrupt, motiveless change. A person acting by caprice switches course without warning, explanation, or regard for consequences; a monarch rules by caprice when decrees follow the mood of the hour; the weather obeys the caprice of the wind when it shifts without pattern. The noun implies the absence of any underlying law or rationale — the action is a bolt from nowhere, and this is exactly what makes it at once dangerous, fascinating, and faintly playful. It contrasts with a whim's mildness by being more dramatic, and with an impulse's blind drive by being a change of mind rather than a single push to act. SECONDARY AND DOMAIN SENSES:
  • Music (caprice / capriccio): a lively, free-form, virtuosic instrumental composition not bound by the strict sonata rules — from the Baroque to Paganini's 24 Caprices for solo violin (1820s), where caprice denotes a bravura study in technique and fantasy.
  • Art and architecture (capriccio): a fantastical landscape or view painting that combines real and imaginary buildings, ruins, and figures — the Venetian tradition of Canaletto, Marco Ricci, and Francesco Guardi, in which the artist's "whim" rearranges reality.
  • Fashion and taste: the caprice of fashion, the arbitrary and rapid shift of public taste that designers and markets must chase.
  • Markets, politics, and economics: "the caprice of the market" or "of fortune" — unpredictable, irrational swings in prices, decisions, or outcomes beyond rational control.
  • Psychology and character: capriciousness as a trait of personality, the chronic disposition to sudden changes of mind that makes a person unreliable.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE: Italian capriccio originally meant a sudden start, shiver, or shudder — the involuntary physical jump of fright or cold. The early sources offered two popular explanations. One derived it from capo "head" + riccio "hedgehog," the image of hair bristling on end like a hedgehog's spines, a sign of sudden fright. The other connected it with capra "she-goat" and the goat's famously frisky, unpredictable leaps, or with the "shivering" flesh that fear produces. Both images — a start of fright and a goat's bound — converge on sudden, unmotivated movement: the body's leap became the mind's leap, and the mind's unmotivated leap is precisely a caprice. From the mind the word then jumped to the arts, where the "whim" became the free-form composition (musical capriccio) and the invented landscape (pictorial capriccio). All modern senses, however diverse, remain true to the original idea of action without rule. REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS: Caprice is a noun of formal and literary prose, rarely used in casual speech, where whim, impulse, or moodiness takes over. It collocates with verbs and prepositions that suggest surrender to the irrational — "the caprice of fate," "the caprice of fashion," "at the caprice of," "subject to the caprice of," "victim of his own caprice" — and it is frequently paired with adjectives such as sudden, wanton, idle, and royal. The adjective capricious is far commoner in modern usage than the noun, and the adverb capriciously completes the set. In the arts the Italian form capriccio is used for both the musical and the pictorial senses, so the English word carries a double register: the everyday psychological noun and the artistic loan both descend from the same Italian source, and a music critic may write of a "capriccio" where a novelist writes of a "caprice." Etymologically, the word's association with the goat (Latin capra) is alive in the figurative sense of "skittishness" and even surfaces in the zodiac sign Capricorn — the horned goat — reminding us how far the family has spread from the single leap of a startled animal.
