MUSH and RICKETY bind physical pulpiness to structural fragility, and in the Vance passage the two work as a single ghastly image: the creature's mouth refuses the spoon, and the mush trickles down its glazed skin to fall on the rickety frame of its body. One word describes matter that has lost its form; the other describes a structure that is about to lose its form. Below is the full polysemous exegesis of both words. SECTION 1 — MUSH AS A NOUN In its primary culinary sense, mush is a thick, soft porridge made by boiling cornmeal (maize meal) in salted water or milk — for centuries the staple breakfast and supper of rural North America. Beyond the kitchen, mush names any soft, thick, shapeless mass, and by further extension any cloyingly sentimental material or performance.
  • Frontier history: 17th–18th century American pioneers ate cornmeal mush daily; "fried mush" (slices of chilled, set mush fried in fat) is still a traditional Southern and Appalachian dish. The word entered English in the 17th century as a dialectal variant of "mash," possibly reinforced by German Mus "soft food, porridge" brought by Pennsylvania Dutch settlers.
  • Regional survival: "mush" remains the ordinary word for cornmeal porridge in the American South and Midwest, where it survives alongside grits and cornmeal pudding.
  • Generalized sense: by the 19th century the noun spreads to mean any soft, pulpy, shapeless mass — "mush of snow," "a mush of rotted leaves" — matter that has lost all internal structure, that yields, flows, and stains.
  • Figurative and sentimental sense: from the 20th century, "mush" (and the adjective "mushy") denotes mawkish, cloying sentimentality — romantic slush, undemanding emotion. The bridge is the idea of softness: what is soft to the touch becomes soft-headed or soft-hearted.
  • Related verb and compounds: "to mush" can mean to crush to a pulp; "mushed" = crushed; "mushy" = soft, pulpy, or sentimentally weak. In Arctic idiom, a dog-sled driver commands "mush!" (from the French marchez!, "march!") — a fascinating false-friend unrelated to the food word.
SECTION 2 — RICKETY AS AN ADJECTIVE Rickety means shaky, weak, unsound, or liable to collapse — a structure, body, or piece of furniture that is loosely jointed, wobbly, and threatening to give way. The word's history is medical in origin: it first meant "afflicted with rickets," the childhood disease in which bones soften and bow, and only later generalized to any wobbly object.
  • Medical origin: "rickets" is the disease of softened, deformed bones in children, caused by vitamin D deficiency; "rickety" (17th–18th century) first meant "suffering from rickets." The weak, bowed, tottering body of the rickety child is the literal image behind the word.
  • Generalized structural sense: by the 19th century, "rickety" applies to anything weak and unstable — a rickety old wooden chair, a rickety bridge, a rickety staircase. The transfer runs from the bony weakness of the sick child to the wobbly weakness of any frame.
  • Figurative sense: a rickety argument, a rickety economy, a rickety organization — anything unsound and ready to collapse under pressure. The word always implies imminent failure, the moment before the whole thing gives way.
  • Register: "rickety" is concrete and homely; it describes the old chair on the porch, the stairs in the tenement, the shed in the yard — never grand ruins, which have their own vocabulary (dilapidated, ramshackle, decrepit).
SECTION 3 — THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The two words on this card are opposites that meet in the idea of collapse: mush is matter that has already given way — formless, yielding, wet; rickety is a form that is about to give way — stiff but unsound, trembling on the edge of becoming mush itself. In the Vance portrait, the creature's failing body displays both states at once: the food it cannot eat becomes mush on its skin, while the body that cannot hold it is rickety beneath. Formlessness and fragility are the two faces of the creature's doom.
