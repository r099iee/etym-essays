The past tense snarled belongs to one of English's oldest sound-pictures: the attempt, renewed in every century, to spell the noise an angry dog makes. Its ancestry is not a stately march of sound laws but a kennel of imitations — snar, snarl, snore, sneer, snort — a whole litter of sn- words in which the initial cluster itself seems to sneer, wrinkling the nose exactly as a growling muzzle wrinkles. Linguists call such families expressive or imitative vocabulary: they are born again wherever noses and throats make the same shapes, which is why cousins like Middle Dutch snarren (to growl or rattle) and German schnarren (to rattle, drone) appear independently across the Germanic languages without any single parent form.

In English the trail begins with Middle English snar, a short sharp verb for snapping — a dog's snap, a man's sharp speech — attested in the late 1300s. Around the 1590s speakers lengthened it with the frequentative -l ending, the same ending that builds wrestle out of wrest and trample out of tramp: the single snap became a sustained performance. To snarl was no longer to give one bite but to hold the threat rolling — lip lifted, teeth exposed, throat vibrating — and Elizabethan writers seized on it for villains, shrews, and literal dogs alike. The new verb's genius was fusion: it named sound and face together, so that a reader hears the rumble and sees the fangs in a single syllable.

Meanwhile a lookalike was growing up beside it:

  • The tangle twin (1500s–1600s): a noun snarl meaning a knot or snag, and a verb to snarl meaning to entangle, descended not from growling but from snar, a noose or snare — kin to snare itself. Fishermen's lines snarled, hair snarled, and in time whole afternoons of traffic would snarl too.
  • Collision or kinship: by the 1600s the two verbs were spelt and sounded identically, and scholars still debate whether tangled snarls borrowed menace from growling snarls or whether the likeness is pure accident. Speakers certainly feel them as one word now — a snarled lane, like a snarling dog, resists handling.
  • Humans go canine (1700s–1800s): novelists from Fielding to Dickens made snarled the standard verb of brutish speech — masters snarl at servants, misers snarl over coins — fixing its moral colour: to snarl is to surrender human language for the dog's.
  • The industrial pack (1800s–1900s): machinery inherited the verb — snarling saws, snarling engines, snarling storms — because the age of steam needed animal words for its new roaring beasts, and snarl fitted the acoustics perfectly.

Phonetically the word has been nearly frozen for four hundred years, and for good reason: it is sculpted to its meaning. The sn- onset forces air through a narrowed nose, the -ar- opens the throat into a growl, and the final -l lets the sound roll on indefinitely — snar-r-r-l — which is why children invent it spontaneously and why no reformer has ever dared respell it. In Tolkien's farmyard it performs its original office with perfect economy: two dogs, one hobbit, one conditional threat — snarled if he moved — the whole ancient contract between intruders and guard dogs compressed into three words that every reader understands in the marrow.
