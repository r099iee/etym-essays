Bereaved is the past participle of the verb bereave, used in the reading as an adjective: "the bereaved mother" is the mother whose son has just been killed. The verb is transitive and normally takes of: to bereave someone of a relative, or in the passive, to be bereaved of a child. The paradigm is regular — bereave, bereaves, bereaving, bereaved — though an older irregular participle, bereft, survives alongside it and has specialized in a different direction. The base verb is pronounced /bɪˈriːv/ and the participle /bɪˈriːvd/, with the stress on the second syllable; the -ea- is a long vowel, not a diphthong.

The core modern sense is narrow and solemn: deprived by death of someone close. It is not used of losing a job, a home or a possession; for those, English says deprived, robbed or stripped. Bereaved belongs to the vocabulary of funerals, obituaries, condolence and, increasingly, public policy: "bereaved families", "recently bereaved", "bereavement support". Its emotional register is grave and sympathetic; it names a loss that cannot be repaired.

Senses and grammatical uses

  • Adjective: deprived by death. "A bereaved mother", "the bereaved widow", "bereaved parents". This is the use in the source, where the mother has lost a son through the boy's fall.
  • Collective noun: the bereaved. "The bereaved were comforted by the community." Here the adjective functions as a plural noun meaning bereaved people generally, or the family of a particular deceased person.
  • Past participle in passive constructions. "She was bereaved of her husband at thirty"; "the families bereaved by the crash". The passive is formal and often journalistic.
  • Active verb, formal or literary. "Death bereaved him of both parents." The active use is much rarer than the passive and can sound archaic.
  • Archaic: robbed, despoiled. The older, material sense survives only in historical or deliberately elevated writing, and usually in the form bereft.

The boundary with "bereft"

This is the most important distinction for a learner. Bereaved is the regular participle and refers overwhelmingly to death: a bereaved family has lost someone. Bereft is the older irregular participle, now used chiefly of intangible deprivation — "bereft of hope", "bereft of ideas", "utterly bereft" — and as a standalone adjective meaning desolate and forlorn. The two overlap in the passive construction "bereaved/bereft of", but in ordinary modern English the division is fairly clear: bereaved for the dead, bereft for everything else. Using "bereft mother" would suggest she had been stripped of something abstract, and would strike most readers as an error in this context.

Related vocabulary

  • Bereavement — the state or fact of having lost someone by death; also the period of mourning. "Bereavement leave" is time off work granted for a death in the family.
  • Bereavement counselling — professional support for grief, a twentieth-century development that has made the word part of clinical and social-work vocabulary.
  • Bereavement damages — a fixed statutory sum awarded in some jurisdictions to close relatives of a person killed by negligence; the phrase shows how the word has entered law.
  • Bereft — see above; the irregular participle, now semantically specialized.

The source context

The sentence comes from a discussion of early compensation and talion. A boy falls from a tree, kills his companion, and the judges order that the dead boy's mother send another son up the tree to fall on the killer's neck. The word bereaved is doing quiet but important work: it marks the mother as the injured party whose loss grounds the claim for compensation, and it lends the archaic procedure a recognizably human grief. In the legal anthropology of the passage, the bereaved kin are the ones entitled to satisfaction; the tariff converts their loss into a payable amount.

Register and sensitivity

Because bereaved names a fresh and painful loss, it is used with care in journalism and official writing. "The bereaved family" is respectful; "the grieving family" is warmer and more emotional; "the family" is neutral. The word should not be used attributively of things (a "bereaved house" is poetic at best), and it is not a synonym for lonely, sad or depressed. Its force comes from its specificity: it says that someone has died.
