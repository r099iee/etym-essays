Sorrel's story begins not with a plant but with a taste, and it shows how a single Indo-European root-word can arrive in English twice, by two different roads, wearing two different meanings. The ultimate source is the Proto-Indo-European adjective *sūro-, whose core sense was a sharp, raw, biting quality perceived by the tongue — a sense that each daughter branch then narrowed in its own direction. Lithuanian sūrus means "salty", Latvian sūrs "bitter, salty", and Old Church Slavonic syrъ "raw, damp, uncooked", whose Russian descendant сырой (syrój) still means "raw" today; even Russian сыр (syr), "cheese", is a distant kin, since cheese was originally a raw, moist curd. The family is spread over the whole of the Indo-European world, and the width of its meanings — sour, salty, bitter, raw — tells us the root was ancient and loose before any written record: it denoted a general sharpness, and the later languages assigned it to whatever sharp thing lay nearest at hand. In Germanic this root produced Proto-Germanic *sūraz, "sour, acidic, tart", the direct ancestor of Old English sūr and of modern English sour — our everyday word for acid taste — with siblings Old High German sūr (German sauer, as in Sauerkraut, "sour cabbage"), Old Norse súrr (Swedish sur, Icelandic súr, Danish sur), and Dutch zuur. Every major Germanic tongue keeps this word, and here lies the first great irony of sorrel's history: the English name of the plant is not this native English sour but a French cousin of it — a doublet, in the technical sense, of the very word sour that already grew in English soil. Two English words, sour and sorrel, are thus one Indo-European word twice over, separated by a thousand years and a language frontier. The French road began with the Franks. When the Frankish kingdom extended its rule and its speech into Gaul in the early Middle Ages, the Germanic *sūr crossed the language frontier as a loan into Gallo-Romance, surfacing in Old French as sur or sor, "sour, sharp-tasting". There the plant received its name. Roman botanists and gardeners had known the dock-leaved herb by the Latin lapathum, a word of Greek origin with nothing to do with taste; but French speakers, with a peasant's exactness, coined surele, a diminutive formed on sur with the suffix -elle — "the little sour one" — a name that records not the plant's appearance but its effect on the tongue. The coinage was a brilliant piece of folk-taxonomy, and it evidently spread quickly, because Anglo-French carried it into England in the fourteenth century, and Middle English sorel is attested from about 1400. English, which already had the native sour, welcomed the newcomer as the name of the herb itself, and the word settled in as sorel, sorell, or sourel before standardising as sorrel. The phonetic and orthographic history is short but revealing. The Middle English word was borrowed with the French sound; the modern spelling sorrel fixed the -el ending, and the initial s- has remained stable. What is most striking is the semantic drift of the same root into separate English words: sour kept the pure taste-sense, while sorrel named the plant that tastes sour — and, through that plant, a whole cluster of later senses. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries English cookery made sorrel soup and sorrel sauce, the tart purée of the leaves lending its bite to fish and veal; herbalists called it the cooling, cleansing green, a remedy for scurvy and mouth ulcers; and children chewed the raw leaves for their lemony shock. The word also escaped the plant. The salt of sorrel — potassium hydrogen oxalate — was known from the seventeenth century as a bleaching agent and "salts of lemon", the plant's very acidity turned into a chemical commodity. And because autumn sorrel leaves flush reddish-brown, a separate Old French colour-word sorel, "reddish-brown", gave English the adjective and noun sorrel for a reddish-brown horse — a homograph of the plant-word, descending from the same Frankish stock by a different channel, a permanent caution against assuming that identical spellings share one history. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw the plant-word wander still further. As cultivated vegetables displaced wild greens, common sorrel shrank to a forager's and herbalist's plant, though French cuisine kept oseille (an unrelated Celtic-derived word) prominent, and English gourmet cooking revived sorrel soup. In the Caribbean, the tart red calyces of the roselle (Hibiscus sabdariffa) took the name sorrel from the same perceived acidity, brewing the bright Christmas drink that is sorrel in Trinidadian and Jamaican English. In Tolkien's passage the word returns to its oldest recorded function — the meagre, sharp snack of a hungry traveller — and the sentence's "but it was not much good" is a perfect gloss on the plant's career: sourness is its identity, and sourness, welcome as it is to the starving, is thin comfort. Sorrel, in short, is a word whose entire history is contained in its etymology: it is, from first to last, the little sour one.
