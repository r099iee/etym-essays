- Old English healdan - “to keep, guard, possess” - the everyday verb, and the noun formed from it: a grip, a grasp, control
- 14th–15th c. - “hold” as grasp and grip, in wrestling and in common speech (“no holds barred” later, from the ring) - physical, combative
- later 16th c. - the nautical “hold”, borrowed from Dutch hol “hollow, cavity” - the cargo belly of the ship, spelled with -d by analogy with the verb; the word comes ashore in a wave of Dutch seafaring terms (deck, yacht, skipper)
- 17th–19th c. - the ship’s hold: dark, foul, packed with cargo and, in the slave trade, with human beings (Equiano’s Narrative dwells on it) - the word carries commerce and horror together
- 20th c. - “baggage hold” and “cargo hold” of aircraft - the word migrates from ships to planes without changing meaning
- modern - “on hold”, “hold” stock advice, “no holds barred” - everyday extensions of the word that most speakers never connect with ships at all
