- Old English (c. 9th c.): “prician” — to pierce with a point; a plain physical verb of injury, used of thorns, goads and needles.
- Middle English (13th–15th c.): riding register opens — “prikken” a horse means to spur it, so “pricking” comes to mean riding fast across country; the noun “prik” names any point or mark.
- 16th–17th c.: the moral sense is fixed — the King James Bible’s “pricked in their heart” (Acts 2:37, 1611) anchors “pricked conscience” forever; “prick” also names the archery bull’s-eye, the exact moment (“prick of noon”) and written music (“prick-song”); the vulgar noun “prick” (penis) is recorded from the 1590s.
- 18th–19th c.: gentle domesticity — pricked fingers in sewing rooms, “pricked-up ears”, sailors pricking the chart, gardeners pricking out seedlings.
- 20th c.: the bare noun hardens into a general insult, tarnishing casual usages; the verb keeps its dignity in set phrases — pricked conscience, pricked ears, pricked by regret or curiosity.
- Present: “pricked” lives chiefly in those fixed phrases and in the literal trades of needle, nursery and physician — the seamstress’s pricked hands in Wilde’s story are the full-blooded literal sense the language has never lost.
