- c. 1300, Middle English creken: 'to utter a harsh cry,' used of birds - born a purely animal sound, high, dry, and intermittent, one member of a Germanic clan of 'k-r + velar' imitative verbs.
- Mid-15th century: the verb leaves the bird and enters the world of objects - doors, floorboards, branches, and ships now 'creak,' and the sound is read as the grating protest of rigid matter under strain, the acoustic signature of friction and pressure.
- 1800s-1900s: 'creak' becomes a staple of atmosphere in Gothic, horror, and children's narrative - the creaking gate and groaning house are the prelude to catastrophe, and the proverb 'the creaking gate hangs longest' turns the fragile sound into a figure of deceptive durability.
- Contemporary: the concrete sense is unchanged, but the word has grown a rich figurative life - creaky joints speak of age, a creaky plot or a creaking administration of strain, while the plain, onomatopoeic verb remains one of English's most vivid sensory tools.
