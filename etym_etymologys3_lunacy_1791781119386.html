lunacy was coined in English in the 1540s from lunatic and the noun suffix -cy, on the model of Latin abstract nouns and French nouns in -ie. It is therefore a native English formation from a borrowed adjective, not a direct descendant of a Latin noun. The adjective is much older in English, recorded in the late 13th century as lunatik, and it comes through Old French lunatique from Late Latin lūnāticus, “moon-struck.”

The Latin background

Latin lūna, “moon,” belongs to the same family as lūx, “light,” and lūcēre, “to shine”: the moon is “the shining one,” a feminine derivative of a stem that also produced lūmen. The reconstructed pre-form *louksnā shows the loss of the velar before n that also distinguishes lūna from lūx. Lūnāticus was formed from lūna with the adjectival suffix -āticus, the same suffix seen in silvāticus, “of the woods”; it is attested in Late Latin and glossed “moon-struck, suffering from moon-induced madness.” Roman writers already linked the moon with epilepsy and mental disturbance, and Pliny's Natural History gives sustained attention to lunar influences on the body.

Scriptural transmission

The Latin adjective reached medieval Europe most powerfully through the Bible. The Vulgate's Gospel of Matthew (4:24) lists the lunatici among those brought to be healed, and the same term renders the Greek selēniazomenoi, “moon-struck,” in the account of the epileptic boy (Matthew 17:15). Because the word appeared in the Gospel text, it entered sermons, penitentials and medical compendia with scriptural authority behind it: to be moon-struck was not merely a folk diagnosis but a condition named in the holy book.

Medieval and early modern English

Middle English lunatik named a person whose madness was thought to wax and wane with the moon; religious and medical texts use it alongside epileptic, since the two conditions were often conflated. By the 16th century the adjective had produced the abstract noun lunacy, which allowed lawyers and physicians to speak of the condition rather than the person. The word was useful precisely because it was vague: it could cover congenital deficiency, episodic psychosis, senile confusion and mere eccentricity, and it carried the implication that the sufferer was not fully responsible.

The asylum era

The 18th and 19th centuries institutionalised the concept. Private madhouses, county asylums and the Lunacy Act of 1845 made “lunacy” an administrative category, with certificates, commissions and inspectors. The word was not merely descriptive; it was the legal instrument by which a person could be deprived of liberty and property. Reformers attacked the abuses of lunacy proceedings, and the scandal of wrongful confinement gave the term a permanent association with paperwork, keys and locked doors.

Retreat and survival

  • 1930. The Mental Treatment Act in England and Wales encourages voluntary admission and officially discourages “lunatic” terminology.
  • 1959. The Mental Health Act replaces the old lunacy framework with a modern statutory scheme.
  • Late 20th century. International classification systems (ICD, DSM) use discrete diagnostic categories; “lunacy” disappears from professional writing.
  • Survivals. The word persists in historical accounts, in older statutes, in the phrase lunatic fringe, and above all in figurative use.

Semantic drift

The trajectory is from condition to institution to metaphor. In the 16th century lunacy is a state of mind; in the 19th it is a legal-administrative machine; in the 20th it becomes a judgement on policies and plans. Each shift weakens the literal force and strengthens the rhetorical one, until “sheer lunacy” can be said of a tax proposal with no thought of mental illness at all.

The moon myth and its critics

The belief that the moon causes madness is ancient and widespread, but it has no support as a general medical theory. Studies of psychiatric admissions and lunar phase have repeatedly failed to find a consistent effect. The word therefore preserves a discarded aetiology inside a live vocabulary — a common pattern in medical language, where the name of a disease may outlast the theory that explained it.

Modern connotation

Today lunacy is a stylistically marked word: formal enough for editorials, archaic enough to sound slightly theatrical, and harsh enough to wound if aimed at a person. In the source sentence its archaic weight is an asset, giving the alternative to routine a quality of settled, almost legal unreason rather than mere confusion.
