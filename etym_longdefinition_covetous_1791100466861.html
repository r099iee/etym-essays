Covetous means greedily desiring, craving, or grasping at something — especially something that belongs to someone else — with wrongful, envious eagerness. It is desire that oversteps right: not admiration, not ambition, but a clutching wish to possess another's goods, status, or person.

Dickens deploys it as the climax of a six-verb pile-up: "a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!" Each participle is a hand-motion — squeeze, wrench, grasp, scrape, clutch — and covetous names the soul behind the hands. Scrooge does not merely hold tight; he eyes others' holdings.

  • Core biblical-moral sense — desiring another's property: "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house." "He cast a covetous eye on his brother's inheritance." This remains the central meaning: envy fused with acquisitiveness. It implies illegitimacy — the thing is not yours and ought not to be taken — and intensity — a gnawing, eye-fixed longing.
  • General acquisitive sense — greedy, avaricious: "A covetous landlord hoarded every penny." "Covetous developers eyed the park." Here the other's ownership is background; the stress is on insatiable grasping. Synonymous with greedy, grasping, rapacious, but more literary and condemnatory.
  • Envious sense — begrudging another's good: "She was covetous of her rival's praise." The desire may be for intangible goods — fame, affection, promotion — not just land or money. The covetous person both wants the good and resents the holder, blending jealousy and envy.
  • Amorous / archaic sense: Older use includes lust: "covetous of his neighbour's wife." The KJV commandment lists house, wife, servant, ox, ass — property and person together. Modern usage usually avoids this for persons, preferring lecherous, predatory, but "covetous eyes" can still carry it.
  • Degree, register, grammar: Formal-literary, moralistic, biblical. Everyday speech prefers jealous, greedy, envious; editorials, sermons, and novels prefer covetous for weight. Adjective, gradable (more covetous, most covetous), typically predicative ("he is covetous") or attributive ("a covetous old sinner"). Takes of: "covetous of money." Adverb covetously, noun covetousness (the sin itself).

Semantic neighbours form a spectrum: envious (pained by another's good), jealous (fearing loss of one's own), greedy (wanting more), avaricious (hoarding money), rapacious (seizing violently), covetous (wrongfully craving another's). Dickens needs the last because Scrooge's sin is precisely comparative: he squeezes others to swell himself.

In short, covetous is the adjective of the Tenth Commandment — the inward grasp behind outward squeezing.

Extra note on covetous envy in biblical commandment, sermons, novels, and modern prose, marking wrongful craving for the goods of others with grave moral weight.
