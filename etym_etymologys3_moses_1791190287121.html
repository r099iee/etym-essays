The name Moses carries its mystery in plain sight. The Torah itself in Exodus 2:10 gives a Hebrew folk etymology: Pharaoh's daughter names him Mosheh saying ki min hamayim meshitihu — for from the water I drew him — linking Mosheh to the verb mashah to draw out. Philology notes the grammar is backwards: mosheh would be active he who draws out, not passive drawn out, which would be mashuy. The narrator, as Sarna observes, puts a Hebrew wordplay into an Egyptian mouth to foreshadow destiny: the drawn-out child will draw his people out.

Since decipherment of hieroglyphs, scholars have seen behind the Hebrew form an Egyptian original: msy to be born, noun ms / mesu child, son, familiar from theophoric names Thutmose child of Thoth, Ramesses child of Ra, Ahmose child of the Moon, with the divine element omitted either by abbreviation or to avoid pagan theonym. Hebrew Mosheh would then be the Hebraisation of Egyptian Mose, son. Greek Septuagint rendered it Mōusēs, Latin Vulgate Moyses / Moses, Middle English Moises, modern Moses with /z/ voicing. Alternative proposals — Coptic mau water + ses saved, or mu water + sa son — popularised as water-saved, restate the same Egyptian intuition in folk form; Kitchen and Yahuda debate phonetic fit, with Yahuda proposing mw-š child of the Nile.

  • Sound development: Egyptian msy /mes/ → Hebrew Mosheh with shin for s and final heh as mater; Greek Mōusēs adds Greek nominative -s; Latin keeps y; English Moises → Moses with Great Vowel Shift and /s/ → /z/ voicing.
  • Morphology: Egyptian verb + theophoric abbreviation → Hebrew proper noun reanalysed as participle of mashah; folk etymology does not change form, only perceived meaning.
  • Attestations: Exodus 2:10 naming; Philo links to Coptic mou water; Josephus gives Egyptian etymology; 1840 Oh Moses oath, 1877 Holy Moses show full naturalisation as exclamation.
  • Cognate names: Musa Arabic, Moïse French, Moisés Spanish, Moshe modern Hebrew, Movses Armenian; all track Septuagint or Masoretic vocalisation.
  • Cultural layering: Egyptian prince name → Hebrew liberator → Greco-Roman lawgiver → Christian type → Islamic messenger → modern given name and mild oath.

The debated nuance is explicit in scholarship: most Egyptologists accept Egyptian mes son as source with Hebrew wordplay superimposed, while a minority stress Kitchen's objection that Hebrew š does not regularly render Egyptian s of that period, preferring a purely Hebrew or Midianite origin. Etymonline summarises the consensus as Hebraisation of Egyptian mes, mesu with Exodus explanation as associative, not linguistic. Either way, English Moses descends textually through Hebrew Mosheh, Greek Mōusēs, and Latin Moses, and its meaning in English is wholly the biblical figure, not water or son in isolation.

English Bibles from Tyndale through King James fixed the spelling Moses and pronunciation with /z/, while hymns, spirituals, and civil-rights oratory made Moses the archetypal deliverer, ensuring the Egyptian-Hebrew name lives as both person and promise.
