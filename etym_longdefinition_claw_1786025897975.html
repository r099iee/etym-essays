The noun and verb claw reach back to the most ancient Germanic stratum of English vocabulary and have never been out of use for a millennium and a half. As a noun, a claw is the sharp, curved, horny nail on the toe or foot of an animal — the talon of a bird of prey, the hooked nail of a cat, bear, or lizard, or the jointed pincer of a crab, lobster, or scorpion. The word can also name a human fingernail or toenail in archaic or poetic use, the split, toothed end of a claw hammer (the "claw" that draws nails), and, in mechanics and engineering, any hooked or forked gripping device — the claw of a claw crane, a clam-shell grab, or the clutch of a machine. As a verb, to claw means to scratch, tear, scrape, or dig with nails or hooked implements, and — by powerful metaphor — to struggle desperately, ruthlessly, or greedily for advantage, position, money, or survival ("clawing one's way to the top").

The word is etymologically Germanic through and through: it descends straight from Proto-Germanic *klawō and is cognate with German Klaue, Dutch klauw, Swedish and Danish klo, and Old Norse kló. Unlike much of the English lexicon, it was never displaced by Norman French after 1066; it survived unbroken, preserving the visceral, physical intensity of the claw — curved, sharp, hooked, and slightly dangerous.

Exhaustive Inventory of Senses

  • 1. The sharp curved nail of an animal (primary noun sense): The horny, retractable (in cats) or fixed (in bears, dogs, reptiles) curved nail on an animal's foot, used for seizing prey, climbing, digging, and defence. Semantic bridge: the anatomical core meaning, continuous from Old English clawu.
  • 2. The pincer or chela of a crustacean or scorpion (extended noun sense): The jointed, grasping front appendage of a crab, lobster, shrimp, or scorpion. Semantic bridge: extension by analogy of shape and grasping function from claws to pincers, a transfer already established in Old English and used for any grasping digit.
  • 3. Human fingernail or toenail (archaic/poetic noun sense): In older and literary English, one's "claws" are one's nails, especially when long or untrimmed. Semantic bridge: the anatomical homology between animal claws and human nails was obvious to early speakers; the same Old English word covered both.
  • 4. The hooked, forked part of a tool (technical noun sense): The claw of a claw hammer (the split, curved end that draws nails), of a crowbar, or of a claw wrench. Semantic bridge: metaphorical transfer of the hooked, gripping shape of an animal claw to the working end of a tool.
  • 5. A mechanical gripping or grappling device (engineering noun sense): The toothed or hooked grab of a crane, a dredging bucket, a clam-shell grab, or a lifting claw. Semantic bridge: twentieth-century engineering borrowed the animal metaphor for any device that grips and tears.
  • 6. To scratch, tear, scrape, or dig with nails (physical verb sense): Raking the claws or hooked nails across a surface to tear, climb, dig, or fight. Semantic bridge: verbalization of the noun, attested in Old English clawian; the verb describes the action the claw exists to perform.
  • 7. To grasp or seize fiercely (intensive verb sense): To clutch, grip, or snatch at something with hooked fingers. Semantic bridge: extension from tearing with nails to frantic grabbing with hands, where the curved fingers imitate claws.
  • 8. "To claw back" (financial/administrative idiom): To recover money, power, or concessions previously granted, especially by taxation or legal action ("the government clawed back the subsidies"). Semantic bridge and warning: the phrase is old — in the 14th–16th centuries "to claw (someone)" meant to flatter or curry favour (to stroke like a cat being clawed pleasantly), and "claw back" could mean "to win back by flattery"; only in the 20th century did it narrow to financial reclamation, a shift that completely reversed the old, forgotten sense of flattery.
  • 9. "To claw one's way" (figurative idiom): To advance slowly, painfully, and ruthlessly against obstacles — "clawing one's way up the corporate ladder." Semantic bridge: vivid metaphor of climbing by digging hooked nails into a sheer surface; the effort is desperate, incremental, and not always pretty.
  • 10. "To claw at" (figurative): To attack verbally, or to struggle with a feeling — "fear clawed at his throat." Semantic bridge: the emotion becomes an animal that scratches from inside.

Register, Connotation, and Idiomatic Range

Claw carries a connotation of primal, physical intensity: it is the vocabulary of predators, of survival, and of ruthless ambition. As a verb it always implies effort — never gentle contact — so "to claw" is stronger than "to scratch" and far stronger than "to touch." The idiom system built on it (claw back, claw one's way, claw at) exploits this energy for financial, political, and psychological meanings, and the word remains equally at home in biology textbooks, tool catalogues, and thrillers. Its staying power is a tribute to its Germanic continuity: a word that has meant the same sharp, hooked thing for fifteen centuries still has plenty of metaphor left to give.
