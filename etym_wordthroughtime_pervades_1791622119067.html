- Early 17th c. (c. 1610s–1650s) — Borrowed into English directly from Latin pervādere; used primarily in physical and natural philosophy to describe physical light, heat, subtle spirits, or cosmic forces traversing and penetrating physical matter.
- Late 17th to 18th c. — Enlightenment natural philosophy (Francis Bacon, Robert Boyle, Isaac Newton) solidified its usage for celestial ethers, gravitational attraction, and divine omnipresence permeating the material universe.
- 19th c. — Underwent an expansive semantic shift into Victorian literary criticism, psychological description, and social commentary; novelists (George Eliot, Charles Dickens) used it to describe prevailing atmospheres of gloom, moral rot, or pervasive joy.
- 20th c. — Institutionalized across sociology, political science, and philosophy to diagnose structural, systemic phenomena (such as cynicism pervading post-war culture or consumerism pervading civic life); widely employed in intellectual histories to trace recurrent themes across an author's oeuvre.
- 21st c. — Ubiquitous in technology ("pervasive computing", ambient intelligent networks) and modern discourse to critique omnipresent digital connectivity and systemic social patterns.
