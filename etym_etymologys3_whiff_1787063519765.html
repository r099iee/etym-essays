The history of whiff is short, transparent and appropriately airy: the word is first attested only in the 16th century, it is probably imitative in origin — a sound-word meant to mimic the very puff of air or breath it names — and its meaning has drifted gently outward from the physical puff to the faint scent, and finally to the intangible trace. Its etymology illustrates how English, at the height of the Renaissance vocabulary boom, could coin a plain vernacular word with no classical pedigree that then did steady service for five centuries.

First attestation and probable origin. The noun appears in the 1580s with the meaning “a sudden gust of wind” and shortly thereafter “a puff of smoke or air”. The Oxford English Dictionary and most etymological authorities classify the origin as uncertain and probably imitative (echoic) or expressive: the voiceless onset wh- followed by a short -i- and a final fricative naturally evokes a soft, explosive puff of air. The same expressive family includes wiffle, whiffle (“to blow in fits and starts”, also “to vacillate”), wiff, and possibly whiffet (a small or insignificant person). No ancestor form can be reconstructed in Old English, Old Norse, or Latin; the word simply appears in the mid-Elizabethan period as part of a cluster of onomatopoeic puff-words. Some dictionaries also note a possible connection to the older whiffle tradition and to dialect verbs meaning “to move quickly through the air”, but none of these is secure enough to be stated as fact.

Renaissance extension from air to scent. Very soon after the gust/puff sense is recorded, the word extends metonymically to what the air carries: from the 1590s onward whiff means “a faint odour or scent”, especially one encountered briefly. The semantic bridge is natural — a smell is only ever experienced as air reaching the nose, so the “puff of air” becomes the “puff of scent”. Shakespeare uses the word in this way: in Pericles the fishermen joke about the “whiff” of a fish, and the participle whiffing appears in several plays for air moving in gusts. This early literary anchoring gave the word respectability in high style even though it was colloquial at heart.

Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries. Through the 17th–19th centuries whiff remains a versatile, largely colloquial word. It is used for puffs of tobacco smoke (a whiff of the pipe), for wafts of scent, for gusts of cold air, and — increasingly in journalism and fiction — for faint signs: by the 19th century phrases such as “a whiff of danger” and “a whiff of the gallows” show the figurative sense fully alive. The verb appears in the same period, both for “to blow in puffs” and “to sniff”. In 19th-century America the word crossed into baseball slang, where “to whiff” a pitch (and later “to whiff out”) came to mean striking out — the batter's bat cutting through empty air instead of the ball, a perfect punning reuse of the “puff of air” idea that gives the sporting term its unmistakable flavour of emptiness and failure.

Twentieth century to the present. In the modern period the figurative sense has become fully established and is now arguably as common as the literal one: editorialists write of “a whiff of scandal”, “a whiff of authoritarianism”, or “the faint whiff of nostalgia”. The literal senses remain in daily use for scents, smoke and air. The baseball “whiff” (both verb and noun) is standard sporting vocabulary. Throughout, the word has retained its original emphasis on faintness and brevity: a whiff, by definition, is never a full blast, never a strong smell, never a lasting trace. That single idea — the merest, most fleeting perceptible presence — has proved remarkably durable across five centuries of use, and it is exactly what Tolkien draws on when he has Smaug breathe “scarcely a snore more than a whiff of unseen steam”: the dragon, even at rest, is present at the very edge of perception, sensed but not quite seen.
