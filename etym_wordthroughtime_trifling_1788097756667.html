- [13th c. — a false tale] Middle English trifle begins as “idle talk, a false or fanciful story, a trick”; to trifle is to deceive or mock.
- [14th–15th c. — deceiving and toying] “To trifle with” means to play with, delude, or mock a person — the source sense: “You have been ___ with me.”
- [15th–16th c. — the small matter] The noun settles into “a thing of little value”; the adjective trifling means “belonging to trifles — trivial, petty, insignificant”.
- [17th–19th c. — politeness and condescension] “A trifling favour, a trifling matter” becomes the polite letter-writer's dismissal; the trifler is the idler of moral essays; Shakespeare's “trifles light as air” fix the noun in literary English.
- [20th–21st c. — the faded shrug and the sharpened slang] “Trifling” is standard for trivial; “trifling with” survives in formal reproach; African-American vernacular sharpens the word for a shiftless, unreliable, good-for-nothing person — the old mockery resurfacing through the triviality.
