• Oaks: Similar because both are grand, long-lived hardwood trees of the temperate landscape; differs because oaks bear acorns and lobed leaves and yield a very different (straight-grained) timber, while elms have serrated leaves, samaras, and interlocking-grained splitting-resistant wood.
  • Ashes: Similar because both are tall deciduous hardwoods often planted for shade and timber; differs because ashes have compound pinnate leaves and winged samaras, and their wood is tough and straight-grained, unlike elm’s twisted grain.
  • Beeches: Similar because both are large smooth-barked shade trees of the European lowlands; differs because beeches have oval smooth-edged leaves and edible nuts, while elms have serrated leaves and samaras.
  • Maples: Similar because both are broad-canopied deciduous trees with wind-dispersed fruits (samaras); differs because maples have palmate lobed leaves, sweet sap (maple syrup), and opposite branching, while elms have serrated leaves and alternate branching.
  • Plane trees: Similar because both are massive, spreading shade trees of streets and riversides with peeling bark; differs because planes (and sycamores in American use) have flaking mottled bark and lobed leaves, while elms have fissured bark and serrated leaves.
  • Limes/Lindens: Similar because both are tall, broad-crowned European shade trees of village greens; differs because lindens have heart-shaped leaves and fragrant flowers, and a different (soft, pale) timber.
  • Poplars: Similar because both are fast-growing deciduous trees of riverbanks and hedgerows; differs because poplars have fluttering rounded or triangular leaves and weak, easily split wood, the opposite of elm’s tough timber.
  • Willows: Similar because both are riparian trees with dense crowns; differs because willows have narrow, often pendulous leaves, grow in wetter sites, and their wood is soft and flexible.
  • Sycamores: Similar because both are broad-canopied shade trees and the names overlap regionally; differs because "sycamore" names the plane (America), the great maple (Britain), or the fig-mulberry (Bible), never the elm genus.
  • Hardwoods (general): Similar because elms are deciduous hardwoods; differs because "hardwoods" is a botanical and industrial class including hundreds of unrelated species.
  • Wych elms: Similar because the wych elm (U. glabra) is itself an elm; differs because it is a distinct native British species with a broad leaf, distinguished from the English and field elms.
  • Ulmus: Similar because Ulmus is the genus to which all elms belong; differs because "Ulmus" is the formal Latin scientific name, while "elms" is the ordinary English word.
