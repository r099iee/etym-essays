The etymology of rite is famously spare and famously disputed, which itself tells a story about Roman religion. Latin ritus, rituum (fourth declension, masculine) meant a religious observance, ceremony, custom, or usage sanctioned by tradition, as in ritu Romano in the Roman manner and ritus sacrorum the rites of sacred things. Unlike caerimonia which stressed awe, or sacramentum which stressed oath, ritus stressed correct, customary form: doing the thing as it has always been done. Livy, Cicero, and Ovid use it for both solemn state religion and foreign customs, already blurring sacred and ethnographic senses. Quintilian extends it to oratorical custom. The word's very plainness — no divine theonym inside — suggests it named procedure, not belief.

Historical linguists have proposed two competing Proto-Indo-European sources. The older nineteenth-century view, enshrined in Pokorny and repeated by Etymonline, derives ritus from PIE *ar-, *h₂er- meaning to fit together, to join, whence Greek artos joint, arariskein to fit, Latin ars, artis skill (a fitting-together), and arma tools. On this account a rite is that which fits parts together in proper order — liturgy as joinery. Michiel de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin (2008) revises this to PIE *h₂reh₁- meaning to count, reckon, think, via a noun *ri-tu- meaning reckoning, calculation, whence also Greek arithmos number and Old English rim number (modern rhyme via confusion). On this account a rite is that which is counted out, reckoned correctly — liturgy as arithmetic. Both agree on a core idea of order, whether spatial fitting or numerical reckoning, and both leave room for doubt, which scholars honestly flag. No Etruscan or Sabine intermediary is attested; the word is Italic Latin from the start.

  • Ritus in Roman law and augury: Roman priests kept libri rituales, ritual books prescribing how to found cities, take auspices, and bury the dead. To err in ritus was vitium, a flaw invalidating the act. Correctness, not sincerity, was paramount — a legalistic view that still colours English rite.
  • Old French rit and Church Latin ritus: Ecclesiastical Latin preserved ritus for liturgical use (Roman Rite, Mozarabic Rite). Old French borrowed it as rit, rite by the thirteenth century for Church ceremony. English took it in the sixteenth century during Reformation debates over ceremonies, first as rite for Roman Catholic observances Protestants questioned.
  • Rite vs. ritual vs. ceremony: Rite (countable act), ritual (system or script, from Latin ritualis via sixteenth-century rituale book), and ceremony (pomp, from caerimonia) diverged in English. One performs a rite, follows a ritual, attends a ceremony.
  • Sound stability: Latin ritus /riːtus/ lost its ending in French to /ʁi/ and regained a spelling -e in English to mark a long vowel: rite /raɪt/ via the Great Vowel Shift, now homophonous with right, write, wright.

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English used rite chiefly for Christian liturgies: rites of baptism, marriage rites, funeral rites, the Rite of Spring being still unimagined. Victorian anthropology — Tylor, Frazer, Robertson Smith — weaponised and widened it. In Primitive Culture (1871) and The Golden Bough (1890), savage rites, blood rites, fertility rites became a universal category for rule-governed symbolic action believed to cause效果. Durant's sentence belongs exactly here: blood drunk sometimes as medicine, sometimes as a rite or covenant. The distinction is functional: medicine aims at cure, rite aims at communion with powers through prescribed form. Twentieth-century liturgical reform (Second Vatican Council's Sacrosanctum Concilium) revived rite as a term of dignity for Eastern Catholic Churches sui iuris, while anthropology subdivided rites into rites of passage (van Gennep, 1909), rites of affliction, calendrical rites, and sacrificial rites.

Modern rite thus holds two poles: solemn insider name (my Rite) and cool outsider label (their rite). Capitalised Rite can denote a whole church tradition (Byzantine Rite) or a Masonic degree system (Scottish Rite). Lowercase rite can dignify (wedding rite) or exoticise (voodoo rite), depending on who speaks. Durant uses the outsider-anthropological sense without overt contempt: a rite is a repeated, rule-bound drinking believed to transfer vital force. Understanding this doubleness — order as holiness for participants, order as data for observers — is key to using the word responsibly today.
