THE EVOLUTION OF "THICKET" The biography of thicket is a study in stability. Where most English words drift, split, and reverse their meanings, thicket has meant almost exactly the same thing since the Anglo-Saxons coined it: a dense, tangled growth of bushes and small trees. Its interest lies less in semantic change than in the two things hidden inside it — the ancient Germanic adjective "thick", and a small native suffix that looks deceptively like a French diminutive. THE ROOT OF DENSITY
  • The PIE root. The deepest ancestor is Proto-Indo-European *tegu-, "thick, dense, fat". Its Indo-European spread is debated (the commonly cited cognate is Lithuanian tigulas, "thick, dense"), but the Germanic reflex is secure and uncomplicated.
  • Proto-Germanic *þikkuz. From this root the Germanic languages formed their adjective for thickness — Old Norse þykkr, Old High German dicchi, Middle Dutch dic, Gothic *þiqqus, and Old English þicce. All of them mean "thick, dense, close-set": the same fundamental idea of things packed tightly together.
  • Old English þicce. Attested from the 8th century onward, þicce gave modern English "thick" — a word that itself went on to develop rich secondary senses (dense as fog, crowded as hair, intimate as "thick as thieves", dull as "thick as two short planks"). But the noun we are tracing was built directly from the adjective's oldest, most concrete sense.
THE FORMATION: THICK + THE COLLECTIVE -ET
  • A native Germanic suffix. Old English formed nouns from adjectives with a small suffix spelled -et, -ot, or -at, meaning roughly "the place or thing characterised by X". Thus þicce + -et = þiccet, "the place where things grow thick". The formation is transparent, native, and ancient.
  • The -et trap. Because later French loanwords used -et as a diminutive ending (coronet, cigarette, kitchenette), a careless reader assumes "thicket" is a "little thick thing". It is not. The word never passed through French; its -et is Germanic and collective. A thicket is not small — it is a whole dense mass.
  • The meaning in Anglo-Saxon. From its first records, Old English þiccet meant a dense growth of shrubs or brush — precisely the tangle of branches a traveller must force through. The sense has been remarkably stable for a millennium.
THE WORD IN THE MIDDLE AGES
  • Middle English thikket. The word passed unchanged in sense through Middle English, doing practical work in hunting and forestry: a thicket is dense cover where game hides and a man cannot easily pass — a place of concealment and of a hunter's luck. It was the vocabulary of the chase and of woodland management, not of the garden.
  • Cover and ambush. In medieval and early modern usage the thicket is a green stronghold: what hides in it is both protected and threatening. This dual nature — obstacle and refuge — is the word's permanent flavour.
THE LITERARY AGE
  • Romantic nature-writing. In the 18th and 19th centuries, Romantic poetry and fiction gave "thicket" a new literary aura: the tangled, unmanaged wild middle of a wood, felt simultaneously as obstacle and refuge, alive with scratches and hidden life. It became a stock emblem of the untamed natural world.
  • The figurative thicket. By the 19th century the word had also gone metaphorical: "a thicket of rules", "a thicket of lies", "a thicket of regulations" — any dense, confusing, obstructive mass. The image is always of pushing through something that catches and blocks.
  • Lewis's use. In Prince Caspian the children "struggled out of the thicket", paying the price in "stings from nettles and pricks from thorns" before stepping into the brightness of the beach. The word is pure physical obstruction: a green barrier that scrapes and bites before letting them through to the sea.
THE MODERN WORD
  • Today "thicket" is still the everyday word for a dense, impassable tangle of bushes and small trees. The diminutive -et has faded from notice entirely, so the word now sounds heavy and solid — a thick, solid thing, which is exactly what it is.
  • In ecology it names a vegetation stage and habitat type (scrub thicket, riparian thicket); in journalism it is a stock metaphor for complexity; in ordinary speech it is the green wall at the edge of a field or wood.
  • Its connotation has never moved far from wildness: thorns, nettles, resistance to passage — and the hidden life that the tangle shelters.
CONCLUSION Thicket is one of the oldest and steadiest words in English: a native Old English compound of the Germanic adjective for "thick" and a collective suffix, unchanged in meaning for a thousand years. It never borrowed from French, never drifted into a new sense — it simply kept its one job, naming the dense, tangled, impassable green that scratches a passer-by and shelters the hidden world inside it. In the novel it does that job with perfect economy, a wall of thorns and nettles set against the brightness of the sea.
