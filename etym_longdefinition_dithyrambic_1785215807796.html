DITHYRAMBIC is an adjective meaning wildly enthusiastic, passionate, rapturous, or unrestrained in style, tone, or expression, especially of praise, writing, or oratory. In the passage behind this card, the pages of J.S. Mill's Autobiography “ring with the DITHYRAMBIC praise” of his wife, the “almost infallible counsellor.” The word labels Mill's tribute as fervent and nearly hymnic — praise delivered with the elevated, intoxicated intensity of an ancient choral ode rather than with sober judgment. It implies that the prose itself has taken on a rhythmic, exultant quality, as if overflowing ordinary discursive measure; the nuance is not merely “great praise” but praise that is ecstatic, almost cult-like, and emotionally overwhelming in its celebration of a single person.

Primary sense — unrestrained enthusiasm of style or manner. In general modern use, dithyrambic describes any utterance, piece of writing, piece of music, or manner of expression marked by feverish, exuberant, uncontrolled enthusiasm. It is applied to praise that is excessive in warmth, to prose or verse that abandons orderly structure for wild transport, to oratory that whips its audience into fervour. The word is almost always used of the manner rather than the matter: a “dithyrambic review,” a “dithyrambic style,” “dithyrambic raptures.”

  • Literary sense — resembling a dithyramb. A dithyramb (from Greek dithyrambos) was the ancient Greek choral hymn sung and danced in honour of the god Dionysus, famous for its ecstatic emotion, elaborate and irregular rhythm, and impassioned diction. Dithyrambic prose or poetry is that which resembles this genre in frenzy, intensity, and rhythmic wildness — a “dithyrambic ode” is a modern imitation of the ancient form.
  • Critical and rhetorical sense. In criticism, dithyrambic is a technical term for writing that abandons sobriety and proportion; a critic calls a poem dithyrambic to mark its hymnic, orgiastic, oratorical excess — whether admiringly or disparagingly.
  • Extended figurative sense. By extension the word describes any emotion or response that is carried away: dithyrambic joy, dithyrambic cheering, dithyrambic worship of a celebrity or a leader. The constant idea is transport — being swept out of ordinary consciousness by enthusiasm.

Register and collocations. DITHYRAMBIC is a literary and critical term, rare in ordinary speech, where “wild,” “over the top,” or “rapturous” would serve. It collocates naturally with abstract nouns of expression: “dithyrambic praise,” “dithyrambic enthusiasm,” “dithyrambic style,” “dithyrambic prose,” “dithyrambic rhythm.” The noun is dithyramb (the ode or the frenzy), and the adjective is often used with an admiring or mildly mocking edge, since the enthusiasm it names is excessive by definition.

Semantic history in brief. The English adjective enters the language in the early seventeenth century as a technical term of classical scholarship — the dithyramb being the choral ode of Dionysus celebrated for its frenzy. The crucial shift came in the nineteenth century, when the word migrated from the name of the genre to a description of manner: any praise, prose, or oratory that is intoxicated in tone and rhythmically exalted became “dithyrambic.” Mill's use in the Autobiography sits exactly at this modern, extended sense — praise so fervent that it takes on the wild character of an ancient hymn.
