manor: (1) a large country house with lands; the principal house of a landed estate; (2) historically, a feudal unit of land under the jurisdiction of a lord, comprising the lord's demesne, tenant farms, and associated rights. Core meaning. In modern English, a manor is above all a large country house — the grand residence of a landowning family, surrounded by its grounds and estates: "a Tudor manor," "the manor house," "an ancient English manor." The word's deeper historical meaning, still alive in history books and legal records, is the feudal territorial unit: a manor was the landed estate — the lord's house, his demesne (home farmland), the tenant holdings, and the common lands — over which the lord exercised jurisdiction. In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Bingley is "provided with a good house and the liberty of a manor": he has leased a house (Netherfield) and obtained the rights of the estate's management — the word here points to the landed estate with its privileges, not merely the building. Feudal-historical sense. After the Norman Conquest, England was organized into manors, each a unit of land held by a lord and worked by peasants. The manorial court, the lord's mill, and the open fields defined rural life for centuries. In this sense a manor is not a house but an institution of land tenure: the Domesday Book of 1086 records manors throughout England. This legal-economic sense is the historical core from which the modern "great house" sense descended: the lord's residence at the center of the estate was the "manor house," and by metonymy the whole estate — and then the house itself — came to be called the manor. Modern senses and shades.
  • Large country house: the everyday modern sense ("a manor in the Cotswolds"), often with "house" ("manor house") or alone ("a stately manor").
  • The landed estate itself: "the manor of Netherfield," the territory with its farms, woods, and rights.
  • British informal/slang: "on my manor" — a police officer's or criminal's local territory or area of operation, from the idea of a "beat" as one's own estate; also in general British slang "one's home area, stamping ground."
  • Legal-historical: a manor as a unit of feudal jurisdiction with its court, demesne, and tenants — still used in property law and history.
Register and collocations.
  • "Manor house," "the lord of the manor" (the estate owner, an honorary title still surviving), "manorial rights," "the manor court."
  • "On my manor" (British slang, police/criminal), "in the manor" (informal, one's home patch).
  • Register: the house sense is neutral-to-elegant and slightly old-fashioned; the feudal sense is historical and formal; the slang sense is colloquial and chiefly British.
Polysemy summary (all senses, living and historical).
  • Large country house with lands — modern, dominant.
  • The landed estate and its rights — modern-formal, historical.
  • Feudal unit of land tenure and jurisdiction — historical-legal.
  • British slang: one's local territory, beat, or area of operation — modern colloquial.
  • Manor house (the lord's residence at the center of the estate) — the concrete building sense.
The semantic bridge. Latin manēre means "to remain, to stay, to dwell." A manoir in Old French was "a dwelling, an abode." The word's whole history is about staying put: the place where a lord remained and dwelt became his estate, the unit of land and rights attached to his residence. From "dwelling" the word extended to "the estate around the dwelling," then to "the feudal jurisdiction attached to that estate," and finally the modern sense settled on the great house itself — while the slang "on my manor" returned the word to territory pure and simple, a lord's domain shrunken to a police beat.
