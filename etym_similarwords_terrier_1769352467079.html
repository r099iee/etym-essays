• Hound: Similar because both are hunting dogs with defined working roles; differs because hounds hunt by scent or sight over open ground (foxhounds, beagles, greyhounds), while terriers are the earth-dogs that follow quarry into underground burrows — the two families divide the hunt between the surface and the ground beneath it.
  • Ratter: Similar because both are dogs whose job is pursuing small burrowing animals; differs because a "ratter" is a functional description of any dog, or even a cat, that catches rats, while a terrier is a specific breed family whose name encodes the earth-digging role itself.
  • Feist: Similar because both are small, spirited hunting dogs; differs because a feist is an American term for a small mixed-breed hunting dog of the southern states, while a terrier is a distinct breed family of British origin with a defined "earth-dog" ancestry.
  • Lurcher: Similar because both are working dogs used by hunters; differs because a lurcher is a cross-bred dog (typically a sighthound crossed with a collie or terrier) bred for poaching and coursing, while a terrier is a pure-bred family defined by burrowing rather than by cross-breeding or speed.
  • Mongrel: Similar because both are ordinary, non-sporting dog categories; differs because a mongrel is any dog of mixed or unknown ancestry, while a terrier, however mixed in practice, denotes a specific recognised breed type with a documented working history.
  • Cur: Similar because both can be small or mixed dogs; differs because "cur" is a contemptuous, dismissive word for a worthless or mongrel dog, while "terrier" is a neutral, even admiring name for a recognised working breed.
  • Watchdog: Similar because both are small, alert dog types in popular imagination; differs because a watchdog is defined by its guarding function, while a terrier is defined by its earth-digging hunting function and only incidentally barks at strangers.
  • Canine: Similar because both refer to dogs; differs because "canine" is the broad scientific term for the whole dog family (and its adjective form covers wolves, foxes, and jackals), while "terrier" is a precise breed-family name within it.
  • Pooch: Similar because both are affectionate everyday words for a dog; differs because "pooch" is informal slang for any dog at all, with no breed or function implied, while "terrier" names a specific small, feisty breed type.
  • Puppy: Similar because both are small-dog words; differs because "puppy" names age (a young dog), while "terrier" names breed and function; a terrier can be any age.
  • Spaniel: Similar because both are British dog breed families of the sporting type; differs because spaniels are flushing and retrieving dogs that work with gamebirds above ground, while terriers work below ground after foxes and vermin — surface worker versus underground worker.
  • Dachshund: Similar because both are short-legged dogs bred to go into burrows after badgers; differs because the dachshund is the German "badger dog" with a long, low, distinctive build, while "terrier" covers a varied group of British earth-dogs of many shapes — the German and the British solutions to the same burrowing problem.
