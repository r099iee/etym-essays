- [Old English period: the literal canopy] - overhangan (over + hang) exists in Old English for physical suspension above — a rock, a cloud, a cliff roofed over a thing; the participle overhung describes what stands shaded or covered from above, purely spatial and without menace.
- [Middle English: architecture and landscape] - the word settles into use for buildings and land: an upper storey overhangs the street, trees overhang a path; the sense of a projecting brow or roof with space left beneath becomes its signature geometry.
- [16th–19th century: the figurative weight] - overhang and overhung turn inward: a danger overhangs, fate hangs overhead, an overhung brow darkens into gloom; the word acquires a note of suspended threat — something arched above that has not yet fallen.
- [20th century: technical re-entry] - climbing and mountaineering adopt the noun overhang for an outward-projecting rock face; finance speaks of an overhang of unsold supply pressing on a market; the older pictorial sense of a roofed-over hollow stays alive in literary description such as Tolkien's dells “overhung with bushes and low trees.”
- [Contemporary sense] - overhung still names the state of being covered, roofed, or shaded from above with space left beneath — a canopy that overarches rather than lies flush — and its figurative twin, overhang, keeps the picture of weight suspended above without contact.
