- Late 16th Century (c. 1580s–1590s) - Originates in Elizabethan England as an expressive, onomatopoeic word imitating a puff of breath; immediately adopted into tobacco culture to describe a single puff, draw, or inhalation of pipe smoke.
- Early 17th Century - Broadens into meteorology and sailing; used to describe a sudden, light gust of wind across the water or a gentle puff of air that rustles sails.
- 18th to 19th Century - Develops sensory and olfactory depth, denoting a fleeting inhalation of any scent; Thomas Carlyle popularizes the vivid historical metaphor "a whiff of grapeshot" (1837) for explosive cannon fire.
- Late 19th to 20th Century - Enters sports colloquialisms (especially American baseball and golf) as an expressive term for swinging through empty air and missing the ball completely (a strikeout); simultaneously flourishes in sociopolitical journalism as "a whiff of scandal" or "a whiff of corruption."
- Contemporary Era - Firmly established in high-register literary criticism and translation studies to denote subtle, lingering stylistic contamination, syntactic flavor, or ideological undertones.
