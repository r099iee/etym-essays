• Contrariness: Similar because both name a disposition to oppose or do the opposite of what is expected; differs because contrariness is the mildest, most colloquial word — mere oppositional stubbornness, often playful — while perversity insists the behavior is unreasonable and unacceptable, and in modern use carries a darker moral charge.
  • Waywardness: Similar because both denote a turning from the expected or proper course; differs because waywardness is softer and more sympathetic, suggesting willfulness that is errant or unpredictable rather than deliberately wrong-headed, whereas perversity implies an intentional, knowing opposition.
  • Obstinacy: Similar because both involve stubborn refusal to change; differs because obstinacy is about clinging to one's own position, neutral about whether it is right or wrong, while perversity specifically describes choosing the wrong or contrary course, often just because it is contrary.
  • Stubbornness: Similar because both describe inflexible persistence; differs because stubbornness is a morally neutral trait of not yielding, whereas perversity adds the idea of turning against reason and doing the unreasonable thing on purpose.
  • Wrong-headedness: Similar because both mean being in error in a willful way; differs because wrong-headedness stresses the mistaken judgment itself — the badness of the thinking — while perversity stresses the disposition to oppose and turn aside, sometimes even knowing one is wrong.
  • Willfulness: Similar because both denote acting according to one's own will against advice or authority; differs because willfulness emphasizes determination and self-will, often admirable in persistence, whereas perversity emphasizes the contrariness of the choice, the desire to do the opposite.
  • Obduracy: Similar because both denote a hardening against reason or persuasion; differs because obduracy is a cold, unyielding hardness of heart or mind, often of sinners, while perversity is a livelier, more active turning aside — a twisted energy rather than a frozen resistance.
  • Depravity: Similar because both can denote moral corruption; differs because depravity is thoroughgoing moral rottenness, a fallen state of wickedness, with no suggestion of the active, contrary energy of perversity; perversity is the twisted turn, depravity the settled ruin.
  • Wickedness: Similar because both can mean morally wrong behavior; differs because wickedness is simply evil-doing, judged by its badness, while perversity specifically names the perverse, contrary quality — the pleasure in doing wrong that is wrong.
  • Perversion: Similar because both come from the same Latin root and denote a turning from the proper course; differs because perversion in modern use has narrowed to the sexual-clinical meaning — a deviation from the sexual or social norm — while perversity keeps the broader sense of contrariness and wayward taste.
  • Caprice: Similar because both suggest whimsy that departs from the expected; differs because caprice is sudden, motiveless whim without the element of knowing opposition, whereas perversity is a deliberate, sustained contrariness.
  • Eccentricity: Similar because both denote departure from the normal; differs because eccentricity is deviation from convention that is odd but harmless and often charming, while perversity is deviation that is unreasonable and morally or rationally unacceptable.
  • Cussedness: Similar because both denote stubborn contrariness in colloquial speech; differs because cussedness is folksy, humorous, and morally light — a stubborn cuss who won't cooperate — while perversity in modern use is more serious and judgmental.
  • Orneriness: Similar because both describe a disposition to be contrary and uncooperative; differs because orneriness is an American colloquialism for irritable, mean-tempered stubbornness, whereas perversity has a formal, even moral, register and a learned Latin pedigree.
