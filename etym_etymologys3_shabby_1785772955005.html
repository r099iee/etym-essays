The history of shabby is a history of surfaces — first skin, then cloth, then character. Deep in Germanic, a scrape-and-scab root produced Old English sceabb and the familiar English scab and scabby, words for crusted itch and mange and, by insult, for contemptible people. The underlying family reaches, with debate, to an Indo-European base *skab- / *skebh- 'to cut, scratch, scrape'; Latin scabere 'to scratch' and scabies 'itch, mange' (which English borrowed directly as the medical word scabies) are parallel Italic reflexes, while the Germanic forms gave Old English sceabb and Old Norse skabbi 'scab'. The exact PIE reconstruction and vowel grade are debated, and not all authorities accept a clean Indo-European etymon for the scab family; what is certain is the Germanic picture of a scraped, roughened, flaking surface.

In the seventeenth century a colloquial twin, shabby, surfaces with a softened onset and a widening job description. The intermediate step is the English dialect noun shab, from the sixteenth century: 'a scab; a scurfy or mangy condition; also a low, contemptible person'. Shab is a variant of scab with the /ʃ/ initial — the sh- spelling that gives shabby its modern shape — and the adjective shabby, first recorded in the 1660s, originally meant 'scurfy, scabby, diseased-looking'. From that skin-deep beginning the word began to travel up the social body. What is scabby is flaking and unclean; what is shabby soon need not be diseased at all — it may simply look as if wear and neglect had raised a kind of social mange on coat, cuff, and carpet.

Eighteenth-century Britain, obsessed with dress as class signal, made the adjective indispensable. Novelists and essayists needed a term for the coat that had been turned once too often, for rooms that kept up a pretense of gentility while the wallpaper peeled. The phrase 'shabby genteel' crystallized this world: poverty that still wears the uniform of respectability, the down-at-heel clerk, the impoverished gentlewoman. The example card inhabits exactly this territory — the elderly painter's shabby, unheated apartment with peeling wallpaper, and the noble dignity that survives it. From that material theater the moral sense was almost inevitable: if clothes could be shabby, so could hospitality, gratitude, and political dealing. A 'shabby trick' treats honour as a fabric worn thin; 'shabby treatment' makes ethics visible as worn cloth. German shows the same drift independently: schäbig, 'shabby, worn, mean, stingy', from Middle Low German schabbich 'scabby, shabby', confirms that the scab-family in West Germanic readily hosts both physical and ethical roughness.

In modern English the dual life remains fully active. Fashion and interior description still depend on shabby for faded sofas and outworn suits; the ethics of everyday life depend on it for petty betrayal — the stingy refusal, the underhanded excuse, the mean trick. Victorian and Edwardian fiction made it a character-sketch default for down-at-heel clerks, misers' offices, and dishonourable bargains, and the association of shabbiness with moral as well as material decline became a cultural habit. The late-modern phrase shabby chic performs a cultural inversion: emerging in the design world of the late twentieth century, it markets the look of deliberate wear and faded elegance as high style, converting the stigma of neglect into a badge of taste — without erasing the older condemnatory sense elsewhere. The same speaker can call a room 'shabby chic' and a friend's treatment 'shabby' in one breath.

Etymologically, speakers have mostly forgotten the scab. Yet the word's slight visceral edge — more cutting than 'worn', less clinical than 'dilapidated' — remembers that first image of an imperfect, flaking surface, and it does so through an accident of phonology: the /ʃ/ onset, born of a dialectal variant of scab, is the sound fingerprint of the whole family. Morphologically the word is a simple derivation (shab + -y), with the regular inflection shabbier / shabbiest, the adverb shabbily, and the noun shabbiness. In the example card, the adjective does delicate work: the apartment is shabby, but the painter retains an air of noble dignity, and the contrast between the failing room and the unbroken person is precisely the tension the word's history has always carried — surfaces that decline while something inside refuses to be worn away.
