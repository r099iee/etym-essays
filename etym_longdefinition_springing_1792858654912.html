Springing, the present participle of the verb spring, names the sudden elastic leap into motion: rising, jumping, or bursting forth swiftly and energetically. In the source sentence Gandalf is springing to his feet — launching upright in a single urgent movement — and the participle catches alarm, vigour, and resolve in one bound.

In the source Gandalf's leap punctuates crisis: something seen or said demands instant action, and the old wizard, staff and all, launches himself erect. The verb honours his suddenness — no slow rising, no creaking joints, but the coiled release of a man half his apparent years. Readers hear the elastic snap in the word itself: springing sounds like what it describes.

Core meaning in detail: To spring is to move by sudden elastic force — the body leaving the ground (springing over a wall), the limbs unfolding (springing to attention), or things emerging rapidly (springs of water, springing surprises). As a participle, springing keeps the motion in progress and the energy coiled: a springing step, a springing trap, tears springing to the eyes. The physics is always the same — stored energy released at once — whether the spring is muscular, mechanical, or emotional.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Leaping suddenly upward or forward (primary sense, from Old English): springing to one's feet, springing from an ambush, athletes springing from the blocks. This is the source-sentence sense and the oldest picture in the word: the body as its own catapult.
  • Arising or emerging swiftly (ancient figurative branch): rivers springing from hillsides, doubts springing to mind, nations springing to arms. The bridge is the fountain-mouth: water bursting from rock looks exactly like a leap, so every sudden emergence borrows the verb.
  • Operating by elastic mechanism (technical, early modern): springing traps, springing locks, springing mattresses — devices that store and release force. The bridge names the metal coil spring itself after the leap it performs, then lends the participle back to everything the coil drives.
  • Seasonal and botanical springing (cyclical sense): leaves springing, crops springing, springing verdure — growth as a slow leap. The bridge crowns the season: spring the season is literally the springing-time, when the year leaps back to life.
  • Causing suddenly — to spring something on someone (idiomatic, 19th c.): springing a question, springing an ambush, springing a leak. The bridge runs through the trap: as a snare springs shut on its victim, so news or plans spring upon the unready — with a leak, the vessel itself springs open.

Register and connotation: springing is bright, vigorous, and optimistic — the participle of energy itself. Even its ambushes are admired for cleverness rather than condemned, and its tears (springing to her eyes) are sympathetic, never servile. Against creeping, trudging, and shuffling, springing is youth's verb at every age: Gandalf earns it precisely because wizards his age are supposed to rise slowly.
