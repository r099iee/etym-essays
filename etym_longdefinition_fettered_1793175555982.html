Fettered is the past participle of the verb fetter: bound with fetters, the chains or shackles fastened round the ankles of prisoners to prevent escape. In its literal sense it describes captives, slaves, and beasts of burden whose feet are chained together so that they can shuffle but never run, the iron made heavier still in the source sentence by twelve riveted chains on a giant hanging from a beam.

In the source sentence the word is at its most grimly physical: an old man of huge form hangs from a beam, ______ with twelve riveted iron chains. The number twelve and the rivets stress utter inescapability; this is not a prisoner awaiting trial but a monster secured, a Koshchei-like captive whose very size demands a dozen chains. The participle works as pure description of bondage made visible.

Core meaning in detail: A fetter is specifically a foot-shackle, distinct from a handcuff (wrists) and a manacle (hands in older use), though poets mix them freely. To be fettered is therefore to have one's stride stolen: the legs remain strong but the gait is reduced to a clanking shuffle. The verb extends naturally from the hardware to the condition, so that one can be fettered to something (chained to a post) or fettered by something (held by debt, custom, or fear).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Chained by the feet (primary literal sense, from Old English): prisoners, galley-slaves, and the fairy-tale giant of the source all wear iron that joins ankle to ankle. The bridge is the noun itself: Old English fetor named the foot-shackle, and the participle simply reports wearing it.
  • Restrained or restricted generally (figurative, from Middle English): fettered by convention, a mind fettered by superstition. The bridge is the body's chain carried onto the spirit: whatever narrows freedom of movement, whether red tape or timidity, is felt as iron on the ankles.
  • Hobbled animals (rural technical sense): horses and camels are fettered or hobbled at pasture so they cannot stray. The bridge is practical husbandry: the same hardware that holds felons holds livestock, and the verb serves both stable and dungeon.
  • Bound by obligation or contract (legal and social sense): fettered by a treaty, fettered to an unhappy bargain. The bridge runs through the oath as chain: promises, debts, and alliances link the promiser as surely as links of iron, and Victorian prose loves the image of nations fettered by secret engagements.
  • Unfettered as the freer opposite (derived idiom): far commoner today than fettered itself is unfettered: unfettered markets, unfettered imagination. The bridge is negation as praise: freedom is defined as the absence of chains, and the negative adjective outlives its root because modern rhetoric prefers celebrating liberty to describing bondage.

Register and connotation: fettered is weighty and archaic in literal use, belonging to dungeons, sagas, and fairy tales, while its figurative use is elevated editorial English: journalists write of economies fettered by regulation and artists fettered by tradition. The word always drags its iron with it, lending even abstract restraint a clanking, dungeon-like menace that limited or restricted can never match.
