- Early English equine cry - first recorded simply as the horse's cry, an onomatopoeic word; for centuries it was used interchangeably with "neigh," with no clear distinction of pitch or register.
- 17th-18th century narrowing - lexicographers and writers begin to split the equine sound family: "neigh" is reserved for the loud, far-carrying cry, while "whinny" becomes fixed as the softer, higher, more trilled variant, often a call of recognition or entreaty.
- 19th-century literary warmth - in Victorian and Edwardian prose and children's literature, the whinny becomes the affectionate, intimate greeting of a beloved horse - low-stakes, trusting, and domestic - while "neigh" keeps its neutral, commanding force.
- Contemporary figurative extension - modern usage adds a human echo: a "whinny" can describe a high, soft, half-suppressed laugh or a querulous voice, while the equine sense remains gentle and endearing rather than alarming.
