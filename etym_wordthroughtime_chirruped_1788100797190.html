- Mid-15th century: "chirp" enters the written record (c. 1440) — an echoic coinage for the small sharp note of birds; everyday, sound-built vocabulary from its first appearance.
- Late 16th century (1570s): "chirrup" is coined as the lengthened, softened variant — a full trilled run of notes, understood by folk etymology as "chirp" + "up" ("cheer up"): the bird that keeps its song going.
- 17th–18th centuries: the word gains a job — coachmen and huntsmen "chirrup" to horses with a sharp clucking encouragement; the bird word becomes a stable-yard signal and runs for two centuries as standard coaching vocabulary.
- 19th century: Victorian cosiness — the chirruping cricket on the hearth (Dickens's The Cricket on the Hearth, 1845) makes the word the emblem of domestic cheer; chirruping sparrows and chicks fill children's books; the word is now small, warm, and reassuring.
- Early 20th century: the horse-chirrup dies with the coach; "chirrup" survives as a literary nature word and a period flavour.
- Today: "chirruped" is a gently antiquated literary past tense — the cheerful small sound of a Victorian-parlour world; ordinary speech prefers "chirped" and "twittered".
