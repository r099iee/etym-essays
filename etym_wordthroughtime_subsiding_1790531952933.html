- Late 17th c. (c. 1680s–1710s) - Introduced primarily into scientific, chemical, and philosophical prose to describe suspended sediment or precipitates settling downward to the bottom of a liquid mixture; perceived as a precise, formal natural-philosophy descriptor.
- 18th c. - Expanded into physical geography and meteorology to depict the falling of flood levels, the draining of marshes, and the abatement of oceanic storms; viewed as an elegant, evocative Latinate term in travel narratives and natural histories.
- Early 19th c. (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Deepened into psychological and emotional registers, describing the gradual abatement of violent passions, grief, manic excitement, or feverish illness; celebrated in literature for capturing subtle emotional transitions from tempestuousness to tranquility.
- Mid-to-Late 19th c. - Formalized in civil engineering, mining, and geology to describe the downward settling or collapse of ground surfaces, mine shafts, and structural foundations.
- 20th–21st c. - Ubiquitous in standard English across physical, emotional, and institutional registers; frequently employed in geopolitical discourse (e.g., tensions or conflicts subsiding), medical reports (inflammation or pain subsiding), and climate reporting (floodwaters or coastal land subsiding).
