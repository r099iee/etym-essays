• Indra: Similar because both refer to the identical supreme Vedic sky-god, thunderbolt-wielder, and ruler of Svarga; differs because Indra is his primordial primary theonym of ancient Indo-Iranian antiquity, whereas Śakra originated as an adjectival honorific epithet specifically emphasizing his absolute functional potency, capability, and might.
  • Purandara: Similar because both are prestigious epic titles of the king of the gods; differs because Purandara specifically means "destroyer of enemy fortresses or citadels," highlighting Indra's siege-breaking military violence against demonic strongholds, whereas Śakra conveys intrinsic personal power and sovereign majesty.
  • Vāsava: Similar because both designate Indra as sovereign over heavenly classes; differs because Vāsava denotes his genealogical role as the chief, lord, and leader of the eight Vasus (elemental deities of nature and light), whereas Śakra denotes unconstrained divine capability.
  • Maghavan: Similar because both are archaic Rigvedic designations of the supreme deity; differs because Maghavan literally means "the bountiful one" or "the generous dispenser of sacrificial gifts and wealth," focusing on his munificence toward poets and priests, whereas Śakra focuses on raw capability and strength.
  • Vajrapāṇi: Similar because both celebrate the deity's invincible power over cosmic enemies; differs because Vajrapāṇi is an epithet meaning "he who holds the thunderbolt in his hand," focusing on his iconic physical weapon, whereas Śakra is a broad title of supreme capacity.
  • Devendra: Similar because both identify him as the king of the gods; differs because Devendra is a descriptive tatpuruṣa compound literally translating as "lord/ruler of the devas," serving as an administrative title of office, while Śakra is an ancient personal name.
  • Zeus: Similar because both are sovereign thunder-wielding patriarchal kings of the gods in Indo-European pantheons; differs because Zeus descends linguistically from PIE *Dyeus Ph₂tēr (sky-father, cognate with Vedic Dyaus Pitā), whereas Śakra embodies the dynamic storm-warrior who supplanted the older sky-father.
  • Jupiter: Similar because both govern thunder, atmospheric storms, state oaths, and sovereign victory; differs because Jupiter (Iuppiter Optimus Maximus) was the supreme civic deity of the Roman state and senate, whereas Śakra functions within the cyclical karmic cosmos of Indian myth.
  • Thor: Similar because both are fierce Indo-European weather gods who battle cosmic serpents/giants with thunder weapons (Mjölnir vs. Vajra); differs because Thor is a rough-hewn warrior champion subordinate to Odin, whereas Śakra is himself the enthroned king of the gods.
  • monarch: Similar because both denote supreme sovereign rulers holding undisputed dominion; differs because monarch is a secular political noun for a mortal crowned head of state, lacking the divine, cosmic, and storm-controlling dimensions of Śakra.
  • potentate: Similar because both etymologically derive from roots denoting great power and ability (*śak- / *pot-); differs because potentate is often used in modern English with connotations of autocratic, arbitrary, or pompous earthly rule, whereas Śakra retains sacred mythic dignity.
  • sovereign: Similar because both designate supreme executive authority over a realm; differs because sovereign is a broad political and constitutional concept, while Śakra is a specific mythological divinity ruling the celestial heavens.
