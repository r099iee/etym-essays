incitement is a noun naming both the act and the product of rousing, urging, or spurring a person, a group, or a whole people toward action, feeling, or thought. In its primary, still fully productive sense it denotes (1) the act of inciting — the action of moving someone to do something, typically by persuasion, exhortation, appeal, or the arousal of passion — and (2) the thing that incites: a stimulus, goad, spur, instigation, or influence that sets the mind or the will in motion. The word is built transparently on the verb to incite plus the nominal suffix -ment, which English had been absorbing through Anglo-Norman since the thirteenth century to form nouns of action or result (as in encouragement, appointment, punishment). An incitement is therefore, grammatically and semantically, both "the inciting" and "that which incites" — the doing and the spur.

The sense is morally neutral in its origin: Latin incitāre meant simply "to put into quick motion, to urge forward, to spur (a horse)", and the moral colouring the word takes on depends entirely on the context in which it is used. In the dedication to On Liberty (1859), John Stuart Mill deploys the word with a deliberately positive, ennobling force: he declares that Harriet Taylor's "exalted sense of truth and right" was his strongest incitement, and that her approbation was his chief reward. Here the incitement is not a lure, a bribe, or a goad to wrongdoing but the sustained moral-intellectual power that animated "all that is best" in his writings — the very engine of his authorship, placing Harriet Taylor at the generative centre of his intellectual life. Mill chooses the word precisely because, in mid-Victorian elevated prose, it could still carry this high, almost Platonic force: a rousing of the whole man by a beloved and trusted judge.

Across its history the noun has developed a broad spread of senses and registers, best surveyed point by point:

  • The act of rousing or urging (act-noun sense) — "the incitement of the troops by their general", "incitement to rebellion": the deliberate stirring of persons or bodies of persons toward a specified course. This is the oldest English sense, following the verb into the language in the later sixteenth century.
  • The spur, stimulus, or influence that rouses (thing-sense) — "her praise was an incitement to further effort"; the concrete or abstract goad that sets the will in motion from outside the agent.
  • The legal sense (modern criminal law, 20th–21st centuries) — "incitement to violence", "incitement to hatred", "incitement to riot", "incitement to commit a crime": in many jurisdictions incitement is a substantive offence or a mode of liability, defined as urging or encouraging another person to commit an offence. This is now the dominant sense in news, law, and political discourse, and it has narrowed the everyday meaning of the word toward the condemnatory.
  • The rhetorical and political sense — the inflammatory speech or writing that provokes crowds, mobs, or nations; closely bound up with inflammatory, demagoguery, rabble-rousing, and sedition, and central to modern debates about free speech, hate speech, and the limits of incitement.
  • The psychological and behavioural sense — in experimental and social psychology, the "inciting stimulus" or "inciting event" that triggers a response in an organism: technically any antecedent condition that raises the probability of a behaviour, morally neutral by definition.
  • The older religious and moral sense — "incitements to virtue", "incitements to godliness" (17th–19th centuries): a spurring toward the good, the commendatory use of which Mill's dedication is the late and lofty echo, showing how far the word once ranged toward praise.

Historical semantic bridge. Every one of these senses flows from a single Latin image: in- "into" + citāre "to put into motion, summon, urge" (the frequentative of ciēre "to set in motion, rouse"), the same root that gives English cite, excite, resuscitate, and solicit. To incite is to set the will in motion by pressure applied from outside — the image of a rider spurring a horse, or a summons that sets a man moving. Mill exploits that image for moral elevation; criminal law, three centuries later, borrowed it for the speech that sets a crowd in motion toward violence. The underlying metaphor is constant; the moral sign has swung from commendation through neutrality to condemnation, depending on who is being moved and toward what.

Boundaries and collocations. Incitement is to be distinguished from stimulus (any morally neutral condition evoking a response), from incentive (a calculated future reward offered in advance), from motive (an internal reason within the agent), and from instigation and provocation (which in modern usage usually imply egging on to wrongdoing). It collocates strongly with to: "incitement to violence", "to hatred", "to rebellion", "to action"; and with verbs such as constitute, amount to, provide, offer. In law and journalism the word is now predominantly condemnatory; in formal or historical prose, as in Mill, it may still carry its older, ennobling force. It is the nominal counterpart of the verb incite and stands at the head of a family that includes inciter, inciting, and the near-synonymous instigation.
