Bickering is the present participle and gerund of the verb bicker: to engage in petty, noisy, and repetitive contention about trifles — a squabble conducted in rapid, snappish exchanges rather than a serious argument. Because the verb is essentially habitual and continuous in meaning, the participle bickering emphasises the ongoing, wearying quality of such disputes: bickering does not resolve; it simply goes on, like rain pattering on a roof or a flame flickering. In its earliest English use the verb meant “to skirmish” or “to exchange light, sharp blows,” and a secondary imitative sense describes quick, light, repeated sounds or movements — water rippling over stones, rain pattering, fire flickering — which survives in literary usage to this day. Primary sense
  • Petty quarrelling: to argue about small, trifling matters in a noisy, repetitive, ill-tempered way; the word covers the rapid exchange of small complaints, taunts, and contradictions that never reaches the dignity of a real argument. “The children’s constant bickering over the toy drove their parents to distraction.”
  • Habitual and continuous: bickering is by nature ongoing; it is the wearying, unresolved squabble that continues for hours without profit.
Secondary sense (imitative)
  • To flicker, ripple, or patter: of a flame, light, water, or rain, to move or sound with quick, light, repeated motion — the same rapid, repeated pattern applied to sound and movement rather than words. “The stream bickered over the pebbles.” This sense is recorded from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and was taken up by the Romantic and Victorian poets (Southey, Tennyson, Dickens).
Etymological bridge The two senses are not accidental homonyms but two branches of one image: repeated light striking. The oldest English sense of bicker (late fourteenth century) was military — to skirmish, to exchange repeated light, sharp blows in the small encounters of border warfare. When the word shifted from blows to words (fifteenth to sixteenth century), the “exchange of sharp, rapid blows” became the “exchange of sharp, rapid retorts,” and quarrelling about trifles was seen as a kind of verbal skirmish. The imitative sense of flickering and rippling simply keeps the same pattern of rapid repetition in the world of sound and light. All three senses — skirmish, squabble, flicker — share the root image of repeated, rapid, light striking. Word-use note (The Hobbit) In The Hobbit, Chapter 2 (‘Roast Mutton’), the participle describes exactly what Gandalf has engineered: “It was the wizard’s voice that had kept the trolls bickering and quarrelling, until the light came and made an end of them.” Hidden in the dark, Gandalf has been mimicking first one troll’s voice and then another’s, so that each believes his companions are insulting him and contradicting him; their natural contentiousness does the rest. The word bickering is chosen with care: the trolls’ dispute is not a noble debate or a decisive fight but a foolish, repetitive exchange of taunts and objections, precisely the kind of squabble that can be sustained for hours without profit — and it is that very habit of petty quarrelling, endlessly renewed, that keeps them arguing in the open until dawn, when the rising sun turns them to stone. Their bickering is thus not merely comic noise; it is the mechanism of their doom. Neighbours and boundaries
  • Quarrelling is the most general term and may cover serious disputes; bickering restricts the dispute to trifles.
  • Squabbling stresses the noisy, undignified character of the exchange; bickering stresses its rapid, repetitive, snapping quality.
  • Wrangling suggests prolonged, argumentative dispute, often with a contentious edge; arguing is neutral; feuding is long-lasting and often hostile on a larger scale.
  • In the imitative sense, flickering (of flame), rippling (of water), and pattering (of rain) are the near-synonyms of bickering.
Origin in brief Bicker is a Middle English word, first recorded about 1380–1400 as bikkeren, a frequentative built on the sound-base *bik-, “to strike, thrust, peck” — with relatives in Middle Dutch bikken and Middle Low German bicken (“to hack, cut, strike”) and, in the Romance world, Old French bequer, “to peck with the beak,” from bec, “beak.” The frequentative suffix -eren marks repeated action, and that repetition is exactly what bickering still means.
