An Indo-European color root. Ruddy belongs to one of the oldest word-families in the language: the Indo-European root *h₁rewdʰ-, meaning "red, reddish," the same root that gives English red, and one that appears across the whole family — Latin ruber and rufus, Greek erythros, Sanskrit rudhira (blood), and Celtic derivatives. What is distinctive about ruddy is that it does not come from the plain Germanic adjective for red but from a special ablaut grade of the same root, the *rūd- form, which produced an Old English noun rūd meaning "redness, a red or ruddy complexion."

Old English formation. Old English joined this noun with the common adjective-forming suffix -ig (the ancestor of modern -y) to make rudig, attested from around the ninth century, meaning "reddish, of a red color" — and, centrally, "having a red face or complexion." The word was thus, from its very first appearance, a complexion word: the redness of health and vigor written on the skin. It coexisted with rēad (the direct ancestor of red) but specialized toward warm, healthy, living redness rather than the bare color itself.

Middle English and the healthy glow. Middle English continued rudy and roudie as standard descriptive adjectives, used above all of faces, cheeks, and blushes — the ruddy face of the healthy countryman, the ruddy cheeks of youth. The word also broadened to red or reddish things generally: ruddy firelight, ruddy sunset, ruddy wine. Its social value was consistently positive: to be ruddy was to be alive, warm, well-fed, and strong — the complexion of the hearty and the honest, a visible sign of good health in an age when pallor meant illness.

Early modern and the rise of the intensifier. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries ruddy remained a stock poetic adjective for glowing redness. Shakespeare's "ruddy drops that visit my sad heart" shows the word reaching beyond complexion to vivid bodily redness. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, British dialect and slang developed the word's second life: as a euphemistic disguise for "bloody," used as a mild swear and intensifier ("ruddy well," "ruddy awful"), appearing in print from the early twentieth century. This shift took a warm color word and made it, in a separate register, a printable stand-in for profanity — one of the oddest journeys any color word has made.

Natural history and the modern world. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, naturalists pressed ruddy into service as a taxonomic color label: the ruddy duck, the ruddy turnstone, the ruddy shelduck, and plants with reddish parts all take their names from the color term. Modern descriptive prose keeps the primary senses fully alive — ruddy cheeks after a walk, ruddy firelight, ruddy sand — and the word retains a distinctly warm, old-fashioned, storybook flavor, ideal for fantasy atmospheres like the ruddy light of a dragon's hoard.

Summary. From the Indo-European root for red, through the Old English noun rūd (redness) and the adjective rudig (reddish, red-faced), ruddy has spent a thousand years meaning warm, healthy redness — and, since the twentieth century, also serving as a mild British oath. A color word that went from complexion to expletive while never losing its glow.
