Evolution of "debarred." The verb DEBARRED leads back to the most concrete of images: a bar of wood laid across a door. The Old French verb debarrer, "to bar out, to shut out with a bar," combined de- ("away, from") with barrer ("to bar"), built on the noun barre ("a bar, a beam"). That noun barre is not a native item of classical Latin vocabulary: it entered the language of the late Empire through the vulgar register, very likely from a Gaulish or other Celtic/substrate word meaning a point, a spike, or a wooden pole. Its ultimate origin therefore lies beyond secure Indo-European reconstruction, and any reconstructed PIE form for it is conjectural — a caveat that must be stated rather than papered over. What matters for the history of debarred is that the barrier was physical: a gate barred against an intruder, a door bolted against entry, a person shut out by the simple act of dropping a beam across the threshold.

1. From beam to institution (the medieval law). The medieval law courts turned this physical bar into an institution. In Anglo-Norman England, the bar was the wooden rail that separated the judges and the advocates from the body of the court; the word bar came to name both that rail and the legal profession that pleaded behind it, and the phrase "called to the bar" still survives in legal English. From the fourteenth century the verb debarren, together with its English form debar, entered the language of the law to describe a formal exclusion: debarring a claimant from a plea, debarring an offender from the city, debarring a party from pursuing an action. The semantic route is mechanical and transparent — a person was debarred as if a bar had been dropped across the entrance to a right, a privilege, or a place — and the word carried, from the first, the authority of courts and statutes. In ecclesiastical and civic usage the same image served: a person could be debarred from the sacraments, from office, or from the guild, each time with the picture of a barrier that only authority could remove.

2. The figurative turn (the mind as a barred door). Modern English has kept the legal and administrative core of the word. To be debarred is to be formally excluded — from public office, from professional practice, from a competition, from public contracts — and the noun debarment names the administrative act of such exclusion, a word now current in the jargon of procurement, professional regulation, and international trade (suspension and debarment regimes, debarred lists, debarment for misconduct). At the same time, however, the word developed a figurative force that carries the physical image into the mind. Because the barrier could be internal as well as external, a principle, a scruple, or a belief could debar a person from a thought — exactly as, in the sentence preserved in this card, the philosopher John Stuart Mill was "debarred from thinking so" by his own belief in the equality of the sexes. Here the bar has moved indoors: the conviction acts as a beam dropped across the doorway of an idea, making the thought not merely improper but impossible for the thinker. This mental, principled sense is a striking late development of a word born in the most material of images.

3. The modern balance. Today the word sits at the formal end of the register: legal and administrative prose uses debarred and debarment with precision, while ordinary speech prefers barred, excluded, or prevented. The word has thus preserved both halves of its history — the institutional barrier of the courts and the principled barrier of the mind — and in both it retains the authority and gravity of a bar across a door.

  • Pre-Latin substrate: *barra "bar, beam, stake" — a vulgar Latin item, very likely from a Gaulish/Celtic source; no secure PIE ancestor (origin uncertain).
  • Old French: barre "bar, beam; bar of a gate; bar of a court" → debarrer "to bar out, shut out with a bar" (de- + barrer).
  • Anglo-Norman/Middle English (14th–15th c.): debarren "to shut out, exclude, preclude"; the bar of the court becomes the institutional image.
  • Medieval law: debarring a claimant, offender, or party — formal exclusion by authority; ecclesiastical and civic uses (sacraments, office, guild).
  • 18th–19th century: figurative extension to mental and moral exclusion, as in Mill "debarred from thinking so" — a principle as a bar across a thought.
  • Contemporary: legal and administrative core retained (debarment in procurement, professional regulation); in speech replaced by barred, excluded, prevented.
