The history of snarling is the history of a sound humans have imitated for as long as they have lived beside dogs. Its ancestor is not a grand classical root but a scrap of echoic Germanic vocabulary: the syllable snar, shaped by bared teeth and a wrinkled nose, which sounds like the very growl it names. Middle Dutch and Middle Low German snarren meant to growl, grumble, or rattle, and Middle English borrowed or shared the same bark of a word as snar, to growl or snap at, by the late fourteenth century.

The decisive step came in the 1590s, when English fitted the short verb with its frequentative -le suffix — the same ending that turns gab to gabble or prate to prattle — creating snarl for the repeated, sustained growl rather than a single snap. The new form was immediately useful: it distinguished the simmering warning from the momentary bark, and writers applied it within decades to quarrelsome humans, scolding voices, and personified winds as well as to dogs. The participle snarling rode the same wave, naming hostility in progress.

Stages of the journey:

  • Echoic Germanic beginnings: short snar-type forms across Dutch, Low German, and Middle English, all imitating the throat-and-nose constriction of the growl itself, with no classical pedigree behind them.
  • The frequentative lift of the 1590s: snarl emerges as the iterative of snar, fixing the sense of a kept-up growl; early attestations already pair the sound with the bared-teeth expression.
  • Seventeenth-century spread: from kennels to pamphlets, the verb and participle migrate to human quarrels, so that divines and dramatists alike denounce snarling opponents and snarling tongues.
  • The novelist's adjective: Gothic and Victorian fiction makes snarling the stock audible signature of villains, curs, and storms, cementing the bridge from animal warning to human ill temper.
  • Modern headline shorthand: twentieth-century journalism generalises the participle to any sustained hostility — snarling rows, snarling exchanges — where readers instantly hear the tone.

Phonetically almost nothing has moved: the consonant cluster sn- still forces the nose to wrinkle as the word is spoken, and the dark vowel plus trilled or tapped r still rumbles in the throat, so saying snarling remains a small performance of the growl. Semantically the word has been remarkably stable — six centuries of bared teeth — because the behaviour it names never changes: dogs still guard bones, crowds still hiss, and prisoners, like the one in the source sentence, still alternate between snivelling submission and snarling defiance when the truth is wrung out of them.

Early attestations reward the effort: Shakespeare's contemporaries already set snarling curs on stage, and by the eighteenth century the participle had become a favourite of satirists who heard snarling envy in every rival's review. Tolkien, with a philologist's ear, restores the kennel literalism — his prisoner's snarling is animal before it is moral — while modern headlines complete the circle, letting ministers snarl and markets snarl with the same throaty menace. The frequentative suffix that made the word has long since gone quiet in English, but in snarling it still does its old work: one snap ends, a snarl keeps going.
