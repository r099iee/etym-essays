Proto-Indo-European: *(s)kem- "to cut, shear, pare, shorten" (also reconstructed as *(s)kembh- "to cut, mangle"; both forms are debated — see note below; c. 4500–3500 BCE) → Proto-Germanic: *skamma- / *skammaz "short, cut short, clipped" (adjective; reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Old Norse: skammr "short, brief" (adjective; attested in runic inscriptions and sagas, c. 8th–13th c.), with neuter singular skamt "shortly; a short portion, a small amount" → Middle English: scant "short, deficient, falling short of full measure" (borrowed from Old Norse skamt via northern Danelaw dialects; attested from c. 13th–14th c.) → Early Modern English: scant + -y (adjectival suffix) → scanty "small, insufficient, meager" (first attested 1650s) → Modern English: scanty "small, insufficient in quantity; skimpy in covering" ◄ TARGET Debated / nuanced stages: - The PIE reconstruction is genuinely debated. Many authorities set up *(s)kem- "to cut, shear," while others prefer *(s)kembh- "to cut, mangle, bite off" (a root seen in Greek and in words for mutilation). The Germanic adjective *skamma- "short" is nonetheless secure, attested through Old Norse skammr and related Germanic forms. - The semantic bridge from "short" (linear length) to "insufficient" (quantity) happened within Old Norse and early Middle English, where a short measure came to mean a meager supply; this is an attested polysemy, not an invented link. - The -y formation of scanty from scant (1650s) is a straightforward English-internal derivation, well documented in dictionaries.
