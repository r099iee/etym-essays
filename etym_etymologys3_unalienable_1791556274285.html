Proto-Indo-European Roots and Latin Morphology

The word unalienable is a hybrid linguistic construction uniting a native Germanic negative prefix with a deeply rooted Latinate legal base. The core lexical root reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European demonstrative adjective *h₂élyos ('other, another, different'). This root was extraordinarily prolific throughout the Indo-European family, generating Greek ἄλλος (állos, 'other'), Old Irish aile, Gothic aljis ('other'), and Old English elles (the ancestor of modern English else).

In the Italic branch, Proto-Italic *aljos developed into the Classical Latin adjective alius ('another, other, different'). From alius, Latin formed the secondary possessive adjective aliēnus, signifying 'belonging to another, of another person, foreign, strange, exotic'. In Roman civil law, aliēnus took on immediate economic and legal significance: property was either suus (one's own) or aliēnus (belonging to another). To describe the act of transferring property from oneself into the ownership of another person, Roman jurists coined the first-conjugation verb aliēnāre ('to make another's, to transfer ownership, to convey property, to sell, to estrange'). By affixing the verbal suffix -ābilis ('capable of being'), Imperial Latin jurists formed the adjective aliēnābilis, denoting that which is legally capable of being transferred, sold, or conveyed to a third party.

Medieval Feudal Law and the Evolution of Alienation

Following the collapse of the Western Roman Empire, Roman property concepts were heavily transformed by feudalism. In feudal tenure, land was not held in absolute individual ownership (allodial title), but as a fief granted by a superior lord in exchange for military service and fealty. Consequently, land was strictly non-transferable without the explicit consent of the feudal overlord. The verb passed through Old French as aliéner and the adjective as alienable (attested in the 13th century), describing estates that could lawfully be transferred.

When the term was absorbed into Middle English around the late 14th and 15th centuries as alienable, it functioned primarily within real property law. Simultaneously, canon lawyers debated whether ecclesiastical lands—the patrimony of the Church and the poor—could ever be alienated by bishops, concluding that sacred benefices were essentially inalienable because mortal churchmen were merely temporary stewards, not sovereign owners.

The Germanic Prefixation and Early Modern Dualism

In Early Modern English during the early 17th century (circa 1610s), English writers began prefixing alienable with the native Germanic negative prefix *un- (descended from PIE *n̥- via Proto-Germanic *un-). Throughout the 16th and 17th centuries, English writers felt complete morphological freedom to attach un- to Romance loanwords ending in -able, creating pairs such as unforgivable, unjustifiable, and unavoidable. Simultaneously, classical scholars who preferred strict Latinate harmony formed inalienable using the Latin negative prefix in- (also from PIE *n̥-). For over a century, unalienable and inalienable existed in free variation, appearing alongside one another in legal treatises, parliamentary debates, and philosophical tracts.

The Enlightenment Philosophy of Natural Rights

The crucial semantic shift that transformed unalienable from a narrow property concept into a revolutionary political doctrine occurred during the European Enlightenment. Political philosophers grappling with the nature of sovereign authority sought to determine whether a people could legitimately surrender their freedom to an absolute monarch:

  • Hugo Grotius and Thomas Hobbes: Early contract theorists argued that sovereign power was derived from the people, but Hobbes maintained in Leviathan (1651) that once the social compact was made, individuals alienated their natural rights to an absolute sovereign to escape the violent state of nature.
  • John Locke's Revolution: In his Second Treatise of Government (1689), Locke fundamentally challenged Hobbes. Locke argued that human beings possess natural rights to life, liberty, and estate that originate directly from God in the state of nature. Crucially, Locke demonstrated that these rights are not commodities owned like cattle or furniture; they are moral trusts. Because a human being does not possess arbitrary power over their own life, they cannot morally or logically alienate that life or liberty to a tyrant. Thus, fundamental liberties are inherently non-transferable.

The 1776 American Watershed and Modern Legacy

When Thomas Jefferson drafted the Declaration of Independence in June 1776, he selected unalienable to express the foundational premise of American liberty: 'endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights'. Intriguingly, historical textual scholarship reveals that while Jefferson's handwritten 'Rough Draught' reads unalienable, the official broadside printed by John Dunlap on the night of July 4, 1776, also preserved unalienable, whereas subsequent reprintings, including John Adams's copy and editions published in Europe, frequently altered it to inalienable.

During the 19th century, abolitionists led by Frederick Douglass continually weaponized the Declaration's unalienable rights to dismantle the legal fiction that human beings could be reduced to transferable property. In 1848, the Seneca Falls Convention modeled its Declaration of Sentiments on Jefferson's text, asserting the unalienable rights of women. Although twentieth-century international instruments such as the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights favored the Latinate spelling inalienable, unalienable remains indelibly enshrined in the American civic soul as an immortal testament to intrinsic human sovereignty.
