• Colophon: Similar because both appear at the close of an extensive manuscript or sacred literary composition to provide concluding meta-information; differs because a Western colophon (from Greek kolophōn, 'finishing touch / summit') typically records scribal data, printer details, dates of copying, and production notes, whereas a phalaśruti is a theological declaration of karmic and spiritual rewards promised to the reader or listener.
  • Benediction: Similar because both bestow blessings and invoke divine favor at the conclusion of a sacred liturgy or sermon; differs because a benediction (Latin benedictio, 'well-speaking') is an active pastoral prayer asking God to bless the congregation, whereas a phalaśruti functions as a canonical contract articulating specific karmic fruits and guarantees inherent in the acoustic power of the text itself.
  • Doxology: Similar because both are liturgical verses positioned at the end of sacred hymns or prayers; differs because a doxology (Greek doxologia, 'praise-word') is an explicit hymn of glorification directed upward toward the Divine (such as the Gloria Patri), whereas a phalaśruti directs its attention downward toward the human recipient, detailing the fruits accrued by the practitioner.
  • Epilogue: Similar because both constitute a final structural section appended to a long narrative work; differs because an epilogue (Greek epilogos) serves a dramatic or narrative function by revealing the subsequent fates of characters after the plot concludes, whereas a phalaśruti transcends the narrative to address the audience's real-world spiritual and physical destiny.
  • Envoi: Similar because both represent a short concluding stanza or stanzaic address at the end of a long formal poem; differs because an envoi (French envoyer, 'to send') in European fixed verse (such as the ballade or sestina) typically dedicates the poem to a royal patron or summarizes its moral lesson, lacking the transformative soteriological power of a phalaśruti.
  • Apotropaic closing: Similar because both are final textual formulas designed to ward off misfortune and bring positive fortune; differs because an apotropaic charm focuses primarily on defensive magic to repel demons and the evil eye, whereas a phalaśruti offers a comprehensive positive matrix of worldly health, wealth, progeny, and ultimate liberation (mokṣa).
  • Postscript: Similar because both are appended after the main body of a written work has formally ended; differs because a postscript is an afterthought or supplementary note added to a letter or essay, whereas a phalaśruti is an essential, highly deliberate, and sacred architectural pillar of Hindu composition.
