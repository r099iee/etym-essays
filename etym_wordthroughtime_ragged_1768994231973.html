- Borrowed in the 14th century from Old Norse, where it described rough, shaggy surfaces — a coarse, hairy covering rather than torn cloth.
- Through the 15th and 16th centuries it moved onto fabric and dress, naming clothes worn at the edges into jagged points, loose threads, and open holes.
- In the 18th and 19th centuries it became a social diagnosis: "ragged" marked the poor, the unmended shirt, the bottom edge of society's order — reported flatly, as a fact of weather.
- Modern English keeps the literal torn garment but extends it figuratively — ragged breath, ragged melody, a ragged line of battle — anything uneven, broken, and left unfinished.
