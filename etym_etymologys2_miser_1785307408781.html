The chain for miser runs from Latin through French into English. The Indo-European stage is genuinely debated and is noted as such.

  • PIE *mizd- "wage, recompense" (securely reconstructed only in this "reward" sense: Sanskrit mīḍha-, Greek misthós, Gothic mizdō, English meed) or alternatively PIE *meis- "to feel distress, to be miserable" — both proposals for the ancestry of Latin miser are minority hypotheses; the link to *mizd- rests on the theory that a hireling's hired state was a wretched one. The connection is debated and not secure; an alternative view treats Latin miser as of unclear or possibly substrate origin.
  • Proto-Italic *meis-ro- "wretched, unfortunate" (reconstructed from Latin; this stage inherits the uncertainty of the root).
  • Latin miser "wretched, unhappy, unfortunate, pitiable" (attested from the earliest Latin literature; an adjective of compassion and lament, with no money sense whatever).
  • Latin derivatives: miseria "wretchedness, distress" (→ English misery), miserābilis "pitiable" (→ miserable), misericordia "pity, compassion" (→ misericord), miserērī "to pity" (→ commiserate).
  • Late Latin / Anglo-Norman / Middle French misere "wretchedness" and misēr "a wretched person" (the French nouns carry the Latin adjective's meaning into the Romance vernaculars).
  • Middle English miser "a wretched, miserable, pitiable person" (attested 15th century, borrowed from Anglo-Norman / Old French; the original compassionate sense).
  • English miser — semantic shift in the 16th–17th centuries: "a person who lives wretchedly for the sake of hoarded gold, one who hoards wealth and spends as little as possible" (the money sense fixed by about 1600, as the word's center of gravity moved from the victim of fate to the grasping hoarder).
  • Modern English MISER "a hoarder of wealth who lives in self-imposed poverty; one who begrudges every coin spent" — with the adjective miserly (16th century) and the abstract noun miserliness.

Debate / nuance note: The deep pre-history of Latin miser is unresolved. The root *mizd- is on firm ground only in its "wage, recompense" meaning, and the attempt to connect miser to it (on the theory that a hireling's hired condition was a wretched one) is a minority view; the alternative *meis-, "to feel distress", is likewise conjectural, and some scholars treat miser as of obscure, possibly non-Italic origin. What is certain is that Latin miser "wretched, pitiable" and the attested French and English forms are secure from their earliest records, and that the semantic shift from "wretched person" to "hoarder of wealth" is an English development of the 16th–17th centuries.
