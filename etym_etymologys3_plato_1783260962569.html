The name Plato descends from the ancient Greek Πλάτων (Platōn), a personal name formed on the adjective πλατύς (platys), "broad, flat, wide," the regular Greek reflex of the Proto-Indo-European root *plet-/*plat-, "to spread out, to be broad and flat." The biography of the word is therefore twofold: first the history of a root of spatial breadth, and second the astonishing career of a gymnasium nickname that came to name the fountainhead of Western metaphysics. The Indo-European root of breadth:
  • The root *plet-/*plat- "to spread, be broad" is among the most productive in the family. In Greek it produced πλατύς (platys) "broad," πλάτος (platos) "breadth," πλατεῖα (plateia) "broad street" — the word that, travelling through Italian piazza and Spanish plaza and French place, lies beneath every town square and "place" in Europe — and πλάτανος (platanos), the plane tree, so called for its broad spreading leaves, the very tree under which Plato is said to have taught.
  • In the Germanic branch the root surfaced as Proto-Germanic *flataz, "flat," giving Old English flet "floor, dwelling," Old Norse flatr "flat," and modern English flat; German flach "flat, shallow" is the same line. In Indo-Iranian, the zero-grade form *pl̥th- yielded Sanskrit prath-, "to spread, extend," with práthas "breadth, expanse," cognate through the same ancient spread of the root.
  • The phonetic development in Greek is regular: PIE *pt- clusters and the laryngeal treatment of the root's final consonant produced the distinctive Greek form with -tu-, giving platys where Latin, which lost the root early, kept only tangential forms. The word thus shows the classical Greek habit of preserving, in the "broad" adjective, a PIE root of spatial expansion that other branches developed separately.
From nickname to name:
  • According to the ancient tradition preserved in Diogenes Laërtius (Life of Plato, 3.4), the philosopher was born Aristocles, son of Ariston, of a distinguished family claiming descent from the Athenian king Codrus. At the wrestling school of Ariston of Argos he received the nickname Platon, "the Broad," because of his broad shoulders and chest — some sources say his broad forehead — and the nickname so completely displaced his birth name that posterity knows him only as "the Broad One."
  • The practice of gymnasium nicknames was ordinary in classical Athens; what is extraordinary is that this particular physical sobriquet became, in the course of the fourth century BC and after, the standard name of the most influential philosopher who ever lived. Later tradition invented secondary explanations — that he was called "broad" for the breadth of his style or the range of his thought — but the ancient biographical sources are consistent on the physical origin.
  • The form itself is a normal Greek personal-name formation: platys + the -ōn suffix of agent and nickname nouns, exactly as a wrestler with huge shoulders would be nicknamed Platon, "Broadie."
From person to doctrine to adjective:
  • The name entered Latin as Plātō and passed unchanged into every European language. From it sprang the Platonists, the Platonist school, and Platonism; the Academy he founded kept the philosophical sense alive for nine centuries, and Neoplatonism (Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus) carried the name to the very threshold of the Middle Ages.
  • The modern adjective platonic has drifted furthest from the biography. From Plato's Symposium, where the lover is led by eros from a single beautiful body toward the Form of Beauty itself, the phrase "Platonic love" came, in Renaissance and post-Renaissance usage, to mean a love that is spiritual, intellectual, and non-sexual; today "just platonic" means simply "not romantic." The philosophical "Platonic Form" (the ideal archetype) keeps the name closer to its root meaning: the eternal "broad" pattern behind things.
  • In modern mathematics and philosophy of science, "Platonism" again denotes the belief that abstract entities exist independently of the mind — a technical reuse of the ancient school-name for a modern position.
Sound, culture, and literary afterlife:
  • The word suffered no meaningful sound shift in English; it arrived as a learned Latin-Greek proper noun in the Renaissance and was fixed early in the form Plato. Its pronunciation, with the stress on the first syllable and a long open a (as in "play-toe"), reflects the standard Latin-Greek prosody rather than the Greek accentuation Platón.
  • Macaulay's reference to "the masterpieces of Sophocles, of Demosthenes, and of Plato" belongs to a long tradition — reaching back to antiquity and crystallised in the Renaissance — of naming Plato as the supreme prose summit of Greek literature, the philosopher whose dialogues were the school-books of the Roman and Byzantine worlds. The Byzantine "pedants" of the passage could still read and interpret him; the word thus carries, in that context, the full weight of a civilisation's preserved but embalmed learning.
  • Whitehead's aphorism that the European philosophical tradition "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato" is only the most famous of the name's modern metonymies; "Plato" has become, in effect, a synonym for philosophy's founding canon, the measure against which all later thought is placed.
Socio-cultural factors: The career of the word illustrates how a proper name becomes an institution. A purely physical nickname, "the Broad," attached to a wrestler-in-training, was borne through the Academy, the Roman copyists, the Neoplatonic schools, the Renaissance translations of Ficino, and the modern university into a common adjective of everyday English. The spread root behind the name — the same root as English flat and the European place "square" — is a fitting etymology for the philosopher whose theory of Forms imagined a realm of perfect, unvarying, "flat and broad" archetypes standing behind the flux of the visible world.
