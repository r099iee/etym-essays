Rafts is the plural of raft: flat buoyant platforms designed to float on water, used for crossing rivers, transporting goods, rescue at sea and recreation. The defining feature of a raft, as opposed to a boat, is that it has no hull — no hollow body — but floats on its own buoyancy: a classic raft is a platform of logs lashed or pegged together; a modern raft is often an inflatable rubber or PVC structure. In the source context (“He had not boats or rafts enough for his host”), Tolkien's usage is the ancient one: rafts of lashed timber carrying an army and its stores across the Long Lake after the dragon's destruction of Esgaroth.

The word's senses form a clear family:

  • Floating platform (core sense, late 15th century): a flat structure of timber — or of barrels, planks, reeds or inflated skins — used to float on water. Log rafts, reed rafts (as on Lake Titicaca), and improvised escape rafts all answer to the name.
  • Life raft (20th century): a buoyant, often inflatable, craft carried on ships and aircraft for emergency evacuation; the modern technical sense, standardised in maritime safety practice.
  • Recreational raft: the inflatable raft of whitewater rafting, a sport in which crews paddle rubber rafts down rapids; the verb “to raft” (travel by raft) and the gerund “rafting” are now firmly attached to this sense.
  • “A raft of” (colloquial, US, 1837): a large number or collection — “a raft of complaints”, “a raft of new laws”. The semantic bridge: a raft is a heap of logs bound together; the heap-image generalised to any large quantity. The precise origin of this sense is debated (see etymology fields), but in use it is simply “a great many”.
  • Technical extensions: in construction, a “raft foundation” is a concrete slab that floats a whole building on soft ground; in geology and oceanography, “ice rafting” is the transport of rocks and sediment by floating ice; in biology, “raft” describes floating mats of vegetation or the egg-masses of some insects. All are the same image: something flat that carries a load on water.

The historical bridge that created the English word is worth stating plainly: in Middle English, raft meant “a beam, a log, a rafter” — a single piece of timber. Because a floating platform is precisely a bundle of such timbers, the word shifted from the part (the log) to the whole (the platform of logs), and by the late 15th century the modern sense was established. The same Germanic root survives in the ordinary word rafter, the sloping beam of a roof — a reminder that a raft and a roof-beam are, etymologically, the same thing.

Grammatically, rafts is a regular plural; the singular raft also serves as a verb (“to raft timber downstream” — to transport by raft; “to raft” — to travel by raft), and the noun appears in the fixed colloquial frame “a raft of + plural noun”. In register, the platform senses are neutral and everyday (nautical, recreational, emergency), while “a raft of” is informal American usage, common in journalism and business writing but historically rarer in formal British prose. The word is a small showcase of how a plain word for a log became a word for a boat, a life-saving device, a sport and a quantity.
