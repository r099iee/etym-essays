Single linear chain from the earliest reconstructable stage to ALE: 1. Origin debated: the Germanic word is either (a) connected to a Proto-Indo-European root *h₂elut- / *alu- (also written *alut-), "bitter, intoxicating drink," related to Latin alūmen "alum, a bitter salt" — the theory that the word described the bitter or sharp taste of the drink; or (b) a non-Indo-European substrate word for an intoxicating beverage, borrowed into Germanic at an early date. Both hypotheses are presented in the handbooks; neither is certain. (The connection to alūmen is the more widely repeated account, but the lack of firm non-Germanic cognates keeps the substrate theory alive.) 2. Proto-Germanic *aluþ- (stem *alu-, oblique *aluþ-), "ale, beer, intoxicating drink," attested indirectly: the word was borrowed into early Finnic as *olut (Finnish olut, Estonian õlu, Votic õluto, "beer"), which dates it to the period of early Germanic–Finnic contact, well before the attested Germanic languages; the short form *alu appears as a runic word on early Germanic bracteates and amulets (c. 5th–6th c. CE), sometimes interpreted as "ale, blessing." 3. Old English ealu (also ealo, ealuþ-), neuter u-stem, "ale, the fermented malt drink" (attested c. 8th–11th c.; the genitive ealoþ is preserved in compounds like ealoþbenc, "ale-bench"), the everyday drink of the Anglo-Saxon hall, celebrated in Beowulf. 4. Middle English ale, "ale, malt liquor" (12th–15th c.), the form surviving into the modern period; the word also named the community feast (church-ale, bride-ale > bridal). 5. Modern English ALE (16th c. onward), first the plain word for malt liquor, then (with the rise of lager in the 19th c.) the technical name of the top-fermented family of beers. Note on the debated stage: only the deepest origin is genuinely uncertain — the *h₂elut- "bitter" connection is traditional and widely taught (it links the word to alūmen "alum") but rests on a single assumed semantic bridge ("bitter drink") and lacks solid cognates outside Germanic, so a substrate origin remains possible. The Germanic, Old English, Middle English, and modern stages are fully attested and uncontested.
