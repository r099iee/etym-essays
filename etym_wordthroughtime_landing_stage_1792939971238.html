- Old use — landings without stages: for centuries boats nose into banks and passengers wade; the landing is a place, not a structure.
- Early 19th c. — the compound appears: steam-packets and ferry companies build dedicated landing-stages, named in timetables and Acts.
- Victorian rivers — wood everywhere: every reach gets its broad wooden stage for excursion steamers, market barges, and ferrymen.
- Late 19th c. — iron and pontoon: tidal cities moor floating stages on hulks, with hinged brows to the shore.
- 20th c. — concrete quays: many timber stages rot unreplaced as roads steal river traffic, leaving the word to novels and preserved quays.
- Today — heritage and leisure: surviving landing-stages serve pleasure boats, rowing clubs, and riverside walks, cherished precisely for their woodenness.
