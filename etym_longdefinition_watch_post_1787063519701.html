Watch-post (also written watch post or watchpost) is a compound noun formed from watch ("the act of keeping vigil or guard") and post ("a place of duty, a station"). It denotes a specific position or station at which a person — a sentinel, sentry, guard, scout, or lookout — is stationed to observe, guard, or give warning, especially in military, naval, or frontier settings. In the present card, Tolkien's "old watch-post" is an elevated station on the mountain where watchers could observe the terrain, which the dwarves dare not climb because it is too exposed to view.

Core meaning: a military or observation station.

  • Sentinel station: a fixed point where a guard performs sentry duty, often at a camp edge, gate, bridge, hilltop, or pass, from which he can survey approaching persons or dangers.
  • Observation point: a position chosen for maximum visibility of enemy movement or of the surrounding country — functionally identical to an "observation post."
  • Lookout station: in seafaring and coastal contexts, a height or tower from which ships, reefs, or incoming vessels are watched for.
  • Signal station: in older military and naval usage, a post from which signals of approach or danger were passed to the main body.

Extended and figurative senses.

  • Any fixed place of observation: in civilian use, a high point (a tower, a cliff, a rooftop) from which one keeps watch over a landscape, an event, or a crowd.
  • Position of responsibility (figurative): by extension, a role or job that requires vigilance, e.g., "his watch-post at the ministry was to monitor the negotiations." This is a transparent metaphorical extension of the military sense.
  • Historical and colonial usage: in 19th-century military and travel writing, "watch-post" often refers to a small fortified lookout or signal station along a frontier, coastline, or pass.

Related terminology and semantic boundaries. The word overlaps with several near-synonyms but is distinct from each:

  • Watchtower: the physical structure, whereas watch-post emphasizes the position or function rather than the building.
  • Sentry post / guard post: more strictly military and concerned with guarding access, whereas a watch-post emphasizes watching and observing.
  • Outpost: a station pushed forward into hostile or remote territory, carrying a sense of isolation, whereas a watch-post need not be remote.
  • Observation post (OP): modern military jargon for a position from which artillery fire is directed; almost identical in meaning but a different, more technical collocation.
  • Watch-house / guardhouse: the building where the guard is quartered, not the station itself.

Morphological and etymological character. As a compound, watch-post is a "hybrid" word: the element watch is native Germanic, descended from Old English, while post was borrowed from French (ultimately Latin), entering English in the 16th century. The compound itself is recorded from the 19th century in military and travel writing. Its hyphenated spelling marks it as a transparent, still-organic compound rather than a fully fused lexical unit, and in modern usage the unhyphenated watch post and closed watchpost are also found. English has a long and productive habit of joining a native word with a borrowed one in exactly this way (compare lookout, landmark, watchdog beside the more Latinate observation post).

Register and nuance. The word is chiefly literary, historical, or military today; in modern everyday speech one more often says "observation post," "lookout point," or simply "guard post." In the archaic, slightly romantic register of Tolkien's prose it fits naturally alongside other old military vocabulary, and its two transparent parts make its meaning instantly clear even to a reader meeting it for the first time. In fantasy and historical fiction generally, "watch-post" is a favored word for the lonely, windswept place where someone keeps vigil — at once practical and evocative of watchfulness, exposure, and duty.
