The history of whack is the history of a sound becoming a word, then multiplying into idioms. It belongs to the large expressive family of English impact words — thwack, smack, crack, thump, wham, bash — whose forms are shaped less by regular sound laws than by the desire to sound like what they describe. For that reason historical dictionaries treat it with caution: there is no PIE root to reconstruct, only an imitative impulse and a documented variant relationship with thwack.

Middle English already possessed thwakken / thwacken, attested around the 14th century meaning to strike or pat with something flat. It survived into Early Modern English as thwack, both noun and verb, familiar from schoolroom beatings, cudgels, and Puritan prose: a thwack was a heavy, flat, punishing blow. Phonologically thwack begins with a consonant cluster that is unstable in rapid or dialectal speech, and English has several examples of th- alternating with w- or losing its frication in expressive words. Sometime in the late 17th to early 18th century a de-aspirated or simplified variant whack emerges in colloquial use. The first clear lexicographic record is a noun in 1722 meaning a smart, sharp, resounding blow, followed by a verb in 1739 meaning to strike such a blow. The dates matter: this is Augustan London, Grub Street, prize-fighting, cudgelling, and a vigorous slang press that loved short, punchy monosyllables.

  • Blow to verb: Once the noun was established, conversion to a verb was automatic in English. To whack was to deliver a whack — with a stick, hand, bat, or any implement. 18th- and 19th-century sporting and school texts use it freely: whack a ball, whack a boy's knuckles, whack a donkey onward.
  • Share, portion, and attempt: From the idea of a blow dealt in turn came 18th-century senses of a share or portion — one's whack — and the turn to act. American English in the early 19th century generalized this into take a whack at it / have a whack at it meaning to try. The semantic bridge is transparent: to have a go is to have a hit, as in cricket or baseball. P. G. Wodehouse, Mark Twain and later American journalism spread this democratic, can-do sense.
  • Out of whack: By the late 19th century, especially in American mechanics' slang, out of whack meant out of order, misaligned, not working properly. The image is of a mechanism that has been knocked askew or that no longer delivers its proper snap. It becomes one of the most durable idioms, still dominant today.
  • Underworld to kill: In 20th-century American organized-crime slang, to whack someone came to mean to murder, to hit in the Mafia sense. First widely attested mid-20th century and popularized by films and The Sopranos, it is a euphemistic extension of to hit: a hit, a hitman, to whack. The violence is lexicalized as mere impact.
  • Wack / whack as bad: Late 20th-century hip-hop slang produced wack meaning bad, lame, worthless, usually spelled wack but pronounced identically and often confused with whack. Some lexicographers derive wack separately from wacky, others from whack in the sense of out of whack. The overlap reinforced the idea of whack as off, wrong, broken.

Phonetically nothing irregular happened after 1722: initial /hw/ simplified to /w/ in most modern accents, vowel /æ/ stayed stable, final /k/ gives the abrupt stop that makes the word feel like impact. The spelling with wh- rather than w- preserves its link to what, when, whack and visually distinguishes it from wacky in British usage, though American slang often merges them.

Socio-culturally whack travelled from low slang to respectability and back to slang. Johnson did not dignify it; 19th-century dictionaries labelled it colloquial or vulgar. Cricket and baseball made whack the ball respectable. Wodehouse — the source of "I had another whack at it" — uses it as breezy upper-class slang for an attempt, stripping it of violence and keeping only cheerful persistence. American crime reporting then re-violentized it. Today a speaker can "have a whack at" a crossword, complain the printer is "out of whack," and watch a film where a mobster is "whacked," all without sensing contradiction. That polysemy is held together by a single embodied schema: a short, sharp application of force, literal or metaphorical.

Literary attestations track this arc: 18th-century cant dictionaries for the blow; 19th-century sporting papers for whacking balls and horses; early 20th-century Wodehouse, Wallace and American humorists for the try; mid-20th-century police and newspaper crime pages for the murder; late 20th-century mechanics and computing for out-of-whack systems. Each new domain did not replace the old but layered on top, leaving modern whack as a compact case study in how an onomatopoeic monosyllable can, within 300 years, acquire senses spanning play, work, crime and evaluation.
