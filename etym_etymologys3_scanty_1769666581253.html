Origins: The Cutting Root

The story of scanty begins in the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *(s)kem-, whose core meaning was "to cut, shear, pare, or shorten." (Some scholars reconstruct the more intensive form *(s)kembh-, "to cut, mangle," and there remains honest scholarly debate over which shape is correct.) What matters for the history of the word is the persistent image at the root: an object or measure that has been clipped, trimmed, or pared down. From this single idea of "cutting short," the entire modern family — scant, scanty, scantily, scanties — grows by one consistent thread of meaning.

Proto-Germanic: *skamma- "Short"

  • The cutting root passed into Proto-Germanic as the adjective *skamma- (masculine *skammaz), meaning "short, cut short, clipped."
  • The Germanic form is reconstructed from solid descendants: Old Norse skammr "short," Old High German scamm, and related Germanic adjectives.
  • Notably, the root's Slavic relatives developed a parallel but distinct sense of miserliness and scarcity — Old Church Slavonic skǫpŭ "stingy, meager" and modern Russian skupoj "stingy" — showing how the Indo-European "cut short" image could drift toward both shortness of measure and stinginess of character.

Old Norse: skammr and skamt

  • In Old Norse the adjective skammr meant "short, brief," applied to length of body, time, and extent.
  • Its neuter form skamt functioned adverbially ("shortly") and as a noun meaning "a short portion, a small amount."
  • Viking raiders and settlers carried this vocabulary across the North Sea; the Danelaw (9th–11th centuries) planted Norse words deep in the northern and eastern dialects of England.

The Borrowing: Old Norse skamt → Middle English scant

  • From Norse skamt came Middle English scant, attested from the 13th–14th centuries in the sense "short, deficient, falling short of full measure."
  • English already possessed the native adjective short (Old English sceort); the borrowed scant settled into a specialized niche — not mere shortness of length but a measure that falls short of what is required, a portion trimmed to insufficiency.
  • The initial sk- cluster of the Norse loan was retained (unlike native words, which had palatalized sk- to sh- in Old English), giving English the distinctive scant/scanty pronunciation with /sk/.

The -y Formation: scant → scanty

  • Around 1650, English speakers formed the descriptive adjective scanty by adding the adjectival suffix -y (from Old English -ig) to scant.
  • The new adjective could stand attributively (a scanty meal) and predictively (the rations were scanty), making it more flexible than the base.
  • Early usage centered on food, provisions, income, and allowance — the vocabulary of want and hard times.

Semantic Drift: From Provisions to Clothing

  • Through the 18th and 19th centuries, scanty extended from supplies to clothing: scanty dress, scanty attire, and scanty covering described garments that covered little of the body.
  • In Victorian prose the clothing sense carried moral shading — "scanty attire" hinted at immodesty or poverty, echoing the word's ancient link to insufficiency and want.
  • By the 20th century the noun plural scanties (brief undergarments) and the adverb scantily ("scantily clad") had crystallized the skimpy-clothing sense into everyday vocabulary.

Modern Usage and Register

  • Today scanty remains a formal-leaning descriptor of insufficiency in quantities (rations, evidence, details, means) and of revealing clothing.
  • It has a faintly literary flavor, preferred over the blunter skimpy in careful prose.
  • In the source sentence from C.S. Lewis's Prince Caspian, "their scanty supper" evokes the gloom of a defeated company with barely enough to eat — a textbook use of the word's central sense.

Summary

scanty descends from an Indo-European image of cutting short, passes through a Germanic adjective for "clipped," a Norse word for "short," and a Danelaw borrowing meaning "falling short of full measure," before -y converts it in 1650 into the modern adjective for insufficient quantities and skimpy covering.
