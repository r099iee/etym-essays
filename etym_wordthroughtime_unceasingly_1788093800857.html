- (late 16th c.) The adjective “unceasing” appears - first in theological contexts: unceasing prayer, unceasing torments - a solemn, even terrifying word of the pulpit.
- (17th c.) “Unceasingly” emerges as its adverb - describing God's mercies and man's duties; still elevated, still grave.
- (18th c.) The adverb descends into plain description: rain, pain, work, and noise continue “unceasingly” - neutral, reportorial, stripped of awe.
- (19th c.) Romantic and Victorian prose rekindle it as an intensity word - unceasingly anxious, unceasingly kind, unceasing labour - continuity felt as emotional pressure, not just clock time.
- (Modern) A formal, faintly literary adverb of continuity: in journalism it competes with continuously, constantly, relentlessly, while still carrying a thread of the old gravity of “cease” - the word has not stopped being about stopping.
