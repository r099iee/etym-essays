despaired

1. Primary sense (the verb). To despair is to lose all hope, to reach the settled conviction that the desired outcome will never come; despaired is its simple past tense and past participle. The verb is most often used intransitively — "I despaired" — or with the preposition of: "to despair of something or someone" means to give up hope in them or it ("He despaired of ever finding a job"). It can also take a clause, "I despaired that I would ever succeed." The past participle form appears in the passive construction "all hope despaired of" — a formal, literary use in which the thing hoped for is left without hope.

2. The noun and its family. Beside the verb stands the noun despair, "the complete loss or absence of hope," and the adjectives desperate and despairing. Desperation is despair converted into reckless energy; desperate (from Latin dēspērātus "hopeless") has wandered to mean both "driven to extremes by hopelessness" and, more loosely, "urgently intense" ("a desperate need"). Despairing, the participial adjective, describes the person or mood in the grip of despair. The family shares one image: hope is the vital force of the soul, and despair is its deletion, not merely its dimming.

3. The etymology as a semantic key. The word is built from the Latin prefix dē- ("down from, away, without," a privative — the same prefix in deactivate, demobilise, deconstruct) and spērāre "to hope," so dēspērāre means literally "to un-hope," "to be without hope." Despair is thus not a low or weak hope but the absence of hope itself: the heart's lights going out one by one until nothing is left, as the mnemonic for this card puts it. This is the historical semantic bridge that makes the word so much stronger than "discouragement" or "disappointment."

4. Theological and literary senses. In Christian tradition despair has an ancient spiritual weight: it is the abandonment of hope in God's mercy, folded into the medieval catalogue of the deadly sins as acedia or spiritual sloth, and it was said to be the one sin that cannot be repented because it refuses the very channel of repentance. Writers exploited the gravity — Spenser's Despair tempts the Redcrosse Knight to suicide in The Faerie Queene (1590), Milton gives the allegory of Despair a place in Paradise Lost, and Shakespeare builds tragic scenes on the word's music. In the nineteenth century the word carried into philosophy: Kierkegaard called despair "the sickness unto death," the self-refusal of the self, and existential writers after him kept the word for the felt meaninglessness of existence.

5. Modern and technical nuances. In psychology and medicine, clinical language tends to separate "hopelessness" (the state, an objective condition of expectation) from "despair" (the felt, agonised experience of it), and research on "learned helplessness" describes the behavioural shutting-down that despair produces. In everyday speech the word remains the strongest member of its set: to say "I despaired" is to say hope was, at that moment, entirely gone — which is precisely why the recovery that follows in the passage feels redemptive.

6. In this context. The speaker — Doctor Cornelius, a half-Dwarf living in hiding — says "I have often despaired; but something always happens to start me hoping again." The verb is used bare and intransitive, and it names the troughs in a lifelong emotional rhythm: the seasons of total hopelessness that followed his fruitless searches through the mountains and woods for traces of the Old Narnians. The sentence is structured as a wave — "I have often despaired" sinks, "but something always happens to start me hoping again" lifts — and the weight of the word is precisely what makes the recovery meaningful. The past form despaired adds its own small etymology: the Germanic dental preterite -ed is itself descended from a PIE root meaning "to do," so that "I despaired" is, at bottom, "I did despair."

  • Verb: despair / despaired (of) — to lose all hope in or of something.
  • Noun: despair — the complete loss or absence of hope.
  • Adjectives: despairing (in the grip of despair), desperate (hopeless; recklessly driven), despaired-of (formal: abandoned as hopeless).
  • Related: desperation, hopelessness, despondency, dejection, acedia.
