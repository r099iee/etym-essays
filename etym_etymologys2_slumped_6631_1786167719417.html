DERIVATION CHAIN — SLUMPED
R → Proto-Germanic *slump- / sound-symbolic *sl- cluster of 'sliding, slipping, sinking' — no securely reconstructable PIE root exists; the word belongs to the expressive Germanic family of sl- words for smooth, slippery, sliding motion (English 'slip', 'slide', 'sleek', 'slush', 'sloppy'), which may descend from a PIE *slei- / *sleibh- 'slippery, slimy' background but are treated by most etymologists as partly imitative.
E → Scandinavian dialect *slumpa (Norwegian slumpa) 'to fall heavily, to slip, to happen by chance' — and Low German slumpen 'to fall, to happen' — the immediate Germanic sources cited for the English verb.
D → English slump (1670s) 'to fall or sink suddenly into a slushy or soft mass; to slip' — borrowed from the Scandinavian/Low German stock into dialectal English.
C → English slump (18th–19th c.) 'to fall or sink suddenly and heavily, losing posture and tension' — extended from bogs and soft ground to bodies and objects.
B → English slump (1888) 'a sudden heavy fall in prices' — the economic noun, and thence the whole family of figurative slumps (performance, morale, ratings).
A → English SLUMPED (past participle) 'fallen or sunk suddenly and heavily' — the physical and figurative sense of the card.
Debate note: The etymology is genuinely uncertain beyond the Scandinavian/Germanic borrowing stage. The earliest recorded English sense ('to sink in mud') suggests a sound-symbolic origin — the word's shape mimics a soft mass meeting wet ground — and most authorities (OED, Etymonline) describe it as imitative or of obscure origin, possibly related to 'slip' / 'slide'. No PIE root can be confidently reconstructed; the *slei- connection is conjectural, and the exact route from Scandinavian into English is undocumented. The later senses (economic slump, 1888) are securely dated but figurative extensions of the physical one.
