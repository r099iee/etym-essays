- Old English: slēan means "to strike, beat, smite" — the verb is about blows in general, and only context makes it lethal; heroic poetry uses it of warriors and swords, and the derived glosses render both "to strike" and "to kill"
- Middle English: slēn is the ordinary everyday word for killing; the participle slayn/slaine; "the slain" begins its long life as the collective name for battle dead
- 1611: the King James Bible gives the word its defining register — "Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him" (Genesis 4:8), and "a Lamb as it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6); readers meet slay, slew, slain on page after page of scripture
- 17th–19th c.: epic poetry, sermons and battle dispatches keep "the slain" as the dignified collective for the fallen; "slain and wounded" standardizes in military reporting
- early 20th c.: American slang "you slay me" — to overwhelm with laughter — lets the epic verb decay into camp
- mid-20th c.: "slain in the Spirit" enters charismatic Christianity for ecstatic prostration
- 1980s–2010s: ballroom, drag and Black queer cultures refurbish "slay" as the verb of ultimate performance — "slay!" becomes global pop slang; "slain" itself stays scriptural and epic
- Today: "slain" is journalism's grave word for officers and victims, scripture's word for Abel, and fantasy's word for dragons — never ordinary, always final
