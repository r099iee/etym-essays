Mid-17th c. — Scientific and philosophical genesis: emerged in the writings of English natural philosophers (e.g., Henry More, Robert Boyle) to describe subtle fluids, aether, or divine spirit passing through and occupying all physical matter.
- Late 17th–18th c. — Atmospheric and sensory expansion: adopted by poets (such as John Dryden, Alexander Pope, and James Thomson) to evoke all-encompassing natural phenomena—ambient light, gentle fragrances, and evening air diffusing through landscapes.
- 19th c. — Romantic and psychological interiority: harnessed by Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Keats) and Victorian novelists (George Eliot, the Brontës) to describe ubiquitous emotional climates, ambient gloom, unspoken moral corruption, or transcendent spiritual presence.
- Late 19th to early 20th c. — Sociological and aesthetic criticism: established as an indispensable critical term for identifying overarching thematic unity, subtle ideological currents, or atmospheric tone in literary masterpieces and art history.
- 21st c. — Digital and cultural ubiquity: actively used to describe the ambient, non-localized nature of modern phenomena—such as algorithmic surveillance, pervasive background anxiety, or the pervasive corporate aesthetic across digital platforms.
