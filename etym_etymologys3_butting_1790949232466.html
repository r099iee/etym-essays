The verb butt and its participle butting reflect a classic historical trajectory in the English language: a term born in ancient Germanic physical conflict, filtered through Gallo-Romance feudal vocabulary following the Norman Conquest, expanded into medieval artisan crafts, and ultimately revolutionized by late 19th-century colloquial slang.

1. Germanic Roots and the Gallo-Romance Filter:

The deepest etymological layer of butt rests in the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *bʰew- or *bʰaw-, meaning "to strike, hit, or knock." In the Proto-Germanic language family, this developed into the strong verb *bautaną ("to beat, pound, strike"), which also yielded Old English bēatan (modern English beat) and Old Norse bauta.

When Germanic tribes, particularly the Salian Franks, settled in Roman Gaul, their Germanic dialect contributed the verb *bōtan ("to strike, thrust") to the evolving Gallo-Romance speech. In Old French and Anglo-Norman, this became the common verb boter or bouter, meaning "to strike, thrust, push forward, or strike with the horns." This Romance verb also gave English other related loanwords, including rebut (to push back), abut (to touch boundaries), and button (originally a small round bud that pushes out).

2. Middle English Borrowing and Animal Husbandry:

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman vocabulary permeated the everyday vernacular of England. By the early 13th century, Middle English texts attested butten with the primary physical sense of an animal (such as a ram, goat, or stag) lowering its forehead and violently striking or thrusting with its horns or skull. Early agricultural treatises and fables used the term to describe territorial animal combat.

Simultaneously, the word developed nominal applications. The thicker, blunt end of an object—such as the thick base of a tree trunk, the stock of a firearm, or the rear handle of a tool—became known as the butt (the part that strikes or pushes against something).

3. Craft Trades and Architectural 'Abutment':

During the 14th and 15th centuries, medieval English carpenters, masons, and shipwrights adopted butt into technical trade jargon. When two square-cut wooden beams or stone blocks were brought together end-to-end without an overlapping joint, they were said to butt against one another, creating a butt joint. Land surveyors utilized the term to describe properties that touched boundaries (butts and bounds), reinforcing the core sense of direct, flush physical contact.

4. Slang Revolution: The Birth of 'Butting In' and 'Butting Heads':

The most dramatic modern evolution of the word occurred in late 19th-century American English. Urban slang transformed the vivid physical imagery of a charging goat into human social interaction. Around 1885–1890, American newspapers and colloquial speech popularized the phrasal verb to butt in, depicting an annoying, presumptuous busybody thrusting their head uninvited through a conversation, doorway, or ticket queue.

By the early 20th century, British writers like P.G. Wodehouse readily integrated this colorful Americanism into dialogue, having exasperated characters plead: "For heaven's sake, don't you start butting in!" Soon after, the phrase butting heads entered international English as a universal idiom for stubborn, intractable interpersonal deadlock, completing the word's journey from livestock pasture to boardroom psychology.
