Don as a verb means to put on a garment, accessory, uniform, or other wearable item, typically with a sense of deliberate or formal dressing rather than casual wearing. It describes the action of clothing oneself, as in to don a coat, to don a helmet, to don gloves, and by extension to assume a role or appearance, as in to don a disguise.

In everyday modern use, don is more formal and literary than put on. While put on is neutral and universal, don suggests ceremony, duty, preparation, or effort. Safety manuals instruct workers to don protective equipment, astronauts don spacesuits, surgeons don gowns and masks, and judges don robes. The verb focuses on the moment of dressing, not the state of wearing: you don a jacket, then you wear it.

Don also functions as a noun with wholly separate meanings, which must be distinguished. As a noun with capital D, a Don can mean a Spanish gentleman or nobleman, a university teacher at Oxford or Cambridge, or a Mafia boss. These noun senses come from Latin dominus master, not from the verb, but they share spelling and pronunciation.

  • Core verbal sense — to put on clothing: to dress oneself in a specific item. Example: to don a raincoat. This is the dominant modern sense in both fiction and technical prose, implying intentional action. The semantic bridge from general do on to specialised dress is frequency: putting clothes on was the most common thing done on oneself.
  • Extended sense — to assume or adopt: to don a disguise, to don an air of confidence, to don a new identity. Here clothing becomes metaphor for behaviour. The reasoning is ancient — roles are garments — and don preserves it compactly.
  • Ceremonial and professional sense: to don vestments, robes, uniform, insignia, PPE. Jargon in military, medical, legal, and academic contexts. The nuance is correctness and ritual; don implies proper procedure, not just covering the body.
  • Noun senses — Spanish gentleman, academic, crime boss: Don Pedro, an Oxford don, a Mafia don. These mean a man of authority or respect. They developed from Latin dominus through Spanish don as a title of respect, then transferred to Oxford fellows and to Italian-American crime leaders viewed ironically as patrons. Unrelated etymologically to the verb but identical in form today.
  • Archaic and dialectal shades: to do on a fire, to do on a pot — to put on for cooking — survive in Scots and northern dialects, reminding us that don once covered any putting-on, not only clothes.

Register is key: don is formal, slightly bookish, and often humorous when applied to ordinary dressing, as in commenced to don trousers. It collocates with uniform, robe, gown, habit, armour, mask, helmet, gloves, and with abstract nouns like disguise, mantle, persona. It is transitive and regular: don, donned, donning. Its opposite is doff, to take off, now even rarer and more archaic. In short, don compresses intention, propriety, and transformation into three letters: to don is never merely to cover skin, but to enter a prepared state.
