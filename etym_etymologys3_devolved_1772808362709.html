The verb DEVOLVED carries its history visibly in its shape: it is de- + volved, and the volv- part is the Latin verb volvere, "to roll, turn, revolve." The story of the word is the story of how a physical image of rolling down a hillside became the technical language of constitutional transfer — and then, in the modern world, the everyday political word for handing power from central governments to regions and nations. The deepest layer is Proto-Indo-European *wel-, a root of turning and rolling that proved one of the most productive in the whole family. From it come Latin volvere (rolling, turning), Greek eiléō (to wind), Sanskrit vṛ-/val- (to turn, revolve), and Germanic forms behind English "wallow" and the loan-word "waltz." Latin volvere generated a remarkable family of abstract words in English through prefixed compounds: revolvere "to roll back" gave revolution (originally a revolving, a turning-round — and only secondarily the political overturn that Gibbon's century celebrated); evolvere "to roll out" gave evolve and evolution (the unrolling of development); involvere "to roll in" gave involve; and convolvere "to roll together" gave convoluted. The whole cluster shares the basic picture of something rolled, wound, or turned. The specific ancestor of our word is the compound dēvolvere: dē- "down, away from" + volvere "to roll." In Classical Latin its literal senses were "to roll down, to roll away, to fall down" — a stone rolling down a slope, a body tumbling, a river flowing down. But Latin law and political discourse quickly found a metaphor in the image: when a person died without direct heirs, or when a right or duty was passed on, the object was said to roll down onto the next holder. This is the legal sense of "falling to by succession," and it is exactly the bridge from physics to government. By the medieval period, legal Latin had a fully developed idiom: powers and obligations devolve (roll down) on heirs, successors, or a higher body when the prior holder vacates them. The word entered English in the 14th–15th century through Middle French, where dévoluer / devolver carried the legal sense "to transfer, to pass on." English devolve was used of property, rights, and authority passing by succession, and of duties being transferred. The past participle devolved, which is the form on this card, is the natural narrative participle: "power devolved on," "the rights devolved to the heir." Gibbon, writing in the eighteenth century, uses it with full legal-historical force: the Roman Senate "appeared to possess the sovereign authority, and devolved on the emperors all the executive powers of government." The word enables him to describe the transfer as orderly and quasi-constitutional — power rolling down from the Senate onto the emperors — while the physical metaphor quietly registers the loss of height and dignity involved. The Senate does not storm or seize or conquer; it rolls the executive powers downhill. The modern political history of the word is equally rich. In the late twentieth century, especially in the United Kingdom and Spain, "devolution" became the standard term for transferring central-government powers to regional or national assemblies — the Scottish Parliament, the Welsh Senedd, the Northern Ireland Assembly — and to the autonomous communities of Spain (and earlier, the regional governments of Italy and France). "Devolved powers," "devolved administrations," and "devolved government" are now fixed terms of constitutional English, used daily in news and legislation. The word's rolling-down image maps perfectly onto the political reality: powers flow downward from Westminster or Madrid to Edinburgh, Cardiff, Belfast, or the regions, reversing (in part) the very process Gibbon describes, in which power rolled from the Roman Senate down onto the emperors. A further semantic extension deserves note: "devolve into" meaning "to degenerate into." When journalists write that a protest "devolved into violence," or a debate "devolved into chaos," they use the rolling-down image for a worsening — things rolling downhill out of control. This sense is a late, figurative offshoot of the same Latin compound, and it shows how the word's gravitational picture remains alive even at its most abstract. One might say the word's history is itself a form of devolution: from the rolling of stones to the rolling of powers to the rolling of arguments into disorder, with the participle devolved marking each completed transfer. In Gibbon's sentence the word sits precisely at the hinge between the physical and the political: the executive powers of the Roman state roll down from the sovereign Senate onto the emperors, and with that downhill movement the constitutional balance of the Republic quietly disappears.
