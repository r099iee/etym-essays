WODEN — single chain. PIE *weh₂t- 'to blow, to inspire, to excite' (reconstructed; the root of Latin vātēs 'seer, prophet', attested from classical Latin, and Gaulish *uātis 'seer') -> Proto-Germanic *wōdaz 'mad, furious, possessed, inspired' (reconstructed) -> Proto-Germanic *Wōdanaz 'god of frenzy and inspiration, the frenzied one' (reconstructed; cf. the independent Norse god-name Óðr) -> Gothic wōþs 'mad, possessed' (4th c., Wulfila's Bible) / Old Norse óðr 'furious, raging' and Óðinn (9th-13th c. Eddaic usage) / Old High German wuot 'madness, rage' and Wuotan (8th c. glosses) / Old English wōd 'mad' and Wōden 'the god' (8th-11th c., Anglo-Saxon sources, including royal genealogies) -> Old English Wōdnesdæg 'Wednesday, Woden's day' (attested, 9th-11th c.) -> Middle English Wednesdai -> Modern English Wednesday; the god-name itself survives as English Woden (as in Macaulay, 19th c.). NOTE ON THE CHAIN: The exact form of the PIE laryngeal (hence *weh₂t- vs. *wāt-) is debated among Indo-Europeanists, but the root's identity is accepted. The god-name *Wōdanaz is a well-attested formation on the adjective *wōdaz 'mad, frenzied, inspired', and the Old English reflex Wōden is secure.
