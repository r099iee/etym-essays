PADDLED — the practised, noiseless propulsion of a small boat At its plainest, PADDLED is the past tense and past participle of the verb paddle: to propel a boat through the water by means of a paddle, a short, broad-bladed oar grasped in both hands and worked in free, dipping strokes, the blade lifted clear at the end of each stroke and slipped back into the water without any fixed pivot. Where a rower sits facing backward and pulls on long oars locked in oarlocks, a paddler faces the direction of travel and strokes freely, and it is precisely this freedom, smallness, and quiet that the word carries into every one of its senses. SENSE 1 — the boat verb (the core sense). Transitive and intransitive: to paddle a canoe, to paddle across the lake, he paddled it quite quietly. The action is small-scale and primitive: short strokes, a blade slipped in and drawn through without fuss. Unlike rowing, which slaps and creaks and requires benches and oarlocks, paddling is inherently a quiet, low, practised motion — the verb of dugout canoes, kayaks, coracles, and of Gollum's little boat on the dark subterranean lake. SENSE 2 — the implement (noun). A paddle is the tool itself: a short shaft with a broad flat blade at one or both ends, used to propel a small craft. From the same noun radiate a family of flat, blade-like boards: the flat mixing blades of kitchen machinery (the paddle attachment of a stand mixer), the flat bats used in table tennis (the North American paddle for ping-pong), and the fixed vanes or boards of a water-wheel or steamship wheel — all sharing the image of a flat blade pushed through a resisting medium. SENSE 3 — wading (chiefly British). To paddle is also to walk barefoot in shallow water, dipping the feet in short, repeated strokes: children paddle in the sea and splash in a paddling pool. The semantic bridge is the repeated, dipping, stroking movement shared with propelling a boat — the feet play the part of the blade, churning the water in the same steady, small-scale rhythm. SENSE 4 — swimming. A dog paddle, or doggy paddle, is a simple swimming stroke made with short, alternate, paw-like strokes of hands and feet; to dog-paddle is to swim this way. The same image of short, repeated strokes governs it, and it is the sense most visible in waterfowl: ducks paddle across a pond by stroking their webbed feet, exactly as a human paddler strokes the water with a blade. SENSE 5 — punishment (chiefly US). To paddle someone is to beat or spank them with a flat board or similar implement; a paddling is the act, and a paddle is the flat disciplinary board itself. The historical bridge is purely material: the flat, blade-like board used to propel a boat is the same flat board used to discipline, so the implement lent its name to the punishment, much as birch and cane name both the twig and the beating it delivers. SENSE 6 — figurative and idiomatic extensions. To paddle one's own canoe (US, 1840s) means to be self-reliant, to manage one's own affairs without help: the canoeist does his own work, strokes for himself, and owes no man a passage. To paddle through describes laborious effort, working steadily against resistance. A related modern metaphor appears in stand-up paddleboarding and, more loosely, in business talk of "paddling hard beneath the surface" — visible calm produced by hidden, constant effort. Every figurative use keeps the core idea of steady, self-generated, unassisted forward motion. SENSE 7 — technical compounds and the steam age. Paddle-wheel (1688), paddle-boat (1787), and paddle-steamer (1843) name vessels driven by flat boards mounted on a rotating wheel at the side or stern, which paddle the water as the wheel turns. The 19th-century compound paddle your own canoe became the American motto of self-reliance. In the 20th and 21st centuries the noun has grown again: paddle-board, paddleboarding, and stand-up paddleboarding (SUP) extend it to a large floatable board propelled with a long single-bladed paddle. POLYSEMY SUMMARY. The entire family radiates from one physical image: a flat, broad blade moved in short, repeated, stroking strokes through water or its substitutes. A boat is moved, a child's feet dip, a duck's feet churn, a swimmer paws the water, a mixer beats, a water-wheel thrashes, and a disciplinary board strikes — but the motion is always the same small, steady, dipping stroke, and the word never entirely loses its undertone of quiet, practised, primitive effort. IN THE HOBBIT. In the passage, Tolkien writes of Gollum: "He had a little boat, and he paddled it quite quietly about the lake... never a ripple did he make." The verb is chosen with exact care. It is not rowed: rowing implies oarlocks, benches, and a bigger craft, and work that slaps and creaks. Paddled gives short, free, dipping strokes, the whole operation small-scale, primitive, and — above all — noiseless. The nuance is one of eerie, practised silence: Gollum, a "small slimy creature" of the dark water, moves his little boat with a boatman's instinctive quiet, and the understated verb lets Tolkien make sinister what in a holiday scene would be innocent. The tone is hushed, watchful, and faintly grotesque — a lone, bent creature gliding across black water in a silence so perfect that even his dangling feet raise no ripple. NEAR-SYNONYM BOUNDARIES (the boat-motion field):
  • Rowed: a bigger craft, oarlocks, benches, back-to-front posture, whole-body pulling strokes that slap and creak — paddling is free, small, quiet, forward-facing.
  • Sculled: a single oar worked over the stern in a figure-of-eight trick of balance — paddling uses a broad blade in free strokes.
  • Punted: a long pole thrust against the river bottom, used in shallow, slow water — paddling has no ground contact.
  • Sailed: wind and rigging, no human muscular stroke at all — paddling is pure muscle power.
  • Drifted: no propulsion, simply carried by current or wind — paddling deliberately drives the craft.
  • Waded: walking barefoot in shallow water, no boat — the verb's childlike twin sense, sharing only the dipping motion.
  • Dog-paddled: swimming by pawing the water with hands and feet — propulsion of the body through the water, not of a craft.
