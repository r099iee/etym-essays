Meadow is one of the oldest English words for managed grassland, and its etymology is the key to the landscape it describes: the meadow is the mown place. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *meh1- "to mow, to cut" is attested across the family in the vocabulary of harvesting. Latin metere means "to reap, harvest, mow," and from its participle comes the Latin messis "harvest, crop," preserved in such modern English items as the mower's trade and, through the Romance languages, in words for harvest and reaping; Greek amao "to mow, reap" belongs to the same root, as do forms in other branches. The root sits in the agrarian core vocabulary of Indo-European, the set of words that goes back to the farming communities of the steppe and its settled heirs, and its presence in so many branches shows that cutting the crop was an ancient, shared, everyday activity long before any single language wrote it down. Within Germanic the root built a feminine noun for the place where the cutting happens. Proto-Germanic *medwo, formed from the mow-stem with a feminine noun suffix, means literally "the mowing-land" — the parcel of grassland whose defining feature is that it is mown for hay. This is a different agricultural concept from the arable field, which is ploughed and sown, and from the pasture, which is merely grazed: the meadow is land managed by the scythe. The Germanic word family is coherent across every branch. Old English maed, with oblique stem maedwe, means "meadow"; Old Saxon and Middle Low German have forms of the same noun; Old High German mat and its modern descendant Mahd mean "mowing, mown grass"; and the verb from the same root appears as English mow with its participle mown, German mahen, and their relatives. Every branch preserves the same logic: meadow-land is harvest-land, the place where the scythe works in summer. Old English maed was a prominent and common word. In the Anglo-Saxon land charters it appears among the enumerated resources of an estate — wood, meadow, pasture, arable — because the meadow's hay crop was the winter fodder that kept the livestock alive, arguably the single most valuable resource of the medieval rural economy. The word's early legal and manorial currency is the reason it survives with such a precise meaning: the meadow was a unit of value that the law knew and measured. The spelling history is ordinary but worth noting. The oblique form maedwe supplied the basis of the modern shape, while the unextended maed survived in the poetic and dialectal mead, which is exactly the same word in older dress — "in the mead," "the mead's green." Middle English shows the full scribal scatter — mede, medwe, medewe, medowe, medow — and the standard spelling meadow crystallized in the early modern period, with the plural meadows recorded throughout. The sound change is regular: the Old English long ae and the medial d softened and simplified as the language moved toward modern pronunciation, and the final -we of the oblique stem was absorbed into the modern -ow spelling. The semantic development is remarkably stable, which is itself a fact about the English countryside. The word has meant "low-lying, often moist grassland, kept for hay or pasture, especially beside streams or below arable slopes" from the Old English period to the present day. The water-meadow, the flood-meadow, the hay-meadow, the meadowsweet (the flower of the hay-meadows), and the meadowlark all descend from this core: the word names not wild grassland but managed grassland, the green middle band of the landscape between tillage and waste. The water-meadow deserves special mention because it is the precise image behind Tolkien's phrase "meadows beyond, sloping down to the river." English water-meadows are river meadows deliberately managed so that the flood water spreads over the grass, warming it and increasing the hay crop; the system is documented from the medieval period and was a celebrated piece of agricultural engineering in the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries. When the meadows of the Shire slope down to the river, Tolkien is drawing, knowingly or instinctively, on the deepest English sense of what a river-meadow is: grass made fertile by water. In the ecological English of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word gained a conservation charge. A "species-rich meadow" is an unploughed, unmown-until-July habitat whose wildflower and invertebrate diversity is now rare and protected; the word, which for a thousand years named a working field, has come to name a threatened treasure. In the language of gardening and landscape design the meadow returned as a designed feature, a wildflower meadow planted to replace the formal lawn. Both modern senses descend transparently from the old mowing-ground: the hay-field that no one ploughs is exactly the species-rich grassland that conservation now protects. Literary and cultural use has been equally continuous. The pastoral tradition from Chaucer through Spenser's "mede," Milton's "meads," the Romantics, and Tolkien uses the meadow as the emblem of settled rural peace — green, soft, watered, fertile. It is no accident that the Shire's view from Bag End's round windows runs "garden, and meadows beyond, sloping down to the river": the word concentrates an entire rural ideal, the mown grass of a comfortable, productive, cared-for landscape. The plural in the text multiplies that ideal into a middle distance, and the etymology guarantees that the image is not vague: these are mowing-grounds, the very fields whose hay keeps hobbit hospitality supplied. The word's three-thousand-year journey from a steppe farming root meaning "to cut" to the green band of an English river-valley in a fantasy novel is a perfect example of how the deepest agricultural vocabulary still carries its original landscape logic. Summary bullets: - PIE *meh1- "to mow, cut": agrarian harvest vocabulary, attested in Latin metere and Greek amao. - Proto-Germanic *medwo "mowing-land": feminine noun built on the mow-stem; the same root gives English mow and German Mahd. - Old English maed/maedwe "meadow": prominent in Anglo-Saxon estate charters; the oblique stem shapes the modern form. - Middle English mede/medwe/medowe: scribal variety, then the standard spelling meadow. - Modern meadow: low-lying hay or pasture grassland, often moist and watered; water-meadow, flood-meadow. - Modern ecology and design: species-rich meadow, wildflower meadow — conservation and aesthetic value added to the agricultural core. - Poetic and literary: pastoral emblem of peace; Tolkien's "meadows beyond, sloping down to the river".
