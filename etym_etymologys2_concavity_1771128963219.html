PIE *keua- / *ku- 'to swell; vault, hole, hollow, arch' (c. 4000-3000 BCE) -> Proto-Italic *kawos 'hollow' -> Latin cavus 'hollow, hollowed out, excavated, concave' (attested from the 1st c. BCE; Varro, Lucretius, Vergil) -> Latin concavus 'hollowed out entirely, arched inward, concave' (com- intensive prefix + cavus; attested in classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) -> Late Latin concavitas 'hollowness, the state of being hollow or concave' (abstract noun in -itas, attested in late-antique Latin) -> Middle French concavite 'a hollow place; the state of being concave' (attested c. 14th-15th c.) -> English CONCAVITY 'a hollowed or curved-in surface; the quality of being concave' (attested c. 1590s, in the technical vocabulary of Renaissance science). Note on the debate: the PIE base behind Latin cavus is commonly reconstructed as *keua- / *ku- 'to swell; to vault, hollow', and scholars disagree about whether the 'swell' sense and the 'hollow, arch' sense are one root or two originally separate homophonous roots; Pokorny's lexicon, in particular, distributes the forms between *keu- 'to bend, arch' and a separate 'swell' etymon. The question does not affect the attested Latin-to-English chain above, but it is honestly recorded here because no single PIE reconstruction is universally accepted. The English spelling follows the French -ite / -te suffix rather than the Latin -itas directly.
