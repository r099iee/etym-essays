Kingship unites two ancient Germanic elements: king and -ship, each with a deep Indo-European pedigree. King begins in PIE *genh1-, to beget, give birth, produce — one of the most fertile roots, giving Sanskrit janas "birth, people", Greek genos "race, kind", Latin genus, gens, genius, gentle, general, Old Irish gein "birth", and Germanic *kunja- "kin, family, race" (Gothic kuni, Old Norse kyn, Old English cynn, modern kin, kind). From *kunja- with the belonging-suffix *-ingaz (son of, belonging to, as in Ætheling "son of nobles"), Proto-Germanic forms *kuningaz, literally son of the kin, descendant of the noble stock, hence leader of the tribe. Cognates prove common inheritance: Old English cyning, Old Saxon kuning, Old High German kuning, Old Norse konungr, Gothic kuniggs (in compounds). The original sense is elective-tribal: the man the kin chooses from its best blood, not yet an absolute monarch.

-Ship begins in Proto-Germanic *-skapiz, state, condition, quality, office — seen in Gothic -schafts, Old High German -scaft, modern German -schaft (Freundschaft "friendship"), Dutch -schap, Old Norse -skapr, Old English -scipe. Its base is the verb *skap- "to create, ordain, appoint" (Old English scieppan "to create", modern shape), from PIE *(s)kep- "to cut, scrape, shape". To add -ship is to name the shaped state: friend-ship the state of friends, lord-ship the state of lords, king-ship the state of kings. Old English already has cyning-scipe for office, realm, and royal manner together.

  • Old English cyningscipe (9th–11th c.): Alfred and Ælfric use cyningscipe for royal power, kingdom, and majesty without distinction. A king's cyningscipe is his war-band leadership, law-giving at the witan, and protection of church. Pronunciation /ˈky.ning.ʃi.pe/ with front rounded y and open ending. The word is political theory in miniature: kingship exists because kin exists.
  • Middle English kingschipe (12th–15th c.): after the Norman Conquest, cyning loses y to i (king /kɪŋ/) and -scipe weakens to -schipe, -ship /ʃɪp/. Chroniclers distinguish kingship (office) from kingdom (land) more sharply than Old English did, under French roiaume influence. Coronation ordines and mirrors for princes debate the duties of kingship: justice, mercy, defence, largesse.
  • Early-modern sacral kingship (16th–17th c.): Tudors and Stuarts exalt kingship as divine: James VI and I writes The True Law of Free Monarchies, Shakespeare stages failing kingship in Lear and Richard II. Execution of Charles I (1649) abolishes kingship itself, then Restoration (1660) restores it as if resurrected. The suffix -ship proves its abstraction: you can abolish kingship while kings live in exile.
  • Romantic-moral kingship (19th c.): Scott novels, Carlyle's hero-worship, and Ruskin's lectures make true kingship a labour theory: the best kings do most and take least, like Tydides spearing Trojans or Redgauntlet spearing salmon. Ruskin's distant contemplation of existent kingship is a verdict: Hanoverian courts show office without ideal. The word becomes reproach and aspiration together.

Phonetically king shows regular Germanic k- from PIE *g- by Grimm's law, nasal -ng /ŋ/, and vowel lowering from y to i. -Ship shows palatalisation of sk- to sh- /ʃ/ in Old English and reduction of final vowel to /ɪp/. Stress stays on king: /ˈkɪŋ.ʃɪp/ in Received Pronunciation.

Today kingship survives in scholarship, liturgy, and fable — historians of Anglo-Saxon kingship, the Feast of Christ the King, Aragorn learning kingship — a reminder that king once meant son of the kin, and -ship the shape of duty he must fill.
