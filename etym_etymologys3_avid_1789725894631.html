The evolution of AVID turns vice into virtue without changing appetite. Its secure base is Latin avere to crave, long for, desire eagerly, a verb of bodily wanting attested from early comedy onward, with derivatives avarus miserly, grasping and avaritia greed, one of the seven vices. From avere Latin formed avidus eager, vehement, greedy, insatiable, used of hungry wolves, covetous heirs, and eager learners alike. Whether avere continues a PIE root for desire — Pokorny’s *au- or *h2ew- — is judged unproven by Michiel de Vaan, who leaves the deeper origin open; OED and Etymonline likewise begin securely at Latin. Honest history therefore starts with Roman craving, not with the steppe.

Phonetically the journey is short and learned. Latin avidus with short a and long i passes into Middle French avide greedy, hungry, keeping spelling and stress, then into English in the 1760s as avid on the wave of Augustan Latinism that also gives torpid, vivid, placid. Early pronunciation follows French-Latin /ævɪd/ with short TRAP vowel, modern RP /ˈævɪd/ with initial stress, spelled without final -e to mark shortness. The noun avidity (fifteenth century via French, earlier than the adjective) and adverb avidly frame it as learned moral vocabulary. No popular sound shift obscures the borrowing; it is a book word for book hunger.

  • Greedy borrowing: Johnsonian English uses avid for grasping eyes, avid ambition, avid thirst for gold, firmly pejorative. Burke, Gibbon, and Austen deploy it for characters consumed by wanting. The construction avid of, avid for governs objects of lust. Sense is indistinguishable from covetous except for Latin prestige.
  • Redemption through reading: Victorian self-improvement revalues hunger when books are the food. Avid reader — scarcely found before 1850 — becomes by 1900 the flagship collocation in school reports, obituaries, and publishers’ blurbs, joined by avid naturalist, botanist, collector. Samuel’s avid reader who sated himself in books is the perfect Edwardian instance: gluttony language (sated) deliberately yoked to praiseworthy object, turning bone idleness at lessons into heroic appetite at literature. OED citations show the complimentary sense overtaking the greedy one in frequency without displacing it.
  • Fandom and market: twentieth-century leisure and commerce generalise avid to any loyal devouring: avid fans, supporters, viewers, consumers, gardeners, gamers. Voracious remains the stronger animal metaphor; avid is its domesticated, approving cousin. Literary attestations from Woolf (avid eyes) to contemporary reviews keep both branches alive, disambiguated by object: avid for power censures, avid for books commends, but stomach growls in both.

Culturally AVID thus records Protestant England learning to love appetite: Roman greed for gain becomes schoolboy greed for stories, and the boy who would not work is forgiven because he hungered. To be sated in books is still, etymologically, to feed greed until full — only the menu has been sanctified.
