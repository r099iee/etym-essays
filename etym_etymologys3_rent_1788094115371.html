I. RENT 'torn' — the native Germanic lineage of rend

The RENT that appears in 'their cries and howls rent the air' belongs to the native English verb rend, and its history is the history of that verb's past tense. Old English rendan 'to tear, cut, lacerate' is attested from the 8th century (notably in Mercian glosses), and it descends from a West Germanic base *randijan- whose deeper ancestry is genuinely obscure: no certain cognates survive outside West Germanic, and no PIE root can be securely reconstructed. Some scholars have compared it with Germanic *randa- 'edge, border, rim' (the source of English rand 'strip, border' and of the -rand in names like Randolf), on the idea that to rend is to tear something along its edge or rim; but this link is speculative and is not accepted by the major etymological dictionaries (the OED marks the origin as unknown). The honest scholarly position is that rend is a Germanic isolate beyond a certain depth — one of those everyday words of great age whose pedigree is lost.

  • Old English (8th–11th c.): rendan means 'to tear, cut, lacerate'; it is an originally strong-class verb whose past tense is rende/rente. The simplex appears in glosses, and compounds such as torendan 'to tear apart' are attested. The meaning is purely physical: tearing cloth, splitting wood, lacerating flesh or hair — and the mourning custom of tearing one's garments made the verb culturally important in a Germanic and biblical world alike.
  • Middle English (12th–15th c.): renden (also rinden) continues with the same violent senses; the Wycliffite Bible (1380s) uses it for the tearing of garments and of the temple veil. The past form rente is now identical in shape to the freshly borrowed French rente 'income, revenue', creating the homographic collision that persists to this day.
  • Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): rend and its past rent acquire a permanent biblical register through Tyndale's New Testament (1526) and the Authorised Version (1611): 'the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom' (Matthew 27:51), 'rend your heart, and not your garments' (Joel 2:13). Shakespeare uses rent of torn cloth, flesh, and air (Macbeth's imagery, c. 1606); Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) uses it of the earth torn by earthquakes.
  • Morphology: the past rent belongs to the small -t past class shared with send/sent, lend/lent, spend/spent, bend/bent — dental preterites. Rend was attracted into this class, which is why the past is rent and not *rended; the form has been stable since Middle English.
  • 18th–19th c. — the retreat: from the 18th century the tear-sense steadily withdraws from everyday speech into literary, journalistic, and religious prose. By the 19th century its productive home is metaphor: nations and parties are 'rent' by conflict (the mid-century American press speaks of a Union 'rent asunder'), silence is 'rent' by a cry, the ground is 'rent' by an earthquake.
  • 20th–21st c. — fossilisation and survival: the physical sense survives in technical registers (a 'rent' in geological strata, a 'rent' in a membrane in biology) and in fixed literary phrases ('rent the air', 'rent asunder', 'a rent in the curtain'). The noun 'rent' = 'tear' is still taught and used by writers seeking an archaic gravity; in ordinary conversation it has been replaced by tear, rip, and split.

Key attestations: OE glosses of rendan; Wycliffite Bible (c. 1382–1395); Shakespeare, Macbeth (c. 1606); Authorised Version (1611); Milton, Paradise Lost (1667); 19th-century political rhetoric of a 'rent' nation; modern journalistic 'rent by factionalism'. The word's trajectory is a textbook case of semantic narrowing plus register shift: an everyday verb of physical tearing became a literary word of violent division, kept alive by scripture and poetry.

II. RENT 'payment' — from PIE *deh₃- 'to give' to the landlord's bill

The payment sense of RENT is a completely separate word that collides with the tear-word only in spelling and sound. Its chain begins with the PIE root *deh₃- 'to give', which produces Latin dare 'to give' (whence date, dative, donor, and the Romance donner/donare family). From dare, Latin builds reddere — re- 'back' + dare — 'to give back, restore, return', the source of render and surrender via Old French rendre. The feminine past participle reddita, 'things given back', was pressed into service as a noun meaning 'income, revenue, that which is returned to its owner': in late Latin and early medieval administrative language, reddita/*rendita came to mean the regular income of an estate, especially the annual sum owed to a lord for the use of land. The shift from reddita to *rendita reflects regular Vulgar Latin assimilation of the geminate and nasalisation before the dental; the starred form is reconstructed from the Romance outcome, not directly attested.

  • Old French (12th c.): *rendita develops regularly into Old French rente, 'revenue, income, annual payment'; medieval French rente covers annuities, pensions, interest on loans, and land payments alike — any regular, recurring income. The verb rendre 'to give back' accompanies it.
  • Entry into English (late 12th–13th c.): English borrows rente via Anglo-French manorial and legal usage: first 'income, revenue', then specifically 'payment made by a tenant to a lord for land'. The word appears in manorial rolls and in Chaucer (late 14th c.), who uses rente for income and revenue generally. The spelling settles as rent by the 16th century.
  • Semantic narrowing (15th–17th c.): whereas medieval rente meant any regular income, modern rent narrows to the payment for the use of property — land and buildings. The older breadth survives in French rente 'annuity, government bond' and in English rentier 'one who lives on investment income' (borrowed from French in the 19th century).
  • The verb (c. 1500): 'to rent' in the sense 'to hold or grant in return for rent' appears around 1500; by the 19th century it covers hiring cars, boats, rooms, and halls; in American English it becomes the standard verb for both sides of a tenancy, while British English prefers 'let' for the landlord's side.
  • Legal and economic elaboration (16th c.–present): a dense legal vocabulary grows — rack rent, ground rent, peppercorn rent, rent roll, rent arrears, rent control. In classical economics (Ricardo, 1817), 'economic rent' names payment for a factor of production beyond its opportunity cost; in the late 20th century public-choice economists coin 'rent-seeking' for the pursuit of such unearned income. The word has come full circle: from 'regular income' to 'payment for land' to a technical term for unearned surplus.
  • Phonetic shape: Middle English rente /ˈrɛntə/ → early Modern English rent /rɛnt/, identical in pronunciation to the tear-word — the homophone/homograph collision that makes modern rent a textbook example of convergent etymology.

Key attestations: Latin reddita in medieval estate documents; Old French rente in 12th-century charters; Middle English rente in manorial rolls and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (c. 1387); Shakespeare's 'rent' for payment (The Merchant of Venice, c. 1596–98); Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817) for economic rent; Tullock's coinage 'rent-seeking' (1967). The payment word, unlike the tear word, has only grown in importance over six centuries — from feudal dues to the trillion-pound global rental economy.
