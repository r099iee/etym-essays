1. The nag that became a knight. The story of “chivalry” begins, improbably, with a humble horse. Latin had equus, the noble war-horse of the Roman cavalry, but the everyday speech of the western empire used caballus — the workhorse, the pack-animal, the nag. The word is attested in Plautus and Catullus, and it was the vocabulary of soldiers, farmers and grooms rather than of poets: the Romance languages built their words for “horse” on it (Italian cavallo, Spanish caballo, Portuguese cavalo, French cheval), while classical equus survived only in learned words like “equestrian”. Where caballus itself came from is debated: the usual account makes it a loan from Gaulish or another Celtic speech (compare Welsh ceffyl, Breton kefel), and a substrate origin — Etruscan or Mediterranean — is possible; what is certain is that the word belonged to the non-classical stratum of Latin, the very stratum that became the languages of medieval Europe.

2. From horseman to knight. In Late Latin, caballārius is the man who works with horses — the groom, the horse-soldier — and from it Old French made chevalier, at first simply “horseman”. The feudal centuries turned the horseman into the knight: the warrior who could arm, mount and fight on horseback became the armoured elite of the medieval host, bound to his lord by homage and fealty, rewarded with fiefs, distinguished by the ceremony of knighthood — the accolade — and by the symbols of sword, spurs and banner. The twelfth century, the age of the Crusades and the great tournaments, saw the chevalier transformed from a military function into a social estate with an ethos: the romances of Chrétien de Troyes and the lyric of the troubadours — French and Provençal poets — fused the warrior code with the cult of courtly love, so that the knight’s services were owed in equal measure to God, to his lord and to his lady. When Pater writes of the “notions of chivalry and romantic love” that Francis of Assisi received “from a French source”, he is pointing at exactly this twelfth-century fusion — the chivalric-courtly complex whose native ground was the France of the troubadours.

3. The code and the orders. Old French chevalerie named the body of knights and the quality of chevaliers; Middle English took it in both senses. From the fourteenth century the ideal became institutional: the great orders of chivalry — the Garter in England (1348), the Golden Fleece in Burgundy (1430), the Annunziata in Savoy — codified honour into statutes, and manuals of chivalry (Ramon Llull’s Llibre de l’orde de cavalleria, translated into English as The Book of the Ordre of Chyvalry, the heraldic and didactic literature of the fifteenth century) set out the virtues expected of the knight: loyalty, truth, largesse, courtesy, prowess, piety, and the defence of the weak — the widow, the orphan, the Church. Froissart’s Chronicles, the Hospital and Temple orders, the biographies of Boucicaut and Bayard, “the knight without fear and without reproach”, all belong to this phase, in which chivalry was both a real discipline and a constantly confessed ideal.

4. Decline and satire. The late medieval and early modern world kept the word while emptying or bending the institution. The Hundred Years’ War and the wars of the Roses showed chivalry as a code routinely honoured in the breach; the invention of gunpowder made the armoured horseman obsolete; and Cervantes delivered the most devastating verdict in literature when Don Quixote (1605–1615) turned the chivalric romance into the manifesto of a madman. Yet even as the mailed cavalryman disappeared from the battlefield, “chivalry” migrated upward into manners: the courteous conduct of the gentleman toward ladies and inferiors — the rituals of deference, protection and politeness that the modern age calls chivalrous. Burke made the famous lament in 1790, grieving that the age of chivalry was gone with the fall of the old European order; the phrase “the age of chivalry” thereafter meant the whole picturesque past of honour and courtesy.

5. The romantic and modern afterlives. Romanticism and Victorian medievalism restored the ideal with enthusiasm: Scott’s Ivanhoe, Tennyson’s Idylls of the King, the Gothic Revival and the Pre-Raphaelites all re-crowned the knight errant, and chivalry became part of English education as a model of manly honour. In the twentieth century the word acquired a contested aftertaste: “chivalry is dead” began as a rhetorical complaint and became a cliché of gender politics, where the courteous protective gesture is alternately treasured as respect or condemned as paternalism. Historians now use the word with professional caution, distinguishing the literary ideal from the social practice, while idiomatic English keeps both faces — the tournament and the held door — in a single word whose ultimate root, a coarse Roman word for a pack-horse, is perhaps the least chivalrous base a noble ideal could have.
