Grocer is a noun naming a retailer of food and household provisions — historically a wholesale dealer in bulk (or "gross") goods, later the familiar high-street shopkeeper of tea, sugar, spices, dried fruit, tinned goods, and dry stores. The social image the word carries is solidly middle-class, commercial, and unheroic: aprons, brass scales, wooden counters, paper bags, and a permanently respectable shopfront rather than swords, horses, or adventure. Etymologically it descends from the idea of "selling in gross" (in bulk), a fact that leaves a permanent flavour of quantity, storage, and supply in the word. When Gloin says of Bilbo that he "looks more like a grocer than a burglar," the insult is a precise piece of comic social classing. The hobbit's round, comfortable respectability reads as shopkeeping, not as the lean cunning of a hired thief; a grocer is the very emblem of safe, settled commerce — the last thing a dragon-expedition needs. The dwarf's contempt is bourgeois-detached, slightly disdainful, and richly ironic for the reader who knows that this "grocer" will in fact out-adventure them all. The word weaponizes ordinary English trade against romantic heroism, and the comedy depends on the reader sharing the stable, unglamorous image of the trade. Primary senses of grocer
  • Retail seller of food and household provisions — the modern core sense: the shopkeeper of the corner grocery store.
  • Historical — a wholesale dealer in bulk ("gross") goods, especially provisions, spices, and dry stores, in the 14th–18th centuries.
  • The shop itself, by metonymy and colloquial extension — "the corner grocer"; "going to the grocer's."
  • Institutionally — the Grocers' Company, one of the great London livery companies, which fixed the word in the world of medieval and early modern wholesale provision.
Polysemy, compounds, and modern uses
  • Grocer's apostrophe — the humorous name for the misuse of an apostrophe in simple plurals ("potato's", "CD's"), attributed to sloppy hand-painted shop-sign lettering; the term derives from the trade's association with such signs.
  • Grocery / groceries — the goods sold and the shop itself; "grocery" is now also a mass noun for foodstuffs bought at a store, and "groceries" is the everyday term for a shopping trip's contents.
  • Greengrocer — a specialist seller of fresh fruit and vegetables; the compound shows how productive the -grocer element remains.
  • Figurative "grocer" — a jibe for a comfortably bourgeois, unadventurous, or timid person; exactly the force of Gloin's insult to Bilbo.
  • In the supermarket age — "grocer" persists as the homely, old-fashioned alternative to "supermarket," "store," or "retailer," and carries a warm, nostalgic, small-shop connotation.
  • Big Grocery — a modern journalistic and business phrase for the large supermarket chains, showing the word's continued life in commercial discourse.
Register and collocation Grocer collocates with corner, local, shop, chain, and with the possessive form "the grocer's"; it pairs naturally with tea, sugar, spice, and provisions. Its register is domestic and commercial, unromantic and respectable — the very atmosphere that makes the word such an effective counterweight to "burglar" in the dwarves' assessment of Bilbo.
