• Great Pestilence: Similar because it is the exact medieval contemporary term for the 1346–1353 pandemic; differs because Great Pestilence is the authentic historical Latin/Middle English designation, whereas Black Death is the modern historiographical standard name.

  • Bubonic Plague: Similar because bubonic infection was the predominant clinical manifestation of the Black Death; differs because bubonic plague is a specific medical and pathological diagnosis that can occur in any era or outbreak, whereas the Black Death denotes the specific historical 14th-century pandemic event (which also included pneumonic and septicemic strains).

  • Pestilence: Similar because both refer to deadly epidemic diseases; differs because pestilence is a generic, archaic biblical noun applied to any fatal contagious disease or divine scourge without specific historical date or pathogen.

  • Plague: Similar because the Black Death was an outbreak of Yersinia pestis; differs because plague can refer broadly to any widespread affliction, biblical plague of insects, or generic epidemic, whereas the Black Death specifies the 1346–1353 disaster.

  • Justinian Plague: Similar because both were catastrophic global pandemics caused by Yersinia pestis; differs because the Justinian Plague (541–549 CE and recurring until ~750 CE) struck the Byzantine Empire and Late Antique Mediterranean during the reign of Emperor Justinian I, eight centuries prior to the Black Death.

  • Antonine Plague: Similar because both were epoch-defining pandemics that crippled great empires; differs because the Antonine Plague (165–180 CE) struck the Roman Empire, was caused by smallpox or measles rather than Yersinia pestis, and occurred in classical antiquity.

  • Pandemic: Similar because the Black Death was a pandemic of unprecedented scope; differs because pandemic is a general epidemiological classification denoting any disease spreading across multiple continents or worldwide (e.g., COVID-19, 1918 influenza).

  • Mortality: Similar because the Black Death was contemporaneously termed the "Great Mortality"; differs because mortality is a general statistical or philosophical noun referring to the condition of being subject to death or the measure of death rates.

  • Contagion: Similar because the Black Death involved virulent infectious spread; differs because contagion refers to the physical transmission mechanism or the infectious agent itself rather than a named historical disaster.

  • Scourge: Similar because the Black Death was viewed as a devastating historical scourge; differs because scourge is a figurative, moral, or religious term for any cause of great suffering, affliction, or divine punishment (e.g., war, famine).
