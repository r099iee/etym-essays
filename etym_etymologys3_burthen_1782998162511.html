BURTHEN: A WEIGHT THAT SOUNDS ANCIENT The story of burthen begins in the deep Indo-European past with the root *bʰer-, 'to carry, bear, bring forth', one of the most productive carry-roots in the entire family. Its reflexes span every branch: Sanskrit bhárati 'he carries', Latin ferō and ferre 'to carry, bear', Greek phérō 'to carry', Avestan baraiti, and the Germanic verb *beraną, which became Old English beran and our own bear. The word reached Germanic carrying its full semantic load — to carry in the arms, to endure, and to give birth, a meaning still alive in English (to bear a child) and in German gebären 'to give birth'.
  • Forming the noun: Proto-Germanic took the reduced (zero) grade of the verb root and added an abstract noun-suffix containing -þ-, producing *bur-þ-injō, literally 'that which is carried'. This is the same mechanism that gave German Bürde and Old Norse byrðr; the dental suffix is the very -th- that burthen preserves in its spelling.
  • Sound history: the zero grade *bʰr̥- > PGmc *bur- shows the syllabic r resolving to -ur-; the medial cluster -r-þ- is stable in Old English byrþen. In Middle English the dental fricative /θ/ was variously kept (burthen, birthen) or hardened to the stop /d/ (burden, birden) according to dialect; modern standard English generalized the /d/ form, while the -th- spelling survived in the archaizing register and in nautical documents.
Old English byrþen was already a double word. In the glosses and in prose it meant a physical pack or load ('he bare his byrþen on his back'), but from the earliest Christian literature it also carried the moral and spiritual weight of sin, guilt, and duty — the language of penance and of the soul's trials. That double life, physical and moral, is the seed of everything burthen later became.
  • Middle English variation: in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries burthen, burden, birthen, and birden coexisted; Chaucer and the Wycliffite Bible use both spellings. Printers began to standardize burden, but burthen refused to die because the Bible would not let it.
  • The Authorised Version (1611): the translators chose burthen for the Hebrew maśśā', 'a weight, a load', in such verses as 'Cast thy burthen upon the LORD' (Psalm 55:22) and 'For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light' (Matthew 11:30 — here 'burden', showing the two spellings in free variation even within the same book). This scriptural currency made burthen the sound of solemn obligation.
  • Nautical use: independently, English shipping law used burthen to mean a vessel's carrying capacity or tonnage ('a ship of 400 tons burthen'); the word thus had a precise, measurable, commercial-technical life alongside its poetic one.
The Romantics were the great revivers. Wordsworth's 'That burthen of my own unnatural self' in The Prelude (1805) deliberately reaches for the -th- form: it slows the line, echoes the Psalms, and makes the speaker's weariness feel inherited and ancient rather than merely personal. Coleridge, Keats, and Shelley all use burthen in the same deliberate way, and the Victorian poets kept the habit. Meanwhile plain burden carried on as the ordinary word for loads, obligations, and the legal 'burden of proof'.
  • Socio-cultural split: by the twentieth century the two spellings had diverged in tone rather than meaning — burden neutral and universal, burthen consciously archaic, scriptural, and weighty. A modern writer choosing burthen is making a stylistic decision about register, not a grammatical one.
  • Where it survives: burthen lives today in poetry, liturgical prose, hymns, historical nautical texts, and the fossil phrase 'beast of burthen'; its meaning — a load that is felt, carried, and finally shaken off — remains one of the most enduring metaphors of the language.
