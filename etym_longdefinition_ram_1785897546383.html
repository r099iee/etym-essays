RAM: An adult male sheep — the uncastrated male of the domesticated species Ovis aries, kept for breeding, valued for its horns, wool, and meat — and, by a long and productive metaphor, any heavy instrument or machine that delivers a powerful, concentrated impact: the battering ram of medieval siege warfare, the naval ram (or ram bow) of ancient and 19th-century warships, the hydraulic or pneumatic ram used in modern engineering, and, by a further modern twist, the computer memory module called RAM. As a verb, to ram is to strike, drive, cram, or pack with violent force. The word therefore straddles biology and engineering, rural husbandry and urban warfare, the animal's head-butting instinct and the piston's mechanical shove — and every one of its major senses can be shown to flow from that single, vivid image of a horned head hitting something hard.

Core senses and current polysemy:
  • Male sheep (the animal): the uncastrated adult male of the domestic sheep, kept chiefly for breeding and for wool and mutton; in pastoral idiom the ram is the leader and the emblem of virility, stubbornness, and brute force. Collocations: a breeding ram, a ram's horn, to climb with the stubbornness of a ram. In the source context a "large coal-black" ram leads a flock of snow-white sheep, and the blackness of the leader carries both visual force and a faint menace.
  • Battering ram (siege engine): the heavy beam, originally a great tree-trunk, often tipped with iron and in classical practice even carved or sheathed with an iron ram's head, swung or driven against gates, walls, and fortifications to break them down. The transfer from animal to engine is ancient: the beast that charges and butts was the natural image for a machine that charges and smashes, and the same image appears independently in Latin (aries, literally "ram") and Greek (krios, also "ram").
  • Naval ram (ram bow): a projecting beak or reinforced prow on a warship, designed to ride up over and then puncture an enemy hull below the waterline; used from classical antiquity (Greek and Roman triremes and quinqueremes) through the ironclad fleets of the 19th century, when whole classes of ramming warships were built and the ram was a decisive weapon.
  • Hydraulic/pneumatic ram (engineering): a cylinder containing a piston that is driven by fluid or air pressure to exert enormous, concentrated force — the ram of a press, a pile-driver, a punch, or a forging hammer; also the "hydraulic ram", a self-acting water pump (invented by Joseph Montgolfier in 1796) that uses the momentum of a water hammer to lift a portion of water to a height greater than its source.
  • Computing RAM (Random Access Memory): the acronym that names a computer's working memory — the volatile store in which the machine keeps the data and instructions it is currently using. This is not an etymological descendant of the sheep; it is a 20th-century initialism that happens to be homophonous with the old word, and the accidental pun has produced jocular compounds such as "to ram a programme into memory" or "a RAM upgrade". The collision is a pure coincidence of sound, but speakers feel the old force behind it.
  • The Ram (astrology and astronomy): Aries, the first sign of the zodiac, and the constellation Aries — the golden-fleeced ram of Greek myth that carried Phrixus and Helle across the sea. Here the word is pure symbolism, the horned animal writ large across the sky, and the zodiacal Ram is one of the oldest astrological emblems, surviving in the symbol ♈.
  • The verb to ram: to drive or thrust with force (to ram a stake into the ground, to ram a message home), to strike violently against (to ram into the back of a car), and, in the compounds ramming speed, ram raid (a burglary committed by driving a vehicle into a shopfront), and to ram something down someone's throat. The verb is recorded from the 15th century and is the source of most of the word's mechanical life.
  • Ramrod and related implements: the rod used to ram gunpowder and shot down the barrel of a muzzle-loading firearm (whence stiff as a ramrod, i.e. unbending, rigid), and by extension any stiff rod or unbending person; also the rammer, the tool used to pack earth or powder.

How the senses hang together — the historical bridge: The oldest meaning is the animal, recorded in English from before the year 725 (Old English ramm, ram). The battering engine was already known to the Romans — their aries, literally "the ram", was the same siege device named after the same butting animal — and English speakers applied the same image independently: by the 14th century a "ram" could be a siege weapon in English, and by the 15th century the verb "to ram" meant to strike or stuff with force. The naval ram is a return to classical practice, revived with spectacular effect in the 19th-century ironclad era; the hydraulic ram belongs to 18th–19th-century engineering, when the old word was re-employed for a machine that pounds with a piston. The computing RAM (1950s onward) is a much later homophone that no careful lexicographer would connect to the sheep; yet in everyday speech the two have merged in sound, and the pun "more RAM for your sheep" still works. The zodiac Ram is mythological, drawn from the golden-fleece story that also gives the sky its Aries.

Register and connotation: In animal husbandry the word is plain and technical; in siege and naval history it carries a flavour of brute force; in engineering it is neutral and mechanical; in computing it is ubiquitous modern jargon; in astrology it is symbolic. The figurative uses ("to ram home a point", "ramrod-stiff", "ram raid") keep the original violence: something rams because it is heavy, hard, and unstoppable — like a horned head driven by instinct. The word's ultimate origin is not securely known (see etymologyS2), which is itself a reminder that even the most common words can arrive from a place history has forgotten.
