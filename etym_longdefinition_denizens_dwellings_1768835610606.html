denizens — A denizen is an inhabitant, a permanent resident of a particular place; in historical English law the word also named a foreigner admitted by royal grant to residence and to certain rights of citizenship — a naturalised “insider.” The word therefore carries a legal, almost feudal flavour of belonging: the denizen is not merely present but of the place, granted standing within its boundaries. In the passage being corrected, the denizens are the people of the “outer quarters,” the district beyond the castle walls, and the sentence sets them against “those who lived within the walls.” The word does delicate social work: it gives the outer folk a formal status and a name of their own — they are denizens, not vagrants or peasants — while simultaneously marking the boundary that divides them from the privileged interior. The annual ritual of the first June morning, when the entire population of the clay dwellings had sanction to enter the Grounds, frames their existence as one of tolerated, ritualised exclusion: they belong to the estate’s world, but only as outsiders who must be admitted each year to the place where the carvings are displayed.
  • Primary sense (inhabitant): a person, animal, or plant that permanently occupies a given place — denizens of the forest, denizens of the deep.
  • Historical legal sense: in English law, a foreigner admitted to residence and certain civic rights by royal grant or letters patent, ranking below a full natural-born or naturalised citizen.
  • Ecological/zoological extension: from the 19th century the word peoples habitats and depths — “denizens of the sea,” “denizens of the jungle” — with a faintly romantic, quasi-zoological sweep.
  • Register note: denizen is formal, faintly archaic, and gently hierarchical; it dignifies ordinary residents with the vocabulary of law and custom in a way that plain inhabitant or resident does not.
dwellings — A dwelling is a place of residence — a house, cottage, flat, or other structure in which people live. The word is formed from the verb dwell “to live, reside, remain” plus the suffix -ing that turns verbs into nouns of result or place. Unlike house, which stresses the physical structure, and home, which stresses the emotional centre, dwelling is a formal, almost administrative word for the act of residing made into a place: a legal register in which people do not have homes but occupy dwellings. In the passage the “clay dwellings” are the poor, earthen-built homes of the outer quarters, and the word’s formal, institutional tone matters: the sentence is recording a census-like fact about where people live, and the humble material — clay — contrasts sharply with the grandeur of the “Grounds” they may enter only once a year.
  • Primary sense (residence): any place where someone lives, from a cave to a palace; “dwelling” is the general, official term for a residence.
  • Legal and technical use: “dwelling-house” (a building used as a residence), “dwelling unit” (a self-contained place of residence in planning law), “dwelling” as the neutral noun of property registers and censuses.
  • Figurative / dated extension: the verb dwell also means “to linger, to keep the mind fixed on” (to dwell on a memory), and by a poetic leap a “dwelling” can be the seat of a quality — the dwelling of light, the soul’s dwelling.
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: house = the physical building; home = the emotional and familial centre; residence = the formal, official place of abode; dwelling = the general, often impersonal or administrative place of habitation.
Read together, the two words map the social geography of the tale: the denizens are granted a name and a status, and the clay dwellings give them a place — but both words keep them firmly outside the walls, formalised, known, and excluded.
