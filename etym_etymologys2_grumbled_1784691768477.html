No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon: the word is onomatopoeic / echoic, built on the syllable grum-, a low rolling rumble (cf. also grunt, growl, and Latin grumīre "to grunt"). → Middle Dutch grommen "to growl, grumble, grunt" (echoic) — and/or Middle French grommeler "to murmur, mutter, grumble" (echoic frequentative, attested from the 12th c.) → English grumble (verb) "to make a low rumbling sound; to murmur discontent" (c. 1580) → grumbled (past tense and past participle, in regular use from the 16th c. onward) → grumbled. Note on the debated stage: the exact route into English is uncertain. The verb closely resembles both Middle French grommeler and Middle Dutch grommen, and handbooks observe that the echoic base was produced independently across Germanic and Romance (cf. German grummeln, "to rumble, mutter"); no single direction of borrowing can be proven. The English relatives rumble (c. 1400) and growl (1650s) are independent formations on the same sound pattern, not ancestors. The later family grump, grumpish, grumpy grows out of grumble in the 18th century but is not part of grumbled's own derivation.
