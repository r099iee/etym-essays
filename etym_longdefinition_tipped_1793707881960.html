The verb "to tip," in its various forms and applications, is a remarkably polysemous word in the English language, encompassing a range of meanings that, while seemingly disparate, often share a subtle underlying semantic thread related to movement, contact, or extremity. The form "tipped" is the past tense and past participle of this versatile verb. One of the most common and fundamental senses of "to tip" is **to cause something to lean, tilt, or fall over**, often by disturbing its balance. This can be a transitive action, as in "He tipped the chair over," or an intransitive one, "The boat tipped dangerously in the storm." This sense implies a shift from a stable, upright, or level position to an unstable, inclined, or fallen one. The degree of tipping can vary from a slight inclination to a complete overturning. For instance, one might "tip a glass" to pour out its contents, or a heavy object might "tip over" if its center of gravity shifts too far. This meaning is often associated with a sudden, decisive, or accidental movement. The context "The big root just twisted round and tipped me in!" clearly aligns with this sense, implying that the root caused the speaker to lose balance and fall into something. This action suggests a forceful, perhaps unexpected, displacement. A closely related but distinct sense is **to strike or touch lightly and quickly**, often with the end or point of something. This can be seen in phrases like "to tip a hat" (a quick, slight raising of the brim as a greeting), or "to tip a ball" in cricket (to lightly touch it with the bat). This sense emphasizes a brief, often delicate, physical contact, usually with the extremity of an object or body part. It implies a momentary interaction rather than a sustained force. This meaning often carries a connotation of swiftness and minimal impact. Another significant and widely recognized meaning of "to tip" is **to give a gratuity or a small sum of money** to someone for a service rendered, particularly in hospitality or service industries. This is typically an act of appreciation beyond the standard payment. For example, "She tipped the waiter generously." This practice, while common today, has a specific historical and cultural context, evolving from earlier forms of patronage and recognition. The amount of the tip can vary, and it is often seen as a reflection of the quality of service received. This sense is almost exclusively transitive, requiring an object (the person being tipped). Furthermore, "to tip" can mean **to provide secret or confidential information or a warning** to someone, often to give them an advantage or to alert them to a potential danger or opportunity. This is frequently used in the phrasal verb "to tip off." For instance, "The police were tipped off about the illegal operation." This sense implies the discreet transfer of valuable, often privileged, knowledge. The information provided is usually intended to influence an outcome or decision. It suggests a clandestine or unofficial communication channel. In a more literal and older sense, "to tip" can also mean **to furnish something with a tip or point**, or to cover the end of something. For example, "to tip an arrow with steel" or "to tip a cane with rubber." This usage derives directly from the noun "tip," referring to the extremity or apex of an object. Conversely, it can also mean **to remove the tip or end of something**, as in "to tip a plant" to encourage bushier growth. These senses are less common in everyday modern English but are still encountered in specific contexts, particularly in crafts, gardening, or historical descriptions. The verb also appears in various idiomatic expressions:
  • To tip the scales: To be the deciding factor in a situation, often implying a slight but crucial shift in balance. "His testimony tipped the scales in favor of the defense."
  • To tip one's hand: To reveal one's intentions or advantages, often inadvertently. This comes from card games where one might accidentally show their cards. "He tipped his hand too early in the negotiation."
  • To tip over the edge: To push someone or something to a breaking point or a critical state. "The constant pressure finally tipped her over the edge."
  • To tip-top: An adjective meaning excellent or in perfect condition, derived from the idea of being at the very best "tip" or "top."
The semantic development of "tip" is complex, with several distinct etymological roots contributing to its rich polysemy. The core idea of a "point" or "extremity" (from the noun "tip") underpins the senses of furnishing or removing a tip. The idea of a "light touch" or "tap" likely evolved into the sense of causing something to tilt or fall, as a light touch on an unstable object can cause it to move. The "gratuity" and "secret information" senses are later developments, possibly stemming from the idea of a small, quick transfer (of money or information) or a slight, decisive action. The word's adaptability and the subtle connections between its meanings make it a fascinating subject for linguistic study, reflecting how language can evolve to capture nuanced actions and concepts through seemingly simple forms. The context "tipped me in!" specifically evokes the dynamic action of being caused to fall or be pushed into something, highlighting the verb's capacity to describe sudden, impactful movements.
