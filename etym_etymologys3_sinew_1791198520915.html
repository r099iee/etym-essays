Sinew is a direct inheritance from Old English sinu, itself from Proto-Germanic *senawō, ultimately from the PIE root *sneh₁- meaning to sew, spin, or twist. The etymology is therefore a material metaphor: sinews are the body's sewing-threads, the cords that stitch muscle to bone.

The PIE root *sneh₁- underlies a wide family of sewing and spinning words: Latin nere to spin, Greek nein to spin, English needle, sew, snare, and Sanskrit snāvan sinew. The semantic link is twisting fibres into cord. Proto-Germanic took the extended o-grade or suffixed form *senawō to name animal tendon specifically, because tendon looks and behaves like twisted cord: pale, fibrous, immensely strong in tension. Cognates confirm the antiquity: Old High German senawa, Old Norse sin, Gothic *sina, Dutch zenuw, German Sehne tendon and bowstring.

In Old English, sinu (plural sinwa, seonowe) covered a broader field than modern anatomy:

  • Tendon and ligament: the white fibrous bands joining muscle to bone, as in the heel-sinew.
  • Nerve: Anglo-Saxons did not sharply distinguish tendon from nerve; both were stringy channels of power and feeling. Medical texts use sinu where modern texts would say nerve.
  • Strength in general: already in Beowulf-era poetry, sinews stand for bodily power.

Middle English sinewe shows Norman-influenced spelling with -ew- and French anatomical vocabulary pressing in (tendon, nerve, muscle entering from Old French and Latin). Rather than being displaced, sinew specialized: tendon became the technical anatomical term, nerve took the neurological sense, and sinew retained the older, more visceral sense of tough animal cord and figurative strength. Chaucer and later hunting treatises speak of deer-sinews for bowstrings and stitching, preserving the craft context seen in the source sentence about cords of animal sinew binding wooden handles.

Phonologically, the development is regular:

  • Proto-Germanic *senawō to Old English sinu: fronting and weakening of unstressed vowels, loss of final -w vocalization patterns varying by dialect (West Saxon seonu vs Anglian sinu).
  • Middle English lengthening and glide: sinewe pronounced /ˈsinəwə/ collapsing to /ˈsinjuː/ with palatal glide before u, spelled -ew.
  • Modern /ˈsɪnjuː/: shortening of the stressed vowel and retention of /nj/ cluster, distinct from sinewy /ˈsɪnjuːi/.

Socio-cultural threads run through the word's life:

  • Prehistoric technology: sinew as glue, thread, and bowstring — chewed or pounded sinew yields collagen glue; twisted sinew makes the finest pre-modern cord, superior to plant fibre for bows and snowshoes because it stretches and recoils.
  • Biblical and classical translation: the King James Bible (1611) uses sinews in Job, Ezekiel's valley of dry bones ("I will lay sinews upon you"), and Daniel, fixing the solemn, bodily sense for centuries of readers.
  • Figurative economic and political use: from the 17th century, "sinews of war" (money), "sinews of power," "sinews of the state" — strength imagined as tendons bracing a body politic.
  • Modern fitness and anatomy: sinewy now praises lean, corded muscularity in athletes, dancers, and racehorses, while sinew itself sounds archaic-poetic beside clinical tendon.

Literary attestations trace this arc from heroic verse to frontier craft: Beowulf's severed sinews, Shakespeare's "sinews, shrunk up" in age, Milton's sinewy angels, Melville's whaling chapters on cutting sinews, and ethnographies describing Indigenous sinew-backed bows and sinew-sewn clothing. The source context belongs to this deep technological memory, where animal sinew is not waste but high engineering material. Modern English thus keeps two sinews: the anatomist's tendon and the poet's strength.
