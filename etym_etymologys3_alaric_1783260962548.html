The name Alaric is a two-element Germanic royal compound of exactly the same class as Theodoric, but with a first element of genuinely debated ancestry. The second element is certain: *-reiks, 'ruler', is the Gothic word for 'ruler' or 'king', and it is one of the great loanwords of the migration age, borrowed by the Germanic peoples from their Celtic neighbours — Proto-Germanic *rīks derives from Celtic *rīg- 'king', the word of Vercingetorix and the Gaulish -rix names, itself from the Indo-European root *h₃rēǵ- 'to rule, set straight' that also produced Latin rex and Sanskrit rājan. The story of this loan is told fully in the tree of Theodoric: the same *-reiks sits in Ermanaric and in Gothic reiks of Wulfila's Bible, and in the adjective *rīkijaz 'powerful, mighty' that eventually gave English rich and German Reich. The first element, however, is where scholarship divides. The mainstream reading, established in Schönfeld's standard dictionary of old Germanic personal and tribal names, takes it as Gothic ala-, 'all, wholly', from Proto-Germanic *allaz 'all', giving *Alareiks, 'ruler of all'. The same ala- 'all' is the first element of the Alemanni, the tribal confederation whose name means 'all-men' (whence French Allemand and Spanish Alemán, 'German'), and of the Old Norse royal name Alrekr. But a minority of onomasticians have proposed instead Gothic aljis, 'other', making the name 'ruler of others' — a reading that the earliest spellings (with -ala-, not -alja-) and the tribal parallel weigh against, though it must be honestly reported as a live alternative. To complicate matters further, *allaz itself has no secure Indo-European cognates outside Germanic; proposals linking it to PIE *h₂el- 'all' or to *al- 'to grow, nourish' are aired, but none is undisputed, so the deep ancestry of 'all' must be left honestly open. The historical man who gave the name its resonance is Alaric I, king of the Visigoths (reigned 395–410). Of a noble Gothic house, Alaric served the empire as a federate commander and rebelled when pay was withheld, leading his people first through Greece and then into Italy. Three times he threatened or besieged Rome, and on 24 August 410 his Goths entered and sacked the eternal city — the first foreign capture of Rome in eight hundred years, an event that shattered the idea of Rome's inviolability and produced Augustine's City of God. Yet the sack was, by the standards of such things, restrained, and the testimony of Orosius is that Alaric, a Christian, ordered his men to spare those who took refuge in the churches of the apostles and to restore the consecrated vessels of St Peter's to the church. It is precisely this double face — the destroyer of Rome who yet respected its Christian God — that Macaulay exploits when he names Alaric first among the 'zealous Christians' who founded Teutonic dynasties in the provinces of the Roman empire. Alaric died at Consentia (modern Cosenza) in 410, and the legend that his followers diverted the river Busento, buried him in its bed, and then restored the stream to conceal the grave became one of the set-pieces of romantic historiography and verse, the subject of a famous ballad by August von Platen. The name's afterlife in the historical record is instructive. Jordanes, in his Getica (mid-sixth century), and the late-antique chroniclers record the form Alaricus, from which English Alaric descends directly; Gibbon and Macaulay both used the name in its plain Latin form. A second king, Alaric II (reigned 484–507), carried the name into the law and letters of the West: he commissioned the Breviarium Alaricianum, a compendium of Roman law issued in 506 that preserved the Theodosian code for the Roman subjects of his Visigothic kingdom — the last great legal monument of the collapsing Roman world in the West. Defeated and slain by Clovis at Vouillé in 507, he closed the Visigothic chapter in Gaul. Alaric, then, is a name that spans the whole tragedy of the age: 'ruler of all' in its confident etymology, it was borne by the king who sacked Rome and by the king who codified its law, and it survives in English only as a relic of a vanished Gothic world — a name whose two elements, 'all' and 'ruler', gather up the borrowed Celtic word for kingship, the contested Indo-European roots for 'all', and the rise and fall of the Visigothic kingdom in a single syllable's history. KEY STAGES IN ONE LINE
  • Sound shift: the same Celtic loan *rīg- -> *rīks as in Theodoric; the Gothic first element ala- 'all' keeps the plain a-vowel of *allaz.
  • Semantic drift: 'ruler of all' slid from literal claim of total kingship to the historical type of the barbarian conqueror and, through Alaric II, to a patron of Roman law.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the name carried dynastic ambition; its posthumous fame was shaped by Christian historiography (the restrained sack, the spared churches) and by romantic mythography (the Busento legend).
  • Literary attestations: Jordanes' Getica (6th c.) records Alaricus; the Breviarium Alaricianum (506) bears the second king's name; Gibbon and Macaulay (18th-19th c.) and Platen's ballad (19th c.) fix the modern sense.
