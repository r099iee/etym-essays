The noun verbiage denotes an excess of words — language that is wordy, redundant, or more verbose than the meaning strictly requires. It names not how something is said but how much is said, and it always carries a pejorative tinge: verbiage is the surplus of words that buries meaning rather than conveying it. In the sentence that anchors this card — "These lines prove the intensity of Mill's feeling, which is not afraid of abundant verbiage" — the word is used with a double edge: it acknowledges that the intensity of John Stuart Mill's emotion overflowed into a lavish, uncontrolled abundance of prose, while gently censuring the lack of restraint that could not imagine how that flood of words would strike others. The nuance is thus both admiring of sincerity and critical of excess: the feeling is "not afraid" of being copious, but the copiousness itself is verbiage. Core senses and shades of meaning:
  • Primary sense — excessive wordiness: the use of more words than are needed; a superfluity of words that obscures rather than clarifies meaning.
  • Concrete mass noun: the actual body of redundant words themselves, considered as a thing — "the verbiage of the report," "legal verbiage," "empty verbiage."
  • Critical, dismissive register: verbiage is almost always used with disapproval; it is the word of the editor, the reviewer, and the plain-style advocate attacking padded prose.
  • Jargon and officialese: in modern usage, "legal verbiage," "bureaucratic verbiage," and "corporate verbiage" name the opaque, puffed-up language of institutions, in which many words carry little precise meaning.
  • Neutral-technical use (linguistics and rhetoric): in some formal contexts verbiage denotes verbal expression or the wording of something without the pejorative charge — "the verbiage of the contract" — though even here the neutral sense is comparatively rare and always threatened by the dominant critical one.
  • Mannerism and padding: in literary criticism verbiage names the padding that writers insert to inflate an argument or an essay — words added to reach a length or to sound weighty, rather than to say something new.
Register, grammar, and collocations:
  • Register: formal-to-informal critical vocabulary; learned but not archaic. Verbiage is standard in editorial, journalistic, legal, and academic prose, and familiar in everyday complaint ("cut the verbiage").
  • Grammar: a mass (uncountable) noun; the plural (verbiages) is rare and jocular or technical. Typical constructions: "a mass of verbiage," "abundant verbiage," "to strip away the verbiage," "buried under verbiage."
  • Common partners: "empty verbiage," "legal verbiage," "bureaucratic verbiage," "sheer verbiage," "layers of verbiage," "needless verbiage," "verbiage-free."
  • Family and related forms in English: verbose, verbosity, word (from the same Indo-European root through Germanic), verb and verbal (from Latin verbum), adverb (ad + verbum), proverb, verbatim.
Historical and etymological bridge:
  • Verbiage entered English in the early eighteenth century, borrowed from French verbiage, which had been coined in French (c. 16th–17th century) from verbe, "word," with the suffix -iage (from Latin -āticum), which denotes a collection or quantity of something.
  • It thus literally means "a mass or heap of words," and the pejorative sense — an excessive, diffuse abundance — was native to the French word from the start.
  • The Latin source is verbum, "word, utterance, verb," from the Indo-European root *werh₁-, "to speak, say," the same root that lies behind English word (via Germanic *wurdą) and Greek rhetoric (via rhēma and rhētōr).
  • Verbiage is one of a set of French borrowings that carried a built-in criticism of language itself; unlike neutral "wordiness," it presents surplus words as a substance to be cleared away.
Synonyms in brief: verbosity (the general quality of using too many words), wordiness (the plain everyday term), prolixity (tedious drawn-out length), redundancy (unnecessary repetition), circumlocution (speaking around a subject), pleonasm (redundant expression of the same idea). Verbiage uniquely names the redundant words themselves as a disposable mass.
