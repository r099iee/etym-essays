Icicles are tapering, pointed spikes of ice that form when water drips or trickles from a roof, ledge, branch, or similar edge and freezes as it falls, growing downward into long, narrow, usually conical or spear-shaped columns. The word is almost always used in the plural — icicles — because in nature they arrive in rows and fringes; a single hanging spike is an icicle, but speakers reserve the plural for the characteristic winter scene: icicles hanging from eaves, gutters, cliffs, bridges, and twigs. Formation is a slow drip-feed: a film of meltwater creeps to the edge, freezes, thickens, and lengthens, each new drop adding a fresh ring, so a long icicle shows visible internal growth-bands and a pointed tip made by the last, smallest drops. This is the same physics as a stalactite, but with water frozen instead of mineral-laden water dripping in a cave.

Primary uses and physical descriptions. In describing winter scenery, icicles names:

  • Roof and eave icicles — the classic hanging fringe; where gutters are blocked they grow long, and their weight and falling can be a hazard (ice-dam damage, injury from falling spikes).
  • Natural icicles — from cliffs, frozen waterfalls, overhangs, and rock ledges, and the transient micro-icicles of dripping taps and car bumpers.
  • Optical and poetic icicles — clear ice acting as a lens and prism, glittering “like crystal daggers” (in Wilde’s phrase in the story) — the perception that drives nearly all literary uses of the word.

Extended and figurative senses. From the physical thing, icicle has bred a small family of secondary uses:

  • Shape-transfer — any long thin pointed hanging object is metaphorically an icicle: icicle-shaped glass pendants, icicle Christmas lights, icicle stitches in knitting and crochet (a pattern of pointed scallops), icicle nail-art.
  • Coldness of manner — an emotionally cold person is occasionally called an icicle (alongside “frosty” and “cold fish”); the metaphor is stable but rarer than “ice queen”, because icicle stresses the pointed, brittle, hanging quality as much as the cold.
  • Colour and brand use — “icicle” names a pale grey-blue or white shade in fashion (icicle blue), in cosmetics, and in frozen-dessert and cocktail imagery.
  • Scientific and technical senses — engineers and glaciologists use icicle for drip-formed ice on structures, a hazard on power lines and aircraft (where icing research studies icicle growth), and the word appears in physics as the paradigm of a growing freezing front (a Stefan problem).

Grammar and boundaries. The word is a plain countable noun: one icicle, two icicles. It should not be confused with stalactites (mineral, on cave ceilings, formed over centuries), rime or hoarfrost (ice deposited directly from vapour, not from dripping water), or glaciers (which share the Old Norse root jǫkull but are moving masses of ice, not spikes). The singular/plural contrast matters in the story: the streets “bright and glistening” with “long icicles like crystal daggers” — the plural is the picture; winter itself is pluralised into a fringe of daggers.
