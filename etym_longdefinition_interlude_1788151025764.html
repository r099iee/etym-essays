interlude (noun) — an intervening period of time, a pause or break between two events, phases, or conditions; equally, a brief piece of performance — dramatic, musical, or cinematic — inserted between the larger parts of a work. The word is built from Latin inter "between" and lūdus "play," via interlūdium, "a play or intermission between plays": the modern noun has always meant something that happens between other, larger things, and it keeps the flavor of play, lightness, or relief even when used of time.

Primary sense — an interval of time. In ordinary modern use, an interlude is a relatively short period that interrupts or separates longer stretches: "a peaceful interlude between wars," "an interlude of calm in a stormy career," "the brief interlude of ice-free warmth between glaciations" (the exact use on this card). Unlike a mere "gap" or "pause," the word suggests that the intervening period is experienced as an episode with its own character — a season of something different (peace, happiness, oddity) cut into the surrounding run of things.

Secondary senses — the performance family.

  • Theatrical sense. Historically, an interlude was a short stage piece — often comic, allegorical, or farcical — performed between the acts of a longer play, or in its own right at court or in a great house. In the medieval and Tudor periods "interlude" names a whole minor genre of English drama (fifteenth–sixteenth centuries), short moralities and comedies played by small traveling companies. The bridge from the Latin etymon is exact: inter "between" + lūdus "play" — a play between plays.
  • Musical sense. In music, an interlude is a short passage connecting larger sections — between verses of a song, between movements, or within a church service between acts of worship. The 19th-century development of symphonic and operatic interludes (the intermezzo) made the word standard in program notes, and modern popular music keeps it for short transitional or atmospheric tracks on an album.
  • Figurative and casual use. "A comic interlude," "a romantic interlude," "a strange interlude" (the title of Eugene O'Neill's 1928 play): the word slides easily into describing any brief, memorable episode in a life or narrative, whether welcome or merely odd. The connotation is nearly always brevity and change — an interlude is what happens while the main story is temporarily elsewhere.
  • Functions and register. Interlude is neutral-to-elegant, slightly bookish in the temporal sense and technical in the theatrical and musical senses; it is a count noun with a natural plural ("lyrical interludes") and combines freely with adjectives ("a quiet interlude," "a surreal interlude").

Semantic reasoning — the historical bridge. All senses flow from the single Latin construction inter "between" + lūdus "play, game." A theater interlude is literally a little play dropped between bigger plays; because such pieces were light, the word generalized to any light or brief interval in the middle of a larger sequence; because the theater borrowed the word for musical intermissions, music acquired the same terminology. The temporal sense (an interval between ice ages, wars, or seasons) is thus a late, wide generalization of what was originally a term of stagecraft — and it retains the therapeutic undertone of the original: an interlude is a space to breathe between the great works.

Grammar, distribution, and company. Count noun: "a five-minute interlude." Register: literary-neutral; in temporal use often chosen precisely to avoid the clinical flavor of "interval" or the flatness of "break." Near-synonyms: interval, intermission, intermezzo, entr'acte, pause, respite, hiatus, lull, gap, breathing space, episode, digression. Related forms: the adjective interludial (rare) and the verb to interlude (rare, of inserting interludes). The word rhymes in structure with prelude ("before the play") and postlude ("after the play"), its exact siblings in the Latin ludus family.
