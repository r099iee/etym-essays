Hastings is a town and borough on the coast of East Sussex, in south-eastern England, and one of the Cinque Ports. It is internationally famous for a battle that was not fought in the town itself: on 14 October 1066 Duke William of Normandy defeated and killed King Harold about seven miles inland, on the ridge then called Senlac and now occupied by the town of Battle. “Hastings” therefore names three things at once — a seaside town, a medieval port, and the decisive battle of English history.

The town: geography and character

Hastings occupies two valleys that open onto the Channel, divided by the headland of the East Hill and the West Hill. The old town clusters around the fishing beach and the church of St Clement; the Victorian new town spreads eastward along the seafront. The town is built where the Weald meets the sea, and its economy has always depended on that meeting.

  • The beach-launched fishing fleet — the largest of its kind in Europe; boats are still hauled up the shingle rather than moored in a harbour.
  • Cinque Port — Hastings was one of the five original head ports (with Sandwich, Dover, Hythe and Romney), obliged to supply ships and men in exchange for privileges.
  • Castle and cliffs — the ruins on West Hill, the sandstone cliffs of Fairlight and the country park above them.
  • Architecture — Regency and Victorian terraces, the pier of 1872, the East Hill lift of 1902, and the net shops: tall black wooden sheds for drying nets, unique to the town.

The battle: 14 October 1066

After landing at Pevensey on 28 September, William moved east to Hastings and fortified a camp. Harold, having defeated Harald Hardrada at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, marched south in about a fortnight and took up position on the ridge of Senlac. The English formed a shield wall; the Norman army attacked with archers, infantry and cavalry. The battle lasted from morning until dusk, and Harold was killed in the final collapse of the English line.

The name “Battle of Hastings” is a conventional label derived from the nearest town of consequence, not from the battlefield. Medieval sources call the site Senlac (“sand-stream” in Old English, though the name may be a Norman invention). Battle Abbey was founded by William on the spot, supposedly as a penance, and its high altar stood on the place where Harold’s body was found.

Medieval and early modern town

Hastings prospered as a Cinque Port in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, but the harbour silted up and the town declined. French raids in the fourteenth century destroyed much of the old town, and the silting of the Brede and the shifting of the coastline reduced the port to a fishing village. By the eighteenth century it was small, poor and smugglers’ country — the Hastings smugglers were famous enough to give the town a minor literary afterlife.

Victorian resort and modern town

The railway arrived in 1851, and Hastings reinvented itself as a seaside resort for respectable families. The pier, the promenade, the White Rock pavilion and the funicular lifts date from this period. In the twentieth century the town added a university centre, a large arts festival and a substantial fishing and tourism economy; since the 1990s it has also attracted artists and relocaters priced out of London.

Hastings beyond Sussex

The name travelled with the empire and with emigrants:

  • Hastings, New Zealand — a city in Hawke’s Bay, founded in the 1870s and named, according to local tradition, after Warren Hastings, the first Governor-General of Bengal.
  • Hastings, Minnesota — a town on the Mississippi, named after Henry Hastings Sibley, the first governor of Minnesota.
  • Hastings, Nebraska — a city and county seat, named after a railroad promoter.
  • Hastings, Barbados — a village in Christ Church parish.
  • Hastings-on-Hudson — a village in New York State.
  • The Hastings Cutoff — the shortcut across the Great Salt Lake desert promoted by Lansford Hastings, which doomed the Donner Party in 1846.

The surname and the title

Hastings is also an English surname, originally a locative name meaning “from Hastings.” Its most famous bearers include Warren Hastings (1732–1818), the first Governor-General of Bengal, whose impeachment trial in 1788–95 became a public sensation; the Marquis of Hastings; and Hastings Banda (c. 1898–1997), the first president of Malawi. The surname is unrelated to the battle except through the place.

Modern idiomatic uses

  • “The Battle of Hastings” — the standard name of 1066, used metonymically for the Norman Conquest as a whole.
  • “1066 and all that” — the battle is the central joke of the popular comic history, and “Hastings” functions as the punchline of the English schoolroom narrative.
  • “Hastings” as a heritage destination — the town markets itself as “1066 Country,” with the abbey, the battlefield and the annual re-enactment.
  • “Hastings” in insurance and law — occasional use as a case name or company name, with no connection to the town.

Semantic bridges

The word’s history is a sequence of metonymies. An Old English folk-name, “the people of Hæsta,” became a place; the place gave a convenient label to a battle fought several miles away; the battle became a synonym for the conquest; and the conquest made the name familiar to every English-speaking schoolchild. In the modern world the same name is also a New Zealand city, a Nebraska town and a surname — all derived, at however many removes, from a small Sussex settlement.
