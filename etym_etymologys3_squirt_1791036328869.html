Squirt is a textbook echo-word: its history is short, its phonetics do the work, and scholars agree that chasing it to PIE is futile. The Oxford English Dictionary first finds squirten in early-fifteenth-century medical and technical prose for ejecting liquid forcibly in a thin stream — blood squirting from a vein, water from a syringe. No Old English ancestor exists; no secure French, Norse, or Low German source has been proven, though lexicographers note look-alikes such as Middle Low German swirt- eddies or dialectal Scandinavian spurt-words. The consensus is that Middle English speakers coined an imitative cluster squ- (as in squeeze, squash, squeak) plus a burst coda -irt (as in spirt, spurt, dirt) to mime compression, constriction, and sudden release. The word sounds like what it does.

Early Modern English quickly nominalised the verb. A squirt is both the action-jet and the instrument: sixteenth-century surgeons, apothecaries, and gardeners own syringes and squirts (clyster-syringes, perfume squirts, garden squirts). Shakespeare's era knows the phrase; Ben Jonson and pamphleteers use squirt for petty pamphlets and sudden expulsions. Phonologically almost nothing changes after 1500 except the Great Vowel Shift fixing the vowel as /ɜː/ in RP (/skwɜːt/) and regular past-tense squirted replacing any strong forms that never existed — echo-words rarely develop ablaut.

  • From liquid to human: the leap from jet to junior is metaphor by size-contempt. A person likened to a squirt is small, easily expelled, quickly dismissed — flicked away like a drop. Naval and public-school slang of the early 1800s is the bridge: midshipmen, apprentice clerks, and fags called squirts or little squirts by seniors. The diminutive little becomes almost obligatory, doubling the belittlement. By late Victorian slang dictionaries squirt is glossed as a short, insignificant fellow, an urchin, a whippersnapper.
  • Socio-cultural context: hierarchical all-male institutions (navy, schools, offices, newsrooms) needed a word for juniors who presume. Squirt filled it without the criminality of punk or the class sting of guttersnipe. Interwar comic fiction — Wodehouse above all — domesticated it for country-house warfare: Bertie, Jeeves, Tuppy, and Angela trade squirt, stinker, tick, blister as soft missiles. American English added the squirt gun (water pistol), reinforcing the toy-small association.
  • Related but distinct: the marine sea-squirt (ascidian) is named for the same jet action (it squirts water when touched) but belongs to natural-history compounding, not insult. Squirt-fan, squirt-gun belli are modern compounds. The verb also developed pornographic and medical extensions in the twentieth century that standard dictionaries mark vulgar and that have no bearing on the Wodehouse sense.
  • Literary attestations: fifteenth-century surgical manuals; 1590s pamphlets (a squirt of ink); Smollett's naval squirts; Dickensian office squirts; Wodehouse's Right Ho, Jeeves and short stories where Angela is the little squirt; mid-century American little squirt as avuncular tease in film and comics.

Why does the insult stay mild? Because its phonetics stay playful. The initial skw- is laboratory-comic (squawk, squeak, squelch, squish), and the short vowel plus final -t cuts the word off like the jet it names — abrupt but tiny. To call someone a squirt is thus to perform superiority while signalling that the offence is not worth a bigger word. Angela can be a little squirt precisely because the speaker claims to be big enough to flick her away — and then, as the novel shows, discovers she flicks back.
