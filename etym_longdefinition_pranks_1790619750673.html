Pranks — plural of prank — denotes mischievous tricks, playful stunts or irreverent provocations perpetrated to amuse, embarrass, startle or subvert, ranging from schoolboy jokes to avant-garde typographic sabotage; in the present sentence Dada-Futurist assaults on print are called pranks that sought to pique not only the bourgeoisie but alphabetic reason itself.

The core modern sense is light mischief with a victim and a laugh: to play/pull a prank, phone prank, senior prank, harmless prank. Agency is playful, effect is temporary disruption, moral colour depends on scale — affectionate if consensual, cruel if humiliating. Dictionaries distinguish prank (mischievous trick) from practical joke (often overlapping) and stunt (publicity-seeking feat).

  • 1. Playful trick / practical joke: hiding objects, fake messages, whoopee cushions, April Fools; shared features are deception, surprise reveal, laughter; differs from fraud because no lasting gain, from bullying because ideally reversible and consented.
  • 2. Artistic-literary prank as provocation: Duchamp signing a urinal, Dada reading manifestos in noise, Futurists scattering type; here prank borrows schoolyard lightness to describe serious aesthetic sabotage, bridging via delight in rule-breaking. This is the activated sense: typographic pranks as epistemological jokes.
  • 3. Archaic verb/adjective: to prank up, pranked out: 16th-19th-century to prank meant to adorn, dress showily, plume oneself (“pranked in ribbons”); Shakespeare, Spenser use it; distinct etymologically but semantically suggestive — to dress up letters in showy type is literally to prank them.
  • 4. Extended: stunt, caper, antic, lark: media pranks, political pranks (Yes Men, culture-jamming), student rag-week pranks; emphasizes public performance and press coverage, bridging from private joke to collective happening.
  • 5. Negative pole: malicious trick, hazing: when pranks injure, exclude or destroy, the word retains levity that can minimize harm (“just a prank”); legal and school discourses therefore contest the label, insisting on harassment versus prank.
  • 6. Collocational range: to play, pull, stage, engineer a prank; prank call, prank video, senior prank, media prank; prankster as agent noun with admiring-mischievous tone versus trickster (mythic) and joker (verbal).

Pragmatics: calling avant-garde acts pranks both dignifies school humour and trivializes high art — a characteristic media-theory move (McLuhan, Ong) that treats technologies as toys whose misuse reveals structure. The verb to pique in the source (to irritate, to stimulate curiosity) captures prank logic: sting the bourgeois reader into noticing the medium.

Contemporary digital life has inflated the word: prank videos, prank channels, prank culture on YouTube/TikTok monetize surprise, blurring consent and cruelty, while prank as verb (“he pranked her”) newly transitives. The avant-garde sense invoked here anticipates that inflation: print pranks were viral media before networks.
