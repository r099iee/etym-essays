Primary moral-religious sense. The central and oldest sense of wicked is "evil, morally wrong, sinful, or transgressing what is right". It describes people, actions, thoughts, intentions, and institutions that are hostile to goodness or to moral and religious law: a "wicked" tyrant, a "wicked" lie, "wicked" ways. Historically the word carries an unmistakable religious charge — in the King James Bible "the wicked" names the godless, the sinful, and those who oppose the righteous, and the abstract noun wickedness is one of the Bible's key terms for sin and moral corruption. Even in secular use, wicked retains this flavour of outright, deliberate depravity and moral turpitude rather than mere error or weakness.

  • Of persons: evil-minded, malevolent, morally corrupt, unrepentant — stronger and more condemnatory than "bad", more visceral and less intellectual than "immoral" or "evil".
  • Of actions/deeds: sinful, iniquitous, heinous, deserving of condemnation ("a wicked crime", "wicked schemes"); in the fairy-tale register, cruelty and malice ("the wicked witch", "a wicked stepmother").
  • Milder/mischievous sense: from the 18th–19th centuries, a playful extension to children and mischief — "a wicked little grin", "a wicked sense of humour" — where the original condemnation is softened to roguish delight.
  • Intensifier and approval (slang): chiefly in New England (especially Boston) US English, "wicked" is an intensifier meaning "very" ("wicked cold", "wicked hard") and, in British and international youth slang, an exclamation of approval meaning "excellent, brilliant" ("that trick was wicked!”).
  • Technical "wicked problem": in design and planning theory, coined by Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber (1973), a "wicked problem" is a social or policy problem that is resistant to solution because it has no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and no testable solution — a problem so tangled that every attempted fix alters the problem itself.

Semantic bridge. The various senses of wicked are connected by a single thread: intensity and deviation from a norm. The moral sense marks a radical deviation from goodness (something violently wrong); the mischievous sense marks a charming deviation from obedience; the slang sense marks a radical deviation from the ordinary — and radical deviation, once admired rather than condemned, becomes the basis for "wicked" as an intensifier of extremes and, finally, as a term of approval. This is the same semantic "reversal" or "negative-to-positive" drift seen in English in words like bad ("bad" = good in US slang), awesome, terrific, and sick: a word denoting excess or extremes flips its evaluation while keeping its core idea of intensity.

Grammar and register. As an adjective, wicked is normally used attributively and predicatively ("a wicked man", "the act was wicked"), and it has the derived adverb wickedly and noun wickedness. In the Bostonian slang of the late 20th century it functions as a pure adverbial intensifier ("wicked awesome") and even pre-poses to adjectives in a non-standard way. In high literary and religious prose, wicked commands full moral seriousness; in the fairy-tale idiom ("wicked witch", "wicked queen") it signals archetypal villainy; and in casual youth slang it is a term of unqualified approval. No other common English adjective spans so wide a range, from Old-Testament condemnation to Boston street-corner enthusiasm.
