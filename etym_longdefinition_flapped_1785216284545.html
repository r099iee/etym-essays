Flapped (the past tense and past participle of flap) here means 'moved (something broad and flat) loosely up and down or back and forth through the air.' In the context 'He flapped his hand in the dark,' the verb is chosen with great precision: Bilbo, in pitch darkness, cannot see, so his hand does not reach, point, or feel in a directed way; it beats the emptiness like a bird's wing, cutting the air in short, flat, helpless strokes. The word's central sense is the motion of a wing, a sail, or a flag in the wind, and applying it to a human hand makes the hand itself seem wing-like — a single broad, flexible plane sweeping nothing. The tone is at once clumsy, comic-pathetic, and futile: all effort and no result, the body asserting itself blindly against empty darkness, the hand a loose blade that strikes nothing. There is no panic (that would be flailing) and no purpose (that would be waving or feeling); there is only the repetitive, flat, losing sweep of a limb in the dark. The past tense reports the whole motion as a single completed, slightly ridiculous act.

Distinguishing it from similar verbs sharpens the picture: waved is deliberate, social, and directed at someone; groped is purposeful tactile searching, the hand seeking contact; fumbled is clumsy handling of objects; beat suggests force and rhythm against something; flailed is wilder and more violent, thrashing side to side; fluttered is light, small, and tremulous. Flapped uniquely combines broadness (the whole hand as one plane), flatness (no fingers doing fine work), and looseness (no control or tension) — exactly the image of a man beating blind air, and the reason it captures a hand in the dark without implying either panic or purpose.


Polysemy and the full modern range of flapped / flap:
  • Core physical sense: to move a broad flat thing up and down or to and fro — a bird flaps its wings; a flag flaps in the wind; a sail flaps when the wind fails; a hat or cloth is flapped to fan. Flapped reports the single completed act.
  • Transitive 'strike' sense (archaic): in Middle English flappen meant 'to strike or slap'; the sense survives in the noun 'a slap, a light blow' (to give someone a flap) and in phrases such as the flap of a tail.
  • Intransitive 'flutter loosely': of thin broad things attached at one edge and free at the other — loose clothing, an earflap, a tent door — beating or swinging in moving air; this is the sense at work in 'the flag flapped in the gale.'
  • Noun senses: a flap is a broad piece attached at one edge (pocket flap, earflap, tent flap); in aeronautics an aircraft flap is a hinged surface on the wing edge that alters lift; in surgery a skin flap is living tissue moved to repair a wound; in meat-cutting the flap is a cut of flesh. Bridge: every noun sense keeps the image of a broad flat piece flexing at a pivot.
  • Figurative 'agitation' sense (chiefly British, informal): to be 'in a flap' or to 'flap' is to be in a state of agitated panic or confusion — 'don't flap!', 'he got into a flap over the exam.' Bridge: the physical loss of control in a loose, aimless flapping becomes emotional loss of composure; the wing-beat of a panicking bird is turned inward as fluster.
  • Ornithological and zoological jargon: in birding and biology, 'flap' names wing-flapping locomotion as opposed to gliding or soaring; 'flapping flight' is the technical term for powered, oscillating flight.
  • Slang and derived formations: flapjack (a pancake, a 'flapped' cake flipped in the pan) is a related formation; flapper (eighteenth century, 'a young bird; a hand or broad flat thing') became famous as 1920s slang for a fashionable, restless modern young woman — the noun for a thing that flaps, then the byword of the Jazz Age.

The word's history — from a hard blow, to a wing-beat, to a loose repeated flutter, to nervous panic — is fully available in the single past tense flapped: the sentence in the context activates the wing-sense and lets the comic-pathetic weight of the other senses hang faintly behind it. A hand that flapped is a hand that beat the air like a wing and accomplished nothing, and that is precisely the picture of helplessness in darkness that the word was made for.
