ADUMBRATES (verb, third-person singular present of adumbrate): to outline or indicate something vaguely and in advance, to cast the shadow of what is to come. In this sentence from a treatise on English metre the author declares that Part One "adumbrates some of the main arguments that follow": his first part does not yet develop those arguments — it sketches them lightly and provisionally, so that the reader glimpses their shape without yet seeing the full reasoning. The verb is chosen for its scholarly caution and for its pictorial root: it derives from Latin umbra, "shadow", so the outline is a shadow cast forward by the thing itself, suggestive and provisional rather than final — the reader is handed a silhouette, not a photograph.

The literal sense of the word is "to shade, to cast a shadow over, to outline faintly". In its oldest English use it meant to darken or shade a drawing, to fill in the shadowed parts of a sketch; a painter or engraver adumbrates a figure before adding detail. From this concrete beginning the word moved to its dominant modern senses, in which the shadow is cast not over a canvas but over the future.

  • To foreshadow or prefigure: to give an anticipatory indication of something later — the arguments are adumbrated before they are argued; the events adumbrated before they occur.
  • To outline or sketch roughly: to give the general form of a plan, theory, or policy without filling in the detail — "the report adumbrates a possible reform".
  • To indicate vaguely or partially: to disclose only a shadow of the full matter, deliberately withholding precision.
  • Rare negative sense — to overshadow, obscure, or conceal: carrying over the force of Latin adumbrāre "to cast a shadow over"; this strand never displaced the dominant senses and survives only as an archaic corner of the word's range.
  • Adumbration (noun, c. 1560): "a faint sketch or outline", also "a shadowy representation"; in philosophy and theology, "an imperfect image or type of something greater".
  • Adumbrative (adjective, rare): "serving to shadow out or foreshadow".
  • In art criticism and photography the word is occasionally revived in its literal sense, of light and shade in a drawing.

The historical bridge from "to shade" to "to foreshadow" is metaphor: a shadow thrown ahead of a moving object anticipates the object, and the same image of an outline cast forward gives the word its temporal, forward-looking force. Adumbrate collocates with abstract objects — arguments, themes, plans, policies, theories, possibilities — and almost never with concrete physical objects. Its register is strongly literary and academic: it belongs to the vocabulary of the essay, the treatise, and the lecture, and it characteristically appears in the passive or with a scholarly subject ("the chapter adumbrates the thesis"). In careful use it implies more than outline and less than exposition: the thing adumbrated is announced and sketched, but its full defence is deferred. It is a word that promises, and holds back.
