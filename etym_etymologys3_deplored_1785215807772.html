Deplored is a word of grief, and its biography begins in tears. The English verb deplore, first recorded in the mid-sixteenth century, was taken from the French deplorer, which in turn descends from the Latin compound deplorare — "to weep aloud over, bewail, lament." The Latin verb joined the intensifying prefix de- ("down, thoroughly, completely") to plorare, "to cry out, weep aloud." The ultimate source of plorare is uncertain — the modern etymological dictionaries treat it cautiously — but the traditional scholarly account derives it from the Proto-Indo-European root *pleu-, "to flow, swim, float," the image being that of tears flowing forth: the weeping person is, etymologically, someone whose sorrow overflows. Whether or not that derivation is correct, deplorare in Classical Latin meant precisely to pour out grief over someone lost, and it was a strong word, reserved for the inconsolable; Cicero can deplorare the death of a friend, and the phrase deplorata res est, "the matter is wept over," came to mean "the case is hopeless." The root *pleu- had a broad family that shows how much of European vocabulary floats on the same current: in the Indic branch, Sanskrit plavate, "he floats, swims," and pluta, "flooded"; in Greek, pleo, "I sail," the sea-borne cousin of the flowing root; in the Germanic languages, the words for flowing water and drifting motion — Old English flowan, our flow; flod, our flood; Old English flotian and Old Norse fljota, our float; German fliessen, "to flow," with fliegen, "to fly." Latin itself kept the water-image in pluere, "to rain," whence pluvial and pluviometer. If plorare belongs to this root, it is the "flowing" of the voice in weeping, and deplorare is that weeping poured out completely.

The word crossed into English in the sixteenth century through French — deplorer in Old and Middle French, attested from the eleventh and twelfth centuries with the sense "to lament, mourn" — and for the first three centuries of its English life it kept its full classical gravity: to deplore was to grieve deeply and openly, often to mourn the dead. This is the sense in which John Stuart Mill used it in the dedication of On Liberty (1859). Harriet Taylor Mill, his wife and intellectual companion, died in Avignon in 1858; Mill's dedication to her memory — "the beloved and deplored memory of her who was the inspirer, and in part the author, of all that is best in my writings" — is a monument of Victorian elegiac prose. The participle carries the whole ceremony: not mere regret, but active, flowing mourning for the person he called both "friend and wife," and the word's Latin weight is precisely what makes the sentence feel monumental rather than sentimental. Mill chose the most sorrowful verb available, and the choice is significant: in the mid-nineteenth century deplore still meant, first and foremost, to weep for.

Even as Mill wrote, however, the word was shifting. The same verb that could deplore a death could already deplore a vice, and across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the censure sense — "to disapprove of strongly, to condemn" — grew until it became the dominant meaning. A twentieth-century statesman deplores an act of violence, an editorial deplores corruption, a committee deplores the state of affairs: the tears have dried and the word has become a formal, public instrument of moral reproach, sorrowful rather than weeping, judgmental rather than elegiac. The noun deplorability and the adjective deplorable followed the same path: deplorable meant "lamentable, to be deplored" in the eighteenth century, and in modern colloquial use it has hardened further into a word for the shameful and disgraceful ("deplorable behaviour," "a deplorable condition"). Today the mourning sense survives mainly in literary or archaic phrasing — in elegies, dedications, and historical fiction — while the condemnatory sense rules the daily paper. Mill's sentence therefore reads, to a modern ear, with a double resonance: the primary sense is the classical one of tears, but the word's modern life as an instrument of moral judgment lies just behind it, and in this particular dedication the two converge — he mourns her and, in mourning, enshrines her as the source of his values, deploring (in the moral sense) the loss that grief alone cannot heal. A word that began as the overflowing of tears has become a formal gesture of public regret, and the whole drift is compressed in the participle on the dedication page: deplored, wept over, and — in the moral register — found irreplaceable.
