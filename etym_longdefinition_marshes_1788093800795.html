Marshes — the plural of marsh — are tracts of low-lying land that are soft, wet, and at least seasonally waterlogged, typically covered with grasses, rushes, sedges, and reeds rather than trees, and often treacherous underfoot. The plural is the natural form when speaking of wetland regions (a chain of marshes along a river, tidal marshes around an estuary), and that is exactly how Tolkien uses it: the fugitive goblins of the Battle of Five Armies are hunted "into the marshes about the Forest River", where the greater part of them perish in the trackless wet.

Core meaning:

  • A marsh is a wetland dominated by herbaceous (non-woody) plants, with water at or near the surface for long periods; it stands between dry land and open water.
  • Hydrology: marshes are fed and refreshed by groundwater, rivers, tides, or surface runoff, which keeps their water mineral-rich — a key difference from rain-fed acidic bogs.
  • Ground conditions: soft mud, standing pools, dense reeds; unpredictable footing, hidden channels, and floods — historically the terror and the refuge of travellers.

Types of marsh (scientific subdivision):

  • Freshwater marshes: inland, along rivers and lakes; rich in reeds, sedges, and bird life.
  • Salt marshes (also "saltings"): tidal coastal wetlands, flooded by saline water twice a day; zoned from mudflat to upper marsh; vital nurseries for fish and wading birds.
  • Tidal freshwater marshes: estuaries where the tide checks the river but salinity stays low.
  • Regional variants: prairie potholes, bayous, everglades — family members of the same waterlogged character.

Everyday and technical senses and compounds — the word radiates into many domains:

  • Marsh gas: methane produced by rotting vegetation under anaerobic marsh water; the gas Alessandro Volta collected bubbling from the marshes of Lake Maggiore in 1776, later identified as methane.
  • Marsh fever: the old English name for malaria, so called because the disease was believed to rise from the miasmas ("bad air") of marshes; the Italian "mal'aria" carried exactly the same theory.
  • Marsh mallow — and the marshmallow!: the plant Althaea officinalis, Old English mersc-mealwe, "marsh-mallow", grows in marshes; its mucilaginous root was boiled with sugar to make a soothing sweet — the modern marshmallow candy keeps the name though it no longer contains the plant.
  • Marsh harrier, marsh marigold, marsh orchid, marsh warbler: species named for their habitat; the compounds mark the marsh as a distinct, study-worthy ecosystem.
  • Figurative use: "a marsh of detail", "to slog through the marshes" of bureaucracy — waterlogged, progress-swallowing ground as metaphor for obstruction.
  • Proper nouns: the Mesopotamian Marshes of Iraq (traditional homeland of the Marsh Arabs, largely drained in the 1990s and partially restored after 2003), the marshes of the Camargue, the Norfolk Broads, and Dickens's "marsh country" of Kent, where Great Expectations begins and ends.

History of human perception:

  • For centuries marshes were classed as waste — unploughable, feverish, and inhabited only by outlaws and birds; kings and monasteries sponsored drainage to convert them to pasture and arable land.
  • Modern ecology reversed the verdict: marshes are among the most productive habitats on Earth, filtering water, buffering floods and storms, storing carbon, and nursering fish — so "the marshes" are now protected, restored, and celebrated rather than drained.

Summary: MARSHES = waterlogged, soft, reed-grown wetland tracts — ecological treasure that ordinary language long treated as waste and danger.
