biff is an informal, chiefly British (originally American) verb meaning to hit, punch, or strike someone or something sharply, usually with the fist, hand, or a blunt object, and typically once, quickly, and with audible force. "I'll biff you where you stand" is a comic threat meaning "I'll hit you right here and now, on the spot". The word conveys a short, smart blow rather than a prolonged beating — a single whack delivered in irritation or jest.

The core image is acoustic: biff sounds like the blow it names. It belongs to the family of echoic impact words — bash, bang, bonk, whack, thump, wallop — and shares their playground and comic-strip flavour. It is less brutal than smash or batter, less technical than punch or strike, and funnier than all of them. A mother biffs a cheeky child on the arm, a schoolboy biffs a ball over the fence, Bertie Wooster is threatened with being biffed for tutting. The direct object is the person or thing hit; the instrument and location can be added ("biff on the nose / jaw / head"). Past and participle are regular: biffed, biffing.

  • Primary sense — to punch or slap: to deliver a blow with fist or open hand. Example: "He biffed him in the jaw." The bridge from sound to action is onomatopoeia: the cry "Biff!" uttered as a blow lands becomes the verb for landing it. Force is moderate; intent ranges from playful to angry, but the word itself keeps it light.
  • Sporting extension — to hit a ball hard: in cricket and golf slang, to biff means to strike the ball vigorously, often without finesse. Example: "He biffed it to the boundary." Here accuracy yields to power; a "biffer" is a lusty hitter. The same sound-image motivates the shift from hitting people to hitting balls.
  • Noun sense — a blow: a biff = a whack or punch ("a biff on the nose", attested 1881). This nominalisation lets the word serve as threat or report: "Give him a biff" = hit him once. Australian slang extends it to dismissal ("to get the biff" = be sacked), as if struck off a list.
  • Figurative and dated senses: Green's records a rare "to kill, murder" extension in early American underworld use, now obsolete. Modern euphemistic "biffy" (toilet) and acronymic BIFF (film festivals) are unrelated homographs and must not be confused.
  • Register and connotation: informal, jocular-menacing, schoolboy and services slang. Unlike neutral "hit", "biff" miniaturises violence into farce — ideal for Wodehouse, where Aunt Dahlia's threat is funny precisely because "biff" makes assault sound like a comic sound effect. It would be out of place in police reports or medical notes, where "strike" or "assault" is required.

Grammatically simple and transitive, biff contrasts with near-synonyms by sound and weight: punch is the neutral fist-blow; smack suggests open hand and noise; thump is heavier and duller; wallop is bigger and more painful; biff is quick, sharp, and faintly comic. Learners should hear the word as its own sound effect: say "BIFF!" with a clap — that clap is the meaning.
