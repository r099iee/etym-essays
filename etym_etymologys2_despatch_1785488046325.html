1. PIE *ped- "foot" (reconstructed; 5th–3rd millennium BCE) — the root of "foot" in nearly every Indo-European branch (Greek poús, Latin pēs, Germanic *fōt-). 2. Latin pēs, pedis "foot" (attested from c. 200 BCE). 3. Latin pedica "a fetter, a foot-shackle" (attested from c. 100 BCE) — a snare or hobble for the feet. 4. Vulgar Latin *dispedicāre "to free from a fetter, to unshackle" — dis- "apart, away" + pedica (reconstructed; unattested, but the inferred ancestor of the Romance verbs of haste and sending). 5. Old Italian dispacciare "to send off hastily, to speed along" (13th century) — the relay most scholars favour; sibling reflexes include Old Spanish despachar "to expedite, send off" and Old French despeechier "to hurry, make haste". 6. Middle French despescher "to hurry, to dispatch, to send off quickly" (14th century), from the same Romance stock (forms with and without the -d- competed). 7. English dispatch "to send off with haste; to settle business promptly" (early 16th century, c. 1510), with the variant spelling despatch (influenced by French despescher) — hence the noun "promptness, expedition" (16th century) → English DESPATCH (this card). Note on debate: the OED marks the word's origin "uncertain". The Romance intermediate is disputed: some derive English dispatch from Italian dispacciare, some from Spanish despachar, and a minority derive it from Latin expeditāre "to set free, to expedite" (from expedīre). All routes agree on the core image — releasing the feet from a fetter — but the precise relay, and whether the -d- and -sp- forms are one family or two, is not settled. The derivation from pedica is the mainstream view; the expeditāre route remains a minority theory.
