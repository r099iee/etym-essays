SLABS is of obscure, probably Scandinavian origin, with no reconstructable Proto-Indo-European etymon. Its earliest senses cluster around wet, slippery matter (slush, mud), which later transferred to broad flat surfaces. The probable source is an expressive Germanic base, and every member of the family is best treated as a sound-symbolic creation rather than an inherited word. Stage 1 — Expressive Germanic base *slabb- "wet, slippery, slimy stuff" (sound-symbolic; sl- marks wetness and flatness): reconstructed from the Scandinavian and Low German evidence below; no fixed date, and no Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed. Stage 2 — Sister expressive forms (attested, parallel, not ancestors): Norwegian slabbe "slush, mud, a splash of wet stuff"; Swedish dialect slabbe "slippery, slimy"; Middle Low German slabben "to dabble, soil"; Middle Dutch slabben "to soil, splash"; English slabber / slobber "to wet with spittle", borrowed from the same stock. Stage 3 — Middle English slabbe (c. 1300) "a large flat piece of timber, especially the rough outer slice of a log left when it is squared": the first English record of the word, already in the flat-mass sense, with the wet sense surviving only as dialect. Stage 4 — English slab (14th century) "a thick flat piece of timber", then "a thick flat piece of stone" (building sense): paving slabs, stone slabs, a tombstone slab; the concrete slab appears in the 20th century. Stage 5 — Transferred and extended senses (16th–19th centuries): the dialectal adjective slab "thick, viscous, gluey" (16th century) preserving the old slimy sense; transferred uses of "slab of bread / chocolate / meat" (19th century); the mortuary sense "the cold slab" (19th century); geological and tectonic slab (20th century). Stage 6 — English slabs (plural — the exact form of this card). DEBATED AND NUANCED POINTS: The etymology is uncertain by the admission of the dictionaries; the OED keeps the origin open, and no Indo-European root can be honestly reconstructed. The flat-stone sense is a transfer within English from the idea of a broad, flat, spreading mass. German schlabbern "to slobber" and the English sl- family of wet, soft, spreading matter (slop, slush, slurry, slime) are sister expressive creations, not ancestors. No genuine cognates exist in Sanskrit, Hindi, Latin, or Greek.
