PIE *sel- / *sl̥- "to jump, to leap, to spring" (reconstructed root of springing/jumping motion; the precise laryngeal shape is debated; c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE) → Latin salīre "to leap, to jump, to spring" (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE; also the base of saltus "a leap" and the frequentative saltāre "to dance, leap about") → Latin dēsilīre "to leap down or off" (prefix dē- "down, off" + salīre; attested in Classical Latin) → Latin dēsultor "a circus rider who leaps from one galloping horse to another" (agent noun from dēsilīre; attested in Classical Latin, e.g. in Pliny and Suetonius) → Latin dēsultōrius "of or pertaining to a desultor; hence skipping about, inconstant, superficial" (adjectival formation with -ōrius; attested in Classical Latin) → English desultory "lacking a plan, purpose, or continuous course; jumping from one thing to another" (borrowed from Latin in the late 16th century).

Note on the debate/nuance: The PIE root of Latin salīre is usually reconstructed as *sel- "to leap, jump," but the exact vocalism and laryngeal details are debated by authorities, and some connect the family differently; the Latin chain from salīre onward, however, is securely attested at every stage. The Sanskrit line (e.g. sṛ- "to run, flow, move") is sometimes compared to the Latin leap-family, but the exact equation is debated and should be cited only with caution; English desultory is a direct Latin loan, not a loan from any Indo-Aryan form.
