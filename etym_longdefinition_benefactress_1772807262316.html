When the speaker tells Yu-cun that the enslaved Ying-lian is his “great benefactress,” the feminine noun works with deliberate shock and irony. A benefactress is a woman who confers good—money, patronage, rescue—on a person or cause. Here the good was done not by the girl herself in her present misery, but through her father Zhen Shi-yin, who had once supported the penniless student Yu-cun. Calling Ying-lian the benefactress collapses father and daughter into a single line of grace: she is the living reminder of the kindness that made Yu-cun’s career possible. The feminine form sharpens both the pathos and the moral claim—this helpless girl is, legally and emotionally, the human face of Yu-cun’s old debt. The tone is formal, almost ceremonial (“great benefactress”), and heavily ironic against her status as a purchased slave. Core meaning. A benefactress is a woman who gives money, gifts, or practical help to a person, institution, or cause. It is the feminine counterpart of benefactor and, in strict morphology, simply “benefactor” plus the feminine suffix -ess: “a female doer of good.” The word emphasizes active conferral of benefit rather than mere goodwill; a benefactress is someone whose support has tangible results—a building endowed, a student saved, a charity kept alive. The good she does is real, remembered, and often bound up in a relation of gratitude and obligation. Etymological and conceptual bridge. The word is built from Latin bene “well” and facere “to do, make”: a benefactor (and so a benefactress) is literally one who does well, one whose deeds are good. The historical bridge from “doing well” to “giving money” runs through patronage: in a gift-economy world the most obvious way of doing good for another person is to support them materially. Benefactor thus slides from “one who acts well” to “one who bestows benefits,” and from the medieval endowment of churches and the early-modern patronage of scholars to the modern donor of hospitals, libraries, and the arts. The feminine suffix -ess (Greek -issa → Late Latin -issa → Old French -esse → English -ess) marks the giver as a woman. Polysemy and secondary senses (exhaustive):
  • Core sense: a woman who gives money or practical help to a person or cause; “the benefactress of the village school.”
  • Institutional or philanthropic sense: a female donor or patron of a charity, hospital, school, university, or the arts; often with of (benefactress of the arts).
  • Personal and private sense: a woman who rescues or supports a particular individual—the sense of the passage, where the “benefit” is a past kindness remembered as a debt.
  • Historical and ecclesiastical sense: a female founder or endower of a religious house, chantry, or church (medieval and early modern usage).
  • Transferred or ironic sense: anyone, even one who never gave a coin, whose existence constitutes a benefit to another—the rhetorical revaluation at work in the Yu-cun recognition scene.
  • Morphological or linguistic sense: benefactress exemplifies the productive English feminine agent suffix -ess (cf. actress, hostess, heiress, enchantress, patroness).
  • Modern usage note: since the later twentieth century the -ess forms have declined in fashion; benefactress now sounds dated and literary, and neutral modern prose prefers “female benefactor,” “patron,” or simply “benefactor.”
Register and collocations. Formal, historical, and literary. Common collocations: great / generous / anonymous benefactress; benefactress of the arts / of a school / of the poor; to act as benefactress to … . In this passage the word revalues Ying-lian: not a disposable commodity, but the daughter of the man who “did well” by Yu-cun—and therefore someone to whom honour still owes a return. The near-synonyms (benefactor, patron / patroness, donor, philanthropist, saviour, rescuer, sponsor) draw the lines of gender, agency, register, and scale, and are set out fully in the similarWords list.
