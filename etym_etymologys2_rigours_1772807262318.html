Chain for the single root word “rigours” (plural of rigour): - PIE *reig- / *reik- “to stretch, be stiff, be tight” (reconstructed; the exact vocalism is debated between *reig- and *reik-; the Germanic branch gives Proto-Germanic *raikjaną “to stretch out, reach” → Old English rǣcan → English reach, and German reichen “to reach, suffice”). - Latin rigēre “to be stiff, numb, rigid” (attested classical; from the same “stretch/tighten” family). - Latin rigor “stiffness, hardness, numbness (from cold); severity, inflexibility” (attested classical; e.g. rigor hiemis “the stiffness of winter”). - Latin phrase rigor mortis “stiffness of death” (medical Latin; borrowed into English c. 19th century as a fixed term). - Old French rigor / rigour “harshness, severity” (12th–13th century). - Middle English rigour “severity, harshness, strictness” (14th century). - Modern English rigour (British spelling) / rigor (American spelling) “severity; exactness, thoroughness” (16th century onward). - Plural rigours “harsh, demanding conditions” (17th century onward; the form in this note). Sisters: Latin rigidus “stiff, hard” → English rigid, rigidity; Latin rigorōsus → English rigorous. Note: the proposed PIE root *reig- “stretch” also underlies Germanic reach; the reconstruction is standard in the handbooks, but the exact root vocalism (i versus e) is debated, and an exact Indo-Aryan cognate of rigor is not securely attested—do not invent a Sanskrit twin.
