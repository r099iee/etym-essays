1. PIE *men- 'to stand out, project, rise' (Pokorny *men- 'hervorragen'; the root of prominences and heights). Note — homonym guard: a separate PIE *men- 'to think, have in mind' gave Latin mens, English mind, mental, mention, comment; do not merge the two roots. 2. Italic *mont- 'a projecting mass, a mountain' (the root with a -t- extension; the Celtic cognates Welsh mynydd, Cornish meneth, Breton menez confirm PIE antiquity). 3. Latin mons, montis 'mountain' (attested from Plautus onward, 3rd–2nd c. BCE). 4. Vulgar Latin *montāre 'to climb, go up a mountain' (a verb coined from mons, mont-; attested indirectly through Romance and Romance loans). 5. Old French monter 'to go up, ascend, rise, climb onto' (attested from the 10th–11th c.). 6. Middle English mounten 'to ascend, climb, rise' (13th–14th c., via Anglo-French). 7. English mount (verb: 'mount a horse', 'mount the stairs'; noun: 'Mount Everest') → mounting (present participle / verbal noun). 8. English mountings (plural verbal noun — 'mountings of the mind', Wordsworth's exact form in The Prelude, 1805). Debated nuance: the reconstruction of the PIE root as *men- 'to stand out' is standard but the -t- extension seen in *mont- is an Italo-Celtic shared innovation whose precise function is debated; the consensus is that Latin mons and Celtic *moni- both continue the same root, making 'projecting mass = mountain' a formation as old as the family itself.
