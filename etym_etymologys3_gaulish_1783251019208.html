The word Gaulish is a late English formation — Gaul plus the ancient suffix -ish — and behind that modest surface lie two entirely different names that European history has rolled into one. The classical name is Latin: the Romans called the country Gallia and its people Galli, a name whose own origin the Romans had forgotten and modern scholars can only propose. The common proposal connects Galli with a Celtic word for power and valour — Old Irish gal ‘warlike ardour,’ Welsh gallu ‘ability, power,’ Gaulish compounds in -galos ‘having power’ — making the Gauls “the mighty ones”; but the evidence is circumstantial and the derivation must be labelled a theory, not a fact. Whatever its origin, Gallia and Gallic gave Europe its learned, Latinizing names: the “Gallic Wars” of Caesar, the Gallo-Roman civilization, the adjective gaulois in French, and the celebrated pun by which the Gallic cock (gallus in Latin) became the emblem of France. But the English word Gaul does not actually descend from Latin Gallia. It comes from French Gaule, and Gaule is of Germanic origin — a word the Franks brought with them. Deep beneath it lies a Celtic tribal name, Volcae, the Gallic confederacy of southern Gaul, whose warriors the Germanic peoples came to know first along the Rhine; the Germanic tongues generalized this single tribal name into *Walhaz, “a foreigner,” the word for any Celt or Roman who was not of the Germanic world. From *Walhaz flowed an astonishing family. Old English wealh named the Briton, and gave English Wales, Welsh, and Cornwall (the “Britons of the horn”); the same root hides in walnut — the “foreign nut,” imported from the Celtic/Roman world — in Walloon and Wallachia, and in German welsch, still used for Romance speakers. Among the Franks the word was reshaped: Germanic w- hardened to gu- in Gallo-Romance, and so Frankish *Wālha became Old French Gaule, the name of the land of the Gauls. English borrowed Gaule in the fifteenth century as Gaul, and from the early seventeenth century attached the native suffix -ish (Old English -isc, from Proto-Germanic *-iskaz, from Proto-Indo-European *-isko-, the same suffix as Greek -iskos and Latin -iscus) to form the adjective Gaulish, “of the Gauls,” and then, by natural extension, the name of their language. The word arrived just in time for the modern discovery of that language. Although the Gauls had been fully Romanized by the 6th century AD — the language died out under the pressure of Latin — they had not been silent: Gaulish speakers left inscriptions from the 3rd century BC onward, written in the Greek alphabet in southern Gaul (the Massalia region) and in the Latin and Old Italic alphabets farther north. The modern reconstruction of Gaulish — the science of the Coligny calendar, the Larzac and Chamalières lead tablets, the pottery of La Graufesenque — has given Gaulish its richest sense: a language of hundreds of votive, funerary, and administrative texts, with a calendar, numerals, personal names, and religious vocabulary. The word's semantic career tracks the fate of its referent. For the Romans, the Galli were a formidable but barbarous enemy, defeated by Caesar and Romanized; Gallic accordingly carried a tinge of the otherness Rome conquered. In the medieval and early modern period, French learned tradition kept gaulois for the ancient and legendary past — the Gauls as the supposed ancestors of the French nation — while English borrowed Gaul and Gaulish for the historical peoples and, later, their language. In the nineteenth century, comparative philology gave Gaulish a precise scientific home: the Continental Celtic language, sister to Celtiberian and the ancestor of nothing, but the substratum that left its mark on French place-names (Paris from the Parisii, Lyon from Lugdunum) and on French vocabulary. Today the word stands in the source sentence as the emblem of a lost but recoverable voice — “vibrant, complex” — the native tongue of France that Latin displaced and scholarship has partly brought back, a small English compound of a Germanic “foreigner” word and a PIE suffix carrying three millennia of European linguistic history.
