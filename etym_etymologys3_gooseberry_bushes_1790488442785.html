A. GOOSEBERRY — the fruit and bush name. English gooseberry is first recorded in the 1530s for the thorny northern fruit and by extension its bush. The transparent reading is goose + berry: goose from Old English gos, from Proto-Germanic *gans, from PIE *ghans- "goose" (cognate with Latin anser, Greek khen, Sanskrit hamsa, Old Irish geiss), with regular voicing and vowel shift *gans to gos to goose /guːs/; plus berry from Old English berie, from Proto-Germanic *bazja- (Old Norse ber, Old High German beri, German Beere), of uncertain deeper origin with no secure PIE root. OED editors defend this literal analysis: plant names yoking animals and fruits — cranberry (crane-berry), gooseberry, wolfsbane, horse-chestnut — are commonly inexplicable, and want of appropriateness (geese do not eat the berry specially) is no ground to reject it.

The rival theory treats goose- as folk-etymology. Middle Dutch has croesel for gooseberry, German Krausebeere / Krauselbeere for crisped berry, from kraus "crisp, curly, rough", describing the hairy, veined fruit. Old French grosele / grosele (modern groseille) for redcurrant-gooseberry, latinised as grossularia, is itself from Germanic by Watkins' account. On this view English sailors or gardeners heard groser / croesel / kraus- and refashioned it as familiar goose-, just as sparrow-grass refashions asparagus. Skeat (1898) and Klein derive the whole kr-/cr- cluster from a Germanic base meaning "to bend, curl; bent, crooked; rounded mass". OUPblog (Liberman, 2009) leaves the question open, noting parallels like cranberry and the merging of geese, fools, and devils in gooseberry fool and Old Gooseberry (devil, 1797). The responsible statement is therefore: attested English form from 1530s is goose + berry; whether goose- continues PIE *ghans- or refashions continental kraus-/gros- is debated, with OED favouring the former and continental dictionaries the latter.

  • Phonetics and cultivation: goose /guːs/ + berry /ˈberi/ compress to /ˈɡuːsbəri/ (northern /ˈɡʊzbəri/), plural gooseberries, attributive gooseberry-bush, gooseberry pie, gooseberry season. Tudor herbalists list it among kitchen fruits; 18th-century Lancashire weavers breed hundreds of show kinds named old rough red and hairy amber; Victorian manuals praise northern cool for flavour. The name never leaves the garden: no gooseberry becomes aristocratic.
  • Idiom growth: to play gooseberry (third wheel) from early 1800s, gooseberry fool (cream pudding, 1719), Old Gooseberry (devil, 1796), gooseberry eyes and gooseberry season (silly-season news) follow. Each exploits hairiness, greenness, and odd-one-out status. If goosebury (goose-place, bury as manor) underlies Old Gooseberry as Liberman playfully suggests, the devil is Old Fool of Goose-place — fitting for a taboo name, but unproven.

B. BUSH / BUSHES — the shrub word. English bush (plural bushes) is of multiple origins, all Germanic. OED distinguishes an inherited Old English noun *busc / *bysce (unattested except in place-names and surnames: wiðibuscemære 1020, atte Busse 1275, Bushey from *bysce) and an early Scandinavian borrowing *buskr (Old Icelandic buskr as nickname, buski bush, Old Swedish buske, Danish busk), both cognate with West Frisian bosk, Dutch bosch / bos, Old Saxon busk (in bramalbusk bramble-bush), Old High German busc / bosc, German Busch, from West Germanic *busk- "bush, thicket, wood". Danelaw place-names (Busby 1086 from Buschebi, Gowbusk, Goatbusk) prove northern busk- with /sk/, southern names prove busc with /ʃ/ after assibilation. Middle English merges them as busk, bush, bussh (c. 1200) meaning many-stemmed woody plant smaller than a tree.

Further origin beyond West Germanic is uncertain and disputed. Post-classical Latin has boscus / boscum "wood, woodland, firewood" (9th c., frequent 11th c., giving Italian bosco, Spanish bosque, French bois), clearly related but direction debated: older handbooks had Germanic borrow from Latin, recent work has Latin borrow a Germanic *busk-, perhaps extended from the IE base of be (to swell) or a substrate word. De Vaan lists no secure PIE. Pronunciation /bʊʃ/ with plural /ˈbʊʃɪz/ is regular. The compound gooseberry-bush (bush specified by fruit) is 18th-century garden English; plural -es after sibilant is regular. Ruskin's garden full of gooseberry-bushes thus joins a debated goose-fruit to a debated bush-wood, both homely, both northern, both secure from 1500s whatever their depths.

  • Garden economy and show culture: by 1800 Lancashire cotton towns stage annual gooseberry shows weighing berries to drams and grains, with printed lists of prize kinds and growers; cottagers prune to single stems, mulch with manure, net against birds, and bottle green berries for winter. The bush thus carries industrial working-class pride, not gentry leisure. Perth manse and Bridge-end town gardens share the same thrift: rows sloping to water, children set to picking, jam-pots lined in larders.
  • Bush plural and hyphen mechanics: bush + -es gives bushes /bUshiz/ by regular sibilant plural; hyphen in gooseberry-bushes marks attributive compound before pluralisation, distinguishing fruit-bushes from mere bushes. Victorian seed catalogues alternate gooseberry bushes open and gooseberry-bushes hyphened; modern style prefers open or single word, but Ruskin-era hyphens stress the unit. Plural rows, not single specimen, signal fullness.

Both halves therefore meet in thorns: gooseberry thorns scratch pickers, bush thickets hide game and lovers, and garden rows discipline both into jam. The compound plural remembers labour — planting, pruning, picking, cooking — in one breath.
