Undergrowth names the crowded low layer of a wood: the brambles, ferns, saplings, and shrubs that grow beneath the tall canopy, tangling ankles and hiding ground where high boughs admit dappled light. In the source sentence it appears by stark absence: there was no undergrowth, the forest floor lying clean and open, a sign — to experienced walkers — either of deep shade, of grazing beasts, or of a wood kept strange and swept by powers that do not allow the usual tangle to rise.

Core meaning in detail: Every mature wood has vertical storeys: canopy above, shrub layer in the middle, field layer of herbs at knee height, and moss at foot level. Undergrowth is the general name for all the woody and half-woody tangle below head height but above the leaf litter: hazel coppice, blackthorn suckers, bramble arches, holly clumps, young ash and oak waiting for a gap in the light. It shelters nesting birds, litters cubs, slows walkers, and feeds deer. Its absence, as Tolkien notes, is as eloquent as its presence: a wood with no undergrowth feels pillared, hollow, and watchful, sound travelling far, movement exposed, the walkers suddenly visible to whatever watches between the stems.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Dense low vegetation beneath tall trees (primary sense, from the 18th c., the source sense): to push through undergrowth, to clear undergrowth, a nightingale singing in the undergrowth. The bridge is direct composition: what grows under the growth that overtops it, the lower storey defined by the upper.
  • Anything low, subordinate, and tangled that thrives beneath a dominant overstorey (figurative): the undergrowth of a city, of bureaucracy, of rumour. The bridge carries the woodland structure into society: as saplings jostle dimly below oaks, so minor officials, minor villains, and minor gossip thrive dimly below great powers, dense, hard to clear, and persistent.
  • Unwanted hair or secondary growth on the body (colloquial extension): the undergrowth of a beard, to trim the undergrowth. The bridge is bodily humour: thick hair sprouting beneath the styled surface is likened to brambles beneath beeches, and barbers borrow the forester word with a wink.
  • Young regeneration and coppice shoots (forestry technical): to manage undergrowth for pheasants, for hazel, for firewood. The bridge narrows the general tangle to its useful part: the cut-and-come-again shoots that foresters tend on rotation, ecology as economy.
  • Obscurity and neglect made visible (literary): gardens run to undergrowth, minds run to undergrowth. The bridge is moralised neglect: what is uncut, unweeded, and unlit grows tangled, whether in acres or in souls, and writers from Austen to Golding use the thicket as shorthand for what happens when care withdraws.

Register and connotation: undergrowth is practical and slightly ominous, the gamekeeper and infantry word rather than the flower book word. It promises difficulty: thorns, ticks, hidden ditches, and things that move unseen. Clean woods feel safe and eerie by turns, but woods thick with undergrowth feel alive and resistant, hiding both refuge and ambush. Tolkien uses the absence to unnerve: no undergrowth means no cover, no softening, no familiar woodland mess, only tall stems and open sightlines where walkers cannot hide.
