- Ancient World to Middle Ages (Pre-history) “ludus duodecim scriptorum and tables” - Romans played on twelve-line boards; medieval Europe played *tables* (French *tables*, Old English *tæfl*), the direct aristocratic predecessor without modern name.
- 1630s–1640s “coining of back-gammon” - the compound *back-gammon* appeared in Early Modern English to denote a specific new variation of tables featuring hitting and forced return of checkers.
- 18th c. “gentlemen’s codification” - Edmond Hoyle published *A Short Treatise on the Game of Back-Gammon* (1743), establishing standardized tournament rules and elevating it to a quintessential gentlemanly intellectual pursuit.
- 19th c. to early 20th c. “drawing-room domesticity” - entrenched as the staple domestic pastime of English country houses (the Wodehouse world), where respectable relatives passed evenings by the hearth.
- 1920s–1970s “doubling cube and international craze” - the invention of the doubling cube in 1920s New York ignited high-stakes gambling; the 1970s saw a massive global tournament boom among the international jet set.
- Present “digital and global classic” - enjoyed worldwide both as a physical heirloom pastime in parlors and cafes and as a thriving online competitive game driven by sophisticated AI neural net engines (e.g., Extreme Gammon).
