- 16th c. (Tudor Era, c. 1520s–1570s) — Emerged in Early Modern English as piddle ("to pick at food squeamishly, eat without appetite"); piddling arose soon after to describe trivial, squeamish, or petty behavior.
- 17th c. (Jacobean & Restoration) — Expanded into literary and academic satire, applied scathingly to pedantic critics, minor poets, and trifling scholars who fussed over irrelevancies.
- 18th c. (Augustan Era) — Deployed by Alexander Pope and Jonathan Swift to mock contemptibly small financial amounts and petty intellectual disputes; nursery sense of "piddle" (to urinate lightly) developed alongside.
- 19th c. (Victorian Era) — Consolidated in both British and American colloquial speech as a favorite rhetorical adjective for paltry budgets, minor administrative squabbles, and negligible gains.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains its pungent rhetorical vitality in cultural commentary, journalism, and philosophy to denounce superficial tinkering that avoids confronting core structural crises.
