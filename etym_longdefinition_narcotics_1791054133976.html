narcotics (plural noun, singular narcotic) most strictly denotes drugs that induce stupor, numbness, and sleep while relieving pain — prototypically opioids such as opium, morphine, heroin, codeine, and fentanyl. In the source sentence it is used in an older, broader pharmacological sense: plant preparations thrown into water to benumb fish. In loose modern legal and journalistic use it means any illicit or controlled drug, including stimulants that are not pharmacologically narcotic.

Exhaustive senses in current English:

  • Strict pharmacological class (core sense): Opioid analgesics that depress the central nervous system, cause drowsiness, constrict pupils, slow breathing, and risk dependence. Hospitals use narcotics for surgical and cancer pain under strict control. Example: Morphine is a powerful narcotic.
  • Legal / law-enforcement category (extended sense): Any drug listed as controlled or illegal — heroin, cocaine, methamphetamine, cannabis in prohibitionist jurisdictions — as in narcotics squad, narcotics trafficking, narcotics conviction. This is technically loose (cocaine stimulates rather than stupefies) but dominant in headlines and statutes such as the U.S. Narcotics Control Act.
  • General stupefacient (historical / ethnobotanical sense): Any stupefying substance, including fish poisons (piscicides), as in the Tahitian passage. Nineteenth-century travel writers freely called Derris root, Barringtonia nuts, and mullein seeds narcotics because they drunk fish into floating.
  • Figurative sedative: Anything that dulls alertness — television called an electronic narcotic, routine called a narcotic. This metaphor preserves the numbness idea while dropping chemistry.

Domain usages sharpen the picture. In medicine, narcotic is increasingly replaced by opioid to avoid legal confusion; charts distinguish narcotic analgesics from non-narcotic analgesics (paracetamol, NSAIDs). In pharmacy, narcotics require locked cabinets, counts, and prescriptions; antagonists such as naloxone reverse them. In law, narcotics triggers mandatory sentences, international extradition, and Single Convention schedules. In anthropology, plant narcotics include opium poppy, coca (loosely), kava, betel, and fish-stupefiers. In literature, narcotics carry decadent, dreamy, or criminal connotations from De Quincey to Burroughs to Narcos.

Semantic bridges explain the polysemy. Greek narkē (numbness, torpor, the numbing electric ray) gave narkōtikos (benumbing medicine). Nineteenth-century physicians grouped all sleep- and stupor-producers together, then legislators grouped all feared drugs together, transferring the drowsy aura to any illicit powder. The fish-poison sense bridges directly: what benumbs a limb will benumb a fish. Hence the same word covers surgical morphine, street heroin, Tahitian huteo pulp, and metaphorical television.

Grammar and register: narcotics is usually plural tantum when meaning the class or trade (narcotics are, narcotics smuggling), but countable when meaning doses or types (three narcotics were found). Tone is clinical in medicine, severe and pejorative in law, romanticised in counterculture. Collocations include peddle narcotics, narcotics anonymous, narcotics dog, under the influence of narcotics. Learners should distinguish narcotic (stupor-producer) from narcissistic (self-loving) and narcoleptic (sleep-attacked) — common confusions from shared nar- onset.
