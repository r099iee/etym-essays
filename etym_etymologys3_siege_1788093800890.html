The sitting root. The chain of siege begins at the Indo-European root *sed- “to sit”, one of the most productive roots in the family: it yields Latin sedēre “to sit” (source of sedate, sedentary, session, assiduous, preside, president), English sit, set, saddle, settle (through Germanic), Greek hédra “seat”, Sanskrit sad- “to sit”. From Latin sedēre, late spoken Latin formed a noun for “a seat, a place of sitting” — reconstructed as *sedicum, with the -icum suffix of neuter substantives — which passed into the Romance vernaculars: Italian sedia “chair” and seggio “seat”, Portuguese sede “seat, headquarters”, and French siège. Two other members of the same root-family deserve notice here, because they show how the “sitting” image bred military words independently: Latin obsideo “to besiege” (ob- + sedēre), from which English obsess descends (through obsessus, literally “besieged, beset”), and Latin assidēre “to sit by”, source of English assize (a court session) and of the Romance verbs for “besiege” seen below.

Old French: from chair to battlefield. In Old French the word appears as sege (later siège) with the primary sense “seat, throne”. The great semantic event of its history is the military transfer, complete in Old French itself: an army that camps before a town and refuses to move is an army that has “taken its seat”, and the technical phrase — mettre le siège devant une ville, “to set the siege before a city” — names the act of sitting. The transfer is attested in French chronicle and epic by the 11th–12th centuries, and it is this double French sense (seat / military investment) that English borrowed wholesale through Anglo-Norman after 1066. The two senses were not felt as separate by medieval speakers; the metaphor was living — a besieging host visibly “sat” in its camp before the walls.

Middle English: both senses side by side. English took the word in the 13th century (earliest recorded uses c. 1200–1300) in the form sege, with both meanings operative. (1) The seat sense: Chaucer writes of chivalric siege and majesty — in the Knight’s Tale the Amazon queen Ipolita is described as “asseged” beneath the walls of her city, while knights throughout the romances are “sett in ther segys of honour”; the crown of this sense is Malory’s Siege Perilous, the dangerous empty seat at the Round Table reserved for the Grail knight who alone may sit there — a wordplay possible only because siege still meant “chair” in Arthurian English. (2) The military sense, dominant by c. 1300, given the siege-heavy warfare of late medieval England and France: the Hundred Years War (the sieges of Calais 1346–47, Harfleur 1415 — where the English army stood for weeks before the town, the very scenario the word conjures — Orléans 1428–29) and the Wars of the Roses made sege a staple of chronicle, romance and administrative record. The word also budded minor abstract senses in Middle English: “rank, station” and “assault (of illness)”.

Spelling and sound: sege → siege. Old French offered both sege and (in later, central French developments) siege with the -ie- digraph. Middle English used sege, syge, sege; the French-influenced spelling siege won out in the 15th–16th centuries, and the pronunciation settled as /siːdʒ/ — the palatal -g- before a front vowel giving the /dʒ/ of modern English, exactly as in college, village, refuge. By this time the word’s connection to “sitting” had long been opaque to English speakers — nobody “hears” sedēre in siege — which is what allowed the military sense to function as the sole modern meaning while the seat sense quietly died.

Early Modern and literary career. In the 16th–17th centuries siege was a word of everyday politics and war. Shakespeare deploys it across the history plays — the sieges of Angiers (King John) and Harfleur (Henry V, whose “Once more unto the breach” speech is delivered by besiegers at the wall) — and metaphorically of psychological pressure and pursuit. English translators of the classical military writers (Frontinus, Polybius) and the Elizabethan tacticians (Barret, Ward) regularised the technical vocabulary of “siege-works, sap, counterscarp, mine”, fixing the word at the centre of early modern warfare. The 17th-century sieges of the Civil War (Basing House 1645, Colchester 1648) and the Williamite wars (Derry 1689) kept the word vivid in the first English newspapers.

The 19th century: state of siege and the romantic siege. The French Revolution and its aftermath gave Europe a new legal coinage — état de siège, a declared emergency in which military authority overrides civil government, formalised in French law of 1791 and 1849 — translated into English as state of siege and adopted across the 19th-century empires. Simultaneously the romantic novel made “laying siege to a heart” a staple metaphor of courtship. The great 19th-century sieges — Sebastopol (1854–55), Lucknow (1857), Paris (1870–71), Khartoum (1884–85), Mafeking (1899–1900) — filled the world’s press with the word and fixed its modern associations: endurance, starvation, bombardment, relief.

The 20th–21st centuries: metaphor and memory. The world wars produced the modern archetypes: the siege of Leningrad (1941–44, roughly 872 days, over a million civilian dead) has become the measure of endurance in the Western memory; “under siege” is now used of governments, industries, celebrities and even software under concentrated attack; “siege mentality” (from mid-20th-century social psychology) describes the permanent-defensive psychology of isolated groups; “siege economy” describes nations organised under lasting blockade or sanctions. Meanwhile the ancient “seat, throne” sense is dead in English outside Malory and dictionaries, and the word’s centre of gravity has moved to the modern urban siege — Sarajevo (1992–96), Aleppo, Mariupol (2022). Most English speakers meet siege first in history (Troy, Constantinople, Leningrad) and then in metaphor (“under siege”) — a perfect illustration of a militarised word that never shows its “sitting” ancestry.
