BALE — evolution essay

The word "bale" (the bundle) is a traveler: born of the Indo-European root that swelled and blew, carried into Germanic as a round object, handed to the Romance-speaking world as a bundle, and brought back into English in the Middle Ages as a unit of trade. Its deeper ancestry lies in the reconstructed PIE root *bhel-, "to swell, to blow, to inflate," one of the most productive roots in the family, from which descend ball, balloon, bellows, bole, folly, and many another inflated thing. In Proto-Germanic the root produced *balluz, "a ball, a round object" — Old Norse bǫllr, "ball," and, in the other Germanic dialects, forms for anything rounded and swollen. From this "roundness" comes the later Germanic word for a bundle, reconstructed as Frankish *balla, "a round bundle": a package is essentially a little ball of goods, wrapped and bound until it is round.

In the Romance languages the Frankish form prospered. Old French balle means "a round bundle, a bale, a pack of goods," and from French the word entered Middle English in the 13th century (c. 1300) as bale, "a round bundle of goods, tightly bound." From the first it was a mercantile word: a bale was a standard unit of packed merchandise — wool, cotton, hay, straw, paper, cloth — compressed and corded so tightly that it could be handled, stacked, shipped, and sold by the bale. The word's whole history is bound up with commerce: the wool trade of medieval England, the cotton trade of the Atlantic economy, the hay trade of the farm, the paper trade of the printer. In the 14th–16th centuries "bale" settled firmly into this role, and in the 17th–19th centuries it belonged to the language of shipping, warehouses, and agricultural labour — a thing of logistics, of the dock and the barn, too heavy and too tightly bound to be opened without ceremony.

In the 19th century the noun produced a back-formation verb, "to bale" — to compress and bind goods into bales, the farmer baling hay, the mill baling cotton — and the word became both the cargo and the act of making cargo. The modern period has extended "bale" by metaphor to any bulky, bound mass: a bale of belongings, a bale of rags, a bale of wire; and it is this extended sense the passage uses, where Bellman's expedition gear is packed as a "bale of belongings," the travelers' very lives stowed as merchandise. The mercantile ring quietly persists: a bale is what one sells, ships, and stacks, and to call one's personal belongings a bale is to feel them as freight, heavy and corded and impersonal.

Two confusions must be carefully separated. First, "bale" the bundle is not the Old English word bale (bealu), "evil, misery, ruin," which survives in the poetic "bale-fire" and in Tolkien's archaisms; the two are accidental homographs, unrelated in origin. Second, the homophone verb "to bail" / "to bale" out, meaning to scoop water from a boat (from Old French baille, "bucket") and, in aviation, to parachute out, is a third, unrelated word. The bundle-bale alone descends from the swelling PIE root, and it has kept its round, bound, swelled character for a thousand years: from a Germanic ball to a Romance bundle to an English unit of trade to, in the passage, the compressed mass of a traveler's gear.

PRYING — evolution essay

The history of "prying" is really two histories of two different verbs that English has stubbornly fused into one. The older of the two is the verb "to pry," meaning to look closely, to peer narrowly, to strain the eyes at something. It first appears in Middle English in the late 13th century (c. 1290) as prien, and its origin is genuinely unknown: the OED marks it as of uncertain etymology, and no secure PIE, Germanic, or Romance ancestor can be reconstructed — the word may be expressive or imitative, related perhaps to "peer" (from Old French perer, "to appear, to come into view") or simply coined for the action of squinting at something. In its earliest centuries "to pry" carried no blame at all: it simply meant to look closely and carefully, to examine with effort — a merchant prying at a coin, a sailor prying at the horizon, a scholar prying at a text. The look was effortful but innocent.

The word's moral history turns in the 17th century. Two forces converge. First, the older "pry" (to look) began to pick up the sense of looking where one has no business looking, of searching out what is hidden or private — the natural extension of a word for strained, peering attention, turned now on other people's affairs. Second — and decisively — a second, unrelated verb "to pry" entered the language: the American and Scots form of "to prise," "to prize," "to force open with a lever," from Old French prise, "a grip, a hold, the act of taking," a word of the taking-and-grasping family. This lever-pry (17th century) meant, and means, to wrench open something shut — to pry a lid off a box, to pry a door ajar, to pry a secret out of someone.

The two homophones were, from the start, thoroughly confused — and the confusion was productive. Because the lever-pry wrenches open what wishes to stay shut, the look-pry came to feel like a lever working on privacy: a prying eye prises secrets open, a prying question wedges into what a person keeps closed. The moral shading was acquired in exactly these terms: by the 17th–18th centuries "prying eyes" and "prying questions" stand for invasive curiosity that respects no privacy, and the participial adjective "prying" hardens into a wholly negative word — the look that does work, that pokes and wrenches and forces its way in. The semantic fusion is so complete that modern speakers do not feel two words at all: "to pry into someone's business" and "to pry open a box" are one word to the English ear, and the image of the crowbar colors the eye.

In the modern period "prying" is unambiguously censorious. A prying neighbor, a prying reporter, a prying glance — the word names curiosity that violates what wishes to remain concealed, and it is used almost always as criticism, in prose from the law (the prying eye of the state, prying questions in a court) to the domestic (prying relatives, prying eyes at the window). The passage gives the word a sly and telling twist: Bellman's torch beam is "prying," turned "into the shadows" — the light itself is given the quality of an intrusive, levering eye, wedging into the dark to prise out the cave's secrets. The image compresses the whole history of the word: a straining look, a lever forcing what is shut, and a trespass upon what wants to stay hidden.
