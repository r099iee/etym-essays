- 1st Century BCE to 4th Century CE (Classical & Late Latin) - Classical verb *dēputāre* meant to prune, reckon, or esteem; in Late Latin, it shifted to allocating, assigning, or appointing a person to a specific public office or duty (*dēputātiō*).
- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French *députation*, originally denoting the formal administrative act of appointing or commissioning an agent, proxy, or substitute.
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor Era) - Shifts into legal, ecclesiastical, and governmental discourse to describe both the delegation of sovereign authority and the formal commissioning of judicial officers.
- 17th to 18th Century (Stuart & Georgian Eras) - Concrete collective meaning emerges decisively: a chosen body of citizens, merchants, or parliamentarians sent to present an official address, grievance, or petition to the Crown, Parliament, or mayor.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Reaches ubiquitous usage in municipal politics, colonial administration, and the rising trade union movement; satirized by Oscar Wilde in *The Remarkable Rocket* as the ultimate delusion of bureaucratic grandeur.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues in formal parliamentary, municipal, and Commonwealth civic procedure (e.g., "hearing a deputation"); preserved in US law enforcement as the formal commissioning of deputy officers.
