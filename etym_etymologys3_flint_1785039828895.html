The biography of FLINT is the biography of one of the most ancient technologies of the human species, and the word itself has preserved that antiquity. Its reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root, *(s)plei-, means "to split, cleave, splice" — and this is exactly the property that made flint valuable: its conchoidal fracture causes it to break into sharp-edged, curved shards, a behavior known as knapping that prehistoric humans exploited to make hand-axes, scrapers, arrowheads, and blades for hundreds of thousands of years. The PIE root radiates widely: Greek plínthos "brick, tile" is a lump split from the block (whence English plinth); Old Irish slind "brick" is a Celtic cognate; and the Germanic offshoot *flintaz, by dropping the mobile s-, became Old English flint.

In the Germanic branch the root hardened into a noun for the hard rock itself. Old English flint is recorded from the earliest centuries of English — in poetry and glosses — with a double identity already present: the stone that splits into sharp tools, and the stone that "gives off sparks when struck." Even in Old English the word was transferring to human character: a flinty heart is one that cannot be moved, an image that has never left the language. Cognates across the northern languages confirm the word's antiquity: Middle Dutch vlint, Old High German flins, Old Norse flint, Danish and Swedish flint. The same PIE root entered English by other routes as well: splint (a thin strip or armor strip, from Middle Low German), splice (from Dutch splissen, "to join rope ends"), and split (from Scandinavian splitta) all encode the same idea of cleaving apart.

  • Flint as the tool-stone (Stone Age to Bronze Age): for most of human prehistory, flint (and its relative chert) was the cutting material of the species. Knapping, the controlled splitting of flint nodules into blades, is the oldest skilled technology we know, and flint mines (e.g., at Grimes Graves in England) are among the earliest human industrial sites. This is the primary, literal sense that gave the word its staying power.
  • Flint as fire-starter: striking flint against iron pyrite or steel produces white-hot sparks; the flint-and-steel pair (with tinder) was the universal fire-making kit from prehistory until the 19th-century match. The pairing "tinder and flint" became a fixed phrase, which Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit as a measure of speed.
  • The flintlock (1680s) and the age of firearms: the flintlock musket named its mechanism for the stone that struck the spark in the pan; for two centuries the "flint" was the beating heart of firearms, and flint-knapping survived as a skilled trade supplying gun-flints.
  • Figurative hardness: from Old English flinty onward, the stone became the standing metaphor for moral and emotional hardness — a flinty heart, a flinty gaze — and by c. 1700 skinflint coined the miser who would skin a flint to save a penny.
  • Industrial and pop-culture afterlives: flint-glass named brilliant lead-crystal glass from its flint-silica content; the name Flintstone puns on the Stone Age; and the flint-and-steel kit survives in camping and bushcraft. The word never retreated from the language because the material never lost its uses.

Sound-shift notes: the PIE mobile s- in *(s)plei- was lost in the Germanic branch, yielding the *fl- onset (the same loss seen in Greek vs. Germanic for this root). The Old English noun flint preserved the Germanic shape with the familiar -nt- cluster; Middle English kept flint essentially unchanged, and Modern English /flɪnt/ is almost a phonetic fossil of a word already old in the eighth century. Tolkien's "tinder and flint" thus drags the entire 4,000-year biography of fire-making into a single proverb of speed.
