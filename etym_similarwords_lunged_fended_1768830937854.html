• thrust: Similar because it is the generic term for a forward push of a weapon; differs because a thrust is any forward stab, while a lunge is specifically the deep, extended body movement that carries the thrust.
  • stab: Similar because both are sword attacks; differs because stabbing emphasizes the piercing point and violent insertion, not the extended forward posture of the whole body.
  • pounced: Similar because both are sudden forward attacks; differs because pouncing implies leaping down upon prey from above, a downward spring rather than a horizontal extension.
  • charged: Similar because both are committed forward attacks; differs because a charge is a full-speed run against an opponent from a distance, not a single extended thrust.
  • sprang: Similar because both describe sudden forward motion; differs because springing stresses the leap itself — feet leaving the ground — while a lunge keeps the feet grounded and extends the body.
  • darted: Similar because both are quick forward movements; differs because darting suggests small, quick, zigzag motion, without the deep extension of a lunge.
  • dived: Similar because both are whole-body forward movements; differs because diving implies head-first plunging, often downward and into something.
  • plunged: Similar because both are sudden, forceful movements forward; differs because plunging suggests diving or falling into something, not the controlled extension of an attack.
  • jumped / leapt: Similar because both involve sudden movement toward a target; differs because jumping emphasizes leaving the ground, while the lunge's essence is grounded extension for reach.
  • rushed: Similar because both are rapid forward advances; differs because rushing is a hurried, general forward movement, without the focused extension of a single thrust.
  • jabbed: Similar because both are quick attacking movements; differs because a jab is a short, sharp punch or poke, while a lunge is the full-bodied, long attack.
  • shot (as in 'shot forward'): Similar because both describe sudden, explosive forward motion; differs because 'shot' is a metaphor of projectile speed without the controlled athletic posture.
  • defended: Similar because 'fend' is the aphetic form of 'defend' and they share the core meaning of protection; differs because defend is the broad, intact verb of guarding and protecting, while fend is leaner and means the specific act of warding off.
  • warded (off): Similar because to fend off is to ward off — both mean to keep a threat at a distance; differs because warding suggests watching and guarding against, while fending is a sharper, more active striking away.
  • parried: Similar because both are sword defenses that deflect an attack; differs because parry is a precise fencing term for turning aside the blade with one's own, while fend is a more general word for beating back anything.
  • blocked: Similar because both stop an attack; differs because blocking is a static, absorbing stop of a blow, while fending actively deflects and pushes it away.
  • repelled: Similar because both mean to drive something back; differs because repel suggests driving an enemy or force back at scale, while fend is a single, personal deflection.
  • repulsed: Similar because both mean to beat back an attack; differs because repulse suggests turning back a larger assault and also carries a sense of disgust, which fend does not.
  • averted: Similar because both mean to turn away a danger; differs because averting is often preemptive (averting a disaster before it comes), while fending is a direct, physical response to a blow already in motion.
  • diverted: Similar because both redirect a threat; differs because diverting implies changing the course of something, while fending pushes it back toward its source.
  • guarded: Similar because both belong to protection; differs because guarding is continuous watchfulness, while fending is a single, momentary act.
  • shielded: Similar because both protect against attack; differs because shielding interposes a barrier, while fending actively knocks the attack aside.
  • beat back / fought off: Similar because both describe active resistance to an attack; differs because these are more colloquial and emphasize sustained resistance, while fend off is a single economical deflection.
  • countered: Similar because both answer an attack; differs because countering implies a deliberate counter-attack, while fending is primarily defensive deflection.
  • stood up to: Similar because both involve resisting a threat; differs because standing up to is moral and verbal courage against pressure, while fending is physical.
  • managed / got by (for 'fend for oneself'): Similar because to fend for oneself is to manage one's own needs; differs because manage is broader and less urgent, while fending implies keeping need at bay with one's own effort.
