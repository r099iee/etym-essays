barbarous — uncivilized, savage, wild; brutal and cruel; in its oldest sense, foreign and of unintelligible speech. In the literary passage this card draws on — "not again in all the world's turning will there be terrains so wild and barbarous to try whether the stuff of creation may be shaped to man's will" — the word is applied not to people but to landscapes, and that displacement is the key to its force. "Barbarous" here is not moral accusation but cosmic description: the terrains are barbarous in the root sense of resisting intelligibility and order — lands that refuse to be read or tamed, hostile to the human grid of roads, fences, and names. The word stands inside a question that is really a test — whether the raw material of creation can be "shaped to man's will" — and "barbarous" names precisely the resistance that shaping must overcome. The tone is prophetic and almost theological: the wildness is absolute and unrepeatable ("not again in all the world's turning"), and the pairing "wild and barbarous" wraps the physical sense (wilderness) around the moral one (savagery) so that the landscape itself seems to bristle with menace and refusal. Beneath the moralized modern sense, the passage reaches back to the word's ancient acoustic core: these are terrains that speak a language man cannot parse. The word is richly layered, with senses that have accreted over two and a half thousand years:
  • Of unintelligible speech; foreign (the original, now archaic core): to the ancient Greeks, anyone who did not speak Greek was barbaros — literally "bar-bar," the reduplicated imitation of speech that could not be parsed. The sense survives in the rhetorical term barbarism (an impropriety of language, a mingling or corruption of speech) and in scholarly use.
  • Uncivilized, uncultured, lacking civilization (the classical-historical sense): non-Greek or non-Roman; outside the pale of civilized custom; rude, unpolished, uncultivated in arts and manners. From Herodotus onward this is the standard classical and imperial use, applied to peoples judged by their distance from Greek or Roman culture.
  • Wild, savage, untamed (of places as well as peoples): applied to landscapes, weather, and conditions that resist human control — barbarous terrain, a barbarous coast, barbarous cold. This is the sense the passage uses, and it preserves the oldest intuition: what cannot be read, ordered, or tamed is barbarous.
  • Cruel, brutal, merciless, inhumane (the dominant modern sense): since the 18th century the moral sense has crowded out the descriptive one: barbarous acts, barbarous treatment, barbarous cruelty — savagery judged by the standard of humanity and condemned outright.
  • Harsh, unpolished, aesthetically unrefined (of style and art): a quiet secondary use for writing, music, or ornament that is rough, unkempt, or grating — barbarous metre, barbarous ornament — keeping the older descriptive meaning alive in aesthetic criticism.
  • Fierce, unrestrained (in informal and admiring extensions): in some registers the word, like its Romance cousins (Spanish bárbaro, "great, fantastic"), can tilt toward awe rather than condemnation — barbarous grandeur, barbarous energy — admiring the very wildness it fears.
Its synonyms carve up the spectrum of "uncivilized" with meaningful differences. Barbarous is the strongest moral term: it condemns acts or conditions of cruelty and savagery and carries the weight of civilization's judgment against its outside; barbaric, though often used interchangeably, tilts toward the primitive and grand — barbaric splendor, barbaric music — admiring what it also fears; savage stresses ferocity and rawness and, like "barbarous," can describe untamed nature ("savage wilderness"), which makes it the closest rival in this sentence; uncivilized is descriptive and literal, naming the absence of civilization without necessarily condemning; primitive is anthropological and developmental, implying an early stage of culture without moral charge; brutish and bestial stress the animal, sub-human character of the cruelty; crude and unrefined belong to the aesthetic register; and rude carries the archaic sense of rough and uncultivated. What keeps "barbarous" distinct is its double edge — it is simultaneously the word for what is outside civilization and for what is cruel within it, a fusion that lets the passage apply it to a landscape and make the land itself seem to threaten the human.
