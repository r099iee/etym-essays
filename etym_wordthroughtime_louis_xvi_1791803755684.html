- 1754–1774 — born Louis-Auguste, duc de Berry; the name Louis carries the prestige of a dynasty and a saintly ancestor, Louis IX, but the boy is known by his territorial title.
- 1774 — he becomes Louis XVI at nineteen and is initially greeted as a virtuous, reforming king; the numeral is a neutral marker of dynastic continuity.
- 1789–1792 — the name becomes contested: he is “king of the French” under the constitution of 1791, and republicans call him Louis Capet to deny his title.
- 21 January 1793 — the execution turns “Louis XVI” into the name of the Revolution's most famous victim, a martyr for royalists and a tyrant for republicans.
- 1814–1830 — the Restoration rehabilitates him; his brother reigns as Louis XVIII, and the name becomes a symbol of the lost old order.
- 19th c. — Carlyle (1837) and Dickens (1859) fix the image of the doomed king, while “Louis XVI” also becomes the label of a neoclassical decorative style.
- 20th–21st c. — historians reassess him as a more deliberate political actor than the old caricature allowed, and the style sense survives in the antiques trade alongside the historical one.
