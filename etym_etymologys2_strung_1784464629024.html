PIE *strenk- / *streng-ʰ- “tight, stiff, drawn tight, taut” (reconstructed root; also written *streng-; c. 4500–3500 BCE; the exact shape and vowel grade are debated in handbooks, see note below) → Proto-Germanic *strangiz “cord, rope, string” (reconstructed; derived from the root's “tight/taut” sense; continued by Old Norse strengr “cord, string,” Old Saxon streng, Old High German strang, Middle Dutch strenge) → Old English streng “a cord, a rope, a string; a tendon or sinew; a line” (attested from the 8th century CE; the noun is native Germanic) → Middle English streng / string “a cord, a string” (12th–14th centuries CE), with the denominative verb strengen / stringen “to furnish with a string, to bind with a cord; to stretch taut” → Early Modern English string “to thread (beads, gems, lights) on a cord; to fit with a string; to stretch a cord tight” (the transitive verb of threading and tensioning, 16th century CE) → Modern English STRUNG — the card's target form: the strong past tense and past participle of string (this card) NOTE on the debate: the deeper Indo-European pedigree of Germanic *strangiz is reconstructed as *strenk-/†streng- “tight, stiff,” related to Latin stringere “to draw tight, bind” (the source of English strict, constrict, stringent), but the precise root shape and laryngeal/vowel structure are uncertain and different handbooks arrange the “taut” family differently. Within English, note that the verb string was originally weak (regular) — the older past is stringed — and the strong form strung arose in early modern English by analogy with the sing/sang/sung, ring/rang/rung class; it was established as standard by the 17th century. The noun string and its verb are the same native Germanic word, not a loan.
