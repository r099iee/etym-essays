loft (noun): a room or space directly under the roof of a building, typically one that is bare, unfinished, or given over to storage; also a gallery or upper floor in a church, barn, or workshop (a hayloft, an organ loft, a sleeping loft). The word enters Mervyn Peake's prose at a precise narrative moment in Titus Groan: having marched the reader up to “the top floor of the north wing,” the narrator performs a downward reclassification — the grand name “hall” is withdrawn, and the space is demoted to the more candid, humbler word “loft.” Peake's sleight of hand is tonal: a “Hall of the Bright Carvings” sounds like a ceremonial, vaulted, public room, whereas a loft is a garret, an attic, a space whose very name implies ceiling-beams, shadow, dust, and disuse. The effect is ironic grandeur — the treasures of the carving collection are housed in what amounts to a glorified attic, and the reader's expectations of courtly architecture collapse into a painterly scene of a single dim window, lowered shutters, and scarce light. The word also carries an architectural precision: a loft is defined by its position under the roof-pitch, with sloping walls or half-walls, and by its lack of the finished character that would entitle it to the name “hall.” It is the castle's uppermost chamber, filled with the same upper air that feeds the seven candelabra below.

Like many words, loft is a polysemous word whose senses have grown and shifted across eight centuries. The modern dictionary lists:
  • Roof chamber / attic (the core sense, from the 13th century): an unpartitioned space directly under the roof, used for storage — hay in the hayloft, lumber in the lumber-loft, grain in the granary loft. This is the sense Peake invokes, with its overtones of beams, dust, and disuse.
  • Gallery or raised floor: the organ loft of a church, the choir loft, the gallery at the back of a hall — a platform raised above the floor, still “in the air” in the manner of the Norse root from which the word descends.
  • Modern urban loft (20th century): a large, open living or working space converted from an industrial upper storey — “loft apartment”, “artist's loft”, “loft-style”. Here the word's register has inverted: from cramped and humble to fashionable and sought-after, so that the term Peake used to demote a grand hall now advertises luxury real estate.
  • Verb sense: to loft, to hit, throw, or kick a ball high into the air (in golf, baseball, and football); also to raise or hoist something upward. This is the verbification of the “raise into the air” idea preserved in the Old Norse root.
  • Attributive and derivative uses: a loft bed (a bed raised on a platform), loft insulation, loftsman (a draughtsman who lays out full-scale working plans), and the derived adjective lofty — first “high in position” (14th century), then “elevated in mind or spirit”, a moral grandeur the dusty chamber itself could never claim.

The word's whole history is a descent from the sky to the attic, and then a re-ascent to fashion. In Old Norse, lopt meant both “air, sky” and “upper room”, because the space immediately beneath a roof is filled with air: the element-word was transferred to the enclosed volume of air at the top of a house. English took the room-sense in the thirteenth century, and for six hundred years the loft was the plainest of functional spaces — farm, church, workshop. In the twentieth century the great inversion occurred: artists moved into abandoned industrial upper storeys and made them airy live-work studios, and “loft” shifted from cramped and humble to fashionable and sought-after, so that the word Peake used to demote a grand hall now advertises prestige. The semantic bridge from air to attic is the oldest and most beautiful in the word's history, and it is still visible in the Scandinavian languages, where Danish loft means “ceiling, attic” and Swedish loft means “attic”.
