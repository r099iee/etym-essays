Larceny is one of the great survivals of medieval English law, but its ancestry is Roman. Latin latrō (genitive latrōnis) meant "robber, bandit" and, earlier, "mercenary soldier". The derived noun latrōcinium covered robbery, brigandage and piracy — the armed depredations of latrōnes outside the settled order of Roman law. Roman jurists distinguished furtum (theft, a matter of private delict) from rapina (robbery) and from latrocinium, the wider condition of banditry and piracy. The word thus began not with a shoplifter but with an armed man on a road.

Latin latrōcinium passed into Old French as larcin, "theft, robbery", and from Anglo-Norman larcine into Middle English in the late fourteenth century. English law gave the borrowed word a precision it had never had in French: larceny became a specific felony, defined by the taking and carrying away of another's goods with felonious intent. The older, vaguer sense of "robbery" survives only in the etymological background and in the rare learned doublet latrociny.

The medieval offence

  • Felony and clergy. Larceny was a felony, punishable by death, but benefit of clergy allowed anyone who could read to claim trial in an ecclesiastical court. The twelve-penny threshold for grand larceny grew out of this system: grand larceny was the serious, clergy-stripped form; petty larceny the minor form.
  • Elements. Medieval and early modern judges built up the technical requirements — trespassory taking, asportation, property of another, intent to steal — through case law rather than statute.
  • Blackstone. William Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765–69) gave the offence its classic English exposition, distinguishing larceny from robbery, burglary and receiving, and fixing the vocabulary that American law would inherit.

Codification and abolition

The nineteenth century brought statutes: the Larceny Act of 1861 consolidated the old law, and the Larceny Act of 1916 restated it. But the common-law framework was increasingly strained by modern commercial crime. Embezzlement, false pretenses and larceny by trick had been created to fill gaps, and the resulting patchwork was notoriously difficult to explain to juries. The Theft Act 1968 swept it away in England and Wales, replacing larceny and its satellite offences with a single crime of theft: dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with intention permanently to deprive. Scotland, whose law is separate, had its own doctrine of theft and never used larceny in the English sense.

American survival

American jurisdictions largely kept the word. Many state codes retain grand larceny and petty larceny, often grading them by the value of the property taken; New York's grand larceny in the first through fourth degrees is a familiar example. The Model Penal Code instead consolidates theft offences, and federal law uses "theft" and "larceny" in different contexts. The result is that an American lawyer may still speak of larceny as a live charge, while an English lawyer treats it as history.

Semantic boundaries

The word's endurance depends on the distinctions it encodes. Larceny requires a taking from the owner's possession; embezzlement requires prior lawful possession; false pretenses requires the victim's consent to transfer title; robbery requires force or fear; burglary requires unlawful entry. These boundaries are not pedantry: they determine which offence can be charged, what must be proved, and what sentence follows. In the reading, "petty larceny" signals the lowest rung of this hierarchy — a small taking, by a common man, yet treated in the Fijian example as more heinous than a chief's murder, because rank, not value, governed the penalty.

Figurative afterlife

Outside the courtroom, larceny retains a slightly archaic, emphatic flavour. "Literary larceny" accuses a writer of plagiarism; "petty larceny" in politics dismisses a scandal as small but real; "grand larceny" inflates it. The metaphor works because the word still carries the image of a hand closing on someone else's property — the latro of the Roman road, reduced to a courtroom noun.
