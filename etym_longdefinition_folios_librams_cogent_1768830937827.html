This card carries three words from a single sentence of Jack Vance describing the Sage's library: "untidy folios collected by the Sage, leather-bound librams setting forth the syllables of a hundred powerful spells, so cogent that Turjan's brain could know but four at a time." The words cover the format, the content, and the intellectual force of the books.

PART 1: FOLIOS

folios is the plural of folio, a word with two connected meanings: a large sheet of paper folded once in half to form two leaves (four pages) of a book; and a large-format book printed on such sheets — one of the grandest and most unwieldy sizes in the history of printing. The word descends from Latin folium, "a leaf."

  • 1. Printing and bibliography: A folio is a book format in which large sheets are folded once down the centre, making two leaves or four pages. In folio was the largest of the standard book sizes, reserved for the most imposing works — Bibles, atlases, collected plays — and produced books so large they had to be read at a desk.
  • 2. The sheet or leaf itself: In papermaking and bookbinding, a folio is a single sheet of paper, and by extension a leaf or page of a book. "Keep this letter with your other folios."
  • 3. Famous folios: The Shakespeare First Folio (1623) is the most famous book in English literature — the word has come to stand for the great collected volume that preserves a legacy.
  • 4. Accounting and law: In bookkeeping, a folio is a page or sheet number used to cross-reference entries between ledgers; "folio" numbers appear in legal and financial records.
  • 5. Botany: In botany, folium is a leaf, and the foliate words of English (foliage, foliar, foliation) all descend from the same Latin source.

PART 2: LIBRAMS

librams is a literary coinage by Jack Vance (1950): the plural of libram, a heavy, ornate, ancient, or highly specialized book of magic — specifically a book of esoteric spells and sorcerous lore. Vance built the word as a deliberate archaism on Latin liber (genitive librī), "book," which originally meant "the inner bark of a tree" (the material on which the Romans first wrote). The form "libram" evokes the Latin accusative librum, giving it a pseudo-classical gravity appropriate to a book of spells.

  • 1. The Vancean sense: An ancient, forbidden, magical book — the sorcerer's grimoire of the dying-earth world. In the sentence, the librams are leather-bound and set forth "the syllables of a hundred powerful spells."
  • 2. The pseudo-Latin form: libram is not a standard English dictionary word; it is a manufactured archaism that reaches past the modern Romance words (French livre, Spanish libro) to the ancient Latin root, and readers recognize it as a coinage with built-in depth.
  • 3. Association with liber "bark": Because Latin liber originally meant tree-bark, the word carries an image of a book that must be unpeeled layer by layer, like bark from a tree — a text of secrets that must be opened carefully.
  • 4. In fantasy literature generally: Words of this shape (libram, tome, codex, grimoire) are the standard furniture of fantasy magic, and Vance's coinage fits the pattern of invented-but-plausible archaisms.

PART 3: COGENT

cogent means clear, logical, forceful, compelling, and irresistibly convincing — applied almost always to arguments, reasons, evidence, and cases. It derives from Latin cōgens, "compelling, driving together," the present participle of cōgere ("to drive together, force, compel"), from con- "together" + agere "to drive."

  • 1. Argument and reasoning: A cogent argument compels assent; a cogent reason is one that carries the mind along by its own force. "She offered a cogent explanation of the strange events."
  • 2. Law and jurisprudence: In legal usage, cogent evidence is evidence so compelling that it leaves no room for reasonable doubt; "cogent reasons" are those a court must accept.
  • 3. The force-of-logic sense: The word is about the force of reasoning, not of emotion — a cogent argument convinces by logic; a persuasive one may convince by charm.
  • 4. Vance's twist: Vance applies the word to spells: the syllables are "so cogent that Turjan's brain could know but four at a time." The spells are like arguments whose force is so great the mind can only hold a few at once — a striking extension of the word from intellectual conviction to magical compulsion.

How the Three Words Work Together

The three words build a portrait of a library from outside in: the folios are the physical format — large, unwieldy books of leaves; the librams are the magical content — ancient books of spells, leather-bound; and cogent describes their intellectual (here almost physical) force — so compelling that a human brain can hold only four syllables of them at a time. Format, content, and force are one continuous scale of weight.
