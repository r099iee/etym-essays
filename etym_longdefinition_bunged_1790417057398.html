bunged (past tense and past participle of the verb bung; frequently used participially as an adjective): Having been tossed, thrown, or crammed haphazardly, carelessly, or forcibly into an enclosure or shared space; having been stopped or sealed tightly with a wooden plug (bung); or, in diverse colloquial and physiological expressions, having suffered nasal congestion, mechanical clogging, bruising, or having received an illicit bribe or cash tip.

The term displays a rich, colorful polysemy across British vernacular, traditional coopering trades, medicine, and underworld slang:

  • Colloquial British Locomotor & Situational Sense (Crammed / Thrown Together): Placing, tossing, or thrusting people or objects carelessly, abruptly, or unceremoniously into a specific location or arrangement. Used with prepositions such as in, together, into, or down (e.g., "bunged all the clothes into the suitcase"; "bunged the keys on the hall table"). In the source context ("bunged together in close association in a secluded spot"), P.G. Wodehouse captures the comical, arbitrary suddenness of two incompatible individuals being thrust together into an inescapable romantic predicament.
  • Traditional Coopering & Brewing Sense (Sealed with a Stopper): In brewing, winemaking, and coopering, having driven a wooden or rubber stopper (the bung) into the hole (the bunghole) of a cask, keg, or barrel to make it airtight and watertight for fermentation or transport (e.g., "the ale barrel was bunged tight before shipment").
  • Physiological & Somatic Congestion ("Bunged Up"): In everyday medical and colloquial speech, suffering from obstructed nasal passages, sinus blockage, or respiratory congestion caused by a head cold or allergies (e.g., "my nose is completely bunged up"); or, mechanically, describing drains or pipes blocked with debris.
  • Ophthalmic & Physical Trauma ("Bunged Eye"): Describing an eye that is bruised, blackened, and swollen shut following a punch or physical impact (e.g., "the prizefighter emerged from the ring with a bunged eye").
  • British Slang for Tipping and Bribery ("Bunged a Tenner"): Handing someone an informal tip, gratuity, or illicit monetary payoff to secure favorable treatment or silence (e.g., "he bunged the doorman a fiver to jump the queue").

Semantic Bridges & Metaphorical Shifts:

The semantic evolution of bunged demonstrates how an earthy, tangible trade tool—the wooden barrel plug—gave rise to a sprawling family of physical and psychological idioms. The noun bung entered Middle English from Middle Dutch and Old French coopering terminology, designating the stout wooden stopper used to seal the bunghole of a beer or wine cask. Because driving a bung into a barrel required force and resulted in a tight, crowded seal, 16th-century speakers began using the verb bung to mean stopping up any opening. From this arose the physiological image of a blocked nose being "bunged up" (literally plugged like a barrel) and a swollen, bruised eye being "bunged up." In the 18th and 19th centuries, the physical action of thrusting a plug into a hole was generalized into thrusting or shoving any object or person into a confined space—yielding the breezy British colloquialism of "bunging" clothes into a drawer, money into a palm (a bribe), or two reluctant people "bunged together" into a country cottage.
