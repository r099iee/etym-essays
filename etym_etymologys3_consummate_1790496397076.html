Every letter of consummate remembers addition. It begins far back in Proto-Indo-European *uper-, meaning over, above, beyond — the root that gives Sanskrit upari, Greek hyper, Germanic over, and Latin super. Latin built upon super the adjective superus (upper) and its superlative summus (highest, topmost), formed with the ancient superlative suffix *-m̥mo- that also yields infimus, optimus, maximus. From the feminine summa (the highest thing) came by ellipsis summa pecunia — the top line of the account — shortened to summa, a sum, a total. Roman schoolboys, merchants, and surveyors lived by summae: totals cast up, heaped measures, reckonings closed. To the Romans summing was not abstraction but heaping: grain poured until the bushel brimmed.

With intensive prefix con- (together, wholly), Latin formed consummare: to sum together, to bring to the sum, to finish, perfect, accomplish. Cicero uses it for rounding off a speech, Columella for finishing the harvest, Seneca for perfecting virtue, the Vulgate for Christ's consummatum est — it is finished — from the Cross. The past participle consummatus supplied both the English verb and adjective: what has been summed is both completed (verb) and complete (adjective). Late Latin added consummatio, completion, perfection, and in theology the consummation of the world — history summed and closed. Phonology is regular: con- assimilates nothing before s, the double -mm- preserves the boundary con-summare, and English keeps Latin stress on the second syllable for the verb while retracting it for the adjective.

  • Middle English legal solemnity (1400s-1500s): Caxton, Malory, and chancery clerks borrow consummaten for acts that must be more than done — they must be sealed: to consummate a will, a treaty, a pilgrimage. The marriage sense specialises early under canon law: consent (consensus) makes marriage, bodily union consummates it, and unconsummated unions may be annulled. Paston letters and ecclesiastical courts already plead non-consummation exactly as modern courts do. The solemnity sticks because sealing was sacramental.
  • Renaissance perfection (1500s-1600s): humanists extend the participle into general praise and blame: Elyot's consummate governor, Shakespeare's consummate thief (Autolycus), Milton's consummate darkness. Hamlet's consummation devoutly to be wished fixes the noun's high style for three centuries: consummation is death-as-fulfilment, not mere ending. Adjectival stress retracts to /ˈkɒnsəmət/ on the analogy of separate, delicate, cleanly splitting noun-adjective from verb.
  • Evangelical domestication (1700s-1800s): conduct books, housekeeping manuals (Mrs Rundell, Mrs Beeton), and schools like the Misses Rice's make consummate housewife a standard accolade: the woman in whom cookery, needlework, accounts, nursing, and government of servants are all summed. Ruskin's mother, trained at Croydon and finished by practice, is exhibited as the type — formidable, tireless, complete. The same decades produce the consummate villain of melodrama and the consummate artist of the Royal Academy: completion praised or hissed according to content.
  • Modern split accent, united sense (1900s-present): dictionaries now list verb /ˈkɒnsəmeɪt/ and adjective /ˈkɒnsəmət/ as distinct headwords sharing one etymology, with consummation and consummately bridging them. Diplomats consummate deals, lovers consummate marriages, critics acclaim consummate performances — three registers, one heaped measure. The persistent double m guards the boundary against consume (from com- + emere, to take wholly, devour): English keeps consummate with two m's completes, consume with one devours as a schoolroom maxim.

Ruskin's formed housewife thus carries two millennia of heaping in her apron: Roman totals, Vulgate last words, canonical sealings, Shakespearean climaxes. To call her consummate is to say the account of her training casts up without error — every column added, the sum double-underlined, nothing carried forward.
