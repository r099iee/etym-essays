• Augustus: Similar because it denotes the same man after 27 BCE; differs because it is a religiously coloured title granted by the senate, not an inherited family name, and it evokes the settled emperor rather than the revolutionary heir.
  • Octavius: Similar because it is the root of the adoptive name and the name he bore at birth; differs because it belongs to the gens Octavia before the adoption, whereas Octavianus marks the transfer into the Julii.
  • Octavian: Similar because it is the ordinary English form of the same name; differs only in being the anglicised, later spelling preferred by modern historians, while Octavianus is the Latinising variant.
  • Caesar: Similar because it is the name he actually used after 44 BCE; differs because it points to the Julian clan and to a claim on the dictatorship, not to his Octavian ancestry.
  • Divi filius: Similar because it was his coin legend and public style after 42 BCE; differs because it asserts divine sonship of the deified Caesar rather than naming a family.
  • princeps: Similar because it became his constitutional designation; differs because it means “first citizen” and describes a political position, not a lineage.
  • imperator: Similar because it is the military acclamation behind the English word “emperor”; differs because it was won from soldiers in the field and could be held by any victorious general.
  • Octavia: Similar because it is the feminine of the same nomen and belongs to the same family; differs in gender and person, denoting his sister, the wife of Antony.
