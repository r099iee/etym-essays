jiggered (adjective and past participle of jigger) — (1) astonished, confounded, taken aback, chiefly in the British exclamation "I'll be jiggered!"; (2) chiefly British, broken, ruined, spoilt ("the engine's jiggered"); (3) slightly intoxicated, chiefly American, from the jigger, a small measure of spirits. In the passage Peter breaks off and says, "Well, I'm—I'm jiggered," and the word is a mild exclamation of astonishment and consternation. The specific nuance is the British colloquial oath of surprise of the form "I'll be jiggered!"—the speaker is confounded, taken aback, baffled by what he has just discovered, and the word lets out that shock without resorting to any real profanity. The card's quick-explanation glosses it in terms of intoxication, and it is true that jiggered can mean "drunk" (from the jigger, a small measure of spirits); but in this sentence the governing sense is the exclamatory one of startled disbelief, reinforced by the stage detail that Peter's voice "also sounded queer"—his composure has cracked because the hall's exact match to Cair Paravel has confounded his understanding. The tone is genteel, slightly dated, and half-jocular: an Edwardian-schoolboy exclamation, mild enough for children's literature yet vivid enough to convey real mental rattling. 1. The primary senses
  • Astonished, confounded (the sense of this card): the exclamatory jiggered, recorded from the 1830s in the fixed phrase "I'll be jiggered!" (and "I'm jiggered!"), is a minced oath—a polite, nonsense substitute for a profane one—and most lexicographers read it as a euphemistic softening of "I'll be buggered!", i.e. "I'll be damned": the speaker turns a strong word into a harmless near-twin, and the absurdity of the substitute actually increases its expressive force. It is the gentlest member of a whole family of self-directed oaths (I'll be blowed!, I'll be blessed!, I'll be damned!).
  • Broken, ruined, spoilt (chiefly British): from the verb to jigger, "to jerk about, to tamper with, to spoil"—"it's all jiggered up"—jiggered came to mean damaged beyond repair, the mild, almost affectionate cousin of buggered.
  • Slightly intoxicated (chiefly American): from jigger, the bartender's small measure of spirits, jiggered meant "having had a jigger or two"—mildly drunk. This is the sense the card's quick-explanation offers, though the context of astonished disbelief calls for the first sense.
2. Secondary senses and uses
  • The noun jigger and its family: jigger (19th century, from jig + the agentive suffix -er) means a person who jigs, and then—in the great Victorian habit of calling any little thing a jigger—a small device, gadget, or thing; the bartender's measure; a fisherman's jigger (a lured hook or a sparkler used for ice-fishing); and a jigger, in golf, an early iron club (a mid-iron) used for approach shots.
  • The verb jigger: to move jerkily, to tamper with, to spoil ("to jigger something up"); the participle jiggered in "jiggered up" is the broken sense; "jigger" also appears in the game sense (a jigger is a place in certain card games) and in the dance-world as a jiggerbug.
  • Register and date: the exclamatory jiggered is Edwardian and faintly comic; a modern speaker would say gobsmacked or dumbfounded, so the word now works as deliberate period flavour—the sound of a gentleman being jolted out of his composure, his voice gone queer.
3. Semantic bridge
  • The thread joining all three senses is the verb to jigger, "to jerk, to shake, to jolt": a man who is jiggered has been jolted—whether by a shock to his composure (the exclamation), by a mechanism that has been shaken to pieces (the broken sense), or by the jolt of a dram of spirits (the intoxicated sense). From the jerky motion of the jig itself to the jolted man, the word's history is one continuous picture of sudden, rattling displacement.
