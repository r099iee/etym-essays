- Early 13th c. - Emerged in Early Middle English as the prepositional phrase a mis / on misse, meaning literally "in error, astray, off the mark" in archery, travel, and judgment.
- 14th c. - Solidified into a unified adverbial form in Middle English (Chaucer, Gower), used to describe moral failings, sinful deeds, or erroneous speech ("to doon amys").
- 16th c. - Elizabethan literature transitioned amiss into a versatile predicative adjective, popularized in drama (Shakespeare) to signal ominous disruptions in political and domestic order ("Something is amiss").
- 17th-18th c. - Idiomatic collocations crystallized in social and polite correspondence, particularly "to take amiss" (take offence) and the litotes "not come amiss" (be welcome).
- 19th-21st c. - Established as a staple of mystery fiction, detective procedural narratives, and formal elevated dialogue, retaining its classic understated tension of suspected disorder.
