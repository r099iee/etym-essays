Mooting reaches back to the Germanic assembly ground. Its ultimate root is widely taken as Proto-Indo-European *med- ("to measure, take counsel, consider, heal" — the same root behind Latin mederi "to heal," Greek medomai "to plan," and English medicine in a distant sense). In Germanic this yielded a verb *motaną / *mōtijaną meaning to meet, encounter, come together, and a noun *mōtą meaning a meeting, assembly, or appointed gathering. Old English preserves both: mētan (to meet) and (ge)mōt (a moot, council, folk-assembly — as in witena gemot, the meeting of wise men that advised kings). Place-names still record it: Moot Hall, Moot Hill, Moot Point once meant the hill where the assembly gathered.

The Norman Conquest displaced the institution but not the word. As shire moots gave way to manorial courts and Parliament, moot narrowed in Middle English (Chaucer, Langland, legal year-books) to a formal argument or disputation upon a hypothetical case, especially the exercises in the Inns of Court where law students pleaded mock cases before benchers. By the 16th century moot is firmly a noun for a debating exercise ("a moot case"), a verb for arguing it ("to moot a point"), and an adjective for points fit only for such exercise — debatable yet without live effect. Shakespeare already plays on this doubleness: debatable matters are moot matters.

Phonologically the development is economical: Old English gemōt /mōt/ with long o passes through Middle English open-syllable lengthening and then the Great Vowel Shift to modern /muːt/. Spelling stabilizes as moot (avoiding confusion with mote "speck" and meet / meat), and the regular -ing participle gives mooting. The legal-educational compound moot court (attested 17th c.) and modern mooting competition preserve the oldest institutional memory in plain sight.

  • Assembly era (pre-1300): the moot is real governance — freemen debate dues, boundaries, guilt. To moot is to bring business before the community.
  • Inns-of-Court era (1400-1700): the moot becomes pedagogy — students moot hypothetical pleas to learn pleading. The word acquires its "merely hypothetical" color.
  • General deliberative era (1700-1900): Parliament, press, and committees generalize moot to any proposal for discussion. Burke, Mill, and Victorian newspapers moot reforms, motions, and amendments.
  • Transatlantic split (19th-20th c.): American courts develop the doctrine of mootness — a case whose controversy has evaporated will be dismissed as moot. Hence the paradox foreign learners meet: in London a moot point needs debate; in Washington it no longer does. Both flow logically from student-debate practice: arguable but inconsequential.

In contemporary discourse mooting thrives in two niches that rarely meet: the newsroom ("a cabinet reshuffle is being mooted") where it signals trial-balloon politics, and the law school ("she moots for Gray's Inn") where it signals advocacy training. The source sentence's sneer — "mooting precisely the same drivelling scheme" — draws on the newsroom sense while weaponizing the student-exercise overtone: you are not governing, only talking, and badly. Etymologists note minor debate over whether *med- or a narrower *mōd- ("meeting") is the exact PIE source, but the Germanic-to-English assembly chain itself is undisputed and richly documented in charters, place-names, and legal records.
