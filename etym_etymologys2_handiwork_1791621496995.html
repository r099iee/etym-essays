This compound word descends from Old English handgeweorc, formed from two ancient Germanic roots: hand + geweorc (from weorc).

1. hand

PIE (c. 3500 BCE)
*(s)kond- / *kónt-us (debated)
"to seize, grasp, take hold of"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*handuz
"the hand; the grasper, the seizing limb"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
hand / hond
"the human hand; manual power or agency"
↓
hand

2. geweorc (work)

PIE (c. 3500 BCE)
*werǵ-
"to do, act, perform, execute work"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*ga- + *werką
"together, collective" + "work, deed, structure"
↓
Old English (c. 8th c. CE)
geweorc
"collective work, labor, fortification, creation"
↓
Old English (compound) (c. 9th c. CE)
handgeweorc
"creation of one's own hands, manual deed, structure"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.)
handiwerc / handywerk
"work of the hands; artisanal product, creation"
(collective prefix ge- phonetically reduced to -i-)
↓
Modern English (c. 16th c.)
handiwork
"work done by hands; product of mind, agency, or craft"

Note: The connecting element -i- is not the modern adjective handy, but the direct historical survival of the Old English collective prefix ge- (as in geweorc), which eroded to Middle English -i- and was subsequently rationalized by folk etymology.
