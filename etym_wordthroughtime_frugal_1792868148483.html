- Roman — the virtuous citizen: frugalis names the sober, useful man who lives off his land's fruits; Cato makes it a moral crown.
- Late antiquity — the Christian poor in spirit: monks and fathers baptise frugality as holy simplicity, blessed are the spare.
- 1590s — English adopts it: frugal enters as a learned compliment for thrifty housekeepers and sober magistrates.
- 18th–19th c. — the political virtue: Franklin's industry and frugality, Victorian thrift crusades, and empire budgets all preach the frugal purse.
- 20th c. — wartime and waste-not: ration books, make-do-and-mend, and post-war austerity make frugality patriotic duty.
- Today — the green virtue: minimalists, misers of carbon, and economists of the planet reclaim frugal as the style of survival — and Tolkien's joke keeps its kitchen warmth.
