Whirligig is a compound, and it marries two very different histories: an ancient verb of turning and a humble, half-sound-mimicking noun for a toy. The element WHIRL. The first element, whirl, goes back to Proto-Indo-European *kʷer-, "to turn, to whirl," the ancestor of a great family of turning-verbs. In Proto-Germanic this became *hwerbaną, "to turn, to move about," which produced Old English hweorfan, "to turn, to go, to change," Old High German hwerban (whence German werben, "to woo, to recruit," a turning about in search of something, and Wirbel, "vortex, whirl"), and Old Norse hverfa, "to turn, to vanish." From the Scandinavian forms came Old Norse hvirfla, "to spin round," and its noun hvirfill, "a whirl, a circle" — and it was this Viking verb that entered Middle English as whirlen around 1300, giving English whirl, whirlwind, whirlpool, and ultimately whirligig. The English word thus stands at the meeting-point of native and borrowed: the Old English hweorfan (with its derivative hwyrfel, "a circle, a ring," surviving in dialectal wharve, a spindle-whorl) was reinforced rather than replaced by the Norse whirlen, and both flows fed the same stream. The element GIG. The second element, gig, is a different creature: first recorded in Middle English as gigge, "a whipping-top," a spinning toy lashed into motion. Its origin is obscure and almost certainly expressive — the word hums with the sound of the thing, and the same buzzing, silly-sounding family produced giggle and, probably, jig (via Old French gigue and Middle High German gîga, "a fiddle," modern German Geige). In later English gig wandered far from the toy-shop: it meant a flighty girl, then a light two-wheeled carriage (eighteenth century), then a musician's one-off engagement (twentieth-century jazz slang), then simply any job. The word kept its whirring, buzzing, slightly silly heart through all of these. The compound. By the fifteenth century English had spliced whirl and gig into whirligig, "a thing that whirls," first recorded about 1440, and the word promptly began spinning through meanings: a toy, a pinwheel, a roundabout or merry-go-round, the spinning water-beetle Gyrinus (from the 17th century), and — by the crowning metaphorical leap — a fickle, inconstant person, and the very reversals of fortune. Shakespeare fixed the figure for ever in Twelfth Night: "the whirligig of time brings in his revenges," where the spinning toy becomes the emblem of time's giddy, inescapable turnings. The register has always been colloquial and faintly comic, which is exactly what makes it the perfect material for a dwarf's oath in Prince Caspian: "Whistles and whirligigs!" is a flustered, toy-stuffed substitute for profanity, and the word's three giddy syllables enact the mental whirling it names. Aftermath. Through the 18th and 19th centuries whirligigs spun in gardens and at fairs as ornamental wind-driven spinners of every sort, and the word slowly took on an old-fashioned, whimsical, almost archaic flavour — children's literature, fairy-tale diction, and period dialogue. In the twentieth century the word's functional descendant arrived in the hand as the fidget spinner, a gadget that restores the pure, pointless, comforting rotation the whirligig always had, even as the word itself has kept its antique, toy-boxed charm. The compound thus brings together the oldest reconstructable root of turning in the Indo-European world and a half-echoic, half-playful English toy-word, and it has spent five centuries spinning cheerfully between them.
