- (c. 700–1100) - Old English sculdor: a plain everyday word for the body part; no figurative uses are recorded — the shoulder is simply the joint of the arm.
- (1200s–1500s) - Middle English schulder: still anatomical; by the 1400s the projecting-part metaphor begins — a "shoulder" of a hill or of a road is spoken of as the rounded flank of the land.
- (1500s–1600s) - The verb appears: "to shoulder" = to push with the shoulder; then "shoulder arms" (military drill); the burden-metaphor ("shoulder the load") follows naturally from literal carrying.
- (1600s–1800s) - Idioms multiply: "shoulder to shoulder" (military solidarity), "on one's shoulders" (responsibility), "cold shoulder" (1816, Scott) — the word becomes one of English's richest idiom-sources.
- (1800s–1900s) - Engineering sense: "shoulder of the road" (the roadside edge) becomes official in road-building; "hard shoulder" of motorways follows in the 20th century.
- (1900s–today) - Modern extensions: "shoulder season" (travel), "shoulder surfing" (screen-peeping), "shoulder check" (hockey/driving) — the ancient body-word keeps generating new technical senses.
- (today) - Dominant perception: a fully ordinary body word with a vast idiomatic shadow; the landscape sense (as in Tolkien's mountain-spur) remains a living poetic transfer of the body's shape onto the land.
