- Old English — strips and tracks: swaðu names a mown swath, a broad strip cut through grass, the ancestor idea of breadth.
- Middle English — winding bands: swathen means to wrap in bands or swaddling clothes, moving from field-strip to linen-strip.
- 16th–17th c. — nursery and sickroom: infants swathed, wounds swathed, the dead swathed — protection, healing, and reverence in one gesture.
- 18th c. — polite dress: throats and heads swathed against draughts and damps; the word smells of flannel, modesty, and care.
- 19th c. — weather and poetry: hills, valleys, and towers are swathed in mist and moonlight; novelists borrow the sickroom word for landscape.
- Today — care and atmosphere: nurses still swathe limbs while writers swathe scenes, the literal and the lyrical wrapped together.
