- 13th to 14th Century (Middle English) - Borrowed from Old French *humble* (from Latin *humilis*, from *humus* "earth"); the superlative *humblest* emerged in religious treatises to describe the most devoutly submissive monks and saints before God.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English & Shakespeare) - Used extensively in courtly correspondence and drama as the ultimate formula of deferential etiquette (*"your humblest servant"*) and feudal hierarchy, contrasting noble lords with the humblest peasants.
- 18th Century (Augustan & Romantic Eras) - Celebrated in Romantic poetry (William Wordsworth, Robert Burns) which discovered profound spiritual truth, dignity, and beauty in the humblest rustic creatures and common wildflowers.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Deployed widely in Victorian social novels (Charles Dickens) and satire (Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket* in 1888), highlighting the comical contrast between pompous aristocratic pride and the humblest realities of life.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as the superlative of modesty and unpretentiousness, widely utilized in biographical profiles, scientific descriptions of microscopic life, and polite self-deprecation.
