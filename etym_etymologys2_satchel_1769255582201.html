Chain for satchel: 1. Hebrew saq / Akkadian šaqqu (and Aramaic saqqā) "sackcloth, sack, coarse bag" (attested 2nd–1st millennium BCE; the Semitic stock word for a bag of coarse cloth). 2. Greek σάκκος (sákkos) "sackcloth, a bag of coarse cloth" (borrowed from the Semitic word; attested in Greek from c. 5th c. BCE, e.g., in Herodotus). 3. Latin saccus "a sack, bag" (loan from Greek σάκκος; attested 1st c. BCE onward, used for bags, purses, and money-sacks). 4. Late Latin saccellus "a little sack, small bag" — a diminutive of saccus formed with the suffix -ellus (attested c. 4th–6th c. CE). 5. Old French sachel / saschel "a small bag" (from Latin saccellus; attested from c. 11th–12th c. CE). 6. Middle English sachel "a small bag" (borrowed from Old French; attested c. 13th–14th c. CE; spellings include sachel, sackell, and later satchel). 7. Modern English satchel (from c. 15th–16th c.; the modern spelling with medial t settled in the early modern period). Debated / nuanced note: The medial -t- in the English spelling and pronunciation (sạtch-el, /ˈsætʃəl/) is a well-known historical puzzle. The Old French and Latin forms have no t — saccellus / sachel — and the English t seems to have arisen in Middle English spelling (satchel) possibly by dissimilation or analogy with words like "hatchel" or through a Norman scribal variant. The t is purely orthographic/pronunciational accretion; it does not reflect the Latin etymon. The Semitic origin of the ultimate root is agreed (the Greek σάκκος is a loanword from Semitic), but the precise donor language — Hebrew vs. Akkadian vs. Aramaic — is debated since the word is shared across Semitic with the same basic form and meaning.
