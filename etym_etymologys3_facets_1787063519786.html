facets descends through Latin faciēs 'appearance, form, face' and French facette 'a little face', and its history shows a word travelling from the human body to gem-cutting and finally into abstract thought.

From Latin faciēs

Latin faciēs (stem faciē-) meant 'form, shape, appearance' and then 'the face, the countenance'. It belongs to an ancient and slightly mysterious family. Classical grammarians and modern scholars alike have often linked it to facere 'to make', as though the face were 'that which is made or shaped', but the formal relationship is difficult, and most philologists now treat the ultimate derivation as uncertain. The same stem appears in superficies 'surface' — literally 'the upper face' — which gave English superficial.

French face and facette

Old French inherited face 'face, countenance, appearance' by the 10th century, and this is the source of English face in the 13th century. The diminutive facette — literally 'a little face' — was formed in French with the suffix -ette and was used from the 14th century both for a small face or aspect and for the small cut faces of a gem. French gem-cutters, among the finest in medieval Europe, made the term the standard word for a cut gem's face, and French lapidary terminology passed it to the rest of Europe.

English career

  • 1620s: facet enters English directly from French in the gemmological sense — the small polished face of a cut stone — attested in lapidary and jewellery texts.
  • 19th century: the figurative sense 'an aspect or side of a thing' appears, first of problems and situations, by straightforward extension: a complex issue, like a diamond, can be examined facet by facet.
  • 20th century: the transferred sense expands into psychology and biography ('facets of the personality', 'multi-faceted'), and the word enters science and computing — crystallography, then computer graphics, where a 'facet' is a polygonal face of a modelled surface.

Cultural resonance

The gemmological origin keeps the figurative use sparkling: to speak of the 'facets' of a thing is to imply it has hidden lights, revealing a different beauty or truth from each angle. Tolkien's description of the Arkenstone — a globe with a thousand facets that shone 'like silver in the firelight, like water in the sun, like snow under the stars, like rain upon the Moon' — leans directly on this double life of the word, cut faces and many-sidedness fused in a single image of glancing, changeful light.

The borrowing channel and the suffix

English took the word ready-made from French at a moment when French dominated the language of luxury crafts: jewellery, cooking, tailoring, and heraldry all supplied English with their technical vocabulary between the 14th and 17th centuries. The diminutive suffix -ette deserves notice, because it is the same suffix that later gave cigarette, statuette, and serviette: a facette is, structurally, a 'little face', and that littleness is what the modern figurative use still feels — a facet is always smaller than the whole it ornaments, a single plane on a larger object. When the word moved into computing in the late 20th century (a polygon in a rendered mesh), the translation was effortless precisely because the geometry had been there for four hundred years.
