TYRANNIZES is the third-person singular present tense of the verb tyrannize: "to rule or treat someone despotically, cruelly, or unjustly; to exercise absolute, lawless, or capricious power over another." In the sentence from The Story of the Stone, Lady Wang confides to her niece: "There is only one thing that worries me. I have a little monster of a son who tyrannizes over all the rest of this household." The verb is deliberate hyperbole — a joke with a thorn in it. Taken politically, to tyrannize is to govern as an absolute, lawless master, by caprice and fear rather than by right or consent; applied to a spoilt boy, the word inflates his power into absurdity while confessing its reach. Bao-yu's "tyranny" is domestic: his moods, tantrums, and pre-eminence as the grandmother's darling determine the weather of the household, and everyone — servants, sisters, even parents — must orbit his whims. Lady Wang speaks with the half-laughing, half-vexed tone of a mother describing a "little monster", both proud and exasperated, and the Latinate weight of tyrannizes over all the rest of this household gives the joke its comic grandeur: a child-king reigning over a nursery-kingdom. Yet the hyperbole is not pure fun — it quietly tells the reader that Bao-yu already exerts an irrational, privileged power over the whole family, foreshadowing the moral anarchy of his fate.

Polysemy and the full range of senses:

  • Despotic rule of a state — the original, political sense: a sovereign rules lawlessly, for his own pleasure, without constitutional check; "the emperor tyrannized over his provinces". This is the classical and Renaissance sense, freighted with the Greek theory of the tyrant.
  • Domestic or personal domination — the verb applied to family, household, or workplace: a parent, spouse, employer, or dominant figure may "tyrannize over" dependants. This is the sense at work in Lady Wang's sentence, inflated to comic grandeur.
  • Figurative rule of a passion or force — "he let ambition tyrannize over his conscience"; a habit, fear, or desire may tyrannize a life, meaning to rule it absolutely and destructively; here the verb needs no human tyrant at all.
  • Construction with "over" — the dominant modern pattern: "tyrannize over someone"; a direct transitive object ("tyrannizing the household") is also attested but is rarer and now faintly archaic.
  • The -ize morphology — the suffix is Greek -ízein, marking the verb as a Greek-style formation; this learned suffix makes tyrannize heavier and more ceremonious than native verbs like "boss" or "bully".
  • Historical and literary resonance — from the Athenian tyrants of the archaic age to the "tyranny of the majority" of modern political theory, the verb and its family carry an unbroken charge of illegitimacy and abuse of power.

Boundary comparisons sharpen the word's exact force. Rules or reigns is neutral sovereignty — the exercise of legitimate power — and carries none of the abuse that tyrannizes implies. Dominates is broader still: domination may be by force, personality, or argument, and it can be passive (a mountain dominates a view), with no political or cruel colouring. Oppresses names injustice — the grinding, burdensome misuse of power against the weak — but it describes the condition of the ruled more than the conduct of the ruler, and it never has the amused, domestic spin Lady Wang needs. Bullies is the everyday, small-scale member of the family — petty, physical or verbal, the behaviour of the playground — whereas tyrannizes inflates the same behaviour to the register of despotism, comic or sinister. Subjugates and enslaves name conquest and outright ownership, too extreme for household tyranny; dictates concerns the giving of orders rather than the abuse of power; terrorizes stresses the fear instilled rather than the capricious rule. In sum, tyrannize is the grandest word for petty cruelty: it lends a spoilt child the gravity of a despot and a despot the pettiness of a spoilt child.
