- Old English (c. 1000) - Coined as *snyflan* / *snofl*, referring strictly to nasal mucus, phlegm, and running of the nose.
- 14th c. (Middle English Emergence) - Appears in Langland's *Piers Plowman* (*snyvelen*), shifting from pure physiology to describe snuffling speech and weeping beggars.
- 16th–17th c. (Contempt & Whining) - Solidifies into a general term of contempt for tearful complaining, weak cowardice, and self-pitying lamentations.
- 18th–19th c. (Hypocrisy & Cant) - Adopted in satirical and moral literature to attack false piety, religious cant, and grovelling sycophancy.
- 20th–21st c. (Everyday Colloquialism) - Retains dual role: literal physical description of a head cold and runny nose alongside a sharp insult for spineless, cringing complainers.
