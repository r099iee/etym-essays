- Roman antiquity: antiquare is a legal act of “making old” — rejecting a proposed law as already old and unfit — so the word is born with a dismissive charge.
- 16th–17th c. English: “antiquate” enters as a Latin loan; “antiquated” first used to mean simply “grown old,” close to “ancient,” still fairly neutral.
- 18th–19th c.: the Enlightenment and the reform era load the word with criticism — antiquated customs, antiquated privileges, antiquated institutions are the targets of reformist writers.
- 19th–20th c.: applied to people and machines; “antiquated ideas,” “antiquated methods” become standard criticism of laggards in politics and industry.
- Late 20th–21st c.: the word flourishes in technology commentary — antiquated systems, antiquated infrastructure — implying inefficiency and being overtaken by progress rather than total disuse.
