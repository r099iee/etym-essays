THE EVOLUTION OF 'VISAGE'

Visage begins, appropriately, in the eyes. Its ancestor is the Indo-European root *weid-, 'to see' — a root so fundamental that it split into two branches across the ancient world: one meaning to see with the eyes, one meaning to know with the mind, because to have seen is to have known. Latin built vidēre 'to see' from it, and from vidēre came the past participle vīsus, 'seen', 'the sight, the appearance'. The decisive step for our word came when Vulgar Latin extended vīsus with the suffix -aticum, the maker of nouns of quality and relationship, to form vīsāticum — 'that which is seen', the appearance. Old French contracted this to visage, and from the 11th century visage was the plain, everyday French word for the face — as it remains today, entirely unremarkable in Paris, while in London it became one of the most self-conscious words in the language.

  • Old French to Middle English: when the Norman court and clergy carried their speech to England after 1066, visage came with them. English borrowed it around 1300, at first as a courtly and chivalric word: the faces of ladies, knights and saints were 'visages'. The native word 'face' (from Latin facies via French) and the Germanic 'countenance' competed with it; for two centuries the three words jostled in the same high register.
  • Renaissance and Shakespeare: the 16th–17th centuries were the great age of 'visage'. Shakespeare uses it some thirty times, always with dignity — in Othello 'I saw Othello's visage in his mind' makes the word the vehicle of a whole theory of love as vision; in the history plays a 'noble visage' and a 'threatening visage' face each other across the battle lines. The word is the face raised to the pitch of drama and scripture. The King James translators and the devotional poets likewise reach for it when a face carries supernatural weight.
  • The 18th–19th centuries: 'visage' holds its place in formal prose, epic verse and the portrait painter's vocabulary, while everyday speech gravitates to 'face'. The Romantic poets keep it for faces charged with meaning: Wordsworth's 'The Child is Father of the Man' — the very poem echoed on this card — treats the human face as a palimpsest of future history. Dickens and the Gothic novelists give 'visage' to their most expressive and most terrible faces; in the new art of photography and the illustrated press the word is chosen whenever a face is meant to be studied rather than merely seen.
  • The 20th–21st centuries: the word has narrowed into a conscious stylistic elevation. In ordinary speech it is archaic or mock-heroic; in serious prose it is reserved for the face at its most legible and fateful. Cormac McCarthy's austere, biblical register is exactly where the word still lives: 'All history present in that visage' depends on the ancient logic of the word — a visage is a face that is seen, read, and remembered. In seven centuries the word has done almost nothing but keep its meaning: it remains, precisely, the face as an object of sight, the seen face, the face that tells the story of a life; and whenever English wants to look a face in the eyes and read its history, it reaches for the word that was born from the verb 'to see'.
