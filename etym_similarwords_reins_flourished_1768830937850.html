• bridle: Similar because it is the head-gear by which a horse is controlled, including the bit and the straps that connect to the reins; differs because the bridle is the whole controlling apparatus on the horse's head, whereas the reins are specifically the two straps held in the rider's hands.
  • bit: Similar because the reins act upon it, transmitting the rider's pressure to the horse's mouth; differs because the bit is the metal mouthpiece itself, inside the horse's mouth, not the straps in the hand.
  • halter: Similar because it is a head-strap used to lead or restrain a horse; differs because a halter has no bit and is used for leading and tying on the ground, not for riding control.
  • headstall: Similar because it is the part of the bridle around the horse's head; differs because it refers to the frame that holds the bit, not the hand-held straps.
  • harness: Similar because it is a set of straps used to control or attach a draught animal; differs because a harness is the full working rig (collars, traces, straps) that connects a horse to a cart or plow.
  • leash: Similar because it is a strap used to hold and control an animal; differs because a leash restrains a dog or similar animal at close range on the ground and carries no idea of riding or steering.
  • lead rope / lead line: Similar because it is a strap attached to a halter for leading a horse; differs because it is used for ground work, not mounted control.
  • tether: Similar because it is a rope or chain that confines an animal to a fixed spot; differs because a tether anchors rather than steers, and its purpose is restriction, not direction.
  • curb: Similar because it is a means of restraint (and, as a verb, means 'to check or hold back'); differs because a curb is a check or restraint in general, and in riding specifically a chain or strap beneath the chin; it lacks the paired, hand-held steering function of the reins.
  • lines (chiefly U.S.): Similar because it is the ordinary American word for the pair of driving reins; differs because 'lines' is most at home in driving and carriage use rather than riding.
  • traces: Similar because they are straps of a harness; differs because traces connect the collar to the vehicle and pull the load, rather than guiding the animal.
  • martingale: Similar because it is a controlling strap of riding tackle; differs because it restrains the horse's head from raising too high, acting on the horse rather than being held in the rider's hands.
  • check (noun): Similar because 'the reins' are a check — a means of stopping or restraining — and 'to check a horse' is to hold it back on the reins; differs because 'check' is a general verb of stopping and has no leather-and-bit machinery behind it.
  • control / command (figurative): Similar because 'the reins' are the standard figure for control, and both words name the power to direct; differs because they are abstract nouns of authority, not physical straps.
  • brandished: Similar because it means to wave a weapon menacingly, a showy sweeping motion; differs because brandishing carries a direct threat of violence, while flourishing can be purely ceremonial or ornamental without menace.
  • waved: Similar because it means to move something back and forth through the air; differs because 'waved' is neutral and does not imply showiness, ceremony, or a weapon.
  • swung: Similar because it means to move something in an arc; differs because swinging is a general mechanical motion and lacks the display or ornament of a flourish.
  • swept: Similar because it means to move in a wide, smooth arc; differs because sweeping suggests clearing a space rather than showing something off.
  • twirled: Similar because it means to spin or rotate something showily; differs because twirling is a circular, gymnastic motion, typically of a baton or cane, not a forward sweeping stroke.
  • flaunted: Similar because it means to display something ostentatiously; differs because flaunting is about showing off a possession or quality (often tastelessly), whereas flourishing is a physical motion, often of a weapon or document.
  • displayed: Similar because it means to show something deliberately; differs because displaying is static presentation, without the sweeping movement.
  • paraded: Similar because it means to show or march ostentatiously; differs because parading involves moving oneself or one's goods publicly, not waving a single object.
  • thrived / prospered: Similar because 'flourish' shares the core meaning of healthy growth and success; differs because thriving and prospering describe the state of being successful, while flourishing retains the image of blooming and can be used of plant life and of artistic style.
  • bloomed / blossomed: Similar because they are the literal horticultural ancestors of 'flourish,' meaning to open into flower; differs because they apply literally to plants, whereas 'flourish' has generalized to any vigorous development.
  • flowered: Similar because it shares the same Latin root (flōs, flower) and means to bloom; differs because 'flowered' stays literal or refers to decorative flower patterns, while 'flourished' has broadened into thriving and waving senses.
  • ornamented / embellished: Similar because a 'flourish' is an ornamental stroke and 'to flourish' can mean to embellish; differs because embellishing adds decoration to a thing, whereas flourishing is the decorative gesture itself.
