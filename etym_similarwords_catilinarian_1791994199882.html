• Subversive: Similar because both denote covert actions intended to overthrow or undermine established political authority; differs because subversive is a broad modern term applying to peaceful cultural or ideological undermining, whereas Catilinarian carries the specific classical resonance of desperate aristocratic intrigue, armed treason, and rhetorical denunciation.
  • Treasonous: Similar because both involve the ultimate betrayal of one's allegiance to the state; differs because treasonous is a formal, universal legal and moral designation, whereas Catilinarian specifically evokes internal factional cabals, populist debt agitation, and classical Ciceronian drama.
  • Conspiratorial: Similar because both characterize clandestine, collusive schemes orchestrated by secret factions; differs because conspiratorial can apply to mundane, corporate, or petty plots, whereas Catilinarian is restricted to high-stakes political rebellion threatening the state apparatus.
  • Philippic: Similar because both refer to legendary classical orations of searing political invective; differs because Philippic derives from Demosthenes' attacks on King Philip of Macedon (and Cicero's later speeches against Mark Antony) aimed at external autocrats and tyrants, whereas Catilinarian focuses on internal domestic conspirators subverting the republic from within.
  • Seditious: Similar because both refer to incitement of rebellion against lawful governmental authority; differs because seditious focuses strictly on the act of inciting civil unrest through speech or pamphlets, whereas Catilinarian encompasses the entire systemic enterprise of military rebellion, arson, and aristocratic demagoguery.
  • Insurrectionary: Similar because both denote organized armed revolt against the sovereign power; differs because insurrectionary typically connotes a mass or grassroots rebellion, whereas Catilinarian implies an elite-driven, patrician cabal exploiting popular distress.
