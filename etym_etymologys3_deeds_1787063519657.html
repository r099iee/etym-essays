Deed is one of the oldest words in the English language, descending directly from the core vocabulary of the Anglo-Saxons, and it illustrates beautifully how a plain, neutral word for "doing" slowly gathered moral, heroic, and finally legal weight without ever changing its fundamental meaning.

The Indo-European inheritance. The word continues the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeh₁- "to put, to place, to set", a root of enormous reach that also lies beneath English "do", Latin facere ("to make, do"), Greek τίθημι (tithēmi, "I place, I put"), and Sanskrit dhā- "to place, put" (as in dharma, "what is fixed or established"). From *dʰeh₁- was formed a verbal noun *dʰéh₁-tis, "a putting, a doing, an act", which in the Germanic branch developed regularly into Proto-Germanic *dēdiz. The phonological history is characteristic: the root vowel was lengthened by the effects of the following laryngeal, and the final *-tis produced the *-diz of Germanic. In the West Germanic branch this became *dādi, and in Old English it surfaced as dǣd. The word is thus an exact cognate of Old High German tāt, Old Norse dáð, Gothic gadēþs, and Dutch daad — all meaning "deed, act".

Old English: the everyday word. In Old English, dǣd simply meant "an act, an action, something done", and it was one of the most common abstract nouns in the language. It occurred in everyday proverbial and legal prose, in poetry, and above all in religious writing, where it began very early to polarise morally: "good deeds" (gōde dǣda) versus "evil deeds" (yfele dǣda). The phrase "in word and deed" (on wordum and on dǣdum) is ancient, contrasting speech with action. This early Christian-moral coloration is the crucial first step in the word's drift: a neutral term for "doing" becomes a term for morally significant doing.

Middle English: morality and the law. Middle English inherits dǣd as dede, and the moral sense deepens: the word is central to homilies, saints' lives, and penitential literature ("good deeds" as the fruits of faith), while the heroic alliterative tradition begins to use it for valour — "deeds of arms". Meanwhile, in the 14th and 15th centuries, the legal mind takes over the word in a new and specific way: a "deed" becomes the written, sealed, and witnessed instrument by which rights, especially rights to land, are transferred or obligations are created. The bridge is transparent: a legal document is the "act" of the parties made permanent, an act reduced to writing. The phrases "act and deed" and the modern legal sense "an instrument under seal" date from this period.

Renaissance to present: heroic deeds. In Elizabethan and Jacobean English, "deeds" is thoroughly at home in the language of epic and romance — "deeds of derring-do", "warlike deeds", the "renowned deeds" of kings and knights — the register that Tolkien self-consciously revives when he writes, in The Hobbit, of "wars and deeds which were now only a legend". Simultaneously the legal sense became fixed and professional: a deed of grant, a deed of conveyance, a trust deed. In modern usage the everyday, neutral sense has largely been captured by the plainer words "acts" and "actions", while "deeds" itself has retreated into two brightly coloured corners: the heroic/literary (epic, legend, proverb: "actions speak louder than words", "kind deeds") and the legal (a signed and witnessed document). The word now often feels slightly elevated or old-fashioned — precisely the dignified tone Tolkien needed for a town's vanished glory.

  • 8th c. – Old English dǣd, the everyday word for any act.
  • Early medieval – polarised into "good deeds" and "evil deeds" in religious prose.
  • 14th–15th c. – legal sense: a "deed" as a written, sealed, witnessed instrument transferring property.
  • 16th–17th c. – heroic register: "deeds of arms", "warlike deeds" in epic and romance.
  • Modern – both registers persist; the neutral "doing" sense is now served by "acts" and "actions".
