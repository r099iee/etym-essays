The plural BANNOCKS carries one of the oldest bread-words of the British Isles into modern English, and its biography is bound up with the hearth, the Highland croft, and the long reach of the Scottish diaspora. The English history of the word begins in the Northumbrian dialect of Old English, where the form bannuc was recorded in glosses around 1000 AD with the homely meanings "a bit, a piece, a cake." The word is generally agreed to be a loan from a Celtic source: its nearest relatives are Scottish Gaelic bonnach "cake" (dialectally bannach) and Old Irish bairgen "bread, loaf," and behind these lies a reconstructed Proto-Celtic *bannā, "peak, top, rounded mound." The semantic leap is a pleasingly concrete one — the swelling, rounded lump of dough on the griddle was seen as a little hill, so the word for a mountain-top became the word for a cake. There is, however, a second, older theory, recorded by the lexicographers of past centuries, which makes the Gaelic word a loan from Latin pānicium, "a bread-cake," a derivative of pānis "bread." Latin pānis itself belongs to the great Indo-European root *pa-, "to protect, feed," the family of Latin pāscere "to graze" and, surprisingly, of English food, fodder, foster, pasture, and pastor — literally "a feeder." Whichever route is correct, the two stories agree on the destination: a coarse, flat hearth-bread whose very name suggests roundness and swelling, and which settled early into the Northern dialects and Scots. From the medieval period onward the bannock was the staff of life of the Scottish Highlands and the Northern English countryside. Because the staple grains of the north — barley and oats, and in times of scarcity pease — do not yield the strong gluten of wheat, the dough would not rise in an oven loaf; instead it was kneaded without yeast and baked on a hot stone, on an iron girdle, or among the embers of a peat fire. Every district had its own bannock: the barley bannock of the Lowland farm, the oat bannock of the Highland bothy, the pease bannock of the crofter's table. The word belonged to the everyday, unpretentious speech of the household, and it is no accident that it appears in folk song, proverb, and children's rhyme rather than in high literature. Sir Walter Scott and the Scottish poets used it precisely for that homely, native colour, and in England the word survived as a dialect term for any large round flat loaf. The great diaspora carried the word across the Atlantic. In the fur-trade era, the Hudson's Bay Company issued a simple flour-and-water bread to its voyageurs and traders; because the staple food of the Scottish traders had been the bannock, the name travelled with the ration. First Nations and Métis communities of Canada adopted it, adapted it — adding baking powder, frying it in pans over campfires — and made it their own, so that today bannock is simultaneously a Scots hearth-bread and a beloved Indigenous pan-bread, one of the most striking instances of a food word being re-homed in a new culture. In the 19th century the word also produced the Selkirk bannock, a rich, fruited, oven-baked loaf from the Borders that became a ceremonial centrepiece. By the time J.R.R. Tolkien wrote The Hobbit (1937), "bannock" was already archaic and regional in England — and that was exactly why he chose it. In the Elves' song at Rivendell, "The faggots are reeking, / The bannocks are baking!" the word does a great deal of quiet work. It sets the Last Homely House in the pastoral, northern, Anglo-Saxon-and-Celtic world Tolkien loved; it promises plain, honest, hearth-made food rather than courtly delicacy; and it carries the ancient Gaelic and Scots connotations of welcome, of a household making ready for its guests. The BANNOCKS baking in the embers of the song are not simply bread — they are the immemorial sound and smell of hospitality itself, a flat, round, steaming reassurance after the cold and danger of the Misty Mountains. A word that began as a "little hill" of dough on a Celtic hearthstone thus ends its journey baking in an Elvish valley, still meaning, after more than a thousand years, comfort and welcome.
