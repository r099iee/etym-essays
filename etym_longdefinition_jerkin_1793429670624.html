A jerkin is a short, close-fitting outer jacket, waistcoat, or sleeveless doublet, typically worn by men throughout the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries across Western Europe. Worn over the inner doublet or linen shirt, the jerkin was designed to conform tightly to the natural contours of the upper torso, ending at or slightly below the hips. While the earliest Renaissance varieties were occasionally fashioned with sleeves—often laced, paned, or detachable—the prevailing and most recognizable form of the garment was sleeveless, designed to maximize freedom of movement for the arms while providing warmth, structural dignity, and physical protection to the chest, back, and vital organs.

In the source text from Jonathan Swift’s Gulliver’s Travels (1726), the word appears in its classic early modern defensive role: Lemuel Gulliver, shipwrecked on the strange shores of Lilliput and pinned helplessly to the earth, finds himself assaulted by volleys of tiny arrows and microscopic spears: ‘some of them attempted with spears to stick me in the sides; but by good luck I had on a buff jerkin, which they could not pierce.’ Here Swift draws upon the everyday historical reality of the mariner and traveler: a heavy buff-leather jerkin functioned as the common man’s body armor. Its dense, oil-cured animal hide was tough enough to absorb sword thrusts, glancing blows, thorny underbrush, and inclement weather—and in Gulliver’s surreal predicament, proved completely impervious to the diminutive weaponry of his captors.

Materiality and Sartorial Construction: The jerkin occupied a unique dual register in European material culture, serving simultaneously as coarse working-class workwear and lavish aristocratic display:

  • Buff Leather (The Defensive Workhorse): Fashioned from heavy oxhide or cowhide treated with fish oil or animal fats, buff jerkins were pliable, water-resistant, and extraordinarily tough. They were standard issue for cavalry troopers, foot soldiers, pikemen, sailors, and foremen, offering affordable, unarticulated torso protection without the cumbersome weight and expense of steel plate armor.
  • Courtly Wool, Suede, and Velvet (Aristocratic Splendor): Among the nobility of Tudor, Elizabethan, and Jacobean courts, jerkins were executed in luxurious materials such as Italian velvet, corded silk, fine broadcloth, and Spanish suede. These were intricately decorated with elaborate surface treatments, including pinking (punching decorative slits into the leather), slashing (cutting patterns to reveal contrasting linings underneath), and embroidery in gold, silver, and polychrome silk threads.
  • Fastenings and Hardware: Jerkins were secured down the center front by an array of functional fasteners depending on social rank: rustic horn or wooden buttons, rawhide laces tied through hand-stitched eyelets, metal clasps, pewter frogs, or ornate cast silver buttons.

Polysemic Senses and Domain Usages:

  • 1. Historical Renaissance Torso Garment (Primary Historical Sense): An essential element of male costume from c. 1515 through the late 1600s, worn over the doublet for extra insulation, silhouette shaping, and daily sartorial presentation.
  • 2. Military Protective Vest & Light Armor (Martial Tactical Sense): A thick leather defensive over-garment worn by infantrymen, huntsmen, and archers to deflect blades, arrows, and pikes during warfare and field hunting.
  • 3. Twentieth-Century Military Issue Workwear (Modern Combat Sense): The specific military leather jerkin adopted by the British Army during the First World War (1914–1918) and maintained throughout the Second World War. Lined with thick woolen blanket material and fastened with four horn buttons, this sleeveless brown leather vest was issued to frontline trench troops, drivers, sappers, and artillery gunners to shield against biting wind, cold rain, and trench mud without impeding weapons handling.
  • 4. Occupational and Artisan Uniform (Craft Sense): A durable, stain-resistant leather apron-vest worn by blacksmiths, tanners, wheelwrights, cooperers, and farriers to protect against flying sparks, sharp wood splinters, and hot iron slag.
  • 5. Contemporary Fashion, Equestrian, and Subcultural Revival (Modern Aesthetic Sense): In modern tailoring, fashion design, and costuming, the term designates rustic leather waistcoats, gilets, and bodywarmers, particularly within equestrian hacking circles, fantasy costuming, steampunk attire, and historical reenactment communities.

Register and Connotations: To modern ears, jerkin evokes the tactile, earthy world of Elizabethan England, historical swashbuckling adventure, and sturdy yeoman resilience. Unlike the effete connotations sometimes attached to the courtly silk doublet, the jerkin carries an indelible association with honest physical toil, martial grit, and practical self-preservation. In Shakespearean drama (such as Prince Hal’s famous banter in Henry IV, Part 1 regarding a ‘buff jerkin, a most sweet robe of durance’), the garment functions as an immediate cultural shorthand for the street-level vitality of soldiers, bailiffs, and common rogues.
