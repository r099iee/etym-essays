Bulgaria (Bulgarian Bŭlgariya) is a country in south-eastern Europe, occupying the eastern part of the Balkan Peninsula between the Danube and the Black Sea, with Romania to the north, Serbia and North Macedonia to the west, Greece and Turkey to the south. It is a modern nation-state, a member of the European Union since 2007 and of NATO since 2004, and the heir of one of the oldest continuous state traditions in Europe. The name is a national and territorial proper noun, and nearly all of its secondary senses are historical layers rather than metaphors.

Primary sense: the modern state. The Republic of Bulgaria covers about 111,000 square kilometres and has a population of roughly 6.5 million, concentrated in Sofia (the capital), Plovdiv, Varna, Burgas, Ruse and Stara Zagora. Its official language is Bulgarian, a South Slavic language written in Cyrillic. The state is a parliamentary republic with a unicameral National Assembly; it adopted the euro in 2026 after a long preparation period. The country is a major producer of rose oil, sunflower oil, wheat and wine, and its Black Sea coast is a significant tourist destination.

Historical layers of the name. "Bulgaria" has denoted several different political entities, and the differences matter:

  • Old Great Bulgaria (7th century) — the short-lived Bulgar confederation north of the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov, associated with Kubrat.
  • Volga Bulgaria (9th–13th centuries) — a Muslim Bulgar state on the middle Volga, in modern Tatarstan, destroyed by the Mongols; it has no territorial continuity with the Balkan state but shares the ethnonym.
  • The First Bulgarian Empire (681–1018) — founded south of the Danube by Asparuh, Christianised under Boris I in 864, and raised to its zenith under Simeon I; ended with Basil II's conquest.
  • The Second Bulgarian Empire (1185–1396) — centred on Tarnovo, ended by the Ottoman conquest.
  • The Ottoman period (1396–1878) — "Bulgaria" survives as a geographic and ecclesiastical term (the Bulgarian Exarchate was established in 1870) rather than a state.
  • The modern state — a principality from 1878, fully independent in 1908, a kingdom until 1946, a People's Republic until 1989, and a republic thereafter.

Cultural and linguistic senses. "Bulgarian" is the adjective and the name of the language. Bulgarian is a South Slavic language with a postposed definite article (kniga "book", knigata "the book"), a lost infinitive, a rich evidential system and a vocabulary much influenced by Old Church Slavonic, Greek and Turkish. It was the first Slavic language to be written, and the Cyrillic script developed in the First Bulgarian Empire in the late 9th century at the Preslav school. "Old Bulgarian" is a synonym for Old Church Slavonic in Bulgarian scholarship, a usage that sometimes surprises Western readers.

Tertiary and technical senses.

  • Microbiology: Lactobacillus bulgaricus is the bacterium classically associated with Bulgarian yoghurt; the epithet is a Latinised geographic adjective, and the product is a national point of pride.
  • Botany and perfumery: "Bulgarian rose" (Rosa × damascena) and "Bulgarian rose oil" refer to the Kazanlak valley industry, a byword for high-grade attar.
  • Mathematics: "Bulgarian solitaire" is a card-and-pile puzzle studied by combinatorialists since the 1980s.
  • Sport and fitness: the "Bulgarian split squat" (a rear-foot-elevated lunge) and "Bulgarian bag" (a weighted training implement) are now international gym terms, only loosely connected with the country.
  • Espionage history: the "Bulgarian umbrella" was the ricin-tipped umbrella used in the 1978 assassination of Georgi Markov in London, a phrase that entered Cold War folklore.

Confusables. Bulgaria is the country; Bulgar is a member of the Turkic steppe people who founded the first Balkan state; Bulgarian is the modern nationality, language and adjective. Bessarabia is a different region (modern Moldova and south-western Ukraine) and is not etymologically connected, despite the similar sound. Bulgari is the Italian luxury brand, spelled without the final a.

Register. The word is neutral and geographical in ordinary use. Its historical senses are scholarly; its technical senses are scientific or journalistic. It carries no pejorative force, though in the 19th century Western travel writing sometimes used "Bulgarian" to evoke Ottoman atrocity, after the "Bulgarian horrors" controversy of 1876.
