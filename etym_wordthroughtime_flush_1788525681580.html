- [Late 13th–14th c.] - Middle English fluschen emerges in hunting and heraldic contexts, meaning to start up suddenly or burst forth into flight (of game birds) - [perception: vigorous, rustic, sporting vocabulary of foresters and falconers]
- [15th–16th c.] - semantic expansion via onomatopoeia and French flux: denotes sudden rushing torrents of water and the crimson rush of blood to the face - [perception: vivid, physical, capturing bodily passions and surging natural streams]
- [17th–18th c.] - enters joinery, carpentry, and masonry to describe surfaces that meet in an unbroken plane; card-playing adopts flush for hands of uniform suit - [perception: precise mechanical craftsmanship and gentlemanly gaming slang]
- [19th c.] - Romantic and Aesthetic literature (Keats, Tennyson, Wilde) elevates the word to a delicate emblem of poetic awakening, floral color, and youthful passion - [perception: tender, lyrical, emotionally resonant aesthetic standard]
- [20th–21st c.] - technological specialization in aviation (flush rivets), computing (flushing cache/buffers), medicine (vasomotor flushing), and typography (flush left/right) - [perception: ubiquitous, highly efficient technical and expressive term across multiple industries]
