Proposed PIE origin — *steg- / *stak- "to be stiff, to stand firm; a stake, pole" (reconstructed; the connection is DEBATED but widely accepted for the Germanic *stak- set). - Proto-Germanic *stakaną "to thrust, push" (reconstructed; from the PIE base *stak-, cf. English stake, stack) → Old Norse staka "to push, cause to stumble" (attested) → Old Norse stakra "to push, shove, totter, stagger" (frequentative formation, attested) → Middle English stakeren "to totter, reel, stagger" (attested from the early 14th c.; a Scandinavian loan) → Middle English stagger (altered form, attested from the mid-15th c.) → English stagger "to walk or move unsteadily as if about to fall" → past participle staggered "moved unsteadily; also shocked, bewildered" (transitive figurative sense attested from 1550s) → **STAGGERED** (Modern English). Notes and debates: - The derivation is often given as ON stakra, frequentative of staka "to push," from Proto-Germanic *stak-; the connection to PIE *steg-/*stak- "to be stiff, stand firm; stake" is a reconstruction and the OED treats the ultimate origin as uncertain ("probably Scandinavian"), so the PIE step should be read as the proposed, debated ancestor rather than an assured fact. - The alteration of Middle English stakeren to stagger in the mid-15th century involves the change of -k- to -g- and a metathesis-like reshaping; the details of this change are not fully explained and are best described as an obscure dialectal alteration. - The frequentative suffix -er- (as in stakeren) is the same pattern seen in wander (from wend), flicker, and glimmer: it marks repeated, iterative action — here, repeated pushing that makes a body reel. - Related senses branching off the English verb (not stages of the chain but useful record): transitive stagger "to bewilder, amaze" (1550s); noun stagger (c. 1600); the staggers "a reeling veterinary disease of horses" (1570s); the scheduling/arranging sense "to offset in time or position" (attested 1856). The same Germanic stem gives the siblings stake and stack (the "stiff pole" image) and the continental cognates Middle Dutch staggeren, Dutch staggelen, and German staggeln, all with the tottering sense.
