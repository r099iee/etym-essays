rowlocks — plural noun; in British English (American English: oarlocks) the fittings mounted on the gunwale (top edge) of a rowing boat that hold the oars in place and act as the pivot point, or fulcrum, against which the rower pulls. Each rowlock cups the shaft of the oar and lets it rock fore-and-aft through a stable arc, allowing the rower to apply power through the blade. Without rowlocks, an oar would slip and slide out of position with every stroke.

Primary sense — nautical hardware. A rowlock is the mechanical interface between oar and boat. Traditionally it was a U-shaped metal or wooden fitting, or a pair of vertical wooden pegs (thole-pins) driven into the gunwale, or simply a notch cut into the top strake. The oar shaft is laid into the rowlock; the load of the stroke presses the shaft against the back of the fitting, and the rowlock's fixed position converts the rower's pull into forward thrust.

  • Mechanical function: The rowlock is the fulcrum of a lever — the oar is the lever arm, the water provides resistance at the blade, and the rowlock is the fixed pivot. It simultaneously locks the oar laterally (preventing it from sliding sideways off the boat) and allows free fore-and-aft rocking.
  • Physical forms: closed or open "gates" on racing shells, U-shaped metal swivels on working boats, thole-pins in traditional craft, and simple gunwale notches in the most ancient designs. Modern racing rowlocks are swivelling "gates" that open and close around the oar and rotate as the blade is feathered.
  • Positioning: Mounted on the gunwale (side rail) amidships or slightly aft of centre, one per oar position; in sculling (two oars per rower) two rowlocks per seat, offset to stagger the oars.
  • In context: In the source passage the "jolting noise of the _____" is the rhythmic clack-and-creak of oars working loosely in their rowlocks as the boat rises and falls in the swell — a sound distinctive to rowed small boats.

Polysemy & secondary/related senses. As a plural noun, rowlocks names the whole set of fittings on a boat; it anchors a small cluster of associated nautical and modern usages:

  • Regional synonymy: Rowlock (BrE/Commonwealth) vs oarlock (AmE) name the identical device; oarlock is the more transparent compound ("oar + lock") and dominates North American usage.
  • Pronunciation trap: Standard British pronunciation is /ˈrɒlɪk/ — the two syllables "row" + "lock" collapse into something close to "roll-ick" — which is why the word is sometimes misspelled or misread as rollock. The spelling rowlock masks the reduced vowel of the first syllable.
  • Figurative extension of the "lock" element: Though rowlocks itself stays concrete, the "lock" element carries the ancient sense of "fastening, enclosure" (Old English loc), so a rowlock is literally "the thing that locks the oar into rowing position" — a physical metaphor of constraint and security at sea.
  • Compound relatives: The word shares its family with oarlock, thole-pin, rowing gate, swivel, and the colloquial rowlock/rollock spelling variant found in older nautical writing.

Historical bridge & semantic reasoning. The compound is transparent: Old English rōwan "to row" + loc "fastening, lock." Older English craft used a single thole-pin or a withe loop; the innovation of a U-shaped "lock" fitting that gripped the oar on both sides and served as a pivot dates from the medieval period, and the word rowlok is attested in Middle English (c. 1450). The semantic logic is that of a mechanical pivot-plus-fastener: to row you need a lock. The word thus encodes the engineering insight that oars must be both held in place (locked) and free to pivot.
