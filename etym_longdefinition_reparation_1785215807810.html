The noun reparation denotes the making of amends for a wrong or injury, usually through compensation, restitution, or the restoration of what has been damaged or lost. It names both the act of making good a harm and the material or symbolic thing offered by way of amends. In the sentence that anchors this card — "Mill made a substantial reparation for the calamity for which he was responsible by inducing the aggrieved author to accept half of the £200 which he offered" — the word refers to the aftermath of the notorious episode of 1835, when the only manuscript of Carlyle's History of the French Revolution, lent to Mill, was destroyed by a servant who used it to light a fire. Mill insisted on compensating the aggrieved author, and the "substantial reparation" named here is the £100 that Carlyle, induced by Mill's persistence, finally accepted out of the £200 offered. The word thus moves between the concrete — an actual payment making good a material loss — and the grave, dignified formality of acknowledged responsibility. Core senses and shades of meaning:
  • Concrete amends: payment, compensation, or the giving of something to make good a loss or damage, as money, goods, or restoration of property.
  • Moral and personal amends: the satisfaction offered for a wrong done to a person — an apology, a gesture of atonement, or restitution of a harmed relationship; "to make reparation to someone for something."
  • Legal usage: the act of compensating for harm or loss, whether voluntary or ordered by a court; closely tied to the law of damages, restitution, and redress.
  • Repair and restoration (older, concrete sense): historically the word first meant the act of repairing or restoring a damaged thing — mending a building, a wall, a garment — and this physical sense survives in the plural-phrase and in the closely related verb "repair."
  • Historical and political sense (plural, reparations): compensation demanded from a defeated or responsible nation for war damage and injustice — most famously the German war reparations after 1918 and 1945; in modern public discourse, "reparations" also names demands for compensation for slavery, colonialism, and systemic injustice.
Register, grammar, and collocations:
  • Register: formal, grave, and dignified. Reparation belongs to the language of law, diplomacy, morality, and history, and it carries a seriousness that lighter words like "apology" do not.
  • Grammar: usually a mass (uncountable) noun; the plural reparations denotes payments or compensation made for large-scale harm (war reparations). Common constructions: "to make reparation for," "a reparation to," "substantial reparation," "reparation of a wrong."
  • Common partners: "war reparations," "make reparations," "financial reparation," "reparation for the injury," "reparation owed," "demand reparation."
  • Distinctive contrast: reparation stresses the restoration of what was lost or the satisfaction of a wrong, whereas compensation stresses payment for loss and restitution stresses the return of a specific thing.
Historical and etymological bridge:
  • Reparation ultimately rests on Latin reparāre, "to restore, mend, make ready again," from re- ("again, back") + parāre ("to make ready, prepare, provide"). The concrete meaning — mending or rebuilding — was the earliest.
  • English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century (Middle English reparacioun), chiefly with the concrete sense of repairing or restoring; the moral and legal sense of "making amends for a wrong" developed in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
  • The plural reparations acquired its huge historical-political meaning in the early twentieth century, when the word became inseparable from the indemnities imposed on defeated nations after the First World War, and later from demands for historical justice — a dramatic widening from private mending to global reckoning.
Synonyms in brief: compensation (neutral, often legal, no necessary fault), restitution (returning something to its rightful owner or condition), amends (general, often moral), damages (narrow court-ordered award), redress (setting right an injustice), atonement (moral/religious expiation), indemnity (financial security or compensation). Reparation uniquely bridges the physical act of repair and the moral act of making good a wrong.
