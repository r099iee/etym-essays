- c. 1300 - English first borrows feint (spelled faint/feint alike) as an adjective meaning 'feigned, false' or 'spiritless, weak', straight from the Old French participle; the 'weak' strand will harden into FAINT.
- 1680s - the noun arrives as fencing and drill terminology from French feinte: a false attack in swordplay; perception: technical, precise, duellist's jargon.
- 18th century - the word leaves the salle d'armes for the camp and the world: military manuals describe feints against fortresses, and novelists write of feints of sentiment and intention; perception: crafty but respectable - skilful rather than wicked.
- 19th century - prizefighting adopts the feint (jab-and-draw), literature makes the 'feint of ...' construction standard, and the paper trade's 'feint ruling' (from the old spelling of faint) splits off a harmless commercial sense.
- 20th century - both World Wars make the feint a formal part of deception doctrine (dummy tanks, sham landings), boxers and footballers feint by reflex, and espionage fiction loves the word; perception: professional, cool, precise.
- 21st century - political and corporate commentary uses feint for any calculated misdirection ('a feint to distract from the real agenda'), and fighting-game players speak of feints in esports; perception: sharper and colder than 'trick' - the word of the strategist, not the grifter.
