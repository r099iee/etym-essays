Undercuts is a compound, and its story is the story of two very different ancestors meeting in one word: under, a solid native English preposition that has been in the language since its beginning, and cut, a mysterious newcomer that appears only in the thirteenth century. Together they have generated a family of meanings so wide that a single spelling now covers dragons' deaths and barbers' shears.

1. The ancestry of UNDER. The first half descends from PIE *n̥dʰér ("under, below"), which became Proto-Germanic *under and then Old English under ("under, beneath, below; among"), attested from the eighth century. It is one of the oldest, most stable words in the language, so old that its meaning has barely shifted in fifteen hundred years. As a prefix, under- developed three productive ideas in Old and Middle English: position (underneath), subordination (under-king), and insufficiency (undercooked, underpaid).

2. The mystery of CUT. The second half is far more exotic. English inherited no word for "cut" from Old English; the language made do with ceorfan (carve) and snīþan (cut, slice). Then around 1250, in Middle English, cutten appears out of nowhere and begins driving the native words aside. Its origin is unknown. No convincing Germanic ancestor is attested, and scholars have proposed everything from a Scandinavian form to a continental dialect word to a Welsh borrowing; none is proven. The OED frankly records it as of uncertain origin. What matters historically is that the newcomer won: within two centuries cut and its compounds had displaced the native vocabulary, and English gained one of its most productive word-bases.

3. The compound is coined (14th century). Once both halves were available, English combined them: the verb undercut appears in the fourteenth century with the literal sense "to cut from beneath" — undercutting branches, wood, meat, or turf. The noun followed, naming the notch or stroke made from below. For the first two centuries the word stayed close to this physical picture.

4. The figurative leap (15th–16th centuries). Very early, the physical image took a moral turn: to undercut a plan, a reputation, or an honour was to destroy it secretly, cutting away its foundation. This is the sense of undermine long before undermining was a mining word of wider currency. The sneaky, from-below attack became the defining mental picture.

5. The fighting sense (17th–19th centuries). In fencing manuals and accounts of swordplay, an undercut is a rising cut delivered from beneath the opponent's guard — a clever, slightly dishonourable stroke. The nineteenth-century prize ring borrowed the word for a rising blow from below; boxers knew the "undercut" decades before "uppercut" became standard in the 1930s. Tolkien's 1937 sentence — "stabs and jabs and undercuts" in the art of dragon-slaying — preserves exactly this older fighting vocabulary.

6. The commercial revolution (late 19th century). The industrial age gave the word its modern engine-room sense. By the late nineteenth century, to undercut meant to undersell a competitor, to offer lower prices that cut the ground from under a rival business. The image of cutting at the foundation was perfect for the rhetoric of price wars, dumping, and wage competition, and undercut became a standard term of economics and journalism.

7. The modern spread (20th–21st centuries). The twentieth century broadened the word in every direction: arguments and authorities are undercut by evidence and leaks; cliffs and riverbanks are undercut by erosion; tennis and golf strokes are undercut for backspin; and in the 2010s the barber's undercut — hair shaved beneath, long on top — made the word a fashion headline. Each new sense is the same compound re-read: something cut underneath.

8. Summary. From an ancient preposition and a mysterious newcomer, English built a verb, and from that verb grew a family: the sword-stroke of the dragon-slayers, the price-war weapon of commerce, the erosion of cliffs, the spin of a tennis ball, and the cut of a barber. Undercuts is a masterclass in how a simple compound can keep one image — the strike from below — alive through eight hundred years of new worlds.
