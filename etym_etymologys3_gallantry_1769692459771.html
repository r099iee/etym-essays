From merry-making to medals: the evolution of GALLANTRY

The linguistic biography of gallantry is a vibrant story of how festive Frankish merry-making evolved into medieval courtly love, Renaissance elegance, and modern military honor. The word's origins lie in the West Germanic root meaning "well, good, pleasing" — the same root that, staying in England, produced native English well, wealth, and welfare. On the continent, however, the Franks carried the word *wala into the Romance-speaking regions of Gaul, and there it took an unexpected turn. In Old French the Germanic initial w- hardened to g-, and the root produced the noun gale: pleasure, high spirits, festivity, and merry-making. From gale the French coined the verb galer, meaning to live sumptuously, make merry, put on fine clothes, and enjoy life to the fullest. At this stage the word has nothing to do with courage or courtesy: it is the vocabulary of celebration.

The courtly turn of the fourteenth century. During the 14th century, French society transformed the present participle of galer into the adjective and noun galant. A man who was galant was not merely cheerful; he was dashing, stylishly dressed, spirited, and attentive to ladies in the tradition of courtly love that the troubadours had cultivated in the High Middle Ages. The social background matters: the late medieval and Renaissance courts made polished self-presentation a career skill, and the galant man — the courtier who combined wit, dress, gallantry in speech, and graceful deference — became the ideal of aristocratic male behavior. This ideal was codified in conduct literature such as Castiglione's Book of the Courtier (1528), and it spread across Europe.

From galant to galanterie. As court culture flourished during the Renaissance, French formed the abstract noun galanterie to name the art of paying elegant compliments, displaying courtly manners, and courting women with polished grace. Neighboring languages borrowed and built on the same root: Italian produced gala (festive attire and celebration, source of English gala) and galante; Spanish produced galán (a handsome courtier or leading man) and galantería. English, importing the word through French (with the spelling influenced by Italian galante), took up both gallant and gallantry in the late 16th century — gallantry itself is attested from the 1590s. Sound-wise, the French ga- of galant was anglicized as /ɡæ-/ and later /ɡˈæ/, while the modern RP pronunciation /ɡˈaləntɹi/ shows the English treatment of the French stress and vowel quality.

The double life in English. In English, gallantry acquired a dual personality that has lasted to the present. On the battlefields of the 17th and 18th centuries, it came to describe dashing heroism, conspicuous courage, and noble spirit under fire. This is the sense enshrined in military institutions: the British "Medal for Gallantry" and the naming of honours "for gallantry" treat the word as the official vocabulary of exceptional courage. In the drawing-rooms of 18th- and 19th-century polite society, however, gallantry specialized in the polished performance of romantic politeness, flattering compliments, and gentlemanly attentiveness toward women. These two senses are not as far apart as they seem: both presuppose a man who is gallant, one whose conduct is marked by spirit and display, whether the display is courage in danger or courtesy in love.

Gallantry in Austen and the modern register. By the time Jane Austen wrote Pride and Prejudice, "gallantry" had become a key item of social currency: gentlemen were expected to practice it, but sharp-witted heroines like Elizabeth Bennet could satirize it when it substituted for genuine feeling. Her remark that Bingley's admiration owed "no thanks to his gallantry" draws the line between the real (involuntary admiration of beauty) and the performed (conventional compliments) — a distinction the comedy of manners lives on. In modern English the social sense has aged: "gallantry" toward women now sounds old-fashioned, even patronizing, and is usually used with irony or historical flavor, while the military sense keeps its heroic, formal register in medals and citations. The word thus carries in miniature the whole history of aristocratic manners — from a party, to a courtly compliment, to a medal for courage.
