Matins (also Mattins) is the morning office of prayer in the Western Christian Church — the canonical hour appointed for the first public worship of the day. In the Roman Catholic Divine Office before the reforms of Vatican II (1970), Matins was the great night office, originally sung around midnight or before dawn, consisting of psalms, lessons from Scripture and the Fathers, responsories, and, in its Sunday and festal form, the hymn Te Deum. It was historically paired with Lauds, the office of praise at daybreak, so that "Matins and Lauds" together formed the dawn worship of the monastery. In Anglican usage, Matins (or Morning Prayer) is the principal morning service of the Book of Common Prayer.

In Wordsworth's Prelude (the source context of this card), the word is used figuratively: "Days of sweet leisure, taxed with patient thought Abstruse, nor wanting punctual service high, / Matins and vespers of harmonious verse!" — the poet's daily practice of verse is a "punctual service" (a service kept punctually, as the canonical hours are kept), with Matins as its morning office. Poetry is thus imagined as a liturgy performed morning and evening.

Principal senses today:

  • Liturgical (Roman Catholic): the night office of psalms and readings, celebrated before dawn; since 1970 officially called the "Office of Readings" and detached from the hour of day, though "Matins" survives in traditionalist and historical usage.
  • Liturgical (Anglican): Morning Prayer, "Matins/a" or matins, the daily morning service of the Book of Common Prayer (Morning Prayer of 1549 and 1662), with its canticles Venite, Te Deum, and Benedictus.
  • Historical devotion: in medieval Books of Hours, matins is the first of the Hours of the Virgin, lavishly illuminated and recited by the laity.
  • Figurative and poetic: any morning observance, ritual, or creative discipline — "the matins of the birds" (the dawn chorus), "his morning matins" (a private practice).
  • Etymology-anchored use: "matutinal" (of the morning) is the adjective from the same Latin root; "matinée" (a morning performance) is the modern French offshoot — the word family radiates from Matuta, goddess of the dawn.

The historical bridge of the word is straightforwardly chronological: the Christian community inherited the Jewish and early-Christian custom of night and dawn prayer; the Latin church named the dawn office from mātūtīnus, "of the morning"; Old French matines carried the term into the vernaculars of medieval Europe, and Middle English adopted it in the 13th century.

Register: Matins is now a specialist, historical, or formal word — in speech it survives chiefly among church musicians, liturgists, and in traditionalist Anglican and Catholic circles. As a cultural reference it preserves the texture of monastic time-keeping, when the office of the night was the monk's greatest labour.
