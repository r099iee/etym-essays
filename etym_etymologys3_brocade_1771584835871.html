From pointed teeth to embossed silk: the story of brocade. The biography of brocade begins not with cloth but with a jab. Its earliest recoverable ancestor is the Latin adjective broccus, used of teeth that project or jut forward — a word the Romans themselves seem to have borrowed, for it has no secure Indo-European pedigree. Scholars suspect a pre-Latin source: some point to a Gaulish (Celtic) word for the badger, whose pointed snout and gapped grin offered a memorable image, and the occasional appearance of Celtic-looking forms for "pointed" across the Mediterranean suggests a substrate word that Latin absorbed early. What matters for our word is the mental picture broccus fixed: something sharp, prominent, sticking out. This image of projection is the germ from which the whole family grows, and it explains why a fabric could eventually be named for a tooth. No PIE ancestry can be responsibly asserted for broccus, and any tree that forces it into a Proto-Indo-European root is inventing history.

From broccus the Romance languages bred a productive family. Vulgar Latin took the adjective as a noun, brocca, "a pointed object, a spike," and from that spike two branches diverge. One is a vessel: Italian brocca, "a pitcher or ewer" (so called, it is thought, from its jutting, pointed mouth), which surfaces in French as broc, "a jug." The other, richer branch is vegetal and technical. In Italian, brocco meant "a sprout, a shoot, a stump, a nail" — and, by extension, any small projecting thing; the diminutive broccolo, "a little sprout, a cabbage shoot," gave English broccoli in the seventeenth century, and the "spit, skewer" branch of Gallo-Romance gave English broach and brooch.

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, when Lucca, Venice, and Genoa were the textile workshops of Europe, found a use for the projecting image at the loom: broccare meant "to stud, to prick, to work with raised figures," and broccato named the past participle — cloth "studded" with raised work, the extra threads that float across the back of the silk and loop up into embossed flowers and arabesques. It is a small marvel of etymological coherence that the textile, the vegetable, the ewer, the spit, and the brooch should all be named for the same thing sticking out.

  • Origin and debate: broccus is a Latin adjective of substrate origin, possibly Gaulish; no PIE root can be responsibly reconstructed, and the badger hypothesis, though attractive, is unproven.
  • Sound and form: Latin broccus → Italian brocco / broccare / broccato → French brocart → English brocade; the terminal -ade in English follows the Romance abstract/collective pattern found in parade and colonnade.
  • Semantic drift: "pointed, jutting" → "a spike, a sprout, a projecting thing" → "to stud or emboss" → "cloth studded with raised figures" → "rich figured silk."
  • Socio-cultural context: the word rode the silk trade — the brocades of Lucca and Venice in the 14th–15th centuries, the French brocarts of the 17th century, the English import records of the 1650s; in English it entered as an exotic mercantile term and became the fixed name of court and church luxury.
  • Literary attestations: from the mercantile ledgers and poets of the seventeenth century (Dryden and the Restoration stage use brocade as shorthand for aristocratic display) to the Victorian novelists who set their heiresses and dowagers in "gold brocade," the word has always signalled inherited, ceremonial wealth.

Modern use has widened the word from silk to any figured cloth — brocaded velvet, brocaded damask — and softened its register from pure aristocratic luxury to refined traditional ornament, still upmarket but now bought by the home decorator. Yet the etymological image holds: the pattern is not printed or stitched on, it is "pricked out," studded into the weave like the jutting tooth that gave the word its name.
