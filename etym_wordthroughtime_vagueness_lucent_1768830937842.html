vagueness:
- The wandering sense (16th c.) - vague enters English with its Latin image of roaming: what is vague is unsettled, straying, not yet fixed in place
- The mental sense takes hold (17th-18th c.) - vagueness names speech and thought that refuse definition - an indefinite outline, an imprecise meaning - a quiet reproach in criticism
- The atmospheric turn (19th-20th c.) - the word drifts from meaning to perception: vague horizons, vague longings, the curious vagueness of a land whose edges will not hold still
- Contemporary - it covers both a censure (vague writing) and an aesthetic (vague premonitions), always keeping the image of something that drifts away from definition

lucent:
- A learned borrowing (late 16th c.) - lucent enters as a high literary word for shining, glowing, from Latin lucens, 'shining'
- Light without edges (17th-19th c.) - poets and prose stylists use it of air, water and flame that glow softly and evenly, transmitting light while dissolving form
- Contemporary - rare and ornamental, it names a brilliance that illuminates yet blurs; its kin lucid has carried off the intellectual 'clear', leaving lucent purely luminous
