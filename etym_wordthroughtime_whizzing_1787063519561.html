- Middle English (c. 1350–1450) - Emerged in Middle English as 'whisen' / 'whissen', an echoic verb imitating the sound of arrows, stones, or wind cutting through the air.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th Century) - Solidified in spelling as 'whiz' / 'whizz', used by Shakespeare and Renaissance playwrights for the sound of fiery meteors and weapons in combat.
- 18th to 19th Century - Expanded alongside gunpowder ballistics to describe the terrifying flight of musket balls, cannonballs, and early railway locomotives.
- 20th Century - Broadened into cognitive and colloquial idioms ('whizzing through work') and mechanical appliances (food processors whizzing ingredients).
- Contemporary English - Widely established across physical, literary, psychological, and domestic registers to convey high-speed motion with sensory vividness.
