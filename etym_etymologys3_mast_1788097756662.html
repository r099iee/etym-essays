MAST is one of the oldest words of the sea in English — a term that all the Germanic peoples already shared when their ships first met the Atlantic, and whose history tracks the whole development of sailing, from the single pole of a prehistoric boat to the antenna towers of the wireless age.

The Indo-European horizon: PIE *mazdos. The reconstructed ancestor is *mazdos, “a pole, staff, mast”, an Indo-European noun of carpentry whose most distant European relatives are the mast itself and the Latin mālus, “mast, upright pole”. The comparison is old and standard: Germanic *mastaz (English mast, Old Norse mastr, Dutch mast, German Mast) and Latin mālus are usually united under *mazdos, the Germanic forms showing *-zd- preserved and the Latin form showing its reduction, a change sometimes described as the “Sabine” treatment of the cluster. The details of that Latin change are debated in the handbooks (de Vaan’s etymological dictionary treats mālus as of uncertain etymology while noting the usual derivation), and an old, now generally rejected suggestion connecting the word with Greek mastós “breast” (as “swelling, protuberance”) is no longer followed; the standard line, with its caveat, is the one recorded here. What the distribution shows is that a technical maritime word for the sail-pole existed in Indo-European times — testimony that the speakers of the proto-language, or their immediate successors, built and rigged vessels with sails.

The Germanic sea-world: *mastaz. By the time of the earliest Germanic records the mast was part of the common technical vocabulary of the North Sea and Baltic peoples: Old English mæst renders Latin mālus in early glosses, Old Norse mastr appears in the sagas and in runic and skaldic verse, and the word is present in Old High German (mast) and Gothic (*mastus on the evidence of derivates). The uniformity of the word across Germanic points to shared seafaring knowledge long before the Viking Age — the age of the clinker-built longship, whose single mast stepped amidships carried a square sail of wool or linen, was already ancient when the Viking raids began.

Old English mæst and the scriptorium. In Old English, mæst is a working word of ships and rigging, appearing in glosses, prose, and verse, always in the concrete sense of “the ship’s sail-bearing pole”. The compound mæsten, “to provide with a mast”, is recorded early, and the word’s stable form — mæst — shows no significant sound change on the way to Middle English mast: the East Anglian and Northern spelling with -a- (rather than the metrical -æ-) was fixed by the fourteenth century, and modern MAST has simply continued it.

  • Prehistory: PIE *mazdos “pole, mast” — a sail-pole word older than any written document in Europe.
  • Germanic era: *mastaz across all branches — a shared sea-vocabulary of the North Sea and Baltic.
  • Old English: mæst in glosses and prose; the single-mast ship of the Saxon and Viking age.
  • Middle English: mast fixed in spelling and sense; the word grows with the growing ship.

The age of sail: the mast as world-tree. From the later Middle Ages to the nineteenth century the mast was the largest single piece of wood most people ever saw — the mainmast of a first-rate man-of-war rose over 100 feet, built of Baltic pine and fir shipped as “mast timber” to the naval dockyards. Rigging science multiplied the poles: foremast, mainmast, mizzenmast; topmast, topgallant mast, royal mast — each a mast above a mast, so that the carpenter’s word scaled upward with the ship. The common sailor lived “before the mast”, forward of the foremast, in the forecastle; Richard Henry Dana’s Two Years Before the Mast (1840) made the phrase the title of a famous book and the emblem of the sailor’s calling. Meanwhile the word climbed into ceremony and journalism: a flag at “half-mast” (lowered to honour the dead) is recorded from the seventeenth century, and “masthead” — the top of the mast — was transferred in nineteenth-century newspaper practice to the printed name and imprint at the top of the front page, so that a ship’s word came to head every newspaper in the language.

The wireless age: mast goes ashore. With radio telegraphy, the twentieth century took the mast onto land: the “radio mast” and later the “television mast” are tall lattice or tubular structures carrying aerials, guyed like the shrouds of a ship — the engineer’s vocabulary consciously borrowed the sailor’s. In modern yachting the word returned to the sea in new materials (the weight-saving hollow mast, the modern carbon-fibre mast of the racing yacht), and the ship’s mast, in memory and photographs, still stands at the centre of every harbour image — as it does in Oscar Wilde’s The Happy Prince (1888), where the Swallow, having seen the whole world, sits on the mast of a great vessel and feels how little the wonders of travel matter beside the Prince’s sacrifice. A note of caution for the learner: the homophone mast “beech-mast, oak-mast” — the fallen nuts on which swine and game are fattened — is a different word, from a Germanic root meaning “food, fattening” (perhaps continuing PIE *mad- “moist, juicy”), and has no connection with the ship’s mast except the accident of identical spelling and sound.
