Buffeted belongs to a small, colourful word family whose ultimate origin is genuinely obscure — the kind of word historical linguists call 'expressive' or 'imitative', born in the sound and gesture of a blow rather than descended in a neat line from a reconstructed root. Its documented history, however, is perfectly clear, and runs from medieval France to modern English without interruption.

The medieval French base. Old French had the noun bufe (also bofe), recorded from about the eleventh century, meaning 'a blow, a slap, a cuff with the hand or fist'. The word is of uncertain origin; the most widely accepted view is that it is onomatopoeic or expressive — the sound and feel of a soft, dull blow (compare the English dialect buff, 'a blow', and buff, 'to strike', and the modern biff, all of the same imitative stamp). Some scholars have proposed a Germanic source, and a few a Celtic one, but none is securely documented; the honest verdict is that the root is a sound-symbolic creation, coined and re-coined in the same shape across languages.

From noun to verb: buffeter. From the noun bufe, Old French formed the frequentative verb buffeter, 'to strike repeatedly, to cuff, to slap again and again', and by the twelfth century the noun buffet itself is attested in French in the sense 'a blow, a slap'. The verb, with its frequentative suffix, already carries the crucial nuance of repetition: a single buffet is a blow, but to buffet is to rain blows.

Borrowing into English. English borrowed the noun buffet, 'a blow or stroke with the hand', in the thirteenth century from French buffet, and the verb to buffet, 'to strike or knock about', in the fourteenth century from French buffeter. The borrowing belongs to the great wave of French vocabulary that entered English after the Conquest and during the later medieval centuries, and it arrived already equipped with its modern grammatical shape. Chaucer and his contemporaries use the noun freely for blows exchanged in quarrels and in battle.

Early modern senses. In early modern English to buffet meant literally 'to cuff, to strike with the hand or fist', and it is common in the Authorised Version of the Bible (1611), where Saint Paul speaks of being 'buffeted' — struck, beaten, harassed. Shakespeare uses the verb and noun with relish for physical blows and for the figurative battering of fate. The word was also pressed into meteorological service early: ships, shores, and later trees and buildings were 'buffeted by the wind' and 'buffeted by the waves', a use that remains entirely standard today.

The figurative turn. By the seventeenth century the metaphorical sense was firmly established: to be buffeted is to be knocked about not only by fists and storms but by fortune, misfortune, adversity, and fate. Milton and his contemporaries write of being 'buffeted by fate'; the eighteenth century extends the image to 'buffeted by the world'; and the nineteenth century adds the institutional and economic victims — 'buffeted by the market', 'buffeted by events'. The common thread is the victim's passivity: the buffeted person does not strike back but is simply knocked this way and that.

Modern usage. In modern English buffeted remains vigorous in three zones: the literal (wind, weather, seas, crowds), the journalistic-figurative (governments, companies, celebrities 'buffeted by' scandal, criticism, or recession), and the literary, where it survives in dignified prose as the verb for being beaten about by life. Its association with bruised — as in 'bruised and buffeted' — is a fixed collocation for the aftermath of any thorough knocking about.

A note on the unrelated buffet. The word must not be confused with the homograph buffet (the sideboard or self-service meal), which descends from an entirely different Old French bufet meaning 'stool, bench, sideboard', of equally obscure but unrelated origin. The two words sound alike in Modern English but have never had anything to do with one another.

Summary of the chain:

  • bufe (Old French, 11th c., 'a blow, a slap') — of expressive, imitative origin
  • buffet (Old French, 12th c., noun, 'a blow, a slap')
  • buffeter (Old French, frequentative verb, 'to strike repeatedly, cuff')
  • buffet (English noun, 13th c., 'a blow or stroke')
  • to buffet (English verb, 14th c., 'to strike, knock about')
  • buffeted (English past participle/adjective, 'struck repeatedly, battered, knocked about by blows, wind, or fortune')

The history of buffeted is short, French, and violent: an expressive word for a slap passes from the hand of a medieval striker into the storm and the metaphor of fate, and in every use it has kept the sound and the sense of being knocked about without end.
