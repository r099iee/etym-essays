(Origin uncertain; probably imitative/expressive — an onomatopoeic Germanic formation suggesting a sudden crashing or rustling noise, cf. Middle Low German brasken “to be noisy, to crackle, to boast” and Dutch bras “noise, bravado”; the earliest pre-English shape is unattested and the ultimate origin is unknown) (prehistoric, before the 16th c.) → Scots and Northern English dialect: brash, noun (16th c.) — “a sudden violent rush or crash; a sudden shower of rain; a short sudden bout of illness” → English dialect and technical usage: brash, noun (17th–19th c.) — “fragments of broken rock or ice; brittle, crumbling timber; lopped branches” (also in brash ice) → English: brash, adjective, “rash, hasty, impetuous in temper” (first attested mid-19th c.) → Modern English: brash, adjective, “loud, tactless, aggressively self-assertive; (of appearance) garishly bright and showy” (present-day) NOTE (debated/nuanced): Many popular etymologies link brash to the Proto-Indo-European root *bhreg- “to break” (through a hypothesized Germanic *brask-), and the noun senses of “breaking”, “crash”, and “burst” make that connection plausible; however, the standard dictionaries treat the origin as uncertain and probably imitative, and the *bhreg- linkage is not securely attested in the direct line, so no PIE stage is asserted with confidence here.
