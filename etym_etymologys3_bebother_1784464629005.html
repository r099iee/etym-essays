Bebother is a transparent English compound whose two halves have very different biographies: one half is a securely attested Germanic inheritance, the other a recent colloquial word of uncertain, probably Anglo-Irish origin. Reading the word is therefore to read two histories at once — the ancient story of the intensive prefix be- and the modern, mostly unwritten story of bother itself.

The prefix be- continues Old English be-/bi-, from Proto-Germanic *bi-, itself usually traced to the PIE preposition *h₁epi “at, on, near, by.” Across Germanic the same element does the same work: German be- (besetzen “to occupy,” bedauern “to pity,” bemerken “to notice”), Gothic bi-, and Old Norse bi- all build transitive, intensive, or “around/about” verbs. In English the prefix has been extraordinarily productive over a thousand years: beset (“to surround, attack”), bedevil (“to plague,” 1570s), bewilder (17th century, from wilder “to be lost”), belittle (1780s, attributed to Thomas Jefferson), befog, befuddle (19th century), becalm, become (be- + come, “to come to be”), and behoove. The prefix typically does one of three things: it transitivizes an intransitive verb, it adds an “around/over/about” spatial nuance, or — as here — it intensifies, making the action total and directional. Attached to bother, it yields “to bother thoroughly” or “to bring bother upon” — a full besieging with annoyance rather than a light nudge.

The base bother is a different matter entirely. The verb and noun rise in English in the early eighteenth century (often cited around 1718) and gain currency especially through Anglo-Irish literary use — Jonathan Swift, Richard Brinsley Sheridan, and the wider Irish-English corridor of the period. One strong etymological proposal, favoured by the OED, links English bother to Irish bodhar “deaf” and related formations that imply deafening with noise or wearying with clamour — hence trouble, pestering, fuss: to bother someone is, at the root, to deafen them with the racket of importunity. A competing or complementary line points to Scots bauther (and the wider pother/puther fuss-family), a verb of troubling, confusing, or making a to-do. English bother also sits naturally in the sound-symbolic cluster of pother and dither — words whose murmured b/p-vowel-th-r shapes express fuss and complaint. Either way, bother enters standard English as a colloquial word for vexation and importunity, and from the first it had a life as an interjection — “bother!” — the natural mild oath of a person vexed.

Timeline of bebother:

  • c. 1718: bother rises in English as verb and noun, strong in Anglo-Irish literary currency.
  • 18th–19th centuries: bother! consolidates as a gentle imprecation; the be- prefix continues to build new intensives.
  • Late 19th century: bebother is coined as the jocular intensive “to bother thoroughly,” used in mild oaths and comic complaint — “bebother it!”
  • 20th century: the word becomes a period-flavoured fossil of gentle imprecation, used in children’s stories and light domestic comedy.
  • 1937: Tolkien fixes it in modern memory as the second, homely half of Bilbo’s curse in The Hobbit — “Confusticate and bebother these dwarves!” — the plain, fully legible kitchen wish that follows learning’s thunder.

Semantically the word never drifts: it always means “to bother thoroughly, to vex,” and its distinctiveness is its mildness — an oath that vexes without wounding. The be- prefix supplies theatrical totalness; the murmur of bother underneath keeps it comic. In Tolkien’s scene the pairing is perfectly judged: the mock-Latin thunder of confusticate is answered by the homely, native, furniture-level word bebother, so that Bilbo’s curse descends from pseudo-scholarship to pure domestic grievance in a single breath.
