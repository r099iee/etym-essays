The journey of Tsarevna begins, improbably, in republican Rome with the family nickname of one ambitious clan. Caesar was the cognomen — the branch-name — of the Julii, borne most famously by Gaius Julius Caesar, and after Augustus made himself first emperor the name hardened into a title: every emperor was a Caesar, and the word travelled with the legions and the scriptures to the very edges of the known world, gathering sanctity as it went.

The next link is Gothic. When Bishop Wulfila translated the Bible into Gothic in the fourth century, he needed a word for the Greek Kaisar of the New Testament — render unto Caesar — and he wrote kaisar, fixing the imperial title in a Germanic tongue. From Gothic or from early direct contact with Byzantium the word passed to the Slavs as cěsarĭ, Old Church Slavonic цѣсарь, attested from the ninth-century Moravian mission onward, where it meant emperor in the grand Byzantine sense, the ruler in Constantinople, and by extension any supreme sovereign.

In East Slavic mouths cěsarĭ slowly contracted. Centuries of rapid church pronunciation wore the long word down to tsar, царь, a clipped, sibilant monosyllable with thunder in it. Muscovite grand princes used it tentatively of themselves, and in 1547 Ivan IV was crowned tsar in full form, making the contraction the official style of the Russian autocrat. To this stem the language added its native daughter-suffix -evna, the same feminine patronymic element that makes Ivanovna from Ivan, producing tsarevna, literally tsar's-daughter, on the exact model of korolevna, king's daughter, from korol, king — itself an old borrowing of the name Karl, Charlemagne.

For two centuries tsarevna was a working court title. The daughters of the tsar lived in the terem, the secluded women's quarters of the Kremlin, learnt needlework and scripture, and were married — often after conversion negotiations — into the great families of the realm or to foreign princes. Peter the Great's Table of Ranks and his importation of German court styles demoted the native word in official use in favour of grand duchess, but the peasantry, who never saw the court, kept tsarevna glowing in another world entirely: the wonder-tale, where Afanasyev's collectors found her by the hundred — frog-tsarevnas, warrior-tsarevnas like Maria Morevna, tsarevnas asleep behind iron doors.

  • Rome to the Danube: a family nickname becomes the supreme title of the civilised world, then a Gothic Bible-word, crossing from empire to barbarians without losing its majesty.
  • The Slavic contraction: missionary cěsarĭ wears down over six centuries into Muscovite tsar, gaining in menace what it loses in syllables, and Ivan the Terrible's coronation seals the change.
  • The daughter-suffix: native Slavic morphology does the rest, building tsarevna and tsarevich as transparent compounds, tsar's-daughter and tsar's-son.
  • The English borrowing: Ralston's Russian Folk Tales of 1873 and Bain's collections carry the word into English exactly in its fairy-tale sense, so that English has never used it bureaucratically, only romantically.
  • The modern afterlife: revolution abolished the rank in 1917, but opera, ballet, fantasy fiction, and parental irony keep the word warm, a century-old exile that never went home.

So tsarevna is a small museum of European history: a Roman nickname, a Gothic scripture-word, a Byzantine imperial claim, a Muscovite contraction, a Slavic suffix, and an English fairy-tale borrowing — six strata of conquest, faith, and storytelling packed into three syllables, ending, as the best stories do, with three sisters in a city set amid pleasant meads.
