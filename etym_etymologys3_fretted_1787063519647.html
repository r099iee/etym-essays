THE WORRYING, GNAWING VERB: FROM OLD ENGLISH FRETAN

The dominant modern verb fret descends from Old English fretan "to devour, eat up", a strong verb (class V: fretan — fræt — frǣton — freten) formed from the intensive prefix for- ("completely, away") plus etan "to eat". The literal meaning was the eating of animals: fretan was the word for beasts feeding. Its exact cognates are German fressen (Modern German "to eat, of animals"), Dutch vreten, and Gothic fra-itan — all built on the same prefix-plus-eat compound. The intensifying for- gives the sense "to eat up entirely, consume greedily". The underlying root is Proto-Indo-European *h₁ed- "to eat", one of the most stable roots in the family: Latin edere "to eat", Greek édein, and Sanskrit átti all descend from it.

PHONETIC SHIFT: FROM FRETAN TO FRET

The Old English verb fretan underwent the ordinary reductions of late Old English and Middle English: the final -an inflection was lost, the medial vowel weakened, and the two-syllable compound for-etan collapsed into a single syllable. By Middle English the form was freten/frete, and by Early Modern English fret. The strong past forms (fræt, freten) were levelled to the weak freted/fretted in the 15th–16th centuries, giving the modern fretted on this card. The word's brevity — one short syllable, one final consonant — is the residue of a once-two-syllable compound.

SEMANTIC DRIFT: FROM DEVOURING TO WORRYING

The semantic history is a textbook case of metaphor hardening into everyday meaning:

  • Old English: "to devour" — of animals eating prey; also of fire and disease "eating up" a house or a body.
  • Middle English (13th–14th c.): "to gnaw, wear away, corrode" — rust frets iron, water frets stone, moths fret cloth; the eating is now slow and incremental, not a single devouring.
  • 14th–15th c.: "to vex, irritate, annoy" — what gnaws at iron now gnaws at the temper: "fretted with cares", "fretted by flies".
  • 16th c. onward: "to worry, be anxious" becomes dominant — the internalisation is complete; fret is now almost entirely psychological, and the physical sense survives only in literary and technical registers ("the river frets the cliff").

The chain devour → gnaw → corrode → vex → worry is one of the cleanest semantic-drift sequences in English, and it explains why fretful means both "peevish" and "anxious": the same gnawing that eats iron eats peace of mind. Tolkien's barrels — which "creaked and fretted" — sit at the ancient end of this chain: wood that is fretful is wood that is being eaten at, strained, worn by the current.

THE DECORATIVE HOMONYM: FROM OLD FRENCH FRETE

Independently, English borrowed a second fret from Old French frete (12th–13th c.), "an interlaced lattice or trellis work". The Old French word's own origin is uncertain — it is usually traced to a Frankish (Germanic) source, possibly *fretil or the verb fretier "to bind with a ferrule", but the details are debated and no secure pre-Old-French ancestor is attested; the dictionaries mark the origin "uncertain, probably Frankish". This fret entered English in the 14th century as a noun for interlaced decorative work — the Greek-key pattern, the fretwork of screens and ceilings — and as a verb "to decorate with interlacing". The guitar fret (16th c.) is a specialised offshoot: the metal ridge that divides the fingerboard was originally a "fret" or band — the same word as the ferrule or band that bound a tool-handle — so a guitar's frets are literally little metal bands that "fret" the neck. The verb "to fret a note" (press a string against a ridge) and the noun "fretted instrument" come from this line, and "fretted" as an adjective means both "worried" and "equipped with frets" — two worlds sharing one spelling.

SOCIOCULTURAL CONTEXT AND LITERARY ATTESTATIONS

  • Old English: fretan appears in homilies and glosses for devouring beasts and consuming fire (Wulfstan's Sermo Lupi ad Anglos uses eating-and-consuming imagery for the destruction of England).
  • Middle English: Chaucer uses freten of corrosion and of worry — the word already straddles both worlds by the 14th century.
  • 16th–17th c.: Shakespeare uses fret both ways — the physical ("the canker frets the rose") and the psychological ("fret me not" — irritate me); Coverdale's 1535 Bible (Psalm 37) fixes "fret not thyself" as the word of biblical consolation.
  • 18th–19th c.: the worry sense is universal in domestic fiction (Austen, Dickens: Mrs Bennet fretting); Romantic poetry revives the physical sense for streams and cliffs.
  • 20th c.: "fret" survives in blues and country lyrics ("frettin' and worryin'"), in technical woodwork (fret-saw, fretwork), and in music (fretted guitar). Tolkien's "creaked and fretted" barrels are the physical sense's last great literary flowering.
  • 21st c.: the two homonyms are fully fused in popular perception — a fret-saw and a fretful baby share a spelling, and both carry the idea of intricate, gnawing activity.
