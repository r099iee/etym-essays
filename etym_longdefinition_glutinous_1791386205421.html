The adjective glutinous describes substances that are glue-like, viscous, adhesive, and tenaciously sticky in physical texture, as well as speech, prose, or behaviour that is cloying, overly sentimental, or unctuously sweet. In the source context from P. G. Wodehouse (“with the Bassett something less snappy and a good deal more __________ was obviously indicated”), Bertie Wooster uses the word figuratively with sharp comedic effect. Madeline Bassett is an incurably sentimental romantic who famously believes that the stars are God’s daisy chain and that rabbits are gnomes in disguise; consequently, any romantic communication directed toward her cannot be “snappy” (brisk, modern, casual, or detached), but must instead be thoroughly glutinous — drenched in sticky, syrupy, mawkish sentiment that clings suffocatingly to every sentence.

In material and scientific registers, glutinous maintains a precise physical definition denoting fluids, pastes, and organic excretions characterized by high viscosity, internal cohesion, and surface tackiness. Unlike purely liquid fluids that flow freely, a glutinous substance exhibits resistance to deformation, stretching into elastic threads or forming a binding film that adheres stubbornly to solid surfaces. This physical property bridges directly into multiple technical disciplines:

  • Gastronomy and Food Science: The term is globally recognized in culinary contexts through “glutinous rice” (Oryza sativa var. glutinosa), also known as sticky rice, sweet rice, or pearl rice. Crucially, in nutritional and biochemical science, this designation refers purely to the grain’s physical stickiness and glue-like cohesion when steamed, caused by an exceptionally high concentration of amylopectin and a near-total absence of amylose starch. Paradoxically, glutinous rice contains zero dietary wheat gluten (gliadin and glutenin proteins), making it entirely safe for individuals with celiac disease despite frequent consumer confusion caused by the morphological root of its name. In classical saucemaking and soup preparation, glutinous denotes stocks, reductions, and demi-glaces that have been simmered for prolonged periods with marrow bones, cartilage, and connective tissue, thereby dissolving vast quantities of collagen into gelatin, which leaves a rich, sticky, gelatinous coating on the lips and palate.
  • Botany, Mycology, and Zoology: In botanical taxonomy, glutinous characterizes plant leaves, stems, or seed coats covered in viscous, sticky mucilage or glandular hairs (such as the mucilaginous coating of soaked chia and flaxseeds, or the carnivorous glandular tentacles of Drosera, the sundew, whose glutinous droplets trap unsuspecting insects). In mycology, species identification keys routinely employ glutinous to describe the cap (pileus) or stem (stipe) of mushrooms coated in a thick, slippery, gelatinous slime layer when moist (for example, the cap of Suillus luteus, the slippery jack, or various Hygrophorus species). In zoology, it characterizes the protective mucus coats of snails, slugs, and hagfish, the sticky silk secretions spun by orb-weaver spiders to snare airborne prey, and the projectile slime ejected by velvet worms to immobilize targets.
  • Pedology and Geology: Soil scientists and civil engineers describe heavy, waterlogged clays, silts, and muds as glutinous when they exhibit extreme plastic cohesion, clinging tenaciously to excavators, spades, and footwear, creating a heavy, suctioning morass that impedes movement.
  • Figurative and Stylistic Polysemy: When applied to literature, rhetoric, or human relationships, glutinous functions as an evocative pejorative for sticky emotionalism. It implies a lack of intellectual backbone, an unctuous or obsequious social demeanor, or a literary style so bogged down in maudlin sentimentality, lush adjectives, and saccharine metaphors that it stifles the reader. A glutinous melody is excessively sweet and syrupy; a glutinous apology is unpleasantly oily and overacted; a glutinous romantic overture is suffocating rather than endearing.
  • Polysemy Bridge: The unifying conceptual bridge connecting these diverse meanings is the sensory experience of adherence and entrapment. Just as physical glue (Latin gluten) or sticky starch traps the fingers, slows physical movement, and refuses to be cleanly wiped away, metaphorical glutinousness — whether in the form of Madeline Bassett’s romantic expectations or an oily bureaucrat’s flattery — traps the recipient in an inescapable, clinging web of sugary affection or syrupy rhetoric from which one cannot extricate oneself without embarrassment.

Grammatically, glutinous is a qualitative adjective that admits of degree (more glutinous, most glutinous). It produces the adverb glutinously and the abstract nouns glutinousness and glutinosity. It collocates frequently with physical nouns like rice, mass, substance, fluid, broth, mud, clay, secretion, as well as abstract nouns like sentimentality, prose, affection, charm, dialogue. It should not be confused with gluttonous (greedy for food, derived from Latin glutto), an entirely distinct lexical item despite their nearly identical phonetic profiles in rapid English speech.
