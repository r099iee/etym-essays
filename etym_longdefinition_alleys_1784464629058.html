Alleys (the plural of alley) are narrow passageways between buildings, walls, or other structures — lanes too tight for a main street, built or left for access, drainage, short cuts, and service rather than for formal traffic. The singular alley also denotes a garden walk, a tree-lined path, and, in sport, the long narrow lane of a bowling alley, but the dominant modern sense — and the sense on this card — is the cramped connecting way between urban buildings. Etymologically the word comes from the Old French noun alée, "a going, a walk, a passage," built on the verb aler "to go," so the core image is always linear space for passing through. In the account of Smaug's first fury from The Hobbit — "he went back and crept in through the Front Gate and routed out all the halls, and lanes, and tunnels, alleys, cellars, mansions and passages" — the plural sits inside a deliberately exhaustive inventory of the Mountain's interior geography. The list ranges from grand public halls down to the narrowest human-scale corridors, and alleys contributes the cramped connecting ways that a dragon's bulk and malice can still scour out. The tone is grimly systematic, almost bureaucratic in its completeness: no category of space, from hall to cellar to alley, escapes the routing. The word is an inventory noun, and its humility in the list is the point — even the lowliest passage is found and destroyed. Core senses.
  • Narrow passageway between buildings (primary): a tight, often shadowed way between walls or houses, used for access to back entrances, for drainage, for short cuts, and for the delivery of goods — the "back alley" of urban geography.
  • Garden walk / tree-lined path (older, closer to French allée): a bordered way for strolling, an avenue through a park or garden, retained from the original French sense and now formal or literary.
  • Bowling lane (sports): the long narrow lane on which bowling or skittles is played — "a bowling alley," a "ten-pin alley"; by extension the building that houses such lanes.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and compound senses.
  • Blind alley: a cul-de-sac, a passage with no way out; figuratively a course of action that leads nowhere — a dead end, a line of enquiry that yields nothing.
  • Back alley: the rear, unglamorous lane behind buildings, associated with refuse, tradesmen's entrances, and, in genre fiction and crime writing, danger and the lawless margins of the city.
  • Alley cat: a stray or street cat, from the urban alley as the habitat of unowned animals.
  • Alleyway: a synonym and near-doublet, emphasizing the way-like nature of the passage.
  • In bowling, "alley" as metonymy: the playing surface or the establishment, and the specialized compounds (skittle alley, duckpin alley, ten-pin alley).
Historical and semantic bridges. The bridge from "a going" to "a narrow street" is the notion of a path along which one goes. Old French alée, from aler "to go," named any walk or passage, from a grand formal avenue to a simple way. Middle English borrowed the word with both senses; over the centuries the grand avenue sense retreated toward the formal French allée, while the everyday English word specialized downward to the narrow, tight, built passage between walls — a lane too small for a main street. The sports sense grows out of the same "passage" idea: the bowling alley is a long, straight lane along which the ball goes. The figurative blind alley applies the space image to the course of an enquiry that can go no further. The semantic constant, from the twelfth-century French noun to the modern urban alley, is a linear way of going, not a place of dwelling. Differentiation. A lane may be rural or urban and is often wider or more road-like; a passage or passageway is general connectivity; a corridor is typically indoor, roofed, and planned; a tunnel stresses underground boring; a street is a full public way with a name and frontages; a cul-de-sac is a dead end. Alleys keeps the specific sense of a narrow built passage between walls. The card preserves the plural — the inventory of routed spaces requires the plural because Smaug's destruction is total, emptying every class of passage, including the alleys, within the Mountain.
