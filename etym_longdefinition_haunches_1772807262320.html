Haunches names the lower rear region of the body taken as a single combined mass: the buttocks and the thighs considered together, whether of a human being or of a four-legged animal. It is a deliberately physical, solid word. Where hip points to the joint and buttock to the gluteal mass, haunch refuses to isolate the parts: it treats the whole zone from hip joint through buttock to upper thigh as one functional block of flesh and bone, the mass on which a body squats, perches, or balances its weight.

IN THE PASSAGE

When the usher 'permits himself to perch one of his haunches sideways on the edge of a chair', the phrasing is minute social choreography. He does not sit fully and does not stand; he deposits only one hip-and-thigh on the rim of the furniture, body still half-ready to rise, so that the seat is claimed and disclaimed at the same moment. It is an embodied compromise between Yu-cun's invitation ('as a friend of my early, hard-up days you are entitled') and the usher's own consciousness of rank. The word haunches gives the gesture a blunt, almost animal contour — more huntsman's or butcher's anatomy than drawing-room elegance — which is exactly why the comedy works: the flunkey's deference leaks through his very posture, and the magistrate's private room is invaded by a hint of the stable and the market.

PRIMARY SENSES

  • Human anatomy: the hip-and-buttock mass with the upper thigh, especially as the bearing surface for squatting or sitting; nearly always in the plural, and canonically in the phrase on one's haunches ('to squat back on one's haunches').
  • Animal anatomy: the hind part of a quadruped, the region of the hip and upper hind leg; standard in veterinary, hunting, and sporting description ('powerful haunches' of a horse or dog).
  • Culinary: a joint or cut of meat from the hind leg, above all in the fixed expression haunch of venison (also mutton, pork); here the word is ordinary and prized — a haunch is a choice roast for feasts.
  • Architecture and engineering: the 'haunch' of an arch or beam is the thickened lower part of an arch between the springing line and the crown, or the section of a beam between its support and its loaded part; a technical extension that rests on the same idea of a hip-like bulge or swelling.

HOW THE SENSES BRIDGE

  • The anatomical core (the hip-buttock-thigh region, especially as it curves outward in an animal) is the source image.
  • From that physical shape came the culinary sense: the haunch is the meat of the hind limb, the region where the muscle mass is greatest and most prized.
  • The architectural sense grew independently through shape-resemblance: the haunch of an arch is the hip-like thickening where the curve springs from its support, exactly where a quadruped's haunch joins the trunk.
  • The postural idiom (sit back on one's haunches) links human and animal: it describes a squat resting the weight through the hips, the posture of children, hunters, watchmen, and beasts.

REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS

  • sit / squat / settle back on one's haunches; rest on one's haunches; a haunch of venison / mutton; narrow / powerful haunches (of animals or athletes); the haunch of an arch (technical).
  • Register is literary, descriptive, or technical. Applied to a person, haunches sounds coarse, physical, and slightly rural; it is a body-part word with an animal after-taste. In hunting, cookery, and veterinary contexts it is plain professional usage.
  • The plural haunches is the normal form for the paired parts; the singular haunch is used for a single side or for the meat joint.

BOUNDARIES WITH NEAR-SYNONYMS

  • Compared with hips, haunches adds the buttock and upper thigh and carries a heavier, more solid, more quadrupedal feel.
  • Compared with buttocks, haunches is not purely gluteal: it includes the whole hip-thigh region and avoids the clinical and the crude extremes.
  • Compared with hindquarters, haunches is less strictly veterinary and can be used of a seated person.
