parricide — In the passage the hanged man is executed for "parricide" — the killing of a parent or other near relative — and the word lands with the full weight of the oldest domestic taboo. It identifies the condemned man not by name but by the crime that marks him as a breaker of the most fundamental bond, the violation of the source of one's own life. In a frontier scene of public execution, "parricide" carries a Roman gravity: this is the crime for which Roman law reserved the harshest imaginable punishment, and the word itself sounds formal, legal, and final, dropping into the sentence like a sentence passed. The tone is grim, archaic, and absolute — the crowd at the crossroads watches the death of someone who has destroyed his own origin.

Primary sense. "Parricide" is the killing of a parent or other near relative; it names both the crime and, by extension, the person who commits it ("a parricide"). In Roman law the word (parricidium) covered the killing of a father or mother, and by extension of other near kin, and the Republic extended it even to the state — the "fatherland" — so that a traitor could be punished as a parricide. The word is the broadest and most solemn of the -cide family.

Secondary senses and the -cide family. Because it covers any near relative, "parricide" sits at the head of a family of narrower words: patricide (killing one's father), matricide (killing one's mother), fratricide (killing one's brother), sororicide (killing one's sister), infanticide (killing a child), filicide (killing one's own child), uxoricide (killing one's wife). Where homicide is the neutral legal term for any killing of a human being, parricide is freighted with moral horror — the murder of one's own flesh and blood. Regicide (killing a king) parallels it as a crime against a symbolic father, but parricide remains the more intimate and the more unbearable.

Register, collocations, and the spelling trap.

  • Collocations: "tried for parricide," "convicted of parricide," "the crime of parricide," "a parricide" (the person). Its register is legal, historical, and literary — a word of courts, chronicles, and tragedy rather than everyday speech.
  • Spelling trap: despite the "-patri-" sound, the word is spelled "parricide," with -rr-. This is the fossil of a folk etymology: the Romans believed the word came from pater "father," and the doubled -rr- hardened that belief into spelling, even though modern scholarship reads the older form paricīda as "killer of an equal" (from pār "equal" + -cīda).
  • Extended uses: figuratively, "parricide" can name the destruction of one's own country, institution, or tradition — a metaphorical killing of one's origins — which is exactly the Roman extension of the crime to the fatherland.

hamlet — The second word of the pair is the crossroads "hamlet": a small settlement, a cluster of houses smaller than a village and typically without its own church. In the passage the hanged man is displayed "in a crossroads hamlet" — the tiny rural settlement is the stage of the public execution, and the word sets the grim scene in the smallest, most remote unit of human habitation.

Primary sense. A hamlet is a small settlement — a handful of houses, a cluster of dwellings, often no more than a crossroads, a green, or a few farms, and characteristically lacking a church of its own (belonging to the parish of a neighbouring village). It is the smallest unit of settled place in administrative and topographical speech: smaller than a village, and far smaller than a town or city.

Secondary senses and the Shakespearean shadow. The word gained a second, unshakeable life in 1600 as the name of Shakespeare's Danish prince, Hamlet — a coincidence of name (the prince's name, from the Danish Amleth, a legend of the same root) that has made "Hamlet" one of the most famous proper nouns in English. In common speech the common noun "hamlet" still means the tiny settlement, but the literary ear hears the ghost of Elsinore behind it, and "a Hamlet" can even be used playfully for a solitary, brooding figure. Topographically, the word appears in place-names and in administrative divisions (a hamlet in Canadian rural municipalities, a civil hamlet in local government) and in the US in the sense of an unincorporated community. The semantic core — a small cluster of dwellings — has been stable since the 14th century; only the Prince and his story were added to it.

Summary of polysemy. PARRICIDE: (1) the killing of a parent or near relative; (2) the person who commits it; (3) figuratively, the destruction of one's own country or origins (the Roman "fatherland" extension). HAMLET: (1) a small settlement, smaller than a village; (2) an administrative/topographical unit; (3) Shakespeare's prince and the brooding-hero type; (4) loosely, any tiny cluster of dwellings. The passage weds the two: the gravest crime and the smallest settlement, hanged at the crossroads of both.
