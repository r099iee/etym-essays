The word is echoic (onomatopoeic), not inherited from Proto-Indo-European: English forged it directly from the sound of a heavy, dull impact, and no PIE root is reconstructable. [The deepest stage is therefore an imitative sound-pattern, not a reconstructed root.] → Imitative sound-pattern *thump- (an invented word whose shape — voiceless th-, short low vowel, closing labial-plosive cluster -mp — mimics the dull thud of a heavy mass falling or striking), coined independently in more than one branch of North-Western Germanic; no attestation survives before the English record → North-Western Germanic echoic cluster on the same template: Scandinavian tumpa (to stamp, walk heavily, thump; cf. Norwegian dialectal tumpa, trampa) and English dialectal thump, with parallel formations thud, thwack, bump, dump, all built on the same acoustic model → Early Modern English thump (verb: to strike or beat with a heavy, dull blow; noun: such a blow and the dull sound it makes), first recorded in the 16th century CE → 16th century onward: thumping (present participle and verbal noun — continuous or repeated heavy striking; also "a beating, the sound of heavy blows") → 18th–19th centuries: thumping (participial adjective — "very large, emphatic, striking": a thumping lie, a thumping great hole) → 20th century: thumping (intensifying adjective in British political idiom — "a thumping majority") → Modern English THUMPING — the card's target form: the participle of thump ("heavy beating") and the intensifying adjective ("emphatic, remarkably large"). NOTE on the debate: because thump is expressive vocabulary, its exact lineage cannot be traced the way an inherited word can; the Scandinavian tumpa parallel shows the same template was coined independently in several dialects, and some dictionaries treat thump as a rhyming formation within the thud/bump/dump set rather than as a borrowing from any single source. No stage between the echoic pattern and the 16th-century English record is directly attestable.
