- 14th–15th centuries — English course (from OF) includes ‘ordinary, common quality’ as well as ‘path/sequence’; the later adjective still spelled like the noun.
- 16th century — spelling coarse emerges to mark the quality adjective as senses diverge; meaning shifts from neutral ‘ordinary’ toward ‘rough, inferior, unrefined’ in materials (cloth, grain).
- 17th century — manners sense thrives: coarse speech, coarse manners; adverb coarsely appears for both rough process and vulgar behavior; particle/texture contrasts with fine/finely become idiomatic in crafts.
- 18th–19th centuries — dictionaries codify dual sense; novelists use coarsely for low humor and rough characters; technical manuals standardize ‘coarsely ground/powdered’ instructions.
- 20th–21st centuries — culinary English (‘coarsely chopped onions’) is ubiquitous and non-pejorative; social sense remains mildly condemnatory (‘laughed coarsely’); speakers rarely connect the word to course ‘curriculum’ despite shared history. Public perception: either grain size or lack of refinement—not ‘during the course of.’
