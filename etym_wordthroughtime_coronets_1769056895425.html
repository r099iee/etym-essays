coronets
- 15th century — "coronet" enters English from Old French coronete as a small crown: a headdress for noble ranks below the sovereign, deliberately lighter and lesser than a full crown.
- 17th–19th centuries — English heraldry codifies it into a quasi-legal code: each peerage rank (duke, marquess, earl, viscount, baron) wears its own strictly regulated arrangement of leaves and pearls, so a coronet becomes a crown that tells your rank at a glance.
- 19th century onward — the word goes metonymic: "to succeed to the coronet" stands for aristocratic dignity itself, and the plural "coronets" begins to suggest a whole graded nobility as a class, reduced in time to dusty heaps of metal.
- Contemporary — a formal, heraldic, faintly archaic word of historical fiction and ceremonial; it has receded before "crown" in ordinary speech, leaving it the badge of a vanished, rank-ordered, and now humbled world.
