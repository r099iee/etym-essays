faltered is the past tense and past participle of the verb to falter, meaning to move, speak, or act unsteadily and hesitantly; to stumble in step or voice; to waver and nearly give way. It describes a momentary collapse of smoothness — the sudden loss of steady forward motion, whether of the feet, the tongue, the breath, or the will. In the fragment of dialogue from C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian that generated this card, the word is pure emotion: Caspian, startled awake, cannot produce the word "Nurse" steadily; his voice "falters" — breaks, wavers, stumbles — before he "burst into tears." The word sits exactly between speech and body: a faltering voice is a voice that has lost its footing, and Lewis chooses it because it makes the physical stumble and the emotional collapse the same event. The "N—Nurse" with its dash already shows the tongue tripping; "faltered" confirms that the boy is being undone by a grief or dread too large for steady articulation. The tone is pitying and unadorned — no archaism here, but the plain, precise verb for a child's breaking voice.

Principal senses (polysemy). Falter has three great provinces, and all three are live in modern English:

  • Unsteady movement (the oldest sense): to stumble, totter, or lose one's footing; to move with an uncertain, wavering step. "The runner faltered on the last lap," "his steps faltered with fatigue." Here the word images the body losing its balance.
  • Hesitant, breaking speech (the literary core): to speak unsteadily, with a voice that wavers, breaks, or dies away. "Her voice faltered as she told the story," "he faltered out an apology." The voice-sense matters most to literature because broken speech shares the very physiology of the staggering walk: the tongue stumbles, the breath catches, the phrase breaks in the middle.
  • Wavering of will, courage, or purpose (the moral/figurative sense): to hesitate, to weaken, to nearly give way in resolve. "Their courage faltered," "his resolve never faltered," "the plan faltered under criticism." This is the sense used of nations, economies, plans, and faith.
  • Transferred physical and mechanical uses: a flame or light "falters" (flickers and nearly dies); an engine or a voice-mail signal "falters" (loses power or steadiness); a conversation "falters" (loses momentum). In all of these the image is the same: forward motion or steady output interrupted and on the point of failing.
  • Negated idiom of steadiness: "without faltering," "never faltered," "did not falter" are the standard way to say rock-steady resolve or execution — the word's most common modern use may actually be its negation.

Near-synonyms and boundaries.

  • Stammered / stuttered are about the mechanics of speech — repetitions and blocks — and carry a clinical or diagnostic ring; they cannot describe a horse, a plan, or a candle stumbling.
  • Hesitated names a pause of the will but has none of the physical collapse; one hesitates before acting, but one can falter mid-act, mid-step, mid-word.
  • Wavered suggests oscillation, a trembling between options, usually voluntary or weak, whereas falter implies a genuine failure of capacity.
  • Tripped / stumbled are physical and momentary and are rarely used of the voice or of courage.
  • Flagged means losing energy over time (a flagging conversation), not a sudden break.
  • Floundered means helpless thrashing in an unfamiliar medium — deeper and more desperate than a mere falter.
  • Tottered, wobbled, lurched are all physical unsteadiness but without falter's ready transfer to voice and will; totter suggests weakness, wobble suggests instability, lurch a sudden jerk.

The distinguishing core of falter is the sudden loss of smooth forward motion — whether of foot, voice, or resolve — and its readiness to describe both people and their speech, which is exactly the double sense Lewis exploits.
