The historical evolution of semantic spans from ancient Indo-European cognitive perception to Greek divination, Classical philosophical signification, late 19th-century French historical philology, 20th-century formal logic, and 21st-century artificial intelligence.

1. Indo-European Roots & Greek Signification (*dʰyeh₂- to σῆμα and σημαίνω)

  • The PIE Root *dʰyeh₂- / *syeh₂-: Historical linguists trace the Greek root family back to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *dʰyeh₂- (or nasalized *dʰy-eh₂-), meaning "to notice, observe, look closely at, or contemplate." This root is also ancestral to Sanskrit dhyāna (meditation, contemplation, which travelled through Chinese Chan to Japanese Zen).
  • Ancient Greek σῆμα (sēma): In Homeric Greek (8th c. BCE), σῆμα (sēma) denoted a physical mark, token, boundary marker, funeral mound, or miraculous heavenly omen/portent through which the gods communicated their will to mortals.
  • Verbal & Adjectival Expansion (σημαίνω to σημαντικός): From the noun arose the verb σημαίνω (sēmainō, "to show by a sign, to give a signal, to indicate, to mean"). By the 4th century BCE, Aristotle in his logical treatises (notably De Interpretatione) used the verbal adjective σημαντικός (sēmantikos, "significant, expressive, having the property of meaning") to distinguish vocal sounds that convey conventional human meaning (such as spoken words) from meaningless natural noises (such as an animal's cough).

2. 19th-Century Philological Coining: Michel Bréal and French Sémantique

  • The Birth of Modern Semantics (1883–1897): For centuries, the Greek adjective remained a specialized rhetorical term. In 1883, French historical linguist and philologist Michel Bréal published an influential essay in L'Annuaire des études grecques titled "Les lois intellectuelles du langage," where he coined the French noun la sémantique and the adjective sémantique.
  • Bréal's Intellectual Objective: Bréal recognized that 19th-century linguistics was overwhelmingly dominated by comparative phonetics and morphology (sound laws like Grimm's and Verner's Laws). He argued that language study needed a systematic science dedicated to the "intellectual laws" that govern how words change their meanings over time (metaphor, narrowing, broadening, pejorative drift). His 1897 book Essai de sémantique: Science des significations established semantics as a major academic discipline.

3. English Adoption & 20th-Century Philosophical Shift

  • Entry into English (1894–1900): The adjective semantic was first adopted into English in 1894 in philological reviews discussing Bréal's papers, and received full institutional adoption with Mrs. Henry Cust's 1900 English translation of Bréal's Semantics: Studies in the Science of Meaning.
  • The Pragmatic & Analytical Expansion (1920s–1930s): In the 1920s, British critics C. K. Ogden and I. A. Richards published The Meaning of Meaning (1923), popularizing the semantic triangle (symbol, thought, referent). Concurrently, Polish-American scholar Alfred Korzybski launched the "General Semantics" movement in 1933 (Science and Sanity), asserting that human psychological maladjustment stems from confusing linguistic maps with objective physical territory.
  • Tarskian Semantics & Formal Logic: Polish logician Alfred Tarski revolutionized philosophical semantics in 1933 with his semantic theory of truth, defining truth in formalized languages without self-referential paradoxes. This established semantics (the study of relations between expressions and objects) alongside syntax (relations among expressions) and pragmatics (relations between expressions and speakers), a tripartite division formalized by Charles W. Morris and Rudolf Carnap in 1938.

4. Computational Linguistics & Contemporary AI (Late 20th–21st c.)

  • Programming Languages & Compilers: With the advent of computer programming in the 1950s–1970s (BNF grammar, denotational semantics by Dana Scott and Christopher Strachey), semantic became a vital technical term in software engineering to distinguish code that compiles grammatically from code that executes meaningful logic.
  • Semantic Web & Vector Embeddings: In the 2000s, Tim Berners-Lee championed the Semantic Web to structure internet knowledge into interconnected ontological triples. In 2010s–2020s deep learning, transformer architectures and large language models operationalized distributional semantics (Firth's 1957 dictum: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps") by encoding semantic relationships into dense mathematical vector representations.
