detritus
- 18th c. - English borrows detritus from Latin detrītus, "worn away"; it enters as a strictly geological term for fragments of rock ground down by erosion — a dry, technical word of the new science.
- 19th–20th c. - the word widens beyond geology to any loose waste — glacial, organic, marine, cosmic — and to figurative extensions ("the detritus of empire", "the detritus of daily life"); its register shifts from pure jargon to a faintly scientific word for scattered remnants.
- 21st c. - standard in ecology and literary prose, detritus always implies process — things worn away over time — and so keeps a formality that plain "debris" lacks.
anterior
- 16th c. - English borrows anterior from Latin anterior ("foremost"); it arrives as a technical spatial term of anatomy and geometry, meaning "located at the front".
- 17th–19th c. - a temporal sense ("earlier, prior") develops alongside the spatial one; the word becomes the fixed axis-word of anatomy (anterior/posterior) and a formal substitute for "front".
- 20th–21st c. - rare in ordinary speech but exact in science, and rare enough in literature that an author using it deliberately marks the prose as clinical — the language of a report rather than a story.
Cyclopean
- 17th c. - English forms Cyclopean from the Greek Cyclops, the one-eyed giants of legend; it is first applied to the vast irregular stone walls of Mycenae and Tiryns as a genuine archaeological term ("Cyclopean masonry").
- 18th–19th c. - the adjective generalises from masonry to anything on a gigantic scale, "as if built by Cyclopes", keeping its charge of ancient, alien, non-human engineering.
- 20th–21st c. - a fixture of weird and cosmic fiction for vast, eerie, engineered constructions, the word remains simultaneously a precise technical term and a mythic, awe-struck adjective.
conduit
- 14th c. - English borrows conduit from Old French conduit ("a channel or pipe") as a purely functional engineering word for the channels that carried water.
- 16th–18th c. - it names the aqueducts, pipes, and covered drains of towns and cities; municipal, mechanical, purposeful in register.
- 19th–21st c. - it extends to new utilities (gas, electricity, cables) and then to metaphor — a "conduit" of power, information, or aid; the word always implies deliberate construction, a channel built to conduct something toward a destination.
