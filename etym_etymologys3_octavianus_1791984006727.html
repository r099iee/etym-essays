Octavianus belongs to the oldest layer of Latin personal nomenclature, and its history is really three histories at once: the history of a Roman clan name, the history of an adoptive suffix, and the history of a historiographical convenience.

The gens Octavia and the name Octavius

The gens Octavia was a plebeian clan of Velitrae, a town in the Alban hills south-east of Rome, and Suetonius opens his life of Augustus by conceding that the family was only equestrian, not senatorial, in the generation before the dictator's niece married into it. Names of this shape — a stem plus the gentilic suffix -ius — are the commonest type of Latin nomen, and many descend from a personal name, a place or a trade. The traditional explanation of Octāvius is that it contains octāvus, “eighth”, and belongs with the ordinal surname-group Quintus, Sextus, Decimus: a name given to an eighth child or an eighth son. That explanation is old and plausible, but it is an inference from the shape of the name, not a statement preserved in any Roman author.

  • Latin octō “eight” continues PIE *oḱtō(u) and is one of the most stable numerals in the Indo-European family (Greek ὀκτώ, Sanskrit aṣṭa, Gothic ahtau, Old English eahta).
  • Octāvus is the inherited ordinal “eighth”, formed on the same ancient pattern as septimus and decimus; it survives in English “octave” and “octavo”, both through medieval Latin ecclesiastical and book-trade usage.
  • The same numeral gives “October”, the eighth month of the old Roman year before January and February were prefixed to the calendar, and the learned compounds “octet”, “octagon” and “octopus” (the last from Greek, not Latin).
  • If the birth-order explanation is right, Octavius belongs with a whole class of Roman names that fossilised family history: Quintus the fifth, Sextus the sixth, Decimus the tenth, Primus and Secundus.

The suffix -ānus and the grammar of adoption

Roman adoption was a legal act that transferred a man from one familia to another, and the name had to record both facts. The suffix -ānus was the ordinary adjective of belonging, productive in ethnic and civic names such as Rōmānus, Albānus and Montānus; added to a nomen it produced a second surname of origin. The pattern is older than Octavian: Publius Cornelius Scipio, adopted by a son of Scipio Africanus, became Scipio Aemilianus, “Scipio of the Aemilii”, and the same device marks the adoptive names of later senators. In Octavian's case the order is praenomen + nomen of the adopter + cognomen of the adopter + -anus, so the name reads as a miniature biography: Gaius, of the Julii by adoption, formerly of the Octavii.

From adoption to Augustan title

Caesar's will was read in Rome in March 44 BCE while Octavius was at Apollonia; the adoption was posthumous and required a formal adrogatio before the comitia, which followed in May. For a few years the heir used the name Gaius Iulius Caesar, adding Divi filius, “son of the deified”, after Caesar's consecration in 42 BCE — a claim visible on coins as CAESAR DIVI F. The -anus form was useful chiefly to everyone else, because it identified the young man without conceding his claim to be Caesar. Once the senate granted the name Augustus in 27 BCE, the adoptive name lost its public function; Augustus himself never styled himself Octavianus in official documents.

The historians' two-name rule

Suetonius narrates the early life under “Octavius” and then switches to “Augustus”; Cassius Dio uses Ὀκταουιανός for the period before 27 BCE and Αὔγουστος afterwards. Modern scholarship inherited this convention through the nineteenth-century German handbooks and turned it into a rule: “Octavian” before 27 BCE, “Augustus” after. The rule is useful but artificial — it imposes a clean break on a career that was continuous, and it can mislead readers into thinking the name changed at the same moment as the constitution.

  • Greek sources transliterate the name as Ὀκταουιανός, and Byzantine writers keep the form for the pre-imperial phase.
  • Italian preserves the family name as Ottaviano, French as Octavien, Spanish as Octaviano; English “Octavian” follows the French-Latin type, while “Octavianus” is a bookish Latinism.
  • The feminine Octavia was borne by the emperor's sister, the wife of Antony and mother of Antonia Minor, and became a common Renaissance and modern given name.

Modern afterlife

In English the name now lives almost entirely in classical scholarship and in historical fiction. Shakespeare's Julius Caesar (1599) brings “Octavius” on stage as a cool young avenger, and later novels and films use “Octavian” for the same moment of the story. As a given name it is rare but not extinct, especially in Romania and in families with classical tastes; as a common noun it does not exist. Its modern connotation is therefore double: precise chronological label and faint hint of cold political calculation.
