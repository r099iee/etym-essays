Derivation chain for SQUEAMISHNESS (from root *skeuh-, "to be shy, to shun"): 1. Proto-Indo-European *skeuh- (to be shy, timid; to shun, avoid) — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE. 2. Proto-Germanic *skeuhaz (shy, timid; easily frightened; shrinking from) — reconstructed, c. 1st millennium BCE. 3. Frankish *skiuh- / *skēuh (shy, timid; to shrink from) — reflected in early Romance loans, 5th–8th c. CE. 4. Old French esquiver (to shun, avoid, dodge, shrink from) — from Frankish, attested 10th–12th c. CE (also as eschiver). 5. Anglo-French / Old French escoymous, escoimous, esquaimous (fastidious, easily disgusted, squeamish) — 12th–14th c. CE. 6. Middle English squaymous, squeamous, squemous (fastidious, delicate, easily nauseated by food) — attested from the mid-15th c. CE. 7. Early Modern English squeamish (easily nauseated; over-delicate; prudish) — 16th c. CE (Spenser, Shakespeare). 8. Modern English SQUEAMISHNESS (the state of being squeamish; formed by adding the noun suffix -ness) — 17th c. CE. Note on debate: the exact pathway from the Germanic "shun" root to the -m-/-oy- forms of the French adjective is not fully settled. Some scholars connect escoymous to a different source, possibly the Latin scammonia (scammony, a purgative plant whose harsh resin induced violent nausea and griping), which would tie the word directly to a medicine that literally made people sick. The intrusive -m- and the shifting vowels (-oy-, -oi-, -ai-) are not completely explained by regular sound change and are best treated as attested but opaque dialectal developments. The chain above follows the most widely accepted reconstruction: Frankish *skiuh- → Old French esquiver "to shun" → Anglo-French escoymous "fastidious" → Middle English squaymous → squeamish.
