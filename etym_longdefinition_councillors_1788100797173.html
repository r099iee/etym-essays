COUNCILLORS are the members of a council — a body convened to deliberate, advise or govern. In modern British English the word means specifically an elected member of a local authority (parish, town, borough, city or county council), the everyday counterpart of the historic alderman. In Wilde's sentence the "Town Councillors" are the municipal dignitaries of the Prince's city, and the story uses them to satirise official vanity: one of them "wished to gain a reputation for having artistic tastes", and later a committee rejects the statue as "not quite so useful". The word thus carries, even outside the story, a mild charge of self-importance — the councillor as the man of the committee room who judges everything by reputation.



  • The municipal sense (dominant today). Since the municipal reform of the 19th century — notably the Municipal Corporations Act 1835 in England and Wales — councillors have been elected by wards to serve on the councils that run local services: housing, planning, roads, parks, licensing. They serve fixed terms, sit on committees, and are presided over by a mayor or chair; a modern councillor represents a ward, holds surgeries for constituents, and is accountable at elections. The Local Government Act 1972 reorganised the system into counties and districts, but the office name survived unchanged.

  • The general assembly sense. More broadly, a councillor is a member of any deliberative body: an ecclesiastical council, a guild or corporation board, an advisory council, a university court. The common thread is collective deliberation about public or corporate business — the word keeps the deliberative core it inherited from Latin consilium, "deliberation, advice, assembly".

  • Historical sense — royal and legal adviser. Originally (13th–16th centuries) the word meant an adviser of the crown or of a great household: the King's Councillors, the Privy Councillor, the councillors of a chartered town. This older sense overlapped with counsellor, "one who gives counsel"; only in the 16th–17th centuries did the spelling councillor become tied to membership of a council, while counsellor remained with the giving of advice — and, in law, with counsel at the bar (a King's/Queen's Counsel, "K.C."/"Q.C.").

  • Orthographic and regional variation. British usage prefers councillor (double l); American usage prefers councilor (single l), and American English additionally uses councilman and councilwoman. The double-l spelling reflects association with council; the single-l spelling follows the older counsellor pattern. Pronunciation is identical: /ˈkaʊnsələ/.

  • Satirical and literary charge. In fiction "councillor" often carries a comic-satirical aura — the pompous official who knows the appearance of things better than their substance. Dickens's municipal worthies and Wilde's Town Councillors are the type: men anxious for reputation, artistic tastes and civic dignity, prototypes of the image-conscious local politician. The word itself stays neutral, but two centuries of satire have tinged its associations.

  • Semantic bridge. Every sense descends from Latin consilium, "deliberation, advice, assembly": the word moved from "giver of counsel" (a personal function) to "member of a council" (an institutional role) as royal, civic and ecclesiastical councils hardened into permanent bodies in the Middle Ages, and the 16th-century spelling reform cemented the institutional meaning by re-attaching the word orthographically to council — so that today a councillor is defined by the chamber he or she sits in, not by the advice he or she gives.


In context. In The Happy Prince the Town Councillors admire the statue with pseudo-artistic affectation — one praises its beauty "only not quite so useful" — and later debate replacing it with a practical monument; the word encodes the story's satire of municipal materialism, in pointed contrast with the Prince's own costly and unsanctioned charity.
