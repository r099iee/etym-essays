• Munching: Similar because both mean chewing; differs because munching is steady and quiet, with a closed mouth and no urgency, while champing is vigorous, noisy, and full of eagerness, working the jaws emphatically.
  • Crunching: Similar because both are noisy eating; differs because crunching stresses the brittle breaking sound of the food and says nothing about eagerness, while champing stresses the rhythmic, eager working of the jaws.
  • Gnawing: Similar because both are persistent biting; differs because gnawing is slow and wears down something hard, as bone or wood, with intent, while champing is quick, repeated, and emphatic, with appetite or impatience.
  • Gnashing: Similar because both involve the teeth working together; differs because gnashing is grinding the teeth in rage or pain, not in feeding or eagerness.
  • Chomping: Similar because both name the same noisy jaw-work and share the same imitative family; differs because chomping is the heavier, more casual American variant, while champing is the standard British form that carries the bit-idiom.
  • Chewing: Similar because both mean working food between the jaws; differs because chewing is neutral and general, while champing names the emphatic, noisy, eager subset.
  • Masticating: Similar because both mean chewing; differs because masticating is the clinical, technical term, while champing is vivid, onomatopoeic, and animal.
  • Biting: Similar because both involve the teeth closing on something; differs because biting is a single general act of the teeth, while champing is repeated, noisy, rhythmic biting in feeding or impatience.
