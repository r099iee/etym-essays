Intrusted is a prominent 17th–19th-century spelling variant of the past participle and past tense of the verb entrust (or intrust), meaning to commit, deliver, or confide something precious—such as property, a secret, an office of state, or a sacred duty—into the care, keeping, or disposal of another person, under full reliance upon their fidelity, integrity, and prudence. The word bridges Germanic concepts of steadfast personal loyalty with Latinate prefixes of delegation, establishing a solemn covenant of stewardship between the confiding party and the recipient.

In the source context from an eighteenth-century Scottish Enlightenment historical essay—"Ingenious men, possessed of leisure, are apt to push their researches beyond the period in which literary monuments are framed or preserved; without reflecting, that the history of past events is immediately lost or disfigured when intrusted to memory and oral tradition, and that the adventures of barbarous nations, even if they were recorded, could afford little or no entertainment to men born in a more cultivated age"—the word is employed with exquisite philosophical irony. To intrust something implies handing it over to a safe, faithful, and competent guardian. Yet the author argues that committing the memory of past events to unwritten oral tradition is a disastrous misplacement of trust: human memory is a fickle, frail, and treacherous custodian, incapable of guarding historical truth against distortion and oblivion.

Core Meaning and Fiduciary Mechanics:

The conceptual foundation of intrusted requires three parties or elements: a precious object, charge, or truth (the deposit); a confiding source who yields possession; and a trustee who accepts custody with an implied moral or legal covenant of care. Unlike simple giving, lending, or transferring, to intrust something does not relinquish ultimate ownership; rather, it delegates temporary administration and responsibility. Grammatically, it functions both as a transitive verb taking a prepositional phrase ("intrusted to someone's care", "intrusted with a vital mission") and as a participial adjective modifying committed charges.

Polysemy and Senses Across Domains:

  • Fiduciary and Custodial Committal: Depositing financial wealth, real property, wards, or physical valuables into the hands of a legal trustee or guardian under binding legal obligations of good faith (e.g., "funds intrusted to the family solicitor"). The bridge stems from the formal legal codification of the law of trusts in English chancery courts.
  • Epistemic and Intellectual Custody (The Source Sense): Committing ideas, historical records, sacred truths, or private confidences to an intellectual vessel, such as human memory, oral song, or confidential correspondence. The bridge metaphorically treats abstract historical facts as a sacred physical treasure deposited into the keeping of human memory.
  • Political and Constitutional Delegation: Bestowing executive authority, military command, diplomatic power, or sovereign office upon a public official or representative (e.g., "the vast executive powers intrusted to the President by the Constitution"). The bridge views democratic and constitutional governance as a fiduciary trust granted by the sovereign people to their magistrates.
  • Intimate Emotional and Vulnerable Confidence: Sharing one's deepest vulnerabilities, secrets, or life's happiness with a close friend or lover, trusting that they will never exploit or betray that exposure. The bridge translates financial and political stewardship into the delicate realm of interpersonal vulnerability.
  • Religious and Divine Stewardship: In Christian theology and homiletics, the belief that talents, wealth, health, and mortal time are divine gifts intrusted by God to human stewards to be accounted for at the Last Judgment.

Orthographic and Historical Status:

The spelling intrust and its past forms intrusted were the overwhelmingly dominant, standard choice throughout seventeenth-, eighteenth-, and early nineteenth-century British and American literature, statecraft, and jurisprudence. It was the spelling utilized in the United States Constitution, the Federalist Papers, and by towering stylists including John Locke, David Hume, Edward Gibbon, George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, and Jane Austen. This variant reflected the neoclassical preference for assimilating French-derived prefixes to Latin in-. Although twentieth-century lexicography gradually standardized on entrusted, the form intrusted remains fully valid, carrying a distinctive dignity and historical gravitas.
