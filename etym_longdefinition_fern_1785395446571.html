A FERN is a flowerless, vascular plant of the division Pteridophyta (or the broader group Pteridophytes), which reproduces by spores rather than by seeds or flowers, and which typically bears finely divided, feathery fronds that uncurl from a coiled spiral (the fiddlehead) as they grow. Unlike mosses, ferns have true roots, true stems, and true leaves with a water-conducting vascular system; unlike flowering plants, they never produce flowers, fruits, or seeds, propagating instead by dust-like spores shed from sporangia usually clustered on the underside of the fronds. Ferns range in size from tiny filmy creepers of damp rock to the great tree-ferns of the tropics, and they occupy every habitat from rainforest floor to high mountainside. In the goblins' chant — "Burn, burn tree and fern!" — from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the fern stands for the low, ancient undergrowth of the forest floor, the second great class of living things that fire destroys: the trees above, the ground-cover below. The pairing gives the line its terrible completeness, as the goblins vow to burn the woods from canopy to floor, leaving nothing between. Ferns also carry deep associations of dampness, shadow, and prehuman antiquity — they dominated the Carboniferous coal forests long before flowers existed — so that the word quietly signals wildness and geological age, making the fern the perfect emblem of everything ancient that fire consumes. The word's polysemy is modest but real, and its uses spread across botany, folklore, and culture:
  • The plant itself: the single spore-bearing plant with feathery fronds — "a clump of ferns grew by the stream". The oldest and core sense.
  • The collective: fern as a mass noun for the plant-cover of the forest floor — "the ground was deep in fern", "fern and bracken". Tolkien's line uses this collective, habitat sense.
  • Tree fern: a tall tropical fern with a woody-looking trunk, Cyatheales, growing many metres high; a fern that looks like a tree but is no closer to one.
  • Maidenhair fern (Adiantum): a delicate, fan-leaved fern of shaded banks, ornamental since antiquity and named for hair-fineness of its stalks.
  • Boston fern (Nephrolepis exaltata): the common houseplant fern of Victorian and modern interiors, a symbol of the parlour.
  • Bracken (Pteridium aquilinum): not a synonym but one coarse, aggressive species, the invasive fern of heaths and open woodland, the most widespread fern on earth.
  • Fern-seed and fern folklore: medieval belief that fern "seed" (actually spores) conferred invisibility — Shakespeare's "We have the receipt of fern-seed, we walk invisible" — linking the word to magic and the invisible.
  • Fernery: a cultivated place for growing ferns, often a glass case, an emblem of the Victorian fern craze (pteridomania, 19th century).
  • Fiddlehead: the young coiled frond before it unrolls, an edible delicacy in spring — the same organ at an earlier stage.
  • Ferny and fern-like (adjectives): covered with or resembling ferns; "ferny" names both the habitat and the feathery texture.
  • Metaphoric fern: in literature, the fern is the emblem of antiquity, wildness, and damp primeval growth — older than flowers, indifferent to gardens.
The boundaries around the word sharpen its meaning:
  • Bush or shrub: a fern is neither — it has no woody stem or trunk at all; its fronds rise directly from a creeping rootstock or rhizome.
  • Moss: tiny, cushion-like, and non-vascular — a fern can stand waist-high or taller, with true veins and leaves and a water-conducting system; mosses are older and simpler than ferns.
  • Bracken: not a synonym but one coarse species (Pteridium aquilinum), the common invasive fern of heaths and woods; every bracken is a fern, but most ferns are not bracken.
  • Undergrowth: an ecological term for the whole ground layer of a wood, of which ferns are only one part.
  • Herb: in botany, a non-woody flowering plant — a fern is not one, having no flowers and reproducing by spores.
  • Leaf: the ordinary flat blade of a flowering plant; a fern frond is a whole compound structure of many leaflets, leaf and stem in one, and is called a frond precisely to mark the difference.
Collocations and register: "fern frond", "fern brake" (or "a brake of ferns"), "tree fern", "maidenhair fern", "Boston fern", "fern forest", "fern-seed", "fern gully", "fernery", "a clump of ferns", "feathery fern", "fern of the forest floor". The register is standard and largely neutral, but the word carries a permanent undertone of the wild and the ancient: ferns predate flowers by hundreds of millions of years, and a fern forest in writing is almost always a place of green silence, shadow, and deep time. The word itself, as its etymology shows, was named for the feathery, wing-like appearance of its leaves — a visual identity the plant has kept across millennia of naming, and one that makes it the natural companion of words like feather, wing, and plume.
