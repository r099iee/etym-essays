obituary (noun / adjective; plural: obituaries; colloquial: obit): A published public announcement and biographical tribute recording the recent death of an individual, detailing the circumstances of their passing, summarizing their life achievements, character, career milestones, and major contributions, and listing surviving family members and memorial service arrangements; extended metaphorically to any critical post-mortem assessment, retrospective essay, or formal declaration marking the demise or obsolescence of an institution, technology, movement, or cultural era.

The term spans journalistic literature, social customs, historical documentation, and metaphorical cultural commentary:

  • Primary Journalistic & Literary Genre: In professional news journalism, an obituary is not a mere paid death notice (which is a brief classified listing), but a substantive, carefully reported biographical essay. Prominent newspapers (such as The New York Times, The Guardian, or The Times of London) maintain dedicated obituary desks that craft literary profiles celebrating the complex lives, idiosyncrasies, triumphs, and failures of notable artists, scientists, politicians, and everyday pioneers.
  • Social & Familial Record (Paid Notices): In regional and local communities, family-written obituaries serve as formal public notices of mortality, providing genealogical records (parents, spouse, children, grandchildren), acknowledging hospice caregivers, detailing funeral or memorial service logistics, and requesting charitable donations in lieu of flowers.
  • Historical & Monastic Necrology: In ecclesiastical and medieval history, an *obituary* (or *obit-book* / *necrology*) was a monastic or cathedral register recording the death dates (anniversaria) of bishops, monks, monarchs, and benefactors so that commemorative masses and prayers for their souls could be offered on the exact anniversary of their passing.
  • Metaphorical & Cultural Demise: In cultural, technological, and political commentary, the word is used metaphorically to pronounce the end, failure, or obsolescence of a historical phenomenon (e.g., "writing an obituary for the internal combustion engine", or "the editorial was an obituary for bipartisan politics").
  • Adjectival Sense (Pertaining to Death): As an adjective, *obituary* describes anything relating to, recording, or commemorating a death (e.g., "an obituary notice", "obituary columns", or "obituary poetry").
  • Etymological Roots: Derived from Medieval Latin obituārius ("pertaining to death"), from Classical Latin obitus ("departure, a going down, setting of the sun, death"), the past participle of obīre ("to go towards, go to meet, go down, perish"), constructed from ob- ("toward, against") + īre ("to go"). Death is literally framed as a "departure" or "going down."
