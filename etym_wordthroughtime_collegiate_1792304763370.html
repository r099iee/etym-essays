- 15th c. (Late Middle English, c. 1430s) — Borrowed from Latin collēgiātus and Old French strictly in ecclesiastical canon law to designate a collegiate church (governed by a chapter of canons without a bishop's throne).
- 16th–17th c. (Tudor & Stuart England) — Expanded from church architecture to include the autonomous endowed residential colleges of Oxford and Cambridge, describing university halls and their corporate bodies of fellows.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment & Colonial America) — Transferred across the Atlantic to describe early American colonial colleges (Harvard, William & Mary, Yale), emphasizing corporate charters and shared faculty governance.
- 19th c. (Victorian Era & American Expansion) — Skyrocketed across North America with the rise of collegiate athletics (early rowing regattas, gridiron football) and specialized campus publications, yielding the famous Merriam-Webster Collegiate Dictionary (1898).
- 20th–21st c. — Cemented globally in higher education marketing, NCAA intercollegiate sports, collegiate apparel branding, and organizational theory (collegiate vs corporate leadership models).
