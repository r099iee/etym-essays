The yeomanry begins in a linguistic fog that scholars have never quite dispelled — fitting, perhaps, for a word that came to stand for plain English honesty. The Middle English yoman or yeman of the fourteenth century meant a household servant of middling rank: above the grooms and pages, below the esquires, trusted with the pantry, the bows, or the lord’s chamber. Chaucer’s Yeoman, the Knight’s servant with his mighty bow and his sheaf of peacock arrows, fixes the type forever — competent, wood-wise, and free. Guesses at a deeper root abound, from young man (the junior attendant) to a lost word for a district or township (the village man), but none commands consensus, and honesty requires the story to start where the records start: with the household archer.

What followed was one of English history’s great promotions. The yeoman archer, multiplied across the shires, became the weapon that won Crécy, Poitiers, and Agincourt, and the word rose with the bowstring. By Tudor times a yeoman was a freeholder cultivating his own land — classically, land worth forty shillings a year, the freehold that carried a vote for the knight of the shire. Statesmen, preachers, and poets united in his praise: Latimer’s famous sermon remembered the yeomanry of his father’s day, Cromwell recruited his Ironsides from such men, and every eulogy of England’s happy middle rank — free, armed, Protestant, and propertied — gathered round the noun. The collective yeomanry, formed with the same -ry that gives peasantry and gentry, bottled that whole social poetry into a single word.

From pantry to parade-ground:

  • Medieval hall — service: the yoman guards the pantry and strings the bow, the dependable middle of the household hierarchy.
  • The Hundred Years’ War — glory: yeoman archers become the terror of French chivalry, and the class-name acquires its martial halo.
  • Tudor enclosure — property: the yeoman settles into his freehold, votes his forty shillings, and becomes the textbook example of English liberty rooted in land.
  • 1793–94 — alarm: revolutionary France threatens invasion and English Corresponding Societies spread reform; Pitt’s government authorises county troops of Yeomanry Cavalry, officered by landlords, mounted on farmers’ hunters.
  • Peterloo, 1819 — disgrace: the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry sabre an unarmed reform crowd, eleven die, and radicals from Cobbett to Shelley brand the yeomanry as the landlords’ hired blades.
  • Empire to armour — survival: yeomanry regiments serve mounted in South Africa and Palestine, mechanise between the wars, and fight as armour, artillery, and signals in the Second World War.
  • Today — ceremony: the Royal Yeomanry carries the county titles into the Army Reserve, and the Yeomen Warders guard the Tower — the pantry servant, the freeholder, and the trooper reconciled in scarlet.

Cobbett’s use catches the word at its most contested hour. To the Surrey landlord the yeomanry cavalry were gallant patriots keeping French principles and English levellers at bay; to Cobbett they were farmers bribed with uniforms and ale to ride down their starving neighbours — men who, as he insists, put down themselves and their landlords, since the taxes and enclosures the volunteers defended were hollowing out the very farming class they sprang from. History never quite settled the quarrel, which is why the word still carries two temperatures: pride on the parade-ground, suspicion in the radical press. Beneath both lies the older music — the forty-shilling freeholder, the bowman of Agincourt, the pantry-man of Chaucer’s pilgrimage — the stubborn English dream that freedom lives, armed and unmortgaged, somewhere between the cottage and the hall.
