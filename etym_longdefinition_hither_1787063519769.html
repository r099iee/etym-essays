Hither is an adverb of place meaning "to or toward this place" — the direction of movement ending at the speaker's location or at a place pointed to by the speaker. It answers the question "where to?" in exactly the way its modern relative here answers "where?" The word is now firmly archaic and literary, but for over a thousand years it was a completely ordinary, everyday English adverb, the standard word a speaker used to say "come to this place."

Primary sense: motion toward the speaker

At its core, hither expresses goal-directed movement toward the speaker or toward the deictic centre. If the speaker says "come hither," the hearer is commanded to travel in the speaker's direction; "bring it hither" means "bring it to me, to this spot." Unlike here, which can name a static location ("I am here"), hither always implies approach and arrival — it is a true adverb of motion, roughly "to here."

  • Directional core: "to this place," "this way," "in this direction" — the terminal point of a journey of approach.
  • Deictic anchor: the reference point is the speaker ("me" / "us"); in the classic triad the other two directions are thither ("to that place," away from the speaker) and whither ("to which place?," interrogative).
  • No object: it is a pure adverb; it never takes a direct object, and it is not used with a following preposition — the direction is already contained in the word itself.

Secondary and idiomatic senses

Modern English has no separate "secondary meanings" of hither in the way a noun like spring does, but the word has developed several idiomatic, fixed-phrase, and derivative functions that carry its meaning forward:

  • "hither and thither" — "in every direction, aimlessly, scattered about." From a directional pair ("to here and to there") it became a general idiom for disorderly or purposeless movement; a folksy synonym is "hither and yon" (with dialectal yon = "that place yonder").
  • "come-hither (look / glance)" — since the mid-19th century, an attributive adjective in flirtatious language: a look that silently invites someone "to come here" to the speaker. This is the word's most alive modern survival and a genuine semantic extension — from direction of motion to seductive invitation.
  • "hitherto" — a fused form meaning "up to this time, until now." This is the temporal offshoot: hither + to shifted from space ("to this place") to time ("up to this moment"), one of English's classic space-to-time transfers.
  • "hither side" — a prepositional-locative use, "on this side (of)" a boundary, as in "the villages on the hither side of the river"; chiefly 19th-century and earlier.
  • Legal and formal relics — in older legal and administrative prose "hither" survives in phrases such as "all goods brought hither," and it remains in some fixed bureaucratic and academic locutions by inertia.

Register, perception, and current status

By the 18th century everyday speech had replaced hither with "here" (typically as "come here," "bring it here"), and the word retreated into higher registers. Today it is perceived as:

  • Archaic–literary: normal in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and older prose ("Bring him hither to me"); in modern writing it is used for solemnity, period flavour, or humour.
  • Rhetorical and poetic: chosen deliberately for cadence, rhyme, or grandeur; it carries a consciously elevated or mocking-archaic tone in news headlines, fiction, and comedy.
  • Understood by all, produced by few: every native speaker recognises it, but almost nobody uses it spontaneously except in the fixed idioms above.
  • Regional survivals: the older form hider and related forms persist in some northern English and Scottish dialects, keeping the un-levelled spelling alive on the ground.

Semantic neighbours and boundaries

  • here (static location) vs hither (motion toward) vs hence (motion from here, "from this place").
  • thither ("to that place," away) and whither ("to which place?," interrogative/relative) complete the four-way system inherited from Old English.
  • toward(s) is the modern prepositional replacement, but it requires an object and is neutral in deictic direction ("toward me" vs "toward you").

Hither is thus best glossed as the archaic directional counterpart of here: a word whose single core sense — "to this place" — has stayed stable for a millennium and a half while its social register moved from everyday core vocabulary to the edges of the literary language.
