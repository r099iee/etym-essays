UNBEFITTING is an English-internal compound formed in the early 17th century from two independent elements of very different histories: the negative prefix un-, one of the oldest parts of the Germanic vocabulary, and the base befitting, built on the adjective fit, whose ultimate origin is genuinely uncertain. The two strands are traced in isolation below, then joined at the compound. CHAIN 1 — the un- prefix (the ancestral negative): 1) PIE *ne- "not" (the ancestral negative particle of the entire Indo-European family, reconstructed; also the base of Latin in-, Greek a-/an-, and Sanskrit a-/an-) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Germanic *un- "not" (negative prefix, from the zero-grade *n̥- of *ne-, with the PIE syllabic nasal resolving to *un-) — projected c. 2500–500 BCE. 3) Old English un- "not" (the ubiquitous negative prefix of Old English, attested from the earliest records; as in ungefrǣg "unheard of," unlæd "unfortunate"). 4) Middle English un- "not" (continuous; the productive privative prefix of Middle English, as in unkind, untrue, unknown). 5) Early Modern English un- "not" (still fully productive; the prefix combined freely with participles and adjectives, including befitting, to form unbefitting) — c. 1600. 6) English unbefitting — UNBEFITTING (early 17th century; "not befitting, not suitable or proper to a person's rank or an occasion"). CHAIN 2 — the base fit (origin genuinely uncertain; two competing hypotheses): HYPOTHESIS A (native, from Old Norse): 1) Old Norse fit "the webbing of a water-bird's foot; a knitted or plaited thing" (attested in Old Norse; the image of something knit or woven together, hence "well put together") — c. 9th–13th c. 2) (semantic bridge, probable but not certain) Middle English fit (adj.) "well-suited, apt, proper" — the notion being "well-knit, well put together, hence fitted" — first attested c. 1400 in northern Middle English. HYPOTHESIS B (loan, from the Low Countries): 1) Middle Dutch vitten "to suit, befit, fit" (attested; a verb of fitting and suiting) — c. 14th c. 2) Middle English fit (adj.) "well-suited, apt, proper" — explained as a loan from the Middle Dutch/Low German verb of fitting, brought in through North Sea commercial and maritime contact — first attested c. 1400. JOINT LINE FROM THE ADJECTIVE (secure regardless of which hypothesis is right): 3) English fit (v.) "to make suitable, adapt, furnish with what is needed" (back-formed from the adjective or from the noun, c. 1500). 4) English fitting (adj.) "appropriate, becoming, suitable" (participle of fit, 1580s). 5) English befit (v.) "to suit, be fitting for, be proper to" (be- + fit, 1610s); its participle befitting (adj.) "appropriate, becoming, proper to one's rank" (1610s–1620s). 6) English unbefitting — UNBEFITTING (early 17th century; un- + befitting, "not appropriate, not proper to a person's rank or station"). NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The derivation of the adjective fit is the single most contested point. The OED favours a Scandinavian origin, comparing Old Norse fit "webbing, plaited thing," but explicitly marks the adjective's etymology as "obscure"; a rival account derives it from Middle Dutch vitten, and neither hypothesis commands universal assent. Deeper PIE ancestry cannot be reconstructed on either account — no PIE root is claimed for fit, and folk etymologies connecting it to Latin aptus or to PIE *ped- "foot" are rejected by modern scholarship. (2) By contrast, the derivation of the prefix un- from PIE *ne- is secure and uncontroversial, a cornerstone of Indo-European comparative grammar.
