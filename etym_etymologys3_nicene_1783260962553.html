The adjective Nicene is a word that travelled from the battlefield to the altar, and from a small Bithynian town to the centre of Christian orthodoxy. Its biography is a study in metonymy: a city that happened to host a council gave its name to the theology the council defined, and that theology in turn shaped the faith of Europe for sixteen centuries.

Root and city. The story begins with the Greek noun nī́kē, "victory", a word as old as Greek literature and inscribed, famously, as the goddess of victory on the balustrade of the temple of Athena Nike in Athens. Its deeper origin is genuinely unknown: modern scholarship, following Robert Beekes and others, treats nīkē as very likely a Pre-Greek substrate word, and no Indo-European root commands general assent, though a connection to *neik- "to reach, overcome" has been mooted. Around 301 BC a city in Bithynia was founded by Antigonus Monophthalmus and renamed by his rival and successor Lysimachus as Nīkaia, after his wife (or daughter) Nicaea; the name was transparently understood as "city of victory". For centuries it was an ordinary Hellenistic town on a lake, famous for its grain, its stoas, and its fine wines.

The council and the creed. Everything changed in 325 AD, when the Emperor Constantine summoned some three hundred bishops to Nicaea to settle the controversy opened by Arius of Alexandria, who taught that the Son was a created being, "there was when he was not", and so not fully divine. The council, dominated by the emperor's pressure and the theology of Athanasius, condemned Arius and issued a creed declaring the Son homoousios — "of one substance" — with the Father. That short Greek word became the hinge of western and eastern theology alike. The creed was expanded at the First Council of Constantinople in 381 into the Niceno-Constantinopolitan text recited in churches to this day. From that moment the adjective Nicene left geography behind: "Nicene faith" no longer meant the faith held at Nicaea so much as the faith of orthodox, Trinitarian, conciliar Christianity, defined against Arianism and every later subordinationist teaching.

Sound and form. Formally the word came into English through Latin. Greek Nīkaia entered Latin as Nīcaea (Pliny knew the city), and the Church formed the adjective Nīcaenus ("of Nicaea"), as in fides Nicaena, the Latin of the council documents and the Creed. English adopted the adjective in the 16th century — the age of the Reformation and of the great doctrinal debates in which the Nicene Creed was a central battleground between Rome and the reformed churches — and "Nicene Creed" is attested in English prose from the 1540s and 1550s. The English form preserves the Latin stem Nicaen- with the Latin adjective ending stripped down to -e, giving the distinctive "nye-SEEN" pronunciation recorded in modern dictionaries.

Cultural afterlife. In English the word has remained a specialist, ecclesiastical, and historical adjective. It appears almost always in a handful of fixed contexts — the Nicene Creed, Nicene theology, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Nicene orthodoxy — and it carries a precise charge: the definition of the Son's consubstantiality with the Father, and by extension the triumph of orthodox Christianity over Arianism. Gibbon's use in the sentence on this card is exact: the "Nicene theology" is the emblem of a literate, philosophical, Romanized Christianity into which the German princes of the Continent had been drawn, in pointed contrast to the heathen temples of Thor and Woden in Britain. The word that once named a victory city on the shores of a Bithynian lake thus became, by the purest chain of historical accident, the technical name of orthodoxy itself.
