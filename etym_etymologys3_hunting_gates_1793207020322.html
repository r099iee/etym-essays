Two old words meet in hunting-gates, and each brings a thousand years of weather. Hunt begins in the Germanic forest: Old English huntian, to chase game, the daily work and high sport of a society that lived partly on wild meat and wholly on the prestige of taking it. Kings keep vast forests and harsher forest laws; thegns hunt boar and hart; the vocabulary of venery — hart, hind, buck, doe, pricket, sorel — becomes the most elaborate technical language in Middle English, a shibboleth of gentility that Chaucer's Yeoman and aristocratic treatises alike display. The verb itself runs steadily through hunten to modern hunt with little sound change, gathering the gerund hunting as the name of the sport: by the sixteenth century hunting is not merely chasing but the Chase, capitalised in the mind if not on the page, with its servants, seasons, hounds, and etiquette.

Gate begins at the farmyard. Old English geat is the opening and its barrier together — door, gate, gap — the controllable mouth of every enclosure. Then the Vikings complicate it: Old Norse gata, road or way, settles into the Danelaw's towns and fuses with the native word, so that in York and Leeds a gate is a street (Coppergate, Briggate) while in the countryside it remains a barrier. Middle English thus inherits a doubled word, and the countryside keeps the barred half. The great age of the field gate is enclosure: between roughly 1600 and 1850, commissioners and landlords carve some six million acres of open field and common into hedged, walled, gated farms, and the five-barred gate — cheap in oak, repairable by any estate carpenter, jumpable by a good hunter — becomes the hinge of the new landscape. Millions are hung; the pattern barely varies from county to county.

Fox-hunting proper, the organized pursuit of the fox with a pack of hounds across enclosed country, rises with this landscape and needs it. Before enclosure a rider gallops open wolds; after it, every run is a negotiation with gates, hedges, and ditches. The hunting-gate becomes sporting furniture with its own customs: earth-stoppers and hunt servants ride early to free the latches; farmers leave certain gates unlocked on hunting days by ancient courtesy; the huntsman's cry heads up warns the field of a jumped gate ahead; and gate-shutting — closing every gate behind the field so stock does not stray — is drilled into every novice as the first commandment of the hunting field. To jump a locked hunting-gate cleanly, at stride, without breaking the hounds' line, is the ordinary heroism thousands of Victorian and Edwardian riders lived for, and the hunting novel — from Surtees's Jorrocks papers through Trollope's hunting chapters — measures men in exactly these units.

Cobbett, the hunt, and the latch:

  • The political gate: Cobbett hates the hunting world as the armed leisure of the landed interest — the same squires who enclose commons, cut wages, and fill Parliament hunt three days a week over the labourers' country. His hunting-gates are their latches: opened for red coats, shut in the faces of the poor, and impassable to any honest wheeled traffic.
  • The practical gate: beneath the politics lies the traveller's fact — gates stop gigs. Every gate means a halt, a dismount or a servant, a negotiation; a cross-country journey by trap through hunting country is a comedy of latches. The gig belongs to turnpikes with toll-bars that open for coin; the gate belongs to parishes that open for neighbours.
  • The sporting defence: the hunting world answers, then and now, that gates kept and jumped are the countryside's shared furniture, maintained by subscriptions, opening the landscape to riders of every class who follow the field — a defence Cobbett would have laughed out of the parish.
  • After the ban: the Hunting Act of 2004 ends hunting with dogs in England and Wales, but drag-hunting, trail-hunting, and exercising preserve the gate customs; bridleway campaigners and farmers now dispute the same latches their grandfathers jumped, and the five-barred gate has become heritage — photographed, painted, and listed in field guides to old England.

So the hunting-gate stands at the crossing of sport, farming, and politics: oak bars hung by enclosure, jumped by the hunt, cursed by the radical, and kept by the farmer who needs them for bullocks on every day that is not a hunting day. Cobbett names them as obstruction; the field knew them as invitation. Both were right, which is why the gates are still there — five bars between one England and another — long after the arguments of the 1820s have gone quiet and even the hounds have changed their tune.
