Cram in this card is a noun from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937): the concentrated, biscuit-like travel food that sustains Thorin's company — "chiefly cram, of course, and they were very tired of it; but cram is much better than nothing". In the story it is the dwarves' staple ration: dense, dry, filling and endlessly repeatable. The name is Tolkien's playful literalization of the native English verb cram, "to stuff tightly, to press into a confined space" — cram is precisely the food you cram into your mouth and your pack. The word still carries both faces today: a vivid, rough-grained everyday verb, and a fantasy noun beloved of readers.

Primary senses of the English word:

  • Verb, transitive — to stuff, to force into a confined space until full: "cram clothes into a suitcase", "cram a room with chairs", "cram papers into a drawer". The image is pressure and tight packing; the result is fullness to bursting.
  • Verb, transitive or intransitive — to eat greedily, hurriedly or to excess: "cram one's mouth with cake", "cram down a sandwich"; of animals, "to cram poultry" is the old practice of force-feeding fowl for the table — the method still used in modern foie gras production. A person may "cram oneself" or simply "cram".
  • Verb, intransitive — to pack a space full of people: "they all crammed into the lift", "cram into the back seat" — the crowded-transport sense of daily speech.
  • The academic sense (c. 1800, originally British university slang): "to cram" is to prepare for an examination by intense, last-minute memorization — literally stuffing knowledge into the head; hence the noun "a cram" (a bout of such study), "cramming", and "crammer" (a coaching tutor or cram-school). "I crammed all night for the exam" is everyday speech on both sides of the Atlantic, and "cram course" is standard American college slang.
  • Noun, general: "a cram" can mean the act or result of stuffing, or a dense mass ("a cram of people"); dialectally, chiefly in Scottish and Irish usage, "cram" is recorded for "a crumb, a small broken piece of bread" — a tellingly close cousin of Tolkien's biscuit-sense.
  • Noun, Tolkien's fantasy sense: the concentrated travel-biscuit of The Hobbit — hard, dry, sustaining and boring; the narrative's affectionate complaint ("they were very tired of it") makes it the comic, humble forerunner of the sacred elvish waybread lembas in The Lord of the Rings.

Semantic bridges. All senses spring from one physical picture: pressure forcing matter into a limited space. Packing a sack and packing a head are the same action in miniature — the exam sense is a 19th-century university metaphor ("cramming" facts "into" a pupil). The greedy-eating senses are simply the same pressure applied to food and throat. Tolkien's biscuit sits at the junction of all three: a food so dense it must be forced down, so compact it packs easily, and so sustaining that a mouthful counts as a meal. The word's rough, pressed-together sound (/kræm/, with its abrupt initial cluster and closed final consonant) is itself a small piece of mouth-feel matching the meaning.

Grammar and register: "cram" is a regular verb (cram – crammed – cramming); the noun is uncountable in the Tolkien sense and countable in the study sense ("a cram"). Register is colloquial, blunt and vivid — never technical, never euphemistic — which is why it suits both school slang and dwarfish humour.
