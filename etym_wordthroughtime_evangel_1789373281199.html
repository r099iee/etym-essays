- [c. 1300–14th c.] - Enters Middle English from Old French evangile and Ecclesiastical Latin evangelium, primarily signifying one of the four canonical New Testament Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) or the liturgical Gospel reading during the celebration of Mass - [connotation: sacred scripture, divine revelation, apostolic holy text]
- [16th c.–17th c.] - Reinvigorated during the Protestant Reformation as a battle-cry for the pristine biblical message of grace and faith unencumbered by medieval scholastic accretion or papal authority - [connotation: reformist zeal, apostolic purity, unvarnished scripture]
- [18th c.–19th c.] - Undergoes poetic secularization during the Romantic and Victorian eras (notably in Thomas Carlyle, Ralph Waldo Emerson, and Walt Whitman), expanding to denote any impassioned moral, artistic, or socio-philosophical gospel (such as Carlyle's "evangel of work") - [connotation: high moral earnestness, secular redemption, prophetic enthusiasm]
- [20th c.–Present] - Operates in contemporary literary criticism and philosophical discourse as an evocative term for monumental masterworks that offer complete existential or aesthetic salvation, while retaining its traditional theological dignity - [connotation: overarching redemptive vision, literary elevation, poetic resonance]
