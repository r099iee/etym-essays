The root of turning. The biography of PERVERSION opens with the Indo-European root *uert- (or *wert-), “to turn” — a verb of rotation whose descendants split Europe between “turning” and “becoming”. In the Germanic branch the root slid from turning to becoming: Old English weorϾan, “to become”, gave the language worth (the value that something comes to have), weird (from wyrd, “fate” — literally “what comes to be”), and the suffix -ward of toward and forward; German werden still serves as the auxiliary of the future tense. In the Indo-Iranian branch, Sanskrit vártate, “turns, proceeds”, produced vṛtta “conduct”, vartana “change”, and modern Hindi parivartan, “change, revolution”, which today names political transformation in Indian newspapers. Greek, remarkably, lost the root altogether, replacing it with trépō and stréphō, so that the entire family in Western Europe hangs on the Latin side. Rome builds the turn-verb. It is Latin that built our word. vertere, “to turn”, was one of Rome’s most generative verbs, spinning off versus (“turned”, and so verse and version — itself literally “a turning” of a text), vertex (the turning point, whence the head’s crown and the whirlpool vortex), and a swarm of prefixed forms — convertere, subvertere, advertere, invertere, dīvertere — which English has inherited wholesale as convert, subvert, advertise, invert, divert, and divorce. Our word came from pervertere, “to turn thoroughly, to overturn”, with the prefix per- (“through; to the bad”) fixed on the turn-verb. The past participle perversus meant literally “turned the wrong way”, and from it the noun perversiō was formed, “a turning about, a distortion”. Into English by two roads. English took perversion in the fourteenth century, in the great age of clerical and legal Latin borrowing, and it was reinforced by Old French perversion (13th century), itself a learned formation. From the same stem English later received perverse (obstinately contrary, 14th century) and pervert (the verb, “to overturn, corrupt”). The first English senses were theological: a perversion was the turning of a soul, a faith, or a doctrine away from truth — heresy, apostasy, the corruption of right belief. From heresy to law to taboo. The semantic career of the word is a story of escalating moral weight, visible in stages:
  • 14th–16th centuries — theological: the perversion of faith, doctrine, or a soul; the word is a dignified reproach, an accusation that something sacred has been turned against itself.
  • 16th–19th centuries — legal and moral: the word widens to any turning from the true, normal, or proper course — the perversion of justice, of laws, of a text’s meaning; English law fixes the phrase “perversion of the course of justice” as a named offence, and political rhetoric adopts “perversion of truth”. The minister’s sentence lives exactly here.
  • Late 19th–20th centuries — clinical: the specific psychological sense (sexual perversion) floods the word with taboo weight; the medical taxonomy of the fin de siècle, popularized through works such as Krafft-Ebing’s Psychopathia Sexualis, makes it the standard term for deviant sexual desire.
  • Modern register: heavily loaded and indignant; the sexual echo colours every use, so that a minister denouncing “a gross perversion of the ancient laws” unavoidably resonates with a modern reader far beyond the legal reproach of the medieval word.
Socio-cultural bridges. The shift from “turning aside” to “sexual deviance” is a miniature history of how a physical verb of motion acquired a moral axis and then a medical one. Each stage kept the root image of direction — to pervert is always to turn something away from its proper path — and each stage widened the circle of what could be perverted: first doctrine, then law and justice, then the body itself. The word’s power today rests on that accumulated charge: it is simultaneously the precise legal term of the minister’s reproach and a word most speakers first meet in the lexicon of taboo, which is why it can accuse and unsettle at once.
