The compound bogey-stories stitches together two very different pasts: a dark Germanic nursery goblin with no secure pedigree, and a learned Mediterranean word for inquiry that became the English tale. Its history is therefore double, fear on one side and narrative on the other, meeting only in the Victorian nursery where nurses needed both a monster and a telling.

The goblin half — bogey: Middle English bugge names a frightening spectre, the thing behind bugbear, a bear-shaped fright, and bugaboo, a later nursery variant. The word lives in northern and Scots speech as bogle, boggart, and boggle: field sprites, house haunters, will-o-the-wisp frights blamed for tangled ploughs and sour milk. Burns and Scott collect them as folklore, but nurses use them as discipline: be good or the bogle gets you. By the 1800s print standardises bogy and bogey for the nursery monster, and compounds multiply: bogey-man, the personalised fright who lurks in cupboards, and bogey-stories, the tales told about him. Sound change is minimal: the vowel rounds toward the modern oh, spelling wobbles between -ey, -y, and -ie, and the golf and aviation senses later prove how portable the vague dread remains.

The telling half — stories:

  • Greek historía (5th c. BCE): inquiry, knowledge gained by asking, then the written account of what was learned, Herodotus great investigation giving the word its learned weight.
  • Latin historia (classical): history, narrative, story in the grand sense, borrowed whole with its prestige intact.
  • Anglo-Norman estorie (13th c.): chronicle, illustrated tale, the word crossing into England with conquerors books and chapel windows, initial h lost in French mouths.
  • Middle English storie (14th c.): narrative of marvels, saints lives and romances, no longer required to be true: truth and tale divide, history keeping fact and story taking fancy.
  • Modern stories (plural): told tales of every kind, from nursery scares to novels, the everyday word for invented sequence.

Meeting in the nursery: Victorian print joins them because Victorian nurseries needed a dismissive label for what nurses did after tea. Directories of nursemaids, memoirs of Edwardian childhood, and comic magazines all use bogey-stories for the stock repertoire: goblins under beds, wolves beyond gardens, black men in chimneys. The hyphen records the compound youth: not yet one fused word like nightmare, still two ideas held together for one job. Tolkien, raised in just such a nursery, uses it with precision: Fatty nurses belong to that world of kindly threat, and the council must decide whether present wolves are nurse talk or true war.

Modern drift:

  • Public scare sense: journalists and politicians inherit the nurse role, and critics inherit the child grown sceptical, dismissing panics as bogey-stories, fear as technique.
  • Sport and service slang: golf bogey (one over par, from the 1890s song about the Bogey Man haunting the links) and air-force bogey (unknown contact) show the goblin thriving far from bedtime, dread miniaturised into games and radar.
  • Fantasy harvest: writers from Barrie to Rowling re-enchant the nursery fright, giving bogeys bodies, rules, and pathos, so that modern children meet in books the very monsters nurses once improvised, now polished and illustrated.

Why the compound endures: bogey-stories survives because every generation rediscovers the gap between formless fear and formed tale. The bogey supplies the shiver without shape, the story supplies the shape without belief, and together they let hearers practise being afraid in safety. To name them, as Tolkien speaker does, is already half to master them: call the fright a bogey-story and it shrinks to the nursery, refuse the name and it may walk out of the trees for real.
