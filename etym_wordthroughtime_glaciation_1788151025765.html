- [Latin 'glacies': ice; 'glaciare': to turn into ice — the plain vocabulary of cold] - [Perception: everyday and physical; nothing historical about it]
- [17th-century English: 'glaciation' first means simply 'the act of freezing,' the turning of liquids to ice] - [Perception: a kitchen-and-river word, used of frosts and frozen ponds]
- [Late 18th century: French and English science begin to speak of 'glacier' from Alpine dialects (glace 'ice') and of the glaciation of mountain valleys] - [Perception: the word migrates from the freezing of water to the slow sculpting of mountains by ice]
- [1837–1840: Agassiz announces the Ice Age; 'glaciation' is commandeered for the great ice sheets that once buried Europe and North America] - [Perception: a household word for cold becomes one of the most consequential ideas in geology — the earth has been repeatedly frozen over]
- [Late 19th century: 'glaciations' plural — geologists count successive ice ages in the Pleistocene] - [Perception: the word becomes a calendar of planetary chills; each glaciation is an epoch with a name]
- [20th century: Milanković cycles show glaciations are driven by wobbles of the earth's orbit; the last glaciation's retreat is dated to ~11,700 years ago] - [Perception: glaciations become predictable rhythms of the climate system, not catastrophes]
- [Today: climate science watches the opposite process — rapid deglaciation, retreating glaciers; 'the next glaciation' is deferred by warming] - [Perception: the word now points both to deep time and to our own moment: we live in an interglacial, an interlude between glaciations]
