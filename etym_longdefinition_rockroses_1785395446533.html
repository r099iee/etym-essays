Rockroses are low, evergreen shrubs of the genus Cistus and its close allies Halimium and Helianthemum, family Cistaceae — the rock-rose family — native to the Mediterranean basin and the dry, stony slopes of southern and western Europe. They bear single, five-petalled flowers of white, pink, purple, or yellow, with delicate, papery petals that open at dawn and fall by midday, the blossom so like a wild rose that it gave the plant its second element. Tough xerophytes, they root in rock crevices and poor calcareous soils where few shrubs will grow, and they store resin in their stems and leaves; Cistus ladanifer was long gathered for ladanum, a gum-resin prized as incense and perfume since antiquity, and the family's Greek name kístos is itself linked to the box-like seed capsule. The first element of the English name records that habitat — a preference for rocks — while the second records the resemblance of the flower to a rose. In the passage the yellow rockroses stand among thyme, sage, and marjoram on the turf between the boulders: aromatic, sun-loving plants of a wild Mediterranean upland, listed to make the vanished hillside fragrant and particular. The word carries the flavour of hardy, uncultivated, sun-baked natural beauty, and it sharpens the catastrophe that follows: this flowering, resinous slope is suddenly turned to bare, scree-covered rubble by the landslide. The tone of the noun in this context is elegiac enumeration — a catalogue of loveliness whose extinction is measured plant by plant. Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Cistus (true rockroses): The core botanical sense — shrubs of the genus Cistus, especially C. ladanifer, C. incanus, C. salviifolius, native to the Mediterranean and Macaronesia, with papery, short-lived flowers and resinous foliage; the plants that produce ladanum.
  • Helianthemum (sunroses, rockroses in horticulture): A closely allied genus, also called rockrose or sunrose, including the common rockrose Helianthemum nummularium of British chalk downs and the yellow-flowered species that fit Tolkien's "yellow rockroses"; low, tufted, drought-hardy sub-shrubs of poor rocky ground.
  • Halimium and other Cistaceae: The wider family sense — small shrubs of the Cistaceae, including Halimium and Tuberaria, sharing the five-petalled, rose-like flower and the rocky, sun-baked habitat.
  • The rose metaphor: A figurative use — the flowers are "roses" only in appearance (single, papery, five-petalled), and the compound itself states the simile: a rose that grows among rocks. In the plural form rockroses the word stands generically for any of these flowering shrubs.
  • Resin and ladanum (perfumery and history): A functional sense — the rockrose of the genus Cistus is the historical source of ladanum/labdanum, the sticky resin gathered from the stems of C. ladanifer in the Mediterranean and used since antiquity as incense, perfume, and medicine; the word invokes this trade in any discussion of ancient scent.
  • Horticultural and garden sense: In modern gardens, "rockrose" names the sun-loving, drought-tolerant shrubs grown on rock gardens, banks, and poor sunny borders, valued for their abundant but fleeting flowers.
  • Ecological register: In botanical description, rockroses are characteristic of garrigue, phrygana, and calcareous scrub — the word doubles as shorthand for the whole dry, aromatic Mediterranean plant community, as in Tolkien's listing of the herbs of the high country.
  • False-friend caution (not a rose): The word is used with an explicit or implied botanical correction — rockroses are not true roses (Rosa, Rosaceae) but Cistaceae; the likeness lies only in the flower, and the name is folk-botany, not taxonomy.
Historical bridge: the compound rock + rose is a piece of folk-botany, coined in English in 1578 by a simple act of naming — a rose-like flower that grows among rocks. From that single transparent compound the word has spread to cover a whole family of Mediterranean shrubs, to name their resin and its ancient trade, to characterise an entire ecology, and to stand in literature for the hardy, sun-baked, short-lived loveliness of the wild — always carrying the two plain facts of its coinage: it grows on rock, and it flowers like a rose.
