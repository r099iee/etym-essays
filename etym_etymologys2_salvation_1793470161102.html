PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*solh₂-
"whole, intact, uninjured"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*salwo-
"healthy, safe, intact"
↓
Latin (3rd c. BCE)
salvus
"safe, sound, unhurt"
↓
Late Latin (4th c. CE)
salvāre
"to save, deliver, preserve"
↓
Ecclesiastical Latin (4th c. CE)
salvātiō
"deliverance, spiritual salvation"
↓
Old French (11th c.)
salvaciun
"spiritual redemption, divine rescue"
↓
Middle English (c. 1200)
salvacioun
"redemption of mankind from sin"
↓
Modern English (current)
salvation
"deliverance from ruin, sin, or peril"

Note: The PIE root *solh₂- ("whole, well-kept") is also the ancestor of Latin solidus ("firm, whole"), sollus ("entire"), Greek holos ("whole"), and Sanskrit sarva- ("all, entire"). In classical Latin, salvus was an adjective; the verb salvāre and the abstract noun salvātiō were post-classical coinages developed specifically by Church Fathers (such as Tertullian) and popularized by Jerome's Vulgate to translate the Greek Christian term sōtēria.
