1. The Proto-Indo-European Root *telh₂- and Its Cognate Network. The ultimate etymological foundation of extolled resides in the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *telh₂- (or *tolh₂-), signifying "to bear, carry, endure, lift up, or weigh." This root represents one of the most vital mechanical and metaphorical verbs in Proto-Indo-European culture, giving rise to diverse lexical branches across the daughter languages:

  • Hellenic: Ancient Greek tlēnai ("to bear, endure, suffer"), talas ("enduring, wretched"), talanton ("balance, scale, weight, sum of money", the direct source of English talent), and the mythical Titan Atlas (the "Bearer" who supports the celestial heavens upon his shoulders).
  • Germanic: Proto-Germanic *thulaną ("to bear, suffer, endure"), which yielded Old English þolian and Scots thole ("to suffer, endure, abide"), preserving the patient endurance aspect of the root.
  • Celtic: Old Irish tlenaid ("he takes away, steals"), reflecting an aspect of carrying off or removing.
  • Italic: In Proto-Italic, the root developed two primary morphological branches: the nasal present verb *tol-n-ō (which yielded Classical Latin tollere) and the frequentative verb *tol-ē- (which produced Latin tolerāre, "to endure, bear", ancestor of English tolerate). Furthermore, the suppletive past system of Latin ferre ("to carry") utilized this root: the perfect tulī and supine lātum (from *tlā-tum) gave English translate, collate, dilate, and relate.

2. Latin Compounding and Classical Rhetoric: The Genesis of Extollere. In Classical Latin, the simplex verb tollere meant "to lift, raise up, elevate, take away, or remove." It carried profound ritual significance in ancient Roman family law: when a Roman matron gave birth, the infant was placed upon the earth before the father; if the father picked up the child in his arms (the ritual act termed tollere infantem or suscipere), he legally recognized the child as legitimate, claiming it for his household and sparing it from exposure.

To express upward, soaring motion, Latin writers combined tollere with the prepositional prefix ex- ("out of, forth, upward from below"), creating extollere. In its concrete military usage, it meant to hoist banners, raise ramparts, or lift a commander onto the shoulders of his legionaries. However, the golden age of Roman literature—dominated by Cicero, Lucretius, Virgil, and Livy—developed the figurative potential of the verb. In his philosophical dialogues, Cicero frequently spoke of extollere verbis ("to lift up with words, to praise, to eulogize") and extollere ad caelum ("to praise to the very skies"). The verb became an indispensable tool of classical epideictic rhetoric—the branch of oratory dedicated specifically to praise and blame.

3. Renaissance Humanism and Medieval English Adoption. Unlike many basic vocabulary words that entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman French in the immediate aftermath of the Norman Conquest, extol was a direct, learned borrowing from Classical Latin during the fifteenth-century Renaissance. As English humanists sought to translate classical philosophical texts, the Latin Vulgate Bible, and Renaissance humanist epistles into the vernacular, they recognized that native Germanic English (which relied on words like praise, herien, and bletsien) lacked an elevated, monumental Latinate verb to convey majestic oratorical celebration.

The verb was borrowed as extollen in late Middle English, with the past participle appearing as extolled. One of the earliest documented literary appearances occurred in theological and political treatises during the reign of Henry VII, where sovereign princes and saints were extolled for their wisdom, justice, and piety. By the early sixteenth century, the word had established itself firmly in humanist prose, notably in the translations of Erasmus and Sir Thomas More.

4. Scriptural Canonization and Early Modern Literature. The status of extolled as a premier term of reverent praise was cemented during the English Reformation by its inclusion in major English biblical translations. Miles Coverdale's Bible (1535), the Geneva Bible (1560), and ultimately the King James Authorized Version of 1611 made powerful use of the verb. In the Psalms and Isaiah, the faithful are repeatedly exhorted to praise the Almighty: "Extol him that rideth upon the heavens by his name JAH" (Psalm 68:4); "Behold, my servant shall deal prudently, he shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high" (Isaiah 52:13). In these sacred texts, extolled was intimately coupled with exalted, reinforcing its celestial, upward trajectory.

Concurrently, Elizabethan and Jacobean dramatists deployed the word with dazzling effect. William Shakespeare utilized the term in Hamlet (c. 1601), where Osric describes Laertes' extraordinary gentlemanly accomplishments, exclaiming, "his infuse of such a dearth and rareness, as, to make true diction of him, his semblable is his mirror; and who else would trace him, his umbrage, nothing more," to which Hamlet ironically replies, "Sir, his definement suffers no perdition in you; though, I know, to divide him inventorially would dizzy the arithmetic of memory... but, in the verity of extolment, I take him to be a soul of great article."

5. Modern Evolution and Contemporary Lexical Status. Throughout the eighteenth, nineteenth, and twentieth centuries, extolled maintained its high-register standing. During the British Enlightenment and the Victorian era, it became a standard verb in historical works (such as Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire and Thomas Macaulay's History of England) to describe the enduring renown of classical heroes, legal systems, and patriotic triumphs. In twentieth-century and contemporary English, extolled remains a vital, sophisticated verb in literary prose, academic criticism, and serious journalism. While the simplex infinitive extol is often utilized in present-tense reporting ("critics extol the virtues of renewable energy"), the past participle extolled carries a unique, settled gravitas, permanently enshrining individuals, virtues, and masterworks in the enduring pantheon of human admiration.
