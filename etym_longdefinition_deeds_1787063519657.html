Deeds — the plural of deed: (1) acts or actions, especially notable, brave, or morally significant ones; (2) in law, written instruments, executed and witnessed, by which ownership of land is transferred or obligations are created.

The word is one of the oldest in English and carries two vivid registers side by side. In the heroic register, "deeds" means the great acts that make a reputation: "deeds of arms", "warlike deeds", the "renowned deeds" of kings and warriors. It is exactly this sense that appears in the Tolkien sentence, where the wars and deeds of the town's great age "were now only a legend". In the legal register, a "deed" is a formal written document — signed, sealed, witnessed, and delivered — by which property (above all land) is transferred or rights are granted; the plural "deeds" is the everyday word for such documents ("the deeds of the house").

  • Primary sense — an act or action: anything done, considered as a doing. "He was judged by his deeds, not his words." This is the oldest sense, present in Old English; it survives in fixed phrases such as "in word and deed" (both in speech and in action) and "actions speak louder than words".
  • Secondary sense — a great or heroic act: a deed par excellence, a bold, noble, or memorable action: "deeds of valour", "to do great deeds". This sense carries the weight of epic and romance, of chivalry and adventure, and it is the sense Tolkien uses.
  • Tertiary sense — a moral act: in religious and ethical language a deed is an act with moral quality: "good deeds" and "evil deeds", "the reward of good deeds". The moral polarisation is ancient, and from this tradition the word "misdeeds" (evil acts) is formed.
  • Legal sense — a written instrument: since the 14th–15th centuries, a "deed" is a sealed written instrument, the permanent "act" of the parties reduced to writing: a deed of conveyance, a deed of gift, a trust deed, a deed poll. The plural "deeds" most commonly means the title documents of a property. The semantic bridge is transparent: a legal instrument is the act itself made durable and formal.
  • Derived and fixed phrases: "indeed" ("in deed" — in truth, in fact), "good deeds", "deeds of darkness" (evil actions), "the deeds and the titles", "to hold the deeds to a house".
  • Register and modern distribution: in ordinary modern speech the neutral sense has been largely taken over by "act" and "action"; "deeds" now sounds either elevated and literary (heroic register) or professional and legal (the conveyancer's word). This double identity — epic on one side, land-registry on the other — is what gives the plural its peculiar dignity.

Semantic reasoning and history: the whole history of the word is a slow enrichment of a plain noun for "doing". Old English dǣd meant simply "an act, a thing done". Christian moral writing polarised it into good and evil deeds. Chivalric and epic literature made it the token of heroism and reputation. And the law of real property gave it the precise, technical sense of a sealed written instrument. Each new sense was layered on the previous one without ever displacing it, which is why the modern plural "deeds" can sit, in one sentence, beside "wars" as a synonym for exploits, and in another beside "title" as a synonym for property documents.
