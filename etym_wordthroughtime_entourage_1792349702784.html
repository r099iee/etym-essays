- [Late 18th c. (French Enlightenment)] — coined in French from the verb *entourer* ("to surround"), referring literally to spatial surroundings, architectural environs, or the physical border framing an artwork — [perception: understood as an aesthetic and spatial term denoting harmonious ambient setting]
- [Early 19th c. (c. 1820s–1830s)] — semantic shift in post-Napoleonic French court society from inanimate physical borders to the human circle of courtiers, confidants, and diplomatic aides attending high royalty — [perception: viewed as a mark of aristocratic elegance, political intrigue, and imperial dignity]
- [Mid-to-Late 19th c. (Victorian English adoption)] — imported into formal English as a fashionable Gallicism denoting the retinue, travel party, and household retainers of visiting nobility and heads of state — [perception: regarded as a sophisticated, high-register descriptor of royal luxury and diplomatic ceremony]
- [20th c. (Hollywood & Political Modernity)] — broadened rapidly into popular media, political journalism, and the entertainment industry to describe the sprawling personal staff, bodyguards, stylists, and hangers-on surrounding celebrities, musicians, and athletic stars — [perception: celebrated as a hallmark of commercial fame and modern power, while occasionally critiqued as an insular echo chamber]
- [21st c. (Biochemical & Multidisciplinary Metaphor)] — extended metaphorically into pharmacology and cannabis research as the "entourage effect" (the synergy of secondary compounds enhancing primary cannabinoids) and in architectural drafting for figures populating 3D models — [perception: fully integrated into both popular pop-culture vernacular and rigorous interdisciplinary scientific discourse]
