Single root word: ferruginous (Latin-derived; the ultimate source, Latin ferrum, has NO known PIE etymology — it is a loanword). Stage 1 — Latin ferrum "iron": attested from the 3rd century BCE (Ennius, Plautus). No Indo-European etymology can be established; the word is widely believed to be a loanword absorbed by early Rome, possibly from Etruscan or an Italic/Mediterranean substrate that supplied iron technology along with its name. The proposal is debated but is the standard position of modern scholarship (de Vaan, Ernout–Meillet). Stage 2 — Latin ferrugo, ferruginis "rust; rust-colour; rust-fungus of grain": classical Latin, attested 1st century BCE–CE (Cicero, Pliny). A derived abstract noun from ferrum; the colour sense "rust-red" was fixed early, and the plant-pathological sense (rust attacking grain) is attested in the agricultural writers. Stage 3 — Late Latin ferruginosus "rusty, rust-coloured, iron-bearing": Late Latin, 4th–6th century CE. The adjective formed from ferrugo with the suffix -osus ("full of"). Stage 4 — English ferruginous "containing iron; rust-coloured": first recorded c. 1661 in English, adopted from Late Latin ferruginosus chiefly by geological and medical writers (ferruginous earths, springs, sandstone). Note on debate: the only genuinely disputed node is the ultimate origin of Latin ferrum itself. There is no PIE root *ferr-, no Greek cognate, and no Sanskrit cognate; every surrounding Indo-European language coined its iron-word differently (Greek sidēros, Sanskrit ayas, Germanic īsarn). The Etruscan/substrate loan hypothesis is standard but unproven, and the word's pre-Roman history is simply unknown. All later stages (ferrugo, ferruginosus, ferruginous) are secure and uncontroversial.
