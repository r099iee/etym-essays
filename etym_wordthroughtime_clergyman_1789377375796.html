- Late 16th Century (Tudor Reformation) - Emerges in Early Modern English as a compound of *clergy* and *man* to designate ordained Protestant churchmen, distinguishing married English parish ministers from celibate Roman Catholic monastics and friars.
- 17th Century - Embedded within the legal, civil, and ecclesiastical framework of the Church of England; the clergyman serves as a vital civic official maintaining the parish registry of births, marriages, and burials under royal authority.
- 18th Century (Georgian Era) - Achieves comfortable gentlemanly status in English county society; clergymen frequently hold lucrative benefices, mix with the landed gentry, and pursue scholarship, antiquarian studies, or literature (Sterne, Swift, White).
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes a central moral, social, and literary figure in the English novel (Austen, the Brontës, Trollope, Eliot), often torn between worldly ambition, pastoral duty, and the theological controversies of the Oxford Movement.
- 20th Century - Experiences declining social authority and diminishing numbers amidst widespread secularization, while maintaining an essential pastoral presence in community life.
- 21st Century - Often treated as a historical or traditional designation, increasingly complemented by gender-inclusive terms like *clergy member* or *minister* as women are ordained across Anglican and Protestant denominations.
