Elihu (pronounced /ˈel.ɪ.hjuː/, or archaically /ɪˈlaɪ.hjuː/) is a masculine proper name of Biblical Hebrew origin. Translating literally as "My God is He" or "He is my God," the name possesses profound theological, literary, historical, and institutional significance, most notably as the moniker of the youthful fourth speaker in the Old Testament Book of Job and of Elihu Yale (1649–1721), the British East India Company governor and benefactor whose timely patronage caused the Collegiate School of Connecticut to be renamed Yale College.

1. Thematic Dimensions and Cultural Spheres

The name Elihu resonates across four distinct cultural spheres:

  • Biblical and Theological Sphere: In the Hebrew Scriptures, a deeply pious theophoric name borne by several figures, chief among them Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite, whose passionate intervention in Job 32–37 challenges both Job's self-justification and the rigid dogmatism of his three elder counselors.
  • Colonial and Institutional Sphere: The personal name of Elihu Yale, whose financial gift of £562 in goods and a library of 417 books saved Connecticut's fledgling collegiate academy in 1718, establishing Yale University and giving birth to the collegiate demonym "Elis" for its students, alumni, and athletic teams.
  • Onomastic and Puritan Tradition: A hallmark of the seventeenth-century English and colonial American Puritan naming practice, chosen by devout families to replace medieval Catholic saint names with austere, scriptural declarations of monotheistic allegiance.
  • American Statesmanship and Legal History: Borne by prominent American leaders who shaped constitutional and international law, most notably Elihu Root (1845–1937), who served as US Secretary of War, Secretary of State, US Senator, and winner of the 1912 Nobel Peace Prize for modernizing the US Army and creating the Permanent Court of International Justice.

2. The Biblical Character: Elihu in the Book of Job

In literary and theological analysis, the figure of Elihu in the Book of Job remains one of the most intriguing and hotly debated characters in world literature:

  • The Rhetorical Turning Point: Elihu enters the narrative unexpectedly in chapter 32. He is young, and he explains that he waited to speak out of respect for the age of Job's three friends (Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar). However, seeing that the elders have utterly failed to refute Job, his "belly is like wine that hath no vent; it is ready to burst like new bottles" (Job 32:19).
  • Theological Innovation: Unlike the three elders, who insist that Job must have committed secret, heinous sins to merit his catastrophic losses, Elihu introduces a transformative concept: divine chastening is not merely punitive, but pedagogical. God speaks through visions, through illness, and through suffering to turn mortals back from pride and preserve their souls from the pit (Job 33:14–30).
  • Herald of the Whirlwind: Elihu’s concluding speech in chapters 36–37 builds toward a magnificent description of storm clouds, lightning, thunder, and northern golden splendor, functioning as the immediate dramatic overture that sets the stage for God Himself answering Job out of the whirlwind in chapter 38.

3. Historical Impact: Elihu Yale and Yale University

The institutional immortality of the name Elihu was forged through early eighteenth-century trans-Atlantic colonial trade and philanthropy:

  • The Collegiate Crisis: Founded in 1701 in Saybrook, Connecticut, the Collegiate School struggled with severe financial hardship, lacking adequate buildings, library resources, and political stability. After relocating to New Haven in 1716, the trustees desperately needed a wealthy benefactor to complete their new wooden college house.
  • The Mather Appeal: The Reverend Cotton Mather of Boston, displeased with the liberal theological direction of his alma mater Harvard, turned his eyes to the orthodox Calvinist school in New Haven. In 1718, Mather wrote to Elihu Yale—then residing in London as an immensely wealthy retired East India Company governor—appealing to his vanity and pious generosity.
  • The Gift and Naming: Yale shipped goods that yielded £562 12s, together with 417 valuable books and a portrait of King George I. At the commencement ceremony in September 1718, the trustees formally christened the building "Yale College." The moniker Eli (a natural truncation of Elihu) became the enduring nickname for Yale scholars, athletes, and traditions, and in 1903, the senior society Elihu was incorporated at Yale, dedicated to intellectual debate and open fellowship.

4. Pronunciation Nuance and Modern Register

Historically in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century England and New England, the name was pronounced with the accent on the second syllable: /ɪˈlaɪ.hjuː/ (rhyming with Elijah). In modern American English, the initial syllable has shifted forward in popular usage to /ˈel.ɪ.hjuː/ (rhyming with belly-who). Today, the name evokes historical gravity, Puritan heritage, and collegiate prestige, celebrated both in biblical exegesis and in the annals of American university history.
