The word commendation is a compound of a prefix and a verb built on two Indo-European roots. The full chain is given first, then the second element in isolation. MAIN CHAIN (manus "hand" + dare "give" → mandare → commendare → commendatio): PIE *man- "hand" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE) → Latin manus "hand" (attested 1st c. BCE; also source of manual, manuscript, manage, manicure) → Latin mandāre "to put into one's hand, entrust, commit; to order, command" — manus "hand" + dare "to give" (attested 1st c. BCE; source of mandate, command, demand, remand) → Latin commendāre "to entrust completely to the care of; to recommend, praise" — com- (intensive "thoroughly") + mandāre (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. Cicero) → Latin commendātiō, genitive commendātiōnis "commendation, recommendation; a letter introducing a person" (attested 1st c. BCE/CE) → Old French commendacion "praise, commendation" (12th–14th c. CE) → Middle English commendacion / commondacioun "entrusting; praise, recommendation" (14th c. CE) → English commendation "formal praise, approval, recommendation" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) SECOND ELEMENT, ISOLATED (the "give" root *deh₃-): PIE *deh₃- "to give" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE) → Latin dare "to give" (attested 1st c. BCE; the verb that combines with manus in mandāre; also source of data, date, add, edit, tradition) Debate / nuance note: The chain manus + dare → mandāre → commendāre → commendātiō is securely attested in classical Latin (Cicero uses commendare and commendatio for "entrusting" and for "recommending" persons). The only nuance is semantic: the shift from "entrust to care" to "speak well of" happened within Latin itself, driven by Roman social practice, so it is not a later French or English innovation.
