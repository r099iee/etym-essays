The evolution of deprecatingly spans more than four millennia and passes from Indo-European prayer to Roman state religion, from Renaissance theology to Victorian etiquette, and finally from the drawing-room to the software manual. The whole family descends from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *prek-, "to ask, beg, pray, entreat." This was one of the oldest "request" verbs of the family: Vedic Sanskrit praccháti "he asks", Avestan pərəsaiti "he asks", Old Irish arco "I ask", Gothic fraihnan "to ask", and Latin poscō "I demand, ask for" (formed as *prek-sk-) all descend from it. The breadth of these cognates shows how central "asking" was to early Indo-European society: requests, prayers, and legal demands were built from the same verbal material, and the root fed both the language of supplication and the language of command.

In Latin the root produced prex (genitive precis) "prayer, entreaty", the deponent verb precārī "to pray, beseech, beg", and the remarkable adjective precārius "obtained by asking, held at the pleasure of another, uncertain" — the source of English precarious, whose modern meaning "dangerously unstable" preserves the ancient idea that anything merely begged rather than owned hangs by a thread. Roman religion was a contract of reciprocal asking: the worshipper approached the gods with precisely formulated prayers (preces) seeking to avert harm, to secure favour, and to bind the deity to a return of grace. Even the state was involved: the college of priests framed fixed formulas so that no evil could slip through a lapse in wording.

The crucial compounding step came when Latin speakers prefixed dē- "away from, down from" to precārī, creating dēprecārī, "to pray (something) away, to avert by entreaty." In Roman usage one could deprecate a storm, a plague, the wrath of a deity, or — by transfer — the anger of a powerful man; the noun dēprecātiō was both a religious rite of aversion and a legal plea for pardon, the formal prayer of a defendant who begged to be let off. This double life — spiritual and forensic — explains the word's later range: deprecation could be aimed at misfortune, at an accusation, or at a sentence. In Christian Latin the language of deprecation merged seamlessly with the language of intercession and repentance: the liturgy's deprecationes were earnest prayers for mercy, and the penitent "deprecated" the wrath of God as the Roman had deprecated the wrath of Jupiter.

English met the family in the seventeenth century. The verb deprecate (recorded c. 1620) meant "to pray against, to express earnest disapproval of"; in seventeenth-century theology one deprecated the heresy one wished God to remove, and in political prose one deprecated the measure one wished the crown to abandon. Deprecatingly, formed with the participial -ing and the adverb suffix -ly, carried this protest-against meaning into the prose of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, where a gentleman might "deprecate" war, censure, or the levity of youth with the same courteous gravity.

The great semantic shift came in the twentieth century and is a story of etiquette. In a culture that prized modesty, direct self-praise was unacceptable, and self-criticism became the standard polite displacement for it. The reflexive compound self-deprecating (early twentieth century) named the strategy of disparaging oneself in order to deflect praise or to pre-empt criticism, and its adverbial forms now dominate the whole family. Under this pressure, deprecatingly drifted from "with earnest protest" toward "with humble self-disparagement", while the old devotional sense retreated into formal registers, surviving chiefly in phrases such as "he deprecatingly noted" or in the measured regrets of official apologies.

A final, unexpected extension: in late twentieth-century computing, "deprecation" acquired a technical sense — formally marking a feature as obsolete so that it will eventually be removed while still being tolerated in the interim. The underlying idea of "wishing away while politely signalling regret" is exactly the ancient dēprecārī, and deprecatingly occasionally appears in such manuals with its old protest-flavour intact. The word has thus travelled, with a single semantic core of "averting by humble entreaty", from Vedic prayer to Silicon Valley versioning.

Summary of the chain:
  • Sound and form: PIE *prek- → Latin prec- (with the characteristic Italic treatment of the root), prefix dē-, participle dēprecāt-, English deprecate, deprecating, -ly.
  • Semantic drift: "pray (harm) away" → "plead against, protest" → "express disapproval" → "express mild, self-directed disapproval; self-effacement".
  • Socio-cultural engines: Roman state religion and law; Christian liturgy and penitence; Reformation and Enlightenment polemic; Victorian and Anglo-American modesty culture; late-twentieth-century software practice.
  • Literary attestations: seventeenth-century theological and political prose; eighteenth-century sermons and parliamentary oratory; nineteenth-century novels describing apologetic smiles and self-effacing asides; contemporary journalism, comedy theory, and software documentation.
