• Transcription: Similar because both convert spoken or written linguistic material into an alternate written form; differs because transcription records acoustic and phonetic speech sounds (often using IPA), whereas transliteration maps written graphic characters (graphemes) from one script directly into another without regard to acoustic variations.
  • Romanization: Similar because both convert non-Latin scripts into another alphabet; differs because romanization specifically designates conversion exclusively into the Latin (Roman) alphabet, whereas transliteration can take place between any two scripts (e.g. from Greek to Cyrillic, or from Cyrillic to Devanagari).
  • Translation: Similar because both facilitate cross-linguistic communication across script boundaries; differs because translation interprets and transfers semantic meaning from one language to another, whereas transliteration merely converts characters while leaving the original words completely untranslated.
  • Orthography: Similar because both govern the systematic conventions of writing systems and spelling; differs because orthography defines the standardized spelling rules internal to a single language's script, whereas transliteration is an external conversion mechanism operating between two distinct writing systems.
  • Decipherment: Similar because both analyze and resolve written scripts across linguistic barriers; differs because decipherment is the historical and intellectual recovery of an unknown or forgotten writing system (such as Egyptian hieroglyphs or Linear B), whereas transliteration operates on known, codified writing systems.
  • Metaphrase: Similar because both strive for literal, unit-by-unit correspondence; differs because metaphrase is a strict word-for-word semantic translation between languages, whereas transliteration operates at the sub-lexical, letter-by-letter graphemic level.
  • Subliteration: Similar because both involve letter-level substitutions across scripts; differs because subliteration is an archaic or rare technical term denoting imperfect, approximate, or loose character substitution, whereas modern transliteration demands rigorous, bijective mathematical precision.
  • Vocalization: Similar because both clarify written text across scripts; differs because vocalization (such as Arabic harakat or Hebrew niqqud) adds internal vowel marks to an abjad within its native script, whereas transliteration transposes the entire text into an entirely different writing system.
