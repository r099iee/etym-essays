1. Indo-European ritual antiquity: the sacred act of anointing. The philological lineage of Añjana originates in the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₃engʷ- (or nasalized *angʷ-), fundamentally meaning "to smear, anoint, rub with fat, oil, or unguent." In ancient Indo-European pastoral cultures, fat, butter, and animal oils were precious life-sustaining substances used not only for physical nutrition and leather conditioning, but for the sacred consecration of ritual altars, idols, and bodies. This root branched with remarkable fidelity across the Indo-European dialects:

  • The Italic and Western branch — Proto-Italic *ongw- yielded Classical Latin unguere ("to anoint, smear") and the derivative neuter noun unguentum ("ointment, perfume, salve"). Through Old French, Latin inungere entered English as anoint, while ecclesiastical Latin gave English unction and extreme unction. In Germanic, the root survives in Old High German anko ("butter"), demonstrating the original connection to fatty substances.
  • The Indo-Iranian branch — In Proto-Indo-Iranian, the root became *anǰ-, developing in Vedic Sanskrit into the primary class-VII nasal present verb añj (present third-person singular anakti, plural añjanti), meaning "to smear, anoint, ornament, make shiny, polish, smooth, or clarify."

2. Vedic ritualism and the genesis of añjana. In the Rigveda and Yajurveda, the verb añj is an essential liturgical action. During the solemn Somayāga, the priests anoint the sacred sacrificial stake (*yūpa*) with clarified butter (*ghṛta*), singing hymns celebrating the post: "Añjanti tvāmadhvare devayantaḥ" ("The god-seeking priests anoint thee at the sacrifice"). Anointing was believed to impart not merely physical smoothness, but spiritual splendor (*tejas*) and divine visibility. By appending the neuter instrumental/action suffix -ana to the verbal root, Sanskrit formed the nominal derivative añjana: literally "the means of anointing" or "the substance smeared." In ancient Indian medicine and daily hygiene, this substance became specifically identified with eye-salve—a dark medicinal paste compounded from pulverized stibnite (antimony sulfide, sauvīrāñjana), galena, or camphor lampblack mixed with ghee, applied to cool the eyes and protect them from ophthalmia and the searing dust of the Indian summer.

3. The cosmogonic shift: the birth of the Ashta-Diggajas. As Indian religious literature transitioned from the Vedic sacrificial texts to the Epics (*Mahābhārata*, *Rāmāyaṇa*) and Purāṇas, the natural and aesthetic world was systematically personified into cosmic archetypes. In the Mātaṅga-līlā ("The Elephant Sport"), the foundational Sanskrit treatise on elephant science by Nīlakaṇṭha, the creation of elephants is linked to the primordial dawn of the cosmos. When Brahmā hatched the cosmic golden egg (*Hiraṇyagarbha*), he took the two clean shell halves in his hands; over the right half he chanted holy mantras, producing Airāvata and seven other celestial bull elephants, while over the left half he produced eight cow-elephants. These sixteen celestial pachyderms were assigned to steady the eight directions of space (*diś*), becoming the world-bearing Aṣṭa-Diggajas.

4. The naming of Añjana: the glossy black guardian of the West. In this cosmic distribution, each guardian elephant was named for a salient physical, coloristic, or mythic attribute. While Airāvata was luminous white like the foaming milk ocean, and Puṇḍarīka was white like the night-blooming lotus, Añjana was named for his magnificent, lustrous, pitch-black complexion—resembling a mountain carved of black antimony stone or an elephant thoroughly anointed (*añjita*) with shiny black pigment. Assigned to the Western quadrant (under the regency of Varuṇa), Añjana was credited with the cosmic responsibility of anchoring the western horizon against cosmic instability. In Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa (Bālakāṇḍa, Canto 6), the poet establishes a direct genealogical bridge between cosmic myth and historical reality: the supreme war-elephants of Ayodhyā—classified into the noble breeds bhadramandra, bhadramṛga, and mṛgamandra—are explicitly described as the earthly progeny of Añjana and Vāmana, validating the royal dynasty's military might through divine descent.

5. Philosophical metaphor and modern cultural persistence. In the medieval period, the literal meaning of añjana (eye-salve) was elevated into one of India’s most influential philosophical metaphors: jñānāñjana. Adi Shankara and subsequent Vedantic commentators employed the image of eye-salve to explain the removal of metaphysical ignorance (*avidyā*), portraying the Guru as an enlightened oculist. Concurrently, the feminine Añjanā was immortalized in popular devotion as the mother of Hanumān (*Āñjaneya*). Today, the word Añjana bridges thousands of years of South Asian cultural evolution—from an ancient Indo-European root for anointing fat, to an Ayurvedic eye medicine, to a colossal cosmic elephant steadying the western horizon of the ancient universe.
