Loskiel is a surname of Baltic German type, and in English letters it denotes one man: Georg Heinrich Loskiel, anglicised as George Henry Loskiel (1740–1814), a bishop of the Moravian Church and the compiler of the most widely circulated eighteenth-century history of the Moravian missions among the Native peoples of North America. The word is not a common noun and has no figurative life of its own. Its entire semantic content is bibliographic and biographical: it names a person, and through him a book, and through the book a particular window onto colonial missionary history.

Primary sense: the man. Loskiel was born on 7 November 1740 at Rinda in the Duchy of Courland and Semigallia, in present-day Latvia, the son of Georg Heinrich Loskiel, a Lutheran pastor serving the parishes of Rinda, Ārlava and Tukums. Educated at the Moravian college and theological seminary at Barby in Germany, he was ordained a preacher of the Unitas Fratrum in 1765. He rose steadily in the Church: deputy to the presiding presbyter Peter Hesse in 1782, presiding presbyter of the Moravian Church in Livonia in 1785, and leader of the congregation at Saint Petersburg in 1789. He then returned to Saxony, where he helped establish educational institutions at Kleinwelka, near Bautzen, and at Gnadenfrei in Silesia. In 1801 he moved to the United States, became president of the Direction of the Pennsylvania congregations and preacher at Bethlehem, was consecrated bishop in 1802, and served as presiding bishop of the northern district of the American province until ill health forced him to retire in 1811. He was elected to the Church's chief executive board at Berthelsdorf in 1812, but his health prevented him from leaving America, and he died at Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, on 23 February 1814.

The book that made the name. Loskiel's fame rests on a single work: Geschichte der Mission der evangelischen Brüder unter den Indianern in Nordamerika, published at Leipzig in 1789. It appeared in English in 1794 as History of the Mission of the United Brethren among the Indians in North America, translated by Christian Ignatius Latrobe and issued in three parts by the Brethren's Society for the Furtherance of the Gospel in London. The book is a compilation rather than a travel narrative. Loskiel never worked as a missionary in America; he assembled, edited and arranged the reports, diaries and letters sent home by the Brethren's missionaries, above all the voluminous records associated with David Zeisberger and the congregations in Pennsylvania, Ohio and the Ohio Country.

  • Structure. The three parts move from the earliest Moravian contacts and the founding of the mission, through the daily life, converts and persecutions of the Christian Indian towns, to the later wars and removals that scattered the congregations.
  • Sources. The work preserves material that would otherwise survive only in manuscript: baptismal registers, travel journals, remarks on Delaware (Lenape) and Mohican life and speech, and accounts of the Gnadenhütten massacre of 1782.
  • Translation. Latrobe's English version, published for the Brethren's Society, made the book accessible to British and American readers and gave English historiography its standard citation form, “Loskiel”.
  • Other writings. Loskiel also published a Passion and Easter hymn cycle (1781), a volume of prayers and meditations in verse (1813), and a verse narrative of a journey from Bethlehem to the Indian town of Goshen, Ohio, in 1803, printed much later as Extempore on a Wagon (1887).

Secondary sense: the citation and the source. In historical and anthropological writing, Loskiel functions as shorthand for the 1789 history and, by extension, for the Moravian documentary record as a whole. A footnote that reads “Loskiel” tells the reader that a claim about Delaware agriculture, burial custom, captivity or missionary policy derives from the Brethren's own archive. The word thus acquires a second, bibliographic identity: not the bishop but the book, and not the book merely as an object but as a body of evidence whose reliability and bias must be assessed.

Tertiary sense: the quoted witness. The passage in the card illustrates this perfectly. Sumner, arguing that communism is “unbiological”, cites Loskiel for the claim that certain northeastern tribes were “so lazy that they plant nothing themselves” and rely on others to share their produce. The sentence is doing rhetorical work far removed from Loskiel's own purpose. In the history, such observations belong to a missionary ethnography that measured Native economies against European ideals of industry, thrift and settled agriculture. Modern readers treat the quotation as evidence less about indigenous laziness than about the assumptions of eighteenth-century European observers, and about the ease with which a colonial source could be recycled by a Gilded Age sociologist to support a theory of social selection.

The surname itself. The name is recorded in Latvian as Loskīls, an adaptation to Latvian orthography, and in German as Loskiel. Its pre-eighteenth-century origin is not securely documented. Courland was a linguistically mixed duchy in which Baltic German clergy and gentry lived alongside Latvian- and Livonian-speaking populations, and surnames in the region frequently represent Germanised or partially translated forms of Baltic names. Proposals connecting the element Losk- with a Baltic or Slavic etymon remain speculative, and no verified derivation can be asserted. What is certain is the family's social location: a Lutheran pastor's household in the Baltic German clerical estate, educated in German Moravian institutions and mobile across the German, Russian and American provinces of the Church.

Modern connotations. Today Loskiel is an academic name. It appears in bibliographies of Moravian history, in studies of eighteenth-century missions and in discussions of the documentary sources for Lenape and Mohican history. Outside those fields it is unknown, and it carries no emotional charge beyond the mild antiquarian flavour of an old title page. Its interest is that a man who never set foot among the tribes he described became, through careful compilation, one of the most cited European witnesses to their eighteenth-century world — a reminder that the authority of a historical source often belongs to the editor rather than the eyewitness.
