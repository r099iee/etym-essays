censuring is the present participle and gerund of the transitive verb to censure, meaning "to express severe disapproval of someone or something, especially in a formal statement; to condemn or rebuke another person's conduct or character as morally blameworthy." In the specific literary context of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Jane Bennet declares: "I would not wish to be hasty in censuring anyone; but I always speak what I think." The gerund here makes censuring a deliberate, weighty act of moral speech — a formal verdict on someone's character rather than casual gossip or a passing dislike. Jane's scruple is that passing such a judgment is serious and ought not to be done hastily; her own sweetness of temper is defined precisely by her reluctance to pronounce on others' faults. In Regency society, where reputations could be destroyed by a word, the power to censure was real, and Jane's caution is both an ethical and a social virtue.

Primary sense. To censure is to judge adversely, to find fault in a formal and explicit way, and to state that fault publicly. The word implies an authoritative or at least a reasoned standpoint: the person who censures adopts the position of a moral assessor, weighing conduct against a standard. Unlike a merely emotional outburst, censure is pronounced, deliberate, and often recorded — in a speech, a vote, a resolution, or a written judgment.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The word operates across several distinct domains, each with a traceable historical bridge:
  • General moral censure. Expressing stern disapproval of a person's conduct or character, as in Austen's sentence. This is the broadest sense, usable in ordinary speech and writing.
  • Parliamentary and political censure. A "motion of censure" or "vote of censure" is a formal reprimand issued by a legislature or deliberative body against one of its members or against a government, short of removal from office. The bridge is the Roman censor's public marks of disgrace, applied to modern assemblies.
  • Ecclesiastical censure. In church law, a censure is a formal spiritual penalty — excommunication, suspension, interdict — imposed for grave sin. This was historically the dominant sense of the French and Latin words from which English censure descends.
  • Corporate and professional censure. Boards, bar associations, and regulatory bodies issue formal censures of members for misconduct — a written rebuke recorded in the member's file, often public.
  • Self-censure. The reflexive use ("to censure oneself," "self-censure") means self-blame, self-reproach, or the internal restraint of one's own conduct and speech — a psychological sense much used in modern discussion of press and individual conscience.
  • Confusable twin: censoring. Though both words descend from Latin censor, censure (to condemn formally) and censor/censoring (to suppress objectionable material) are distinct: modern "censorship" means removal of content, while "censure" means formal condemnation. The two families split apart in the 16th century.

Register, collocation, and grammar. Censuring as a gerund functions as a noun ("hasty in censuring anyone," "the censuring of the minister"); as a participle it is adjectival ("a censuring tone," "censuring remarks"). Common collocations: "to censure someone for something," "hasty in censuring," "publicly censure," "vote of censure," "motion of censure," "censure for misconduct." The register is formal, literary, legal, and moral; it belongs to the language of ethics, the law courts, the pulpit, and the legislature, and in the novel of manners it carries solemn, judicial weight.
