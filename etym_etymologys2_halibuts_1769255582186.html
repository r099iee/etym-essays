HALIBUTS is the plural of halibut, a compound of haly (“holy”) + butte (“flatfish”), each root with its own chain.

CHAIN 1 — the HALY (“holy”) element:
Stage 1: PIE *kailo-, meaning “whole, uninjured, sound, of good omen” (c. 4000–3000 BCE).
Stage 2: Proto-Germanic *hailagaz, meaning “holy, consecrated, blessed” (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE); formed from *hailaz “whole, hale, healthy” with the -ga- suffix.
Stage 3: Old English hālig, meaning “holy, consecrated, sacred, blessed” (attested from c. 8th–11th c. CE; from earlier hālag).
Stage 4: Middle English holi, holy, also haly, hali, meaning “holy” — the form hali/haly survives as the first element of old compounds (c. 1100–1500 CE).
Stage 5: In the compound haly + butte → Middle English halybutte, halibut, literally “holy flatfish” — the fish eaten on holy days (first recorded c. 1400–1450).
Stage 6: Modern English halibuts, plural “the great edible flatfishes Hippoglossus” (modern English).

CHAIN 2 — the BUTTE (“flatfish”) element:
Stage 1: Uncertain origin. The flatfish word is traditionally linked to the Germanic “thick, blunt, stubby” family: Old English but, buttuc, “the thick end, the butt, the blunt end” (attested c. 1000 CE), and its relatives (Middle Low German butte, Dutch bot, “flatfish”; Old Norse butr, “a log, a stump”).
Stage 2: Middle English butte, “a flatfish, specifically a flat or blunt fish” (c. 1200–1500 CE) — so named from the fish’s thick, blunt, flattened shape.
Stage 3: Compound: haly + butte → halybutte, halibut, “holy flatfish” (c. 1400–1450 CE).
Stage 4: Modern English halibuts, plural (modern English).

Debated / nuanced points: The second element, butte, is not fully secure. The traditional explanation reads it as the Germanic “flat, blunt, thick” word, giving “flatfish”; the fish’s shape justifies the name. Some sources instead connect the butte element to the Old Norse butr “log, stump” or to a distinct pre-Germanic substrate word, and the ultimate PIE form (if any) is unknown — no PIE etymon for the flatfish word is reconstructed with confidence. The first element, “holy,” is secure: halibut is the fish that could be eaten on holy days, when meat was forbidden, and this etymology is reported from the earliest records. The plural halibuts is the ordinary English plural of a fully nativised compound.
