amulet (noun) — /ˈamjʊlɪt/ — a small object — a stone, jewel, pendant, ring, band, or inscribed tablet — worn on the body, or carried, kept, or fastened to a person or place, and believed to protect its wearer against evil, danger, disease, sorcery, or misfortune; a portable charm of defence.
  • Primary sense — the physical protective charm. The amulet is a tangible, wearable object of protection: a jade disc on a neck-cord, a silver medallion, a carved rune bound to the wrist. Its power is passive and apotropaic — it is believed to ward off harm rather than to summon good luck. In the card’s passage — “fitted the amulet holding Laccodel’s Rune to his wrist” — the amulet is exactly such a thing: a wrist-worn object carrying a protective rune, equipment of the working magician, worn next to the skin like a shield that travels with the body.
  • Secondary sense — any protective object kept or carried. By extension the word covers objects not worn but kept: an amulet hung at a door, buried beneath a threshold, carried in a pouch, hung in a vehicle. The functional constant is a protective or luck-bearing agency attached to a person or place by contact, proximity, or inscription. Anthropologists use the term loosely for protective objects across cultures, from Egyptian scarabs to European witch-bottles to modern “lucky” coins and pendants.
  • Modern figurative and technical senses. In fantasy literature and gaming — the cultural register most familiar to modern readers — an “amulet” is an equippable magical accessory granting specific powers or stat bonuses (“amulet of health,” “amulet of protection”). The fantasy sense is a direct descendant of the folk belief, systematised: what the folk charm did by faith, the game amulet does by rule. In mineralogy and gem-craft, “amulet” is used informally for stones prized for protective properties (moonstone, turquoise, tiger’s eye).
  • Distinguishing boundaries. An amulet is worn and is defensive; a talisman may be any object — worn or not — and actively brings fortune or power, often bearing a symbol or engraving that concentrates its agency. A charm is a broader word covering spells, words, and small trinkets alike. A phylactery is a religious box of scripture worn on the person (or, in modern fantasy, a receptacle holding a soul). A mascot brings luck to a group; a fetish is an object believed to possess a spirit or to be itself endowed with power; a relic is a holy remnant, typically of a saint.
  • Register. Amulet is matter-of-fact and practical — the word of the apothecary, the traveller, and the magician, not of the sentimentalist. It lacks the ceremonial grandeur of talisman and the folk warmth of trinket; it is the neutral, almost scientific term for a worn protection, which is why archaeology and ethnography adopted it.
  • Semantic bridge — a remarkably stable word. From Pliny’s fever-amulets in the first century to the wrist-rune of a dying-earth wizard, the meaning has barely shifted in two thousand years: a small object worn to keep harm away. What has changed is the cultural register — from Roman folk-medicine and magic, to the ethnographic curiosity of the 19th century, to the rule-bound accessory of modern fantasy — while the core sense of portable defence has proven almost perfectly stable.
  • Related words. Latin amulētum; French amulette; Italian amuleto; Spanish and Portuguese amuleto; German Amulett. The English word arrived late (1603) and remains bookish and learned.
The amulet is thus the most elementary form of protective magic — a thing worn against the dark. In the reading passage it is precisely the instrument of a careful, unsentimental magician: fitted to the wrist before a journey, it is both jewellery and armour, ornament and ward, and the word’s dry, practical tone matches the wizard’s methodical preparation.
