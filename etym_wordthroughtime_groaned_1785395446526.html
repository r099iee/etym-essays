- [Old English: the sound of grief] - From grānian, the word named a low, deep sound of grief or pain — the vocal lament of mourning, uttered in sorrow rather than complaint.
- [Middle English: complaint and strain] - The sense widened to complaint and to the heavy sound of things under load: wood bending, gates turning, tables burdened — 'groaning' became as much a property of objects as of people.
- [16th–19th century: comic exaggeration] - 'Groan' acquired a theatrical, even comic register of exaggerated suffering, the loud sigh of the put-upon, while still covering genuine bodily distress and exhaustion.
- [Contemporary sense] - An involuntary, deep, prolonged sound of pain, despair, or weariness — or of a thing giving way under weight — and in casual use the word of ironic, self-dramatizing complaint (groaning at a bad pun), keeping the greatest physical weight of the sigh-moan set.
