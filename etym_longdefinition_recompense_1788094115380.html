In the source passage — “From whom should we claim the recompense of our damage, and aid for our widows and orphans?” — the word means compensation: payment or amends made for harm suffered. The speaker is asking from whom the ruined townsfolk can claim repayment for their losses. The word is formal and weighty, the language of grievance and justice rather than of commerce.

The senses of recompense:

  • Compensation for loss, damage or injury (the core sense) — “recompense for damage”, “in recompense for his suffering”. The noun names what is given to make good a wrong or a loss.
  • Repayment, requital of services or merit — “recompense for faithful service”, “a just recompense of their labours”. Here the word means reward, the return given for effort or virtue.
  • Requital in general, good or ill — older usage allows recompense of evil for evil as well as good for good; the biblical “I will recompense evil” (Proverbs 20:22, KJV) shows the word carrying the sense of retribution, the repayment of a wrong in kind.
  • Divine recompense — theological usage: God's recompense in the afterlife, rewarding the righteous and punishing the wicked; “the day of recompence” (Deuteronomy 32:35, KJV) is the day of divine judgment.
  • The verb — “to recompense” means to compensate, repay or reward: “to recompense a loss”, “to recompense someone for their trouble”. The verb is transitive and slightly more common in older prose than in modern speech.

Register and usage. In modern English “recompense” is formal and literary. Legal and insurance language prefers “compensation”, “damages” or “indemnity”; everyday speech prefers “repayment” or “making it up to someone”. But “recompense” remains the word of choice when the speaker wants dignity: in moral argument, in historical fiction, in religious prose and in formal negotiation, “recompense” names the full restoration that is owed — not merely the price of a thing, but the making-good of a wrong. It appears in compounds and fixed formulas such as “in recompense for”, “as a recompense”, and the verb “to recompense someone for something”, and it is frequently paired with words of loss and justice — damage, injury, labour, sacrifice, widows and orphans — because it points to a balance that has been broken and must be restored.

Read as a noun, recompense sits alongside a cluster of near-synonyms that share its market imagery but diverge in register: compensation is the bureaucratic-legal term; remuneration is the payment for work; damages is what a court awards; restitution restores property rather than simply paying for it; redress sets a grievance right; indemnity is contractual protection. What distinguishes “recompense” is that it alone keeps the metaphor of the scales alive — the idea that a wrong has weight, that it must be balanced by an equivalent return, and that the balance is the work of justice rather than of commerce.

The semantic bridge. The word is built on the Latin verb compensāre, “to weigh together, to balance” — from cum “together” and pensāre “to weigh”, a frequentative of pendere “to weigh, to pay”. The image is the balance scale: to recompense is to weigh out an equivalent, to put back on the scales what was taken off. The prefix re- “back, again” makes it “to weigh back”, to restore the balance. Money itself was weighed in antiquity — the Roman pound was a weight of metal — so “weighing” and “paying” were the same act, and the word's whole family (compensation, pension, spend, pound) is built on the scales of the ancient market.
