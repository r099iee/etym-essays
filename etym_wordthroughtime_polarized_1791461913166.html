- 1811, optics - polarize is coined for light whose vibrations are made to align in one plane; the word is technical, experimental and confined to natural philosophy
- 1830s–1860s - magnetism, electrochemistry and crystallography extend it: bodies, molecules and electrodes acquire poles and polarity; the term spreads across the physical sciences
- Late 19th c. - the image of two opposite poles begins to be applied to opinions and groups; polarized starts to mean "drawn to opposite extremes" in political commentary
- Early–mid 20th c. - the figurative sense matures; newspapers describe polarized opinion, polarized blocs and a polarized world, especially in Cold War discourse
- 1960s–1990s - social psychology formalizes group polarization; the word becomes a diagnostic term for media, legislatures and electorates
- Contemporary - polarized is a keyword of anxiety about democracy and social media; it describes algorithmic audiences, entrenched partisanship and "us-and-them" politics, while the optical sense quietly survives on every pair of sunglasses
