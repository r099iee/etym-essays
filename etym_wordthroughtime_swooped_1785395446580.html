- [Old English ancestor swāpan] - A sweeping, rushing motion of wind, storm, and sword — the verb carried no downward or predatory force at all, merely a vigorous, wide, curved sweep through the air or across a field.
- [Middle English split (swōpen)] - The family divided: the long stem swāp- became the gentle horizontal sweep, while the past stem swōp-, still charged with sudden rushing descent, began drifting toward a distinct verb of its own.
- [16th-century specialization] - The word crystallizes as swoop in the vocabulary of the chase: where earlier English said a hawk "sweeps down," the Tudor period said it "swoops" — the descent of the predator, its o-vowel a fossil of the old strong past tense.
- [Modern register] - A swoop is now the raptor's trademark arc — speed, grace, and inevitability in one continuous, controlled strike — extended to any sudden descent or raid (a police swoop); in Tolkien it is predatory yet heroic, the return of wild, high justice.
