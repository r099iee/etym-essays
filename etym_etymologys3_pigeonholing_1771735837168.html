Pigeonholing is a thoroughly English word — not borrowed, not learned, but built at home out of two native or long-naturalized elements, pigeon and hole, and fused by the metaphors of the writing desk. Its history is the history of how a piece of furniture produced a word for intellectual rigidity. Part one, pigeon. The bird's name is onomatopoeic, one of the rare cases where a language's word imitates the sound it names. Late Latin pīpiō, "a young chirping bird," was formed from the imitative verb pīpīre, "to chirp, to peep," which mimics the thin squeak of nestlings — the same root family that gives English peep (through Old French pépier or the verb peep) and Latin pīpilo. Old French took the word as pijon, pigeon, meaning a young dove, and from there it passed into Middle English in the 14th century as pigeon, at first a young dove, later any dove, and finally the urban bird of the streets. In the 16th century the bird's foolish, cooing, easily-caught character gave it a figurative sense in English: a pigeon was a dupe, a gullible person easily plucked — the ancestor of "stool pigeon," a decoy. (In French the same word, pigeon, means both the bird and "dupe.") Part two, hole. The noun hole descends through the Germanic line from Proto-Indo-European *kel-, "to cover, conceal, hide" — the same root that gives Latin cēlāre, "to hide" (whence conceal, occult), Greek kalóptein, "to cover" (whence apocalypse, "uncovering"), and, remarkably, English hell, literally "the concealed place" of the dead. The Germanic formation *hulą meant "a hollow, a covered cavity," and Old English hol is attested in the 8th–9th centuries for a hole, hollow, or cave. Middle English hole (12th–13th c.) generalized the word to any cavity or opening, and modern English hole is simply its unchanged descendant. The semantic thread is "that which is hollowed out and covered over" — a recess, a niche, a cavity. The two words met in the furniture of the early modern office. Writing desks and cabinets of the late 17th century were fitted with rows of small square compartments for sorting letters and papers, and these compartments were called pigeonholes, attested from the 1680s. The image is immediate: the tiers of small recesses, each with a paper tucked inside, resembled the nesting boxes of a dovecote with pigeons roosting in them. The desk itself was the dovecote. From the noun came the verb: in the 1840s, to pigeonhole meant the literal act of filing papers away into these compartments. The figurative leap followed almost immediately, carried by the logic of the metaphor itself:
  • Filing as forgetting. A paper filed in a pigeonhole is put away — set aside, often out of sight. Hence, from the mid-19th century, to pigeonhole a proposal, request, or bill was to set it aside without action, to shelve it. The pigeonhole becomes a place where things go to be ignored.
  • Filing as classifying. Each compartment holds a certain kind of paper; sorting into pigeonholes is classification. By the late 19th century, to pigeonhole a person or thing was to assign them to a fixed category — with the damning implication that, like a paper in a too-small compartment, they are forced to fit and cannot escape.
  • The desk as the bureaucratic mind. The rise of offices, filing systems, and administrative bureaucracy in the 19th and 20th centuries made the pigeonhole the universal image of organized thought — and of thought that organizes at the price of distortion.
By the early 20th century the figurative sense had completely displaced the literal: to pigeonhole now means, above all, to assign something or someone to a rigidly restrictive category, and the gerund pigeonholing names the act. In the passage, the word does its work through this whole history. Gibbon, the author says, "has not taken a place as the originator of any large new conceptual framework, or any novel way of pigeonholing the human past." The praise is double-edged and deliberate: to pigeonhole the past would be to file its messy, flowing reality into small fixed compartments — the way an office clerk sorts letters, the way a taxonomist pins specimens. Gibbon instead narrates; he does not compartmentalize. The word also inherits the slight contempt of its ancestors — the gullible pigeon, the dark concealed hole — so that pigeonholing is never quite respectful of its object. It is the occupational habit of the schematizer, the system-builder, the mind that would rather file history than tell it. Phonetically the compound is transparent: pigeon (from French pigeon) + hole (native English), with the stress on the first element in the gerund, pigeonholing. The form itself records the two streams of English vocabulary — the Norman-French bird and the Anglo-Saxon cavity — fused in a desk metaphor, and the word's career from 1680 to the present shows how a single homely image, the dovecote of the office desk, could grow into a global idiom for intellectual reduction.
