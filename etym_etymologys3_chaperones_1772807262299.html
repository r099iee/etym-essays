The biography of chaperones begins with a piece of clothing. Late Latin cappa named a cape or hooded cloak, a simple covering for the shoulders and head worn across the late antique and early medieval West. The word itself is of obscure origin: standard references can take the record back to cappa, but no further, and the favourite guess — that it derives from Latin caput 'head' and meant at bottom a head-covering — is plausible but unproven, so a careful account of the word's prehistory simply stops there rather than inventing a PIE root. Whatever its birth, cappa was enormously productive. It passed into Old French as chape, 'cape, cope, covering,' the source of English cape (a garment) and the liturgical cope, and into the Romance vernaculars as Italian cappa and Spanish capa, 'cape.' From chape, Old French built the diminutive chaperon, attested from the twelfth century with the sense 'hood, soft cap.' This was a specific and highly visible piece of medieval dress: knights wore a chaperon, a loose hood with a long liripipe tail, and the garment framed and protected the head in a way no other article of clothing quite did. The diminutive is important, because it shows speakers repeatedly re-making the word as 'little cape' — a small hooded covering — and that repeated diminutive formation is the pivot on which the whole later history turns. The decisive change was a metaphor. As a hood covers and shelters the head, so, in later medieval and early modern French, a chaperon came to mean a person who 'covers' another — specifically an older woman who accompanies a younger, unmarried woman in public, screens her from impropriety, and shields her reputation from gossip. The garment and the guardian were united by the idea of protection: to chaperone someone is, etymologically, to throw a hood over their conduct. The figurative sense is attested in French in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and was fully fixed by the seventeenth, when the word was also used of a man who accompanied a lady in public. English borrowed chaperon in 1720, at the height of the eighteenth century's elaborate courtship etiquette, with exactly this social meaning: a woman who escorts a young unmarried woman in public to preserve decorum and respectability. It arrived at the moment when London polite society was institutionalising the rules of the ball and the promenade, and the word fitted the new vocabulary of chaperonage (the practice) and of being 'out' in society. The spelling chaperone, with a final -e, appears in the nineteenth century, probably shaped by association with French feminine forms, and it became the dominant form; the verb to chaperone, and the plural chaperones used on this card, follow naturally. Since then the word has travelled far from the medieval hood. In the twentieth century the core social sense generalised beyond gender and courtship: any responsible adult supervising a group of young people — on a school trip, at a dance, on an excursion — is now a chaperone. The same protective image has also been re-metaphorised in two technical registers: a medical chaperone is a third party present during an intimate examination, and a molecular chaperone is a protein that guides other proteins to fold correctly, a usage adopted in biochemistry in the 1980s because the helper-protector metaphor was felt to be exact. Sister garment words — cape, cap, cope — share the cappa ancestry but never developed the social sense; and the word chapel, from Late Latin capella, is often popularly linked to cappa through the story of St Martin's cloak, though that connection is debated and belongs to a separate, if neighbouring, history. What is not debatable is the durability of the image: from a hood over the head, to a guardian over a reputation, to a protein guiding another to fold, the chaperone has always been the thing that covers, shelters, and guides.
