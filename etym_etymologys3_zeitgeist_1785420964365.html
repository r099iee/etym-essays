The noun ZEITGEIST is a modern German compound of two ancient Indo-European roots, and it carries a philosophy of history inside its spelling. Its two halves must be traced separately before the compound is examined, because each element has its own long biography, and the meaning of the whole depends on the distinct histories of its parts. The first element: Zeit ("time"). The German word Zeit descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *dā- / *dī-, "to divide, cut up," a root that appears to have underlain the notion of time as something marked off into portions — the divisions of the day, the seasons, the hours. From this root Proto-Germanic formed *tīdi-, "a division of time, a season, an hour," and the Germanic languages split the inheritance in characteristic ways. Old English had tīd, "time, season, hour," the ancestor of English tide, which in older English meant precisely "time" — as in Yuletide, eventide, and the tides of the year — before it narrowed, in the sixteenth century, to the periodic rise and fall of the sea, whose times are still called tides. A parallel Germanic formation, *tīmōn, produced Old English tīma and modern English time itself. In the continental branch, Old High German zīt, attested from the eighth and ninth centuries, meant "time, season, period," and this is the direct ancestor of modern German Zeit. The physical image at the root is of time as something cut up and marked off — an image that survived in the idiom "the tides of history," where time flows and turns in measured phases. The second element: Geist ("spirit"). The German word Geist descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *gʰeys-, "to be frightened, startled, excited," a root whose oldest sense is dynamic rather than static: a spirit is, at the root, something that starts or excites, something awe-inspiring. The Germanic evidence is clear: Gothic usgaisjan, "to terrify, startle," preserves the root's original force, and the same word-family produced the English word ghost from Old English gāst, "spirit, soul," which kept the older, supernatural sense. In the continental branch, Old High German geist, attested from the eighth and ninth centuries, already carried the Christian and philosophical senses of "spirit, mind, intellect," and in German the word developed further toward "wit" and "genius." Where English ghost became increasingly specialized and even frightful, German Geist grew more intellectual — the spirit as the mind of a person, a people, or an age. The compound: Zeitgeist. The compound Zeitgeist, "spirit of the age," was coined by Johann Gottfried Herder in 1769, in his Kritische Wälder, to express the idea that each historical epoch possesses a distinctive collective spirit that shapes its thought, art, and institutions. The concept was taken up and systematized by G. W. F. Hegel and the historical school of the nineteenth century, for whom the Zeitgeist was a determining force in history; it became central to historicism, which insisted that each age must be understood on its own terms, in the light of its own spirit. The word entered English in the mid-nineteenth century — first recorded in 1848, in the wake of Carlyle's German sources and Matthew Arnold's criticism — at first italicized as a foreign coinage, then increasingly naturalized.
  • Socio-cultural ascent. From rarefied philosophy, Zeitgeist spread in the later nineteenth century into literary criticism and cultural history, where it named the intellectual temper of an era grasped at a high level.
  • Modernist use. In twentieth-century criticism, the word lost its italics and widened into general arts and culture commentary, keeping an august, epochal register. Empson uses it exactly so: the Zeitgeist "in general," with Eliot "in particular," was calling for the revaluation of the Victorian poets.
  • Present-day dilution. In journalism, media, and marketing the word now often means little more than the prevailing mood or fashion of a moment — "capturing the zeitgeist," "in the zeitgeist" — a dilution that critics of loose usage have repeatedly noted.
  • Sound and rhythm. The compound's phonology is striking to English ears: the initial /ts/ of Zeit (a sound English otherwise has only in loanwords), the long diphthong /aɪ/, and the final /ɡaɪst/ — the word is unmistakably German, and that foreignness is part of both its authority and its exoticism.
In Empson's sentence the Zeitgeist is a force larger than any single critic: Eliot gives it voice, but it is the age itself that calls for the reconsideration of the nineteenth-century poets and the recovery of Donne, Marvell, and Dryden. The word, at once technical and atmospheric, lets the critic attribute literary change to history rather than to individual taste — precisely the philosophical weight Herder gave it when he coined it, and precisely the weight it still carries in serious intellectual prose.
