Towed is the past tense and past participle of the verb "to tow" — to draw or pull something along behind a moving power source by means of a rope, chain, cable, or vehicle. It is a word whose technological home has changed three times in its history, from the soil to the sea to the road, without the verb itself ever changing its meaning at all.

The Indo-European root. The verb "tow" descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *dewk-, "to lead, draw, pull", one of the family's great roots of motion and control. On the Latin side of the family, *dewk- produced dūcere, "to lead", with its enormous progeny (duke, duct, conduct, introduce, educate); on the Germanic side it produced verbs of drawing and tugging, among them the ancestors of English "tug" and "tie". The Germanic branch formed an iterative-causative verb *tugōną, "to draw, pull, drag", which appears in Old English as two related verbs: the strong verb tēon, "to draw, pull, go", and the weak verb togian, "to drag, pull, haul". It is togian that is the direct ancestor of "to tow": its form and meaning survive unchanged into modern times, while its sense of general dragging has been gradually specialised to towing behind a boat or vehicle.

Old English to Middle English. In Old English, togian was a blunt word of physical effort, used for dragging anything heavy. In Middle English it appears as towen or tow, still meaning "to drag, pull, haul". The crucial specialisation occurred in the maritime world, which is where the verb found its permanent home for centuries: from at least the Middle Ages, to tow meant to draw a ship, barge, or raft along by a rope attached to a moving boat — exactly the operation Tolkien describes when the raft of barrels is "towed away round the high shoulder of rock into the little bay of Lake-town". In the age of sail, towing was indispensable: sailing vessels could not move against wind and current inside harbours and rivers, so rowing boats, and then steam tugs, took them in tow. The noun "tow", "a rope used for towing", and the idiom "to take in tow" date from this nautical world; the idiom survived metaphorically — "to take someone in tow" is to take charge of and guide them, as a tug guides a ship.

The canal and railway age. The 18th- and 19th-century canal boom gave the word a vast new arena: horse-drawn barges were "towed" along canals from towpaths, and the "tow-boat" or "tug-boat" became a standard commercial vessel. The commercial world coined "towage" as a service and a charge, and the word entered law and commerce as a technical term (a right of towage, a towage contract). It was during this period that English settled the spelling "tow" (from Middle English towen) and the regular past forms "towed" and "towing".

The motor age. In the 20th century the verb transferred from water to land: motorists whose cars broke down had them "towed" to garages, and the "tow truck" (British "breakdown truck" or "recovery vehicle") became an icon of the roadside. The same verb is used for towing caravans and trailers, for towing a disabled aircraft on the ground, and for an aeroplane towing a glider into the air (aero-towing). "Towed" today is thus a perfect example of a conservative word: thousands of years after its Indo-European root meant "to pull", the English past form still means exactly "pulled along behind", whether the puller is a rowing boat, a steam tug, a canal horse, or a tow truck. Its register is completely neutral — technical, legal, literary, and colloquial at once.

Related and cognate words. Within English the verb's relatives include "tug" (a sharp pull; also the tugboat), "tie" (to fasten), and "team" (originally a set of draught animals pulling together); the Latin relatives dūcere, duke, and duct reach the same root from the other side of the family. The verb is distinct from the homograph noun "tow", "hemp or flax fibre" (Old English tōw, of uncertain origin), which appears in "tow-coloured" hair. The regularity of "towed" hides a long and rich history of pulling.

  • Proto-Germanic *tugōną "to draw, pull, drag".
  • Old English togian "to drag, pull, haul" (weak verb).
  • Middle English towen / tow: the sense specialises to nautical towing.
  • 18th–19th c. – the canal age; towage becomes a commercial and legal term.
  • 20th c. – the motor age: towing cars, trailers, aircraft, and gliders.
