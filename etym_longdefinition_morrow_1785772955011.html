Morrow is a chiefly literary, archaic, and poetic noun meaning, in its primary modern sense, "the day following a given day or night" — the next day, the day to come after the present one — and, in an older and now largely poetic or dialectal sense, "morning," the time of first light at the beginning of the day. In narrative prose it functions as the hinge between a completed evening and the action that follows at dawn: armies march on the morrow, lovers part until the morrow, vows are kept or broken on the morrow, and characters "see the company off on the morrow." The word carries a faint archaic glow that its everyday descendant tomorrow has lost through routine use; choosing morrow at once signals elevated, historical, biblical, or lyrical register and a deliberately old-fashioned cadence.

Core senses and their order. The lexical record shows two closely related senses that have competed since Old English, together with a metonymic extension.

  • Sense 1 — "the next day, the following day" (primary in modern use). This is the sense of "on the morrow," "the morrow of a battle," "until the morrow." It is a relational temporal noun: its reference is anchored to a deictic "now" or to some stated event, naming the daylight unit that comes after. It is the sense that survives fused inside the adverb tomorrow (originally "to the morrow").
  • Sense 2 — "morning, the early part of the day, dawn" (older, now chiefly poetic). In Old and Middle English morrow's ancestor meant simply "morning"; the greeting good morrow ("a good morning to you") preserves exactly this sense, and it explains why morrow still can shimmer with the idea of sunrise even when it denotes the coming day.
  • Sense 3 — "the immediate future" (metonymic extension). Less common but attested, morrow can mean "time to come, the near future" in general — "to think only of the morrow" — shading from "next day" to "what lies ahead." This is the same metonymy that lets tomorrow mean "the future" in general (e.g. "the leaders of tomorrow").

The historical bridge between senses. The two core senses are one word, not two homonyms. In Old English morgen named the morning; because the morning that follows a given night is the natural next day in narrative, the word acquired the relational sense "the day following a stated day or night" (compare German Morgen, which still means both "morning" and "tomorrow"). When the longer formation morning took over the pure "early part of the day" slot in Middle English, morrow was left specialized for "the next day" — yet the old dawn flavor never quite evaporated, so modern poetic use can exploit a deliberate ambiguity: morrow is both "the next day" and "the return of morning light."

Register, domains, and fixed phrases. Morrow belongs to poetry, historical fiction, fantasy, liturgy, proverbs, and deliberate archaism; it is rare in business or technical prose except as a stylistic flourish. Fixed phrases include on the morrow, the morrow morning (historical), the morrow after, good morrow, the older spelling to morrow (the etymon of tomorrow), and literary till the morrow.

Derivatives and relatives. The most important derivative is tomorrow — Old English tō morgen "on/to the (following) morning," fused and reduced to a single word by the 13th century — which became the ordinary, unmarked word for "the next day," leaving bare morrow to sound older and more literary. Related Germanic cognates across the family — German Morgen, Dutch morgen, Danish/Norwegian morgen, Swedish morgon, Icelandic morgunn, Gothic maurgins — preserve the morning/tomorrow duality that English split among morning, morrow, and tomorrow.

Antonyms and contrastive poles. The natural opposites are yesterday, yestereve, tonight, and this day; in the morning sense, the opposite is evening or nightfall. The emotional palette of morrow is expectant, formal, solemn, or gently nostalgic — never technical and never slang.
