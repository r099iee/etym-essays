- [Late 14th–15th c. Middle English] — Borrowed from Middle Dutch/Middle Low German *loteren* ("to wag, totter, dawdle"), describing lazy tramps, vagrants, and beggars who shirked agrarian work — [perception: despised by feudal landowners and burghers as sinful sloth, deceitful vagrancy, and social parasitism]
- [16th–17th c. Tudor & Stuart Vagrancy Laws] — Codified into draconian English Poor Laws against "sturdy rogues and vagabonds" who spent their days *loitering* rather than laboring; punishable by whipping, stocks, and forced labor — [perception: treated as a criminal precursor to theft, begging, and public disorder]
- [Late 18th–19th c. Romantic & Pastoral Era] — Reclaimed by Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Clare) as a virtuous, meditative, and sensory embrace of nature (*"a pleasant loitering journey"*), breaking free from clock-time and urban factories — [perception: elevated in literature as an artistic, contemplative refusal of mechanised capitalist hurry]
- [Late 19th–20th c. Industrialization & Jim Crow/Urban Ordinances] — Urban police codes introduced "No Loitering" statutes, frequently weaponized by authorities against union strikers, unemployed laborers, youths, and racial minorities congregating on sidewalks — [perception: viewed by police as suspicious public disorder, and by civil rights advocates as a discriminatory tool of social control]
- [21st c. Military Technology & Flânerie] — Dual modern trajectory: in defense jargon, denotes autonomous "loitering munitions" (suicide drones) hovering over battlefields; in cultural theory, celebrated as radical walking, slow living, and resisting digital hyper-productivity — [perception: split between high-tech lethal surveillance and counter-cultural psychological rebellion]
