Missionary is a Counter-Reformation coinage built on Latin missio (mission-), noun of action from mittere, miss- "to send, dispatch, release." Mittere traces to PIE *smit- / *mit-? Most etymologists (de Vaan, Watkins) connect it to PIE *smeit- "to throw, send," seen in English smite in its old sense to strike/throw. Classical missio meant a sending, dismissal (military discharge: missio honesta), not religion. The religious sense — divine sending to convert — develops in Late and Church Latin under the influence of biblical mittere apostolos and the Jesuit concept of missiones exterae from the 1540s. Ignatius of Loyola and Francis Xavier's Asian missions (1542 onward) institutionalize missio as overseas evangelism.

French mission ( "charge, religious expedition" ) is attested by the 1620s for Jesuit missions in Canada (Nouvelle-France) and the Levant. English borrows mission by the 1590s (Shakespeare: "perform my mission"?), and missionary appears by 1642-1659 in Presbyterian and Jesuit reports: "missionary priests," "missionary churches." The OED's first clear missionary (noun) is 1659 in Heylin's Cosmographie for Jesuit "missionaries" in America. The adjective missionary (missionary society, missionary spirit) follows by the 1680s. The -ary suffix is Latin -arius (one connected with), as in legionary, visionary.

  • Jesuit invention (1540-1650): Missio shifts from Roman discharge to Catholic evangelical dispatch. Xavier in Goa/Japan, Matteo Ricci in China, Brébeuf in New France write home missio litterae. Protestant observers borrow the Catholic term polemically.
  • Protestant adoption (1650-1800): Moravians, SPG (Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, 1701), and later Baptists and Methodists embrace missionary for their own envoys to Native Americans, Africans, Pacific Islanders. The quoted passage — "reports a missionary" surprised by Indigenous gentleness — typifies 18th-19th-century missionary ethnography that both admired and sought to convert.
  • Great Century of Missions (1800-1914): Livingstone, Carey, Hudson Taylor; missionary becomes a heroic Victorian vocation, with missionary societies, hymns ("From Greenland's Icy Mountains"), magazines, museums. Meaning expands to wife/child of missionary (missionary kid), missionary station, missionary position (the last a 20th-century facetious extension).
  • Secularization (1900-present): Missionary broadens to any zealous advocate: missionary for clean energy, missionary zeal for CrossFit. Simultaneously post-colonial critique problematizes missionary as agent of cultural imperialism; many churches rebrand as "mission workers," "partners in mission." Adjectival missionary position (sexual) from Kinsey-era anthropology (incorrectly attributed to missionaries teaching it) adds an ironic slang layer.

Phonetically missionary shows the Latin -t- → -ss- assimilation (mittere → missus → missio) plus English stress and rhotic variation: RP /ˈmɪʃənri/ (three syllables, often /ˈmɪʃənəri/ with epenthetic schwa), General American /ˈmɪʃəˌnɛri/. Orthographically double-s preserves Latin miss-. No serious etymological debate: mittere → missio → mission → missionary is unanimous; only the deeper PIE root (*smeit- vs *mit-) is uncertain, noted cautiously in de Vaan. The word's history is thus less about sound change than about religious institutional innovation giving an old Roman administrative noun a global evangelical career, then a secular metaphorical afterlife.

Literary attestations: Jesuit Relations (1632-1673) from New France, the source of many "surprising gentleness" reports; David Brainerd's diary among the Delaware (1740s); Livingstone's Missionary Travels (1857); Conrad's Heart of Darkness ironically inverts missionary idealism; Barbara Kingsolver's The Poisonwood Bible (1998) retells missionary tragedy from family perspective.
