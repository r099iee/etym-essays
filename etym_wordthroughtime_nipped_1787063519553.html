- Middle English (late 14th c.) - [Sharp Pinching & Snapping Bite] - Emerged in Northern Middle English (*nippen*, likely of Germanic/Low German origin) meaning to pinch with fingers, bite with teeth, or catch in pincers.
- Late Medieval to Early Modern (15th–16th c.) - [Blight of Frost & Cold Weather] - Broadened to describe biting winter cold or sharp frost damaging tender crops, giving rise to the horticultural phrase *'nip in the bud.'*
- 16th–17th Century (Renaissance & Metaphorical Checking) - [Suppression & Biting Sarcasm] - Expanded into social and psychological domains, describing biting satirical remarks (*a nipping wit*) or cutting short dangerous ambitions before they mature.
- 19th–20th Century (Victorian & British Idiom) - [Quick Movement & Rapid Errands] - In British and Commonwealth colloquial English, developed the dynamic sense of moving briskly, popping into a shop, or making a rapid brief journey (*'he nipped down the pub'*).
- 21st Century (Contemporary) - [Arachnid/Animal Biting, Horticultural Idiom & Brisk Action] - Retained across narrative prose for sharp animal/insect bites, the universal idiom *'nipped in the bud,'* and British casual parlance for swift errands.
