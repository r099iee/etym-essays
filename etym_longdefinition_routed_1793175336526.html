Routed is the past tense and past participle of rout in its military sense: to defeat utterly, to break an enemy formation so completely that ordered battle collapses into disorderly flight. A routed host is not merely beaten but unmade — ranks dissolved, standards abandoned, soldiers become fugitives — and the word carries the noise of that unmaking: the shouting pursuit, the thrown-down weapons, the road jammed with the fleeing.

In the source sentence — Who hath routed this great host — the sense is exactly this battlefield one, lifted in the archaic diction of the wonder-tale. Some power has shattered an entire army, and the question is asked in wonder and dread, as heroes ask it over the plain of the slain. The perfect tense hath routed gives the deed a completed, almost miraculous finality: the host was great, and now it is nothing.

Core meaning in detail: Rout as a verb of defeat is inseparable from its noun: a rout is both the act of breaking and the broken crowd itself, the fleeing mass. To rout is therefore to produce a rout — to convert an army into a mob in a single stroke, whether by shock charge, panic rumour, or, as in fairy tales, by sorcery or single-handed heroism. Military writers distinguish it from mere defeat: a defeated army retreats, a routed army disintegrates, and pursuit after a rout is traditionally merciless because the routed cannot turn and fight.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Defeated utterly, put to disorderly flight (primary sense, the tale's own): Napoleon's army was routed at Waterloo's close — broken beyond rallying. The bridge runs from Old French route, a broken band: the enemy is broken the way a stick is broken, snapped from formation into splinters.
  • Driven out, expelled decisively (extended sense): as in the scandal routed him from office, ousted completely, sent fleeing from position. The bridge is metaphorical pursuit: disgrace does what cavalry does, and the victim flees the post as soldiers flee the field.
  • Hollowed or scooped out, as with a tool (the other rout, woodworking): a carpenter routs a groove with a router, cutting a channel in timber. The bridge is a separate etymology entirely — this rout comes from root, to dig and grub — and the two verbs are homonyms that happen to share a spelling, distinguished only by context.
  • Snoring loudly (archaic Scots and northern dialect): to rout in one's sleep is to snore, the sleeper roaring like a Rout of men. The bridge is onomatopoeic: the noise of the broken host becomes the noise of the sleeper, both being loud, rude sounds.
  • Assembled in a disorderly crowd (archaic noun-turned-verb): in older English a rout could be any tumultuous company, and to rout meant to gather riotously. The bridge is the crowd itself: the same word names the mob standing and the mob fleeing.

Register and connotation: routed is martial and emphatic — sports pages, history books, and fairy tales, rarely the office. It congratulates the victor and humiliates the vanquished in a single syllable: to say a team was routed is to say the contest was no contest. Unlike the neutral defeated, it always implies collapse, chaos, and flight, and it borrows a shiver of the archaic even in modern use, as if every rout echoed with hooves.
