The biography of invidious begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *weid-, "to see." Across the Indo-European family this root performs a famous semantic trick: because to know is to have seen, the perfect tense of "see" became the verb "know" — Greek oîda, Sanskrit véda, Gothic wait, and Old English wāt all mean "I know" while preserving the fossil "I have seen." The root thus branches in two directions, physical sight and intellectual knowledge. In Greek it yields ideîn, "to see," and the philosophically charged eîdos, the Platonic "Form" that underlies English idea; in Indo-Iranian it yields Vedic véda, the sacred knowledge of the Rigveda, inherited in Hindi as ved and in Odia as beda; in Slavic it yields Old Church Slavonic viděti and Russian videt′, "to see"; in Celtic Old Irish fis, "knowledge"; in Germanic Gothic witan and Old English witan, whence English wit, wise, and wisdom. Sight and knowledge, insight and witness, are woven together in the root from the beginning.

The Latin innovation: the poisoned gaze

The decisive innovation happens in Latin, where vidēre, "to see," is compounded with the preposition in-, "upon": invīdēre literally means "to look upon," but the gaze is poisoned — to look upon another's good with an evil eye, to envy. The noun invidia, "envy, grudge, ill-will," was personified in Roman literature: Invidia lurks at the door of Ovid's Metamorphoses, pale and sickly, her tongue eating itself, the emblem of the resentful look. From invidia the adjective invidiōsus was formed with the "full of" suffix -ōsus, and it carried a built-in double face: "envious" toward the subject who feels, "hateful, exciting hatred" toward the object that provokes. This ambiguity is the key to the whole later history, because it is exactly what eventually splits the family into two English words.

The two English arrivals

  • Envy (late 13th c.): Latin invidia passed through Old French envie into English as envy around 1275, and envious arrived about 1300 through Old French envieus. These are the feeling-words: they describe the person who resents another's good. The French route preserved the subjective, emotional face of the Latin family.
  • Invidious (c. 1600): the adjective was borrowed directly from Latin invidiōsus in the early modern period, with no French intermediary. From the first it took the objective, provoking face of the Latin word: not the person who envies, but the act, remark, or distinction that provokes envy. The early modern learned loan kept a technical, almost legalistic flavour, the flavour of a word taken straight from the source rather than worn smooth in the vernacular.

Semantic development in English

  • 17th century — what stirs envy: the earliest English sense is "such as to excite ill-will or resentment against a person; envious in its effect." The word attaches to the provoking act: an invidious comparison, an invidious office, an invidious preference. Here the "hateful, odious" sense also appears — an invidious action is one that makes enemies.
  • 18th century — unfair discrimination: the word shifts toward the quality of a judgment that discriminates unfairly between persons — the sting is in the inequality, not in the object's intrinsic badness. The phrase "invidious distinction" enters the language, and with it the word becomes a term of moral and political criticism: to draw an invidious distinction is to separate persons on grounds felt to be unjust.
  • 19th century — critical polemic: Ruskin's Preface shows the word in full literary operation. He anticipates that his singling out of Turner will "sound invidious" — woundingly partial, offensively preferential — and he deflects the charge by insisting that any such appearance flows not from the design ("the tenour of the work") but from the work's incompleteness. The tone is wary, forensic, self-aware: the critic knows exactly how his words will be felt.
  • 20th–21st century — legal and political: "invidious comparison" and "invidious distinction" remain alive in law and political speech, now almost always naming judgments that rank or single out people unfairly. In American constitutional law the phrase "invidious discrimination" denotes classifications that impose unequal burdens without a reasonable basis. The resentment the word stirs is the core of its meaning: even in the courtroom it keeps the ancient image of the resentful gaze.

The eye and the look

What makes invidious more than a synonym of unfair or odious is the eye. The word is built on sight, and its whole career keeps the visual frame: envy is a way of looking, and an invidious act is one that draws the envious look upon itself. Ruskin's sentence is therefore perfectly chosen for a critic — a man whose profession is seeing and judging — to acknowledge that his judgments may be seen as wounding. The word's etymology, its history, and its modern legal uses all converge on the same point: invidious things are those that, once looked upon, rankle.
