Pines is the plural of a tree-name whose origin is, surprisingly, not about leaves or height but about sap. The story of the word begins in the fat, sticky resin that streams from the tree's bark, passes through Latin and the Mediterranean, enters English with the Anglo-Saxons, and ends on a roaring mountainside in a dwarves' lament — while a homonym, the verb “to pine,” runs alongside on a completely separate track of pain and longing. To tell the history of pines honestly is to tell both stories and keep them apart. 1. A root of fatness and sap: PIE *peyH- The tree-name descends from Latin pīnus, and behind the Latin adjective stands a reconstructed Indo-European root whose sense is “to be fat, be swollen, be rich in juice”: *peyH- / *pey-. The semantic logic is immediately visible to anyone who has ever broken a pine twig: the tree is drenched in resin, the sticky, oily sap that fills its bark, its cones, and its wood. A pine is, at root, “the fat tree,” “the resinous tree” — named for what it oozes. The same root family produced Latin pinguis, “fat,” and the Greek pīya, “rain-fed pasture, rich nourishment,” and it is the ancient source of the pitch, tar, and turpentine that flow from the tree to this day. Some specialists, it must be said, prefer a Mediterranean or substrate origin for pīnus, and the deeper Indo-European link is treated with caution in the handbooks; but the sap-image — the pine as the tree rich in resin — is the oldest, surest meaning the name carries. 2. Latin pīnus and the Mediterranean pine In classical Latin, pīnus names the pine, the stone-pine of the Italian landscape, the resinous tree of the hills. The Romans knew the tree intimately: its pitch sealed their ships, its tar coated their rope and tackle, its wood made masts and beams, and its cones and seeds were food. The pine was sacred to Cybele and associated with the god Attis; it stands in Roman poetry as the tree of the shore and the hillside, the fixed landmark of the lītus, the shore. Virgil's pines line the sea-coast; Pliny records the uses of its resin in detail. The word pīnus is securely attested in classical Latin from the second century BCE, and from Latin it passed into the Romance languages — Italian pino, French pin, Spanish pino — and, by a separate early borrowing, into the Germanic speech of the Anglo-Saxons. 3. Old English pīn: the Saxon pine The Anglo-Saxons took the Latin word directly, as pīn, recorded in the compound pīntrēow, “pine-tree,” and pīnbēam. This is an early Latin loan into English — from the same era as strēt (street), cēap (cheap), and other continental vocabulary that came with Roman influence — and it shows how old the tree's presence was in the English mental landscape, even though much of early Britain was oak, beech, and birch country. The word settled quickly: Middle English pine / pyne names the tree, its timber, and its wood, and by late Middle English the pine had acquired its permanent literary colours — northern, evergreen, wind-sounding, resin-scented, a tree of the heights and the cold. Chaucer and the medieval poets use the pine as the tall, dark tree of the forest and the mountain. 4. The homonym that must not be confused: to pine Running alongside the tree-name, and sharing its spelling exactly, is the verb to pine — to waste away with longing, to grieve and languish. This verb has a different birth. Old English pīnian meant “to torment, afflict, cause pain,” built on the noun pīn, “pain, punishment, torment,” which the Anglo-Saxons borrowed from Latin poena, “punishment, penalty, pain” (the source of English penalty, pain, and penance). Over the centuries the meaning of pīnian softened and internalized: from “to be tormented” it came to mean “to suffer or languish,” and finally “to waste away with longing, to yearn” — “she pined for home.” The two words are unrelated; their identity is pure coincidence of spelling. Careful etymology — and careful reading — keeps the tree and the longing apart: the roaring pines of the heights are the resinous tree of *peyH-, not the aching verb of poena. 5. The pine in English literature and landscape From the early modern period onward, the pine carried a double reputation. Practically, it was the timber tree par excellence — the masts and spars of the Royal Navy, the matchsticks, boxes, and furniture of every carpenter's yard, the pulp of the paper-mills — and economically one of the most important trees in the world. Imaginatively, it was the tree of northern wilderness and the sublime: Wordsworth's and Coleridge's pines, the pines of the Lake District fells, the dark, roaring pines of the Scottish Highlands and of the German Romantic forest. The literary pine is always wind-haunted, resin-scented, and slightly ominous — the tree of the heights where the weather speaks. In this tradition stands Tolkien's stanza: the pines are not a backdrop but a sound — “the pines were roaring on the height” — a whole forest given voice, wind and darkness made choral, exactly the note the Romantic and ballad tradition taught the word to strike. 6. The plural in the song In the dwarves' lament, the plural PINES does what the word has always done best: it makes a landscape a presence. Not one pine but the pines — a ridgeline of them, roaring in the storm while the winds moan below — become the acoustic image of approaching doom. The tree's ancient identity as “the resinous tree,” fat with sap and pitch, adds its own under-odour of tar, smoke, and wilderness to the ballad gloom; and the homonym-verb, “to pine,” hovers at the edge, deepening the melancholy with its separate sense of longing. The card's form is the plural tree-word, and the whole weight of the forest's history — sap, timber, storm, and song — stands behind the simple noun.
