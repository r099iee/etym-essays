Ailing is the present participle of the verb ail used as an attributive adjective, and in modern English the adjective is far commoner than the verb. It means “suffering from illness or poor health” and, by extension, “in a weak or declining condition”. In the source, “his ailing throat and lungs” describes a chronic, troublesome weakness — the phrase is not a diagnosis but a sympathetic summary of recurrent ill health.

Morphologically it is ail + -ing, and the form does double duty: it is a true participle in progressive constructions (“he was ailing for weeks”) and a lexicalised adjective in attributive ones (“an ailing parent”). It has no comparative, is rarely intensified by “very”, and resists nominalisation: “the ailing” is unusual and usually jocular.

Senses

  • Of a person: chronically unwell — in poor health, infirm, often with the implication of age or a long-running illness rather than a sudden attack. “His ailing mother”, “the ailing patient”, “she has been ailing since the winter”.
  • Of a body part: weak or diseased — “ailing throat”, “ailing lungs”, “ailing heart”. This is the source's use; it now sounds slightly old-fashioned, since medical writing prefers “diseased”, “weak” or the name of the condition.
  • Of an enterprise or institution: weak, unprofitable, struggling — “ailing economy”, “ailing industry”, “ailing bank”, “ailing airline”, “ailing football club”. This is the dominant modern use and is almost always attributive.
  • Of a system or abstract entity: declining, ineffective — “ailing democracy”, “ailing health service”, “ailing peace process”. The metaphor treats the institution as a patient.
  • Verb, transitive, archaic and literary — “to trouble, afflict”: “what ails you?” is the one idiom in general use, and it is often revived in journalism (“what ails the economy?”).
  • Verb, intransitive, regional — “to be ill”: “he has been ailing this past month”. This survives chiefly in Scottish, Irish and older rural English usage.
  • Noun, archaic — “ail” meaning a trouble or illness, surviving in the plural “ails” (“the aches and ails of old age”) and in the derived noun “ailment”.

How the senses are related

The verb's oldest sense is transitive: a thing or a person could ail someone, that is, trouble or afflict him. From “to be troubled” came the intransitive “to be ill”, and from the participle came the adjective. The shift is the familiar one from affliction to sufferer, visible also in “suffer” and “pain”: the word moves from what is done to a person to the state the person is in. The modern dominance of the adjective reflects the retreat of the verb into the set phrase “what ails you?” and into regional speech; the adjective took over the everyday work.

Register and connotation

  • Softer and more sympathetic than “sick”, more formal than “poorly”, less final than “dying”. It suggests chronic weakness rather than acute crisis.
  • Of a person it can sound old-fashioned, euphemistic or faintly patronising; “my ailing father” is affectionate but distances the speaker from the illness.
  • Of an institution it is a journalistic cliché, useful because it implies decline without naming a cause or an agent.
  • It carries no technical medical status: a doctor would not write “ailing” in a case note.

Typical collocations

  • ailing + relative: mother, father, wife, husband, parent, patient, relative
  • ailing + organ: throat, lungs, heart, health, body
  • ailing + institution: economy, industry, bank, airline, club, newspaper, currency, sector
  • ailing + abstract: democracy, system, business, service, process
  • adverbs: chronically, seriously, gravely, desperately, financially ailing

Grammar and usage notes

  • Attributive position is normal; predicative “the economy is ailing” is possible but rarer and slightly literary.
  • It is not used of things that are merely broken: an “ailing car” sounds humorous or personifying.
  • Avoid “very ailing”; write “seriously ailing”, “gravely ailing” or “chronically ailing”.
  • In “what ails him?” the verb is transitive and the trouble is the subject of the answer: “what ails him is old age”.
  • Spelling: one l, one n; “ailling” is a common error.
  • The participle is not normally passive: “the ailed patient” does not exist, because the intransitive sense has absorbed the older transitive one.

Why the word matters here

Cicero's throat and lungs were the stated reason for the Eastern tour, and the source treats his health as the practical motive behind the journey that reshaped his prose. “Ailing” is exactly the right word for a condition serious enough to justify a long absence but too vague to be a diagnosis; it keeps the focus on the strain rather than on a disease.
