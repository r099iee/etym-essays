- Old English (c. 9th–11th Century) - Attested as *hwanon* / *hwanone*, functioning as the directional interrogative and relative adverb meaning 'from where / from what source'.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th Century) - Developed an adverbial genitive suffix *-es* (forming *whannes*, *whennes*), mirroring parallel directional formations like *hennes* (hence) and *thannes* (thence).
- Early Modern English (16th–18th Century) - Stabilized as *whence* in the Authorized King James Bible, Shakespeare, and Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*, flourishing as a staple of elevated literary, philosophical, and legal prose. The tautological phrase 'from whence' became common in popular usage despite strict grammatical redundancy.
- 19th–21st Century - Gradually receded from everyday spoken English into an elevated, archaic, formal, or poetic register, highly prized in high-style literature, historical drama, and philosophical discourse for its rhythmic elegance and concise expression of origin.
