pretension — a claim, or the assertion of a claim, to something, most often a status, quality, or right that the claimant does not in fact possess; by extension, the outward showiness of one who makes such a claim. In Ruskin's sentence — "The reputation of the great artist is established on too legitimate grounds to be affected by the ignorant sarcasms of pretension" — the word is shorthand for the would-be critics of Turner, men who stake out claims to artistic knowledge and authority that their own ignorance exposes as hollow. The crucial nuance is that pretension is not merely error but assertion beyond warrant: the word pictures someone stretching a claim out in front of himself, a false front held up for inspection. Ruskin opposes pretension to reputation "established on too legitimate grounds": the artist's fame is a settled, earned thing, while the critic's pretension is an empty gesture that cannot touch it. The tone is dry, scornful, and triumphant — the pretentious are dismissed without being refuted. Polysemy in historical order. The word entered English in the mid-fifteenth century as a neutral legal and rhetorical term, and its senses have layered up since:
  • Sense 1 (mid-15th c., now archaic/technical): "an assertion, allegation, objection, or signification" — the innocent act of putting a claim forward, in law and formal discourse, with no pejorative force.
  • Sense 2 (c. 1600, the core modern sense): "an unfounded or excessive claim; the assertion of a right, quality, or standing not actually possessed" — always pejorative, and the sense that governs Ruskin's sentence.
  • Sense 3 (17th–18th c.): "an aspiration or ambition," usually softened in the plural — "literary pretensions," "pretensions to gentility," "pretensions to the throne."
  • Sense 4 (law and politics): a title or claim to an estate, office, or crown; the royal "Pretenders" of 1688–1745 were claimants to the English and Scottish thrones.
  • Sense 5 (aesthetic, from the 19th c.): showy self-importance, the quality of being pretentious; "no pretension" = unassuming simplicity, the opposite of display.
  • Obsolete and rare senses: "a pretext," "a false appearance," and, in philosophy, the act of putting forward a proposition for examination.
The semantic bridge. The whole family grows out of Latin praetendere, "to stretch (something) in front," used first of a bodily act — a soldier stretches his shield before his body, a man holds a cloth before a door to conceal what lies behind it — and then extended to speech: to hold up an excuse or screen before one's real purpose, i.e. "to allege, to plead as a pretext." The past participle praetēnsus gave Medieval Latin praetensio, "a putting forward," so that a claim is conceived as something literally stretched out in front of the claimant for inspection. Modern English still feels the image in the colloquial "stretch the truth" and "overreach." This is why pretension is distinct from mere assertion: it always carries the picture of a claim pulled out beyond its true reach. Boundaries against near-synonyms.
  • Claim is neutral and may be perfectly legitimate (a claim to one's inheritance); pretension is always a claim regarded as unearned.
  • Presumption means overstepping, taking liberties beyond one's place; pretension is the specific assertion of a standing one does not have.
  • Affectation is mannered behaviour adopted to impress — the visible style of the pretentious person — whereas pretension is the underlying assertion of merit, of which affectation is the outward garb.
  • Pretentiousness (from the adjective pretentious, 1852) is the quality of being showily self-important; pretension names the claim itself, often in the plural.
  • Arrogance and conceit inflate one's own worth; pretension reaches further, claiming a social or intellectual position one has not earned.
  • Ostentation is showy display of wealth or importance; it displays what one has, while pretension claims what one lacks.
  • Airs is the informal plural noun for affected superiority — "putting on airs" — the spoken-language equivalent of pretension.
Grammar and register. Pretension is a countable abstract noun formed from the verb pretend (via Latin) with the noun suffix -ion (process or state). It is constructed with to ("pretension to genius"), of, or absolutely. The plural pretensions softens the word toward mere ambitions ("social pretensions"), while the singular keeps an accusatory edge; in Ruskin the singular functions as a collective, naming the whole class of false claimants to artistic authority in one dry gesture. The adjective pretentious (1852) and adverbs pretentiously, pretensionless (rare) are later coinages. The parallel noun pretense (British pretence, early 15th c. via Anglo-French) means the act of claiming or feigning in a particular instance — "under false pretenses" — and must not be conflated with pretension: pretense is the feigned act, pretension the standing claim of worth. Modern use. In contemporary English pretension is most alive in (1) criticism of art and culture, (2) social observation ("the pretensions of the newly rich"), (3) political discourse ("pretensions to greatness"), and (4) literary prose, where it retains its etymological image of the stretched claim. It is frequently paired with hollow, ignorant, absurd, or mere. The word remains double-edged: in the singular it accuses, in the plural it merely records ambition, and a "man without pretensions" is quietly praised as unassuming.
