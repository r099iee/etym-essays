suffusion:
- Medical description (16th c.) - borrowed from Latin suffusio for blood suffusing the face and humours suffusing the eye; a strictly physiological word for what rises from beneath the skin
- Extension to colour and light (17th-18th c.) - spreads from the body to any spreading of light or colour from within a surface, keeping the sense of an internal, upward pour
- Literary and painterly use (19th-20th c.) - favoured by poets and prose stylists for a blush of colour that saturates rather than coats, gaining a soft, organic warmth
- Contemporary - rare in everyday speech, it lives in descriptive and literary registers, always retaining the idea of colour poured up from beneath

scarlet:
- A precious cloth (13th c.) - the word names a costly red-dyed fabric reserved for nobles and prelates, not yet a colour
- From cloth to hue (14th c.) - the prized kermes-dyed red displaces the fabric sense, and the colour keeps the cloth's air of rank and ceremony
- Chromatic idioms (19th c. onward) - scarlet fever, scarlet letter, scarlet woman; the word remains the brightest, most commanding of reds
