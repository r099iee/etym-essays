Larders (plural of larder) are cool rooms, large cupboards, or dedicated stores where household food — especially meat, preserves, butter, cheese, cured provisions, and other perishables — is kept ready for use. The word's name remembers its oldest contents: the lard, the cured pig fat that was the medieval kitchen's universal preservative, cooking medium, and store of winter protein. Before the age of refrigeration, the larder was characteristically cool and well-ventilated (often north-facing or sheltered), with stone or slate shelves, mesh- or gauze-covered windows to keep out flies, and hooks for hanging game and bacon; it was the practical heart of domestic food security, the room where a household's surplus was converted into comfortable continuity. The plural larders in Tolkien's line is not mere inventory: it names the sealed heart of Bag-End comfort, the private stock that defines a well-ordered hobbit life.

In Bilbo's flustered monologue, Gandalf's offhand command for cold chicken and pickles, and Bilbo's shock that the wizard seems to know those larders as well as he does, mark an intrusion into the most intimate layer of domestic sovereignty. The hobbit is not merely asked to feed guests; he is catalogued as a storehouse, a wizard reading his pantry like an open ledger — plenty made vulnerable, hospitality turned inside out by guests who already map the cupboards before the host has finished his sentence. The word's etymological core — fat, preservation, salted plenty — underscores how deeply the violation strikes: the very stores that guarantee a respectable household's winter are being inventoried and consumed by uninvited strangers.

Polysemy must be surveyed carefully, because larder has a precise historical meaning, a blurred modern one, and a set of figurative extensions. First, the historical sense: the cool meat- and fat-store of a medieval or early modern household, whose name descends from the storage of cured pig fat (“lard”). Second, the generalised modern sense: any large food cupboard or walk-in pantry, cool or not, used for everyday provisions — in modern kitchen design, a “larder cupboard” is simply a tall, well-organised food store, and the word is often revived for its cosy, traditional flavour. Third, the figurative sense of reserves: nature's larder, the ocean's larder, a larder of knowledge — any abundant source of supply, likened to a well-stocked storehouse. Fourth, the occupational sense fossilised in the surname Larder and in the obsolete lardiner / larderer: the household officer who kept the keys to the meat-store, a rank of real domestic power in great medieval and Tudor households. Fifth, the collocational and idiomatic uses: a well-stocked larder, a bare larder, to raid the larder, a larder-diet — all built on the image of the store as the measure of a household's comfort.

The word must be set against its domestic neighbours, whose old specialisations survive in their histories. A pantry (from Old French paneterie, Latin panis ‘bread’) holds bread and dry goods and takes its name from the loaf, not the pig. A cellar is subterranean, for long-term storage of wine, casks, and root vegetables, its name from Latin cellarium, a storeroom. A buttery originally held butts of ale (from Latin buttis ‘cask’), not butter. A spence or stillroom in a great house kept preserves, spices, and cordials; a scullery was for washing. Modern usage has blurred these boundaries — a larder may now be any walk-in food cupboard — but the old divisions are fossilised in the words themselves: lard, loaf, cask, and cellar each stamped its origin on the room that stored it.

Register, collocation, and usage. The word belongs to cookery books, household manuals, estate inventories, and novels of domestic life rather than to formal or technical prose. It collocates with stock, restock, raid, bare, empty, well-stocked. In Tolkien's line the plural is precise: hobbit culture is defined by larder and pantry in the plural, by surplus converted into comfort, and it is precisely that architecture of provision that the dwarves' arrival begins, one command at a time, to dismantle.

  • Generalised modern sense: any large food cupboard, walk-in pantry, or cool store for provisions.
  • Figurative sense: nature's larder, the ocean's larder — any abundant source of supply.
  • Occupational sense: lardiner / larderer, the keeper of the household meat-store; surname Larder.
