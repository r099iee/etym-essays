Manoeuvres is the British plural of manoeuvre, a word that travels from the human hand to the battlefield to the drawing-room intrigue, and its spelling with oe preserves its French passport. To understand its depth we must start with two separate Proto-Indo-European roots that only meet in Latin: *man- meaning hand, source of Latin manus, and *h₂op- meaning to work, to produce, source of Latin opus, opera, and operari. In Classical Latin they had not yet fused into one word; Romans said manu operare, to work by hand, an ordinary agricultural and artisanal phrase for tilling, building, or handling tools.

In Late Latin and early Romance this phrase coalesces. Old French manovre in the thirteenth century still means hand-labour, day-work in the fields, or the gang doing it — a humble, peasant sense. By Middle French the spelling manœuvre with the ligature stabilises, and the meaning begins its great upward social climb under the pressure of the early modern military revolution. Vauban, Turenne, and the drill-masters of the seventeenth century needed a term for the ordered handling of troops, ships, and artillery as if they were tools in the hand of the commander. Manoeuvre becomes the art of moving bodies in space: evolutions on the parade ground, sailing formations, siege approaches. English borrows it directly in the 1750s, first as a military technicality spelled manoeuvre after French, with the verb to manoeuvre and the plural manoeuvres / maneuvers.

Phonetic adaptation into English involved anglicisation without full naturalisation, which is why the British spelling keeps oe and the American spelling simplifies to eu. Received Pronunciation renders it /məˈnuːvəz/, with stress on the second syllable, reduced first vowel, and voiced /v/ plus plural /z/, exactly mirroring French manœuvre /manœvʁ/ but with English stress and without nasal vowels. The singular/plural contrast manoeuvre / manoeuvres is purely English morphology added to a French base.

Semantic drift from 1750 to today follows a classic path from concrete technique to abstract cunning:
  • Military tactical movements: troops performing autumn manoeuvres on Salisbury Plain; naval manoeuvres in the Channel. Neutral, professional, admiring of skill.
  • Nautical and vehicular handling: to manoeuvre a ship, a car, a pram through a tight gap. Skill with space, still neutral.
  • Political, legal, and romantic scheming: his low manoeuvres, parliamentary manoeuvres, matrimonial manoeuvres. Here the word acquires moral colour — cleverness shading into trickery, exactly the Wodehouse use where low manoeuvres means shabby, underhand plots rendered null and void.
  • Medical and physical manoeuvres: the Heimlich manoeuvre, obstetric manoeuvres. A return to the hand: skilled hand-work on the body.
The pejorative branch is crucial for reading Wodehouse. By the nineteenth century, journalists and novelists routinely used manoeuvres for backstairs intrigue, and the adjective low makes the judgment explicit: not brilliant strategy but mean, selfish scheming. Berties low manoeuvres being rendered null and void is comic military metaphor applied to clubland plotting.

Culturally, the word carries French prestige even when used for disreputable behaviour, which creates irony. To call something a manoeuvre flatters it with a veneer of Napoleonic science while accusing it of shiftiness. Twentieth-century usage extends it to business manoeuvres, tax manoeuvres, and diplomatic manoeuvres, always with that double edge of admiration for dexterity and suspicion of motive. The plural manoeuvres thus encodes three thousand years of history: a hand that works, a general who moves regiments like fingers, and a bounder whose little plots come to nothing.
