Four root words — four independent chains: 1. GLOOM — native Germanic formation, no secure PIE ancestry (origin debated): - Middle English gloumen (also glommen), verb "to look sullen, to frown, to stare moodily" (attested c. 1300–1400). SOURCE UNKNOWN: handbooks suggest a Scandinavian dialectal glome "to stare thoughtfully, to look blank" (Norwegian) or a Middle Low German glūmen "to look morose"; no single source is securely established. - English gloom, noun "darkness, dimness" (18th c.) — a back-formation from gloomy (16th c., "sullen, morose"); the darkness-sense is secondary, transferred from the mood-word. NOTE ON DEBATE: the popular link to the PIE shine-root *ghel- (whence glow, glimmer, gold) is REJECTED in the handbooks (OED, Etymonline) — no morphological path leads from *ghel- to gloumen, and *ghel- is a family of positive brightness. The final node is GLOOM. 2. EQUIVOCAL — a Latin compound of two separate roots: - Latin aequus, "level, even, flat; equal, fair, just" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE). DEEPER PRE-ITALIC ORIGIN UNKNOWN: de Vaan records no secure PIE etymology; an ancient *aikʷo- is posited but its ultimate source is lost — this is stated rather than guessed. - Latin vocāre, "to call, to summon, to name" (attested 2nd c. BCE) < PIE *wekʷ-, "to speak, to utter" (reconstructed; cognates: Sanskrit vāk "voice, speech", Greek épos "word", Latin vox "voice"). - Latin aequivocus, "of equal voice; ambiguous" (classical/medieval Latin) — formed as aequus + vocāre to translate the Greek homōnymos ("bearing the same name"); the Latin compound gave rise to the modern English word. - English equivocal, "of uncertain signification; capable of two interpretations" (17th c., 1600s), borrowed via late Latin aequivocus; the noun equivocation and verb equivocate follow. Final node: EQUIVOCAL. 3. BAFFLED — origin obscure, route via Scottish/French debated: - Probable source: Old French bafouer (also baffe, "a blow, slap"), an imitative word of contempt, "to mock, to abuse, to subject to ridicule" (13th–16th c.); some handbooks point instead to Scottish baff "to treat with public disgrace" (from bauchle / French bafouer). NOTE ON DEBATE: the OED records the early sense "to disgrace publicly, to subject to infamy or mockery" (16th c., c. 1540s) and leaves the continental source uncertain between French bafouer and a Scots usage. - English baffle, verb: sense 1 "to disgrace, to subject to ridicule" (16th c.); sense 2 "to foil, frustrate, defeat a plan or pursuit" (17th–18th c.); sense 3 "to bewilder, confound the understanding" (19th c.). - English baffled, past participle / participial adjective "confused, frustrated, unable to understand" — the form on this card. Final node: BAFFLED. 4. INCONGRUOUS — Latin compound, both halves securely attested: - Latin congruere, "to come together, to meet, to agree, to correspond" (attested 1st c. BCE), formed as con- "together" + ruere "to rush, to fall, to hasten" (PIE *er- / *h₃er-, "to move, to stir"; cognate with English run via Germanic *rinnan-). The image: things "run together" into agreement. - Latin congruus, "fitting, agreeing, suitable" (attested 1st c. BCE–CE), the adjective built on congruere. - Latin incongruus, "not agreeing, inconsistent" — in- (negative prefix) + congruus (attested in post-classical Latin). - English incongruous, "not in harmony; out of keeping; inconsistent" (17th c., 1610s), borrowed from Latin incongruus (with the -ous adjectival ending). Final node: INCONGRUOUS.
