• Rushes: Similar because of the same wet-ground habit and the shared root-word; differs because "rush" is the broader family (Juncus, with soft round stems) while bulrush picks out the larger, coarser plants — a bulrush is "the great rush".
  • Cattails: Similar because the exact American name for Typha species; differs by region and image — "cattail" names the sausage-shaped brown spike, while "bulrush" is the older, biblical-flavoured term.
  • Reeds: Similar because tall wetland plants of the same habitats; differs botanically — reeds are true grasses (e.g., Phragmites) with feathery plumes, while bulrushes are rushes, sedges or Typha.
  • Sedges: Similar because common marsh plants ("sedges have edges" — triangular stems); differs because sedges (Carex) are small-to-medium plants of distinct families from the great bulrushes.
  • Papyrus: Similar because the specifically Egyptian bulrush — the biblical "ark of bulrushes" is a papyrus basket; differs because papyrus is one species (Cyperus papyrus), historically bound to writing and boats.
  • Club-rush: Similar because the botanists' name for many British bulrushes (Scirpus, Schoenoplectus); differs because it is the narrow technical name, not the folk word.
  • Flags: Similar because large marsh plants at the water's edge (yellow flag, sweet flag); differs because they are iris-like plants, not bulrushes.
  • Tules: Similar because the great bulrush of Californian wetlands (Schoenoplectus acutus); differs by region — a western American name for the same tribe of plants.
