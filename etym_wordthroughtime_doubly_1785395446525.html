- [14th century: formation] - Formed in English from double plus the adverbial suffix -ly, the word first meant simply 'in two ways' or 'in a twofold manner' — a plain adverb of manner.
- [15th–17th century: degree over count] - Its sense settled on intensity rather than repetition: to do something doubly was to do it to twice the degree or force, so that the word came to multiply a quality instead of counting occurrences.
- [18th–19th century: the adverb of consequence] - 'Doubly' became the favourite emphatic for security and caution redoubled by experience ('doubly sure', 'doubly guarded'), carrying a dry rhetorical weight — things done harder precisely because they were once done badly.
- [Contemporary sense] - An idiomatic, emphatic adverb meaning 'to twice the normal degree or extent', still trading on the compressed fold of two grounds into one word, but now at home in plain speech and journalistic prose alike.
