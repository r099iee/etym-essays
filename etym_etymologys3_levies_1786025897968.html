LEVIES descends from one of the most useful roots in the Indo-European family — a root meaning "light" — and its history shows how an abstract idea about weight came to govern the two great instruments of state power: armies and taxes. The word's story runs from Proto-Indo-European through Latin and Old French into medieval English law, and from there into the vocabulary of modern finance and government.

1. The Indo-European Root *legʷʰ-

The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *legʷʰ-, "light, having little weight." The root's family in the ancient languages is extensive: in Latin it produced levis, "light," and through that the verb levāre ("to lighten, raise"); in Greek it produced elachys, "small"; in Germanic it produced lung (etymologically "the light organ") and, through Latin borrowings, the entire modern family light, lever, levitate, alleviate, relieve, elevation, and relevant. The core image is simple and bodily: weight and its opposite. To make something light is to lift it; to lift it is to raise it; and "to raise" is the very idea on which levies is built.

2. Latin: levis and levāre

Classical Latin inherited levis, "light in weight," from *legʷʰ-, and formed from it the first-conjugation verb levāre, "to make light, lighten, lift up, raise, elevate." The verb's range in Latin is instructive: levāre could mean to lift a physical weight, to lighten a burden, to raise one's voice, to elevate one's rank, or to relieve distress. It is the direct ancestor of English levitate (via the Late Latin levitas, "lightness"), lever, alleviate ("to lighten"), and relieve (via relevāre, "to lift again"). The past participle levātus, "raised," provided the base for the Romance verbs.

3. Old French: lever and levée

In the Gallo-Romance of the early Middle Ages, Latin levāre became Old French lever, "to raise, lift up," still alive today in modern French lever ("to get up, raise") and in borrowings like English levée (the ceremonial morning reception of a king, literally "a rising"). From the feminine past participle of lever came the noun levée, "a raising, a lifting." By the 12th century, levée had acquired its two fateful institutional meanings in feudal France: the summoning of vassals and knights to military service (a "raising of arms") and the collection of feudal dues, tolls, and taxes owed to the suzerain (a "raising" of revenue). Both meanings rest on the same concrete picture: the lord reaches down and raises men from their fields and money from their pockets into the service of the crown.

4. Anglo-Norman and Middle English (c. 1400)

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman administration brought levée into the legal and military vocabulary of England, where it was adapted as levie or levy (attested from around 1400). In medieval English law, to levy was a fundamental royal prerogative: the king could levy war, levy men, and levy taxes, and the noun named both the act and the things raised. The phrase "levy of war" entered the legal language of treason; "to levy money" entered the language of finance. The word's double meaning — troops and money — matched the reality of medieval warfare, in which raising an army and raising the funds to pay for it were two sides of one royal operation.

5. Early Modern Development: County Levies and Public Debate

In the 16th and 17th centuries the military sense developed a new political edge. The Tudor and Stuart monarchs raised county levies — locally recruited forces — and the question of who might lawfully levy troops and for how long became a constitutional battleground. The English Civil War (1642-1651) was fought over, among other things, the right to levy men and money: Parliament's claim to control the levy stood at the centre of the conflict, and the phrase "the power of the purse" is itself a descendant of this debate over who could levy what. The distinction between temporary, locally raised levies and a professional standing army — long a political anxiety in Britain — fixed the word's military sense in historical memory even as standing armies became the norm.

6. Modern Fiscal Expansion (19th-21st c.)

In modern English the fiscal sense has flourished while the military sense has receded into historical and literary writing. Governments levy taxes, duties, fines, and assessments; municipalities levy rates; the verb is standard in finance and law. The plural levies is used both for multiple taxes and, in historical or fantasy writing, for bodies of conscripted troops — exactly the sense of the source passage, where a general's army is reinforced by "their levies."

  • Sound change: Latin [le.w.aː.re] → Old French [ləˈve] → Middle English [ˈle.vi] → modern [ˈlev.i], with the plural levies [ˈlev.iz]; the final Latin infinitive was trimmed in Romance, and the English form stabilized as a two-syllable word.
  • Morphology: The noun and verb are the same form, a feature inherited from the French past participle used substantively.
  • Key literary and historical attestations: medieval charters and statutes, Civil War polemics, and modern historical prose and fantasy fiction all use the military sense; fiscal levy is ubiquitous in journalism and administrative law.
  • Summary: The word is a perfect demonstration of the path by which a PIE adjective meaning "light" became, two thousand years later, the standard English word for the compulsory raising of men and money.
