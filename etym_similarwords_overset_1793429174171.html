• Capsize: Similar because both specifically describe a vessel being overturned or rolled upside down in the water; differs because capsize is the standard, modern nautical and everyday term (borrowed in the late eighteenth century from Spanish capuzar, ‘to sink by the head’), whereas overset is the older, native Germanic term that was the dominant nautical word in Swift and Defoe’s era but is now literary and archaic.
  • Overturn: Similar because both describe rotating an object so that its top is at the bottom or it rests on its side; differs because overturn is the direct, standard modern physical term for vehicles, furniture, and court verdicts, whereas overset carries older literary, nautical, and psychological nuances.
  • Upset: Similar because both descend from identical Germanic directional-verbal compounding (over-set vs up-set) and share physical overturning, plan disruption, and emotional turmoil; differs because upset has become the universal everyday word for emotional irritation, mild anger, or stomach illness, having lost its primary physical nautical weight, whereas overset implies complete toppling and severe emotional derangement.
  • Upend: Similar because both involve tipping an object out of its normal vertical orientation; differs because upend specifically means setting an object onto its end or turning it perpendicular to its base, whereas overset implies catastrophic inversion or toppling over completely.
  • Overthrow: Similar because both describe toppling power or stable arrangements; differs because overthrow strongly emphasizes violent political rebellion, military defeat, and the deliberate toppling of rulers or tyrants, whereas overset often describes accidental toppling caused by storms, rough terrain, or unintended blunders.
  • Keel over: Similar because both describe a vessel or person rolling over; differs because keel over focuses on the ship’s keel turning upward or an individual fainting and collapsing flat on the floor, carrying a slightly more colloquial and visual tone.
  • Subvert: Similar because both mean to destroy the foundation or order of something; differs because subvert (from Latin subvertere, ‘to turn upside down from beneath’) is an abstract, intellectual term implying covert, insidious undermining of institutions, morals, or truth, whereas overset is direct and physical.
  • Destabilize: Similar because both involve the loss of balance; differs because destabilize is a modern technical, political, and economic term denoting the gradual undermining of equilibrium, whereas overset denotes the catastrophic moment of actual collapse and inversion.
  • Derail: Similar because both describe the violent interruption of progress; differs because derail uses a nineteenth-century industrial railway metaphor (jumping the tracks), whereas overset is rooted in maritime waters and horse-drawn carriages.
  • Unhinge: Similar because both describe the mental breakdown of composure; differs because unhinge employs an architectural metaphor (a door torn from its hinges), implying severe madness or psychological detachment, whereas overset implies emotional overwhelm and loss of balance.
