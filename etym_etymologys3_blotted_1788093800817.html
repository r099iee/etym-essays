1. An obscure origin. Unlike most English words, blot has no secure etymology and therefore no reconstructible Indo-European chain. The noun is first attested in English around 1300 with the sense "a stain, a spot", and the OED notes that a connection with Old Norse blettr "spot, stain" — preserved in modern Icelandic blettur — is possible but unproven, since no early Norse attestation of the form survives in manuscripts. Other Germanic and Romance origins have been proposed but none commands assent. Etymology demands candour here: the honest statement is that the word appears in Middle English without a documented ancestor, and its deeper history is simply unknown. This is itself a fact worth noting — not every common English word can be chased back to an Indo-European root.

2. The noun first, then the verb. The history of the word runs from noun to verb. The noun blot (c. 1300) meant a stain or spot, above all an ink stain on parchment — the natural accident of the medieval scriptorium and counting-house, where a slip of the pen left an irreparable dark mark on an expensive page. From this noun the verb blotten "to stain, spot, defile" was formed in the early fifteenth century, and soon acquired its crucial secondary sense: to cancel or delete writing by covering it with ink, so that "blotting" became a method of erasure long before the eraser existed.

3. "Blot out": the great biblical idiom. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries turned "blot out" into one of English's most solemn verbs of erasure. The Wycliffe and King James traditions gave it divine force: "blot out my transgressions" (Psalm 51:1), "blot out all mine iniquities" (Psalm 51:9), "blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us" (Colossians 2:14), and the warning that a name may be blotted from the book of life (Revelation 3:5). To be "blotted out" in this register is to be erased from existence, memory and the divine record alike — the most total form of annihilation the language then offered. The profane sense ran parallel: armies and chronicles "blotted out" cities, names and peoples, so that "blot out" came to mean "destroy utterly" as well as "cancel writing".

4. The moral blot. From the sixteenth century the noun also carried social and moral weight. "A blot on his name", "a blot on the escutcheon", "the blot of slavery" — the stain on paper became the stain on honour, and a person's reputation could be "blotted" (tarnished, disgraced) exactly as a page could be spoiled. This figurative sense made "blotted" a word not only of paper but of character and public esteem.

5. Blotting paper and everyday life. With the sixteenth-century spread of blotting paper — absorbent paper used to dry fresh ink — the verb acquired the opposed sense "to dry by absorbing": one blots a wet signature or a saturated letter. For three centuries this was the word's most common everyday use, in the office, the schoolroom and the post, so that "blotted" could mean either "stained" or "dried" depending on context. The two senses coexisted because both concerned ink: one left a mark, the other removed excess moisture.

6. The modern word: science and darkness. In the twentieth century the word took an unexpected scientific turn. In 1975 the biochemist Edwin Southern described a technique for transferring DNA fragments onto a membrane for detection, which became known as the Southern blot; the name was then playfully extended as the Northern blot and Western blot for RNA and protein. The jargon puns on the ancient stain sense — evidence is literally imprinted as a spot or band — giving "blot" a fresh, specialised life in molecular biology. Simultaneously, in poetry and fantasy, "blotted out" kept its force of total erasure: Tolkien's "Now it was lost and gone, blotted in the dark" (from The Hobbit) uses the verb for something swallowed by darkness and annihilated to sight, the modern descendant of the biblical blotting out of names and transgressions. From the scriptorium stain of 1300 to the laboratory membrane of 1975, "blotted" has never lost its link to marks, ink and the power of erasure.
