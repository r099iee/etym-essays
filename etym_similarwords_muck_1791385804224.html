• Manure: Similar because both refer to animal excrement and organic waste utilized as agricultural fertilizer; differs because manure is a more formal, polite, and technical agronomic term (derived from Anglo-French manuvrer, to cultivate by hand), whereas muck is earthy, Anglo-Norse vernacular that also encompasses wet mud, unpalatable food, mining spoil, and bungled tasks.
  • Dung: Similar because both denote animal feces collected from livestock; differs because dung refers strictly and exclusively to the fecal matter itself, whereas muck typically includes soiled straw, urine, composted matter, and mud, and has extensive colloquial usages as a verb and figurative insult.
  • Mud: Similar because both denote wet, sticky, saturated soil or earth; differs because mud is clean earth mixed with water without necessary organic or fecal content, whereas muck specifically implies foul, slimy, decaying, or manure-laden sediment.
  • Mire: Similar because both describe deep, wet, sticky swampy ground; differs because mire (from Old Norse myrr, bog) emphasizes deep, treacherous wetland or quagmire that traps travelers, whereas muck emphasizes agricultural waste, stable grime, or unpalatable food.
  • Sludge: Similar because both refer to thick, semi-solid wet deposits; differs because sludge typically denotes industrial, chemical, sewage, or oil residue settling in tanks or drainage systems, whereas muck is predominantly agricultural, natural soil, or vernacular.
  • Slop: Similar because both are used colloquially to describe repulsive, unappetizing food or liquid waste; differs because slop emphasizes watery, liquid kitchen refuse or swill fed to pigs, whereas muck can be thick, heavy, semi-solid mud, agricultural fertilizer, or excavated stone.
  • Rubbish: Similar because both denote worthless, discarded waste; differs because rubbish refers broadly to dry trash, refuse, and junk, whereas muck emphasizes wet, muddy, dirty, or foul-smelling matter.
