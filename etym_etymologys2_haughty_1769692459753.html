PIE *h₂el- "to grow, nourish, bring up" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; source of Latin alere "to nourish") → Latin alere "to nourish, feed, support" (attested 1st c. BCE; the "grow" sense) → Latin altus "grown, tall, high, deep" — past participle of alere, "grown up, elevated" (attested 1st c. BCE; source of altitude, exalt) → Old French haut / halt "high" (c. 9th–12th c. CE; initial h- added under Frankish Germanic influence, see debate note; source of Modern French haut "high") → Middle English haught / haute "high, lofty, elevated" — borrowed from Old French haut (14th–15th c. CE) → English haughty "arrogantly proud, disdainful" — haught + adjectival -y (16th c. CE, first recorded c. 1520s) → haughty ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The initial /h/ in Old French haut is not inherited from Latin altus (which had no initial h-). It is standardly explained as contamination from Frankish Germanic *hauh "high" (cognate with English high and German hoch) during the period of Frankish dominance in northern Gaul — a classic case of Germanic influence on Romance vocabulary. Some scholars, however, argue the h- was a medieval graphic spelling without phonetic force in all dialects. The French adjective hautain "haughty" shows the same parallel semantic development from "high" to "proud." The English -y form is a native formation on the borrowed adjective haught.
