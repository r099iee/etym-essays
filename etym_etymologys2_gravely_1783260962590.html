Derivation chain for GRAVELY (single root word; the adverb grave + the native English suffix -ly): 1. Proto-Indo-European *gwer- / *gwerh2- "heavy" (reconstructed root; reflexes include Sanskrit guru- "heavy, weighty" — source of guru, "weighty in authority" — Greek barys "heavy", Latin gravis "heavy", Gothic kaurus "heavy", Old English cweorn "quern, hand-mill, literally the heavy stone"), c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE. 2. Latin gravis "heavy, weighty; burdensome; serious, severe, dignified" (attested from the 3rd-2nd c. BCE, e.g., Plautus). 3. Old French grave "heavy, grave, serious" (learned or semi-learned borrowing from Latin gravis; attested 12th c. CE). 4. Middle French grave "serious, weighty, solemn" (attested 14th-15th c. CE). 5. English grave (adjective, borrowed from Middle French in the 16th century; attested c. 1540s). 6. English gravely (grave + -ly, the native English adverb-forming suffix from Old English -lice; attested 16th c.). 7. Modern English gravely (in both the manner sense "with serious dignity" and the degree sense "to a critical extent"), 17th c.-present. Note on the debate: the PIE root is standardly reconstructed *gwer- with a laryngeal *gwerh2- (older handbooks write *gwere-); the laryngeal detail is a technical point of reconstruction, not of semantic dispute. The suffix -ly continues Old English -lice, from Proto-Germanic *-liko, and is native Germanic, not Latin. The adjective grave "burial pit" is a DIFFERENT word (Old English graef) and has no connection with this chain; the similarity is a homographic coincidence.
