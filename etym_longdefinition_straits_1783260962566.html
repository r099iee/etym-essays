straits — plural noun (frequently used with singular reference to a single channel): narrow passages of water connecting two large bodies of water, bounded by land on both sides (e.g., the Straits of Gibraltar, the Straits of Messina, the Bosphorus). By metaphoric extension, straits denotes a position of difficulty, distress, or embarrassment ("in dire straits," "desperate straits"). The singular strait survives as an archaic or formal adjective meaning "narrow, tight, strict" and as a noun for a narrow waterway; the plural is the ordinary modern form in both the geographic and figurative senses. 1. Primary sense: hydrography.
  • A strait is a naturally formed, relatively narrow channel of water that connects two larger bodies of water and separates two landmasses; it is the sea-borne equivalent of a mountain pass, concentrating shipping, currents, and tides.
  • Classic examples: the Strait of Gibraltar (Atlantic to Mediterranean), the Straits of Messina (Tyrrhenian to Ionian, the Homeric home of Scylla and Charybdis), the Bosphorus (Black Sea to Sea of Marmara), the Straits of Malacca, the Strait of Hormuz, the Strait of Magellan, the English Channel (not always called a strait).
  • In Gibbon's sentence, "the Straits of Scylla" means the narrow passage of Messina, the very strait named for the monster — the source of the "mysterious horror" with which ancient Ionians regarded it.
2. Secondary and extended senses (polysemy).
  • Figurative distress: "in straits," "in dire straits," "desperate straits," "hard straits" — a position of difficulty, poverty, peril, or embarrassment. The bridge is etymological: Latin strictus "drawn tight, narrow" → "tight circumstances." The plural is the standard modern form; "straitened circumstances" is the adjective-noun survival of the same image.
  • Place-name element: "the Straits" alone (often capitalized) may name a specific passage, e.g., "the Straits" for the Straits of Gibraltar or (historically) the Straits of Messina; also used of the Malay Archipelago straits in colonial discourse.
  • Popular culture: "Dire Straits" — the British rock band (1977–1995), whose name deliberately puns on the idiom "dire straits," giving the plural noun a well-known modern echo.
  • Obsolete/formal adjective: strait "narrow, tight, strict, rigorous" survives in compounds like strait-laced (strictly proper) and straitjacket (a restrictive garment), and in straitened "impoverished."
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges. The word descends from Latin strictus "drawn tight, narrow, compressed," the participle of stringere "to draw tight, bind, compress," itself from a reconstructed PIE root meaning "tight, rigid, pressed." The geographic sense is the substantivized adjective: a strait is literally "the narrow [water]," the water squeezed between two lands. The figurative sense is a metaphor on the same image: a person in straits is "squeezed tight," in narrow, constricted circumstances with little room to maneuver. The history therefore runs: "tight/narrow" (Latin) → "narrow waterway" (Medieval and Modern English noun) → "tight situation, difficulty" (figurative plural, attested by the 16th century). The plural straits became standard for both the physical channels (a single passage often called "the Straits of X" in the plural, a habit inherited from Latin and French) and for the figurative distress. 4. Grammar and usage.
  • The singular strait (noun) is still used of a single passage but is markedly rarer and more formal; the plural straits is the ordinary form even when one channel is meant ("the Straits of Messina" names a single strait).
  • In the figurative sense the plural is fixed: one says "in straits" or "in dire straits," never normally "in a strait" (though the singular occurs archaically).
  • Pronunciation: /stɹˈeɪts/ — the plural ending -ts is pronounced as a single affricate-final cluster.
5. Register and connotation.
  • In hydrography and geopolitics straits is neutral and technical; in the figurative sense it is slightly formal and emphatic, conveying real peril or poverty ("in dire straits" is stronger than "in trouble").
  • The word carries a double life that is itself memorable: the same form names the world's shipping choke-points and the tight corners of personal misfortune.
