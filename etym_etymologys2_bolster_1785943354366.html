CHAIN: FROM A PIE "SWELL" ROOT TO BOLSTER (5 attested/reconstructed stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *bʰelǵʰ- "to swell, to blow, to bulge" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE). This is the great "swell" root of the Indo-European family, the ancestor of English belly, bellows, billow, and bulge, and the ultimate source of the word for a "swollen" cushion.
  2. Proto-Germanic *belganą "to swell, to be puffed up; to be angry" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–200 CE; attested in Old English belgan "to swell, to be angry"). The full-grade verbal root from which the cushion-noun was formed.
  3. Proto-Germanic *bulstraz "bolster, cushion, long padded support" (reconstructed; formed from the zero-grade of the swell-root plus the suffix *-stra-, literally "a swollen thing"). Cognates across Germanic: Old Norse bolstr, Old High German bolstar, modern German Polster.
  4. Old English bolster "pillow, cushion, long padded support" (attested from the 8th century). The noun is a plain, concrete household word for over seven centuries of English.
  5. Middle English bolster (same sense, 12th–15th centuries), from which the verb was converted in the 16th century (first "to prop up with a bolster," then "to support, strengthen").

→ Modern English BOLSTER (noun and verb; the exact form of this card).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • Some dictionaries connect *bulstraz more simply with PIE *bʰel- "to blow, swell" rather than the extended *bʰelǵʰ-; both reconstructions are defensible, and the exact shape of the PIE base is debated.
  • The verb is a clear sixteenth-century functional shift of the noun (conversion), not a separate borrowing — the dating of the earliest verb citations (16th century) is well attested, but the exact first occurrence in every text type is not fully mapped.
