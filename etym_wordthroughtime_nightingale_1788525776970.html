- [Old English / c. 8th-11th c.] - nihtegale - [literally "night-singer" (niht + gale); celebrated in Anglo-Saxon riddles and Christian poetry for singing through the dark vigil of night]
- [Middle English / 12th-15th c.] - nightegale / nightingale - [developed an epenthetic /n/ before velar /g/; headlined medieval debate poetry in The Owl and the Nightingale as the champion of courtly love and joyous lyric verse]
- [Early Modern English / 16th-17th c.] - nightingale - [firmly woven into Elizabethan lyric poetry and drama (Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet: the dawn debate between the nightingale of love and the lark of morning)]
- [18th-19th c. / Romantic & Victorian Eras] - nightingale - [reached the pinnacle of poetic symbolism in Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale"; applied as an honorific to singers like Jenny Lind and embodied in Florence Nightingale's compassionate nursing mission]
- [Late 19th c. - Present] - nightingale - [immortalized as the tragic heroine of Oscar Wilde's "The Nightingale and the Rose"; remains the universal symbol of pure aesthetic beauty and sacrifice]
