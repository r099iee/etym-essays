CHAIN — THE ETHNONYM Frank: Stage 1 — Proto-Germanic *frankō "javelin" (reconstructed; the traditional etymology of the tribal name, "the men of the javelin", i.e. the spearmen or warriors — DEBATED, see note below). Stage 2 — Late Latin Francus "a Frank" (singular; attested from the late 3rd century CE in Roman sources, including the Latin panegyrics of the Tetrarchic age and the historians of the 4th century, where the Franci appear on the lower Rhine as enemies and federates of Rome). Stage 3 — Old French Franc "a Frank" (the Romance descendant of Francus; also the source of the adjective franc "free" via Latin francus "free, exempt" — see debate note). Stage 4 — Middle English Frank "a Frank" (attested from the 14th century in English chronicles; the plural Franks fixed in historical usage). Stage 5 — Modern English Franks "the Franks" (regular plural, naturalized in historical writing; the same stem underlies France < Francia "the land of the Franks", and the adjective frank "free, candid" via Latin francus). FINAL NODE: FRANKS. DEBATE NOTE: the original meaning of Proto-Germanic *frankō is genuinely contested. The traditional view takes it as "javelin, spear", making the Franks "the spearmen" — a warrior-ethnonym parallel to Saxon (from a word for a knife or sword). A rival view connects the name with the root that yielded frank "free", taking it as "the free people" or "the fierce, bold ones"; the medieval Frankish tradition itself (e.g. the Liber Historiae Francorum) glossed the name as "free". Modern consensus leans toward the weapon etymology for the original tribal name, with "free" developing later in Latin as the conquering Franks became the tax-exempt ruling class of Gaul. The chain from Francus to English Franks is secure on either view.
