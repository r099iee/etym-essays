SOARED is the past tense and past participle of the verb soar: to rise or fly high into the air, usually with effortless, sweeping, majestic motion — whether a bird riding thermals, a dragon blazing upward, or an aircraft climbing. By strong modern extension it also means to increase steeply and rapidly to a high level, especially of prices, quantities, temperatures, or emotions. In the source sentence — "...and up he soared blazing into the air and settled on the mountain-top..." — the dragon, having issued from the Gate, rises blazing in one great vertical rush and comes to rest on the summit: soared carries the whole grandeur of the ascent, effortlessness and immense height combined.

Primary senses

  • To fly or rise high (intransitive, literal): of birds, aircraft, and flying creatures — to mount into the air and remain aloft at great height, often on rising air currents: "the eagle soared above the peaks".
  • To rise to a great height (non-flight): of towers, mountains, flames, and rockets — to climb steeply skyward: "the spire soared above the rooftops".
  • To increase rapidly (figurative, modern): of prices, costs, ratings, temperatures, and figures — to rise sharply and steeply to a high level: "house prices soared last year".
  • To rise in feeling or spirit (figurative): of spirits, hopes, ambitions — to lift emotionally: "his spirits soared at the news".

Secondary and peripheral senses

  • As noun (rare, poetic): a soar — the act or flight of rising high; used archaically or poetically.
  • Music and performance: a singer's voice or a melody "soars" — rises to high, powerful, sustained notes; a band's career "soars".
  • Economics/journalism: stocks, shares, inflation, and rents "soar" — steep, dramatic upward movement; the word is a headline staple.
  • In the source text: the dragon soars blazing — flight and fire fused in one image; the past tense records the completed rush upward from Gate to mountain-top.

Semantic bridge between senses: the core image is a steep, effortless climb to a great height. From physical ascent (birds, flames, towers) the word was naturally transferred to abstract and quantitative ascent — prices rising "as if on wings", spirits lifting, careers climbing — so that modern soar is as common in the financial pages as in nature writing.

Connotations and register

  • Soared is elevated, admiring, and dramatic: it implies height, grace, and scale, never merely speed. A rocket soars; a falling stone does not.
  • Neutral-technical in economics and meteorology ("temperatures soared"); lyrical and majestic in nature and fantasy prose.
  • The word's French ancestry — from "to expose to the air, to fly up" — still colours it: soaring is rising into the air itself, into the breeze, not merely going up.

In the source text, soared is chosen over "rose" or "flew" because the dragon's ascent must be both huge and effortless — a blazing, triumphant climb from the gate to the mountain-top that settles, in the final clause, into stillness on the summit; the word gives the flight its sweep and its grandeur.
