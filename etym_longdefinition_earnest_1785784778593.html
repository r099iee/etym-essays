EARNEST describes conduct, effort, or character that is serious, sincere, and intense — a wholehearted commitment to a purpose that excludes playfulness, pretence, and half-heartedness. It modifies both the actor and the act: an earnest person is sincere in disposition, and an earnest effort is one made with concentrated conviction. Unlike mere seriousness, earnest implies emotional investment and moral weight; unlike enthusiasm, it implies gravity and staying power. The noun and the fixed adverbial phrase in earnest mark the boundary between jest and reality: to do something in earnest is to do it truly and seriously, not in pretence or play.

In the source sentences the word signals moral and political conviction under pressure. The earnest effort to reform a corrupt city administration pits sincere purpose against cynicism and self-interest; the earnest expression that convinces a jury trades on the appearance of transparent honesty; earnest negotiations to avert a diplomatic crisis carry the weight of nations. In each, earnest marks the switch from gesture to genuine commitment — the moment when words become bonded to intention.

  • Grammatical range: earnest functions as an adjective (an earnest request), as a noun (in earnest, 'seriousness of purpose'), and inside the fixed phrase in earnest ('seriously, not in jest'); derivatives include earnestly (adverb) and earnestness (abstract noun).
  • Set phrases and collocations: in earnest; earnest effort, attempt, desire, hope, prayer, plea, wish, conversation, negotiations; an earnest reformer, an earnest expression, earnest money (a distinct legal word).
  • Antonyms: insincere, flippant, facetious, frivolous, jesting, trifling, half-hearted, casual; in jest and in play oppose in earnest.

Polysemy and the two words 'earnest'. Contemporary English contains two distinct words spelled earnest, and any full account must separate them. The adjective and the abstract noun — the subject of this card — are native Germanic words meaning 'sincere, serious, intense' and 'seriousness, zeal'. But there is also a separate legal and commercial noun: earnest (as in earnest money, earnest payment) meaning 'a deposit or pledge given to bind a bargain', which is an entirely different word — a medieval loan through Old French erres from Latin arrha and Greek arrhabṓn, ultimately from a Semitic root meaning 'pledge, surety'. The two homonyms collided in spelling in Middle English and have lived together in the dictionary ever since.

  • Semantic bridge of the native word: Old English eornost meant 'earnestness, zeal, seriousness' and could even name 'a fight, a battle'; the modern senses of sincerity and intensity descend from this root without interruption, with the martial vigour fading to moral seriousness.
  • The cultural keyword: in Victorian England earnestness became a cultural ideal — high seriousness, duty, moral sincerity, the rejection of frivolity — and Oscar Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest (1895) built an entire comedy on the collision between the given name Ernest and the virtue of being earnest.
  • Register: earnest today is a standard, positive word for committed sincerity (an earnest effort); it also has an available mild pejorative — 'a bit earnest' or 'over-earnest' — for humourless or naive zeal.
  • Domain map: religion (earnest prayer, earnest faith), politics and reform (an earnest reformer), diplomacy (earnest negotiations), personal relations (an earnest apology), and, in the separate homonym, law and real estate (earnest money).

Near-synonym boundaries.

  • Earnest vs. serious: serious is the broader word — weighty, grave, or sober, with or without commitment; earnest adds wholehearted sincerity. A serious discussion can be detached; an earnest plea is felt.
  • Earnest vs. sincere: sincere focuses on truth to one's feelings and the absence of hypocrisy; earnest adds intensity, zeal, and purpose. One can be sincere yet casual; an earnest person is never casual.
  • Earnest vs. solemn: solemn implies gravity, formality, and ritual weight, sometimes grimness; earnest implies passionate conviction, even eagerness. A solemn vow binds; an earnest prayer is fervent.
  • Earnest vs. determined / zealous: determined stresses stubborn persistence; zealous, burning ardor; earnest fuses both, minus fanaticism.
  • Earnest vs. grave: grave stresses seriousness of manner or consequence, often with foreboding; earnest stresses committed intention and good faith, which may be cheerful rather than grim.

Etymologically the word is native English, a Germanic inheritance of 'zeal, vigor, struggle' — solid and homespun rather than learned — and its modern dignity carries the trace of its old martial energy.
