- Late 16th Century (c. 1580–1600) - Emerges in Early Modern English as a verb borrowed from Low German or Middle Dutch (*snerren* / *snirren*), originally meaning to snort, breathe noisily through the nose, or laugh with derisive nasal expulsion.
- Early to Mid-17th Century - Shifts decisively from a purely auditory nasal sound to the visual facial expression of curling the upper lip, raising one mouth corner, and looking down upon another with scornful superiority.
- 18th Century (Augustan Age) - Becomes a celebrated literary keyword in satirical verse (Alexander Pope, Jonathan Swift) characterizing cynical drawing-room critics and supercilious literary rivals who wound reputations without direct confrontation.
- 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Deeply psychologized in Romantic poetry (Shelley's *Ozymandias*) and Victorian fiction (Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*, Charles Dickens) as the supreme badge of hubristic aristocrats and cold-hearted villains; nominalized permanently as *a sneer*.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues in modern psychology as the universal facial marker of contempt and moral superiority; widely applied in political journalism and cultural commentary to describe arrogant, dismissive rhetoric.
