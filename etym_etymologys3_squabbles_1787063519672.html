Squabble is one of those English words whose origin is honestly uncertain. The noun and verb both appear in the historical record at almost the same moment, around the year 1600, and both mean essentially what they mean today: to squabble is to engage in a petty, noisy quarrel; a squabble is the quarrel itself. The OED and most etymologists class the word as probably imitative — a "natural" or echoic formation whose spluttering consonant cluster skw- suggests the rapid, spluttering, bubbly noise of people arguing quickly and without dignity. The nearest documented relatives are Scandinavian: Swedish dialect skvabbel, "a dispute, a murmur", and Norwegian skvabba, "to prattle, to babble", and it is plausible that the English word was borrowed from a Scandinavian source and reinforced by native imitation. But no single ancestor can be established: the word has no Indo-European derivation that can be traced, and authorities differ on whether it is a loan, a native coinage, or a blend of both. This is exactly the class of word — casual, colloquial, comic — in which precise etymology is often irrecoverable, because such words are rarely written down in their earliest, spoken life.

Phonetic and formal stability. Since its first appearance the word has been phonetically stable: Early Modern English squabble, with the initial skw- cluster that English is fond of (squash, squeak, squeeze, squint), a short a, a doubled b, and a final light l. The double b is the only orthographic quirk worth noting: it appears because the suffix -le, which forms frequentative verbs, requires a doubled consonant after a short stressed vowel. The frequentative -le is significant for the word’s feel: English verbs of repeated or trivial action are often formed with it (wrangle, giggle, waddle, babble), and "squabble" sits naturally in that class of words for small, repeated, rather ridiculous actions.

Semantic development and cultural career. What is striking about squabble is how little it has changed in four centuries. From the first records, the word has named a quarrel that is (a) small in stakes, (b) noisy in manner, and (c) faintly comic in tone. Seventeenth-century usage speaks of "squabbles in ale-houses"; the eighteenth century, that great age of coffee-house politics, applied the word to the petty disputes of pamphleteers and parish officers; the nineteenth century used it of domestic and village life — Dickensian neighbours squabble, and the word is a natural fit for the comic realism of the Victorian novel. By the twentieth century the word had extended naturally to animals — gulls, sparrows, and dogs squabble over scraps, an extension that confirms the word’s core meaning of trifling, noisy contention — and to politics, where journalists dismiss factional infighting as "squabbles", thereby signalling to the reader that the matter is not serious. The word has also had a modest figurative life, applied to anything that argues noisily and trivially, though this is always an extension of the same basic picture.

In Tolkien. The use in The Hobbit is characteristic of Tolkien’s precise comic touch. The Lake-men and the Wood-elves are ancient peoples of dignity — the word "squabbles about river-tolls" reduces their commercial friction to the scale of street bickering, and the diminutive, dismissive quality of the word tells us both that the dispute is real (tolls were indeed a source of friction) and that it is contained and harmless. The plural form is perfect for the meaning: squabbles are by nature recurring, multiple, small-scale — the sort of friction that comes and goes without ever hardening into war. The word’s four-hundred-year stability of meaning is itself a small historical lesson: some things — noisy arguments over trivia — are so constant in human life that the words for them need never change.
