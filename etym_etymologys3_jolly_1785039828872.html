The adjective JOLLY entered English around 1300 as joly, borrowed from Old French jolif — the ancestor of modern French joli, "pretty, nice" — where it already combined the senses "festive, merry; amorous; handsome." The deeper origin of that French word is one of the classic unsolved problems of Romance etymology, and the two great theories map the two ways a word can be born. The older and still widely repeated account, the one followed in this note's chain, derives jolif from the Germanic world: from a word akin to Old Norse jól, the great heathen midwinter feast whose name came into English as Yule. On this theory jolif began as something like "Yuletide, festal," and the name of the feast spread to the mood it produced — so that to be "jolly" was, at bottom, to be in the spirit of the midwinter holiday, the time of feasting, drinking, and communal cheer. The apparent Italian cognate giulivo, "merry," has often been pressed into service as evidence, since it looks like an independent Romance adoption of the same Germanic stem. The Oxford English Dictionary, however, calls the Norse link "extremely doubtful," pointing to historical and phonetic difficulties, and prefers a purely Romance explanation: jolif as a formation built on Latin gaudēre, "to rejoice," the Indo-European root *gau- that flowered in English joy through Old French joie. On this rival account the jolly of the party is cousin to joy, enjoyed, and enjoyable — a native Romance growth rather than a German import. The question remains genuinely open, and honest dictionaries still flag it. What is certain is what the word did once it arrived in England. The Middle English joly proved enormously productive, and its range was far wider than ours. Beyond "merry, cheerful" it meant "vigorous, strong, youthful" — a knight could be "joly" in his prime; it meant "amorous, even lecherous," the young lover's word; it meant "handsome, well-dressed, splendid" in the language of the courts; it meant "playful, frisky" of animals; and it could even tip into censure, meaning "arrogant, overweening, foolish." The terminal -f of Old French jolif was dropped in Middle English exactly as it was in hastif (becoming hasty), leaving the modern spelling. Through the early modern centuries the word settled into the hearty, convivial, chestnut-roasting register we know: it was the tavern word and the Christmas word at once. From the 1540s jolly developed a second life as an intensifier — "a jolly good fellow," "a jolly time" — a peal of general approval that survives in modern British speech ("a jolly good effort," "jolly well"). By the 1650s it could also mean, with a wink, "somewhat drunk." Its festive heart never faded, however. It generated jollity and jollification, the words for organized merriment; it named the pirate banner the Jolly Roger from the early 18th century, the roistering flag of cheerful violence; it described the Royal Marines as "jollies" for their red coats; and it became the stock word of Victorian Christmas cards and nursery rhyme. Charles Dickens loved it — the fat, smiling, jolly host is a fixture of his festive scenes — and the song "For He's a Jolly Good Fellow" fixed the phrase in the popular ear. In the 20th century the British adverbial uses ("jolly good," "jolly well") and the stereotype phrase "jolly hockey sticks" kept the word both alive and faintly old-fashioned. When the Elves of Rivendell sing "O! tril-lil-lil-lolly, / The valley is jolly, ha! ha!" in Tolkien's The Hobbit, the word is doing its immemorial work: it is turning a valley into a holiday. For seven hundred years jolly has meant feast-time cheer, and the song's nonsense syllables — lolly, lolly, ha! ha! — are simply its musical echo. It is joy without anxiety, mirth without malice, the exact feeling of a warm fire and a safe house after the cold dark of the mountains. A word whose origin may lie either in a pagan midwinter feast or in a Latin verb for rejoicing has ended, either way, as the small English adjective of pure celebration.
