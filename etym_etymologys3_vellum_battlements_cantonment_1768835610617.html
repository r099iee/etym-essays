vellum — The biography of vellum begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *wet-, which meant "a year" — not an abstract span, but the cycle of the seasons by which a young animal is measured. From this root the ancient languages each took their own harvest. In Sanskrit it produced vatsá-, "a calf, a yearling," the animal in its first year, and the word softened toward "child," passing into Hindi as bachchā (a child; also a calf) through the Prakrit vernaculars — a parallel life in which the yearling and the child are the same word. In Greek the root yielded étos, "year," simply and directly, with the initial w- of *wétos vanishing in the regular sound changes of Greek. In Latin it did something subtler: vetus, "old," is literally "having years," one who has accumulated them, while vitulus, "calf," repeats the Sanskrit formation exactly — the young beast counted by its first year. And in Germanic, the same calculus produced *weþruz, the yearling ram, which survives in English as wether, the castrated ram of the flock. It is from the Latin calf that vellum itself descends. Latin vitulus became Old French veel, "a calf," the source of English veal, the calf on the plate; and from veel the French formed velin, "of a calf," designating the parchment made from the calf's skin. English took the word in the later Middle Ages as velym, vellum, at the very time when the illuminated book and the legal charter demanded a writing surface finer than ordinary parchment. The semantic journey is complete: a root meaning "year" names the yearling calf; the yearling's hide is prepared into the finest writing skin of the Middle Ages; and the skin becomes the membrane of the law. When the Earl of Groan throws down his scrolls of vellum, the material silently carries this entire ancestry — the law written, literally, upon the skin of the year. The word's social history then ran a long arc: displaced from the ordinary book by paper from the fifteenth century onward, vellum retained its hold on records, charters, and deluxe volumes, and in modern usage it survives both as a premium material and as a name for imitative "vellum paper," always carrying the patina of permanence and ceremony. battlements — The word battlement belongs to the family of the blow. Its ultimate root is the PIE *bhau-, "to strike, to beat," a root of physical violence whose clearest Latin reflex is battuere, "to beat, pound" — a verb whose own immediate origin is debated (it may be a Celtic loan), but which was certainly a word of the workshop and the battlefield, the beating of metal and the pounding of blows. From battuere, Late Latin built battalia, "fighting exercise, combat," the arena of the blow; Old French took it as bataille, "battle," the great word of medieval war, from which English battle descends. But the Old French also formed a verbal adjective of fortification: bataillier, "to fight, to fortify," and from it bataillement, the fortification built for fighting — the parapet of a castle wall, crenellated so that defenders could strike between the gaps. Middle English received it as batelment in the fourteenth century, at the height of the castle age, and English battlement has kept its military soul ever since: the alternating merlons and crenels are literally architecture for combat, the notch in the parapet being the shooting gap. The word's cultural career, however, has drifted from the practical to the picturesque. Throughout the medieval and early modern periods battlements were functional military architecture — the archer's station along tower and curtain wall. From the late eighteenth century the Gothic Revival recast them as the romantic signature of the castle: the notched skyline became an ornament of memory, reproduced decoratively on Victorian villas, railway stations, and follies, its defence long forgotten but its silhouette universally read as "castle." In the passage, the battlements above the cantonment are exactly this moonlit skyline — the walkway of honour on which the chosen carvers pace, the word quietly uniting its military ancestry with the romance of the notched wall against the night sky. cantonment — Cantonment is a word of corners and allotment. At its deepest reach lies a Pre-Greek substrate word, kanthós, "corner of the eye" — a word of the ancient Mediterranean with no verified Indo-European etymology, whose rim-and-edge senses carried it into Latin as canthus, the iron rim or tyre of a wheel, the hard edge of a thing. From Latin the word entered Italian as canto, "corner, side, edge," a thoroughly everyday word of place; and from canto Italian built cantone, "a large corner, a district" — the corner of territory marked off as a unit. French took cantone as canton, "a corner of territory, a district," and gave it its most famous institutional sense in the Swiss cantons, the self-governing districts of the Confederation. From canton, French formed cantonnement, "the quartering of troops in a district" — the military adoption of the corner-word, the allotting of soldiers to their assigned quarter of the land. English borrowed cantonment in the mid-eighteenth century, and the word flowered in the colonial world, above all in India, where a cantonment was the permanent military station outside a town — with its barracks, parade ground, bazaar, and officers' lines, a self-contained district of empire. The social meaning is constant from root to modern sense: a cantonment is a place assigned, a corner of the map to which a people or a garrison is allotted. In the passage the cantonment of the outer carvers stands at the foot of the wall — the quarter of the excluded, bounded and permanent, whose only view of the castle is the moonlit battlement above them that they are permitted to walk once a month.
