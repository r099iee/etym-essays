frankincense (noun) — An aromatic, hardened gum resin harvested by tapping the trunks of desert trees of the genus Boswellia (chiefly Boswellia sacra, B. carterii, B. frereana, and B. serrata), native to the southern Arabian Peninsula (Oman, Yemen), the Horn of Africa (Somalia, Ethiopia), and parts of India. When placed upon hot charcoal or ignited, the dried resin tears melt and release a dense, fragrant, sweet-balsamic smoke characterised by uplifting citrus, warm conifer, and gentle peppery undertones. For millennia, frankincense has occupied a pre-eminent position in global trade, religious liturgy, embalming, traditional medicine, and high perfumery.

Primary sense — botanical resin and sacred incense. In its most widespread historical and ritual sense, frankincense refers to the dried oleo-gum-resin itself, often termed olibanum in commercial and pharmacological records. Harvesters score the bark of the hardy, gnarled Boswellia tree during dry seasons, allowing the milky sap to exude, coagulate, and oxidise under the desert sun into translucent golden or milky-white droplets known as "tears." In ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, Judea, Greece, and Rome, burning these tears upon temple braziers served as the ultimate offering to the divine. The upward drift of the aromatic smoke symbolised human prayers rising to heaven, while its antiseptic fragrance purified sacred spaces of foul odours and spiritual corruption.

Figurative and literary usages — royal, sublime, or sweet breath. Beyond the material resin, frankincense frequently functions in literature as a metaphor for extraordinary purity, holy sweetness, exalted love, and celestial tribute. In Oscar Wilde’s fairy tale The Nightingale and the Rose, the personified figure of Love is described with mythical sensory richness: "Flame-coloured are his wings... His lips are sweet as honey, and his breath is like frankincense." Here, the word evokes an atmosphere of divine consecrated passion, transcending mortal appetites. Similarly, across English Romantic and Victorian poetry, any air charged with transcendent perfume or sacred stillness is said to breathe or reek of frankincense.

Christian liturgy and biblical typology. In Christian theology and iconography, frankincense is immortalised as one of the three gifts brought by the Biblical Magi (the Three Wise Men) to the infant Christ at Bethlehem, alongside gold and myrrh (Matthew 2:11). Within traditional Christian typological exegesis, each gift bears distinct symbolic weight:

  • Gold — honours Christ’s sovereign kingship.
  • Frankincense — acknowledges his divine priesthood and deity (as incense offered exclusively to God).
  • Myrrh — foreshadows his mortality, sacrificial death, and burial (as a bitter embalming spice).
To this day, Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and high-church Anglican services regularly use frankincense in censers (thuribles) during high masses, solemn vespers, funerary rites, and church dedications.

Modern domains and polysemic applications:

  • Fine Fragrance & Perfumery: Frankincense (often listed as olibanum oil or frankincense absolute) is prized by master perfumers as an exceptional base and middle note. It imparts longevity, amber warmth, resinous depth, and sparkling lemony-pine top facets to oriental, woody, chypre, and liturgical perfume compositions.
  • Pharmacology & Herbal Medicine: Known in Ayurvedic medicine as Shallaki (derived from Boswellia serrata), frankincense extracts containing pentacyclic triterpenic acids (boswellic acids, particularly AKBA) are widely researched and utilised for their potent anti-inflammatory, anti-arthritic, and analgesic properties in managing chronic joint disorders, inflammatory bowel disease, and respiratory conditions.
  • Aromatherapy & Wellness: The distilled essential oil is diffused to promote mental clarity, meditative tranquillity, relaxation, and respiratory comfort, frequently incorporated into luxury skincare for its cell-rejuvenating, astringent qualities.
  • Geopolitical & Economic History: The term designates the historical commodity that powered the ancient "Incense Route," a vast maritime and overland trade network connecting the ports of Oman and the Dhofar region across the Arabian desert to Petra, Alexandria, and Rome, generating legendary wealth for ancient South Arabian kingdoms such as the Sabaeans and Himyarites.

The Compound's Semantic Architecture: The English name frankincense is a direct loan-translation and phonetic naturalisation of Old French franc encens. The adjective franc did not mean "French" in this context, but rather "free, noble, pure, of genuine superior quality" (a meaning inherited from the privileged legal and social status of the Frankish freemen), while encens derived from Latin incensum ("that which is burnt", from incendere "to kindle"). Hence, frankincense literally signifies "pure, noble, high-grade incense", distinguishing this supreme Arabian aromatic from lesser native gums and impure resinous adulterants.
