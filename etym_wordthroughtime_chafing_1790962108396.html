- Late Middle English (c. 1300–1400) - Thermal & friction origin: Borrowed from Old French *chaufer* ('to warm, heat, rub to warm'); strictly meant warming cold limbs by rubbing, or heating liquids on a fire (origin of the *chafing dish*).
- 15th to 16th Century - Physical wear & skin abrasion: Developed the physical sense of rubbing against skin to cause soreness, irritation, or raw wounds from armor, boots, or saddles.
- Late 16th to 17th Century - Metaphorical emotional irritation: Elizabethan drama and literature adopted *chafe* to describe growing angry, irritated, or exasperated ('his blood began to chafe').
- 18th to 19th Century - Restraint & impatience: Narrowed to the specific emotional sensation of writhing or fretting under delay, confinement, authority, or insult ('chafing under the yoke').
- Contemporary Usage - Fully dual-functioning across modern English: ubiquitous in sports/medical language for skin friction and in literary/journalistic prose for impatient resistance to rules, confinement, or grievances.
