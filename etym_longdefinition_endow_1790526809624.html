The author is arguing for a secular, immanent ethics: rather than attempting to artificially endow (gift, bless, or crown) the mundane fabric of daily life with mystical or transcendental halos, ethical reason should honor the ordinary precisely as it is — finite, singular, and fully sufficient in its own earthbound reality.

In its foundational financial and institutional sense, endow means to furnish a university, hospital, charitable trust, museum, or religious foundation with a permanent source of revenue, estate, or invested capital, the income of which is dedicated in perpetuity to supporting an academic chair, research program, or public mission. Historically and legally, it originated in feudal marital law as the act of providing a bride or surviving widow with a legal dower (a life interest in property or income). Through centuries of metaphorical extension, the verb broadened into general psychology and literature to describe nature, providence, or fortune bestowing extraordinary talents, virtues, physical beauty, or intellectual gifts upon individuals.

  • Figurative and Attributive Bestowal (The Source Sense): To furnish, invest, or equip a person, concept, or physical entity with a specific attribute, virtue, symbolic meaning, or metaphysical aura (e.g. "the poet's imagination endows the ruined castle with melancholic majesty"; "ancient folklore endowed the sacred oak tree with healing powers"). The bridge from the legal dowry is clear: just as property is permanently settled upon an institution or spouse, an imaginary or spiritual grace is permanently settled upon the object.
  • Institutional and Financial Endowment (The Classical Civic Sense): To establish a permanent financial foundation or bequeathed trust for an institution, school, or professorship (e.g. "the philanthropist endowed a new research chair in quantum physics at Oxford"; "the museum was generously endowed by nineteenth-century industrial benefactors"). Here, the focus is on perpetual funding and capital preservation.
  • Natural and Providential Gifts (Often in the Passive): To describe innate biological, intellectual, or moral advantages gifted by nature, birth, or God (e.g. "she was endowed with an extraordinary ear for musical harmony"; "the United States Declaration of Independence asserts that all men are endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights").
  • Historical and Marital Law (Dower and Settlement): In feudal, canon, and English common law, the solemn act of settling land, tenements, or goods upon a woman at marriage for her maintenance in widowhood (e.g. "the Book of Common Prayer matrimonial rite: 'With all my worldly goods I thee endow'").
  • Grammatical Construction and Prepositional Patterns: endow is a transitive verb that typically appears in two primary constructions:
    • Active with direct object and preposition 'with': to endow [object] with [quality/fund] (e.g. "to endow the ordinary with transcendental sublimity"; "to endow the academy with a substantial library").
    • Passive participle with 'with': [subject] is endowed with [talent/attribute] (e.g. "endowed with remarkable resilience").
  • Conceptual Nuances and Dignity: endow differs sharply from casual giving verbs like give, grant, or supply. It conveys permanence, profound value, sovereign authority, and lasting transformation. What is endowed is not a fleeting loan or disposable trinket, but an abiding, structural gift that defines the recipient's ongoing identity or institutional capacity.

Stylistically, endow is a noble, elevated Latinate verb suitable for serious philosophical, legal, historical, and literary prose. It evokes generosity, solemn commitment, and the permanent enrichment of the recipient.
