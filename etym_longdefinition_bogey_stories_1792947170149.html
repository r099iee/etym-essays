Bogey-stories are frightening nursery tales built round a bogey, a vague goblin-like monster used to scare children into good behaviour: stories of goblins and wolves and nameless things in the dark, told half in jest and half in warning by nurses, parents, and older siblings. In the source sentence it carries exactly this dismissive nursery sense: Fatty nurses used to frighten him with old bogey-stories about goblins and wolves, and the speaker brushes them aside as childish fancy, not the real peril now under discussion.

Core meaning in detail: The compound joins bogey, a dialect word for a hobgoblin, spectre, or bugbear, something that goes bump precisely because no one can describe it, to stories, invented narratives told for entertainment or moral. A bogey-story is therefore not a formal fairy tale with plot and moral but a loose threatening anecdote: the bogey-man will get you if you stray, the wolves wait beyond the Hedge, the goblin lives in the wood-shed. Its power lies in vagueness: because the monster has no fixed shape, each listening child fills the blank with a private terror, and the story works even when badly told.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Nursery scare-tales told by nurses and parents (primary sense, 19th c., the source sense): tales of bogeys, goblins, wolves, and Old Bogie himself, deployed to keep children from wandering, to enforce bedtime, or simply to tease. The bridge is direct composition: a story whose subject is a bogey, fear domesticated into bedtime theatre.
  • Any baseless scare or alarmist rumour (figurative extension): political bogey-stories, press bogey-stories about invasion or disease. The bridge carries the nursery into public life: grown-up newspapers frighten grown-up children with exaggerated monsters, and critics dismiss the panic as just another bogey-story.
  • Bogey as a thing of dread in golf and aviation (parallel modern senses of the first element): a golf score of one over par, an unidentified aircraft contact. The bridge is the same vague menace: the golfer dreads the extra stroke as the child dreads the goblin, and the pilot calls the unknown blip a bogey because, like the nursery monster, it is feared before it is known.
  • Bogey as nasal mucus (schoolboy slang, 20th c.): to pick a bogey. The bridge is probably the goblin as small impish thing: the little goblin in the nose, half disgusting and half comic, fear shrunk to playground humour.
  • Stories as fibs or excuses (secondary sense of the second element): to tell stories meaning to tell lies. The bridge runs through invention: a story entertains by inventing, a lie deceives by inventing, and the bogey-story sits between, half play and half threat, believed just enough to work.
  • The literary Gothic and fantasy afterlife (modern): from Victorian ghost anthologies to Tolkien's own goblins, professional writers harvest nursery bogeys into literature. The bridge is elevation: what nurses improvised, authors polish, giving the formless fright a name, a lair, and a plot, yet keeping the nursery shiver underneath.

Register and connotation: bogey-stories is affectionately contemptuous: the speaker smiles while dismissing. To call something a bogey-story is to say it belongs to the nursery, not the council of war, kindly meant but unserious. The hyphen marks its homely compound status, half one word and half two, like cook-book tales themselves. Tolkien deploys it to separate childish hearsay from the true shadow in the East: Fatty nurses frightened with wolves, but real wolves now walk, and the old word must be set aside for harder speech.
