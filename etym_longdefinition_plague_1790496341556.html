plague is both a noun and a verb of affliction. As a noun it means, at its gravest, a deadly epidemic disease — especially bubonic plague — and by extension any widespread calamity, torment, or pestering nuisance. As a verb it means to torment, harass, or afflict persistently. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita contrasting two sisters at Mrs Rice's school, where his mother became the pattern girl while his aunt absolutely refused evangelical principles, and became the plague and pet of it — it is the mischievous-tormentor sense, affectionately meant: the aunt teased, disrupted, and exasperated the schoolroom, yet was so charming that teachers and girls adored her anyway. To be the plague and the pet is to be simultaneously the establishment's headache and its darling — naughty, lively, irrepressible, forgiven.

The weight behind that schoolgirl joke is the long European memory of the Plague: the Black Death of 1347–1352 that killed a third of Europe, the Great Plague of London (1665), the plague of Marseille (1720), and the Third Pandemic (1890s–1920s), all caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis carried by rat fleas. Capitalised and with the article — the plague, the pestilence — the word still summons bell-ringing searchers, plague stones, Bills of Mortality, and Defoe's Journal of the Plague Year. Medicine distinguishes bubonic (swollen buboes), septicaemic (blood), and pneumonic (lung) forms, all rapidly fatal without antibiotics; history remembers quarantines, lazarettos, and the red cross chalked on doors with Lord have mercy upon us.

  • Epidemic-disease sense (historical head sense): a virulent contagious fever spreading through populations; e.g. the bubonic plague, victims of the plague, plague pits, plague doctors in beaked masks. Often capitalised when denoting specific pandemics. The bridge to all other senses is visitation: disease imagined as a blow struck from above.
  • Biblical-divine-punishment sense: any calamity sent as judgment; e.g. the ten plagues of Egypt (blood, frogs, lice, flies, murrain, boils, hail, locusts, darkness, death of the firstborn). Here plague translates Latin plaga (blow, wound) and Hebrew nega/makkah (stroke, blow): each plague is a stroke of God's hand. Evangelical schoolrooms like Mrs Rice's lived inside this sense, reading Exodus as current warning.
  • General-affliction sense: any widespread evil or burden; e.g. a plague of locusts, a plague of rats, the plague of knife crime, war is a plague upon nations. The logic is quantitative and moral at once: many sufferers, one tormenting cause.
  • Personal-tormentor sense (source sense): a person — especially a lively child — who teases and tries patience yet remains loved; e.g. you little plague, the plague of the class, the plague and pet of the school. Diminutives and pairings (plague and pet, plague and darling) mark the affection: the torment is mischief, not malice. Ruskin's aunt, refusing evangelical gloom with high spirits, is exactly this delightful nuisance.
  • Verb sense: to plague: to harass, pester, or afflict continuously; e.g. plagued by doubts, to plague a teacher with questions, locusts plagued the fields, an injury plagued his career. Grammar takes by/with for the tormentor (plagued by flies, plagued with guilt). The verb is the everyday survivor: speakers who would never say pestilence daily say stop plaguing me.
  • Interjectional and hyperbolic uses: a plague on both your houses (Mercutio's curse in Romeo and Juliet), avoid it like the plague, plaguey weather (dialectal intensifier). Each shows the word bleached from disease into emphasis while keeping its sting.

From Exodus boils to a Peckham schoolgirl's pranks to a modern plague of emails, the thread never breaks: a plague is what will not leave you alone — whether bacterium, locust, conscience, or laughing aunt — and what must be endured, quarantined, or, in the schoolroom's fond verdict, hugged.
