Waning is the present participle and gerund of the verb wane ("to decrease gradually"), and it describes a process of progressive reduction, decline, or fading away — whether in size, strength, intensity, quantity, or duration. It appears in three grammatical roles: as a continuous verb form (e.g., "the light was waning"), as an adjective (e.g., "a waning moon"), and as a gerund/noun (e.g., "the waning of the year"). In the present card, Tolkien's phrase "at the waning of the year" uses the noun/gerund sense to name the season in which the year is running out — late autumn sliding into winter.

Primary sense: the phases of the moon. The oldest and most common modern usage concerns the lunar cycle. The moon is said to be waning during the period between full moon and new moon, when its illuminated face is seen to shrink night after night from a full disc to a thin crescent to darkness. This sense is paired with waxing, the period of growth between new moon and full moon, and the two verbs have been a fixed astronomical and calendrical pair since ancient times. Astronomically, "waning" describes the decreasing illuminated fraction of the moon as seen from Earth, regardless of any actual change in the moon itself.

Extended senses: general and figurative decline. From the lunar image of gradual shrinkage, wane generalized to any process of diminishing:

  • Of physical quantities: waning light, waning daylight, waning supplies, a waning fire — things that visibly or measurably grow less.
  • Of strength and vigor: waning strength, waning health, waning powers — the ebbing of bodily or mental energy, especially with age.
  • Of abstract forces: waning influence, waning popularity, waning interest, waning enthusiasm — the gradual loss of social, political, or emotional force.
  • Of duration and existence: waning years, the waning hours, waning days — the closing period of a life, a day, or any span of time.
  • Of the year (Tolkien's usage): "the waning of the year" means the season when the year declines toward its end — late autumn, when daylight diminishes and the world turns to winter. This literary collocation draws on the old sense of the year as a cycle that waxes and wanes like the moon.

Jargon and fixed phrases.

  • Waxing and waning: an idiomatic fixed pair meaning alternately increasing and decreasing; in medicine it describes symptoms that fluctuate in severity (e.g., "waxing and waning confusion"), and in common speech it describes enthusiasm, fortunes, or interest that rise and fall.
  • Waning crescent: the technical name of the lunar phase immediately before new moon.
  • Waning gibbous: the lunar phase between full moon and last quarter.
  • Waning immunity / waning antibodies: modern epidemiological jargon (prominent since the COVID-19 pandemic) for the gradual loss of vaccine- or infection-acquired protection over time.
  • On the wane: a fixed prepositional phrase meaning "in a state of decline," used of businesses, careers, empires, fashions, and reputations ("his popularity is on the wane").
  • Astronomical and meteorological use: also applied to tides, glaciers, rivers, or seasons that recede or diminish.

Historical semantic bridge. The modern range of meanings rests on a very old continuity rather than on metaphor alone. Old English wanian already meant "to lessen, diminish, decline," and the moon was the canonical subject from Anglo-Saxon times onward; the same verb could be applied to the tide, the day, the year, a wound, or a life. Thus the word did not so much develop wholly new senses as apply one ancient idea of gradual diminution ever more widely, until by the 16th–18th centuries it had become the standard formal verb for "decline" of any sort, yielding literary uses such as Shakespeare's melancholy progressions in which beauty, pageants, and lives are seen to wane. Today the word carries a slightly literary or formal flavor in its figurative uses; in everyday speech "decrease," "decline," "fade," or "dwindle" are often preferred, but "wane" and "waning" remain alive in science, poetry, and set phrases.

Register and nuance. As an adjective, waning almost always points to something on the way out: a waning tradition, a waning hope, a waning superpower. It is inherently durative — it names a gradual, continuous process, not a sudden collapse — and it often carries a melancholy or elegiac tone, especially in literary contexts such as Tolkien's "waning of the year."
