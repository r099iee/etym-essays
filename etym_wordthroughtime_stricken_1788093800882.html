- Old English strican: “to stroke, rub, glide, move swiftly” — a gentle, tactile verb, no violence in it at all.
- 12th–13th c. ME, under Old Norse influence (strjúka): “to strike” gains its blow — to hit, smite, stamp; the participle stricken is born with the force of the weapon.
- 14th c.: the participle settles into prose — “stricken with illness”, “stricken to the heart”; a man can be stricken by disease exactly as by a sword.
- 16th–17th c.: the biblical phrasing fixes the sacred registers — “stricken in years” (Joshua 13:1, Luke 1:7), “stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted” (Isaiah 53:4); the word carries divine blows.
- 18th–19th c.: “the stricken field” (battlefield) and the first modern compounds — awe-stricken, panic-stricken, grief-stricken, poverty-stricken — the word becomes the standard adjective of overwhelming emotion and want.
- 20th–21st c.: struck takes over the ordinary grammar (the clock struck), leaving stricken wholly to catastrophe — stricken regions, stricken faces, stricken by grief — a specialised, mournful, newsroom-cathedral word.
