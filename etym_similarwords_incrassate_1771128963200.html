• thicken: Similar because both mean to make or become thicker or more dense; differs because thicken is the plain everyday verb usable for soup, plots, or fog, while incrassate is learned, Latinate, and confined to technical or archaic registers.
  • coagulate: Similar because both describe a fluid turning solid; differs because coagulate implies a chemical or biological reaction and an abrupt state change (as when blood clots), whereas incrassate suggests a slow accumulation of density.
  • congeal: Similar because both describe a liquid becoming semi-solid; differs because congeal is tied to cooling or setting (as fat hardens on a cold plate), while incrassate does not require a temperature change.
  • condense: Similar because both describe increasing density; differs because condense means concentrating or compacting volume (often gas to liquid), whereas incrassate describes the substance itself growing thick and viscous.
  • solidify: Similar because both describe matter passing to a firmer state; differs because solidify moves a substance all the way to solidity, while incrassate stops at thickness and viscosity.
  • inspissate: Similar because both are learned verbs meaning 'to thicken'; differs because inspissate (from Latin spissus 'dense') is the rarer and even more specialised near-twin, most at home in old pharmacy and in describing thickened fluids.
  • clot: Similar because both describe a fluid becoming thick and lumpy; differs because clot implies a particular mass or lump (especially of blood) rather than a general densification.
  • curdle: Similar because both describe a liquid becoming thick and separated; differs because curdle names the specific reaction of milk or similar colloids turning to lumps, with a sour or acidic cause.
  • congeal/cake: Similar because both describe thickening into a crust; differs because cake suggests packing into a hard layer or lump, while incrassate is about the fluid itself growing dense.
