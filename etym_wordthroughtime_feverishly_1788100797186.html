- 14th century: the adjective "feverish" is born as a strictly clinical word — a body afflicted with fever, flushed, trembling, restless.
- 15th–16th centuries: "feverish" leaks from body to mind — "feverish thoughts", "feverish dreams" — as humoral psychology reads emotion as bodily heat.
- Late 17th century: the adverb "feverishly" is coined (feverish + -ly) and is used from its first decades both literally by physicians and figuratively by poets.
- 18th century: periodical prose extends the word to collective states — "feverish speculation", "feverish constitutions" — it becomes an instrument of social and political description.
- 19th century: the Victorian age makes "feverish activity", "feverish haste", "feverish excitement" journalistic staples; the word is now chiefly metaphorical, applied to the burning urgency of industrial, commercial and creative life.
- 20th–21st centuries: the medical sense recedes into clinical prose; in ordinary modern English "feverishly" means "with frantic, intense, urgent effort" — the patient tossing feverishly and the team working feverishly against a deadline are the same fire, one literal, one figurative.
