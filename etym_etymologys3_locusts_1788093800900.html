1. The Latin base and its mystery. English locust descends from Latin locusta, first attested in Plautus (early 2nd century BCE) in the sense “lobster”, and used by classical writers indifferently for the crustacean and for the great swarming grasshopper. The origin of locusta is genuinely unknown: Michiel de Vaan's etymological dictionary of Latin (2008) gives it “no etymology”, and the popular guess that it meant “leaper” (from a root *lek-) is unsupported by the evidence. It may be a substrate word or an expressive formation; the Romans themselves clearly felt that the insect and the lobster were similar enough to share a name — both are armoured, jointed, many-legged creatures that move in sudden hops. This one-word-for-two-animals situation has left permanent traces in the languages of Europe: French langouste “spiny lobster” is a nasalised descendant of locusta, and English lobster itself comes from Old English loppestre, a borrowing of locusta adapted to native word-shape. Locust and lobster are therefore doublets: two English words from one Latin word, which is why this card's derivation chain honestly begins at Latin rather than at a reconstructed Indo-European root.

2. The French bridge and the English arrival. The insect sense reached English through Old French locuste (12th century), and Middle English locust is attested from around 1300, almost always in biblical and homiletic contexts — the plague of Egypt, the “devouring locust” of the prophets. For the first centuries of its English life the word was a book-word, tied to Scripture and to the classical natural history of Pliny, who describes locust swarms devastating whole regions. The plural locusts was the dominant form from the start, because the insect's significance is collective: one locust is a grasshopper; a thousand million are a plague.

3. The biblical matrix. No word in this batch carries heavier scriptural baggage. In Exodus 10 the locust is the eighth plague, covering the land “so that the land was darkened” and eating every green thing; in Joel the locust is the army of judgment, and Joel 2:25 promises restoration of “the years that the locust hath eaten”; in Revelation 9 the locusts of the abyss, led by Apollyon, torment the unsealed for five months. John the Baptist's diet of “locusts and wild honey” (Matthew 3:4) added a second strand: because the insects were permitted as clean food (Leviticus 11:22), the Baptist's food was probably real locusts — but medieval exegetes, uneasy with insect-eating, increasingly identified them with the sweet pods of the carob tree (Ceratonia siliqua), called “St John's bread”. That interpretation is now doubted by most scholars, but it planted the botanical sense of “locust” in the European languages.

4. The botanical and American transfers. From the carob the word jumped to unrelated trees with compound leaves and pods: the black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia), introduced to Europe from America in the early 17th century and given its generic name by Linnaeus in honour of the French gardener Jean Robin, and the honey locust (Gleditsia triacanthos). In American English “locust” also attached to the periodical cicadas (Magicicada spp.), whose vast synchronous emergences every 13 or 17 years earned them the popular name “seventeen-year locusts” — a transfer driven by mass appearance and loud buzzing, though cicadas are true bugs (Hemiptera), not grasshoppers, and do not eat crops.

5. The metaphorical career. The figurative use of “locusts” for rapacious multitudes is as old as the Bible itself (Judges 6:5: the Midianites came “like grasshoppers for multitude”), and it has remained productive: armies, tax-farmers, landlords and speculators have all been called locusts. The most famous modern instance is the German Social Democrat Franz Müntefering's April 2005 remark that some financial investors “descend like locusts” (Heuschrecken) on companies, stripping them and moving on — a phrase that became a global headline and a permanent item in the language of political economy. In this register “locusts” denotes value-extraction without attachment: the swarm eats, moves, and leaves desert behind.

6. Science and the present. Modern entomology has reframed the word without defusing it: the desert locust (Schistocerca gregaria) and the migratory locust (Locusta migratoria) switch between solitary and gregarious phases in response to crowding and rainfall, and a single swarm can cover hundreds of square kilometres and consume the equivalent of tens of thousands of people's food in a day. Satellite monitoring in the Horn of Africa, the Middle East and South Asia keeps the ancient symbol scientifically current: “a sea of locusts”, the image in this card's context from The Hobbit, remains a literal description of what a swarm looks like — and Tolkien's sentence, comparing a goblin army to bats and locusts, draws on the word's millennia of apocalyptic association.

7. Conclusion. From an unexplained Latin word that named both an insect and a lobster, through the Bible and the church fathers, the carob tree, the American trees and cicadas, to hedge-fund rhetoric and satellite-tracked swarms, “locusts” has carried one continuous semantic core — the devouring multitude — while accumulating an extraordinary range of collateral referents. Its history is a case study in how a word can be both scientifically precise and permanently mythological.
