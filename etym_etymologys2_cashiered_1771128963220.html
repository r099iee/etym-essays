Latin cassus 'empty, void, hollow, vain' (attested in classical Latin from the 2nd c. BCE; used of empty things, unfulfilled promises, and cases without substance) -> Late Latin cassare 'to make void, to annul, to bring to nothing' (attested in the Latin of the late empire and of Christian writers, c. 4th-6th c. CE; chiefly a legal word, of annulling judgments, marriages, and testaments) -> Old French casser / quasser 'to break, shatter, annul (a law, a vow, a judgment)' (attested c. 10th-12th c.) -> Middle French casser 'to annul an appointment or rank; to discharge' (the legal-administrative sense, 14th-16th c.) -> Dutch casseren 'to formally discharge from military service, to cashier' (a loan from French, in use by the 16th c. in military administration) -> English cashier 'to dismiss from a position of authority, esp. from military office, in disgrace' (attested c. 1590) -> past participle CASHIERED 'dismissed in disgrace' (attested from the early 17th c.). Notes on the chain: (1) The deeper PIE origin of Latin cassus is uncertain and is stated here as unknown rather than guessed; it has been linked speculatively to a root meaning 'to fall' (something fallen being 'void' or 'cast down') or to a root meaning 'to cut' (something cut off being 'empty'), but neither connection is securely attested, so the chain above deliberately begins at attested Latin. (2) The English verb is generally thought to have entered the language through Dutch military usage (casseren), reinforced by French casser; both routes lead back to Latin cassare. (3) The French verb casser also picked up the sense 'to break into pieces', partly under the influence of Latin quassare (from quatere 'to shake, shatter'), but the 'annul, make void' meaning that produces cashier belongs to cassare from cassus. (4) The English noun cashier 'bank clerk, teller' is a different word entirely - from Middle French caisse / Italian cassa 'cash box', from Latin capsa 'box, case' - and shares no etymology with the verb.
