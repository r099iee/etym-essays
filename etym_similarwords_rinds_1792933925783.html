• Peels: Similar because both name the removed outer covering of fruit; differs because peel stresses the act of stripping and suits thin skins like apple or potato, while rind stresses thickness and toughness like orange, melon, or bacon.
  • Skins: Similar because both are natural outer coverings of food; differs because skin is the broad everyday word (grape skins, chicken skin) and can be thin and edible, while rind is always the thick, resistant coat.
  • Zest: Similar because zest is literally grated citrus rind; differs because zest names only the fragrant outermost layer used as flavouring, while rind names the whole covering, pith and all.
  • Husks: Similar because both are dry protective plant coverings stripped before use; differs because a husk is the papery or leafy sheath of corn or grain, while a rind is the fleshy-tough peel of fruit or the coat of cheese and meat.
  • Crusts: Similar because both are hard outer layers with soft insides beneath, gnawed by dogs in the source scene; differs because crust belongs to bread, pastry, and earth, while rind belongs to fruit, cheese, and flesh.
  • Bark: Similar because bark is the tree's rind and the word's own oldest sense; differs because modern bark is reserved for trees, while rind has moved indoors to the kitchen and the dairy.
  • Crackling: Similar because crackling is roasted pork rind at its most delicious; differs because crackling names only the cooked crisped state, while rinds covers the raw, the leftover, and the gnawed scrap as well.
  • Shells: Similar because both are hard coverings protecting something edible; differs because a shell is rigid and usually inedible through and through (nuts, eggs, shellfish), while a rind is leathery-tough yet adjacent to the flesh, sometimes itself worth eating.
