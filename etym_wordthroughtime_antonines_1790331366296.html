- Roman Imperial Era (2nd c. CE) - Originated as the formal cognomen *Antoninus* and family dynastic designation adopted by Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius, and Commodus, representing supreme imperial legitimacy, adoptive piety, and senatorial harmony.
- Late Antiquity & Middle Ages (4th–14th c.) - Commemorated in Latin histories (Historia Augusta, Eutropius) and medieval chronicles as the nostalgic benchmark for benevolent, philosophically guided imperial governance.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment / Edward Gibbon) - Immortalized in Western historiography by Edward Gibbon in *The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776), who canonized 'the Age of the Antonines' as the pinnacle of human happiness, administrative wisdom, and material prosperity.
- 19th to Early 20th Century - Utilized in civilizational philosophy and literature (e.g., Will Durant, Oswald Spengler) as the tragic archetype of an imperial golden autumn—a magnificent plateau immediately preceding catastrophic epidemics and systemic decline.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Re-evaluated with nuanced modern scholarship, balancing their celebrated administrative, legal, and Stoic achievements against the catastrophic demographic impact of the Antonine Plague, social stratification, and the fateful dynastic succession of Commodus.
