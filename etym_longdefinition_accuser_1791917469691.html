Accuser is a countable noun naming the person who accuses: the one who alleges that another has done wrong and, in legal settings, the one who brings or initiates a charge. It is the agent noun of accuse, formed with the productive suffix -er, and its natural counterpart is the accused — the person against whom the charge is made. In the source context, “accuser and accused were asked to choose between two bowls of food”, the pair names the two parties to a dispute submitted to an ordeal: the one who alleges the wrong and the one who denies it.

Core sense

  • The person who makes an accusation. This can be informal (“his accuser spoke to the newspapers”) or formal (“the accuser gave evidence behind a screen”).
  • The complaining party in a legal proceeding. In systems without public prosecution, the accuser is the person who formally brings the case; in modern state systems the accuser is usually the complainant or victim-witness, while the prosecution is conducted by the state.
  • By extension, any source of a charge or blame. A report, a conscience, or public opinion can be personified as an accuser, though this is literary rather than everyday usage.

The legal role in history

  • Roman law. In the iūdicia pūblica — the standing criminal courts — there was no public prosecutor. Any citizen could bring an accusation, thereby acting as accūsātor. The accuser swore an oath, bore the burden of proof, and risked the penalty for calumnia if the charge was proved malicious. Successful accusers might share in the condemned person's property, a feature that encouraged both civic courage and informers.
  • Canon law. Medieval procedure distinguished three ways of starting a case: accūsātiō, where a private person formally charged another and accepted the risk of retaliation if he failed; dēnūntiātiō, a report made without assuming the role of party; and inquīsītiō, the judge's own investigation. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) restricted ordeals and encouraged the third mode, a shift that gradually marginalised the classical accuser.
  • English common law. Prosecution long remained private: the victim or a relative brought the appeal or the indictment, sometimes at personal risk and expense. The growth of public prosecution in the nineteenth century — culminating in the Crown Prosecution Service in 1986 — transferred the accusing function to the state.
  • Modern criminal procedure. The Crown or the People prosecute; the person who made the complaint is the complainant or witness. Yet the older vocabulary survives in the right “to be confronted with the witnesses against him” and in the everyday phrase “face your accuser”.
  • International tribunals. The caption of a case reads Prosecutor v. Accused, preserving in formal dress the ancient opposition between the one who charges and the one who answers.

The asymmetry between accuser and accused

The two words look symmetrical but carry very different connotations. The accused is a legal status protected by the presumption of innocence: to be the accused is to be entitled to a fair trial and to be presumed innocent until proved guilty. Accuser has no such protection built in. It can suggest courage — the person who breaks silence about a powerful wrongdoer — or malice — the person who makes false charges. The same sentence can therefore tilt according to which word is chosen: “she faced her accuser” sounds like a confrontation with an antagonist, while “she faced the accused” sounds like a witness giving evidence against a defendant.

Religious extension: the Accuser

In Jewish and Christian tradition, the Accuser is a title of Satan. The Hebrew śāṭān means “adversary” or “one who opposes”, and in the book of Job ha-śāṭān appears among the heavenly beings as a prosecutor who challenges Job's piety and asks permission to test him. Zechariah 3:1 shows “the Accuser” standing at the right hand of the high priest to oppose him, and the New Testament calls Satan “the accuser of our brethren” (Revelation 12:10). The Greek term diabolos, “slanderer”, reinforced the association. This religious sense gives the English word a dark, archetypal resonance: the accuser is not merely a litigant but the enemy of the innocent.

Modern journalistic and social use

  • High-profile allegations. Reporting on sexual-assault and abuse cases routinely uses accuser, sometimes neutrally and sometimes with a hint of doubt. Victims' advocates often prefer complainant, survivor, or alleged victim, precisely to avoid the adversarial colour of accuser.
  • Anonymity debates. “Anonymous accuser” is a contested phrase: it can signal protection from retaliation or, to critics, an unaccountable charge.
  • False accusation. The compound false accuser is old (it appears in early modern translations of the Bible) and remains common in legal and polemical writing.
  • Political discourse. “His accusers” is a standard way of framing a politician's opponents as a hostile collective, which is why the word is often avoided by careful editors when a neutral term is available.

Grammar, register, and collocations

  • Countable and personal: an accuser, her accusers, a false accuser. The plural is common because allegations often involve several complainants.
  • Common collocations: face one's accuser, confront one's accuser, cross-examine the accuser, anonymous accuser, false accuser, his accusers, the accuser and the accused.
  • Register: neutral-legal to journalistic. In ordinary conversation people say “the person who accused him” rather than “his accuser”, which gives the noun a slightly formal or dramatic flavour.
  • It is not a synonym for prosecutor: the prosecutor is the state's advocate, whereas the accuser is the source of the allegation, and in modern systems these are usually different people.
